## Introduction

In a rural area, a family might swap a goat for several bags of maize, or pay a builder partly in cattle. Exchanging goods directly for other goods is called **barter**. It still happens, but most trade in Zimbabwe now uses money: US dollar notes, Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG), the currency introduced by the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe in April 2024, bank cards, and above all mobile money, which lets a vendor in Mutare be paid by a customer's phone.

Zimbabwe's history also shows what happens when money stops working well. During the hyperinflation of 2007-2008, prices rose so fast that the Zimbabwe dollar lost its value within days, and people returned to barter and to foreign currencies; in 2009 the country moved to a multi-currency system. A local currency was re-introduced in 2019, and ZiG followed in 2024.

This lesson explains why money is needed, what it does, and what makes good money. These ideas come up in both syllabuses, from one-mark multiple-choice items to essays asking you to explain the functions of money.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Barter"}
The direct exchange of goods and services for other goods and services, without the use of money.
:::

:::definition{term="Double coincidence of wants"}
The need, in barter, for each trader to want exactly what the other has to offer, at the same time and in the right quantities.
:::

:::definition{term="Money"}
Anything that is generally accepted as a means of payment for goods and services and in settlement of debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Money supply"}
The total amount of money in an economy at a point in time, usually measured as notes and coins plus various types of bank deposits.
:::

## Barter and its problems

Barter can work in a small community with few goods, and it has some advantages: no money is needed, it builds relationships, and it is useful when money has lost its value. But as trade grows, barter becomes slow and costly.

- **Double coincidence of wants.** A farmer with a goat who needs a plough must find a blacksmith who has a plough **and** wants a goat. Finding such a person wastes time.
- **No common measure of value.** Every good must be priced in terms of every other good. How many bags of maize is a goat worth? How many chickens is a plough worth?
- **Indivisibility.** A cow cannot be split to buy a bag of sugar; the goods on offer may be worth much more than the goods wanted.
- **Difficulty of storing wealth.** Tomatoes rot, animals can die; it is hard to save for the future.
- **Difficulty of deferred payment.** A debt of "two goats next year" causes disputes: which goats, how big?

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: how many prices does barter need?]
With barter, each pair of goods needs its own rate of exchange. With $n$ goods the number of pairs is $\frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$.

1. In a village trading 10 goods: $\frac{10 \times 9}{2} = 45$ different exchange rates must be known.
2. In a market trading 40 goods: $\frac{40 \times 39}{2} = 780$ exchange rates.
3. With money, each good needs only one price: 10 prices for 10 goods, 40 prices for 40 goods.
4. This is why money works as a **unit of account**: it makes comparing values simple.
:::

## Forms of money

Money has taken many forms. What matters is that people **accept** it.

- **Commodity money:** goods with their own value used as money, such as cattle, salt, cowrie shells or gold coins.
- **Coins and notes (fiat money):** token money whose value comes from the government declaring it legal tender and from people's trust, not from the material it is made of.
- **Bank deposits:** money held in bank accounts and transferred by cheque, debit card or bank transfer. In most economies, bank deposits are by far the largest part of the money supply.
- **Electronic and mobile money:** balances held in electronic accounts and moved by card, internet banking or mobile phone. Mobile money is widely used in Zimbabwe for paying traders, school fees and bills.

Cheques, debit cards and mobile-money apps are not money themselves: they are **ways of transferring** money held in an account.

## Functions of money

1. **Medium of exchange.** Money is accepted in payment for goods and services, so the double coincidence of wants is no longer needed. The farmer sells the goat to anyone and uses the money to buy a plough from anyone. This allows specialisation and trade.
2. **Unit of account (measure of value).** Prices of all goods are stated in money, so values can be compared, and firms can keep accounts and calculate profit.
3. **Store of value.** Money can be saved and used later, as long as its value is stable. High inflation destroys this function: during hyperinflation people spend money immediately or switch to foreign currency or goods.
4. **Standard of deferred payment.** Debts and future payments (loans, wages, rents) can be agreed in money terms, making borrowing and lending easier.

## Characteristics of good money

| Characteristic | Meaning | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acceptability | Generally accepted in payment | Without acceptance it cannot be a medium of exchange |
| Durability | Lasts and does not rot or wear out quickly | Can be used many times and stored |
| Portability | Easy to carry, even in large values | Convenient for trade over distances |
| Divisibility | Can be split into small units | Allows small purchases and exact change |
| Homogeneity (uniformity) | Each unit of the same value is identical | Avoids disputes over quality |
| Limited supply (scarcity) | Its quantity is controlled | Keeps its value stable |
| Difficult to forge | Hard to copy | Protects trust in money |
| Stability of value | Its purchasing power changes little | Needed for the store-of-value and deferred-payment functions |

:::example[Example 2: why cattle and hyperinflation money both fail]
Cattle are valued and accepted in many rural areas, so they once served as money. But they are not divisible, not homogeneous (no two cows are worth the same), not portable, and they can die. Zimbabwe dollar notes in 2008 were portable, divisible and uniform, yet they failed for a different reason: their supply grew so fast that they lost their value within days, so they could not store value or serve for deferred payment, and people stopped accepting them. Good money needs **all** the characteristics, especially acceptability and a stable value.
:::

## Measuring the money supply (ZIMSEC)

Central banks measure the money supply in several ways, from the narrowest (most spendable) to the broadest. Definitions differ between countries; a common textbook version is:

- **M1 (narrow money):** notes and coins held by the public + demand (current account) deposits.
- **M2:** M1 + savings deposits and short-term time deposits.
- **M3 (broad money):** M2 + long-term (fixed) deposits, sometimes including deposits with other financial institutions.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 3: money supply in Country X]
A fictional central bank publishes these figures (in kora million, K):

| Item | Amount (K million) |
| --- | ---: |
| Notes and coins held by the public | 820 |
| Demand (current account) deposits | 1,460 |
| Savings and short-term deposits | 1,130 |
| Long-term (fixed) deposits | 690 |

1. M1 = $820 + 1{,}460 = 2{,}280$, that is K2,280 million.
2. M2 = $2{,}280 + 1{,}130 = 3{,}410$, K3,410 million.
3. M3 = $3{,}410 + 690 = 4{,}100$, K4,100 million.
4. Cash is only $820 \div 2{,}280 \times 100 \approx 36\%$ of M1: most money is held in bank deposits.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a cheque or a card money"}
A cheque, card or phone app is a way of **transferring** money in an account. The money is the bank or mobile-money balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up functions and characteristics"}
**Functions** are what money does (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, standard of deferred payment). **Characteristics** are what money must be like (acceptable, durable, portable, divisible...).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing functions without explaining them"}
In "explain" questions, say what each function means and why it matters, ideally with an example: "as a medium of exchange, money removes the need for a double coincidence of wants".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** one function or characteristic per line, using the exact term.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** link each problem of barter to the function of money that solves it: double coincidence → medium of exchange; no common measure → unit of account; perishable goods → store of value; disputes over future payment → standard of deferred payment.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** questions about mobile money or a new currency are answered with the characteristics: is it acceptable, portable, divisible, hard to forge, and stable in value?
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards ask for the **forms, functions and characteristics** of money. ZIMSEC also asks you to outline the money supply measures **M1, M2 and M3**. Cambridge often says **measure of value** or **unit of account**; both are correct.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Barter is inefficient: it needs a double coincidence of wants, has no common measure of value, and goods are hard to divide, store or use for debts.
- Money has been commodities, coins and notes, bank deposits, and electronic and mobile money.
- Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, standard of deferred payment.
- Good money is acceptable, durable, portable, divisible, uniform, limited in supply, hard to forge and stable in value.
- M1 is narrow money (cash + demand deposits); M2 and M3 add savings and long-term deposits.
:::
