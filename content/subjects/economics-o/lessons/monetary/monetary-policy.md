## Introduction

When a bank in Harare raises the interest rate on its loans, a kombi operator may decide not to borrow for a second vehicle, and a family may delay buying a fridge on credit. Multiply such decisions across the whole economy and spending changes. This is the idea behind **monetary policy**: by influencing interest rates, the amount of money in circulation and the exchange rate, a central bank can speed up or slow down the economy.

Zimbabwe's central bank is the **Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ)**. It issues the national currency (since April 2024, the Zimbabwe Gold, ZiG), supervises the banks and runs monetary policy. Zimbabwe's history shows why monetary policy matters: the very rapid creation of money contributed to the hyperinflation of 2007-2008, after which the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned in 2009 for a multi-currency system.

ZIMSEC asks you to distinguish monetary from fiscal policy and describe how monetary policy achieves economic objectives. Cambridge asks you to define the money supply and monetary policy, describe changes in the interest rate, the money supply and the exchange rate, and explain how they help achieve the macroeconomic aims.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Money supply"}
The total amount of money in an economy at a point in time: notes and coins in circulation plus bank deposits.
:::

:::definition{term="Monetary policy"}
The use of changes in interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate (usually by the central bank) to influence economic activity and achieve macroeconomic aims.
:::

:::definition{term="Interest rate"}
The cost of borrowing money and the reward for saving it, expressed as a percentage of the amount per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Exchange rate"}
The price of one currency expressed in terms of another, such as 25 kora per US dollar.
:::

:::definition{term="Central bank"}
The bank that manages a country's currency, money supply and interest rates, acts as banker to the government and the commercial banks, and is lender of last resort.
:::

## The tools of monetary policy

### 1. Interest rates

The central bank sets a key (policy) rate at which it lends to commercial banks, and the banks' own lending and deposit rates follow it.

**A rise in interest rates (tight or contractionary monetary policy):**

- borrowing becomes more expensive, so households borrow less for cars and houses, and firms invest less in machinery;
- saving becomes more rewarding, so households save more and spend less;
- borrowers with existing loans pay more interest, leaving less to spend.

Aggregate demand falls, which reduces **demand-pull inflation**, but output and employment may fall.

**A cut in interest rates (loose or expansionary monetary policy)** has the opposite effects: borrowing and spending rise, helping growth and employment, but inflation may increase.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the effect of higher interest rates]
A fictional central bank raises its rate, and banks raise their lending rate from 8% to 12% and their deposit rate from 4% to 7%.

| Interest rate on loans / on deposits | Yearly interest on a US\$10,000 loan (US\$) | Yearly interest on US\$5,000 of savings (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 8% / 4% | 800 | 200 |
| 12% / 7% | 1,200 | 350 |

1. Interest on the loan rises from 8% × 10,000 = US\$800 to 12% × 10,000 = US\$1,200 a year, an extra US\$400.
2. Interest on the savings rises from 4% × 5,000 = US\$200 to 7% × 5,000 = US\$350 a year.
3. A firm such as a small bakery in Masvingo may decide that a new oven is no longer worth the extra US\$400 interest, so investment falls; households are rewarded more for saving, so consumption falls.
4. With less borrowing and spending, aggregate demand falls and pressure on prices eases.
:::

### 2. The money supply

The central bank can reduce the growth of the money supply by, for example, selling government bonds (taking money out of the banking system), raising the share of deposits banks must keep in reserve, or limiting bank lending. With less money available for lending, interest rates tend to rise and spending falls. Increasing the money supply has the opposite effect. Creating money to pay for government spending (sometimes called "printing money") when output is not rising leads to inflation; in extreme cases, such as Zimbabwe in 2007-2008, to hyperinflation.

### 3. The exchange rate

A central bank can influence its currency's value by buying or selling it on the foreign exchange market, or through interest rates (higher rates attract foreign savings, raising demand for the currency).

- A **fall** in the currency's value (depreciation) makes **imports dearer** and **exports cheaper** for foreigners. Exports rise and imports fall, helping the current account and jobs in export industries, but dearer imported fuel and inputs can cause cost-push inflation.
- A **rise** in its value (appreciation) makes imports cheaper, which helps to reduce inflation, but makes exports dearer.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a fall in the exchange rate]
In a fictional country the currency, the kora (K), falls from K20 = US\$1 to K25 = US\$1.

| | K20 = US\$1 | K25 = US\$1 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Price in kora of a US\$400 import | K8,000 | K10,000 |
| Price in US\$ of a K50,000 export | US\$2,500 | US\$2,000 |

1. An imported part costing US\$400 now costs 400 × 25 = **K10,000** instead of 400 × 20 = K8,000: a rise of 25%.
2. An export priced at K50,000 now costs a foreign buyer 50,000 ÷ 25 = **US\$2,000** instead of 50,000 ÷ 20 = US\$2,500: a fall of 20%.
3. Exports become more competitive and imports less attractive, which helps the current account and export jobs.
4. But firms that rely on imported inputs face higher costs, which may raise prices: a risk to price stability.
:::

## Monetary policy and the macroeconomic aims

| Aim | Monetary measure | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price stability | Raise interest rates; slow money supply growth | Less borrowing and spending → lower demand-pull inflation |
| Economic growth and employment | Cut interest rates; expand money supply | Cheaper credit → more consumption and investment → more output and jobs |
| Balance of payments | Lower exchange rate (or lower interest rates) | Exports cheaper, imports dearer → current account improves |

**Limitations:** changes take time to work (often a year or more); if confidence is low, people may not borrow even at low rates; higher rates hurt borrowers and can raise firms' costs; in an economy where many people use cash or foreign currency, or where few borrow from banks, the effect of interest rates is weaker.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards contrast **monetary policy** (interest rates, the money supply, the exchange rate, run by the central bank) with **fiscal policy** (taxes and government spending, set in the budget). ZIMSEC teaches monetary policy with money and banking; Cambridge with the macroeconomic aims.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying higher interest rates increase spending"}
Higher interest rates make borrowing **dearer** and saving more **attractive**, so spending **falls**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up depreciation effects"}
A weaker currency makes **imports dearer** and **exports cheaper**. Check with a calculation: convert one price at both rates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a change in taxes monetary policy"}
Taxes and government spending are fiscal policy. Monetary policy works through money, credit, interest rates and the exchange rate.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define** monetary policy by naming all three instruments (interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate) and its purpose.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain how it controls inflation:** policy change → cost of borrowing / reward for saving → consumption and investment → aggregate demand → prices. Each link can earn a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** consider time lags, confidence, the effect on borrowers and firms, the exchange rate, and conflicts with growth and employment; then conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The money supply is notes, coins and bank deposits; monetary policy changes interest rates, the money supply and the exchange rate.
- The central bank (in Zimbabwe, the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe) runs monetary policy.
- Higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending, lowering demand-pull inflation; lower rates boost growth and jobs.
- A depreciation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, helping the current account but risking cost-push inflation.
- Monetary policy has time lags and works less well when confidence is low or few people borrow.
:::
