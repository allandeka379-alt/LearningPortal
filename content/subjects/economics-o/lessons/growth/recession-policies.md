## Introduction

Economies do not grow smoothly. In some years output rises quickly; in others it slows down or even falls. A drought can cut the maize and tobacco harvest; a fall in world prices of platinum or lithium can force mines to cut output; a world economic slowdown can mean fewer tourists at Victoria Falls. When a country's real output falls for a sustained period, it is in a **recession**.

Recessions matter because they bring job losses, falling incomes, business closures and pressure on government finances. This lesson is part of the Cambridge IGCSE syllabus (4.5): you must define recession, explain its causes and consequences, and **evaluate** the policies a government can use to promote growth. Evaluation questions ("Discuss whether…") are worth many marks, so learn the strengths *and* limits of each policy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Recession"}
A period of negative economic growth: a fall in a country's real GDP, commonly defined as a fall over two consecutive quarters (six months).
:::

:::definition{term="Aggregate (total) demand"}
The total spending on a country's goods and services: consumption + investment + government spending + (exports − imports).
:::

:::definition{term="Expansionary (reflationary) fiscal policy"}
Raising government spending and/or cutting taxes to increase total demand.
:::

:::definition{term="Expansionary monetary policy"}
Lowering interest rates and/or increasing the money supply to encourage borrowing and spending.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply-side policy"}
Measures that increase the quantity or quality of resources and the efficiency of markets, raising an economy's productive capacity.
:::

## Identifying a recession from data

Countries publish real GDP every quarter (three months). A recession is shown by **negative** changes in real GDP, not by a smaller positive change.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: spotting a recession in quarterly data]
Country Z is a fictional economy. Real GDP is shown at constant prices.

| Quarter | Real GDP (US\$ million) | Change on previous quarter |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 Q1 | 5,000 | – |
| 2025 Q2 | 5,050 | +1.00% |
| 2025 Q3 | 4,990 | −1.19% |
| 2025 Q4 | 4,940 | −1.00% |
| 2026 Q1 | 4,960 | +0.40% |

1. Q2 change = (5,050 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = +1.00%.
2. Q3 change = (4,990 − 5,050) ÷ 5,050 × 100 = −1.19%; Q4 change = (4,940 − 4,990) ÷ 4,990 × 100 = −1.00%.
3. Real GDP fell in **two consecutive quarters** (Q3 and Q4 of 2025), so Country Z was in recession in the second half of 2025.
4. In 2026 Q1 real GDP rose again (+0.40%), so the recession ended, although output (4,960) was still below its peak (5,050).
:::

## Causes of recession

A recession happens when output falls. There are two broad causes.

**1. A fall in total (aggregate) demand.** Firms cut output when they cannot sell what they produce.
- **Consumption** falls if households lose confidence, face higher taxes or higher interest rates, or if prices rise faster than incomes.
- **Investment** falls if firms expect poor sales or if borrowing becomes dearer.
- **Government spending** may be cut to reduce a budget deficit.
- **Exports** fall when trading partners are in recession, when world commodity prices fall, or when the exchange rate rises.

**2. A fall in the quantity or quality of resources (a supply shock).**
- Drought, floods or cyclones destroy crops, livestock and infrastructure.
- Electricity shortages and fuel shortages stop factories and mines working.
- Emigration of skilled workers, war or disease reduce the labour force and its productivity.
- A sharp rise in the price of an essential import, such as fuel, raises costs and forces firms to cut output.

## Consequences of recession

| Group | Likely effects |
| --- | --- |
| Consumers | Lower incomes and confidence; spending falls; some cannot repay loans; fewer goods and services are bought; prices may rise more slowly. |
| Workers | Firms cut jobs and hours, so **cyclical unemployment** rises; wage rises are smaller; the unemployed lose skills over time; some emigrate. |
| Firms | Sales and profits fall; investment is postponed; some firms close; but firms selling cheap essential goods may gain customers. |
| Government | **Tax revenue falls** (less income, spending and profit to tax) while spending on support for the poor and unemployed rises, so the **budget deficit widens**; imports fall, which may improve the current account. |

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a recession and the government budget]
In a fictional country, tax revenue is 22% of GDP. GDP falls by 3% from US\$18,000 million, and government spending on support for poor households rises from US\$900 million to US\$1,050 million.

1. Tax revenue before = 22% × 18,000 = US\$3,960 million.
2. GDP after = 18,000 × 0.97 = US\$17,460 million, so tax revenue after = 22% × 17,460 = US\$3,841.2 million.
3. Tax revenue falls by 3,960 − 3,841.2 = US\$118.8 million.
4. Spending on support rises by 1,050 − 900 = US\$150 million.
5. The budget balance worsens by 118.8 + 150 = **US\$268.8 million**, even though the government changed no tax rate.
:::

## Policies to promote economic growth

### Fiscal policy (expansionary)

The government raises its own spending (for example on roads, dams, schools and clinics) or cuts taxes so that households and firms have more to spend.

- **Strengths:** government spending adds directly to demand; infrastructure spending also raises future productive capacity; tax cuts can target low-income households, who spend most of any extra income.
- **Limits:** it widens the budget deficit and increases government debt; time lags in planning and building projects; if people save tax cuts, demand rises little; if spending goes mainly on imports, domestic output rises little; at full capacity it causes inflation.

### Monetary policy (expansionary)

The central bank (in Zimbabwe, the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe) cuts interest rates or increases the money supply.

- **Strengths:** cheaper loans encourage households to buy durable goods and firms to invest; lower rates may weaken the exchange rate, making exports cheaper; it can be changed quickly.
- **Limits:** if confidence is very low, people and firms may not borrow even at low rates; banks may not lend; lower interest rates reduce the income of savers; too much money creation causes inflation; time lags of a year or more.

### Supply-side policy

Measures such as education and training, investment in infrastructure, lower taxes on profits or income to improve incentives, privatisation, deregulation and help for small businesses.

- **Strengths:** they increase productive capacity, so growth can continue without inflation; they tackle structural problems such as skill shortages and poor infrastructure.
- **Limits:** many take years to work (a training programme takes years to produce skilled workers); they can be costly; some, such as cutting regulation or benefits, may increase inequality or harm the environment.

:::example[Choosing a policy for the cause]
A fictional country enters recession because its main trading partners are in recession and buy fewer of its exports. Total demand has fallen, so the most direct response is to raise demand: lower interest rates and more government spending on infrastructure. If instead the recession was caused by electricity shortages that stop mines and factories, extra demand would mainly raise prices; the right response is supply-side, such as investment in power generation and transmission. **Match the policy to the cause.**
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling slower growth a recession"}
If growth falls from 4% to 1%, output is still rising. A recession requires real GDP to **fall**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a recession always reduces the budget deficit"}
In a recession tax revenue falls and spending on support rises, so the deficit usually **widens**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaluating without a conclusion"}
A *discuss* answer that lists policies without judging which is best for the situation stays in the lower levels.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** a recession is a period of negative economic growth: real GDP falls, usually for two consecutive quarters.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain consequences:** use the four groups (consumers, workers, firms, government) and link each effect to falling output, for example: lower output → firms need fewer workers → cyclical unemployment rises.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Evaluate policies:** for each policy explain how it raises output, then give a limit (time lag, deficit, inflation, confidence). Conclude by matching the policy to the **cause** of the recession and by noting that a combination usually works best.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A recession is a fall in real GDP, commonly two consecutive quarters of negative growth.
- Causes: falls in total demand (consumption, investment, government spending, exports) or in the quantity and quality of resources (drought, power shortages, emigration).
- Consequences: lower incomes, cyclical unemployment, falling profits and closures, lower tax revenue and a wider budget deficit.
- Fiscal and monetary policy raise total demand; supply-side policy raises productive capacity.
- Each policy has limits: deficits, time lags, low confidence, inflation and inequality. The best choice depends on the cause.
:::
