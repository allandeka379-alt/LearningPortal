## Introduction

When a new lithium processing plant opens, when farmers in Mashonaland harvest a bigger tobacco crop, or when more tourists stay at hotels near Victoria Falls, the country produces more goods and services than before. If this happens across the whole economy, year after year, the country is experiencing **economic growth**.

Growth matters because it is the main way in which a country can raise incomes, create jobs and pay for schools, clinics and roads. But growth is not automatically good: it can damage the environment, use up minerals that cannot be replaced, and leave many people no better off if the extra income goes to only a few.

Both syllabuses ask you to define economic growth, explain how it is measured (Cambridge stresses **real GDP**), explain its causes and discuss its advantages and disadvantages. ZIMSEC also asks you to identify the **signs** of growth. Expect calculation questions on nominal and real GDP, and *discuss* questions on whether growth is always desirable.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic growth"}
An increase in the real output of goods and services produced in an economy over a period of time, usually measured by the percentage change in real GDP from one year to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross domestic product (GDP)"}
The total value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Nominal GDP"}
GDP measured at the prices of the year in which the output was produced (current prices). It can rise just because prices rise.
:::

:::definition{term="Real GDP"}
GDP adjusted for inflation, measured at the prices of a chosen base year (constant prices). It rises only when the quantity of output rises.
:::

:::definition{term="Potential output"}
The output an economy could produce if all its resources were fully and efficiently employed. An increase in potential output is sometimes called long-run growth.
:::

:::formula
Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100

Economic growth rate (%) = (real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100
:::

## Measuring growth: why real GDP?

Suppose a country produces exactly the same quantity of maize, cement and haircuts this year as last year, but every price is 10% higher. Nominal GDP rises by 10%, yet nobody has more goods. That is why growth is measured with **real** GDP, which removes the effect of price changes.

![Country X GDP: nominal GDP rises from US\$48,000 million in 2025 to US\$54,000 million in 2026 (12.5 percent) but real GDP in 2025 prices rises only from 48,000 to 49,090.9 million (2.3 percent), because prices rose 10 percent.](media:diagrams/nominal-real-gdp.png "Figure: most of the rise in nominal GDP is higher prices; real GDP grew far less")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: nominal and real GDP in Country X]
Country X is a fictional economy.

| Year | Nominal GDP (US\$ million) | Price index (2025 = 100) | Real GDP in 2025 prices (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 | 48,000 | 100 | 48,000 |
| 2026 | 54,000 | 110 | 49,090.9 |

1. Nominal growth = (54,000 − 48,000) ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **12.5%**.
2. Real GDP in 2026 = 54,000 ÷ 110 × 100 = **US\$49,090.9 million** (2025 prices).
3. Real growth = (49,090.9 − 48,000) ÷ 48,000 × 100 = **2.3%** (to one decimal place).
4. Most of the 12.5% rise in nominal GDP was caused by higher prices. The quantity of output grew by only about 2.3%.
:::

### Real GDP per head

Real GDP tells us about the size of the economy. To judge whether the **average person** has more goods, divide real GDP by the population: **real GDP per head (per capita)**. If population grows faster than real GDP, real GDP per head falls even though the economy has grown.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: growth that does not raise output per person]
| Year | Real GDP (US\$ million) | Population (million) | Real GDP per head (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2025 | 20,000 | 16.0 | 1,250.00 |
| 2026 | 20,600 | 16.5 | 1,248.48 |

1. Real GDP grew by (20,600 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 3%.
2. Population grew by (16.5 − 16.0) ÷ 16.0 × 100 = 3.125%.
3. Real GDP per head fell from US\$1,250.00 to 20,600 ÷ 16.5 = US\$1,248.48.
4. The economy grew, but output per person fell slightly, because population grew faster than output.
:::

### Measurement problems

GDP figures are estimates. In Zimbabwe a large part of economic activity takes place in the **informal sector** (vendors, small-scale farmers, artisans, cross-border traders) and in subsistence farming, which are hard to record. Inflation must be measured accurately before real GDP can be calculated. For the latest official figures, look up ZIMSTAT's national accounts and note the year and the base year used.

## Signs of economic growth

A rise in real output usually shows itself in everyday life. Signs include:

- more goods in shops and more variety of products;
- new factories, mines, shops and businesses opening, and existing firms expanding;
- rising employment and falling unemployment;
- higher incomes and more spending, for example more vehicles on the roads and more mobile phones;
- more tax revenue for the government and more building of roads, dams and houses.

## Causes of economic growth

Growth comes from either **more total spending** (aggregate demand) on the country's output or **more and better resources** that raise the economy's productive capacity.

**1. Increases in total demand.** If households spend more, firms invest more, the government spends more or foreigners buy more exports, firms raise output, *provided there are unused resources* (unemployed workers, idle machines). This uses spare capacity. Once resources are fully employed, extra spending mainly raises prices.

**2. Increases in the quantity of resources.**
- More capital through **investment** in machinery, mines, irrigation and roads.
- A larger labour force, for example through population growth or more women entering paid work.
- Discovering or developing natural resources, such as new mineral deposits or newly irrigated land.

**3. Improvements in the quality of resources.**
- **Education and training** that raise workers' skills (human capital).
- Better **healthcare**, so workers are fitter and absent less often.
- **New technology** and better methods of production.
- Better infrastructure (electricity, roads, communication), which raises the productivity of all other resources.

In terms of the production possibility curve, an increase in the quantity or quality of resources shifts the **whole curve outwards**, while using unemployed resources moves the economy from a point **inside** the curve towards the curve.

:::example[Applying the causes]
A government builds a dam and irrigation canals in a dry district. Farmers who could grow crops only in the rainy season can now grow two crops a year. The quantity of usable land and the productivity of farm labour both increase, so the economy's productive capacity rises: this is a cause of long-run growth. Building the dam also adds to total demand while construction takes place.
:::

## Benefits of economic growth

- **Higher living standards:** more goods and services per person, if output grows faster than population.
- **More jobs:** firms need more workers, so unemployment falls.
- **More tax revenue** without raising tax rates, which the government can spend on education, health and infrastructure, or use to reduce debt.
- **Less poverty:** higher incomes can lift households above the poverty line.
- **More investment and confidence:** growing markets encourage firms, including foreign firms, to invest.

## Costs of economic growth

- **Environmental damage:** more output can mean more pollution, deforestation and land degradation, and higher greenhouse gas emissions.
- **Depletion of non-renewable resources:** mining minerals such as gold, platinum or lithium uses up resources future generations cannot use.
- **Unequal distribution:** the gains may go mainly to owners of firms or to some regions, so income inequality may widen.
- **Inflation:** if demand grows faster than productive capacity, prices rise.
- **Opportunity cost:** to raise investment now, consumption must be lower now.
- **Social costs:** longer working hours, stress, congestion and the movement of people to crowded towns.
- **Current account problems:** rising incomes increase spending on imports.

Whether growth is desirable depends on its **rate**, whether it is **sustainable**, and **who benefits**.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using nominal GDP to measure growth"}
A rise in nominal GDP may be caused by higher prices alone. Growth is measured by the change in **real** GDP.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a fall in the growth rate with a fall in output"}
If the growth rate falls from 5% to 2%, output is still **rising**, only more slowly. Output falls only when the growth rate is negative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming growth makes everyone better off"}
Real GDP may grow while real GDP per head falls (population growing faster) or while the gains go to a small group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define / state:** economic growth is an increase in a country's **real** output (real GDP) over time. Always include the word *real*.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** convert nominal to real GDP with nominal ÷ price index × 100 before working out the percentage change, and divide by the **earlier** year's value.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain a cause:** link the cause to output in a chain, for example: more investment in machinery → more capital per worker → higher productivity → more output.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss whether growth is beneficial:** give benefits (jobs, incomes, tax revenue) and costs (pollution, depletion, inequality, inflation), then conclude on what it depends on: the rate, sustainability and how the gains are shared.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic growth is an increase in real output (real GDP) over time.
- Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100; growth rate = change in real GDP ÷ previous real GDP × 100.
- Real GDP per head shows output per person; it falls if population grows faster than real GDP.
- Signs of growth include new firms, more jobs, more goods and higher incomes.
- Causes: more total demand (using spare capacity) and more or better resources (investment, labour, education, health, technology, infrastructure).
- Benefits: higher living standards, jobs, tax revenue, less poverty. Costs: pollution, depletion, inequality, inflation, opportunity cost.
:::
