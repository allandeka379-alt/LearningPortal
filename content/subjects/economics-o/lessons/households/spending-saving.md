## Introduction

Every month a household decides what to do with its income. Nyasha, a teacher in Masvingo, pays rent, buys food and transport, puts a little into her savings club, and is thinking about borrowing to buy a solar system for her home. Her neighbour, a retired railway worker, lives partly on his savings. A young couple across the road have just taken a loan for furniture.

Households are the largest spenders in most economies, so their choices about **spending**, **saving** and **borrowing** affect firms' sales, jobs, the amount banks can lend, and how fast the economy grows. The Cambridge syllabus (3.2) asks you to explain how **income**, the **rate of interest**, **confidence**, **age** and **culture** influence these choices. You should already know what a commercial bank does.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Disposable income"}
Income left after direct taxes (such as income tax) have been paid and any state benefits received; it is what a household can spend or save.
:::

:::definition{term="Spending (consumption)"}
Household expenditure on goods and services for immediate use, such as food, clothing, rent, transport and airtime.
:::

:::definition{term="Saving"}
The part of disposable income that is not spent: saving = disposable income − spending.
:::

:::definition{term="Borrowing"}
Obtaining money now (from a bank, microlender, savings club or relatives) that must be repaid later, usually with interest.
:::

:::definition{term="Rate of interest"}
The price of borrowing money and the reward for saving it, expressed as a percentage of the amount per year.
:::

:::formula
Saving = disposable income − spending

Saving as a percentage of income = saving ÷ disposable income × 100
:::

## Income

Income is the most important influence on spending and saving.

- As income rises, households spend **more** in total, but they usually spend a **smaller proportion** of their income and save a **larger proportion**. A poor household must spend almost everything on necessities; a rich household can buy all it needs and still have money left over.
- Higher income also makes borrowing easier: banks lend more readily to people with a steady, high income, who can show they can repay.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: income and the share saved]
Three fictional households, monthly figures:

| Household | Disposable income (US\$) | Spending (US\$) | Saving (US\$) | Saving as % of income | Spending as % of income |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Household A | 300 | 290 | 10 | 3.3% | 96.7% |
| Household B | 900 | 810 | 90 | 10.0% | 90.0% |
| Household C | 2,400 | 1,920 | 480 | 20.0% | 80.0% |

1. Saving for household C = $2{,}400 - 1{,}920 = 480$ dollars.
2. As a percentage: $480 \div 2{,}400 \times 100 = 20\%$.
3. As income rises from US\$300 to US\$2,400, spending rises in dollars (US\$290 to US\$1,920) but falls as a share of income (96.7% to 80.0%), and the share saved rises (3.3% to 20.0%).
4. So a shift of income from rich to poor households tends to raise total spending, because the poor spend a larger share of each extra dollar.
:::

## The rate of interest

The rate of interest is both the **reward for saving** and the **cost of borrowing**.

- A **higher** rate of interest makes saving more attractive and borrowing more expensive, so households tend to **save more, borrow less and spend less**, especially on items usually bought on credit, such as cars, furniture and houses.
- A **lower** rate of interest has the opposite effect: saving earns little, loans are cheaper, so spending and borrowing rise.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: how the interest rate changes the cost of a loan]
Farai wants to borrow US\$1,500 for one year to buy a fridge.

1. At 8% simple interest: interest = $1{,}500 \times 0.08 = 120$, so he repays US\$1,620.
2. At 16%: interest = $1{,}500 \times 0.16 = 240$, so he repays US\$1,740.
3. Doubling the rate doubles the interest; some borrowers will now delay the purchase.
4. For a saver with US\$2,000 in a fixed deposit, a rise from 3% to 9% raises yearly interest from $2{,}000 \times 0.03 = 60$ to $2{,}000 \times 0.09 = 180$ dollars, a stronger reason to save.
:::

The effect is not always large: people with very low incomes have little to save whatever the rate, and some people save for a fixed goal (for example school fees) and need to save **less** when interest rates rise to reach it.

## Confidence

**Consumer confidence** is how optimistic households feel about their future incomes and job security.

- When confidence is **high** (the economy is growing, jobs seem safe, prices are stable), households spend more, save less and are willing to borrow.
- When confidence is **low** (fear of job losses, rising prices, political or currency uncertainty), households cut spending, save more "for a rainy day" and avoid new debt. In an economy with unstable money, households may also prefer to hold goods, livestock or foreign currency rather than save in local bank accounts.

## Age

Spending and saving change over a person's life:

- **Young adults** often have low incomes but high spending needs (setting up a home, starting a family), so they **borrow** and save little.
- **Middle-aged people** usually earn the most, so they **save** more, for example for retirement and children's education, and pay off debts.
- **Retired people** have lower incomes and often **dissave**: they spend their past savings or pensions. They usually borrow little.

A country with many young people may therefore have low saving and high borrowing; one with many people approaching retirement may have higher saving.

## Culture

Social attitudes and traditions also matter.

- In some societies debt is seen as shameful, so people avoid borrowing; in others, buying on credit is normal.
- Saving traditions differ: rotating savings clubs, in which members pay in each month and take turns to receive the pot, are common in Zimbabwe and help people save for large purchases. Many families also save in the form of livestock.
- Social obligations, such as supporting extended family members, weddings and funerals, can raise spending and reduce saving.
- Religious rules in some cultures forbid charging or paying interest, so saving and borrowing take other forms.

:::example[Example 3: putting the factors together]
In a fictional country, interest rates fall from 14% to 9%, unemployment is falling, and many young people are entering work. Lower interest rates make loans cheaper and saving less rewarding; rising confidence encourages spending and borrowing; young workers tend to borrow to set up homes. All three factors point the same way: household **spending and borrowing are likely to rise and saving to fall**. Shops and car dealers gain, but banks may worry about rising debts if confidence later falls.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying richer people spend less"}
Richer households spend **more** in total, but a **smaller percentage** of their income. Be precise about totals versus proportions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one effect of interest rates"}
A change in the rate of interest affects **both** saving (the reward) and borrowing (the cost). Mention both to explain the effect on spending.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the link between saving and spending"}
With a given disposable income, more saving means less spending. Say so when you explain an influence.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** use a chain: factor changes → effect on saving or borrowing → effect on spending, for example "higher interest rates raise the cost of loans, so households borrow less and spend less on cars and furniture".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse data:** calculate saving and the percentage of income saved, then describe the pattern as income changes.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** when asked whether a change (say, a cut in interest rates) will raise spending, consider the other influences (confidence, income, age structure, culture) that may weaken or strengthen the effect, and conclude.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
This is a Cambridge topic (3.2 Households); it is not in the ZIMSEC syllabus. Cambridge uses **spending, saving and borrowing**; you may also see **consumption** used for spending.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Saving = disposable income − spending; households can also borrow to spend more than their income.
- Higher income raises total spending but lowers the share spent and raises the share saved.
- A higher rate of interest encourages saving and discourages borrowing and spending.
- High confidence raises spending and borrowing; low confidence raises saving.
- Age (borrow when young, save in middle age, dissave when retired) and culture also shape these choices.
:::
