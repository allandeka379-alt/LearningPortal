## Introduction

Two countries can have the same population but very different numbers of workers. In one, most people are children and many young adults are still in school; in the other, most people are of working age and many women have paid jobs. The **labour force** (the economically active population) is the part of the population that produces the country's goods and services, so its size and quality matter for output, growth and living standards.

The ZIMSEC syllabus (Form 4) asks you to relate the composition of the population to the labour force, explain what affects the labour force's size and composition, and describe ways of controlling population. This lesson links the population topic with the labour and unemployment topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Labour force (economically active population, working population)"}
All the people of working age who are either employed or unemployed and actively seeking work. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
:::

:::definition{term="Working-age population"}
People of an age at which they could normally work. Textbooks commonly use ages 15 to 64; statistics offices may use different limits, so check the definition used in ZIMSTAT's labour force survey report.
:::

:::definition{term="Economically inactive"}
People of working age who are neither working nor seeking work, for example full-time students, homemakers, the retired, the long-term sick and discouraged workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour force participation rate"}
The labour force as a percentage of the working-age population.
:::

:::formula
Labour force = employed + unemployed

Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100
:::

## From population to labour force

The total population divides into people below working age, people of working age and people above it. Of those of working age, some are economically active (employed or unemployed) and some are inactive. So the size of the labour force depends on **how many people are of working age** and on **what share of them are active**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the labour force of Country X]
Fictional Country X (figures in thousands):

| Group | People (thousands) |
| --- | ---: |
| Total population | 15,600 |
| Working-age population | 8,700 |
| Employed | 5,220 |
| Unemployed (seeking work) | 870 |
| Economically inactive of working age | 2,610 |

1. Labour force = 5,220 + 870 = **6,090 thousand**.
2. Participation rate = 6,090 ÷ 8,700 × 100 = **70%**.
3. Check: inactive = 8,700 − 6,090 = 2,610 thousand, matching the table.
4. The labour force is only 6,090 ÷ 15,600 × 100 = about **39%** of the whole population, because many people are children, elderly or inactive. Each member of the labour force supports, on average, more than one other person.
:::

## Composition of the labour force

The labour force can be described by:
- **age** (young, prime-age and older workers);
- **sex** (the shares of men and women);
- **skills and education** (unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, professional);
- **sector** (primary, secondary, tertiary) and **formal or informal** employment;
- **location** (urban or rural).

A labour force with more educated, skilled and healthy workers is more productive, even if it is no larger.

## Factors affecting the size of the labour force

- **Size of the population** and its **age structure**: a population with many people of working age has a large labour force; a young or ageing population has a smaller one relative to its size.
- **School-leaving age and time spent in education**: more years in school or college reduces the labour force now but raises its quality later.
- **Retirement age** and pension arrangements: a later retirement age keeps people in the labour force longer.
- **Participation of women**: social attitudes, childcare, education of girls and job opportunities affect how many women seek paid work.
- **Migration**: emigration of working-age people reduces the labour force; immigration increases it.
- **Health**: illness and disability (for example the HIV epidemic before treatment was widespread) reduce the number able to work.
- **Wages and job opportunities**: higher wages and more vacancies draw people into the labour force; few vacancies create discouraged workers who stop looking.
- **Government policy**: education, pensions, childcare support, immigration rules and labour laws.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: how policies change the labour force]
Country X (from example 1) has a labour force of 6,090 thousand. The government raises the school-leaving age, so 120 thousand young people stay in school instead of seeking work; raises the retirement age, so 45 thousand older workers stay in work; and improves childcare, so 96 thousand women join the labour force.

1. New labour force = 6,090 − 120 + 45 + 96 = **6,111 thousand**.
2. Net change = +21 thousand, a rise of 21 ÷ 6,090 × 100 = about **0.3%**.
3. The policies also change the **composition** of the labour force: fewer teenage workers, more older workers and more women. Keeping young people in school lowers the labour force now but should make it more skilled in future.
:::

## Factors affecting the composition of the labour force

- **Education and training**: vocational colleges, apprenticeships and universities raise the share of skilled workers.
- **Government policy**: free or subsidised education, skills programmes, policies to promote women's employment, rules on child labour.
- **Structure of the economy**: as the economy develops, more workers move from farming into manufacturing and services.
- **Migration**: emigration of skilled workers (the brain drain) reduces the share of skilled workers; returning migrants may bring skills back.
- **Health and life expectancy**: these affect the age profile of workers and the experience they bring.

## Ways of controlling population growth

Where rapid population growth means the labour force grows faster than jobs, governments may try to slow it. Measures include:

- **Family planning** services and contraception available at clinics.
- **Education**, especially of girls: educated women tend to marry later and have fewer children.
- **Better healthcare** that reduces infant mortality, so parents do not need many children to ensure some survive.
- **Raising the legal age of marriage** and enforcing it.
- **Economic incentives**: tax allowances or benefits limited to a certain number of children.
- **Public awareness campaigns** about the costs of large families.
- **Migration policies** that control immigration.

Measures should respect people's rights to choose. Voluntary measures (education, family planning, health) are widely accepted; compulsory limits on family size are controversial and can have serious social costs. Some countries with falling or ageing populations do the opposite and encourage larger families or immigration.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating the labour force with the working-age population"}
The labour force includes only those of working age who are **employed or seeking work**; students, homemakers and the retired are not in it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving the unemployed out of the labour force"}
The unemployed **are** part of the labour force, because they are actively looking for work.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking more education always shrinks the labour force"}
It reduces the labour force in the short run but raises its quality and productivity in the long run.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Relate population to labour force:** explain that the labour force depends on the age structure (how many are of working age) and the participation rate (how many of those are active).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain a factor:** say which way it changes the labour force and why, for example: later retirement → older people keep working → labour force rises.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss population control:** describe two or three measures, explain how each reduces births, give limitations (cost, culture, rights, time), and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The labour force is everyone of working age who is employed or unemployed and seeking work.
- Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100.
- The size of the labour force depends on population size and age structure, years in education, retirement age, women's participation, migration, health, job opportunities and government policy.
- Its composition (age, sex, skills, sector) depends on education and training, government policy, the structure of the economy and migration.
- Population growth can be controlled through family planning, education of girls, healthcare, marriage laws, incentives and awareness campaigns.
:::
