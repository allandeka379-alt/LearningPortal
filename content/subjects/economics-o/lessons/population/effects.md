## Introduction

Is a large population good or bad for an economy? A growing population supplies more workers and more customers for firms, but it also needs more food, schools, clinics and jobs. A country with many children must spend heavily on education; a country with many elderly people must spend heavily on pensions and healthcare. Whether population helps or hinders development depends on its **size relative to resources** and on its **structure** (the balance of ages and of males and females).

ZIMSEC asks you to identify high, low and sustainable population sizes and their effects, and to discuss how population size and composition affect economic development. Cambridge asks you to explain the concept of an **optimum population** and the effects of changes in the size and in the age and gender structure of a population. Expect calculations of the dependency ratio and output per head, and *discuss* questions about ageing or fast-growing populations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Optimum population"}
The size of population that, with a country's existing resources and technology, gives the highest output (income) per head.
:::

:::definition{term="Over-population"}
A situation in which a country has more people than its resources and technology can support at the highest output per head: output per head would rise if the population were smaller.
:::

:::definition{term="Under-population"}
A situation in which a country has too few people to make full use of its resources: output per head would rise if the population were larger.
:::

:::definition{term="Dependency ratio"}
The number of dependants (young people, usually under 15, and elderly people, usually 65 and over) compared with the working-age population (usually 15 to 64), often expressed per 100 people of working age.
:::

:::formula
Dependency ratio = (young dependants + elderly dependants) ÷ working-age population × 100
:::

:::definition{term="Ageing population"}
A population in which the average age is rising and the share of elderly people is increasing, usually because of falling birth rates and longer life expectancy.
:::

## Optimum, over- and under-population

With fixed land, capital and technology, adding people at first raises output per head: more workers allow specialisation and full use of resources. Beyond a point, each extra worker adds less (diminishing returns), and output per head falls.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: finding the optimum population]
A fictional country has fixed land and technology. The table shows its total output at different sizes of population.

| Population (million) | Total output (US\$ million) | Output per head (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 3,000 | 1,500 |
| 4 | 7,200 | 1,800 |
| 6 | 12,000 | 2,000 |
| 8 | 14,400 | 1,800 |
| 10 | 15,000 | 1,500 |

1. Output per head = total output ÷ population, for example 12,000 ÷ 6 = US\$2,000.
2. Output per head is highest (US\$2,000) at a population of **6 million**: this is the optimum population.
3. Below 6 million the country is **under-populated**: more people would raise output per head.
4. Above 6 million it is **over-populated**: total output still rises, but output per head falls.
5. The optimum is not fixed: new technology, investment or the discovery of resources raises total output at every size of population and can raise the optimum.
:::

**Signs of over-population:** high unemployment and underemployment, shortages of land, housing, water and services, overcrowded schools and clinics, environmental damage, low output per head. **Signs of under-population:** labour shortages, resources unused, high wages, markets too small for firms to benefit from economies of scale.

## Effects of an increase in population size

**Possible benefits**
- A larger labour force, which can raise output if there are jobs and capital.
- A larger market, so firms can produce on a larger scale and lower their average costs (economies of scale).
- More mobile and adaptable young workers who bring new ideas.
- More taxpayers, if they are employed.

**Possible costs**
- More spending on schools, clinics, housing and infrastructure, which may not keep up.
- Higher unemployment if job creation is slower than labour force growth.
- Pressure on land, water and forests; environmental damage.
- Lower output per head if the country is already above its optimum.
- More imports of food and other goods, which may worsen the current account.

## Effects of a decrease in population size

A falling population (because of low birth rates, disease or emigration) can mean **labour shortages**, a smaller market, a smaller tax base, unused schools and housing in some areas, and often an older population. On the other hand, there is less pressure on land, services and the environment, and output per head may rise if the country was over-populated.

## Changes in age structure

### A young population (high birth rate)

Many children means a high **youth dependency ratio**: government must spend a lot on education and child health, and families spend much of their income on children, so saving and investment are low. Later, when the children reach working age, there will be a large labour force needing jobs. If those jobs exist, a country can gain a "demographic dividend" of faster growth.

### An ageing population (low birth rate, longer life expectancy)

More elderly people means a higher **old-age dependency ratio**: more spending on pensions, healthcare and care homes, paid for by a shrinking share of workers through higher taxes; possible labour shortages and slower growth; a less mobile workforce. But older people also bring experience, and new markets develop for goods and services for the elderly.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: calculating a dependency ratio]
A fictional country has 6.3 million people under 15, 8.4 million aged 15 to 64 and 0.42 million aged 65 and over.

1. Dependants = 6.3 + 0.42 = 6.72 million.
2. Dependency ratio = 6.72 ÷ 8.4 × 100 = **80**: there are 80 dependants for every 100 people of working age.
3. Youth dependency = 6.3 ÷ 8.4 × 100 = 75; old-age dependency = 0.42 ÷ 8.4 × 100 = 5.
4. Almost all dependants are children, so the burden falls on education and child health. If many working-age people are unemployed, the real burden on each **worker** is even higher than the ratio suggests.
:::

## Changes in gender structure

The balance between males and females can change because of migration, differences in life expectancy, or disease. For example, if many men migrate to towns, mines or other countries for work, rural areas have more women, many heading households and farming alone, while towns have more men. Effects include changes in the labour supply of different occupations, in household incomes (remittances) and in the demand for particular goods and services. Where women have less access to education and paid work, an imbalance can increase poverty.

## Population and economic development

- Rapid population growth can slow development by spreading output over more people, raising dependency, reducing saving and stretching services.
- Slow or negative growth with ageing can slow development through labour shortages and higher pension costs.
- A well-educated, healthy population of working age is a major **resource**: development depends on the **quality** of the population (skills, health) as much as its size.
- Population policies (education of girls, family planning, healthcare, managing migration) can help a country move towards its optimum.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Defining optimum population as the largest or smallest population"}
The optimum is the population that gives the **highest output per head** with current resources and technology.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting all working-age people as workers"}
The dependency ratio uses the working-age population, but some of them are unemployed or not in the labour force, so the true burden on workers may be greater.
:::

:::mistake{title="Seeing population growth as only bad (or only good)"}
Its effects depend on whether there are jobs, capital and resources. Give both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain optimum population:** use output per head, and say that technology and investment can change the optimum.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate the dependency ratio:** add the young and the elderly, divide by the working-age population and multiply by 100. State what the figure means.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss the effects of an ageing or young population:** give effects on government spending, the labour force, saving and markets, then conclude on what the effect depends on (jobs, productivity, policy).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The optimum population gives the highest output per head with existing resources and technology; above it a country is over-populated, below it under-populated.
- A larger population brings more workers and bigger markets, but also more pressure on services, land and jobs.
- A falling population can cause labour shortages and a smaller market, but eases pressure on resources.
- The dependency ratio = dependants ÷ working-age population × 100; young populations need education spending, ageing populations need pensions and healthcare.
- Gender imbalances, often from migration, affect labour supply and household incomes.
- The quality of a population (skills, health) matters as much as its size for development.
:::
