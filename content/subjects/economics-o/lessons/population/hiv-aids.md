## Introduction

HIV (the human immunodeficiency virus) weakens the body's immune system. Without treatment, it can lead to AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome), when the body can no longer fight infections. Southern Africa, including Zimbabwe, has been among the regions most affected by the HIV epidemic. In the years before treatment was widely available, many young and working-age adults fell ill and died, leaving orphans, grieving families and gaps in workplaces and schools.

Today the picture has changed a great deal. **Antiretroviral therapy (ART)** allows people living with HIV to live long, healthy and productive lives, and prevention programmes have reduced new infections. HIV is a health condition like any other: people living with HIV have the same rights as everyone else, and stigma and discrimination make the epidemic worse by discouraging people from testing and seeking treatment.

The ZIMSEC syllabus (Form 3) asks you to identify the causes of the spread of HIV, explain its effects on population and economic development, and suggest solutions. Economists study HIV because it affects the size and structure of the population, the labour force, productivity, government spending and household welfare. For current figures on HIV in Zimbabwe, consult the Ministry of Health and Child Care, the National AIDS Council or ZIMSTAT, and note the date of the data.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="HIV"}
The human immunodeficiency virus, which attacks the immune system. It can be controlled, but not cured, with treatment.
:::

:::definition{term="AIDS"}
Acquired immune deficiency syndrome: the advanced stage of untreated HIV infection, when the immune system is badly damaged and serious illnesses occur.
:::

:::definition{term="Antiretroviral therapy (ART)"}
Daily medicines that keep HIV under control, allowing people living with HIV to stay healthy and to work, and greatly reducing the chance of passing the virus on.
:::

:::definition{term="Prevalence"}
The percentage of a population (often adults aged 15 to 49) living with HIV at a given time.
:::

## How HIV spreads and what has driven the epidemic

HIV is passed on through certain body fluids. The main routes are:
- **unprotected sexual intercourse** with a person who has HIV that is not controlled by treatment;
- **from mother to child** during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding, if the mother is not on treatment;
- **sharing needles, razors or other sharp instruments** contaminated with infected blood;
- **transfusion of unscreened blood** (now rare where blood is screened).

HIV is **not** passed on by everyday contact such as shaking hands, sharing plates, hugging or working together.

**Social and economic factors** that have contributed to the spread:
- **Lack of knowledge** about how HIV is transmitted and prevented.
- **Poverty**, which can push people into risky situations and limits access to healthcare.
- **Labour migration**: workers living away from their families for long periods (for example on mines, farms or as cross-border traders).
- **Gender inequality**: women and girls with little economic power may be unable to insist on safer practices.
- **Multiple concurrent sexual partners** and low use of condoms.
- **Stigma and discrimination**, which discourage people from testing, disclosing their status and starting treatment.
- **Limited access to testing and treatment** in remote areas.

## Effects on population

- **Higher death rates** among young adults and working-age people, especially before treatment was widely available.
- **Lower life expectancy** during the worst years of the epidemic; life expectancy has risen again as treatment has spread.
- **Orphans**: many children lost one or both parents and were cared for by grandparents or older siblings, creating child-headed and grandparent-headed households.
- **Changes in population structure**: fewer working-age adults relative to children and the elderly, so a **higher dependency ratio**.
- **Slower population growth** than would otherwise have occurred.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the dependency ratio of a fictional district]
A fictional district has 52,000 children, 80,000 people of working age and 6,000 elderly people. Suppose illness and deaths reduce the working-age population to 72,000, while the numbers of children and elderly people stay the same.

| | Before | After |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Children (under 15) | 52,000 | 52,000 |
| Working age | 80,000 | 72,000 |
| Elderly | 6,000 | 6,000 |

1. Dependants = 52,000 + 6,000 = 58,000.
2. Dependency ratio before = 58,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = **72.5** dependants per 100 people of working age.
3. Dependency ratio after = 58,000 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = **80.6** (to one decimal place).
4. Each working-age person now supports more dependants, so household income per person falls and saving falls. Keeping people healthy through treatment prevents this.
:::

## Effects on the labour force

- **Loss of workers**, including skilled and experienced workers such as teachers, nurses, miners and farmers, during the years before treatment was widespread.
- **Absenteeism**: workers who are ill, attending clinics without workplace support, caring for sick relatives or attending funerals miss work.
- **Lower productivity** when workers are unwell and when experienced staff are replaced by less experienced ones.
- **Higher costs for firms**: recruiting and training replacements, medical and funeral benefits, and lost output.
- **Child labour and lost schooling** where children leave school to care for relatives or earn money, reducing the future skills of the labour force.

## Effects on economic development

- **Households:** spending on medical care and funerals, loss of income, selling assets such as cattle, and less food production push families into poverty.
- **Government:** higher spending on healthcare, treatment and care for orphans, leaving less for other development projects; lower tax revenue if workers fall ill.
- **Savings and investment** fall as income goes on care; investors may be deterred by labour costs.
- **Agriculture:** lost labour at planting and harvest reduces food output.
- **Education:** teacher shortages and children leaving school reduce human capital.
- **Overall:** slower growth in output and in output per head, and slower progress in health and education indicators.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a workplace programme at a fictional mine]
A fictional mine with 600 workers runs a workplace programme of education, free testing and support for treatment. It costs US\$30,000 a year. As a result, about 1,500 fewer working days a year are lost to illness, each costing the mine US\$24 in wages and lost output, and the mine saves US\$9,000 a year on recruiting and training replacements.

1. Saving from fewer lost days = 1,500 × 24 = **US\$36,000**.
2. Total money benefit = 36,000 + 9,000 = **US\$45,000**.
3. Net benefit = 45,000 − 30,000 = **US\$15,000** a year.
4. The programme pays for itself and also brings benefits not counted in money: healthier workers and families, and lower transmission in the community.
:::

## Solutions

- **Education and awareness** campaigns in schools, workplaces and communities about transmission and prevention.
- **Testing and counselling** that are free, confidential and easy to reach, so people know their status.
- **Treatment for all** who need it: ART keeps people healthy and working, and greatly reduces transmission.
- **Prevention of mother-to-child transmission** through testing and treatment in pregnancy.
- **Condom distribution** and **voluntary medical male circumcision**, which reduce the risk of infection.
- **Workplace policies** that protect workers from discrimination, provide information and support treatment.
- **Fighting stigma and discrimination** so that people seek testing and treatment.
- **Support for orphans and vulnerable children**, such as help with school fees.
- **Funding:** in Zimbabwe the National AIDS Council coordinates the response, funded partly by the **AIDS Levy**, a charge added to income tax and company tax, and partly by donors.
- **Reducing poverty and gender inequality**, which lowers vulnerability.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using stigmatising language"}
Write "people living with HIV", not labels that blame. Examiners reward accurate, respectful answers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only health effects"}
This is an economics topic: link effects to the labour force, productivity, savings, government spending and development.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring treatment"}
Modern treatment has changed the economic picture: people on ART can live long and work productively. Include it in solutions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain causes:** separate the routes of transmission from the social and economic factors (poverty, migration, gender inequality, stigma) that spread the epidemic.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain effects:** use chains, for example: illness among workers → absenteeism and loss of skills → lower productivity → lower output and incomes.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss solutions:** explain how each works, give a limitation (cost, reaching remote areas, stigma), and conclude that a combination of prevention, testing, treatment and support works best.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- HIV spreads mainly through unprotected sex, from mother to child without treatment, and through infected blood or instruments; poverty, migration, gender inequality, lack of knowledge and stigma drive the epidemic.
- Effects on population: higher deaths among working-age adults (especially before treatment), lower life expectancy, orphans and a higher dependency ratio.
- Effects on the labour force: loss of skilled workers, absenteeism, lower productivity and higher costs for firms.
- Effects on development: household poverty, higher health spending, lower saving and investment, slower growth.
- Solutions: education, testing, treatment (ART), prevention of mother-to-child transmission, condoms, workplace policies, fighting stigma, support for orphans, and funding through the AIDS Levy and donors.
:::
