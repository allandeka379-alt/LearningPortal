## Introduction

How many schools will Masvingo Province need in ten years' time? How many new jobs must the economy create for school leavers? How many people will need clinics, water and electricity in Harare and Bulawayo? None of these questions can be answered without knowing how many people live in the country, where they live, how old they are and what work they do.

Population is the starting point for much economic planning. A growing population means more consumers and more potential workers, but also more people who need food, schooling, healthcare and jobs. This lesson explains how population is counted, how it changes through births, deaths and migration, and how it is distributed. ZIMSEC teaches it from Form 1 (the census) to Form 3 (factors affecting population); Cambridge asks you to define birth rate, death rate and net migration and explain why they vary between countries. Calculations of rates and population change are common in multiple-choice and data-response questions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population"}
The total number of people living in a country or area at a particular time.
:::

:::definition{term="Census"}
An official count of every person in a country at a given time, which also collects information such as age, sex, location, education and occupation. In Zimbabwe the census is carried out by ZIMSTAT; the most recent national census was held in 2022.
:::

:::definition{term="Birth rate"}
The number of live births in a year per 1,000 people in the population.
:::

:::definition{term="Death rate"}
The number of deaths in a year per 1,000 people in the population.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural increase"}
The birth rate minus the death rate (or births minus deaths). If deaths exceed births, there is a natural decrease.
:::

:::definition{term="Net migration"}
Immigration (people entering a country to live) minus emigration (people leaving to live elsewhere). It is positive when more people arrive than leave.
:::

:::formula
Birth rate = births in the year ÷ population × 1,000

Death rate = deaths in the year ÷ population × 1,000

Change in population = (births − deaths) + (immigrants − emigrants)
:::

## The census and why it is held

A census counts the whole population on a set night and asks every household questions. It is expensive and takes years to prepare and process, so most countries hold one about every ten years. Between censuses, statistics offices estimate the population using records of births and deaths and sample surveys.

**Why a census is conducted:**

- **Planning public services:** how many schools, clinics, hospitals, houses, roads and water supplies are needed, and where.
- **Economic planning:** estimating the size of the labour force and the number of jobs needed; calculating GDP per head and other indicators.
- **Allocating resources** fairly between provinces and districts according to their population.
- **Identifying trends:** whether population is growing, ageing, or moving from rural to urban areas.
- **Political purposes:** drawing electoral boundaries.
- **Business decisions:** firms use census data to judge the size of markets and where to locate.

**Problems:** high cost; people in remote areas, the homeless or those who move often may be missed; some give false answers; results become out of date as the years pass.

## How population changes

A country's population grows or shrinks because of **natural change** (births and deaths) and **migration**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: rates and population change in Country X]
Fictional Country X has a population of 15,000,000 at the start of a year. During the year there are 450,000 births, 135,000 deaths, 20,000 immigrants and 95,000 emigrants.

1. Birth rate = 450,000 ÷ 15,000,000 × 1,000 = **30 per 1,000**.
2. Death rate = 135,000 ÷ 15,000,000 × 1,000 = **9 per 1,000**.
3. Natural increase = 30 − 9 = **21 per 1,000** (2.1% a year): 450,000 − 135,000 = 315,000 people.
4. Net migration = 20,000 − 95,000 = **−75,000** (net emigration).
5. Population change = 315,000 − 75,000 = **240,000**, so the population at the end of the year is 15,240,000.
6. Growth rate = 240,000 ÷ 15,000,000 × 100 = **1.6%**: lower than the natural increase because more people left than arrived.
:::

## Factors affecting population growth

### Birth rates

Birth rates tend to be **high** where:
- children help on family farms and support parents in old age, so large families are valued;
- infant mortality is high, so parents have more children to ensure some survive;
- access to family planning and education about it is limited;
- girls marry young and have little schooling;
- cultural or religious beliefs favour large families.

Birth rates **fall** as incomes rise, more girls are educated and women take paid jobs, family planning spreads, infant mortality falls, and children become more costly to raise (school fees, housing in towns).

### Death rates

Death rates **fall** with better healthcare, vaccination, clean water and sanitation, better nutrition and education about health. They **rise** with epidemics (such as HIV and AIDS, cholera), famine, war and natural disasters, and as a population ages.

### Migration

People emigrate because of **push factors** (unemployment, low wages, poor services, drought, conflict) and are attracted by **pull factors** elsewhere (jobs, higher wages, better services and safety). Many Zimbabweans have emigrated to work in neighbouring countries and further afield; emigration of skilled workers (doctors, nurses, teachers, engineers) is often called the **brain drain**.

### Government policy

Governments can influence population through family-planning services, education campaigns, incentives (such as tax allowances) for smaller or larger families, healthcare spending and immigration rules.

### Why rates differ between countries

Low-income countries typically have **high birth rates and falling death rates**, so population grows quickly. High-income countries typically have **low birth rates and low death rates**, so population grows slowly or even falls, and the population is older. Differences in income, education of women, healthcare, the importance of agriculture, culture and government policy explain why.

## Distribution of population

### Geographical distribution

This is where people live. Population is denser where there is fertile land, reliable rainfall, water, minerals, jobs and services, and in towns and cities; it is sparse in dry, remote or disease-affected areas. **Urbanisation**, the movement of people from rural areas to towns, raises the share of people living in urban areas and puts pressure on urban housing, water and transport.

### Occupational distribution

This shows how the working population is divided between the **primary**, **secondary** and **tertiary** sectors.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: occupational distribution in a fictional country]
| Sector | Workers (thousands) | Share of workers (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Primary (farming, mining, forestry) | 3,150 | 60.0 |
| Secondary (manufacturing, construction) | 630 | 12.0 |
| Tertiary (trade, transport, services) | 1,470 | 28.0 |
| **Total** | **5,250** | **100.0** |

1. Total workers = 3,150 + 630 + 1,470 = 5,250 thousand.
2. Primary share = 3,150 ÷ 5,250 × 100 = **60.0%**; secondary = 630 ÷ 5,250 × 100 = **12.0%**; tertiary = 1,470 ÷ 5,250 × 100 = **28.0%**.
3. With 60% of workers in the primary sector, this is typical of a developing economy. As it develops, workers usually move first into manufacturing and then into services.
:::

### Age and sex distribution

The **age distribution** divides the population into young dependants (usually under 15), the working-age population (usually 15 to 64) and elderly dependants (65 and over). The **sex distribution** compares the numbers of males and females; migration of men or women for work, and differences in life expectancy, can make it unequal in some areas. ZIMSEC students also study the **population pyramid**, a bar chart of the age-sex structure: a wide base and narrow top show a young, fast-growing population; straighter sides show an older, slow-growing one.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using percentages instead of rates per 1,000"}
Birth and death rates are given **per 1,000** people. A birth rate of 30 per 1,000 is a 3% rate, not 30%.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting migration"}
Population change is natural increase **plus** net migration. Net emigration reduces growth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing net migration signs"}
Net migration = immigrants − emigrants. If more people leave, it is negative.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show the formula, use the population at the start of the year as the base, and state the unit (per 1,000 or %).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain why birth rates differ:** link each factor to the decision to have children, for example: more girls educated → marry later and work → fewer children.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss the census:** give its uses for planning by government and firms, then its problems (cost, missed people, becoming out of date).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use birth rate, death rate and net migration. ZIMSEC also asks about the census and the **geographical, occupational and demographic (age-sex) distribution**, including population pyramids. The Cambridge syllabus from 2027 no longer requires interpretation of population pyramids.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A census is an official count of the whole population, used to plan services, allocate resources, measure the labour force and guide business decisions.
- Birth and death rates are per 1,000 people; natural increase = birth rate − death rate; net migration = immigrants − emigrants.
- Population change = natural increase + net migration.
- Birth rates are high where children are economically valuable, infant mortality is high and family planning is limited; they fall with income, education and urbanisation.
- Population is distributed by location (geographical), by sector (occupational) and by age and sex.
:::
