## Introduction

Look at a till slip from a supermarket in Bulawayo and you will see a line for VAT. Look at a payslip from a mine in Hwange and you will see income tax deducted before the worker is paid. Taxes touch almost every transaction in the economy. In Zimbabwe most taxes are collected by ZIMRA, the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority, and the rates are set each year in the national budget.

Taxes are the main source of government revenue, but they also change behaviour: they can make cigarettes dearer, encourage investment, or narrow the gap between rich and poor. Both boards ask you to classify taxes (direct or indirect; progressive, proportional or regressive), to calculate the tax paid and average tax rates, and to explain the effects of taxation. The rates in this lesson are invented for practice; always check current rates with ZIMRA.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tax"}
A compulsory payment to the government, made without receiving a specific good or service in direct return.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct tax"}
A tax on income or wealth, paid directly to the government by the person or firm that bears it, such as income tax, corporate (company) tax and property tax.
:::

:::definition{term="Indirect tax"}
A tax on spending on goods and services, collected by the seller and passed to the government, such as VAT, excise duties and customs duties (tariffs).
:::

:::definition{term="Average tax rate"}
Total tax paid as a percentage of income: tax paid ÷ income × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal tax rate"}
The percentage of an **extra** dollar of income that is paid in tax.
:::

:::definition{term="Progressive tax"}
A tax that takes a **larger percentage** of income as income rises: the average tax rate rises with income.
:::

:::definition{term="Proportional tax"}
A tax that takes the **same percentage** of income at every level of income.
:::

:::definition{term="Regressive tax"}
A tax that takes a **smaller percentage** of income as income rises: it falls more heavily on the poor.
:::

:::formula{name="Average tax rate"}
Average tax rate (%) = total tax paid ÷ income × 100
:::

## Why governments tax

1. **To raise revenue** for public services: schools, clinics, roads, the police and defence.
2. **To redistribute income**, by taxing high incomes more heavily and paying benefits to the poor.
3. **To discourage harmful goods** (demerit goods) such as tobacco and alcohol through excise duties.
4. **To correct external costs**, such as a tax on pollution or on fuel.
5. **To protect domestic industries** with customs duties on imports.
6. **To manage the economy** (fiscal policy): higher taxes reduce aggregate demand and inflation; lower taxes raise demand and employment.

## Direct and indirect taxes

| | Direct taxes | Indirect taxes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Income tax (PAYE), corporate tax, capital gains tax, property tax | VAT, excise duties on fuel, tobacco and alcohol, customs duties |
| Advantages | Can be progressive; related to ability to pay; revenue is fairly predictable | Hard to avoid; can target harmful goods; collected by sellers; no disincentive to earn |
| Disadvantages | May reduce incentives to work and invest; can be evaded, especially in the informal sector | Often regressive; raise prices and can add to inflation; may encourage smuggling |

Indirect taxes can be **specific** (a fixed amount per unit, such as US\$0.30 per litre of fuel) or **ad valorem** (a percentage of the price, such as VAT).

## Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes

Income tax is usually **progressive**. It is charged in **bands**: income up to a threshold is tax-free, and higher slices of income are taxed at higher rates. The rate on each extra dollar (the marginal rate) rises, so the average rate rises as income rises.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a banded income tax]
A fictional country taxes monthly income as follows: the first US\$300 is tax-free; income from US\$300 to US\$1,000 is taxed at 20%; income above US\$1,000 is taxed at 30%.

| Monthly income (US\$) | Tax paid (US\$) | Average tax rate (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 800 | 100 | 12.5 |
| 2,000 | 440 | 22.0 |

1. Income of US\$800: tax = 0% × 300 + 20% × (800 − 300) = 0 + 100 = **US\$100**. Average rate = 100 ÷ 800 × 100 = **12.5%**.
2. Income of US\$2,000: tax = 20% × (1,000 − 300) + 30% × (2,000 − 1,000) = 140 + 300 = **US\$440**. Average rate = 440 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = **22.0%**.
3. The average rate rises with income, so the tax is **progressive**. The higher earner's marginal rate is 30%, but the average is lower, because the first slices of income are taxed at lower rates.
:::

A **proportional** (flat) tax charges, say, 10% at every income: US\$20 on US\$200 and US\$120 on US\$1,200. The amount paid rises, but the percentage stays the same.

![Average tax rate against monthly income for a banded income tax (first US\$300 free, 20 percent up to US\$1,000, 30 percent above): it rises from 12.5 percent at US\$800 to 22.0 percent at US\$2,000, so the tax is progressive, whereas a proportional tax stays at a flat rate.](media:diagrams/progressive-tax-average-rate.png "Figure: under a progressive tax the average rate rises with income; a proportional tax stays flat")

A **regressive** tax takes a bigger share of a poor household's income. Indirect taxes on necessities are usually regressive, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income on such goods.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: why a tax on cooking oil is regressive]
Two households each buy the same amount of cooking oil and pay US\$6 a month of tax on it.

| Household | Monthly income (US\$) | Tax paid on cooking oil (US\$) | Tax as % of income |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Chipo's household | 200 | 6 | 3.0 |
| Farai's household | 1,200 | 6 | 0.5 |

1. Chipo's household: 6 ÷ 200 × 100 = **3.0%** of income.
2. Farai's household: 6 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **0.5%** of income.
3. Both pay the same amount, but it is a much larger share of the poorer household's income, so the tax is **regressive**.
:::

## The impact of taxation

- **Consumers:** indirect taxes raise prices and reduce real incomes; direct taxes reduce disposable income and spending.
- **Workers:** higher income tax reduces take-home pay and may reduce the incentive to work longer hours, accept promotion or join the formal sector.
- **Firms:** corporate tax reduces profits available for investment; indirect taxes raise costs and prices, which can reduce sales, especially for goods with elastic demand.
- **The government:** gains revenue to fund spending; but very high rates may encourage evasion and avoidance, so revenue may not rise.
- **The economy:** higher taxes reduce aggregate demand (lower inflation, but slower growth and more unemployment); progressive taxes reduce inequality; taxes on demerit goods and pollution can improve resource allocation.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use direct and indirect, progressive and proportional taxes; Cambridge also names **regressive** taxes and the impact of taxes on each group. ZIMSEC uses the term **ad valorem** tax. The "principles" (canons) of a good tax, such as fairness, certainty, convenience and economy of collection, are no longer in the Cambridge syllabus from 2027 but remain useful for ZIMSEC essays.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a tax progressive because rich people pay more dollars"}
Under a proportional tax the rich also pay more dollars. A tax is progressive only if the **percentage** of income paid rises with income.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying the top rate to the whole income"}
In a banded system each rate applies only to the **slice** of income in its band. US\$2,000 in Worked example 1 is not taxed at 30% on the whole amount.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking all indirect taxes hit everyone equally"}
An equal amount of tax is a larger share of a low income, so taxes on necessities are usually regressive.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Classify:** state the type (direct or indirect) and the incidence (progressive, proportional or regressive), and support it with the average rate at two incomes.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** work band by band, show each slice, add the slices, then find the average rate. Give the answer in US\$ and percentages to the precision asked.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss a tax change:** consider each group (consumers, workers, firms, government, the economy), the effect on incentives and inequality, and reach a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Taxes raise revenue, redistribute income, discourage harmful goods, protect industries and manage demand.
- Direct taxes fall on income and wealth; indirect taxes fall on spending.
- Progressive: the average rate rises with income; proportional: constant; regressive: falls with income.
- Average tax rate = tax paid ÷ income × 100; banded taxes apply each rate to its own slice.
- Taxes affect consumers, workers, firms, the government and the economy, including incentives and inequality.
:::
