## Introduction

Each year Zimbabwe's Minister of Finance presents the **national budget** to Parliament. It sets out how much the government expects to collect in taxes (most of it collected by ZIMRA, the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority) and how it plans to spend the money on schools, clinics, roads, dams, the police, the army and support for vulnerable households. The budget also tells everyone whether the government expects to borrow.

The budget is more than a set of accounts. By changing taxes and spending, a government can speed up or slow down the whole economy. This is **fiscal policy**. Both boards examine it: ZIMSEC asks how fiscal policy (government spending and the national budget) helps achieve economic objectives, and Cambridge asks you to calculate deficits and surpluses, explain the areas of spending and explain how fiscal policy helps meet the macroeconomic aims.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Government (national) budget"}
A statement of the government's planned revenue (mainly taxes) and planned spending for the coming year.
:::

:::definition{term="Budget deficit"}
When government spending is greater than government revenue in a year. The gap is usually financed by borrowing.
:::

:::definition{term="Budget surplus"}
When government revenue is greater than government spending in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Balanced budget"}
When government revenue equals government spending.
:::

:::definition{term="National debt"}
The total amount the government owes, built up from past borrowing. Deficits add to it; surpluses can be used to repay it.
:::

:::definition{term="Fiscal policy"}
The use of changes in government spending and taxation to influence the level of economic activity and to achieve macroeconomic aims.
:::

:::formula{name="Budget balance"}
Budget balance = government revenue − government spending (a negative answer is a deficit)

Deficit as a percentage of GDP = deficit ÷ GDP × 100
:::

## Main areas of government spending

| Area | Reasons for the spending | Possible effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Education | Education is a merit good; a skilled labour force raises productivity | Higher incomes and growth in the long run; less inequality |
| Health care | Healthy workers are more productive; health care has external benefits | Longer life expectancy; fewer working days lost |
| Infrastructure (roads, rail, dams, power) | Public goods and large projects private firms may not provide | Lower costs for firms, more investment, jobs during construction |
| Social protection (pensions, grants, food aid) | Support for the poor, elderly and disabled | Less poverty; more equal distribution of income |
| Defence and security | Public goods: national defence, police, courts | Law and order, which firms need to operate |
| Interest on government debt | Paying for past borrowing | Uses money that could fund services (opportunity cost) |

Every dollar the government spends has an **opportunity cost**: money spent on defence cannot also be spent on clinics.

## Fiscal policy

The two tools of fiscal policy are **government spending** and **taxation**.

### Expansionary fiscal policy

Raise government spending and/or cut taxes. Households have more disposable income and the government buys more goods and services, so **aggregate demand** (total spending in the economy) rises. Firms sell more, raise output and hire workers. It is used to fight a **recession** and **unemployment**. It usually makes the budget deficit larger.

### Contractionary fiscal policy

Cut government spending and/or raise taxes. Aggregate demand falls, which reduces pressure on prices. It is used to fight **demand-pull inflation** or to **close a large budget deficit**, and can help the current account because people spend less on imports. It may slow growth and raise unemployment.

### Fiscal policy and the macroeconomic aims

| Aim | Fiscal measure | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic growth | Spending on infrastructure, education; tax incentives for investment | Raises demand now and productive capacity later |
| Full employment | Higher spending, lower taxes in a recession | Higher demand → more output → more jobs |
| Price stability | Lower spending, higher taxes | Lower demand → less demand-pull inflation |
| Balance of payments | Higher taxes reduce spending on imports | Current account deficit narrows |
| Redistribution | Progressive taxes, transfer payments | Takes more from high incomes, gives to the poor |
| Environment | Taxes on pollution, subsidies for clean energy | Changes the costs of polluting and clean choices |

### Limitations of fiscal policy

- **Time lags:** the budget is set once a year, and projects take time to start.
- **Deficits and debt:** expansionary policy means more borrowing, and interest payments take money from other services.
- **Imports:** part of extra spending goes on imports, so less of the boost stays in the economy.
- **Incentives:** higher taxes may discourage work, saving and investment.
- **Conflicts:** cutting a deficit may raise unemployment.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: calculating a budget balance]
A fictional country's budget (US\$ million):

| Revenue | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Income tax | 2,400 |
| Value added tax (VAT) | 1,800 |
| Customs and excise duties | 600 |
| Other revenue (fees, licences) | 300 |
| **Total revenue** | **5,100** |

| Spending | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Education | 1,500 |
| Health | 900 |
| Infrastructure (roads, dams, power) | 1,100 |
| Social protection | 650 |
| Defence and security | 500 |
| Interest on government debt | 400 |
| Other spending | 650 |
| **Total spending** | **5,700** |

1. Total revenue = 2,400 + 1,800 + 600 + 300 = US\$5,100 million.
2. Total spending = 1,500 + 900 + 1,100 + 650 + 500 + 400 + 650 = US\$5,700 million.
3. Budget balance = 5,100 − 5,700 = **−US\$600 million**: a budget deficit of US\$600 million.
4. GDP is US\$40,000 million, so the deficit = 600 ÷ 40,000 × 100 = **1.5% of GDP**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a contractionary package]
The same government raises VAT revenue by 10% and cuts infrastructure spending by US\$200 million.

1. Extra VAT revenue = 10% × 1,800 = US\$180 million, so total revenue = 5,100 + 180 = US\$5,280 million.
2. New spending = 5,700 − 200 = US\$5,500 million.
3. New balance = 5,280 − 5,500 = **−US\$220 million**: the deficit falls from US\$600 million to US\$220 million (0.55% of GDP).
4. This is contractionary: higher VAT reduces households' spending power and lower infrastructure spending reduces demand. It helps close the deficit and reduces inflationary pressure, but construction jobs are lost and long-run growth may suffer because fewer roads and dams are built.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC usually speaks of the **national budget** and teaches fiscal policy under money and banking; Cambridge speaks of the **government budget** and places fiscal policy with the macroeconomic aims. The ideas and calculations are the same.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Getting the budget balance the wrong way round"}
Balance = revenue − spending. If spending is larger, the answer is negative: a **deficit**, not a surplus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the deficit with the national debt"}
The deficit is the shortfall in **one year**; the national debt is the **total** owed from all past borrowing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing fiscal and monetary policy"}
Fiscal policy uses **taxes and government spending**. Interest rates and the money supply belong to **monetary policy**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** total revenue and total spending separately, subtract, and label the answer "deficit" or "surplus". Give a percentage of GDP if asked.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain how fiscal policy helps an aim:** name the measure (cut income tax), the effect on aggregate demand, then the effect on output, jobs or prices.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** weigh the benefits against time lags, the effect on the deficit and debt, imports and incentives, and conclude whether the policy suits the problem.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The budget sets out planned revenue and spending; revenue − spending gives a surplus (+) or a deficit (−).
- Main spending areas: education, health, infrastructure, social protection, defence, and interest on debt.
- Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes to influence aggregate demand.
- Expansionary policy fights recession and unemployment; contractionary policy fights inflation and closes deficits.
- Limitations: time lags, debt, imports, incentives and conflicts between aims.
:::
