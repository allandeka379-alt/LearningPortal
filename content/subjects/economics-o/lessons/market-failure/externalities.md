## Introduction

A brick-maker near a fictional town fires her kiln with wood. She pays for the clay, the wood and her workers. But the smoke drifts over a neighbouring vegetable garden and a primary school, and the trees cut for fuel are not replaced. The gardener's crops suffer, children cough, and the hillside erodes. None of these costs appears in the brick-maker's accounts.

Now think of a farmer who keeps bees. He sells honey, but his bees also pollinate the neighbours' fruit trees, raising their harvests. He receives nothing for this benefit.

These spill-over effects on third parties are called **externalities**. They are one of the main reasons markets fail, and they explain the case for many taxes, subsidies and regulations, such as Zimbabwe's environmental rules enforced by the Environmental Management Agency. Both syllabuses ask you to define the costs and benefits, **calculate** social cost and social benefit, and explain why the market produces too much or too little. No diagrams are needed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Private cost"}
The cost of an activity paid by the person or firm that carries it out: for a firm, its wages, materials, fuel and rent; for a consumer, the price paid.
:::

:::definition{term="External cost (negative externality)"}
A cost of an activity paid by third parties who are not involved in the decision and are not compensated, for example pollution, noise or congestion.
:::

:::definition{term="Social cost"}
The total cost of an activity to society: **social cost = private cost + external cost**.
:::

:::definition{term="Private benefit"}
The benefit of an activity received by the person or firm that carries it out: for a firm, its revenue; for a consumer, the satisfaction or gain from using the good.
:::

:::definition{term="External benefit (positive externality)"}
A benefit of an activity received by third parties who do not pay for it, for example less disease when others are vaccinated, or neighbours' crops pollinated by bees.
:::

:::definition{term="Social benefit"}
The total benefit of an activity to society: **social benefit = private benefit + external benefit**.
:::

:::formula
Social cost = private cost + external cost

Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit
:::

## Why externalities cause market failure

Decision-makers in a market weigh **only private** costs and benefits. A firm produces as long as the extra revenue (its private benefit) covers its extra private cost; a consumer buys if the private benefit is worth the price. Third parties are ignored.

- If an activity has **external costs**, the social cost is higher than the private cost. The decision-maker does more of the activity than is best for society: **over-production or over-consumption**.
- If an activity has **external benefits**, the social benefit is higher than the private benefit. The decision-maker does less than is best for society: **under-production or under-consumption**.

From society's point of view, an activity is worth doing when **social benefit is at least equal to social cost**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the brick kiln]
Each month the kiln earns revenue (private benefit) of US\$23,000 and has private costs of US\$18,000. Smoke damage to crops and health, and soil erosion, cost local people an estimated US\$7,500 a month. There are no external benefits.

1. Private profit = $23{,}000 - 18{,}000 = 5{,}000$ dollars: the kiln owner will keep producing.
2. Social cost = private cost + external cost = $18{,}000 + 7{,}500 = 25{,}500$, that is US\$25,500.
3. Social benefit = private benefit = US\$23,000.
4. Net effect on society = $23{,}000 - 25{,}500 = -2{,}500$: society loses US\$2,500 a month.
5. The market leads to production that is not worth its full cost: an external cost has caused **over-production**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a training course]
Rudo is thinking about a one-year computer course. It costs her US\$3,600 in fees and lost earnings (private cost) and will raise her income by an estimated US\$3,200 over the next few years (private benefit). Her future employer and colleagues would also gain from her skills, an external benefit of about US\$1,100.

1. Private net benefit = $3{,}200 - 3{,}600 = -400$ dollars: Rudo decides **not** to take the course.
2. Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit = $3{,}200 + 1{,}100 = 4{,}300$, that is US\$4,300.
3. Social cost = US\$3,600 (no external cost).
4. Net effect on society = $4{,}300 - 3{,}600 = 700$ dollars: society would gain US\$700.
5. The course is worth taking for society but not for Rudo alone: an external benefit has caused **under-consumption**. A subsidy of more than US\$400 would change her decision.
:::

## Over-production, step by step

The same logic works for each extra unit. The table shows a fictional smelter deciding how many batches of 100 tonnes to produce in a week. Each batch sells for US\$900; the smoke from each batch causes US\$250 of external cost.

| Batch | Extra revenue (US\$) | Extra private cost (US\$) | Extra external cost (US\$) | Extra social cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1st | 900 | 500 | 250 | 750 |
| 2nd | 900 | 600 | 250 | 850 |
| 3rd | 900 | 700 | 250 | 950 |
| 4th | 900 | 850 | 250 | 1,100 |
| 5th | 900 | 950 | 250 | 1,200 |

- The firm compares extra revenue with extra **private** cost: each of the first four batches adds to profit, so it produces **4 batches**.
- Society compares extra revenue with extra **social** cost: only the first two batches are worth more than their full cost, so the best output is **2 batches**.
- The market over-produces by 2 batches. A tax of US\$250 per batch (equal to the external cost) would make the firm count the smoke as a cost and cut output to 2.

## Examples to learn

| Activity | External cost or benefit | Market result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mining or smelting | Air and water pollution, land damage | Over-produced |
| Driving in a city at rush hour | Congestion, fumes, accidents | Over-consumed |
| Cutting trees for firewood | Deforestation, soil erosion | Over-produced |
| Vaccination | Fewer infections in others | Under-consumed |
| Education and training | More productive colleagues and citizens | Under-consumed |
| Planting trees, beekeeping | Cleaner air, shade, pollination | Under-produced |

## Correcting externalities

The aim is to make decision-makers take the external effect into account (to "internalise" it). External costs: indirect taxes equal to the external cost, regulation (emission limits, bans), or tradable pollution permits. External benefits: subsidies, free provision, information. Each has the advantages and disadvantages covered in the lessons on taxes and subsidies and on other interventions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding the wrong things together"}
Social cost = private cost + **external cost**. Do not add benefits to costs. Social benefit = private benefit + **external benefit**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a firm's own costs external"}
Wages and fuel paid by the firm are **private** costs. A cost is external only if it falls on a third party who is not compensated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the good is not produced at all"}
Externalities lead to the **wrong quantity** (too much or too little). Non-provision is the problem of public goods.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** write the formula first ("social cost = private cost + external cost"), substitute, and give units. Then say what the result shows (net loss or gain to society).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** decision-makers ignore third parties → social cost above private cost → over-production (or social benefit above private benefit → under-production).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss a policy:** say how it makes the decision-maker take the external effect into account, then weigh problems such as measuring the external cost, enforcement and side effects.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **private, external and social costs and benefits**. Cambridge says diagrams are not required for market failure. Externalities are also called **spill-over effects** or **third-party effects**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Private costs and benefits fall on the decision-maker; external costs and benefits fall on third parties.
- Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
- Markets weigh only private costs and benefits.
- External costs lead to over-production; external benefits lead to under-production.
- Taxes, regulation and subsidies aim to make decision-makers take external effects into account.
:::
