## Introduction

The price mechanism is good at getting bread, airtime and shoes to the people who want them. But think about the street lights on a road in Bulawayo, a childhood vaccination, or the cigarettes sold by a vendor. Left entirely to the market, street lights would hardly ever be built, too few children would be vaccinated, and too many cigarettes would be smoked. In each case the market gives a result that is worse for society than it could be. Economists call this **market failure**.

This lesson covers three kinds of goods that markets handle badly: **public goods**, **merit goods** and **demerit goods**. External costs and benefits, the other major cause of market failure, have their own lesson, and so does monopoly. Both syllabuses ask you to define these goods, explain why the market fails, and link the failure to the government interventions that try to correct it. Cambridge says no demand-and-supply diagrams are needed for market failure, so you explain it in words.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market failure"}
A situation in which the market, left to itself, allocates resources inefficiently: it produces too much or too little of a good, or does not produce it at all, compared with what is best for society.
:::

:::definition{term="Public good"}
A good that is **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce the amount available to others) and **non-excludable** (people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting). Examples: street lighting, national defence, lighthouses, flood defences.
:::

:::definition{term="Free-rider problem"}
Because no one can be excluded from a public good, people can benefit without paying, so they do not reveal what it is worth to them and firms cannot make a profit from supplying it.
:::

:::definition{term="Private good"}
A good that is rival and excludable: if one person buys and uses it, others cannot, and those who do not pay can be excluded. Most goods sold in shops are private goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Merit good"}
A good that is more beneficial to the consumer and to society than consumers realise, so it is **under-consumed** in a free market. Examples: education, health care, vaccinations, insurance.
:::

:::definition{term="Demerit good"}
A good that is more harmful to the consumer and to society than consumers realise, so it is **over-consumed** in a free market. Examples: cigarettes, alcohol, illegal drugs, gambling.
:::

## Public goods and the free-rider problem

A private good, such as a loaf of bread, is **rival**: if you eat it, I cannot. It is **excludable**: the baker will not give it to me unless I pay. These two features make it easy to sell.

A public good has the opposite features:

- **Non-rival.** When a street light is on, one more person walking under it does not reduce the light available to anyone else. The extra cost of serving one more user is zero.
- **Non-excludable.** Once the light is on, the trader who refuses to pay cannot be kept in the dark.

Because of non-excludability, each person has an incentive to wait for someone else to pay and then use the good for free: to be a **free rider**. If enough people do this, no firm can earn revenue, so the market **does not provide the good at all**, even though everyone would benefit from it. This is the most extreme form of market failure: a **missing market**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: lighting a market street]
Sixty traders work along a dark street in a fictional town. Lighting the street for a year would cost US\$2,400. Each trader values the lighting at US\$55 a year, because customers stay later and theft falls.

1. Total value to the traders = $60 \times 55 = 3{,}300$ dollars, that is US\$3,300 a year.
2. The cost is US\$2,400, so the lighting is worth more than it costs: society would gain $3{,}300 - 2{,}400 = 900$ dollars.
3. A firm asks each trader to pay US\$40 a year ($2{,}400 \div 60$). But once the lights are on, a trader who does not pay still gets the light.
4. Many traders refuse to pay, hoping the others will. If only 25 pay, the firm collects $25 \times 40 = 1{,}000$ dollars, far less than the cost.
5. The firm will not supply the lighting. The town council can provide it and pay for it from local taxes or market fees: **direct provision**.
:::

Some goods are **quasi-public** (partly public): a road is non-rival when it is empty but becomes rival when it is congested, and a toll gate can make it excludable. This is why some roads are tolled.

## Merit goods and under-consumption

A free market produces merit goods, but **too little** of them. There are two reasons:

1. **Imperfect information.** People underestimate the long-term benefits to themselves. A parent short of money may not realise how much an extra year of schooling will raise a child's future earnings, or may not see the need for a vaccine for a disease that seems rare.
2. **External benefits.** The benefits spread to others who do not pay: a vaccinated child cannot pass the disease on, and a better-educated worker makes colleagues more productive.

Because consumption is based only on the private benefit that people perceive, demand is too low, and fewer resources go into these goods than is best for society. Low incomes make this worse: the poorest households may not be able to afford school fees or clinic charges at all.

## Demerit goods and over-consumption

For demerit goods the error goes the other way. Consumers **underestimate the harm** to themselves (for example the future health effects of smoking or heavy drinking), and the consumption also harms **others**: second-hand smoke, road accidents caused by drunk drivers, and the cost to public hospitals. The market therefore produces and sells **too much** of these goods.

:::example[Example 2: the same market, two kinds of failure]
In a fictional district, a private clinic charges for vaccinations and vendors sell cheap spirits. Many families skip vaccinations because they feel healthy now and the fee is a week's food money: the merit good is under-consumed, and outbreaks of measles spread to unvaccinated children in other families. At the same time spirits are cheap and widely sold; heavy drinking causes illness, lost work and road accidents that drinkers did not take into account: the demerit good is over-consumed. The district council might provide free vaccinations at schools, run information campaigns, and tax or restrict the sale of spirits. Each policy targets a different failure.
:::

## How governments respond

| Type of good | Market failure | Common responses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public good | Not provided at all (free riders) | Direct provision paid from taxes |
| Merit good | Under-consumed | Free or subsidised provision, information, compulsory use (for example school attendance) |
| Demerit good | Over-consumed | Indirect taxes, regulation (age limits, bans), information campaigns |

These policies, and their own risks of failure, are covered in the lessons on taxes and subsidies and on other interventions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling any government-provided good a public good"}
A good is public because of its **features** (non-rival, non-excludable), not because the government supplies it. State schools provide education, which is a **merit** good: places are rival and pupils can be excluded.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying merit goods are not provided by the market"}
Markets do supply merit goods (private schools, private clinics), but in **too small** a quantity. It is public goods that the market fails to provide at all.
:::

:::mistake{title="Defining a demerit good as simply 'a bad good'"}
Explain **why** it is over-consumed: consumers underestimate the harm to themselves, and there are harmful effects on others.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** for public goods, state **both** features and explain them with an example: "non-excludable: people who do not pay for street lighting cannot be stopped from using it".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain why the market fails:** for public goods use the free-rider chain (non-excludable → no one pays → no profit → not provided); for merit and demerit goods use information failure and external effects.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** when asked whether the government should provide a good, consider cost, efficiency, fairness and the alternatives (subsidy, regulation), then conclude.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Market failure is an inefficient allocation of resources by the free market.
- Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable; free riders mean the market does not provide them.
- Merit goods are under-consumed because people underestimate their benefits and benefits spill over to others.
- Demerit goods are over-consumed because people underestimate the harm to themselves and others.
- Governments respond with direct provision, subsidies, taxes, regulation and information.
:::
