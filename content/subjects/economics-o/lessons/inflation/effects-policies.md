## Introduction

Inflation does not affect everyone in the same way. A pensioner in Masvingo on a fixed monthly pension finds that it buys less each month. A farmer who borrowed to buy seed and fertiliser may find the loan easier to repay because crop prices have risen. A family that kept its savings in a bank account may see their value melt away.

Zimbabwe's hyperinflation of 2007-2008 showed the most extreme effects: money lost its value so fast that people stopped saving it, shops priced goods in foreign currency or bartered, and in 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned for a multi-currency system. Low and stable inflation, by contrast, causes few problems.

Both boards ask you to explain the effects of inflation on different groups (ZIMSEC also asks about the **functions of money**) and to explain the **policy tools** used to curb inflation; Cambridge asks you to **evaluate** those policies. You will need the lessons on the causes of inflation and on fiscal and monetary policy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Real income"}
The quantity of goods and services that a money income can buy; it falls when prices rise faster than money income.
:::

:::definition{term="Fixed income"}
An income that does not change with prices, such as some pensions or fixed allowances.
:::

:::definition{term="Real interest rate"}
The interest rate after allowing for inflation; approximately the nominal interest rate minus the rate of inflation.
:::

:::definition{term="Menu costs"}
The costs to firms of changing prices often, such as reprinting price lists and relabelling goods.
:::

:::formula{name="Real values"}
Real value = money value ÷ price index × 100

Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − rate of inflation
:::

## Effects of inflation on different groups

| Group | Likely effect | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumers | Lose, unless incomes rise as fast | Each dollar buys less; real income falls |
| Workers | Lose if wages lag behind prices; strong unions may keep pace | Real wages fall when pay rises less than prices |
| People on fixed incomes (pensioners) | Lose | Their income does not rise with prices |
| Savers | Lose when inflation exceeds the interest rate | The real value of savings falls (negative real interest rate) |
| Lenders (banks) | Lose on fixed-rate loans | Money repaid is worth less than money lent |
| Borrowers | Gain on fixed-rate loans | The real burden of the debt falls |
| Producers (firms) | Mixed | Higher selling prices and falling real debts help; rising costs, menu costs, uncertainty and less competitive exports harm |
| Government | Mixed | Tax revenue rises with prices and the real value of its debt falls; but it pays more for goods and wages, and must protect the poor |
| The economy | Harm if high or unstable | Uncertainty discourages investment; exports become less competitive; income is redistributed unfairly |

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: what happens to savings]
Sipho deposits US\$1,000 in a savings account paying 5% interest a year. During the year, prices rise by 12% (the price index goes from 100 to 112).

1. Money value after one year = 1,000 × 1.05 = **US\$1,050**.
2. Real value at start-of-year prices = 1,050 ÷ 112 × 100 = **US\$937.50**.
3. Sipho can buy **US\$62.50** less than he could with his original US\$1,000.
4. The real interest rate is about 5% − 12% = **−7%**: saving is penalised, so people prefer to spend or buy goods that hold their value.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: borrowers and pensioners]
A farmer borrows US\$2,000 for a year at 8% interest, and prices rise by 15%. A pensioner receives a fixed pension of US\$150 a month while prices rise by 20%.

| Person | Money amount (US\$) | Price index (start of year = 100) | Real value at start-of-year prices (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Sipho (savings plus interest) | 1,050 | 112 | 937.50 |
| Borrower (repayment) | 2,160 | 115 | 1,878.26 |
| Pensioner (monthly pension) | 150 | 120 | 125 |

1. The farmer repays 2,000 × 1.08 = US\$2,160. In start-of-year prices this is worth 2,160 ÷ 115 × 100 = **US\$1,878.26**, less than the US\$2,000 borrowed: the borrower gains and the lender loses.
2. The pension of US\$150 is worth 150 ÷ 120 × 100 = **US\$125** in start-of-year prices: a fall of one sixth in real income.
:::

## Inflation and the functions of money

Money has four functions: a **medium of exchange**, a **unit of account** (measure of value), a **store of value** and a **standard of deferred payment**. Low inflation leaves them largely intact. High inflation damages them:

- **Store of value** is the first to fail: money loses its purchasing power, so people hold goods, livestock, land or foreign currency instead.
- **Standard of deferred payment:** lenders refuse to lend in the currency, because repayments will be worth less; contracts are written in a stable foreign currency.
- **Unit of account:** prices change so often that they stop being a useful measure; shops price goods in another currency.
- **Medium of exchange:** in hyperinflation, sellers refuse the currency and people turn to foreign currency or barter, as happened in Zimbabwe in 2008.

## Policies to control inflation

The right policy depends on the cause.

| Policy | Measures | How it reduces inflation | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Monetary | Raise interest rates; slow money supply growth; allow the currency to appreciate | Less borrowing and spending; cheaper imports | Slower growth, job losses; hurts borrowers and exporters; time lags |
| Fiscal | Raise direct taxes; cut government spending; cut the deficit; stop financing it by creating money | Lower aggregate demand | Unpopular; may cut useful services; raising VAT raises prices at first |
| Supply-side | Training, infrastructure, competition, productivity incentives | Raises capacity and lowers costs, so output grows without prices rising | Slow to work; costly |
| Other (in some countries) | Price controls, wage agreements | Directly limit price or wage rises | Shortages and black markets; do not tackle the cause |

**Demand-pull inflation** is best treated by policies that reduce aggregate demand (higher interest rates, tighter fiscal policy). **Cost-push inflation** is harder: cutting demand reduces inflation only by causing lower output and higher unemployment, so supply-side measures that raise productivity, and controlling the growth of wages relative to productivity, are more useful. Inflation driven by **excessive money creation** needs the government to stop financing its deficit by creating money.

:::example{title="Choosing a policy"}
A fictional country's inflation has risen from 4% to 11% after a rise in world fuel prices, while output is falling. A sharp rise in interest rates would reduce spending, but because the inflation is cost-push, it would also deepen the fall in output and raise unemployment. A better mix might be a modest rise in interest rates to stop expectations rising, support to help transport and farming use fuel more efficiently, and targeted help for the poorest households, accepting that inflation will fall slowly as the fuel shock passes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying inflation harms everyone"}
Borrowers on fixed-rate loans and people whose incomes rise faster than prices can gain. Explain who wins and who loses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong policy for the cause"}
Cutting demand is effective for demand-pull inflation, but for cost-push inflation it reduces prices only at the cost of output and jobs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the real interest rate"}
Savers lose only when inflation is **higher** than the interest rate they earn. Compare the two rates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain effects on a group:** say whether the group gains or loses, explain why (real income, real value of debt or savings), and give an example.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain a policy:** name the measure, trace the chain to lower aggregate demand or lower costs, and so to lower inflation.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Evaluate policies:** match the policy to the cause, consider side effects on growth, jobs and the exchange rate, and time lags; then reach a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inflation lowers real incomes of consumers, workers on lagging wages and those on fixed incomes.
- Savers and lenders lose when inflation exceeds interest rates; borrowers gain.
- High inflation damages money's functions, starting with its store of value.
- Monetary and fiscal policies cut aggregate demand; supply-side policies raise capacity and lower costs.
- Choose the policy by cause: demand-pull calls for lower demand; cost-push calls mainly for supply-side measures.
:::
