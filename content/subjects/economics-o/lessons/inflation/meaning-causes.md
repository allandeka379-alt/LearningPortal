## Introduction

Ask an older relative what a loaf of bread cost when they were at school, and the answer will probably sound very cheap. Over time the prices of most goods and services rise. When the **general level** of prices keeps rising, the economy is experiencing **inflation**, and each dollar buys less than before.

Zimbabwe has lived through one of the most extreme inflations in modern history. In 2007-2008 prices rose so fast that they changed several times a day: **hyperinflation**. In 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned and a multi-currency system, mainly using the US dollar, was adopted. A local currency was re-introduced in 2019, and in April 2024 the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe introduced the Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG). Because of this history, inflation is a topic that every Zimbabwean student can relate to.

Both boards ask you to define inflation (and Cambridge, deflation), and to explain and distinguish **demand-pull** and **cost-push** inflation. This lesson is the first of three on inflation; the next two cover measurement and the effects and policies.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inflation"}
A sustained rise in the general level of prices of goods and services in an economy over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Rate of inflation"}
The percentage increase in the general price level over a period, usually one year.
:::

:::definition{term="Deflation"}
A sustained fall in the general level of prices over a period of time (a negative rate of inflation).
:::

:::definition{term="Hyperinflation"}
Extremely rapid inflation, in which prices rise so fast that money quickly loses its value and people stop wanting to hold it.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand-pull inflation"}
Inflation caused by aggregate demand (total spending) rising faster than the economy's ability to supply goods and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-push inflation"}
Inflation caused by rising costs of production, which firms pass on to consumers as higher prices.
:::

:::formula{name="Rate of inflation"}
Rate of inflation (%) = (price level this year − price level last year) ÷ price level last year × 100
:::

## What inflation is (and is not)

- Inflation is about the **general** price level, not one product. If the price of tomatoes rises after a poor harvest while other prices are steady, that is a change in a relative price, not inflation.
- It must be **sustained**: a one-off jump is not inflation unless prices keep rising.
- A **fall in the rate of inflation** (say from 8% to 5%) does **not** mean prices are falling. Prices are still rising, only more slowly. Falling prices would be deflation.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: calculating the rate of inflation]
The cost of a fixed basket of goods for a household in a fictional town:

| Year | Cost of the basket (US\$) | Rate of inflation (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2024 | 250.00 | – |
| 2025 | 270.00 | 8.0 |
| 2026 | 283.50 | 5.0 |

1. 2025: (270 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = **8.0%**.
2. 2026: (283.50 − 270) ÷ 270 × 100 = **5.0%**.
3. The rate of inflation fell from 8.0% to 5.0%, but the basket still became dearer in 2026. Prices rose more slowly; they did not fall.
:::

## Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation is often described as "too much money chasing too few goods". When total spending rises and firms cannot raise output fast enough, especially when the economy is close to **full capacity**, there are shortages and firms raise prices.

Causes of a rise in aggregate demand include:

- higher consumer spending, for example after a cut in income tax or interest rates, or a rise in confidence;
- higher government spending, such as large public-sector pay rises;
- higher investment by firms;
- a rise in exports, for example when world demand for minerals is strong;
- a rapid increase in the money supply and in bank lending.

In words, a diagram of demand-pull inflation would show aggregate demand rising while output can increase only a little, so the price level rises.

![Two aggregate demand and supply sketches: in demand-pull inflation the AD curve shifts right near full capacity, so the price level rises a lot while output rises only a little; in cost-push inflation the AS curve shifts left, so the price level rises and output falls.](media:diagrams/demand-pull-cost-push.png "Figure: demand-pull inflation comes from a shift of AD to the right; cost-push from a shift of AS to the left")

## Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation starts on the supply side. When firms' costs rise, they raise prices to protect their profits, and they may also cut output.

Causes include:

- higher **wages** not matched by higher productivity, for example after a large wage settlement;
- higher prices of **raw materials and energy**, such as fuel and electricity;
- dearer **imports** after a fall in the exchange rate;
- higher **indirect taxes**, such as VAT or fuel duty;
- **supply shocks**, such as a drought that cuts maize harvests and raises food prices.

Cost-push inflation can create a **wage-price spiral**: prices rise, so workers demand higher wages to protect their living standards, which raises costs again, and so on.

## How to tell them apart

| | Demand-pull | Cost-push |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | Rising spending | Rising costs |
| Output (real GDP) | Usually rising | Usually falling or flat |
| Unemployment | Usually falling | Usually rising |
| Typical evidence | Strong consumer spending, credit growth, economy near capacity | Fuel or wage rises, currency depreciation, drought, tax rises |

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: identifying the cause from data]
Two fictional economies both see inflation rise from 3% to 9% in one year.

| Indicator (change over the year) | Economy P | Economy Q |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Consumer spending | +9% | +1% |
| Real GDP | +5% | −2% |
| Unemployment rate | 6% → 4% | 6% → 8% |
| World price of fuel | unchanged | +40% |
| Inflation rate | 3% → 9% | 3% → 9% |

1. Economy P: spending rose strongly, output rose and unemployment fell, while costs did not change. This is **demand-pull** inflation.
2. Economy Q: fuel costs rose by 40% while output fell and unemployment rose. Firms passed higher costs on in prices. This is **cost-push** inflation.
3. The same inflation figure can have very different causes, which is why the right policy differs.
:::

## Excessive money creation

If a government pays for its spending by having the central bank create large amounts of new money, while the output of goods does not rise, more money chases the same goods and prices rise. Expectations make it worse: if people expect prices to keep rising, they spend money as soon as they get it and workers demand higher wages, so prices rise even faster. In extreme cases this leads to hyperinflation, as in Zimbabwe in 2007-2008, when shortages of goods and falling production added to the rapid growth of money.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a rise in one price inflation"}
Inflation is a rise in the **general** price level. A higher price of sugar alone is not inflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying prices fall when inflation falls"}
A lower rate of inflation means prices rise **more slowly**. Only a negative rate (deflation) means prices fall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two causes"}
Demand-pull starts with **spending**; cost-push starts with **costs**. Look at what happened to output and unemployment to decide.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip

:::

:::exam-tip

:::

:::exam-tip
**Data questions:** quote the figures (spending, costs, output, unemployment) as evidence for the type of inflation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; deflation is a sustained fall.
- Rate of inflation = change in the price level ÷ previous price level × 100.
- Demand-pull inflation: aggregate demand rises faster than output can respond, especially near full capacity.
- Cost-push inflation: higher wages, fuel, imports or taxes raise firms' costs and prices; output may fall.
- Excessive money creation can cause very high inflation, as in Zimbabwe in 2007-2008.
:::
