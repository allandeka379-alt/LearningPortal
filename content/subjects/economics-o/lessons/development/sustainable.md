## Introduction

Zimbabwe's natural resources (its forests, wildlife, soils, rivers, minerals and the tourist attractions of places such as Victoria Falls, Hwange and Kariba) support farming, mining, tourism and millions of rural livelihoods. But resources can be used up or damaged: trees cut for firewood and tobacco curing are not replanted, river banks are dug up by gold panners, soils lose fertility, and wildlife is poached. If today's generation uses resources faster than they can recover, future generations will be poorer.

**Sustainable development** is about raising living standards now in a way that does not reduce the ability of future generations to meet their needs. The ZIMSEC syllabus (Form 4) asks you to explain sustainable resource management and how economic agents (households, firms, communities and government) should manage resources, to explain sustainable development and ways of achieving it, and to plan a sustainable development project. Essay questions often ask you to *discuss* the costs and benefits of an initiative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sustainable development"}
Development that meets the needs of the present generation without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable resource management"}
Using natural resources at a rate that allows them to be renewed or replaced, protecting the environment and sharing the benefits fairly, so that resources remain available in the future.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable and non-renewable resources"}
Renewable resources (forests, fish, wildlife, water, soil) can regrow or be replaced if they are not overused. Non-renewable resources (gold, platinum, lithium, coal) exist in fixed amounts and are used up when extracted.
:::

:::definition{term="Newly industrialised country (NIC)"}
A country that has moved in a few decades from depending mainly on agriculture and raw materials to having a large manufacturing and modern services sector, with rapidly rising incomes.
:::

## How economic agents can manage resources sustainably

- **Households:** use energy-saving stoves and solar power, plant and protect trees, reduce waste and recycle, conserve water.
- **Firms:** use cleaner technology, treat waste before releasing it, rehabilitate land after mining, use resources efficiently, follow environmental laws.
- **Communities:** manage shared land, forests, grazing and wildlife together, set rules on use, and share the benefits.
- **Government:** pass and enforce environmental laws, require environmental impact assessments, tax or fine polluters, protect national parks, educate the public and support research.

Using **renewable** resources sustainably means taking no more than they can regrow (for example cutting no more trees than are replanted and grow each year). Using **non-renewable** resources sustainably means using them efficiently, recycling where possible, and investing the income they earn (for example in education and infrastructure) so that future generations benefit after the resource is gone.

## Zimbabwean initiatives

### CAMPFIRE

The **Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources (CAMPFIRE)**, started in the late 1980s, gives rural communities in areas with wildlife the right to benefit from it. Rural district councils, on behalf of communities, earn revenue from safari hunting and tourism, and part of it is used for community projects and household income.

- **Benefits:** gives communities an incentive to protect wildlife rather than poach it; earns income for schools, clinics, boreholes and grinding mills in poor areas; uses land unsuited to crops.
- **Costs and problems:** income depends on tourism and hunting, which can fall sharply; wildlife damages crops and threatens people; disputes over how revenue is shared; some communities receive little.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: sharing the revenue of a community wildlife scheme]
A fictional district wildlife scheme earns US\$84,000 in a year and shares it as follows:

| Use of revenue | Share (%) | Amount (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Households and ward projects | 55 | 46,200 |
| Wildlife management and anti-poaching | 25 | 21,000 |
| District council administration | 20 | 16,800 |
| **Total** | **100** | **84,000** |

1. Community share = 55% × 84,000 = **US\$46,200**.
2. If the scheme covers six wards equally, each ward receives 46,200 ÷ 6 = **US\$7,700**, enough, for example, to repair a borehole or buy desks for a school.
3. Spending 25% on wildlife management keeps animal numbers healthy, so the income can continue in future years: this is what makes the scheme sustainable.
:::

### The Environmental Management Agency (EMA)

The **Environmental Management Agency** is the government body responsible for protecting the environment under Zimbabwe's environmental management law. It requires **environmental impact assessments** before large projects (such as mines, dams and factories) go ahead, monitors pollution, enforces rules on waste, veld fires and river-bank cultivation, and runs public education campaigns.

- **Benefits:** prevents or reduces pollution and land degradation; makes polluters responsible for damage.
- **Costs and problems:** enforcement is costly; fines may be too small to change behaviour; large areas are hard to monitor; firms face extra costs and delays.

### Community share ownership trusts

**Community share ownership trusts** were set up so that communities near large mining and other resource-based companies receive a share of the companies' value or profits, to be spent on community development such as schools, clinics, roads and water.

- **Benefits:** communities gain directly from resources extracted from their area; income can be invested in long-lasting assets for future generations.
- **Costs and problems:** promised funds have not always been paid in full; management and accountability of trusts vary; payments depend on mining profits and mineral prices.

### Recycling

Recycling turns waste (plastic, paper, metal, glass) into raw materials. Many informal workers collect and sell recyclable waste.

- **Benefits:** reduces the use of new raw materials and energy; less waste in dumps and rivers; creates jobs and small businesses.
- **Costs and problems:** collection and sorting are costly; markets for recycled materials can be small; it needs public cooperation.

## Weighing the costs and benefits of a project

Before starting a sustainable development project, planners compare its **costs** (money, resources, any damage) with its **benefits** (income, savings, environmental and social gains), including effects on **third parties** and on the **future**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a solar-powered irrigation project]
A fictional farmers' group plans a solar-powered irrigation pump costing US\$18,000. It will replace a diesel pump, saving US\$2,400 a year on fuel, and allow an extra crop worth US\$3,600 a year.

1. Yearly money benefit = 2,400 + 3,600 = **US\$6,000**.
2. Payback period = 18,000 ÷ 6,000 = **3 years**.
3. Over five years the net money gain = 5 × 6,000 − 18,000 = **US\$12,000**.
4. Benefits not counted in money: no diesel fumes, lower greenhouse gas emissions, and a more reliable food supply in dry years.
5. Costs and risks: the panels and pump need maintenance; water must be used carefully so that the borehole or river is not overused.
:::

### Planning a project

A simple plan states: the **problem** (for example deforestation around a village), the **aim**, the **activities** (a tree nursery, woodlots, energy-saving stoves), the **resources** needed and their cost, **who** will manage it, the expected **benefits** for people and the environment, and how success will be **monitored**.

## Lessons from newly industrialised countries

Several countries, for example in East and South-East Asia, changed from mainly agricultural economies to industrial ones within a few decades. Lessons often drawn from their experience include:

- heavy investment in **education and skills**, especially technical training;
- **high saving and investment** in infrastructure and industry;
- a focus on **exports** and on adding value to raw materials through manufacturing;
- stable policies and long-term planning by government in partnership with business;
- adoption of new **technology**.

Their experience also offers a warning: rapid industrialisation brought serious air and water pollution and loss of forests in some countries, which later had to be cleaned up at great cost. The lesson for sustainable development is to build environmental protection into growth from the start.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying sustainable development means no use of resources"}
It means using resources at a rate and in a way that leaves enough for the future, not leaving them untouched.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing an initiative without its costs"}
For CAMPFIRE, EMA, share trusts or recycling, give both benefits and problems; essays reward balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing statistics"}
Do not quote figures for revenue or projects unless they are given in the question. Use the data provided.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** define sustainable development using the idea of present and future generations, then give an example of a sustainable and an unsustainable use of a resource.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss an initiative:** describe how it works, give two or three benefits and two or three problems, then conclude on whether it promotes sustainable development and what would improve it.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Plan a project:** state the problem, aim, activities, costs, benefits (economic, social, environmental) and how it will be monitored.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sustainable development meets present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
- Renewable resources should be used no faster than they regrow; non-renewable income should be invested for the future.
- Households, firms, communities and government all have roles in managing resources sustainably.
- CAMPFIRE, the EMA, community share ownership trusts and recycling each bring benefits and problems.
- Projects are judged by comparing costs and benefits, including environmental and future effects.
- Newly industrialised countries show the value of education, saving, investment and exports, and the cost of ignoring the environment.
:::
