## Introduction

A grandmother in a rural district looks after four grandchildren on what she earns from selling vegetables and a small remittance from a son working in town. When the rains fail, the vegetables die and the family eats one meal a day. A few kilometres away, a family in town has enough food and a roof, but cannot afford school fees, transport to a clinic or a mobile phone that most of its neighbours take for granted.

Both families are poor, but in different senses. Economists distinguish **absolute poverty**, where people cannot meet their basic needs, from **relative poverty**, where people are poor compared with others in their society. Reducing poverty is one of the main aims of economic development.

This lesson covers Cambridge IGCSE syllabus 5.2: you must distinguish absolute from relative poverty, explain the causes of poverty and explain policies to alleviate poverty and redistribute income. Questions often give household data and ask you to identify who is poor, or ask you to *discuss* which policy would reduce poverty most.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Absolute poverty"}
A situation in which a person's income is too low to buy the basic necessities of life: enough food, clean water, shelter, clothing and basic healthcare.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative poverty"}
A situation in which a person's income is well below the average income of the society in which they live, so they cannot afford the standard of living that most people in that society enjoy. It is often measured as income below a set percentage (for example 50% or 60%) of the median income.
:::

:::definition{term="Poverty line"}
The level of income below which a person or household is counted as poor. An absolute poverty line is based on the cost of basic needs; a relative poverty line is a fraction of average (median) income.
:::

:::definition{term="Redistribution of income"}
Government action that transfers income from richer to poorer people, mainly through progressive taxes, benefits and free or subsidised public services.
:::

## Absolute and relative poverty compared

| | Absolute poverty | Relative poverty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compared with | A fixed standard: the cost of basic needs | Other people in the same society |
| Measured by | An income line based on the cost of food and other necessities | Income below a percentage of median income |
| Can it be eliminated? | Yes, if everyone can meet basic needs | Hard to eliminate: it falls only if income becomes more equally distributed |
| Effect of growth | Falls when the incomes of the poor rise | May not fall if all incomes rise at the same rate |

The World Bank publishes an international poverty line, expressed in US dollars a day at purchasing power parity, to estimate extreme poverty across countries; it is updated from time to time. National statistics offices, such as ZIMSTAT in Zimbabwe, calculate their own poverty lines from the cost of a basket of food and non-food necessities. Look up the latest official figure and note its date before using it.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a relative poverty line]
In a fictional country the median monthly household income is US\$420. The government counts a household as relatively poor if its income is below 60% of the median. A survey of 400 households in one town gives:

| Monthly household income | Number of households |
| --- | ---: |
| Under US\$150 | 34 |
| US\$150 to US\$251 | 58 |
| US\$252 to US\$419 | 95 |
| US\$420 and over | 213 |
| **Total** | **400** |

1. Relative poverty line = 60% × 420 = **US\$252** a month.
2. Households below the line = 34 + 58 = **92**.
3. Share of households in relative poverty = 92 ÷ 400 × 100 = **23%**.
4. If every household's income doubled, the median and the line would also double, so the number in relative poverty would not change, even though absolute living standards would rise.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: absolute poverty and a household budget]
In a fictional district the cost of basic needs is estimated at US\$31 a month per person for food and US\$17 for non-food necessities (shelter, clothing, soap, transport). A household of six people has a total income of US\$250 a month.

1. Cost of basic needs per person = 31 + 17 = US\$48 a month.
2. Cost for the household = 48 × 6 = **US\$288** a month.
3. Income (US\$250) is below the cost of basic needs, so the household is in **absolute poverty**.
4. The poverty gap is 288 − 250 = **US\$38** a month: the extra income needed to reach the line.
:::

## Causes of poverty

- **Unemployment:** households without a wage earner depend on irregular informal earnings or on support from relatives.
- **Low wages:** many unskilled and informal workers earn too little to meet basic needs, even when working long hours. Low skills, weak bargaining power and low productivity keep wages down.
- **Illness and disability:** sickness reduces the ability to work and raises spending on medicines; a long illness can push a household into poverty and force children to leave school to care for relatives.
- **Age:** elderly people without pensions and young people without work experience often have low incomes. Households with many children and few earners have low income per person.
- **Environmental factors:** droughts, floods, cyclones, soil erosion and poor land destroy crops and livestock. Rural households that depend on rain-fed farming are especially vulnerable.
- **Lack of education:** people with little schooling find it hard to get well-paid jobs.
- **Lack of assets and credit:** without land, savings or access to loans, poor people cannot invest in better seed, tools or a small business.
- **Inflation:** rising prices reduce the real value of low incomes, savings and pensions.

## Policies to alleviate poverty and redistribute income

### Policies that raise the incomes of the poor

- **Promote economic growth:** more jobs and higher incomes. Growth reduces absolute poverty if the poor share in it.
- **Improve education and training:** raises skills and future earnings; free or subsidised schooling helps poor families most.
- **Improve healthcare:** free clinics, vaccination and clean water reduce illness and keep people working.
- **A minimum wage:** raises the pay of the lowest-paid workers, though it may reduce the number of jobs if set too high, and it does not help informal workers.
- **Support for small farmers and businesses:** access to credit (microfinance), irrigation, better seed and extension services raise productivity and income.

### Policies that redistribute income

- **Progressive taxation:** higher-income earners pay a larger percentage of their income in tax, which narrows the gap after tax.
- **Transfer payments (benefits):** cash grants to poor households, pensions for the elderly, support for people with disabilities, food aid during drought.
- **Free or subsidised public services:** education, healthcare and water that the poor could not otherwise afford.
- **Lower taxes on necessities:** removing indirect taxes on basic foods reduces the burden on the poor, who spend most of their income on them.

:::example[Evaluating a cash-transfer scheme]
A fictional government gives a monthly cash grant to households headed by elderly people caring for children. **Advantages:** it reaches the poorest households directly, money is spent on food and school costs, and families can choose what they most need. **Disadvantages:** it must be paid for by taxes or borrowing, some benefits may go to households that are not poor if targeting is weak, and it relieves poverty without removing its causes. A judgement: the grant is effective in the short run, but it should be combined with education, health and job-creation policies that let households escape poverty permanently.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up absolute and relative poverty"}
Absolute poverty is measured against basic needs; relative poverty against other people's incomes. A household can be relatively poor without being absolutely poor.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying growth always ends relative poverty"}
If all incomes rise at the same rate, the median rises too, so relative poverty does not fall. Only a more equal distribution reduces it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the drawbacks of a policy"}
Every policy has a cost: taxes or borrowing to pay for it, effects on incentives, time lags or poor targeting. Evaluation needs both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Distinguish:** define both kinds of poverty and give an example of each; mention that absolute poverty is fixed to basic needs and relative poverty moves with average income.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain causes:** link each cause to income, for example: drought destroys crops → farm income falls → the household cannot buy enough food.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss policies:** say how the policy reduces poverty, then give a limitation (cost, incentives, time, targeting), and conclude which policy, or which combination, is best for the cause described.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Absolute poverty: income too low to meet basic needs. Relative poverty: income well below the society's average, often below 50-60% of the median.
- Causes include unemployment, low wages, illness, age, environmental shocks, lack of education, assets and credit, and inflation.
- Policies raise the incomes of the poor (growth, education, health, minimum wage, help for small producers) or redistribute income (progressive taxes, transfers, free public services).
- Growth can reduce absolute poverty; reducing relative poverty needs a more equal distribution of income.
- Each policy has costs and limits, so a combination is usually needed.
:::
