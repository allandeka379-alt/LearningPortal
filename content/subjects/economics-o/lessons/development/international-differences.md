## Introduction

A family in a well-off suburb of Harare and a family in a remote village in Binga live in the same country, but their living standards can be very different: one may have piped water, electricity and a car, the other a borehole a long walk away and no electricity. Differences are even larger **between** countries: the average person in a high-income country may earn many times as much as the average person in a low-income country, live longer and spend more years in school.

Why? This lesson explains the reasons for differences in living standards and in the **distribution of income**, both within a country and between countries. It covers Cambridge IGCSE syllabus items 5.1.2 and 5.4. Examiners often give data on two countries (income per head, sector shares, savings, life expectancy) and ask you to explain *why* they differ. The skill is to link each factor to output, income or welfare in a clear chain of reasoning.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Living standards"}
The material welfare and quality of life that people enjoy, judged by indicators such as real income per head, health, education, housing and access to services.
:::

:::definition{term="Distribution of income"}
How a country's total income is shared among its households or people. An **unequal** distribution means a small share of people receives a large share of income.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
Output per unit of input, usually output per worker or per hour worked.
:::

:::definition{term="Developed and developing economies"}
Developed (high-income) economies have high incomes per head, high productivity, most workers in the secondary and tertiary sectors and high HDI values. Developing (low- and middle-income) economies have lower incomes, a larger primary sector and lower HDI values.
:::

## Differences in the distribution of income

Income distribution can be shown by the share of total income received by each fifth (20%) of households, ranked from poorest to richest.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: comparing income distribution]
Countries X and Y are fictional.

| Group of households | Country X (% of income) | Country Y (% of income) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Poorest 20% | 4 | 8 |
| Second 20% | 8 | 13 |
| Middle 20% | 12 | 17 |
| Fourth 20% | 20 | 22 |
| Richest 20% | 56 | 40 |

1. In Country X the richest fifth receives 56 ÷ 4 = **14 times** the income of the poorest fifth.
2. In Country Y the ratio is 40 ÷ 8 = **5 times**.
3. The poorest 40% receive 4 + 8 = 12% of income in X but 8 + 13 = 21% in Y.
4. Income is much more unequally distributed in X. Even if the two countries had the same income per head, most households in X would be worse off than in Y.
:::

### Why income is unequally distributed within a country

- **Differences in wages:** skilled workers and professionals earn much more than unskilled workers, because of differences in education, training and bargaining power.
- **Ownership of wealth:** people who own land, shares, businesses or property receive rent, dividends and profits as well as wages. Wealth produces income, and income can be saved to build more wealth.
- **Employment and unemployment:** households without a working member have very low incomes.
- **Formal and informal work:** informal workers usually earn less and have no pensions or job security.
- **Region:** towns often have more jobs, higher wages and better services than rural areas.
- **Government policy:** progressive taxes, transfers (such as grants to poor families) and free public services reduce inequality; regressive taxes and poor services increase it.
- **Age, health, gender and family size:** elderly, sick or disabled people, and large families with one earner, tend to have lower incomes per person.

## Why development differs between countries

### Income and productivity

Countries with **higher productivity** produce more per worker, so they have higher incomes per head. Productivity depends on skills, capital, technology and the organisation of production.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: productivity and the size of the sectors]
In fictional Country A, GDP is US\$10,000 million and there are 5.0 million workers. Agriculture employs 65% of workers but produces only 20% of GDP.

| Sector | Workers (million) | Output (US\$ million) | Output per worker (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Agriculture | 3.25 | 2,000 | 615.38 |
| Industry and services | 1.75 | 8,000 | 4,571.43 |
| Whole economy | 5.00 | 10,000 | 2,000.00 |

1. Agricultural workers = 65% × 5.0 = 3.25 million; output = 20% × 10,000 = US\$2,000 million.
2. Output per agricultural worker = 2,000 million ÷ 3.25 million = **US\$615.38**.
3. Output per worker in industry and services = 8,000 million ÷ 1.75 million = **US\$4,571.43**, about 7.4 times as much.
4. Because most workers are in low-productivity farming, average output per worker is only US\$2,000. Moving workers into higher-productivity industry and services, or raising farm productivity with irrigation, better seed and machinery, would raise incomes.
:::

### Population growth

If population grows faster than real GDP, real GDP per head falls. Rapid population growth also means a high proportion of children (a high **dependency ratio**), so more income must be spent on feeding and schooling dependants and less can be saved and invested.

### The size of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors

Developing economies usually have a large **primary sector** (farming, mining), much of it low-productivity or subsistence farming. Developed economies have most workers in the **secondary** (manufacturing, construction) and **tertiary** (services) sectors, where productivity and wages are higher. Dependence on a few primary exports, such as minerals or tobacco, also makes income unstable, because world commodity prices rise and fall sharply.

### Saving and investment

Investment in machinery, infrastructure and technology raises productivity, and investment is financed from saving. Where incomes are low, households must spend almost all their income on necessities, so saving is low; low saving means low investment, low productivity and continued low income. This is sometimes called the **cycle of poverty**. Foreign direct investment, aid and borrowing can help break the cycle.

### Education

Education and training raise skills (**human capital**), so workers are more productive, adopt new technology and earn more. Educated parents also tend to have smaller, healthier families. Low school enrolment and poor-quality schooling hold back development.

### Healthcare

Healthy workers are more productive and absent less often. Poor healthcare means high infant mortality, low life expectancy and diseases that reduce the labour force. Better healthcare raises both welfare directly and productivity.

### Natural resources

Fertile land, water, minerals and tourist attractions can provide income and exports. But resources alone do not guarantee development: if minerals are mined by a few firms, if earnings are not invested, or if there is corruption, countries rich in resources can remain poor. A country with few resources can still develop through education, investment and trade.

:::example[Linking factors together]
A fictional country has high population growth, most workers in subsistence farming and very low saving. Its low incomes leave little to save, so investment in irrigation and machinery is low, and farm productivity stays low. Rapid population growth means a large share of income goes on feeding children, and schools are overcrowded, so the next generation's skills are weak. Each factor reinforces the others. Policies that break the cycle include investment in education and rural infrastructure, access to credit for small farmers, and family-planning services.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without linking them to living standards"}
"Education" earns little on its own. Write the chain: education → higher skills → higher productivity → higher incomes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming natural resources guarantee development"}
Resource-rich countries can stay poor if earnings are not invested or are unequally shared.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing total GDP instead of GDP per head"}
A large country can have a large total GDP but a low income per person. Compare per-head figures.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain why living standards differ:** choose factors that the data show (for example, a large primary sector, low saving, fast population growth) and explain each with a chain to productivity, income or welfare.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse income distribution data:** compare the shares of the richest and poorest groups (for example, as a ratio), and suggest causes such as wage differences, wealth ownership, unemployment and the tax system.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** when asked which factor matters most, weigh at least two, consider how they interact, and conclude, for example that education and investment are the foundation that allows resources to be used well.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Living standards differ within countries because of differences in wages, skills, wealth, employment, region, age and health, and government policy.
- Income distribution can be compared using the shares of income received by groups of households.
- Countries differ in development because of differences in income and productivity, population growth, the size of their sectors, saving and investment, education, healthcare and natural resources.
- Low income, low saving, low investment and low productivity can form a cycle of poverty.
- Resources help but do not guarantee development; how they are used matters.
:::
