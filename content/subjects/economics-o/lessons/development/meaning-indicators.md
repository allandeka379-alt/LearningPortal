## Introduction

Imagine two districts. In the first, a new mine has doubled the value of output, but the nearest clinic is still 30 kilometres away, girls leave school early and most families fetch water from a river. In the second, output has grown slowly, but every village now has a borehole, a primary school and a clinic, and more children reach secondary school. Which district has **developed** more?

Economists distinguish between **economic growth** (more output) and **economic development** (a broad improvement in people's welfare and quality of life). Growth can help development by providing the income to pay for schools, clinics and water, but it does not guarantee it.

ZIMSEC asks you to explain economic development, identify its signs, distinguish it from growth, explain its causes and state the indicators of growth and development. Cambridge asks you to explain **real GDP per head** and the **Human Development Index (HDI)** as indicators of living standards, with the advantages and disadvantages of each. Data questions often give a table of indicators for several countries and ask you to compare them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic development"}
A sustained improvement in the economic and social welfare of a country's people: higher real incomes together with better health, education, housing, access to services and freedom of choice, and less poverty.
:::

:::definition{term="Living standards"}
The level of material welfare and quality of life that people enjoy: the goods and services they can consume and the conditions they live in.
:::

:::definition{term="Real GDP per head (per capita)"}
Real GDP divided by the population: the average output, and so average income, per person, adjusted for inflation.
:::

:::definition{term="Human Development Index (HDI)"}
A composite measure published by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) that combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (gross national income per head, adjusted for purchasing power). It is a number between 0 and 1; the higher it is, the more developed the country.
:::

## Growth and development compared

| | Economic growth | Economic development |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | An increase in real output (real GDP) | A broad improvement in welfare and quality of life |
| Measured by | The percentage change in real GDP | Several indicators: real GDP per head, HDI, life expectancy, literacy, access to services |
| Nature | Quantitative (how much is produced) | Qualitative as well as quantitative (how people live) |
| Time | Can happen quickly, in one year | Usually a long-term process |
| Relationship | Can occur without development (for example if gains go to a few) | Usually needs growth to pay for it |

## Signs of development

- **Higher literacy** and school enrolment, including for girls.
- **Lower death rates**, especially infant and maternal mortality, and **higher life expectancy**.
- Better **infrastructure**: roads, electricity, telecommunications, safe water and sanitation.
- Better **housing** and fewer people living in overcrowded informal settlements.
- A **smaller share of workers in subsistence farming** and more in manufacturing and services.
- Less absolute poverty and a fairer distribution of income.
- More choice and freedom: access to information, political participation and opportunities for women.

## Causes of development

- **Economic growth**, which provides the income and tax revenue to spend on welfare.
- **Investment in human capital**: education, training and healthcare.
- **Investment in infrastructure**: roads, power, water, communications.
- **Good governance**: stable institutions, the rule of law, low corruption, and policies that share the gains widely.
- **Technology and diversification** away from dependence on a few primary products.
- **Trade and foreign investment**, which bring markets, capital and skills.
- Controlled **population growth**, so that rising output is not spread over ever more people.

## Indicator 1: real GDP per head

Real GDP per head is calculated as real GDP ÷ population.

**Advantages**
- Data are widely available and regularly published, so countries and years can be compared.
- It is adjusted for inflation (real) and for population size (per head).
- Income is closely linked to the goods and services people can buy.

**Disadvantages**
- It is an **average**: it does not show how income is distributed. A few very rich people can raise the average while most remain poor.
- It ignores the **informal sector** and subsistence output, which are hard to measure, so it may understate living standards.
- It ignores **quality of life**: health, education, working hours, leisure, pollution, crime and freedom.
- Comparisons between countries need a common currency, and exchange rates do not reflect differences in the cost of living.
- Output may consist of capital goods, exports or military spending rather than goods people consume.

## Indicator 2: the Human Development Index

The HDI combines income with health and education.

**Advantages**
- It is **broader** than GDP per head, measuring health and education as well as income.
- It shows that countries with similar incomes can have very different levels of human welfare, which helps governments see where to spend.
- It uses income adjusted for purchasing power, which improves comparisons of living costs.

**Disadvantages**
- It still leaves out many aspects of welfare: income distribution, poverty, gender inequality, political freedom, environmental quality, housing.
- It uses averages, so it hides differences between regions and groups.
- Data on schooling and life expectancy may be out of date or estimated in poorer countries.
- **Years** of schooling do not measure the **quality** of education.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: comparing two countries]
| Country | Real GDP (US\$ million) | Population (million) | Life expectancy (years) | Mean years of schooling | HDI |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Country A | 27,300 | 6.5 | 58 | 5.1 | 0.561 |
| Country B | 31,200 | 13.0 | 71 | 9.4 | 0.684 |

Both countries are fictional.

1. Real GDP per head: A = 27,300 ÷ 6.5 = **US\$4,200**; B = 31,200 ÷ 13.0 = **US\$2,400**.
2. A's real GDP per head is 4,200 ÷ 2,400 = 1.75 times B's, so on income alone A looks better off.
3. But B's people live 13 years longer on average and have over four more years of schooling, and B's HDI is higher (0.684 against 0.561).
4. Interpretation: A's income may come from an industry such as oil or mining that employs few people, with income unequally shared and little spent on health and education. B has used its lower income to achieve more human development.
5. Conclusion: GDP per head alone would rank the countries the wrong way round for welfare; a broader indicator such as the HDI gives a fuller picture.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: growth without development]
Data for fictional Country X:

| Indicator | 2016 | 2026 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Real GDP per head (US\$) | 1,800 | 2,340 |
| Life expectancy (years) | 61 | 60 |
| Adult literacy rate (%) | 82 | 81 |
| Households with safe water (%) | 64 | 61 |

1. Real GDP per head rose by (2,340 − 1,800) ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **30%** over ten years: there was economic growth.
2. Life expectancy, literacy and access to safe water all fell slightly.
3. So Country X experienced growth without development. Possible explanations: the extra income went to a small group; the government did not spend the extra tax revenue on health, schools and water; or population grew faster in poor areas than services.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating growth and development as the same"}
Growth is an increase in real output; development is a broader improvement in welfare. Always show the difference with an example.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher GDP per head means everyone is richer"}
GDP per head is an **average**. It says nothing about how income is shared.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing HDI components wrongly"}
The three HDI dimensions are **health** (life expectancy), **education** (years of schooling) and **income** (GNI per head). Literacy and access to water are signs of development, but they are not part of today's HDI.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Distinguish:** growth is *quantitative* (more output); development is *qualitative* (better lives). A country can grow without developing.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Compare data:** calculate GDP per head where needed, then compare the welfare indicators, and point out any country that ranks differently on income and on the HDI. Suggest a reason.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss which indicator is better:** give advantages and disadvantages of both, then conclude that the HDI is broader but still incomplete, so several indicators should be used together.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **indicators of growth and development** and the **signs** of development. Cambridge speaks of **indicators of living standards**, naming real GDP per head and the HDI. The ideas are the same.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic development is a sustained improvement in welfare: income, health, education, services and choice.
- Growth is more output; development is better lives. Growth helps pay for development but does not guarantee it.
- Signs: higher literacy, lower death rates, longer life expectancy, better infrastructure and housing, less poverty.
- Real GDP per head: easy to calculate and compare, but an average that ignores distribution, the informal sector and quality of life.
- The HDI combines life expectancy, years of schooling and income per head: broader, but still leaves out distribution, freedom and the environment.
:::
