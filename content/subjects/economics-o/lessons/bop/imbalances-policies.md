## Introduction

A country that imports far more than it exports must find foreign currency from somewhere: by borrowing, by attracting investment or by running down its reserves. If the gap persists, debts grow, the currency comes under pressure and the government is forced to act. A country with a large surplus faces different problems: its trading partners may complain, and its own citizens may be consuming less than they could.

The Cambridge syllabus asks you to explain the reasons for current account deficits and surpluses, their impact on GDP, employment, inflation and the exchange rate, and to **evaluate** the policies available to achieve balance of payments stability. Section B questions often end with a part (d) such as "Discuss whether a depreciation of the currency is the best way to reduce a current account deficit", so you need policies, their strengths and their weaknesses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Current account deficit"}
When the total of debits on the current account (payments for imports of goods and services, primary and secondary income paid abroad) is greater than the total of credits.
:::

:::definition{term="Current account surplus"}
When current account credits are greater than debits.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of payments stability"}
A situation in which the current account is roughly in balance over time, or any deficit or surplus is small and can be financed without difficulty.
:::

:::definition{term="Expenditure-reducing policy"}
A policy that reduces total spending in the economy, including spending on imports, such as higher taxes or higher interest rates.
:::

:::definition{term="Expenditure-switching policy"}
A policy that encourages people to switch spending from imports to domestically produced goods, such as a depreciation of the currency or tariffs.
:::

## Reasons for current account deficits

- **High domestic demand:** when incomes, consumer spending and borrowing rise quickly, people buy more imports.
- **Low international competitiveness:** high inflation compared with trading partners, low productivity, poor quality or high costs make exports dear and imports attractive.
- **A high (appreciated) exchange rate:** exports become dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home.
- **Recession in trading partners:** foreign demand for the country's exports falls.
- **Falling export prices or rising import prices**, for example a fall in the world price of a mineral the country exports, or a rise in the price of oil it imports.
- **Large outflows of primary income**, such as profits of foreign-owned firms and interest on foreign debt.

## Reasons for current account surpluses

- Strong competitiveness: high productivity, quality products, low inflation.
- A low (depreciated) exchange rate that makes exports cheap.
- Weak domestic demand or high saving, so few imports are bought.
- High world prices for the country's main exports, such as minerals or oil.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: is the deficit a problem?]
The table shows data for a fictional country.

| Year | Current account balance (US\$ billion) | GDP (US\$ billion) | Balance as % of GDP |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2024 | −0.8 | 40 | −2.0 |
| 2025 | −2.1 | 42 | −5.0 |
| 2026 | −3.6 | 45 | −8.0 |

1. Balance as % of GDP = balance ÷ GDP × 100. For 2026: −3.6 ÷ 45 × 100 = **−8.0%**.
2. The deficit has grown much faster than GDP: from 2% to 8% of GDP in two years.
3. A deficit of about 2% of GDP is usually manageable. A deficit of 8% that keeps growing is a warning sign: the country must borrow or attract investment of US\$3.6 billion a year, and lenders may lose confidence, putting pressure on the exchange rate.
:::

## Impact of current account imbalances

| Variable | Effect of a persistent deficit | Effect of a persistent surplus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| GDP | Net exports (X − M) reduce aggregate demand, so GDP is lower than it would otherwise be | Net exports add to aggregate demand and GDP |
| Employment | Jobs lost in industries competing with imports and in export industries | More jobs in export industries |
| Inflation | Lower demand pressure; but cheap imports reduce inflation, and a later depreciation can raise it | More demand pressure can raise inflation |
| Exchange rate | More of the currency is supplied to buy imports, so it tends to depreciate | More demand for the currency, so it tends to appreciate |

A deficit is not always harmful. In the short run it lets people enjoy more goods than the country produces, and a deficit caused by imports of capital goods can raise future output and exports. The concern is a **large and persistent** deficit, financed by borrowing, that reflects a lack of competitiveness.

## Policies to achieve balance of payments stability

### Expenditure-reducing policies (reduce a deficit)

- **Contractionary fiscal policy:** higher income tax or lower government spending reduce disposable income and spending, including on imports.
- **Contractionary monetary policy:** higher interest rates reduce borrowing and spending.

*Weaknesses:* lower spending also reduces demand for domestic goods, so output and employment fall; higher interest rates may attract foreign funds and raise the exchange rate, making exports less competitive.

### Expenditure-switching policies

- **Depreciation of the currency:** makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, so spending switches towards domestic goods.
- **Protection:** tariffs and quotas raise the price or limit the quantity of imports.

*Weaknesses:* a depreciation raises the price of imported food, fuel and inputs, causing cost-push inflation; if demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, the deficit may worsen at first. Protection invites retaliation and is restricted by trade agreements.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: how a depreciation switches spending]
The zola, a fictional currency, depreciates from US\$1 = 10 zola to US\$1 = 12 zola.

1. An export priced at 500 zola cost a foreign buyer 500 ÷ 10 = **US\$50**; now it costs 500 ÷ 12 = **US\$41.67**. Exports become more competitive.
2. An import priced at US\$80 cost 80 × 10 = **800 zola**; now it costs 80 × 12 = **960 zola**. Imports become dearer.
3. If foreign buyers respond strongly to the lower price and domestic buyers switch to local goods (elastic demand), export earnings rise, spending on imports falls and the deficit narrows. If demand is inelastic, export volumes rise only a little and the import bill in local currency rises, so the deficit can worsen at first.
:::

### Supply-side policies

Education and training, investment in infrastructure, and policies that raise productivity and encourage competition reduce firms' costs and improve quality. These improve **competitiveness** and tackle the root cause of a persistent deficit, but they are slow and costly.

### Policies to reduce a surplus

A country with a large surplus can use expansionary fiscal and monetary policy (raising domestic spending and imports), allow its currency to appreciate, or reduce barriers to imports.

:::example{title="Choosing a policy mix"}
A fictional country's deficit is caused by a consumer credit boom and a high exchange rate. Higher interest rates would cut the credit boom, but they would also attract foreign funds and push the currency higher. A better mix might be tighter fiscal policy to reduce spending, combined with lower interest rates that allow the currency to depreciate, and long-term investment in skills and transport to raise productivity. The right policy depends on the **cause** of the deficit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying every deficit is bad"}
Judge a deficit by its size relative to GDP, its cause, how it is financed and how long it lasts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the side effects of policies"}
Each policy has costs: expenditure-reducing policies cut output and jobs, depreciation can cause inflation, protection invites retaliation, and supply-side policies are slow.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up depreciation effects"}
A depreciation makes exports **cheaper** abroad and imports **dearer** at home, not the other way round.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain causes:** link each cause to exports or imports, for example "high inflation → exports less competitive → exports fall, imports rise → deficit".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse impact:** use the chain from net exports to aggregate demand, GDP and jobs, and from currency flows to the exchange rate.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss policies:** for each policy explain how it works, then its drawbacks and the conditions under which it works (elasticity, cause of the deficit, time lags), and conclude which is best in the situation given.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Deficits come from high domestic demand, low competitiveness, a high exchange rate, weak foreign demand and adverse price changes; surpluses from the opposite.
- A persistent deficit reduces aggregate demand and jobs and puts downward pressure on the exchange rate; a surplus does the opposite.
- Expenditure-reducing policies cut spending but also output and jobs.
- Expenditure-switching policies (depreciation, protection) redirect spending, but can cause inflation or retaliation, and depend on price elasticity.
- Supply-side policies raise competitiveness and deal with the root cause, but they take time.
:::
