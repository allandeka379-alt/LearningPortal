## Introduction

Every time a Zimbabwean mine sells platinum to a buyer abroad, a tourist from overseas pays for a night's stay near Victoria Falls, a family in Masvingo receives money from a relative working in South Africa or the United Kingdom, or a local importer buys fuel from abroad, money crosses the border. The **balance of payments** is the record of all these transactions between the residents of one country and the rest of the world over a year. Its most important part for both of your syllabuses is the **current account**.

Exam questions on this topic almost always give a table of figures and ask you to calculate a balance (the balance of trade, the balance on services or the current account balance) and then explain what a deficit or surplus means. The calculations are simple subtractions and additions, but you must know which items belong in which section and keep track of minus signs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Balance of payments"}
A record of all the financial transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Visible trade"}
Trade in goods (physical items that can be seen and touched), such as tobacco, gold, fuel and machinery.
:::

:::definition{term="Invisible trade"}
Trade in services, such as tourism, transport, insurance, banking and education, together with income flows between countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of trade"}
The value of visible exports minus the value of visible imports (the balance of trade in goods).
:::

:::definition{term="Current account"}
The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary income"}
Income earned from factors of production across borders: wages of workers, and profits, interest and dividends on investments.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary income"}
Transfers of money for which nothing is given in return, such as remittances sent home by migrant workers, gifts and foreign aid.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of the **balance of trade** (visible exports and imports) and **invisibles**, and calls the income items **net income from abroad** and **current transfers**. Cambridge uses **trade in goods**, **trade in services**, **primary income** and **secondary income**. The calculations are the same: in an answer, use the terms of your own syllabus, or give both.
:::

## The four parts of the current account

| Section | Credits (money coming in) | Debits (money going out) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trade in goods (visible trade) | Exports of goods, e.g. tobacco, gold, lithium | Imports of goods, e.g. fuel, vehicles, medicines |
| Trade in services (invisible trade) | Foreign tourists' spending, transport and insurance sold to foreigners | Residents' holidays abroad, foreign freight and insurance, fees for studying abroad |
| Primary income | Wages, profits and interest earned abroad by residents | Profits and dividends sent home by foreign-owned firms, interest on foreign loans |
| Secondary income | Remittances received, aid and gifts from abroad | Remittances sent abroad by foreign workers, aid and gifts paid out |

For each section: **balance = credits − debits**. A positive balance is a **surplus**; a negative balance is a **deficit**. The **current account balance** is the sum of the four balances.

:::formula{name="Current account balances"}
Balance of trade = visible exports − visible imports

Balance on services = service exports − service imports

Current account balance = balance on goods + balance on services + primary income balance + secondary income balance
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: calculating the balances]
The table shows the current account of a fictional country (US\$ million).

| Section | Credits (US\$ million) | Debits (US\$ million) | Balance (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Trade in goods | 4,200 | 5,100 | −900 (deficit) |
| Trade in services | 900 | 1,150 | −250 (deficit) |
| Primary income | 300 | 850 | −550 (deficit) |
| Secondary income | 1,400 | 200 | 1,200 (surplus) |
| **Current account balance** |  |  | **−500** |

1. Balance of trade = 4,200 − 5,100 = **−900**: a visible trade deficit.
2. Balance on services = 900 − 1,150 = −250. The invisible balance (services plus income flows) = −250 − 550 + 1,200 = **+400**.
3. Current account = −900 − 250 − 550 + 1,200 = **−500**: a current account deficit of US\$500 million.
4. Interpretation: the country spends more on imports of goods and services, and pays more income abroad, than it earns. Large remittances (secondary income) reduce the deficit but do not remove it.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: finding a missing figure]
A fictional country has a current account deficit of US\$350 million. Its balances are: trade in services −180, primary income −420 and secondary income +650 (US\$ million). Its exports of goods are worth US\$2,600 million. What is the value of its imports of goods?

1. The four balances add up to −350, so the balance of trade = −350 − (−180) − (−420) − 650 = **−400**.
2. Balance of trade = exports − imports, so −400 = 2,600 − imports.
3. Imports of goods = 2,600 + 400 = **US\$3,000 million**.
4. Check: −400 − 180 − 420 + 650 = −350. ✓
:::

## Interpreting a deficit or a surplus

A **current account deficit** means that more money is flowing out of the country through the current account than is flowing in. It must be financed: by borrowing from abroad, by attracting foreign investment, or by running down the country's reserves of foreign currency. A small deficit that pays for imports of machinery and equipment can be healthy, because the capital goods raise future output and exports. A large deficit that lasts for many years, especially one that pays for consumer goods, may lead to rising foreign debt, pressure on the exchange rate and shortages of foreign currency.

![Current account of a fictional country in US\$ million: goods balance -900, services -250, primary income -550 and secondary income +1,200 add up to a current account deficit of 500; the large remittances in secondary income reduce the deficit but do not remove it.](media:diagrams/current-account-balances.png "Figure: the four balances add up to the current account balance (negative bars are deficits)")

A **current account surplus** means more money is flowing in than out. It allows a country to build reserves, repay debts or invest abroad. But a surplus can also mean that the country's residents are consuming less than they could, and it may cause friction with trading partners.

:::example{title="Why the sections matter"}
Two fictional countries both have a current account deficit of US\$200 million. In Country A the deficit comes from large imports of mining machinery for new mines that will start exporting in two years. In Country B it comes from imports of consumer goods while exports are falling. Country A's deficit is likely to correct itself as exports grow; Country B's is a sign of a lack of competitiveness and is more worrying. Always look at **which** section causes the deficit, its **size** compared with the country's GDP, and **how long** it lasts.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the balance of trade the current account"}
The balance of trade covers goods only. The current account adds services, primary income and secondary income.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting remittances in the wrong section"}
Money sent home by relatives working abroad is a transfer for which nothing is given in return: it is **secondary income** (a current transfer), not primary income and not an export.
:::

:::mistake{title="Losing minus signs"}
Write each balance with its sign before adding: −900 − 250 − 550 + 1,200 = −500. Adding the absolute values gives a wrong answer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a trade deficit with a budget deficit"}
A current account deficit concerns transactions with the rest of the world. A budget deficit means government spending is greater than government revenue.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** classify each item correctly, for example "spending by foreign tourists is a credit in trade in services".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** set out credits − debits for each section, keep the signs, and label the answer "deficit" or "surplus" with its units (US\$ million).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / discuss:** say what caused the balance, whether it is large compared with GDP, how it is financed, and whether it is likely to last. A discuss answer weighs "good deficit" (capital imports) against "bad deficit" (lost competitiveness) and reaches a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The balance of payments records transactions between residents and the rest of the world; the current account is its main part.
- Visible trade is trade in goods; invisible trade covers services and income flows.
- Current account = trade in goods + trade in services + primary income + secondary income; each balance = credits − debits.
- Remittances and aid are secondary income; profits, interest and wages across borders are primary income.
- A deficit must be financed by borrowing, investment or reserves; judge it by its cause, size and length.
:::
