## Introduction

Every year a finance minister presents a budget speech that sets out what the government hopes to achieve for the whole economy: faster growth, more jobs, low and stable inflation, a healthier balance of payments, a fairer spread of income and the protection of natural resources. These are the government's **macroeconomic aims**. In Zimbabwe they appear in national development strategies and in the budget statements presented by the Minister of Finance.

The aims matter because they affect everyone: whether school leavers find jobs, whether wages keep up with prices and whether the country can pay for its imports of fuel and machinery. But the aims do not always fit together. A policy that boosts growth may also push up prices or imports. Cambridge 0455 asks you to explain each aim, why governments choose it and set targets, and the **conflicts** between aims, and this lesson is the foundation for the lessons on fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Macroeconomic aim"}
An objective a government sets for the economy as a whole, such as economic growth or stable prices.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic growth"}
An increase in a country's real GDP (the volume of output) over time.
:::

:::definition{term="Full employment"}
A situation in which everyone who is willing and able to work at the going wage rate has a job, apart from a small amount of short-term unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Price stability"}
A low and steady rate of inflation, so that the general price level rises slowly and predictably.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of payments stability"}
A current account that is close to balance over time, without large or lasting deficits or surpluses.
:::

:::definition{term="Redistribution of income"}
Changing the way income is shared, usually to reduce the gap between rich and poor.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental sustainability"}
Using resources and producing output in a way that does not damage the ability of future generations to meet their needs.
:::

## The six aims and why governments choose them

| Aim | Why governments want it | Typical target (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic growth | Higher incomes and living standards; more tax revenue for public services | A steady rise in real GDP, e.g. 3% to 5% a year |
| Full employment | Income for households; less poverty; output is not wasted; lower spending on support for the unemployed | A low unemployment rate |
| Price stability | Protects the value of money, savings and wages; helps firms plan; keeps exports competitive | A low, steady inflation rate, e.g. 2% to 5% a year |
| Balance of payments stability | Avoids running down foreign currency reserves or building up debts to other countries; helps keep the exchange rate stable | A current account deficit no larger than a set share of GDP |
| Redistribution of income | Reduces poverty and inequality; gives everyone access to basic needs | A fall in the share of income going to the richest group |
| Environmental sustainability | Protects forests, soils, water and wildlife; avoids future costs of pollution and climate change | Limits on emissions, deforestation or water use |

Governments **set targets** because a number makes the aim clear, allows progress to be measured, and holds the government accountable. A target also guides the people who run policy: for example, a central bank may be given an inflation target.

## Why aims can conflict

Policies that help one aim can harm another. The main conflicts to know are:

1. **Growth (or full employment) versus price stability.** When spending rises quickly, firms hire more workers and produce more, but if the economy is near full capacity, firms also raise prices: demand-pull inflation. Fighting inflation by cutting demand can slow growth and raise unemployment.
2. **Growth versus balance of payments stability.** Rising incomes increase spending on imports (cars, fuel, electronic goods), so the current account deficit may widen.
3. **Growth versus environmental sustainability.** More output from mining, farming and industry can mean more pollution, deforestation and use of non-renewable resources.
4. **Redistribution versus growth.** Higher taxes on high earners and firms can fund benefits for the poor, but they may reduce incentives to work, save and invest.
5. **Price stability versus balance of payments.** Higher interest rates to cut inflation may attract foreign money and raise the exchange rate, making exports dearer.

Conflicts are not certain. Growth driven by **supply-side** improvements (better skills, infrastructure, technology) can raise output without causing inflation, and greener technology can allow growth with less pollution.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: judging performance against targets]
A fictional country, Country X, sets targets for its economy. Real GDP was US\$42,000 million in 2024, US\$43,260 million in 2025 and US\$45,855.6 million in 2026. The table shows the results.

| Indicator | Target | 2025 | 2026 |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Real GDP growth (%) | 4.0 | 3.0 | 6.0 |
| Inflation (%) | 5.0 or less | 4.0 | 7.0 |
| Unemployment rate (%) | Below 8.0 | 9.0 | 7.0 |
| Current account balance (% of GDP) | Deficit no larger than 3.0 | −2.1 | −4.6 |

1. Growth in 2025 = (43,260 − 42,000) ÷ 42,000 × 100 = **3.0%**, below the 4.0% target.
2. Growth in 2026 = (45,855.6 − 43,260) ÷ 43,260 × 100 = **6.0%**, above the target.
3. In 2026 unemployment fell to 7.0% (target met), but inflation rose to 7.0% and the current account deficit widened to 4.6% of GDP (both targets missed).
4. Interpretation: fast growth created jobs, but rising demand pushed up prices and pulled in imports. This shows the conflicts between growth and price stability, and between growth and the balance of payments.
:::

:::example{title="A growth and environment conflict"}
A fictional government wants to raise growth by expanding coal mining and coal-fired power. More electricity would let factories produce more and create jobs, helping growth and employment. But burning more coal increases air pollution and carbon emissions, and opencast mining damages land and water. The government could reduce the conflict by requiring cleaner technology, investing in solar power, or taxing pollution, but each of these raises costs and may slow growth in the short run.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying full employment means zero unemployment"}
Full employment still allows for some short-term frictional and seasonal unemployment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating a conflict without explaining the link"}
"Growth conflicts with inflation" earns little on its own. Explain the chain: higher spending → firms near capacity → costs and prices rise → inflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating price stability as falling prices"}
Price stability means **low and stable** inflation, not deflation. Falling prices can also harm the economy.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain an aim:** define it, then give a reason the government wants it (who benefits and how).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse a conflict:** name the two aims, then use a chain of reasoning: policy → effect on spending or output → effect on the second aim. Data in the question (inflation rising as unemployment falls) is evidence to quote.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** point out that conflicts depend on the cause of growth and the state of the economy, and that supply-side policies can reduce them; finish with a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The six aims: economic growth, full employment, price stability, balance of payments stability, redistribution of income and environmental sustainability.
- Governments pursue them to raise living standards and set numerical targets to measure progress.
- Main conflicts: growth or employment versus inflation; growth versus the current account; growth versus the environment; redistribution versus incentives.
- Conflicts are likely when demand rises in an economy near capacity; supply-side growth can reduce them.
:::
