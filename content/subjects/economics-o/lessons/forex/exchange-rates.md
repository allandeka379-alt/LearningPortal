## Introduction

A Zimbabwean importer buying solar panels from China, a tourist from Europe paying for a safari at Hwange, a family in Bulawayo receiving rand from a relative in Johannesburg: each of these needs one currency to be swapped for another. The price at which the swap happens is the **exchange rate**.

Zimbabwe's own experience shows why exchange rates matter. After the hyperinflation of 2007-2008 the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned and, from 2009, the country used a multi-currency system in which the US dollar and the South African rand circulated widely. A local currency was re-introduced in 2019, and in April 2024 the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe introduced the Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG). The value of the local currency against the US dollar affects the price of fuel, imported food and medicines, the earnings of exporters, and wages.

Both syllabuses expect you to define the exchange rate, explain why currencies are bought and sold, show how a floating rate is set by demand and supply, and explain how changes in the rate affect exports and imports. Simple conversions are common in multiple-choice and data-response questions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign exchange rate"}
The price of one currency expressed in terms of another currency, for example US\$1 = 25 kora.
:::

:::definition{term="Floating exchange rate"}
An exchange rate that is determined by the demand for and supply of the currency on the foreign exchange market, without the government or central bank fixing it.
:::

:::definition{term="Appreciation"}
A rise in the value of a currency in a floating exchange rate system: one unit buys more of other currencies.
:::

:::definition{term="Depreciation"}
A fall in the value of a currency in a floating exchange rate system: one unit buys less of other currencies.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign exchange market"}
The market in which currencies are bought and sold, through banks, bureaux de change and other dealers.
:::

## Why people buy and sell currencies

A currency is **demanded** (bought) by foreigners who want to:

- buy the country's **exports** of goods and services, including tourism;
- **invest** in the country, for example by building a mine or factory, or by buying shares or bonds;
- **save** in the country's banks, attracted by its interest rates;
- **speculate**, hoping the currency will rise in value.

A currency is **supplied** (sold) by its own residents who want to buy **imports**, travel abroad, invest or save abroad, or send money to people in other countries. Remittances flowing into a country increase demand for its currency (or bring in foreign currency); profits sent home by foreign-owned firms increase its supply.

## How a floating exchange rate is determined

In a floating system the exchange rate is a price like any other. Draw a diagram with the **price of the currency** (in another currency) on the vertical axis and the **quantity of the currency** on the horizontal axis. The demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up; the exchange rate is set where they cross.

![Foreign exchange market for the kora: higher export demand shifts demand right from D1 to D2, so at 4 US cents there is excess demand of 200 million kora and the price of the kora rises to 5 cents with 800 million traded, an appreciation of 25 percent (US\$1 = 25 kora becomes US\$1 = 20 kora).](media:diagrams/kora-appreciation.png "Figure: a rise in export demand shifts the demand for a currency right and it appreciates")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the market for a fictional currency, the kora]
The table shows the market for the kora (K), the currency of a fictional country, against the US dollar.

| Price of 1 kora (US cents) | Kora demanded (million) | Kora demanded after the rise in exports (million) | Kora supplied (million) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 900 | 1,100 | 500 |
| 3 | 800 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 4 | 700 | 900 | 700 |
| 5 | 600 | 800 | 800 |
| 6 | 500 | 700 | 900 |

1. At first the market clears at **4 US cents per kora** (700 million kora traded), which is the same as US\$1 = 25 kora (100 ÷ 4).
2. World demand for the country's exports then rises. Foreign buyers need more kora: the **demand curve shifts to the right**, from D1 to D2.
3. At 4 cents there is now excess demand of 900 − 700 = 200 million kora, so the price of the kora rises.
4. The new equilibrium is **5 US cents per kora** (800 million kora traded), or US\$1 = 20 kora. The kora has **appreciated** by (5 − 4) ÷ 4 × 100 = **25%** against the dollar.
:::

### Causes of fluctuations

- **Changes in trade.** Higher exports (or a rise in export prices) raise demand for the currency: appreciation. Higher imports raise its supply: depreciation.
- **Interest rates.** A rise in the country's interest rates attracts savings from abroad (so-called "hot money"), raising demand for its currency.
- **Speculation.** If dealers expect a currency to rise, they buy it now, which itself pushes the price up; expectations of a fall cause selling and depreciation.
- **Inflation.** Higher inflation than in other countries makes exports less competitive and imports more attractive, reducing demand for the currency and raising supply.
- **Investment and remittances.** Foreign direct investment and money sent home by citizens abroad increase demand for the currency.

## Effects on exports and imports

When a currency **appreciates**, the country's exports become **dearer** for foreigners and its imports become **cheaper** at home. When it **depreciates**, exports become **cheaper** for foreigners and imports **dearer** at home. The usual result: depreciation raises the quantity of exports demanded and reduces the quantity of imports; appreciation does the opposite. How large the change is depends on the price elasticity of demand for exports and imports.

A memory aid often used is **SPICED**: **S**trong **P**ound (currency), **I**mports **C**heap, **E**xports **D**ear.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: converting prices]
The kora moves from US\$1 = 25 kora to US\$1 = 30 kora.

1. Each dollar now buys more kora, so the kora has **depreciated**.
2. A tobacco exporter charges 60,000 kora per tonne. Price to a foreign buyer before: 60,000 ÷ 25 = **US\$2,400**; after: 60,000 ÷ 30 = **US\$2,000**. The export is about 16.7% cheaper for foreigners, so sales are likely to rise.
3. An importer buys a pump priced at US\$400. Cost in kora before: 400 × 25 = **10,000 kora**; after: 400 × 30 = **12,000 kora**. Imports are dearer at home, which may add to inflation.
:::

:::example{title="Who gains and who loses from a depreciation"}
**Gainers:** exporters such as tobacco farmers and mines (their goods are cheaper abroad, or they receive more local currency for each dollar earned), tourism businesses, and families receiving remittances in foreign currency. **Losers:** importers and consumers of imported fuel, food and medicines, firms that use imported inputs, and anyone who has borrowed in foreign currency, whose debt is now larger in local currency. A depreciation can also raise inflation through dearer imports.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction of the conversion wrong"}
If US\$1 = 25 kora, to turn dollars into kora **multiply** by 25; to turn kora into dollars **divide** by 25. Check the answer is sensible: a dollar price should be a much smaller number than a kora price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading a bigger number as a stronger currency"}
A move from US\$1 = 25 kora to US\$1 = 30 kora means the kora has **fallen**: you need more kora to buy a dollar.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying depreciation always improves the trade balance"}
If demand for exports and imports is price inelastic, quantities change little, and dearer imports can make the import bill larger.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** exchange rate = the price of one currency in terms of another. Appreciation and depreciation refer to changes in a **floating** rate.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / analyse with a diagram:** describe the axes (price of the currency; quantity of the currency), say which curve shifts and which way, and give the old and new exchange rate.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** for the effects of a change, consider exporters, importers, consumers, inflation, employment and the current account, and note that the result depends on price elasticity of demand and on the cause of the change. Conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.
- In a floating system the rate is set by demand for the currency (exports, inward investment, saving, speculation) and its supply (imports, outward investment).
- More exports, higher interest rates or optimistic speculation cause appreciation; more imports, relatively high inflation or pessimism cause depreciation.
- Appreciation makes exports dearer and imports cheaper; depreciation does the opposite.
- Conversions: multiply to go from dollars to the local currency and divide to go back; the effect on trade depends on price elasticity of demand.
:::
