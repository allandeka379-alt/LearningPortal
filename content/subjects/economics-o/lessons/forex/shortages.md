## Introduction

Zimbabwe imports almost all of its fuel, much of its machinery, many medicines and, in drought years, large quantities of grain. None of these can be paid for in local currency: foreign suppliers want US dollars, rand or other widely accepted currencies. When the country earns less foreign currency than it needs, firms cannot import raw materials, fuel queues form, prices on the parallel (unofficial) market rise and factories cut production.

Zimbabwe has faced serious shortages of foreign currency at several times, for example in the years before the multi-currency system was adopted in 2009 and again in the period before the currency reforms of 2019. ZIMSEC asks you to outline the problems that result from foreign exchange shortages and the possible sources of foreign currency. Questions are usually short essays or parts of a data-response question, so you need clear, developed points.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign currency (foreign exchange)"}
Currencies of other countries, such as the US dollar, the South African rand or the euro, which are used to pay for imports and foreign debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign currency shortage"}
A situation in which the demand for foreign currency (to pay for imports, debts and other payments abroad) is greater than the supply available to the country.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign currency reserves"}
The stock of foreign currency held by a country's central bank (in Zimbabwe, the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe) to pay for imports and defend the currency.
:::

:::definition{term="Parallel market"}
An unofficial market in which foreign currency is bought and sold, usually at a higher price than the official rate, when official supplies are short.
:::

:::definition{term="Remittances"}
Money sent home by citizens working abroad to their families.
:::

## Causes of foreign currency shortages

A shortage happens when outflows of foreign currency are larger than inflows. Causes include:

- **Low export earnings:** exports fall because of droughts (lower tobacco, cotton and maize output), falls in world prices of minerals and crops, or low productivity.
- **High demand for imports:** fuel, electricity, food, machinery and consumer goods; a drought can suddenly increase food imports.
- **Debt repayments:** interest and repayments on foreign loans use up foreign currency.
- **Profits sent abroad** by foreign-owned firms.
- **Capital flight and low confidence:** when people expect the local currency to fall, they move savings abroad or hold US dollars outside the banking system.
- **Illegal outflows**, such as smuggling minerals and under-declaring export earnings.
- **Limited access to foreign loans and investment**, for example when a country has unpaid debts or investors see high risk.
- **An official exchange rate set too high** (overvalued): exporters receive less local currency for each dollar earned, so they have less incentive to export through official channels, while imports look cheap.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: inflows and the foreign currency gap]
The table shows the foreign currency inflows of a fictional country in one year.

| Source | Inflow (US\$ million) | Share (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Mineral exports | 3,600 | 51.4 |
| Farm exports (mainly tobacco) | 1,500 | 21.4 |
| Remittances | 1,200 | 17.1 |
| Tourism | 300 | 4.3 |
| Foreign investment and loans | 400 | 5.7 |
| **Total** | **7,000** | **100.0** |

1. The country needs US\$7,600 million for imports and debt payments. The gap is 7,600 − 7,000 = **US\$600 million**, about 7.9% of what it needs.
2. Mineral exports provide just over half of all inflows (3,600 ÷ 7,000 × 100 = 51.4%).
3. If mineral prices fall by 20% with the same quantities sold, mineral earnings fall by 0.20 × 3,600 = **US\$720 million**, so total inflows fall to 6,280 and the gap more than doubles, to **US\$1,320 million**. The country's dependence on minerals makes its foreign currency supply fragile.
:::

## Problems caused by foreign currency shortages

- **Shortages of imported goods:** fuel, medicines, spare parts and raw materials become scarce; queues and rationing appear.
- **Lower production and job losses:** factories that rely on imported inputs cut output or close; farmers cannot buy imported fertiliser and chemicals.
- **Inflation:** scarce imports and a falling exchange rate push up prices; firms price goods according to the parallel market rate.
- **Parallel markets and multiple prices:** different rates for the same currency confuse prices and encourage corruption and speculation.
- **Difficulty repaying foreign debts**, damaging the country's credit rating and making new loans harder and dearer to obtain.
- **Lower investment:** foreign investors fear they will not be able to send profits home, and local firms cannot import machinery.
- **Falling living standards and emigration**, as real incomes fall and people seek work abroad.

## Possible sources of foreign currency for Zimbabwe

- **Mineral exports:** gold, platinum, chrome, diamonds, lithium, coal and others; adding value by processing minerals locally increases earnings.
- **Farm exports:** tobacco is a major foreign currency earner; cotton, horticulture (flowers, vegetables, citrus), sugar and beef also earn foreign currency.
- **Tourism:** Victoria Falls, Hwange National Park, Great Zimbabwe, the Eastern Highlands and Lake Kariba attract foreign visitors.
- **Remittances** from Zimbabweans working abroad (the diaspora).
- **Foreign direct investment** in mining, farming, energy and manufacturing.
- **Loans and grants** from other governments and international lenders, and aid inflows; loans must be repaid with interest.
- **Manufactured and processed exports** to regional markets in SADC and COMESA.

:::example{title="Choosing how to increase foreign currency"}
A fictional government wants more foreign currency. Borrowing abroad is quick but adds to future repayments. Encouraging tourism and processed exports is slower but builds lasting earnings. Making it easier and cheaper for citizens abroad to send money home through banks and mobile money channels brings remittances into the official system. A sensible plan combines short-term measures with longer-term efforts to diversify exports, so that one drought or price fall does not cause a new shortage.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the central bank can simply print foreign currency"}
A country can print only its own currency. Foreign currency must be earned (exports, tourism, remittances) or borrowed and invested from abroad.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing sources without explaining them"}
For each source say how it brings in foreign currency: "tourists pay in foreign currency for hotels and tours".
:::

:::mistake{title="Using disputed figures"}
Do not invent statistics for Zimbabwe's export earnings or reserves. Describe the causes and effects, and look up official figures from the Reserve Bank or ZIMSTAT if you need them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Outline problems:** give each problem with its effect ("shortage of fuel → transport costs rise → prices of goods rise").
:::

:::exam-tip
**State sources:** name the source and a Zimbabwean example (tobacco, gold, Victoria Falls, diaspora remittances).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss solutions:** weigh quick fixes (loans, restrictions on imports) against lasting ones (export diversification, value addition, tourism, attracting investment), and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A foreign currency shortage occurs when the demand for foreign currency exceeds the supply.
- Causes: low export earnings, high import demand, debt repayments, profit outflows, capital flight, illegal outflows and an overvalued official rate.
- Problems: shortages of imports, lower output and jobs, inflation, parallel markets, debt difficulties and lower investment.
- Sources for Zimbabwe: minerals, farm exports, tourism, remittances, foreign investment, loans and grants, and processed exports.
- Lasting solutions depend on diversifying and increasing exports rather than borrowing.
:::
