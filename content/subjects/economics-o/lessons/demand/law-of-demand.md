## Introduction

Walk past a tuck shop in Gweru on a Monday morning and watch what happens when the price of a loaf of bread goes up. Some customers still buy bread, but they buy fewer loaves; others switch to maize-meal porridge or buns. When the price comes down again, more loaves leave the shelf. That everyday pattern is the **law of demand**, and it is the starting point for almost everything else in this subject: equilibrium price, elasticity, taxes and price controls all build on it.

Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge examine demand in multiple-choice questions, in data-response or structured questions (reading a demand schedule) and in essays (explaining why a demand curve slopes downwards). In this lesson you will learn the precise definitions, how to read and build a **demand schedule**, how to describe a **demand curve** in words, and how to add individual demand into **market demand**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing **and able** to buy at each possible price, over a given period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective demand"}
A want for a good that is backed by the ability to pay for it. Only effective demand counts when economists measure demand.
:::

:::definition{term="Latent (potential) demand"}
A desire for a good that consumers cannot yet turn into purchases, usually because they do not have enough money. It may become effective demand if incomes rise or the price falls.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of demand"}
Other things being equal, as the price of a good rises the quantity demanded falls, and as the price falls the quantity demanded rises.
:::

:::definition{term="Market demand"}
The total quantity demanded by all the consumers in a market at each price: the sum of all the individual demands.
:::

Notice three parts of the definition of demand. It is about **willingness and ability** together, it gives a quantity **at each price** (not just one number), and it refers to a **time period** (per day, per week, per month). "Rudo demands 5 loaves" is incomplete; "Rudo demands 5 loaves a week at US\$1.00 each" is a proper statement of demand.

### Effective and latent demand

Nyasha would like a motorbike to travel from her village to Masvingo, but she has no savings and no loan. Her wish is **latent demand**. If she gets a job and can afford the motorbike at the market price, her demand becomes **effective**. Firms are interested in both: effective demand tells them how much they can sell now, while latent demand tells them where future sales may come from (for example, if they can offer the product more cheaply or on credit).

## The law of demand

The law of demand describes an **inverse** (negative) relationship between price and quantity demanded. The phrase *other things being equal* (in Latin, *ceteris paribus*) matters: we assume that incomes, tastes, the prices of other goods and everything else that affects demand stay the same, and only the good's own price changes.

Why do people buy more when the price falls?

- **Income effect:** a lower price means the same income buys more, so consumers can afford more of the good.
- **Substitution effect:** the good becomes cheaper compared with its substitutes, so some consumers switch towards it.
- **New buyers:** at a lower price, some people whose demand was latent can now afford to buy, so their demand becomes effective.
- **Diminishing satisfaction:** each extra unit usually adds less satisfaction than the one before, so a consumer will buy more units only if the price is lower.

## Demand schedules and the demand curve

A **demand schedule** is a table showing the quantity demanded at each price. The schedule below is for bread bought by three households in a fictional neighbourhood of Gweru (loaves per week).

| Price per loaf (US\$) | Tendai's household | Rudo's household | Farai's household | Market demand (three households) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.80 | 10 | 6 | 4 | 20 |
| 1.00 | 8 | 5 | 4 | 17 |
| 1.20 | 7 | 4 | 3 | 14 |
| 1.40 | 5 | 3 | 3 | 11 |
| 1.60 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 8 |

A **demand curve** is the schedule plotted on a graph. Describe it like this:

- **Price** goes on the **vertical axis** and **quantity demanded** (per time period) on the **horizontal axis**.
- Each row of the schedule is one point; joining the points gives the demand curve, labelled **D**.
- The curve **slopes downwards from left to right**: high prices go with small quantities, low prices with large quantities.
- A change in the good's own price causes a **movement along** the curve, not a new curve. A fall in price is an **extension** of demand; a rise in price is a **contraction** of demand.

![Market demand curve for bread in Gweru plotted from the schedule: it slopes down from 8 loaves a week at US\$1.60 to 20 loaves at US\$0.80; a price rise from US\$1.00 (17 loaves) to US\$1.40 (11 loaves) is a contraction, a movement up along the same curve.](media:diagrams/bread-demand-curve.png "Figure: the market demand curve for bread; a rise in price is a movement along it")

In exams you may be asked to "describe the diagram you would draw". Name the axes, label the curve, say which way it slopes and mark the price and quantity you are discussing (for example "at P1 = US\$1.00, Q1 = 17 loaves").

## Individual and market demand

Market demand is found by **adding horizontally**: at each price, add the quantities that every consumer demands. In the table, at US\$1.20 the market demand is $7 + 4 + 3 = 14$ loaves. Because every household follows the law of demand, the market demand curve also slopes downwards, and it lies further to the right than any individual curve.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: reading the bread schedule]
Use the schedule above. Describe what happens to market demand when the price of a loaf rises from US\$1.00 to US\$1.40.

1. At US\$1.00 the market quantity demanded is $8 + 5 + 4 = 17$ loaves a week.
2. At US\$1.40 it is $5 + 3 + 3 = 11$ loaves a week.
3. The change is $17 - 11 = 6$ loaves a week: quantity demanded **falls** by 6 loaves.
4. On the diagram this is a **movement up along** the market demand curve (a contraction of demand), from P = US\$1.00, Q = 17 to P = US\$1.40, Q = 11. The curve itself does not move, because only the price of bread changed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: from one household to the whole market]
A fictional village near Chinhoyi has 250 households. Each household buys 4 bags of maize meal a month at US\$5 a bag, 3.5 at US\$6, 3 at US\$7, 2.5 at US\$8 and 2 at US\$9. Every household has the same demand.

1. Market demand = number of households × quantity demanded by one household, at each price.
2. At US\$5: $250 \times 4 = 1{,}000$ bags; at US\$6: $250 \times 3.5 = 875$ bags; at US\$7: $250 \times 3 = 750$ bags; at US\$8: $250 \times 2.5 = 625$ bags; at US\$9: $250 \times 2 = 500$ bags.
3. The market schedule is:

| Price per 10 kg bag (US\$) | Market quantity demanded (bags per month) |
| --- | ---: |
| 5 | 1,000 |
| 6 | 875 |
| 7 | 750 |
| 8 | 625 |
| 9 | 500 |

4. Each US\$1 rise in price reduces market quantity demanded by 125 bags a month, so the market demand curve is a downward-sloping straight line.
:::

:::example{title="Is it demand or just a wish?"}
Chipo says: "Everyone in our class demands a new smartphone." An economist would disagree. Many pupils **want** a smartphone (latent demand), but demand counts only the phones they are willing and able to buy at a given price in a given period. If a shop in Harare cut its price sharply, some of that latent demand would become effective demand and the quantity demanded would rise.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out ability to pay"}
Defining demand as "what people want" earns no credit. Demand is what consumers are **willing and able** to buy at each price over a period of time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a price change shifts the demand curve"}
A change in the good's **own price** causes a movement **along** the demand curve (extension or contraction). Only other factors, such as income or the price of a substitute, shift the whole curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding prices instead of quantities"}
Market demand adds the **quantities** demanded at the **same price**. Never add the prices together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting price on the wrong axis"}
In economics, price is always on the **vertical** axis and quantity on the **horizontal** axis.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / define:** one precise sentence is enough, but include all the key words: *willing and able*, *at each price*, *over a period of time*. For the law of demand, say *other things being equal*.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain why a demand curve slopes downwards:** give a reason and develop it, for example "a fall in price makes the good cheaper than its substitutes, so consumers switch to it (substitution effect), so quantity demanded rises".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse / describe the diagram:** name the axes, label D, give the direction of the slope and mark the two price–quantity points from the data. Use the exact figures from the schedule and give units (loaves per week).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use *extension* and *contraction* for movements along the demand curve. ZIMSEC also asks you to distinguish **effective** from **latent** demand; Cambridge asks you to link **individual** and **market** demand. Learn both.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a period of time; only effective demand (backed by money) counts.
- The law of demand: other things being equal, a higher price means a lower quantity demanded.
- A demand schedule is a table; the demand curve plots it with price on the vertical axis and slopes downwards from left to right.
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve: an extension (price falls) or a contraction (price rises).
- Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands: add the quantities at each price.
:::
