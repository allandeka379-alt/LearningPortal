## Introduction

At the end of the month, when civil servants in Bulawayo are paid, butchers sell more meat even though their prices have not changed. In December, demand for bus tickets from Harare to rural homes jumps although fares are the same as in November. In both cases something **other than the price** has changed, and the whole demand curve moves.

Examiners test this distinction every year. A question may describe an event (a pay rise, a health warning, a rise in the price of a related good) and ask you to say what happens to the demand curve, or give two demand schedules and ask you to identify the change. You must know the **conditions of demand**, the direction each one moves the curve, and the difference between a **change in quantity demanded** and a **change in demand**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Change in quantity demanded"}
A movement along an existing demand curve caused only by a change in the good's own price: an **extension** (price falls, quantity demanded rises) or a **contraction** (price rises, quantity demanded falls).
:::

:::definition{term="Change in demand"}
A shift of the whole demand curve caused by a change in a factor other than the good's own price: an **increase** (shift to the right, more demanded at every price) or a **decrease** (shift to the left, less demanded at every price).
:::

:::definition{term="Substitutes"}
Goods that can be used in place of each other, such as beef and chicken, or kombi rides and bus rides.
:::

:::definition{term="Complements"}
Goods that are used together (joint demand), such as cars and petrol, or mobile phones and airtime.
:::

:::definition{term="Normal and inferior goods"}
A normal good is one whose demand rises when income rises. An inferior good is one whose demand falls when income rises, because consumers switch to better-quality alternatives.
:::

## The conditions of demand

The demand curve is drawn on the assumption that everything except the good's own price stays the same. When one of these **conditions of demand** changes, consumers want a different quantity at every price, so the curve shifts.

| Condition of demand | Change | Effect on demand for the good |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income | Rises (normal good) | Increase: curve shifts right |
| Income | Rises (inferior good) | Decrease: curve shifts left |
| Price of a substitute | Rises | Increase: consumers switch to this good |
| Price of a complement | Rises | Decrease: the pair becomes more expensive to use |
| Tastes and fashion | Move in favour of the good | Increase |
| Population | Grows, or more people in the target age group | Increase |
| Advertising | Successful campaign | Increase |
| Expectations | Consumers expect the price to rise soon | Increase now (people buy before the rise) |
| Taxes on income | Income tax rises | Decrease (lower disposable income, for normal goods) |
| Season and weather | Hot season for cold drinks | Increase |

Each of these works the other way when the change is reversed: a fall in the price of a substitute causes a **decrease** in demand for the good, a fall in income causes a decrease in demand for a normal good, and so on.

## Movements along versus shifts

On a diagram with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis:

- **Extension:** price falls, the point moves **down along** the curve D.
- **Contraction:** price rises, the point moves **up along** D.
- **Increase in demand:** the whole curve shifts **to the right**, from D1 to D2. At every price a larger quantity is demanded.
- **Decrease in demand:** the whole curve shifts **to the left**, from D1 to D2. At every price a smaller quantity is demanded.

A useful test: ask "did the good's own price cause this?" If yes, it is a movement along the curve. If no, it is a shift.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a pay rise and the demand for chicken]
The table shows weekly demand for chicken in a fictional town before and after a pay rise for workers in the town (chicken is a normal good).

| Price of chicken (US\$ per kg) | D1: before (kg per week) | D2: after the pay rise (kg per week) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 3 | 500 | 650 |
| 4 | 400 | 550 |
| 5 | 300 | 450 |
| 6 | 200 | 350 |

1. Compare the two columns at the same price: at US\$5, quantity demanded rises from 300 kg to 450 kg.
2. The same happens at every price: each quantity is 150 kg higher. Consumers want more at **every** price, so this is an **increase in demand**.
3. On the diagram the curve shifts to the **right**, from D1 to D2, by 150 kg at each price.
4. Contrast: if the price had fallen from US\$5 to US\$4 with no pay rise, quantity demanded would rise from 300 kg to 400 kg. That would be an extension along D1, not a shift.
:::

:::example{title="Worked example 2: deciding between a shift and a movement"}
For each event, decide what happens to the demand for **kombi rides** in Harare.

- **Kombi fares rise.** The good's own price changes, so quantity demanded falls: a **contraction**, a movement up along the curve.
- **Bus fares on the same routes rise.** Buses are a substitute, so some passengers switch to kombis: an **increase** in demand, the curve shifts right.
- **Many families buy their own cars.** Fewer people need kombis at any fare: a **decrease** in demand, the curve shifts left.
- **The population of the suburb served by the route grows.** More potential passengers at every fare: an **increase** in demand.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a price change shifts the demand curve"}
"The price of bread rose, so demand for bread decreased" is wrong. A change in own price causes a **contraction** in quantity demanded, a movement along the curve. "Demand decreased" means the curve shifted left for another reason.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting complements and substitutes the wrong way round"}
A rise in the price of a **substitute** increases demand for the good; a rise in the price of a **complement** decreases it. Check the relationship before you choose the direction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming every good is normal"}
For an inferior good, such as the cheapest grade of maize meal in some households, a rise in income can **reduce** demand as households switch to better alternatives.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shifting up and down"}
Describe shifts as **to the right** (increase) or **to the left** (decrease). "The demand curve shifts up" is ambiguous and may lose marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** name the condition of demand and the direction: "a rise in the price of a substitute, beef, shifts the demand curve for chicken to the right".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** give the chain of reasoning: event → effect on consumers' willingness or ability to buy → more or less demanded at every price → shift right or left.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe the diagram:** axes (price vertical, quantity horizontal), original curve D1, new curve D2 to the right or left, and, if the question is about a given price, the old and new quantities at that price (for example 300 kg and 450 kg at US\$5).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC often says a **change in demand** versus a **change in quantity demanded**; Cambridge says **shifts** versus **movements along** the demand curve (extensions and contractions). They mean the same thing.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the demand curve: an extension or a contraction.
- A change in any other condition of demand shifts the whole curve: right for an increase, left for a decrease.
- Conditions of demand include income, prices of substitutes and complements, tastes, population, advertising, expectations and taxes.
- A rise in the price of a substitute increases demand; a rise in the price of a complement decreases it.
- Income rises increase demand for normal goods but decrease demand for inferior goods.
:::
