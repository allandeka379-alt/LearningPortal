## Introduction

Walk through the centre of Bulawayo and count the hair salons, takeaway restaurants and clothing shops. There are many of them, and new ones open all the time, yet they are not identical: each salon has its own stylists, location and reputation, and each takeaway its own recipes. A salon can raise its prices a little and keep its loyal customers, but if it raises them too much, customers try the salon next door. This market is neither perfect competition nor monopoly: it is **monopolistic competition**.

Now think of mobile networks, banks, breweries or cement producers. In each of these markets a **few large firms** supply most of the output, and each firm watches closely what the others do. This is **oligopoly**.

Most real markets fall between the two extremes of perfect competition and monopoly. ZIMSEC (Form 3) asks you to describe the features of monopolistic competition and its advantages and disadvantages; an outline of oligopoly is also useful for comparing market structures. Cambridge 0455 does not examine these structures.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monopolistic competition"}
A market structure with many firms selling differentiated (slightly different) products, with low barriers to entry, so each firm has a small amount of control over its own price.
:::

:::definition{term="Product differentiation"}
Making a product different from rivals' products, in reality or in consumers' minds, through branding, design, quality, packaging, location or service.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-price competition"}
Competing for customers by means other than price, such as advertising, branding, loyalty schemes, quality, after-sales service and special offers.
:::

:::definition{term="Oligopoly"}
A market dominated by a few large firms, whose decisions are interdependent, usually protected by barriers to entry.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentration ratio"}
The combined market share of the largest firms in a market. The four-firm concentration ratio (CR4) is the total market share of the four largest firms.
:::

## Features of monopolistic competition

1. **Many sellers and buyers**, each firm small relative to the market.
2. **Differentiated products**: close substitutes, but not identical (different brands, styles, locations).
3. **Low barriers to entry and exit**: new firms can set up fairly easily.
4. **Some control over price**: because of brand loyalty, each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve, but demand is **price elastic** because many close substitutes exist.
5. **Non-price competition** is important: advertising, branding, service and location.
6. **Short-run abnormal profits are competed away in the long run**: new firms enter, attracted by profits, and take customers until only normal profit is earned.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a salon raises its price]
A fictional hair salon in Bulawayo charges US\$8 for a haircut and serves 400 customers a month. It raises its price to US\$9 and keeps 320 customers.

1. Revenue before: $8 \times 400 = 3{,}200$, so US\$3,200.
2. Revenue after: $9 \times 320 = 2{,}880$, so US\$2,880.
3. Percentage change in price: $(9 - 8) \div 8 \times 100 = 12.5\%$. Percentage change in customers: $(320 - 400) \div 400 \times 100 = -20\%$.
4. The salon keeps most of its customers (it is not a price taker: loyal customers stay), but demand is price elastic ($20\% > 12.5\%$) because there are many other salons nearby, so revenue falls by US\$320.
5. This is typical of monopolistic competition: some control over price, but not much. The salon would probably do better to compete on service, style or location.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of monopolistic competition

**Advantages:**

- **Wide choice and variety** of products and styles.
- **Innovation and quality**: firms try to make their product stand out.
- **Prices are kept fairly low** by many close substitutes and easy entry.
- Only **normal profit in the long run**, so consumers are not exploited for long.
- Firms respond quickly to changes in consumers' tastes.

**Disadvantages:**

- **Prices are higher than in perfect competition**: each firm has some price-setting power.
- **Advertising and branding costs** raise prices, and advertising may mislead.
- **Excess capacity**: many firms each produce below the output where their average cost is lowest (too many half-empty salons and restaurants), so they are not productively efficient.
- **Duplication** of facilities and small firms that cannot gain economies of scale.

## Oligopoly: an outline

**Features:**

- **A few large firms** have a large share of the market (a high concentration ratio).
- **Interdependence**: each firm's decisions depend on how it expects its rivals to react. If one cuts its price, the others may follow, so no one gains.
- **High barriers to entry**: economies of scale, high start-up costs, brand loyalty and licences.
- **Products** may be identical (cement, fuel) or differentiated (soft drinks, mobile networks).
- **Non-price competition** is common: advertising, promotions, loyalty schemes and new products, because price cuts are quickly matched.
- **Price rigidity**: prices tend to be stable for long periods, interrupted by occasional **price wars**.
- **Collusion**: firms may secretly agree to fix prices or share out markets, acting like a monopoly. A formal agreement is a **cartel**; OPEC, the organisation of oil-exporting countries, is a well-known cartel of countries. Collusion between firms is illegal in many countries because it harms consumers.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: measuring concentration]
The table shows annual sales in a fictional country's soft-drinks market.

| Firm | Sales (US\$ million) | Market share (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Firm A | 120 | 30.0 |
| Firm B | 90 | 22.5 |
| Firm C | 75 | 18.8 |
| Firm D | 45 | 11.3 |
| All other firms (about 30 small firms) | 70 | 17.5 |
| **Total** | **400** | **100** |

1. Market share of Firm C: $75 \div 400 \times 100 = 18.75\%$, shown as 18.8%.
2. Four-firm concentration ratio: $(120 + 90 + 75 + 45) \div 400 \times 100 = 330 \div 400 \times 100 = 82.5\%$.
3. Four firms sell more than four-fifths of all soft drinks, while about 30 small firms share the remaining 17.5%: the market is an **oligopoly**.
4. (The rounded shares add to 100.1% only because of rounding.)
:::

**Advantages of oligopoly:** economies of scale; large profits can pay for research and new products; competition through quality and innovation; price stability.

**Disadvantages:** prices may be higher than in more competitive markets, especially if firms collude; heavy spending on advertising; barriers keep new firms out; less choice if products are similar.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up monopolistic competition and monopoly"}
Monopolistic competition has **many** firms and **easy** entry; monopoly has **one** firm and **high** barriers. The common word "monopolistic" refers only to each firm's small control over the price of its own brand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying firms in monopolistic competition sell identical products"}
Products are differentiated. Identical products are a feature of perfect competition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting interdependence in oligopoly"}
The key feature of oligopoly is that each firm must consider its rivals' reactions before it changes its price or output.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, low barriers, some control over price (elastic demand), non-price competition, normal profit in the long run.
- Advantages: choice, variety, innovation, fairly low prices. Disadvantages: higher prices than perfect competition, advertising costs, excess capacity.
- Oligopoly: a few large firms, interdependence, high barriers, non-price competition, price rigidity and possible collusion.
- The concentration ratio measures the market share of the largest firms.
:::
