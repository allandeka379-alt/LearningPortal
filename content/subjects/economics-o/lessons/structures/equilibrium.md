## Introduction

Why do vegetable sellers at a busy market earn only a modest living year after year, while a sole supplier of a vital product can earn high profits for decades? The answer lies in how price and output are decided in different **market structures**, and in what happens over time as firms enter or leave.

A firm is in **equilibrium** when it has no reason to change its output: it is maximising its profit (or minimising its loss) where **marginal revenue equals marginal cost**. The type of profit it earns then depends on **average revenue compared with average total cost**. This lesson brings together marginal analysis and market structures. It is examined by ZIMSEC (Form 4): you must describe short-run and long-run equilibrium in each structure, identify the profit-maximising output and compare the structures. Tables are used here in place of diagrams; when you describe a diagram, use the words given below.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Equilibrium of the firm"}
The output at which a firm maximises profit (or minimises loss), so it has no incentive to change output: where MR = MC.
:::

:::definition{term="Short run (market structures)"}
A period in which the number of firms in the industry is fixed and each firm has at least one fixed factor. Firms may make abnormal profits or losses.
:::

:::definition{term="Long run (market structures)"}
A period long enough for firms to enter or leave the industry and to change all their factors. Where entry is free, only normal profit is earned.
:::

## The two steps

1. **Find the output:** produce where MR = MC (in a table, every unit with MR > MC, none with MR < MC).
2. **Find the type of profit at that output:** compare AR (price) with ATC.
   - $AR > ATC$: abnormal profit $= (AR - ATC) \times Q$.
   - $AR = ATC$: normal profit.
   - $AR < ATC$: loss. In the short run the firm still produces if AR covers average variable cost.

## Perfect competition

The firm is a price taker, so **P = AR = MR** at every output.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a firm in perfect competition]
A fictional maker of clay bricks sells in a perfectly competitive market. Its daily costs are:

| Output (batches) | TC (US\$) | MC (US\$) | ATC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 30 | – | – |
| 1 | 50 | 20 | 50.00 |
| 2 | 64 | 14 | 32.00 |
| 3 | 75 | 11 | 25.00 |
| 4 | 86 | 11 | 21.50 |
| 5 | 100 | 14 | 20.00 |
| 6 | 126 | 26 | 21.00 |

**Short run, market price US\$24.**

1. MR = US\$24 for every batch. MC is below 24 up to the 5th batch (14) and above it for the 6th (26), so output = 5 batches.
2. AR = 24 and ATC = 20, so **abnormal profit** $= (24 - 20) \times 5 = 20$, or US\$20 a day.

**Long run.** The abnormal profit attracts new firms. Market supply rises and the market price falls. Entry stops when the price has fallen to the lowest point of ATC, **US\$20**.

3. At US\$20, output is still 5 batches (the 6th batch costs 26 > 20).
4. AR = ATC = 20: **normal profit** only, at the lowest ATC (productive efficiency).

**Short-run loss.** If the price fell to US\$18, the best output would still be 5 batches: TR $= 90$, TC $= 100$, a loss of US\$10. Closing would lose the fixed cost of US\$30, so the firm keeps producing in the short run (AR US\$18 is above average variable cost $(100 - 30) \div 5 = 14$). In the long run some firms would leave, supply would fall and the price would rise back to US\$20.
:::

**Describing the diagrams:** with price and costs on the vertical axis and output on the horizontal axis, the firm's AR = MR line is horizontal at the market price; the MC curve cuts it from below at the profit-maximising output. In the short run, if the price line is above the ATC curve at that output, the rectangle between them shows abnormal profit. In the long run the price line just touches the bottom of the U-shaped ATC curve, where MC also cuts ATC: P = MC = AR = MR = minimum ATC.

## Monopolistic competition

Each firm sells a differentiated product, so it faces a **downward-sloping** AR (demand) curve, with MR below it. It produces where MR = MC and sets the price from its AR curve.

- **Short run:** if AR > ATC at that output, the firm earns abnormal profit (for example, a new, fashionable restaurant).
- **Long run:** because entry is easy, new firms copy the idea and take some of its customers. Each firm's demand curve shifts to the left until **AR = ATC** at the output where MR = MC: **normal profit**. Described as a diagram, the AR curve just touches the ATC curve on its falling part.
- Because this happens to the left of the lowest point of ATC, the firm has **excess capacity** and is not productively efficient; and since price is above MC, it is not allocatively efficient either.

## Monopoly

The monopoly faces the downward-sloping market demand curve (AR), with MR below it.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a monopoly]
A fictional firm is the only supplier of a product. Its daily figures are:

| Output (units) | Price = AR (US\$) | TR (US\$) | TC (US\$) | MR (US\$) | MC (US\$) | Profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 50 | 0 | 40 | – | – | −40 |
| 1 | 46 | 46 | 60 | 46 | 20 | −14 |
| 2 | 42 | 84 | 74 | 38 | 14 | 10 |
| 3 | 38 | 114 | 86 | 30 | 12 | 28 |
| 4 | 34 | 136 | 100 | 22 | 14 | 36 |
| 5 | 30 | 150 | 118 | 14 | 18 | 32 |
| 6 | 26 | 156 | 142 | 6 | 24 | 14 |

1. MR > MC up to the 4th unit (22 > 14); for the 5th, MR = 14 < MC = 18. So output = **4 units**.
2. Price is read from the AR column: **US\$34**.
3. ATC $= 100 \div 4 = 25$; AR > ATC, so **abnormal profit** $= (34 - 25) \times 4 = 36$, or US\$36, the highest figure in the profit column.
4. **Long run:** barriers to entry stop new firms from coming in, so the abnormal profit can continue.
5. Efficiency: the price (US\$34) is far above MC (US\$14), so the monopoly is allocatively inefficient. ATC at 4 units (US\$25) is above the lowest ATC in the table ($118 \div 5 = 23.60$ at 5 units), so it is also productively inefficient.
:::

**Describing the diagram:** the AR curve slopes down, MR lies below it, and MC cuts MR from below at the profit-maximising output. The price is found by going up from that output to the AR curve. The rectangle between price and ATC at that output is the abnormal profit, which remains in the long run.

## Comparing the market structures

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Monopoly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Number of firms | Very many | Many | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | No close substitutes |
| Barriers to entry | None | Low | High |
| Firm's demand (AR) curve | Horizontal (price taker) | Downward-sloping, elastic | Downward-sloping (market demand) |
| Short-run profit | Abnormal, normal or loss | Abnormal, normal or loss | Usually abnormal |
| Long-run profit | Normal | Normal | Abnormal can persist |
| Price compared with MC | P = MC | P > MC | P > MC |
| Productive efficiency in the long run | Yes (lowest ATC) | No (excess capacity) | Usually no |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reading the price from the MC column"}
Output is where MR = MC, but the **price** comes from the AR (demand) column at that output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all firms earn normal profit in the long run"}
Only where entry is free (perfect and monopolistic competition). Barriers allow a monopoly to keep abnormal profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the role of entry and exit"}
Long-run equilibrium is reached because firms enter when there is abnormal profit and leave when there are losses. Explain this movement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** the profit-maximising output from a table by comparing MR with MC row by row; check with TR − TC.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe** short-run and long-run equilibrium by stating the output rule, the profit type (AR against ATC) and what entry or exit does.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Compare** structures with a table of features and outcomes, then **discuss** which is best for consumers, weighing price and efficiency against variety, economies of scale and research.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Every firm maximises profit where MR = MC; AR against ATC shows abnormal profit, normal profit or a loss.
- Perfect competition: P = AR = MR; short-run abnormal profit or loss; long-run normal profit at the lowest ATC, with P = MC.
- Monopolistic competition: short-run abnormal profit; long-run normal profit with AR = ATC, but with excess capacity and P > MC.
- Monopoly: abnormal profit can last in the long run because of barriers; P > MC and usually not at the lowest ATC.
:::
