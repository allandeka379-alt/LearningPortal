## Introduction

Every morning, thousands of crates of tomatoes, onions and leafy vegetables arrive at the big produce markets in Harare and Bulawayo. Nobody plans how many crates each farmer should bring or how many each vendor should buy. Yet, most days, the vegetables are sold and households find what they need. What co-ordinates all these decisions? The answer is **prices**.

This lesson explains what a market is and how prices allocate scarce resources: the **price mechanism**. It links the basic economic problem to the demand and supply topics that follow. Both boards examine it in multiple-choice questions and in *explain* questions such as "Explain how the price mechanism allocates resources". You should already know the three basic economic questions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market"}
Any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together to exchange goods, services or factors of production. It does not have to be a physical place.
:::

:::definition{term="Price mechanism"}
The system in which the interaction of buyers (demand) and sellers (supply) sets prices, and changes in prices allocate resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Market equilibrium"}
The situation in which the quantity buyers want to buy equals the quantity sellers want to sell at the current price, so there is no pressure for the price to change.
:::

:::definition{term="Allocation of resources"}
The way scarce resources (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are shared out between different uses.
:::

## What is a market?

A market exists wherever buyers and sellers can agree a price and exchange.

- **Physical markets:** a produce market, a cattle sale, a bus terminus where vendors sell snacks.
- **Markets without a single place:** buying airtime by mobile money, selling a car through an online advert, the market for foreign currency between banks.
- **Informal markets:** unregistered vendors and cross-border traders selling vegetables, clothes or electronics. These are real markets: prices are set by demand and supply just as in formal shops.
- **Factor markets:** the labour market (workers sell their labour to employers), the market for land (rent), and the market for loans (interest).

### Roles of buyers and sellers

**Buyers** (consumers, or firms buying inputs) decide what to buy and how much, given prices and their incomes. Their willingness and ability to pay is **demand**. They want to pay as little as possible.

**Sellers** (producers and traders) decide what to offer for sale and how much, given prices and their costs. Their willingness and ability to sell is **supply**. They want to receive as high a price as possible.

The price at which they trade is where these two sides meet. When buyers want more than sellers offer, competition between buyers pushes the price up; when sellers offer more than buyers want, competition between sellers pushes it down.

## The three functions of prices

Prices do three jobs at the same time.

1. **Signalling.** A change in price gives information. A rising price signals that a good is becoming scarcer or more wanted; a falling price signals a surplus.
2. **Incentive.** A higher price makes it more profitable to supply a good, so producers have an incentive to move resources into making it; a lower price encourages them to move resources out. For buyers, a higher price is an incentive to buy less.
3. **Rationing.** When a good is scarce, a higher price limits demand to the quantity available. Only those who are willing and able to pay the higher price get the good.

:::worked-solution{title="A rise in demand for tomatoes"}
The table shows a fictional daily market for tomatoes before and after a rise in demand (for example, schools reopening and buying for their kitchens).

| Price per crate (US\$) | Quantity demanded before (crates per day) | Quantity demanded after (crates per day) | Quantity supplied (crates per day) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 900 | 1,100 | 500 |
| 5 | 800 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 6 | 700 | 900 | 700 |
| 7 | 600 | 800 | 800 |
| 8 | 500 | 700 | 900 |

1. Before the change, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied at US\$6: the market clears with 700 crates a day.
2. After demand rises, at US\$6 buyers want 900 crates but sellers offer 700: a **shortage** of $900 - 700 = 200$ crates.
3. **Signalling:** vendors run out early, buyers compete for crates and the price starts to rise.
4. **Incentive:** at US\$7, farmers find it worth sending more tomatoes: quantity supplied rises from 700 to 800 crates.
5. **Rationing:** at US\$7, quantity demanded falls from 900 to 800 crates; some buyers decide not to buy at the higher price.
6. At US\$7, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (800 crates): the new market-clearing price.

**Describing the diagram.** Price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. A downward-sloping demand curve D1 and an upward-sloping supply curve S cross at US\$6 and 700 crates. Demand shifts to the right to D2; the new intersection is at US\$7 and 800 crates. (Shifts and equilibrium are studied in detail in the demand and supply lessons.)
:::

## How the price mechanism answers the three questions

### What to produce?

Consumers "vote" with their money. Goods that many people want to buy have rising prices and profits, so firms produce more of them. Goods that few want have falling prices and losses, so firms produce less and resources move elsewhere.

### How to produce?

Firms choose the method with the lowest cost, and costs depend on the prices of the factors of production.

:::worked-solution{title="Choosing a method from factor prices"}
A small firm can pack a day's output either by hand (method A: 10 workers) or with a hired packing machine (method B: 3 workers plus machine hire of US\$45 a day). Both methods pack the same amount.

When the daily wage is US\$8:

| Method | Workers | Wage cost (US\$) | Machine hire (US\$) | Total cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A (by hand) | 10 | 80 | 0 | 80 |
| B (with a machine) | 3 | 24 | 45 | 69 |

1. Method A: $10 \times 8 = 80$. Method B: $3 \times 8 + 45 = 69$.
2. Method B is cheaper, so the firm uses the machine (capital-intensive).
3. If the daily wage falls to US\$5: method A costs $10 \times 5 = 50$; method B costs $3 \times 5 + 45 = 60$.
4. Now method A is cheaper, so the firm switches to packing by hand (labour-intensive).

The relative prices of labour and capital have decided *how* to produce.
:::

### For whom to produce?

Goods go to those who are willing and able to pay the market price. People's ability to pay depends on their incomes, which come from selling their factors of production: wages for labour, rent for land, interest for capital and profit for enterprise. Those with higher incomes can buy more.

## Strengths and limits

The price mechanism co-ordinates millions of decisions without anyone being in charge, and responds quickly when conditions change. But it allocates by ability to pay, not need; it does not provide public goods; and it ignores costs imposed on third parties, such as pollution. These limits are why governments intervene in markets, a topic covered later.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a market must be a place"}
A market is any arrangement for buyers and sellers to exchange. Mobile money and online sales are markets too.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the functions"}
Signalling is the information a price change gives; incentive is the reason it gives people to change behaviour; rationing is how a higher price limits who gets a scarce good. Name the function and explain it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the price mechanism shares goods fairly"}
It shares goods by ability to pay. Whether that is fair is a normative question.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the chain"}
For *explain how the price mechanism allocates resources*, write the chain: change in demand or supply → shortage or surplus → price changes (signal) → producers and consumers respond (incentive, rationing) → resources move to or from the good.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use numbers when given"}
If a schedule is given, quote the shortage or surplus and the new price and quantity in your explanation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A market is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers together; informal and online markets count.
- Buyers create demand and sellers create supply; their interaction sets the price.
- Prices signal changes, give incentives to producers and consumers, and ration scarce goods.
- The price mechanism answers what (by consumer spending), how (by factor prices and costs) and for whom (by ability to pay).
- It allocates by ability to pay, not need, and does not provide public goods.
:::
