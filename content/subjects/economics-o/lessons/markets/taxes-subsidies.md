## Introduction

When you buy airtime, a cold drink or a litre of fuel in Zimbabwe, part of the price goes to the government. ZIMRA collects value added tax (VAT) on most goods and services, and extra duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco. At the same time, governments sometimes **pay** producers to lower prices, for example by subsidising fertiliser and seed for farmers or reducing the cost of bus fares.

These are two of the most common ways a government changes what a market produces. Taxes make harmful goods dearer so people consume less of them; subsidies make useful goods cheaper so people consume more. In the exam you must show how each one shifts the supply curve (described in words and with a schedule), work out the new price, the tax revenue or subsidy cost, and judge whether the policy corrects the market failure it targets. You should already know how a shift in supply changes equilibrium.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indirect tax"}
A tax on spending on goods and services, collected from the seller, who passes some or all of it on to consumers in a higher price. Examples: VAT, excise duty on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
:::

:::definition{term="Specific tax"}
An indirect tax of a fixed amount per unit sold, for example US\$0.50 per bottle, whatever the price.
:::

:::definition{term="Ad valorem tax"}
An indirect tax charged as a percentage of the price, for example VAT. The tax per unit is larger on more expensive items.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidy"}
A payment by the government to producers (or sometimes consumers) to lower the cost of production and the price of a good, and to increase the quantity produced and consumed.
:::

:::definition{term="Tax incidence (burden)"}
How the tax is shared between consumers (the rise in the price they pay) and producers (the fall in the price they keep).
:::

## How an indirect tax affects the market

A tax per unit is an extra cost to sellers. At every price they now keep less, so they supply less: the **supply curve shifts to the left** (upwards by the amount of the tax). Demand does not change.

**Describing the diagram.** Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis. Demand D slopes down; the original supply S1 slopes up; they cross at P1/Q1. The tax shifts supply to S2 = S1 + tax, which lies **above** S1 by the tax per unit. For a specific tax, S2 is **parallel** to S1; for an ad valorem tax, the gap widens as price rises, so S2 is **steeper** and pivots away from S1. The new equilibrium is where D meets S2: a **higher price P2** paid by consumers and a **lower quantity Q2**. The price producers keep is P2 minus the tax. Tax revenue is a rectangle: the tax per unit (vertical gap between S2 and S1) times Q2.

![A tax of US\$0.50 a bottle shifts supply left from S1 to S2: the price paid by consumers rises from US\$2.00 to US\$2.30 and quantity falls from 1,400 to 1,160 bottles; producers keep US\$1.80 and the tax revenue rectangle is 0.50 x 1,160 = US\$580 a day.](media:diagrams/specific-tax-sugary-drink.png "Figure: a specific tax shifts supply up by the tax; price rises and quantity falls")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a specific tax on a sugary drink]
A fictional government puts a tax of US\$0.50 on each 2-litre bottle of a sugary drink. The daily market in one city:

| Price paid by consumers (US\$) | Quantity demanded (bottles per day) | Quantity supplied before tax | Quantity supplied after tax |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.80 | 1,560 | 1,160 | 560 |
| 1.90 | 1,480 | 1,280 | 680 |
| 2.00 | 1,400 | 1,400 | 800 |
| 2.10 | 1,320 | 1,520 | 920 |
| 2.20 | 1,240 | 1,640 | 1,040 |
| 2.30 | 1,160 | 1,760 | 1,160 |
| 2.40 | 1,080 | 1,880 | 1,280 |

1. Before the tax: equilibrium at US\$2.00 and 1,400 bottles a day.
2. After the tax, sellers supply at each price what they used to supply at a price US\$0.50 lower (for example, at US\$2.30 they keep US\$1.80 and supply 1,160). The new equilibrium is US\$2.30 and 1,160 bottles.
3. Consumers pay $2.30 - 2.00 = 0.30$ more: US\$0.30 of the US\$0.50 tax (60%).
4. Producers keep $2.30 - 0.50 = 1.80$, so they bear US\$0.20 (40%).
5. Tax revenue = $0.50 \times 1{,}160 = 580$, so US\$580 a day.
6. Consumption falls by $1{,}400 - 1{,}160 = 240$ bottles a day, which is the aim of the tax.
:::

### Who pays the tax?

The burden depends on the **price elasticity of demand**. When demand is **inelastic** (cigarettes, fuel), consumers keep buying, so sellers can pass on most of the tax: the price rises a lot, quantity falls little, and revenue is high. When demand is **elastic**, sellers must absorb more of the tax themselves, and the quantity falls a lot. So a tax raises the most revenue on inelastic goods, but cuts consumption most on elastic goods.

## How a subsidy affects the market

A subsidy per unit lowers producers' costs, so they supply more at every price: the **supply curve shifts to the right** (downwards by the subsidy per unit). The new equilibrium has a **lower price** and a **higher quantity**. Producers receive the market price **plus** the subsidy. The cost to the government is the subsidy per unit times the new quantity.

![A subsidy of US\$10 a bag shifts the supply of fertiliser right from S1 to S2: the price farmers pay falls from US\$30 to US\$24 and the quantity rises from 2,800 to 3,280 bags a month; the cost to the government is 10 x 3,280 = US\$32,800.](media:diagrams/fertiliser-subsidy.png "Figure: a subsidy shifts supply right; price falls and quantity rises")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a subsidy on fertiliser]
A fictional government pays suppliers US\$10 for every bag of fertiliser sold to farmers.

| Price paid by farmers (US\$) | Quantity demanded (bags per month) | Quantity supplied before subsidy | Quantity supplied after subsidy |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 22 | 3,440 | 1,840 | 3,040 |
| 24 | 3,280 | 2,080 | 3,280 |
| 26 | 3,120 | 2,320 | 3,520 |
| 28 | 2,960 | 2,560 | 3,760 |
| 30 | 2,800 | 2,800 | 4,000 |
| 32 | 2,640 | 3,040 | 4,240 |

1. Before: equilibrium at US\$30 and 2,800 bags.
2. After: at each price, suppliers offer what they used to offer at a price US\$10 higher. New equilibrium: US\$24 and 3,280 bags.
3. Farmers pay US\$6 less; suppliers receive $24 + 10 = 34$ dollars a bag, US\$4 more than before.
4. Cost to the government = $10 \times 3{,}280 = 32{,}800$, that is US\$32,800 a month.
5. Fertiliser use rises by $3{,}280 - 2{,}800 = 480$ bags a month.
:::

## Taxes and subsidies as cures for market failure

**Indirect taxes** are used on **demerit goods** and goods with **external costs** (tobacco, alcohol, sugary drinks, polluting fuels).

- Advantages: they raise the price and reduce consumption towards the socially desirable level; they raise revenue that can be spent on, for example, health care; the market still works, so consumers keep their choice.
- Disadvantages: with inelastic demand, consumption falls little; they are **regressive** (take a larger share of poor people's incomes); they may cause smuggling and illegal sales; it is hard to know the right size of tax; they raise firms' costs and may cost jobs.

**Subsidies** are used on **merit goods** and goods with **external benefits** (public transport, vaccinations, fertiliser, school books, solar power).

- Advantages: lower prices raise consumption; they help poorer households afford essentials; they can support infant industries and jobs.
- Disadvantages: an **opportunity cost**, as the money could be spent elsewhere or require higher taxes; firms may become inefficient and dependent; subsidies are hard to remove later; some of the subsidy may be kept by producers rather than passed on; subsidised goods may be resold or smuggled.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting demand instead of supply"}
An indirect tax or a subsidy on producers changes their costs, so the **supply** curve shifts. Demand stays where it is; the quantity demanded changes as the price changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the price rises by the full tax"}
The price rises by the full tax only if demand is perfectly inelastic. Normally consumers pay part (the rise in price) and producers part (the fall in the price they keep).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the old quantity for tax revenue"}
Tax revenue = tax per unit × the **new** (after-tax) quantity. Using the old equilibrium quantity overstates revenue.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** set out the price change, the burden on each side and the revenue (tax × new quantity) or the subsidy cost (subsidy × new quantity), with units.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain with a described diagram:** name the axes, the original curves and equilibrium, the shift (left for a tax, right for a subsidy, by the amount per unit), and the new price and quantity.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** link the policy to the market failure (demerit good, external cost, merit good), then weigh effectiveness against PED, cost, fairness and side effects, and conclude.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use **indirect tax**, **specific tax**, **ad valorem tax** and **subsidy**. ZIMSEC often uses VAT and excise duty as examples; Cambridge asks you to draw and interpret the tax and subsidy diagrams, which you can describe in words.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An indirect tax raises sellers' costs: supply shifts left (up by the tax), price rises and quantity falls.
- A specific tax gives a parallel shift; an ad valorem tax gives a steeper, pivoting shift.
- The burden is shared; consumers pay more of it when demand is inelastic.
- A subsidy lowers costs: supply shifts right (down by the subsidy), price falls and quantity rises; the cost is subsidy × new quantity.
- Taxes discourage demerit goods and external costs; subsidies encourage merit goods and external benefits, each with costs and side effects.
:::
