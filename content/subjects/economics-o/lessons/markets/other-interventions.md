## Introduction

Taxes, subsidies and price controls work by changing prices. But governments have other tools. A mine may be told by law how it must dispose of its waste. A fishing company may be allowed to catch only a fixed tonnage of kapenta each year. The state may run schools and clinics itself, or it may take a firm into public ownership, or sell one it owns to private investors.

In Zimbabwe, as in most mixed economies, all of these are visible: government agencies such as the Environmental Management Agency (EMA) enforce environmental rules, the state provides public schools and hospitals, and several large public corporations (parastatals) supply services such as electricity and rail transport. Whether such firms should stay in public hands or be privatised is a recurring debate.

Both syllabuses expect you to **define** each intervention, explain **how** it corrects a market failure, and give its **advantages and disadvantages**. Cambridge also lists **quotas**, for example on extracting natural resources. You should already know what market failure, public goods, merit goods and external costs are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regulation"}
Rules and laws set by the government, enforced by penalties, that control what firms and consumers may do: for example limits on pollution, safety standards, age limits on buying alcohol, or a ban on a harmful product.
:::

:::definition{term="Quota"}
A legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be produced, extracted, caught or imported in a period, for example a maximum tonnage of fish caught per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct provision"}
The government itself supplies a good or service, usually free or below cost at the point of use and paid for from taxation, for example street lighting, defence, state schools and public hospitals.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalisation"}
The transfer of a firm or industry from private ownership to government (public) ownership.
:::

:::definition{term="Privatisation"}
The transfer of a firm or industry from public (government) ownership to private ownership, for example by selling its shares to investors.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **public corporations (parastatals)**; Cambridge speaks of **public sector firms**. Both mean firms owned and controlled by the state. Cambridge includes **quotas** in this topic; ZIMSEC students should know them too, as a form of regulation.
:::

## Regulation

Regulation tackles market failure **directly**. If a factory's smoke harms local people (an external cost), the government can set a maximum level of emissions and fine firms that exceed it. If a good is harmful (a demerit good), it can be banned or its sale restricted, for example no alcohol sales to under-18s. Rules can also force consumption of merit goods: compulsory vaccination before school entry, or compulsory seat belts.

- **Advantages:** simple to understand; can act quickly, especially when a product is very dangerous; the effect does not depend on how consumers respond to prices; penalties raise revenue.
- **Disadvantages:** inspection and enforcement are costly; firms may break the rules if fines are small or the chance of being caught is low; rules are the same for every firm, so they do not reward firms that cut pollution cheaply; too many rules raise costs and discourage enterprise; a ban may create an illegal market.

## Quotas

A quota limits the **quantity**. Governments use quotas to stop natural resources being over-used: fish in a lake, timber in a forest, or water drawn from a river. A free market would over-exploit such resources, because each firm ignores the effect of its own catch on the stock left for others and for the future.

:::example[Example 1: a kapenta quota on a fictional lake]
Fishing companies on a fictional lake caught more kapenta each year, and the catch per boat began to fall as the stock shrank. The government sets an annual quota for the whole lake and divides it among licensed companies. Catches fall at first and kapenta may become dearer, but the stock has time to recover, so fishing can continue for many years. The quota works only if it is enforced: officials must check landings and punish boats that fish illegally. If companies can trade their quota shares, the most efficient firms can buy more of the quota, so the total catch is caught at the lowest cost.
:::

- **Advantages:** directly limits the quantity, so it protects scarce resources; easy to set at a sustainable level if the stock is known.
- **Disadvantages:** hard to know the right level; needs monitoring; may raise prices for consumers; may lead to illegal extraction; existing firms may gain at the expense of new ones.

## Direct provision

Public goods such as street lighting, national defence and flood barriers are **not provided** by the market, because people cannot be excluded and so can "free ride". Merit goods such as education and health care are **under-consumed** if left to the market. Governments therefore provide them directly, paid for by taxation.

- **Advantages:** everyone can use the service regardless of income; ensures public goods exist; raises consumption of merit goods with their external benefits (a healthier, better-educated workforce).
- **Disadvantages:** large cost to the government and taxpayers (an opportunity cost); free services may be over-used, causing long queues; without competition or a profit motive, providers may be inefficient; the government may not know what quantity people want.

## Nationalisation

A government may nationalise an industry to control a **natural monopoly** (such as an electricity grid or railway, where one network is cheapest), to protect **strategic** industries, to save jobs in a failing firm, or to make sure essential services reach poor or remote areas.

- **Advantages:** prices can be set in the public interest rather than for maximum profit; services can be provided where they are needed even if unprofitable; profits go to the government; long-term planning and investment in key infrastructure.
- **Disadvantages:** lack of competition and of the profit motive can lead to inefficiency and poor service; losses must be covered by taxpayers; managers may be influenced by political aims; buying firms from private owners is costly.

## Privatisation

Privatisation moves state-owned firms into private hands, usually by selling shares to investors.

- **Advantages:** the profit motive and competition encourage efficiency and innovation; the sale raises money for the government; the government no longer has to cover losses; private owners can raise new capital for investment.
- **Disadvantages:** a public monopoly may become a private monopoly that raises prices; unprofitable but socially useful services (rural routes) may be cut; jobs may be lost as the new owners cut costs; the government loses future profits; strategic assets may pass to foreign owners.

:::example[Example 2: should a state-owned water company be privatised?]
A fictional city's water company is publicly owned. It loses money, many pipes leak, and some suburbs receive water only twice a week. Supporters of privatisation say private owners would invest to cut leaks and run the company more efficiently. Opponents point out that water is a natural monopoly: there is only one network of pipes, so a private owner would face no competition and could raise prices, and poorer households might cut back on water, harming health. A common compromise is to privatise but **regulate**: a regulator sets maximum price rises and minimum service standards. This shows that interventions are often combined.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing nationalisation and privatisation"}
Nationalisation moves firms **into** state ownership; privatisation moves them **out** of it. Learn one and reverse it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages without linking to market failure"}
Say which failure the intervention corrects: regulation of pollution (external costs), direct provision (public and merit goods), quotas (over-use of natural resources), nationalisation (natural monopoly).
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the intervention always works"}
Every intervention can fail: rules may not be enforced, providers may be inefficient, and privatised monopolies may exploit consumers. This is called **government failure**, and it is what a "discuss" answer must weigh.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** give the action and its purpose: "Privatisation is the transfer of a firm from public to private ownership, usually by selling its shares."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** use a chain: market failure → intervention → change in behaviour → outcome (for example "a quota limits the catch, so the fish stock can recover").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss / evaluate:** give at least two advantages and two disadvantages, consider the context (natural monopoly? essential service?), and conclude, often that a mix of interventions works best.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Regulation sets rules backed by penalties; it acts directly but is costly to enforce.
- Quotas limit quantities, protecting natural resources, but need monitoring and accurate information.
- Direct provision supplies public and merit goods to all, at a cost to taxpayers and with a risk of inefficiency.
- Nationalisation moves firms into state ownership; privatisation moves them into private ownership.
- Each tool has advantages and disadvantages; interventions are often combined (privatise and regulate).
:::
