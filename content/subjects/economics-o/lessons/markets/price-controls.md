## Introduction

When the price of a basic food such as maize meal or bread rises quickly, families struggle and there is pressure on the government to "do something". One option is to make it illegal to sell above a set price. When farmers complain that crop prices are too low to cover their costs, the opposite option is to guarantee a price that will not fall below a set level.

Both are **price controls**: the government overrides the price mechanism and fixes a price that is not the equilibrium price. Zimbabwe has experience of this. During the hyperinflation of 2007-2008, controlled prices on many basic goods were set below what it cost to supply them; shop shelves emptied and goods were sold instead on parallel (black) markets at much higher prices.

Price controls are examined in both syllabuses. You must be able to define them, show their effects with a schedule or a described diagram, calculate the shortage or surplus, and discuss whether they help the people they are meant to help. You should already know how equilibrium price is set and what shortages and surpluses are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Maximum price (price ceiling)"}
A legal upper limit on the price of a good or service. Sellers may not charge more than this price. It only has an effect when it is set **below** the equilibrium price.
:::

:::definition{term="Minimum price (price floor)"}
A legal lower limit on the price of a good or service. The price may not fall below this level. It only has an effect when it is set **above** the equilibrium price.
:::

:::definition{term="Black market (parallel market)"}
Illegal trade in a good at a price above the legal maximum price, which grows when a maximum price causes a shortage.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC usually says **price ceiling** and **price floor**; Cambridge 0455 says **maximum price** and **minimum price**. They mean the same thing, and you can write "a maximum price (price ceiling)" to be safe. Minimum wages are a minimum price in the labour market and are studied with wage determination.
:::

## Maximum prices

A government sets a maximum price to make an essential good affordable, usually for poor households: basic foods, rents, fuel or medicines. If the ceiling is set above the equilibrium price it changes nothing, because the market price is already lower. It matters only when it is set **below** equilibrium.

At a price below equilibrium, quantity demanded **extends** (more people can afford the good) and quantity supplied **contracts** (selling is less profitable). The result is a **shortage**, which the price is not allowed to remove.

**Describing the diagram.** Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis. A downward-sloping demand curve and an upward-sloping supply curve cross at the equilibrium P1/Q1. Draw a horizontal line labelled "maximum price" **below** P1. Where it meets the supply curve gives the quantity supplied (Qs); where it meets the demand curve, further right, gives the quantity demanded (Qd). The gap Qs to Qd is the shortage. Only Qs is actually bought and sold.

![Maize meal market with a maximum price of US\$7, below the equilibrium of US\$9 and 1,100 bags: at US\$7 only 900 bags are supplied but 1,300 are demanded, a shortage of 400 bags a week.](media:diagrams/maximum-price-maize-meal.png "Figure: a maximum price below equilibrium creates a shortage")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a maximum price on maize meal]
A fictional town's weekly market for 10 kg bags of maize meal:

| Price per 10 kg bag (US\$) | Quantity demanded (bags per week) | Quantity supplied (bags per week) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 6 | 1,400 | 800 |
| 7 | 1,300 | 900 |
| 8 | 1,200 | 1,000 |
| 9 | 1,100 | 1,100 |
| 10 | 1,000 | 1,200 |
| 11 | 900 | 1,300 |

1. Equilibrium: at US\$9, 1,100 bags are demanded and 1,100 supplied.
2. The council sets a maximum price of US\$7. Quantity demanded extends to 1,300 bags; quantity supplied contracts to 900 bags.
3. Shortage = $1{,}300 - 900 = 400$ bags a week.
4. Only 900 bags are sold, 200 **fewer** than before the control. Spending on maize meal falls from $9 \times 1{,}100 = 9{,}900$ to $7 \times 900 = 6{,}300$, that is from US\$9,900 to US\$6,300 a week.
5. A maximum price of US\$10 would have no effect, because it is above the equilibrium price of US\$9.
:::

### Problems caused by a maximum price

- **Shortages and queues.** Goods run out; people waste hours queuing, and those who arrive first, not those in greatest need, are served.
- **Black markets.** Some buyers are willing to pay far more than the legal price, so goods are sold illegally at high prices, often higher than the old equilibrium.
- **Allocation by other means.** Sellers may favour friends ("under the counter" sales) or the government may have to ration the good with coupons.
- **Lower supply over time.** Producers earn less, cut output, or switch to uncontrolled products; quality may fall.

### Advantages of a maximum price

- Those who do obtain the good pay less, so it is more affordable for poor households.
- It can stop sellers with market power from charging very high prices, for example in an emergency.
- It may reduce inflation in essential goods for a short time.

To avoid the shortage, a government can combine the ceiling with measures that increase supply: subsidies to producers, releasing stocks from a strategic reserve, or importing the good.

## Minimum prices

A government sets a minimum price to protect producers' incomes, for example a guaranteed producer price for a crop, or to discourage consumption of a harmful good. It matters only when set **above** the equilibrium price.

Above equilibrium, quantity supplied **extends** and quantity demanded **contracts**, giving a **surplus**. To keep the price at the floor, the government (or a marketing board) usually has to buy up the surplus and store it, destroy it or sell it abroad.

**Describing the diagram.** The same axes and curves, with a horizontal line labelled "minimum price" **above** the equilibrium P1. It meets the demand curve at Qd (to the left) and the supply curve at Qs (to the right); the gap Qd to Qs is the surplus.

![Wheat market with a minimum price of US\$350 a tonne, above the equilibrium of US\$320 and 640 tonnes: at US\$350 farmers supply 700 tonnes but only 550 tonnes are demanded, a surplus of 150 tonnes a month.](media:diagrams/minimum-price-wheat.png "Figure: a minimum price above equilibrium creates a surplus")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a guaranteed minimum price for wheat]
A fictional country's monthly wheat market:

| Price per tonne (US\$) | Quantity demanded (tonnes per month) | Quantity supplied (tonnes per month) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 250 | 850 | 500 |
| 280 | 760 | 560 |
| 300 | 700 | 600 |
| 320 | 640 | 640 |
| 350 | 550 | 700 |

1. Equilibrium: US\$320 per tonne and 640 tonnes.
2. The government guarantees farmers US\$350 a tonne. Quantity supplied extends to 700 tonnes; quantity demanded contracts to 550 tonnes.
3. Surplus = $700 - 550 = 150$ tonnes a month.
4. If the government buys the surplus at the minimum price, it spends $350 \times 150 = 52{,}500$, that is US\$52,500 a month, plus storage costs.
5. Farmers sell all 700 tonnes at US\$350, so their revenue rises from $320 \times 640 = 204{,}800$ to $350 \times 700 = 245{,}000$ dollars a month.
:::

### Advantages and disadvantages of a minimum price

- **For producers:** a stable, higher income encourages farmers to stay in farming and invest, and protects food security.
- **For consumers:** they pay more and buy less.
- **For the government:** buying, storing and disposing of the surplus is costly (an opportunity cost: the money could fund schools or clinics), and stored crops can rot.
- **Efficiency:** resources are kept in producing more than consumers want at that price, and inefficient producers are protected.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the ceiling above equilibrium"}
A maximum price only affects the market when it is **below** the equilibrium price; a minimum price only when it is **above** it. A ceiling drawn above equilibrium has no effect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the curves shift"}
A price control does not shift demand or supply. The fixed price causes movements **along** both curves: an extension of one and a contraction of the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying more is sold under a maximum price"}
Demand rises but supply falls, and you cannot buy what is not supplied. The quantity actually traded is the **smaller** of the two, the quantity supplied.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** include both the legal limit and its position: "A maximum price is a legal limit on the price that can be charged, set below the equilibrium price."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show the subtraction and name the result ("shortage of 400 bags a week"). If asked how many are sold under a ceiling, give the quantity supplied.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / analyse:** follow the chain: price fixed below equilibrium → demand extends, supply contracts → shortage → queues, black market, rationing. Describe the diagram with axes, curves, the control line and the gap.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** weigh affordability for those who get the good against shortages, black markets and falling supply, then conclude: the control works best if it is temporary or combined with measures to raise supply.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A maximum price (price ceiling) is a legal upper limit, effective only below equilibrium; it causes a shortage.
- A minimum price (price floor) is a legal lower limit, effective only above equilibrium; it causes a surplus.
- Shortages lead to queues, black markets and rationing; surpluses must often be bought and stored by the government.
- Neither curve shifts: the fixed price causes movements along both curves.
- Price controls help some consumers or producers but at a cost to others and to the government; they work best alongside measures that change supply.
:::
