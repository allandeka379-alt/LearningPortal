## Introduction

Early in the morning at a fresh-produce market in Harare, traders and buyers haggle over the price of tomatoes. If a trader asks too much, crates stay unsold and she lowers her price. If she asks too little, buyers crowd around and she soon realises she could charge more. By mid-morning the price settles at a level where the crates brought to market are roughly the crates buyers want. That settling point is **market equilibrium**.

Equilibrium joins the two halves of the market you have already studied, demand and supply. It is examined in every paper: multiple-choice questions ask for the equilibrium from a schedule, data-response questions ask you to calculate shortages and surpluses, and essays ask you to explain how the price mechanism removes them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Equilibrium price"}
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, so the market clears and there is no pressure for the price to change.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium quantity"}
The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price.
:::

:::definition{term="Disequilibrium"}
A situation in which the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied are not equal at the current price.
:::

:::definition{term="Shortage (excess demand)"}
The amount by which quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at a price **below** equilibrium.
:::

:::definition{term="Surplus (excess supply)"}
The amount by which quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at a price **above** equilibrium.
:::

## Finding equilibrium from a schedule

Put the demand and supply schedules side by side and look for the row where the two quantities are equal. The table is for a fictional daily market for tomatoes.

| Price per crate (US\$) | Quantity demanded (crates per day) | Quantity supplied (crates per day) | Market situation |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 4 | 900 | 300 | Shortage of 600 |
| 6 | 750 | 450 | Shortage of 300 |
| 8 | 600 | 600 | Equilibrium |
| 10 | 450 | 750 | Surplus of 300 |
| 12 | 300 | 900 | Surplus of 600 |

At US\$8, 600 crates are demanded and 600 supplied: the **equilibrium price** is US\$8 and the **equilibrium quantity** is 600 crates a day.

## Describing the equilibrium diagram

- Price on the **vertical** axis, quantity (crates per day) on the **horizontal** axis.
- A downward-sloping demand curve **D** and an upward-sloping supply curve **S**.
- They cross at the **equilibrium point**: read across to the equilibrium price **Pe** (US\$8) and down to the equilibrium quantity **Qe** (600).
- At a price **above** Pe, draw a horizontal line: it meets D first (at a smaller quantity) and S further right. The gap between them is the **surplus**.
- At a price **below** Pe, the horizontal line meets S first and D further right. The gap is the **shortage**.

## How the market returns to equilibrium

**At a price above equilibrium (surplus).** Sellers cannot sell all they have brought, and stocks pile up (tomatoes start to go soft). To clear them, sellers cut their prices. As the price falls, quantity demanded **extends** and quantity supplied **contracts**, until they meet at the equilibrium.

**At a price below equilibrium (shortage).** Buyers cannot get all they want; queues form and goods sell out quickly. Some buyers offer more, and sellers see they can charge more. As the price rises, quantity supplied **extends** and quantity demanded **contracts**, until the shortage disappears.

These changes are **movements along** the demand and supply curves. Neither curve shifts: only the price is adjusting. This automatic adjustment is part of the **price mechanism**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: shortages and surpluses in the tomato market]
Using the tomato table above:

1. Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied = 600 crates at US\$8.
2. At US\$6: quantity demanded 750, quantity supplied 450. Shortage = $750 - 450 = 300$ crates a day.
3. At US\$12: quantity supplied 900, quantity demanded 300. Surplus = $900 - 300 = 600$ crates a day.
4. At US\$12 traders cannot sell 600 crates, so they cut prices; the price falls towards US\$8. At US\$6 buyers compete for the scarce crates, so the price rises towards US\$8.
5. Total spending by consumers at equilibrium = price × quantity = $8 \times 600 = 4{,}800$ dollars a day (US\$4,800), which is also the sellers' total revenue.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: exercise books before the school term]
A fictional stationery market has the schedules below.

| Price per exercise book (US\$) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0.50 | 5,000 | 2,600 |
| 0.60 | 4,400 | 3,200 |
| 0.70 | 3,800 | 3,800 |
| 0.80 | 3,200 | 4,400 |

1. Equilibrium: at US\$0.70, 3,800 books are demanded and 3,800 supplied.
2. Suppose sellers charge US\$0.60. Quantity demanded is 4,400 and quantity supplied 3,200, a shortage of $4{,}400 - 3{,}200 = 1{,}200$ books.
3. Parents who cannot find books offer more; sellers raise prices. Quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts until both equal 3,800 at US\$0.70.
4. Suppose instead sellers charge US\$0.80. There is a surplus of $4{,}400 - 3{,}200 = 1{,}200$ books, so the price falls back to US\$0.70.
5. Spending at equilibrium: $0.70 \times 3{,}800 = 2{,}660$, so US\$2,660.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up shortage and surplus"}
A price **below** equilibrium causes a **shortage** (excess demand). A price **above** equilibrium causes a **surplus** (excess supply). Check which quantity is larger.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the curves shift to remove a shortage"}
When the market moves back to equilibrium, the price changes and the quantities move **along** the existing curves. Nothing shifts unless a condition of demand or supply changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the shortage as a quantity, not a gap"}
A shortage is the **difference** between quantity demanded and quantity supplied, not the quantity demanded on its own.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** "The equilibrium price is US\$8 and the equilibrium quantity is 600 crates a day, because quantity demanded equals quantity supplied." Always give the units.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show the subtraction for a shortage or surplus ($750 - 450 = 300$) and name it ("a shortage of 300 crates").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain how equilibrium is restored:** say what sellers or buyers do (cut prices to clear stocks / bid prices up), and how quantity demanded and quantity supplied respond (extend or contract) until they are equal.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of the **equilibrium point** where demand and supply interact; Cambridge uses **market equilibrium** and **disequilibrium**. *Excess demand* means a shortage and *excess supply* means a surplus in both syllabuses.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; the market clears at the equilibrium price.
- Below the equilibrium price there is a shortage (excess demand); above it there is a surplus (excess supply).
- Shortages push prices up and surpluses push prices down until equilibrium is reached.
- During the adjustment, quantities move along the curves; the curves do not shift.
- On a diagram, equilibrium is where D and S cross; read off Pe and Qe.
:::
