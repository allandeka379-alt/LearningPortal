## Introduction

Every January the price of school shoes in Harare seems to rise, and after a drought the price of groundnuts at Mbare Musika climbs. Prices change because the conditions behind demand or supply change, shifting a curve and moving the market to a **new equilibrium**.

This is one of the most heavily examined skills in economics. A typical question describes an event, then asks you to explain its effect on price and quantity, describe the diagram, or work out the new equilibrium from schedules. The method is always the same, and once you have practised it you can apply it to any market.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="New equilibrium"}
The price and quantity at which quantity demanded again equals quantity supplied after the demand or supply curve has shifted.
:::

:::definition{term="Change in market conditions"}
A change in any factor other than the good's own price that shifts the demand curve or the supply curve.
:::

:::definition{term="Sales (quantity traded)"}
The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium. Spending by consumers (and revenue to sellers) equals price × quantity traded.
:::

## The four-step method

1. **Identify** the event and decide whether it affects **demand** or **supply** (or both).
2. **Decide the direction** of the shift: increase (right) or decrease (left).
3. **Find the disequilibrium at the old price**: a shortage or a surplus.
4. **Explain the adjustment**: the price changes and quantities move along the curves to the new equilibrium. State the new price and quantity compared with the old ones.

## The four basic cases

| Shift | Effect at the old price | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand increases (D shifts right) | Shortage | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (D shifts left) | Surplus | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (S shifts right) | Surplus | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (S shifts left) | Shortage | Rises | Falls |

Notice the pattern. A **demand** shift moves price and quantity in the **same** direction. A **supply** shift moves them in **opposite** directions.

## Describing the diagram

For an increase in demand:

- Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis.
- Original curves D1 and S cross at the original equilibrium, P1 and Q1.
- D2 lies to the right of D1. At P1 there is now a shortage (the gap between S and D2).
- D2 crosses S at a higher price P2 and a larger quantity Q2. The movement from Q1 to Q2 is an **extension of supply** along S.

For a decrease in supply, S2 lies to the left of S1; it crosses D at a higher price P2 and a smaller quantity Q2, and the movement along D is a **contraction of demand**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: school shoes in January]
A fictional shoe market has the schedules below. In January, parents buy shoes for the new school year (a seasonal increase in demand).

| Price per pair (US\$) | D1: November (pairs per week) | S (pairs per week) | D2: January (pairs per week) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 15 | 700 | 300 | 900 |
| 20 | 600 | 400 | 800 |
| 25 | 500 | 500 | 700 |
| 30 | 400 | 600 | 600 |
| 35 | 300 | 700 | 500 |

1. Original equilibrium (D1 = S): US\$25 and 500 pairs a week.
2. The event affects **demand**: more pairs are wanted at every price, so demand **increases** (D shifts right by 200 pairs).
3. At the old price of US\$25, quantity demanded is now 700 but quantity supplied is still 500: a **shortage** of $700 - 500 = 200$ pairs.
4. Sellers raise prices. Supply extends and demand (on D2) contracts until they meet. New equilibrium (D2 = S): **US\$30 and 600 pairs**.
5. Consequences for sales: price rises by US\$5 and quantity sold rises by 100 pairs. Sellers' revenue rises from $25 \times 500 = 12{,}500$ to $30 \times 600 = 18{,}000$ dollars a week.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: groundnuts after a drought]
A fictional market for shelled groundnuts:

| Price per kg (US\$) | D (kg per week) | S1: normal season (kg per week) | S2: after drought (kg per week) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.00 | 5,000 | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| 1.20 | 4,500 | 3,500 | 2,500 |
| 1.40 | 4,000 | 4,000 | 3,000 |
| 1.60 | 3,500 | 4,500 | 3,500 |
| 1.80 | 3,000 | 5,000 | 4,000 |

1. Original equilibrium (D = S1): US\$1.40 and 4,000 kg a week.
2. The drought affects **supply**: 1,000 kg less at every price, a **decrease** in supply (S shifts left).
3. At the old price of US\$1.40, quantity demanded is 4,000 but quantity supplied is only 3,000: a **shortage** of 1,000 kg.
4. The price rises; supply extends along S2 and demand contracts along D. New equilibrium (D = S2): **US\$1.60 and 3,500 kg**.
5. Consequences for sales: price up by 20 cents, quantity sold down by 500 kg. Spending is $1.40 \times 4{,}000 = 5{,}600$ before and $1.60 \times 3{,}500 = 5{,}600$ after, so in this case total spending happens to stay the same (US\$5,600): the higher price exactly balances the smaller quantity. Whether spending rises or falls depends on the price elasticity of demand.
:::

## When both curves shift

Sometimes two things happen at once, for example incomes rise (demand increases) while new firms enter (supply increases). Both shifts **raise quantity**, but they push price in opposite directions, so the effect on price is **uncertain**: it depends on which shift is larger. In an exam, say which variable is certain, which is uncertain, and what it depends on.

:::example{title="Applying the method: Victoria Falls hotels"}
A fictional hotel market in Victoria Falls. Event: a new international airline route brings more tourists, and at the same time two new hotels open.

- More tourists → demand for rooms increases (D shifts right): price up, quantity up.
- New hotels → supply of rooms increases (S shifts right): price down, quantity up.
- Overall: the number of room-nights sold **rises** for certain. The room price may rise, fall or stay the same: it rises only if the increase in demand is larger than the increase in supply.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting both curves for one event"}
Most events affect only one side of the market. A rise in incomes shifts demand; it does **not** shift supply. Supply responds by **extending** along the existing curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'demand rises, so price rises, so demand falls'"}
This circular chain confuses shifts with movements. Correct version: "demand increases (shift right), causing a shortage at the old price, so price rises; quantity supplied extends and quantity demanded contracts along D2 until a new equilibrium is reached".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the consequence for sales"}
Questions often ask about sales or revenue. State the new quantity traded, and for spending multiply price by quantity at each equilibrium.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** follow the four steps in order and use the words *increase/decrease* for shifts and *extend/contract* for movements. Finish with a clear comparison: "the equilibrium price rises from P1 to P2 and quantity rises from Q1 to Q2".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate from schedules:** find the old equilibrium, then the new one using the new column, and state the shortage or surplus at the old price as evidence of the adjustment.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** when two events happen together, or when the size of a shift is uncertain, say what the answer depends on (the relative size of the shifts, or the elasticity of the curves) before you conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Price changes come from shifts in demand or supply caused by changes in market conditions.
- Demand shifts move price and quantity in the same direction; supply shifts move them in opposite directions.
- At the old price a shift creates a shortage or a surplus, which moves the market to a new equilibrium.
- Find new equilibria from schedules by matching the new column with the unchanged one.
- When both curves shift, one variable's direction is certain and the other depends on the relative size of the shifts.
:::
