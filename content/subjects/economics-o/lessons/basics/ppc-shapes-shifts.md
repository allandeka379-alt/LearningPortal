## Introduction

In the lesson on PPC points and movements, the curve for Country X was bowed outwards. Not every PPC has that shape, and no PPC stays in the same place for ever. A new lithium mine, a cyclone that destroys farms and roads, a generation of better-trained school leavers, or thousands of nurses emigrating: each changes what an economy can produce, and each moves the curve.

This lesson covers two things. First, the **shape** of the curve and what it tells you about opportunity cost (ZIMSEC examines the three shapes; Cambridge expects only the usual bowed-out curve). Second, **shifts** of the curve, their causes and their link with economic growth (both boards). You should already be able to read a PPC table and calculate the opportunity cost of a movement.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC asks about concave, straight-line and convex PPCs. Cambridge 0455 examines shifts of the PPC and their causes and consequences for growth, but not the three shapes in detail.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Concave PPC"}
A PPC bowed outwards from the origin. The opportunity cost of each extra unit of a good **increases** as more of it is produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Straight-line PPC"}
A PPC with a constant slope. The opportunity cost of each extra unit is **constant**.
:::

:::definition{term="Convex PPC"}
A PPC bowed inwards towards the origin. The opportunity cost of each extra unit **decreases** as more of it is produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Outward shift of the PPC"}
A movement of the whole curve away from the origin, showing that the economy can now produce more of both goods: an increase in productive capacity (potential economic growth).
:::

:::definition{term="Inward shift of the PPC"}
A movement of the whole curve towards the origin, showing a fall in productive capacity.
:::

## The three shapes

The table shows three different curves for an economy producing food and clothing. Each has the same end points (80 thousand units of clothing or 40 thousand tonnes of food) but a different path between them.

| Food (thousand tonnes) | Clothing, straight line (thousand units) | Clothing, concave (thousand units) | Clothing, convex (thousand units) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 80 | 80 | 80 |
| 10 | 60 | 76 | 46 |
| 20 | 40 | 66 | 22 |
| 30 | 20 | 46 | 8 |
| 40 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

:::worked-solution{title="Opportunity cost along each curve"}
Work out the clothing given up for each extra 10 thousand tonnes of food.

1. Straight line: $80 - 60 = 20$, $60 - 40 = 20$, $40 - 20 = 20$, $20 - 0 = 20$. Constant: **20 thousand units** each time.
2. Concave: $80 - 76 = 4$, $76 - 66 = 10$, $66 - 46 = 20$, $46 - 0 = 46$. **Rising** opportunity cost.
3. Convex: $80 - 46 = 34$, $46 - 22 = 24$, $22 - 8 = 14$, $8 - 0 = 8$. **Falling** opportunity cost.
:::

**Describing the diagrams.** Food on the horizontal axis, clothing on the vertical axis. The straight-line PPC is a straight line from 80 on the clothing axis to 40 on the food axis. The concave curve joins the same end points but bulges away from the origin, flat at first and steep at the end. The convex curve sags towards the origin, steep at first and flat at the end.

### Why the usual PPC is concave

Resources are **not equally suited** to producing every good. When an economy makes only a little food, it uses the land and workers best suited to farming, so the first tonnes of food cost little clothing. As food output grows, it must use resources better suited to making clothing: skilled tailors and factory machines moved onto farms produce little extra food, while a lot of clothing is lost. The opportunity cost rises, and the curve bends outwards. This is the shape you should draw unless a question tells you otherwise.

### Straight-line and convex PPCs

A **straight-line** PPC means resources are equally good at producing both goods, so each unit always costs the same. This can happen when the two goods are very similar, for example two kinds of bread made by the same bakers with the same ovens.

A **convex** PPC means the opportunity cost falls as more of a good is produced. This is unusual. It might happen when producing more of a good brings large cost savings from specialisation, so each extra unit needs fewer resources than the one before.

## Shifts of the PPC

A PPC is drawn for a given quantity and quality of resources and a given technology. If any of these change, the whole curve moves.

### Causes of an outward shift

- **More resources**: discovery of minerals (for example new lithium deposits), more land brought into use by irrigation, a larger labour force from population growth or immigration, more capital goods from investment.
- **Better quality of resources**: education and training, better health care (healthier, more productive workers), improved seed varieties and fertiliser.
- **Better technology**: new machines and methods that produce more from the same resources, for example mobile money cutting the time firms spend on payments.

:::example{title="An outward shift in figures"}
After a programme of investment and training, a fictional economy can produce more clothing at every level of food output.

| Food (thousand tonnes) | Maximum clothing before (thousand units) | Maximum clothing after (thousand units) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 80 | 100 |
| 10 | 76 | 96 |
| 20 | 66 | 88 |
| 30 | 46 | 74 |
| 40 | 0 | 52 |
| 50 | not possible | 0 |

1. At every food output, the maximum clothing is higher: the new curve PPC2 lies outside the old curve PPC1.
2. A combination such as 40 thousand tonnes of food with 50 thousand units of clothing was unattainable before and is attainable now.
3. The diagram shows PPC1 and PPC2 with an arrow pointing outwards from PPC1 to PPC2.
:::

### Causes of an inward shift

- **Natural disasters**: droughts, floods and cyclones destroy crops, livestock, roads and buildings. Cyclone Idai in March 2019, which caused severe damage in Chimanimani and other parts of eastern Zimbabwe, is an example of such a disaster.
  <!-- Reviewer: Cyclone Idai (March 2019, severe damage in Chimanimani and eastern Zimbabwe) is widely documented; no statistics are stated. -->
- **Emigration**, especially of skilled workers such as doctors, nurses and engineers (often called the brain drain).
- **War or conflict** that destroys capital and kills or displaces workers.
- **Land degradation**: soil erosion, deforestation and the exhaustion of mineral deposits reduce the quantity of land.
- **Disease** that reduces the size or health of the workforce.

### A shift affecting one good only

If a change affects only one industry, the curve pivots. A new maize variety with higher yields moves only the end point on the food axis outwards; the end point on the clothing axis stays where it was, because clothing technology has not changed.

## Consequences for economic growth

An outward shift of the PPC is an increase in **potential** output: the economy is able to produce more. The economy only enjoys **actual** growth if it uses the extra capacity. If unemployment rises at the same time, output may stay inside the new curve.

An inward shift lowers living standards unless resources are rebuilt. Rebuilding has an opportunity cost: resources used to repair roads after a cyclone cannot also be used for new schools.

The choice between consumer and capital goods matters too. An economy that chooses more capital goods today is likely to see its PPC shift further outwards in future.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting the curve when unemployment falls"}
Falling unemployment moves the economy from inside its PPC towards the curve. It does not shift the curve, because the quantity of resources has not changed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up concave and convex"}
Concave (the normal PPC) bulges outwards, away from the origin, with rising opportunity cost. Convex sags inwards with falling opportunity cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying an outward shift means output has risen"}
An outward shift shows that more **can** be produced. Actual output rises only if the new resources are used.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Two curves, clearly labelled"}
To show a shift, describe (or sketch) both curves labelled PPC1 and PPC2 with an arrow showing the direction, and keep the same labelled axes.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cause, then effect"}
For *explain one cause of an outward shift*, name the cause, say whether it increases the quantity or quality of resources or improves technology, and finish with "so the economy can produce more of both goods".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Shapes (ZIMSEC)"}
When asked what a shape shows, always link it to opportunity cost: rising (concave), constant (straight line) or falling (convex), with the reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Concave PPC: rising opportunity cost, because resources are not equally suited to both goods (the usual shape).
- Straight-line PPC: constant opportunity cost; convex PPC: falling opportunity cost.
- More or better resources, or better technology, shift the PPC outwards; disasters, emigration, war and land degradation shift it inwards.
- An outward shift raises potential output; actual growth needs the extra capacity to be used.
- A change affecting only one good pivots the curve along that good's axis.
:::
