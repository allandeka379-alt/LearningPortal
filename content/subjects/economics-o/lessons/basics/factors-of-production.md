## Introduction

Think about a loaf of bread bought from a tuckshop in Gweru. Wheat was grown on a farm (land). Farm workers, millers, bakers and drivers worked to make and move it (labour). Tractors, mills, ovens and delivery vans were used (capital). And someone decided to set up the bakery, raised the money and took the risk that the bread might not sell (enterprise).

These four **factors of production** are the resources an economy uses to make every good and service. Each factor is paid a reward, and together the rewards are the incomes people earn. This topic links to scarcity (the factors are the scarce resources), to the PPC (more or better factors shift it outwards) and to later topics on labour and firms. Expect "state", "define" and "explain" questions on it in both ZIMSEC and Cambridge papers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production"}
The use of resources to make goods and services that satisfy wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Factors of production"}
The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and enterprise.
:::

:::definition{term="Land"}
All natural resources: the surface of the earth and what is under, on and above it, such as soil, minerals, forests, rivers, fish and the climate.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour"}
Human effort, both physical and mental, used in production.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital"}
Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machines, tools, buildings, vehicles and roads.
:::

:::definition{term="Enterprise"}
The willingness and ability to organise the other factors of production and to take the risk of starting and running a business. The person who supplies it is an entrepreneur.
:::

## The four factors and their rewards

| Factor | Examples | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Farmland near Chinhoyi, platinum deposits, Lake Kariba fish, sunshine | Rent |
| Labour | A teacher, a miner, a nurse, a vendor's assistant | Wages or salaries |
| Capital | A tractor, a grinding mill, a delivery truck, a factory building | Interest |
| Enterprise | Starting a poultry business, opening a hardware shop | Profit (or loss) |

:::keyfact{title="Capital goods are not money"}
In economics, capital means man-made aids to production such as tools and machines. Money is used to *buy* capital goods; firms that borrow money to buy them pay **interest**, the reward to capital.
:::

### Land

Land is anything provided by nature. Its total supply is fixed (no one can make more of the earth), although the amount of land available for a particular use can change: irrigation can bring dry land into farming, and soil erosion can take land out of use. The reward to land is **rent**.

### Labour

Labour is the work done by people. The quantity of labour depends on the size of the population of working age and how many of them want to work. Its quality depends on skills, education, health and experience. The reward is **wages** (usually paid per hour or per week) or **salaries** (a fixed amount per month or year).

### Capital

Capital goods are made by people to help produce other goods. Building them means giving up consumer goods today, which is why investment has an opportunity cost. Capital wears out over time (depreciation) and must be replaced. Its reward is **interest**.

### Enterprise

The entrepreneur decides what to produce and how, hires the other three factors, and bears the risk. If revenue is greater than all the costs, the reward is **profit**; if not, the entrepreneur makes a **loss**. Profit is what is left after every other factor has been paid.

:::worked-solution{title="Sharing out a farm's revenue"}
Chipo grows tomatoes on 2 hectares near Harare. Her figures for one year are shown below.

| Item | Factor of production or other cost | Amount for the year (US\$) |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Sales of tomatoes | Revenue | 18,000 |
| Rent of 2 hectares | Land: rent | 2,400 |
| Pay of three farm workers | Labour: wages | 7,200 |
| Interest on a US\$15,000 loan at 10% | Capital: interest | 1,500 |
| Seed, fertiliser and fuel | Materials bought from other firms | 3,100 |
| What is left for Chipo | Enterprise: profit | 3,800 |

1. Interest: 10% of US\$15,000 $= 0.10 \times 15\,000 = 1\,500$, so US\$1,500.
2. Total paid out: $2\,400 + 7\,200 + 1\,500 + 3\,100 = 14\,200$, so US\$14,200.
3. Profit, the reward for enterprise: $18\,000 - 14\,200 = 3\,800$, so US\$3,800.
4. If a hailstorm had cut sales to US\$12,000, Chipo would have made a loss of US\$2,200: the entrepreneur carries the risk.
:::

## Changes in the quantity and quality of the factors

The amount an economy can produce depends on how much of each factor it has (quantity) and how productive each factor is (quality).

| Factor | Quantity can change because of | Quality can change because of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Discovery of minerals; irrigation or land clearing; soil erosion, desertification or flooding; exhaustion of mines | Fertilisers, irrigation, conservation farming; or degradation and over-grazing |
| Labour | Population growth; immigration and emigration; changes in school-leaving or retirement age; more women joining the labour force | Education and training; better health care and nutrition; work experience |
| Capital | Investment in new machines and infrastructure; depreciation not replaced | New technology built into machines; maintenance |
| Enterprise | Easier rules for starting a business; access to loans; a stable economy that reduces risk | Business training; mentoring; experience |

:::example{title="Two changes in a fictional town"}
1. A new vocational college opens in a small mining town and trains welders and electricians. The **quality** of labour rises: each worker can do more skilled work and make fewer mistakes.
2. In the same town, the old copper mine runs out of ore. The **quantity** of land (natural resources) falls, so the town can produce less unless it develops other activities.

On a PPC diagram, the first change shifts the curve outwards and the second shifts it inwards.
:::

## Mobility of the factors

Factors can move between jobs (occupational mobility) and places (geographical mobility). Land cannot move geographically but can change its use, for example from grazing to growing crops. Labour can move, but family ties, housing costs and a lack of the right skills can make it slow. Capital such as a hand tool is mobile; a factory building is not. More mobile factors let an economy respond faster to changes in demand.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling money capital"}
Money is not a factor of production. Capital is the machines, tools and buildings money buys. A loan is a way of financing capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the rewards"}
Land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest, enterprise earns profit. Profit is not the reward to capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that enterprise bears risk"}
An entrepreneur is not just a manager. The defining feature is taking the risk of loss. A hired manager earns a salary as labour.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State with an example"}
When asked to *state two factors of production*, add a short example of each from the context in the question ("land, such as the farm's soil").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Quantity versus quality"}
For *explain how a country could increase the quality of its labour*, give a measure (education, training, health care) and its effect (higher productivity: more output per worker).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the right reward words"}
Write rent, wages or salaries, interest and profit. Examiners do not credit "payment" alone.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The four factors of production are land, labour, capital and enterprise.
- Their rewards are rent, wages or salaries, interest, and profit (or loss).
- Capital is man-made aids to production, not money.
- The entrepreneur organises the other factors and bears the risk; profit is what remains after other costs are paid.
- Quantity and quality of factors change with population, migration, investment, discoveries, education, health and land degradation.
:::
