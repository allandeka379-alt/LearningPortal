## Introduction

Chipo has US\$20 left at the end of the month. She would like new school shoes, airtime for her phone, a textbook and a bus fare to visit her grandmother in Masvingo. She cannot have all of them. A tobacco farmer near Chinhoyi has 30 hectares: every hectare used for tobacco cannot also grow maize. The Government of Zimbabwe would like to build more clinics, repair more roads and employ more teachers, but tax revenue only stretches so far.

All three are facing the same problem: there is not enough to go round. Economists call this the **basic economic problem**, and every other topic in the course grows out of it. Questions on scarcity appear in multiple-choice papers and as the opening part of longer structured and essay questions, so precise definitions earn easy marks.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Needs"}
Goods and services a person must have to survive, such as food, clean water, shelter and basic clothing.
:::

:::definition{term="Wants"}
Goods and services people would like to have but can live without, such as a smartphone, a car or a holiday at Victoria Falls. Wants are unlimited: when one is satisfied, another appears.
:::

:::definition{term="Resources"}
Everything used to produce goods and services: land and natural resources, labour, capital goods and enterprise. Resources are finite (limited in supply).
:::

:::definition{term="Scarcity"}
The situation in which resources are too limited to satisfy all of people's wants.
:::

:::definition{term="The basic economic problem"}
Finite resources cannot meet unlimited wants, so choices must be made about how resources are used.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic good"}
A good that is produced with scarce resources and so has an opportunity cost, for example maize meal, cement or a school desk.
:::

:::definition{term="Free good"}
A good that is not scarce: it is available in such large quantities that using it has no opportunity cost, for example air or sunlight.
:::

## Unlimited wants, limited resources

Wants keep growing. A family that buys a radio soon wants a television; once it has a television it wants a satellite decoder. Population growth, advertising and rising incomes all add new wants.

Resources, on the other hand, are limited at any moment in time. A country has only so much fertile land, so many trained workers, so many factories and machines, and so many people willing to start businesses. Even rich countries face scarcity, because their people's wants also grow.

Because wants are greater than the resources available, **choice** is unavoidable. Choosing one thing means giving up something else. That idea, opportunity cost, is covered in its own lesson.

:::keyfact{title="Scarcity is relative, not absolute"}
Scarcity does not mean that a good has run out. It means that the resources available are not enough to satisfy everyone's wants for it. Maize can be scarce in a good harvest year if people want more than is produced.
:::

## Who faces the basic economic problem?

Everyone in an economy faces scarcity, but in different ways.

| Decision-maker | Limited resource | Example of a choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer | Income | Buy a textbook or a pair of shoes |
| Worker | Time and skills | Work overtime or attend evening classes |
| Firm | Finance, land, labour and machines | Buy a new delivery truck or hire two more workers |
| Government | Tax revenue and borrowing | Build a hospital in Gweru or resurface a highway |

:::example{title="Scarcity for a small business in Mutare"}
Farai runs a small furniture workshop in Mutare. He has two skilled carpenters, one workbench and enough timber for about 20 items a week.

1. His resources (labour, capital and raw materials) are limited.
2. Customers want beds, tables, wardrobes and school desks: their combined orders are far more than 20 items.
3. Farai must choose which orders to accept and which to turn away or delay.

This is the basic economic problem at the level of one firm: finite resources, many wants, so a choice must be made.
:::

## Economic goods and free goods

Most goods are **economic goods**. Making them uses land, labour, capital and enterprise that could have produced something else, so they have an opportunity cost and usually a price.

A **free good** is one where there is so much available that one person using more does not leave less for anyone else. Air in the open countryside and sunlight are the usual examples.

Be careful: a good that is given away at no charge is not necessarily a free good. Free primary education or a free health campaign still uses scarce resources (teachers, buildings, medicines). It is an economic good paid for by the government through taxes. Clean water in a city is also an economic good, because pipes, pumps and treatment chemicals are scarce.

:::example{title="Is it a free good?"}
1. Sea water on a beach: a free good, because there is far more than people want to use.
2. Bottled water sold in Harare: an economic good, because bottling, transport and the bottle use scarce resources.
3. A free textbook handed out by the Ministry: an economic good, because paper, printing and distribution use scarce resources even though the pupil pays nothing.
:::

## The three basic economic questions

Because resources are scarce, every society must answer three questions.

1. **What to produce?** Which goods and services, and how much of each? More maize or more tobacco? More schools or more roads?
2. **How to produce?** Which combination of resources and which methods? Should a mine use many workers with simple tools (labour-intensive) or a few workers with large machines (capital-intensive)?
3. **For whom to produce?** Who receives the goods and services? Those who can pay the most, those in greatest need, or everyone equally?

Different economic systems answer these questions in different ways: through prices and markets, through government planning, or through a mix of the two. That comparison is covered in the lessons on economic systems.

:::example{title="The three questions in a village irrigation scheme"}
A village near Masvingo receives a small irrigation scheme.

1. What: the committee must decide whether to grow vegetables for sale in town, or maize to feed families during the dry season.
2. How: it must decide whether to use treadle pumps worked by hand (more labour) or a diesel pump (more capital).
3. For whom: it must decide whether plots go to the households that can pay a fee, or to the poorest households first.

Each decision uses the same scarce land and water, so each is a choice.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying scarcity means a good has run out"}
Scarcity means wants are greater than the resources available, not that there is none left. Everything produced with limited resources is scarce, even when shops are full.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling anything with a zero price a free good"}
A free good has no opportunity cost because it is not scarce. Goods provided free of charge by the government still use scarce resources, so they are economic goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up needs and wants"}
Needs are essentials for survival; wants are everything people desire. In economics all needs are also wants, but not all wants are needs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that rich people and rich countries face scarcity"}
Higher income moves the limit but does not remove it. Time is always limited, and wants keep growing with income.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Define, then apply"}
For *define the basic economic problem*, give both halves for full marks: limited (finite) resources and unlimited wants, which means choices must be made.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain with an example"}
*Explain* questions want a reason: "A government faces scarcity because its tax revenue is limited while demands for health, education and roads are almost unlimited, so it must choose which to fund." One identifying point plus one developed point usually earns two marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="The three questions"}
State each question in full (what, how, for whom to produce) and add a short example for each. In essays, link them to the economic system that answers them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wants are unlimited, but resources (land, labour, capital, enterprise) are finite.
- Scarcity means resources cannot satisfy all wants, so choices must be made: this is the basic economic problem.
- Consumers, workers, firms and governments all face scarcity of income, time, finance or revenue.
- Economic goods use scarce resources and have an opportunity cost; free goods, such as air, do not.
- Every society must decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom.
:::
