## Introduction

Scarcity means that every choice has a cost. When Tendai spends his Saturday helping at his uncle's tuckshop in Bulawayo, he cannot also play in the school football match. When a farmer plants tobacco on a field, that field cannot also grow maize that season. When the government spends money on a new dam, that money cannot also pay for new classrooms.

Economists measure the cost of a choice not only in money but in **what is given up**. This idea, opportunity cost, is one of the most useful tools in economics. It appears in multiple-choice questions, in short "define and explain" questions, in production possibility curve (PPC) diagrams and in essays about government spending. You should already know the meaning of scarcity and the basic economic problem.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost"}
The next best alternative that is given up (forgone) when a choice is made.
:::

:::definition{term="Choice"}
A decision about how to use scarce resources, made because resources cannot satisfy all wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade-off"}
Giving up some of one thing to have more of another.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Only the next best alternative counts"}
If you had five possible uses for your money and chose one, the opportunity cost is **only** the alternative you would have chosen second. It is not all four alternatives added together.
:::

## Why every choice has an opportunity cost

Because resources are scarce, using them in one way means they cannot be used in another. To find the opportunity cost of a decision:

1. List the alternatives that were possible.
2. Rank them in order of preference.
3. Identify the one chosen (first choice).
4. The opportunity cost is the alternative ranked second: the **next best** one.

:::worked-solution{title="Ranking alternatives: Tendai's birthday money"}
Tendai has US\$15 of birthday money. In order of preference he would like: (1) an economics revision guide, (2) a pair of football boots on special offer, (3) a cinema trip with friends, (4) a phone airtime bundle.

1. He buys his first choice, the revision guide.
2. The alternative he would have chosen next is the football boots.
3. So the opportunity cost of the revision guide is **the football boots**.
4. The cinema trip and the airtime are not part of the opportunity cost: he could only ever have had one of the alternatives with the same US\$15.
:::

## Measuring opportunity cost in goods

Opportunity cost is often measured in units of the good given up. This is how it is shown on a production possibility curve.

:::worked-solution{title="A farm in Mashonaland"}
A farmer has 10 hectares. One hectare yields 6 tonnes of maize or 2.5 tonnes of soya beans. She decides to switch 4 hectares from maize to soya beans.

| Crop | Yield per hectare (tonnes) | Output from 4 hectares (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Maize | 6 | 24 |
| Soya beans | 2.50 | 10 |

1. Soya beans gained: $4 \times 2.5 = 10$ tonnes.
2. Maize given up: $4 \times 6 = 24$ tonnes.
3. The opportunity cost of the 10 tonnes of soya beans is **24 tonnes of maize**.
4. Per tonne of soya beans: $24 \div 10 = 2.4$ tonnes of maize.
:::

## Opportunity cost for different decision-makers

### Consumers

A consumer with a limited income must choose between goods. Buying school uniforms in January may mean giving up new kitchen utensils. Knowing the opportunity cost helps consumers get the most satisfaction from their income.

### Workers

A worker's time and skills are limited. Choosing one job means giving up the wages and conditions of the next best job. Choosing to study full-time means giving up the earnings from working.

:::worked-solution{title="Nyasha's training course"}
Nyasha sells vegetables in Gweru and earns about US\$12 a day. She is offered a place on a five-day bookkeeping course.

1. The next best use of her five days is selling vegetables.
2. Earnings given up: $5 \times 12 = 60$, so US\$60.
3. The opportunity cost of attending the course is the US\$60 of earnings she would have made (and the vegetables she would have sold).
4. She should attend if she expects the skills to raise her future income by more than this, for example by keeping better records and cutting waste.
:::

### Firms

A firm with limited finance must choose between projects. A bakery that spends its savings on a new oven cannot use the same money to open a second shop. Firms compare the expected returns of projects, so the opportunity cost of one project is the return the next best project would have earned.

### Governments

A government has limited revenue from taxes and borrowing. Every dollar spent on one programme is not available for another.

:::worked-solution{title="A government budget choice"}
A provincial budget has US\$1,200,000 left for building. It can build one district clinic costing US\$1,200,000 or classroom blocks costing US\$400,000 each.

1. Number of classroom blocks possible: $1\,200\,000 \div 400\,000 = 3$.
2. If the clinic is built, the next best alternative given up is three classroom blocks.
3. The opportunity cost of the clinic is **three classroom blocks**.
:::

## How opportunity cost influences decisions

Opportunity cost makes the real cost of a choice visible.

- A consumer who sees that a new phone means giving up a term's transport money may decide to wait.
- A student who sees that leaving school at 16 means giving up the higher lifetime earnings of a qualified worker may decide to stay on.
- A firm that sees a project earning less than the next best project should not go ahead.
- A government that sees a stadium means giving up two rural hospitals may decide to delay the stadium.

:::example{title="Why the money price is not the whole story"}
A free concert in Harare's city centre has a money price of zero, but going still has an opportunity cost: the afternoon's earnings of a vendor who closes her stall, or the study time of a student before exams. Opportunity cost exists even when nothing is paid.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding up all the alternatives"}
The opportunity cost is the single next best alternative, not the total of every option given up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the item chosen as the opportunity cost"}
The opportunity cost is what is **given up**, not what is obtained. If Rudo chooses a bicycle over a radio, the opportunity cost is the radio.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking free things have no opportunity cost"}
A zero money price does not mean a zero opportunity cost. Time and resources used could have been used for something else.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'the cost of the item'"}
The money price is not the opportunity cost. Always name the alternative given up.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the key words"}
A full definition says **next best alternative** and **forgone** (or given up). "What you give up" alone may earn only part of the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain with a specific example"}
For *explain how opportunity cost affects a government's decision*, name the decision-maker, the choice, the alternative forgone and the effect on the decision: "If the government builds a dam, it gives up the hospital it could have built, so it will compare the benefits of the two."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
When opportunity cost is measured in units, state the units of the good given up ("24 tonnes of maize"), and divide when the question asks "per unit".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
- It exists because resources are scarce: using them one way means not using them another.
- Only the second-ranked alternative counts, not every alternative.
- It can be measured in goods (24 tonnes of maize) or in money earnings given up.
- Consumers, workers, firms and governments all use opportunity cost to compare choices.
:::
