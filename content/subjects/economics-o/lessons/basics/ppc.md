## Introduction

Imagine a country that uses all its land, workers, machines and entrepreneurs to make just two kinds of goods. If it wants more of one, it must give up some of the other. A **production possibility curve** (PPC) shows this trade-off in one simple diagram.

The PPC brings together three ideas you have met already: scarcity (output is limited), choice (the country picks one combination) and opportunity cost (more of one good means less of the other). Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge examine the PPC in multiple-choice questions, in data questions with a table of output combinations, and in essays. Because you will not be drawing on screen, this lesson describes each diagram precisely in words and shows the numbers in tables. In the examination you sketch the diagram from the same description.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production possibility curve (PPC)"}
A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods (or groups of goods) that an economy can produce when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed, with a given state of technology. It is also called a production possibility frontier (PPF).
:::

:::definition{term="Productive efficiency"}
Producing the maximum output from the resources available, so that more of one good can only be produced by giving up some of the other. Every point on the PPC is productively efficient.
:::

:::definition{term="Unattainable combination"}
A combination of goods beyond the PPC that the economy cannot produce with its present resources and technology.
:::

## Building a PPC from a table

A fictional economy, Country X, produces only consumer goods (such as food and clothing) and capital goods (such as machines and tractors). The table shows the maximum it can produce of one good for each amount of the other.

| Combination | Consumer goods (thousand units) | Capital goods (thousand units) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 0 | 50 |
| B | 20 | 48 |
| C | 40 | 44 |
| D | 60 | 36 |
| E | 80 | 22 |
| F | 100 | 0 |

**Describing the diagram.** Put consumer goods on the horizontal axis and capital goods on the vertical axis, each labelled with its units. Plot the six combinations and join them with a smooth curve. The curve starts at 50 on the vertical axis (combination A, no consumer goods), slopes downwards from left to right, and ends at 100 on the horizontal axis (combination F, no capital goods). It is bowed outwards from the origin: it gets steeper as you move to the right. (Why it has this shape is explained in the lesson on PPC shapes and shifts.)

## Points on, inside and beyond the curve

Every combination of the two goods is a point somewhere on the diagram. Where it lies tells you how well the economy is using its resources.

| Position of the point | Meaning | Example for Country X |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On the curve | All resources fully and efficiently used: productively efficient | C (40, 44) |
| Inside the curve | Some resources unemployed or used inefficiently: more of both goods could be produced | G (40, 30) |
| Beyond (outside) the curve | Unattainable with present resources and technology | H (60, 44) |

:::example{title="Why point G is inside the curve"}
At G, Country X makes 40 thousand units of consumer goods and 30 thousand units of capital goods. Combination C shows that, with the same 40 thousand units of consumer goods, it could make 44 thousand units of capital goods.

1. G is below C, so it is inside the curve.
2. The economy could produce 14 thousand more units of capital goods without giving up any consumer goods.
3. This means resources are being wasted: there may be unemployment (for example idle factories during power cuts, or workers without jobs) or inefficient methods.
4. Moving from G to C has **no opportunity cost**, because unused resources are being put to work.
:::

Point H (60, 44) lies beyond the curve: at 60 thousand units of consumer goods the most capital goods possible is 36 thousand (combination D). Country X cannot reach H unless its resources increase or its technology improves, which would shift the whole curve outwards.

## Movements along the curve and opportunity cost

When the economy is on its PPC, the only way to produce more of one good is to move resources away from the other. A movement along the curve therefore shows an opportunity cost.

:::worked-solution{title="Opportunity cost of a movement from C to D"}
1. At C: 40 thousand consumer goods and 44 thousand capital goods.
2. At D: 60 thousand consumer goods and 36 thousand capital goods.
3. Extra consumer goods: $60 - 40 = 20$ thousand units.
4. Capital goods given up: $44 - 36 = 8$ thousand units.
5. The opportunity cost of the extra 20 thousand units of consumer goods is **8 thousand units of capital goods**.
6. Per unit: $8 \div 20 = 0.4$ units of capital goods for each extra unit of consumer goods.
:::

Doing the same for every movement shows how the opportunity cost changes along the curve.

| Movement | Extra consumer goods (thousand units) | Capital goods given up (thousand units) | Capital goods given up per unit of consumer goods |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A to B | 20 | 2 | 0.1 |
| B to C | 20 | 4 | 0.2 |
| C to D | 20 | 8 | 0.4 |
| D to E | 20 | 14 | 0.7 |
| E to F | 20 | 22 | 1.1 |

Each extra 20 thousand units of consumer goods costs more capital goods than the last. That is why the curve gets steeper as you move to the right.

:::worked-solution{title="A movement in the other direction: E to C"}
1. Capital goods gained: $44 - 22 = 22$ thousand units.
2. Consumer goods given up: $80 - 40 = 40$ thousand units.
3. The opportunity cost of the extra 22 thousand units of capital goods is **40 thousand units of consumer goods**.
:::

## Efficient resource allocation

Every point on the PPC is productively efficient, but they are not all equally good for society. Choosing *where* on the curve to produce is the question of **what to produce**.

- A point near A (mostly capital goods) means people consume little today, but the extra machines and tractors can raise output in future.
- A point near F (mostly consumer goods) means a higher standard of living today but little investment for the future.

Resources are allocated efficiently when the economy is on its PPC **and** produces the combination that best matches what people want. Markets, governments or a mix of both make this choice, as the lessons on economic systems explain.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a point inside the curve unattainable"}
A point inside the curve can be reached easily: the economy is already there. It is inefficient, not impossible. Only points beyond the curve are unattainable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading opportunity cost as the new output"}
The opportunity cost of moving from C to D is the 8 thousand capital goods **given up**, not the 36 thousand still produced at D.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a movement from inside to the curve has an opportunity cost"}
Moving from inside the curve to the curve uses unemployed resources, so more of one good can be made without giving up the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Unlabelled axes"}
A sketch without labelled axes (with units) loses marks even if the curve is right.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or sketch precisely"}
Label both axes with the goods and units, label the curve PPC, mark the points with letters and, for a movement, show the change in each good with dotted lines to the axes.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations from a table"}
For the opportunity cost of a movement, write the change in each good, then say "the opportunity cost of X more units of good 1 is Y units of good 2". Divide only if the question asks for the cost per unit.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain inside points"}
For *explain why the economy is inside its PPC*, give a cause (unemployment, idle factories, power cuts, inefficient methods) and its effect (output is below the maximum possible).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all resources fully and efficiently used.
- Points on the curve are productively efficient; points inside show unemployed or wasted resources; points beyond are unattainable.
- A movement along the curve shows the opportunity cost of producing more of one good.
- Moving from inside the curve to the curve has no opportunity cost.
- Society must also choose the best point on the curve: the question of what to produce.
:::
