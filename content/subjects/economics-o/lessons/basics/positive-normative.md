## Introduction

Listen to any radio phone-in about the economy and you will hear two kinds of sentences mixed together. "Fuel prices went up last week" is a claim about what happened. "The government should cut fuel taxes" is a claim about what ought to happen. Economists call the first kind **positive** and the second kind **normative**.

Telling them apart is a basic skill in economics. Positive statements can be settled by looking at evidence; normative statements depend on values, so people who agree on all the facts can still disagree. The ZIMSEC syllabus asks you to distinguish the two (Form 3), usually in multiple-choice questions and short "identify and explain" parts. The skill also helps in essays: a good answer separates the evidence (positive) from the judgement (normative) when it reaches a conclusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Positive statement"}
A statement about what is, was or will be that can be tested against evidence and shown to be true or false. It contains no value judgement.
:::

:::definition{term="Normative statement"}
A statement of opinion about what ought to be. It contains a value judgement, so it cannot be proved true or false by evidence alone.
:::

:::definition{term="Value judgement"}
A view of whether something is good or bad, fair or unfair, better or worse, based on a person's beliefs and values.
:::

## Positive statements: testable claims

A positive statement does **not** have to be true. It only has to be possible to check it. "Zimbabwe's tobacco crop is larger than its maize crop" is positive whether or not it is correct, because you could look at the production figures and find out.

Positive statements often:

- describe facts: "The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe introduced the ZiG currency in April 2024."
- describe cause and effect: "A rise in the price of bread will reduce the quantity of bread demanded."
- make predictions that could later be checked: "Unemployment will fall next year if the new mines open."

## Normative statements: value judgements

A normative statement says what **should** happen or whether something is good or bad. Because it rests on values, evidence alone cannot settle it. Two people can agree on every fact and still disagree about a normative statement.

Watch for signal words, but do not rely on them alone:

| Often signals a normative statement | Often signals a positive statement |
| --- | --- |
| should, ought to, must | is, was, will, has, have |
| fair, unfair, too high, too low | rose, fell, increased, decreased |
| better, worse, best, more important | caused, led to, is related to |
| desirable, undesirable, good, bad | a measurable figure or comparison |

:::example{title="Sorting statements"}
1. "Inflation in Country X was higher in 2025 than in 2024." **Positive**: the price index for both years can be checked.
2. "Inflation is the most serious problem facing Country X." **Normative**: "most serious" is a judgement; someone else might think unemployment is worse.
3. "Raising the minimum wage will reduce employment among young workers." **Positive**: a claim about cause and effect that could be tested with evidence, even though economists may disagree about the result.
4. "The minimum wage is unfairly low." **Normative**: "unfairly" is a value judgement.
5. "The government should spend more on rural clinics than on roads." **Normative**: it says what ought to be done.
:::

### Tricky cases

Some statements look normative but are positive, and the other way round.

- "Many economists believe taxes should be cut." This is **positive**: it reports what economists believe, and you could survey them. The word *should* is inside a reported view.
- "Prices are too high." This is **normative**, even though it contains no *should*: "too high" is a judgement.
- "The government will spend US\$2 million on boreholes." This is **positive**: it is a prediction that can be checked when the money is spent.

:::example{title="Facts and values in one debate"}
Rudo and Farai are discussing a plan to remove the tax on cooking oil.

1. Rudo: "Removing the tax will lower the price of cooking oil." Positive: it can be tested after the tax is removed.
2. Farai: "It will also reduce government revenue." Positive: revenue figures will show it.
3. Rudo: "Cheaper cooking oil for poor families matters more than the lost revenue." Normative: she is weighing the two by her values.
4. Farai: "The revenue is more important because it pays for schools." Normative: a different value judgement.

They agree on the facts (statements 1 and 2) but disagree on the judgement (statements 3 and 4). Evidence could settle the first two; it cannot settle the last two.
:::

## Why the difference matters

- **Economic analysis** is mainly positive: it explains how markets and the economy work and predicts the effects of changes.
- **Economic policy** always involves normative judgements: deciding which effects matter most and who should gain or lose.
- When you evaluate in an essay, your analysis of effects is positive and your conclusion ("on balance the government should…") is normative. Showing which is which makes your argument clearer and earns evaluation marks.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a positive statement must be true"}
"Bulawayo is the capital of Zimbabwe" is false, but it is still positive: it can be checked. Positive means testable, not correct.
:::

:::mistake{title="Relying only on the word should"}
"Prices are too high" and "this policy is unfair" are normative without *should*. "Economists say taxes should fall" can be positive because it reports a belief.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling predictions normative"}
A prediction about the future ("exports will rise") is positive, because it can be checked when the time comes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify and explain"}
For *identify the normative statement and explain your choice*, quote the statement, then point to the value judgement: "It is normative because 'unfair' is an opinion that cannot be tested with evidence."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the test"}
Ask: "Could evidence prove this true or false?" Yes means positive; no means normative.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="In essays"}
Signal your judgement clearly at the end ("In my view…", "On balance…") after presenting the positive analysis of both sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Positive statements describe what is, was or will be and can be tested with evidence.
- Normative statements say what ought to be and contain value judgements.
- A positive statement can be false; it only has to be testable.
- Words such as *should*, *fair* and *too high* often signal normative statements, but always apply the test.
- Economic analysis is mainly positive; policy decisions involve normative judgements.
:::
