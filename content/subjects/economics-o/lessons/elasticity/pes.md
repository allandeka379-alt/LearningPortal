## Introduction

When the price of tomatoes jumps at Mbare Musika, farmers cannot instantly grow more: tomatoes take months to ripen. A factory in Bulawayo making plastic buckets, on the other hand, can run an extra shift tomorrow if buckets become more profitable. Both obey the law of supply, but they respond at very different speeds and by very different amounts.

**Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures that responsiveness. It explains why food prices swing so much after a drought, why some firms can take advantage of a price rise and others cannot, and why governments worry about supply when they set policy. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge expect you to define PES, calculate it, interpret values and curves, and explain its determinants.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price elasticity of supply (PES)"}
A measure of how responsive the quantity supplied of a good is to a change in its price.
:::

:::formula{name="Price elasticity of supply"}
$$
\text{PES} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity supplied}}{\text{percentage change in price}}
$$
:::

Because price and quantity supplied move in the **same** direction (the law of supply), PES is normally **positive**. There is no minus sign to worry about.

## Interpreting PES values and curves

| Value of PES | Description | Supply curve (price on the vertical axis) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic: quantity supplied does not change | Vertical straight line |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic: quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price | Steep upward-sloping line |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary elastic: quantity changes by the same percentage as price | Any straight line through the origin |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic: quantity changes by a larger percentage than price | Gentle upward-sloping line |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic: any quantity is supplied at one price, none below it | Horizontal straight line |

A useful test for straight-line supply curves: a line that cuts the **price** axis is elastic, one that cuts the **quantity** axis is inelastic, and one that passes through the **origin** is unitary elastic.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: tomato farmers (inelastic)]
The price of a crate of tomatoes rises from US\$4 to US\$5. Within the month, farmers supplying a fictional market increase deliveries from 200 to 230 crates a week.

1. Percentage change in quantity supplied $= \frac{230 - 200}{200} \times 100 = +15\%$.
2. Percentage change in price $= \frac{5 - 4}{4} \times 100 = +25\%$.
3. PES $= \frac{15}{25} = 0.6$.
4. Supply is **price inelastic**: farmers can only pick their ripe tomatoes a little sooner or send fewer to other markets. They cannot grow more until the next crop.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a factory with spare capacity (elastic)]
A fictional plastics factory in Bulawayo makes buckets. The wholesale price rises from US\$20 to US\$22 a box, and the factory raises output from 1,000 to 1,250 boxes a week by running a second shift on idle machines.

1. Percentage change in quantity supplied $= \frac{1{,}250 - 1{,}000}{1{,}000} \times 100 = +25\%$.
2. Percentage change in price $= \frac{22 - 20}{20} \times 100 = +10\%$.
3. PES $= \frac{25}{10} = 2.5$.
4. Supply is **price elastic**, because the factory has spare capacity, raw materials in stock and can hire extra workers quickly.
:::

## Determinants of PES

### Time

Time is the most important factor. In the **momentary period** (immediately), supply may be fixed: the fish already caught today is all there is. In the **short run**, firms can use existing machines more intensively, so supply becomes somewhat more elastic. In the **long run**, they can build new factories, plant more land or new firms can enter, so supply is most elastic.

### Spare capacity

A firm working below full capacity (idle machines, workers on short time) can raise output quickly, so supply is elastic. A firm already at full capacity cannot, so supply is inelastic.

### Stocks (inventories)

Goods that can be stored, such as tinned food or cement, can be released from stock when prices rise, making supply more elastic. **Perishable** goods (fresh milk, vegetables, flowers) cannot be stored for long, so supply is less elastic.

### Ease of switching factors of production

If labour and machines can easily move from making one product to another (a clothing factory switching from shirts to school uniforms), supply is elastic. If production needs specialised skills or equipment, supply is inelastic.

### Length of the production period and nature of the product

Crops, livestock, mines and power stations take months or years to expand output, so their supply is inelastic in the short run. Simple manufactured goods and many services can be expanded quickly.

## PES in real situations: farming

Agricultural supply is usually **inelastic in the short run**: once crops are planted, output depends on the weather and the growing season, not on next month's price. This has important consequences:

- When demand rises, or a drought shifts supply left, prices rise sharply because supply cannot respond quickly.
- In a bumper harvest, supply is high whatever the price, so prices can fall steeply and farm incomes suffer.
- Farmers base planting decisions on **last season's** prices, which can cause cycles of shortage and surplus.

Governments and farmers respond with storage (strategic grain reserves), irrigation, faster-maturing varieties and contract farming, all of which make supply more elastic or more stable.

:::example{title="Applying PES: tickets for a big match"}
A football stadium in Harare has a fixed number of seats. For a big match, supply of tickets is **perfectly inelastic** (a vertical supply curve): however high the price, no extra seats can be provided. Any increase in demand therefore raises only the price, not the quantity of tickets.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up PES and PED"}
PES uses the percentage change in quantity **supplied**; PED uses quantity **demanded**. PES is normally positive; PED normally negative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Judging elasticity from slope alone"}
A steep line is not always inelastic. For straight lines, use the axis test: through the origin = unitary; cutting the price axis = elastic; cutting the quantity axis = inelastic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting time"}
Supply that is inelastic this month may be elastic over several years. Always say which time period you mean.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show both percentage changes and the division; give PES to two decimal places and say "elastic" or "inelastic".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** link each determinant to the product in the question: "flowers are perishable, so growers cannot hold stocks to release when the price rises, making supply inelastic".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** in farming questions, consider the short run versus the long run and what farmers or the government can do to make supply more responsive.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price; it is normally positive.
- 0 = perfectly inelastic (vertical curve); 0 to 1 inelastic; 1 unitary (straight line through the origin); above 1 elastic; infinity perfectly elastic (horizontal).
- Supply is more elastic with more time, spare capacity, stocks and easy switching of factors.
- Perishable goods and products with long production periods, such as crops, have inelastic supply in the short run.
- Inelastic farm supply explains why food prices swing sharply after changes in demand or weather.
:::
