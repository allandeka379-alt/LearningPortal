## Introduction

When civil servants receive a pay rise, butchers in Gweru sell more beef, while sales of the cheapest grade of maize meal may actually fall as families trade up. When bus fares rise, kombi operators carry more passengers. Price elasticity of demand looks only at a good's **own** price; two other elasticities measure how demand responds to **income** and to the **price of another good**.

ZIMSEC (Form 4) expects you to calculate and interpret **income elasticity of demand (YED)** and **cross elasticity of demand (XED)**, and to explain their importance to firms and government. The method is the same as for PED: divide one percentage change by another, then interpret the **sign** and the **size**. For these two elasticities the sign carries the meaning, so never drop it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Income elasticity of demand (YED)"}
A measure of how responsive the demand for a good is to a change in consumers' income.
:::

:::formula{name="Income elasticity of demand"}
$$
\text{YED} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded}}{\text{percentage change in income}}
$$
:::

:::definition{term="Cross elasticity of demand (XED)"}
A measure of how responsive the demand for one good (A) is to a change in the price of another good (B).
:::

:::formula{name="Cross elasticity of demand"}
$$
\text{XED} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded of good A}}{\text{percentage change in price of good B}}
$$
:::

## Interpreting YED

| Value of YED | Type of good | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negative (below 0) | Inferior good | Demand falls as income rises |
| Between 0 and 1 | Normal good, necessity (income inelastic) | Demand rises, but by a smaller percentage than income |
| Greater than 1 | Normal good, luxury (income elastic) | Demand rises by a larger percentage than income |

![Number line of income elasticity of demand: negative values (inferior goods) lie below 0, values between 0 and 1 are normal necessities, values above 1 are luxuries; beef at +1.2 is a luxury and the cheapest maize meal at −0.4 is an inferior good.](media:diagrams/yed-scale.png "Figure: where the two worked examples fall on the income elasticity scale (schematic scale, values from the examples)")

All goods with a **positive** YED are **normal** goods. Whether a good is a necessity, a luxury or inferior can differ between households: for a low-income family, a refrigerator may be a luxury; for a high-income family, a necessity.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: incomes rise in a fictional town]
Average weekly household income in a fictional town rises from US\$400 to US\$500. Weekly demand for beef rises from 20 tonnes to 26 tonnes, and demand for the cheapest grade of maize meal falls from 40 tonnes to 36 tonnes.

1. Percentage change in income $= \frac{500 - 400}{400} \times 100 = +25\%$.
2. Beef: percentage change in quantity $= \frac{26 - 20}{20} \times 100 = +30\%$, so YED $= \frac{30}{25} = +1.2$.
3. Beef is a **normal** good (positive YED) and a **luxury** (income elastic, YED greater than 1).
4. Cheapest maize meal: percentage change in quantity $= \frac{36 - 40}{40} \times 100 = -10\%$, so YED $= \frac{-10}{25} = -0.4$.
5. The cheapest maize meal is an **inferior** good for these households (negative YED): as incomes rise they switch to better grades.
:::

## Interpreting XED

| Value of XED | Relationship | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | Substitutes | A rise in the price of B increases demand for A (consumers switch) |
| Negative | Complements | A rise in the price of B decreases demand for A (they are used together) |
| Zero (or close to zero) | Unrelated goods | A change in the price of B has no effect on demand for A |

![Number line of cross elasticity of demand: negative values mean complements and positive values mean substitutes; kombi and bus rides at +0.5 are weak substitutes and cars and petrol at −0.5 are complements.](media:diagrams/xed-scale.png "Figure: where the two worked examples fall on the cross elasticity scale (schematic scale, values from the examples)")

The **size** shows how closely related the goods are: an XED of +2.0 means very close substitutes; +0.1 means weak substitutes.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: kombis, buses, petrol and cars]
a) Bus fares on a route rise from US\$1.00 to US\$1.25. Daily kombi rides on the same route rise from 800 to 900.

1. Percentage change in price of bus fares $= \frac{1.25 - 1.00}{1.00} \times 100 = +25\%$.
2. Percentage change in kombi rides $= \frac{900 - 800}{800} \times 100 = +12.5\%$.
3. XED $= \frac{12.5}{25} = +0.5$: positive, so kombi and bus rides are **substitutes**, though not very close ones.

b) The price of petrol rises from US\$1.50 to US\$1.80 a litre. Monthly sales of new cars at a fictional dealer fall from 50 to 45.

1. Percentage change in price of petrol $= \frac{1.80 - 1.50}{1.50} \times 100 = +20\%$.
2. Percentage change in cars sold $= \frac{45 - 50}{50} \times 100 = -10\%$.
3. XED $= \frac{-10}{20} = -0.5$: negative, so cars and petrol are **complements**.
:::

## Why YED and XED matter

### To firms

- **Planning production:** in a growing economy, firms selling luxuries (YED above 1) can expect fast growth in sales; in a recession their sales fall fastest. Firms selling inferior goods may do better when incomes fall.
- **Product range:** a firm may sell both luxuries and necessities to spread its risk across the economic cycle.
- **Pricing and competition:** a high positive XED with a rival's product warns a firm that a rival's price cut will take many of its customers. A negative XED shows complements that can be priced together (for example a cheap printer with dearer ink cartridges).

### To the government

- **Planning:** if incomes are expected to rise, the government can forecast demand for services such as private transport, electricity or tourism (likely luxuries) and plan roads and power supply.
- **Taxation:** taxes on luxuries raise more revenue as incomes rise, and fall mainly on better-off households.
- **Policy effects:** a tax that raises the price of one good also affects demand for its substitutes and complements. A tax on fuel, for example, reduces demand for cars (a complement) and increases demand for public transport (a substitute).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the sign"}
For YED and XED the sign is the answer: a negative YED means an inferior good; a negative XED means complements. Always include and interpret it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the wrong price in the XED formula"}
XED divides the change in quantity of good **A** by the change in the price of good **B**, the other good. Using A's own price gives PED.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every normal good a luxury"}
A positive YED below 1 is a normal good that is a **necessity**. Only YED above 1 indicates a luxury.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show both percentage changes with the original values as bases, then divide; give the sign and two decimal places.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Interpret:** state the type of good or relationship and what it means: "XED = +0.5, so the goods are substitutes: a 10% rise in bus fares increases kombi rides by 5%".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain importance:** link the value to a decision: a firm's output plans, its pricing against rivals, or the government's tax and planning choices.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Income and cross elasticity of demand are in the ZIMSEC syllabus (Form 4) only; Cambridge IGCSE 0455 examines PED and PES but not YED or XED.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income; XED = % change in quantity of A ÷ % change in price of B.
- YED negative: inferior; 0 to 1: normal necessity; above 1: normal luxury.
- XED positive: substitutes; negative: complements; zero: unrelated. The size shows how closely related the goods are.
- Always keep and interpret the sign.
- Firms use YED and XED to plan output, product ranges and pricing; governments use them to plan services and taxes.
:::
