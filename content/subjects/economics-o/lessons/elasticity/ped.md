## Introduction

When the price of bread rises, households in Bulawayo still buy bread, just a little less. When one brand of fizzy drink becomes dearer, many shoppers simply pick another brand from the fridge. The law of demand tells us that quantity demanded falls when price rises; **price elasticity of demand (PED)** tells us **by how much**.

Firms use PED to set prices, and governments use it to decide which goods to tax. In the exam you will be asked to define PED, calculate it from data, interpret the answer, and describe the shape of demand curves with different elasticities. Calculation questions appear in multiple-choice papers and in data-response and structured questions, so practise the method until it is automatic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price elasticity of demand (PED)"}
A measure of how responsive the quantity demanded of a good is to a change in its own price.
:::

:::formula{name="Price elasticity of demand"}
$$
\text{PED} = \frac{\text{percentage change in quantity demanded}}{\text{percentage change in price}}
$$
:::

:::formula{name="Percentage change"}
$$
\text{percentage change} = \frac{\text{new value} - \text{original value}}{\text{original value}} \times 100
$$
:::

Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, PED is normally **negative**. Economists usually look at its size (ignoring the sign) to decide whether demand is elastic or inelastic. In a calculation question, read the instructions carefully: some ask you to include the minus sign, others to ignore it.

## Interpreting PED values

| Value of PED (ignoring the sign) | Description | Meaning | Demand curve |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes | Vertical straight line |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price | Steep |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary elastic | Quantity changes by the same percentage as price | A curve (rectangular hyperbola): spending is the same at every price |
| Greater than 1 | Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price | Gentle (flatter) |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Any rise in price above the given level makes quantity demanded fall to zero | Horizontal straight line |

Remember that "steep" and "flat" only give a rough idea: compare curves drawn on the **same scale**. The calculation is what settles the question.

### Describing the curves

- **Perfectly inelastic:** a vertical line at one quantity, for example a life-saving medicine that patients must buy in a fixed dose whatever the price.
- **Inelastic:** a steep downward-sloping line; a large fall in price brings only a small rise in quantity (for example salt or bread).
- **Unitary:** a smooth curve bending towards both axes, along which price × quantity is constant.
- **Elastic:** a gently sloping line; a small price cut brings a large rise in quantity (for example one brand among many similar brands).
- **Perfectly elastic:** a horizontal line at one price; a firm selling at the market price can sell any amount, but nothing at a higher price.

## Calculating PED step by step

1. Calculate the **percentage change in quantity demanded**, using the original quantity as the base.
2. Calculate the **percentage change in price**, using the original price as the base.
3. Divide the first by the second.
4. Interpret: state the value, whether demand is elastic or inelastic, and what this means.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: bread (inelastic)]
The price of a loaf at a fictional bakery rises from US\$1.00 to US\$1.20. Daily sales fall from 500 to 450 loaves. Calculate PED and interpret it.

1. Percentage change in quantity demanded $= \frac{450 - 500}{500} \times 100 = -10\%$.
2. Percentage change in price $= \frac{1.20 - 1.00}{1.00} \times 100 = +20\%$.
3. $\text{PED} = \frac{-10}{+20} = -0.5$.
4. Ignoring the sign, 0.5 is between 0 and 1, so demand is **price inelastic**: a 20% rise in price causes only a 10% fall in quantity demanded. Bread is a necessity with few close substitutes.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a brand of soft drink (elastic)]
A fictional brand of soft drink cuts its price from US\$0.80 to US\$0.60 a bottle, and weekly sales rise from 2,000 to 3,000 bottles. Calculate PED (include the minus sign).

1. Percentage change in quantity demanded $= \frac{3{,}000 - 2{,}000}{2{,}000} \times 100 = +50\%$.
2. Percentage change in price $= \frac{0.60 - 0.80}{0.80} \times 100 = -25\%$.
3. $\text{PED} = \frac{+50}{-25} = -2$.
4. Ignoring the sign, 2 is greater than 1: demand is **price elastic**. Each 1% cut in price raises quantity demanded by 2%, because shoppers switch from rival brands.
:::

:::example{title="Working backwards from PED"}
If PED for cooking oil is −0.4 and its price rises by 10%, the percentage change in quantity demanded = PED × percentage change in price = $-0.4 \times 10 = -4\%$. Rearranging the formula like this is a common exam task.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the formula upside down"}
PED is percentage change in **quantity** divided by percentage change in **price**. Dividing price by quantity gives the reciprocal (for bread, 2 instead of 0.5) and the wrong conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the new value as the base"}
Percentage changes use the **original** value. For a price rise from US\$1.00 to US\$1.20 the change is 20%, not $\frac{0.20}{1.20} = 16.7\%$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using absolute changes instead of percentages"}
Dividing a change of 50 loaves by a change of 20 cents gives a meaningless number that depends on the units. Always convert to percentages first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a negative PED means inelastic"}
The minus sign only shows that price and quantity move in opposite directions. Elastic or inelastic depends on the **size**: −2 is elastic, −0.5 is inelastic.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show the two percentage changes, the division and the answer. Give the answer to two decimal places unless told otherwise, and follow the instruction about the sign.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Interpret / explain:** a number alone rarely earns full marks. Add "demand is price inelastic, because the percentage change in quantity demanded (10%) is smaller than the percentage change in price (20%)".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe the diagram:** for perfectly inelastic demand say "a vertical demand curve"; for perfectly elastic "a horizontal demand curve"; for relatively elastic and inelastic compare the slopes on the same axes.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use the percentage-change formula with the original value as the base. ZIMSEC also examines **income** and **cross** elasticity of demand (a separate lesson); Cambridge examines PED and PES only.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- PED measures how responsive quantity demanded is to a change in the good's own price.
- PED = percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price, using original values as the base.
- PED is normally negative; judge elasticity by its size.
- 0 = perfectly inelastic (vertical curve); 0 to 1 = inelastic; 1 = unitary; above 1 = elastic; infinity = perfectly elastic (horizontal curve).
- Always interpret the number in words after calculating it.
:::
