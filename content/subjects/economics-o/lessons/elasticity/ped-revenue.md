## Introduction

A bakery in Mutare raises the price of its bread and is pleased to find that its takings rise. A soft-drinks seller down the road tries the same and sees her takings fall. Why the difference? The answer is **price elasticity of demand**.

Whatever consumers spend on a good is what the sellers receive, so **consumer spending = firms' total revenue**. Whether a price change raises or lowers it depends on how strongly buyers react. This lesson shows the link by calculation and with described diagrams, then explains why PED matters for decisions by households, workers, firms and the government. Both boards ask this regularly, often as the "explain" or "discuss" part of a data question.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Total revenue (TR)"}
The total amount a firm receives from selling its output: price × quantity sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumer spending (expenditure) on a good"}
The total amount consumers pay for a good: price × quantity bought. It equals the sellers' total revenue.
:::

:::formula{name="Total revenue"}
$$
\text{TR} = \text{price} \times \text{quantity sold}
$$
:::

## The rule linking PED and revenue

A price change has two effects on revenue that pull in opposite directions:

- the **price effect**: each unit sold brings in more (after a rise) or less (after a cut);
- the **quantity effect**: fewer units are sold after a rise, more after a cut.

Which effect is bigger depends on PED:

| PED (ignoring the sign) | Price rise | Price cut |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastic (greater than 1) | Total revenue falls | Total revenue rises |
| Unitary (exactly 1) | No change | No change |
| Inelastic (less than 1) | Total revenue rises | Total revenue falls |

A quick way to remember: when demand is **inelastic**, price and revenue move in the **same** direction; when demand is **elastic**, they move in **opposite** directions.

## Describing the revenue diagram

On a demand diagram (price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis), total revenue at any point is the **area of the rectangle** under that point: its height is the price and its width the quantity. When the price changes:

- the area **gained** is the extra quantity sold (or the higher price on units still sold);
- the area **lost** is the lower price on units already sold (or the units no longer sold).

On a **flat (elastic)** demand curve a small price cut adds a wide rectangle of extra sales, so the gain is larger than the loss. On a **steep (inelastic)** curve a price cut adds only a thin strip of extra sales, so the loss is larger.

![Demand for screen repairs is elastic: when the price is cut from US\$10 to US\$8, weekly repairs rise from 100 to 150; the revenue lost on existing sales is 200 dollars (2 x 100) but the revenue gained on extra sales is 400 dollars (8 x 50), so total revenue rises from US\$1,000 to US\$1,200.](media:diagrams/ped-revenue-rectangles.png "Figure: revenue gained (green) and lost (amber) when an elastic price is cut")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a price cut when demand is elastic]
A fictional phone-repair kiosk in Harare cuts its price for a screen repair from US\$10 to US\$8. Repairs per week rise from 100 to 150.

1. PED: % change in quantity $= \frac{150 - 100}{100} \times 100 = +50\%$; % change in price $= \frac{8 - 10}{10} \times 100 = -20\%$; PED $= \frac{50}{-20} = -2.5$, elastic.
2. TR before $= 10 \times 100 = 1{,}000$, so US\$1,000 a week.
3. TR after $= 8 \times 150 = 1{,}200$, so US\$1,200 a week.
4. Revenue **rises** by US\$200. The loss of US\$2 on each of the 100 repairs it already did (US\$200) is smaller than the gain from 50 extra repairs at US\$8 (US\$400).
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a price rise when demand is inelastic]
A fictional milling company raises the price of a bag of maize meal from US\$2.00 to US\$2.50. Daily sales fall from 1,000 to 900 bags.

1. PED: % change in quantity $= \frac{900 - 1{,}000}{1{,}000} \times 100 = -10\%$; % change in price $= \frac{2.50 - 2.00}{2.00} \times 100 = +25\%$; PED $= \frac{-10}{25} = -0.4$, inelastic.
2. TR before $= 2.00 \times 1{,}000 = 2{,}000$, so US\$2,000 a day.
3. TR after $= 2.50 \times 900 = 2{,}250$, so US\$2,250 a day.
4. Revenue **rises** by US\$250: the price effect outweighs the small fall in sales. Consumers also **spend** US\$250 more each day on maize meal, leaving less for other goods.
:::

## Why PED matters

### Firms

- **Pricing:** a firm facing inelastic demand can raise revenue by raising its price; a firm facing elastic demand raises revenue by cutting its price (for example special offers on one brand).
- **Profit:** revenue is only half the story. Selling more after a price cut also raises costs, so a firm checks that the extra revenue covers the extra costs.
- **Making demand less elastic:** firms use branding and advertising to reduce the number of products consumers see as close substitutes.

### Consumers and households

- When demand for a necessity is inelastic, a price rise forces households to **spend more** on it and cut other spending. Poorer households, who spend a large share of income on food and fuel, are hit hardest.

### Workers

- If demand for the product they make is **inelastic**, a firm can pass on a wage rise in higher prices without losing many sales, so workers (or their trade union) are in a stronger bargaining position. If demand is elastic, higher wages that push up prices may cost jobs.

### Government

- **Taxation:** taxes on goods with inelastic demand (fuel, alcohol, tobacco) raise a lot of revenue, because sales fall only a little. Taxes on goods with elastic demand raise less, because sales fall a lot.
- **Discouraging consumption:** if the aim is to cut consumption of a harmful good with inelastic demand, a tax alone may not reduce it much; other measures (education, bans on advertising) may be needed.
- **Prices of public services:** raising bus fares or electricity tariffs raises revenue only if demand is inelastic.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming a price rise always raises revenue"}
A price rise raises revenue only when demand is **inelastic**. With elastic demand, revenue falls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing revenue with profit"}
A rise in total revenue does not guarantee a rise in profit: costs also change when output changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that spending equals revenue"}
If a question asks about consumer spending, the rule is the same: with inelastic demand, a price rise raises what consumers spend on the good.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** work out TR before and after (price × quantity), state the change, and link it to PED in one sentence.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / analyse:** say which effect (price effect or quantity effect) is larger and why, using the PED value.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** for a pricing or tax decision, consider PED, costs, the aim (revenue or reducing consumption), who bears the burden, and whether PED may change over time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Consumer spending on a good equals firms' total revenue: price × quantity.
- Inelastic demand: price and total revenue move in the same direction.
- Elastic demand: price and total revenue move in opposite directions; unitary: revenue does not change.
- On a diagram, revenue is the rectangle price × quantity; compare the area gained with the area lost.
- PED guides firms' pricing, workers' wage bargaining, households' budgets and governments' tax choices.
:::
