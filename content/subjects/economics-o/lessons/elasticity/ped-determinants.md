## Introduction

Why does a rise in the price of salt hardly change how much salt a family in Hwange buys, while a rise in the price of one brand of soap sends shoppers straight to another brand? Both are everyday goods, yet their price elasticities of demand are very different.

In the lesson on calculating PED you learned **how** to measure responsiveness. This lesson explains **why** demand for some goods is elastic and for others inelastic. Examiners often ask you to "explain two factors that make demand for petrol inelastic" or "suggest why demand for a brand of mobile phone is elastic". You need to name each determinant, explain how it works, and apply it to the good in the question.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Determinants of PED"}
The factors that decide how strongly consumers respond to a change in a good's price.
:::

:::definition{term="Close substitute"}
A good that satisfies the same want in a very similar way, so consumers can switch to it easily (for example two brands of cooking oil).
:::

:::definition{term="Necessity"}
A good that consumers regard as essential and buy regularly whatever the price, such as basic food. Demand for necessities tends to be inelastic.
:::

:::definition{term="Luxury"}
A good that consumers can do without or postpone buying. Demand for luxuries tends to be more elastic.
:::

## The main determinants

### 1. Availability of close substitutes

This is the most important determinant. If a good has many close substitutes, a price rise makes consumers switch, so demand is **elastic**. If there are few or no close substitutes, consumers keep buying, so demand is **inelastic**. Kombi rides on a route served by many operators have elastic demand for any one operator; electricity, with no easy substitute for running a fridge, has inelastic demand.

### 2. Proportion of income spent on the good

When a good takes only a tiny share of income (salt, matches, a box of pins), even a large percentage rise in its price makes little difference to the household budget, so demand is **inelastic**. When a good takes a large share (rent, school fees, a car), a price rise has a big effect on what is left, so consumers look harder for alternatives and demand tends to be **more elastic**.

### 3. Necessity or luxury

Households cut back on luxuries (eating out, holidays at Victoria Falls, jewellery) when prices rise, but they keep buying necessities such as maize meal. So demand for necessities is **inelastic** and for luxuries **more elastic**. What counts as a luxury depends on the household's income: a fridge may be a necessity for one family and a luxury for another.

### 4. Habit and addiction

Goods that people buy out of habit or addiction, such as cigarettes and alcohol, have **inelastic** demand: consumers continue to buy them even when prices rise sharply. This is one reason governments tax them.

### 5. Time

Immediately after a price rise, consumers may have no choice but to keep buying. Over time they find substitutes or change their behaviour (for example, buying a more fuel-efficient car or installing solar panels after electricity prices rise). So demand is usually **more elastic in the long run** than in the short run.

### 6. How the market is defined

The narrower the definition, the more substitutes there are, so the more elastic the demand. Demand for "food" is very inelastic; demand for "bread" is less inelastic; demand for "one bakery's bread" is elastic, because other bakeries' bread is a close substitute.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: one brand versus the whole market]
A fictional town's shops raise the price of all cooking oil by 10%, and total sales of cooking oil fall by 3%. On another occasion one brand, "Brand K", raises its price by 10% while other brands keep theirs the same, and Brand K's sales fall by 30%.

1. PED for all cooking oil $= \frac{-3}{+10} = -0.3$: inelastic.
2. PED for Brand K $= \frac{-30}{+10} = -3$: elastic.
3. Explanation: cooking oil as a whole is a necessity with no close substitute, so households keep buying it. Brand K has close substitutes (other brands), so its customers switch.
4. Conclusion: the narrower the definition of the market, the more substitutes there are and the more elastic the demand.
:::

:::example{title="Worked example 2: applying the determinants to petrol"}
Explain why the demand for petrol in Zimbabwe is likely to be price inelastic in the short run.

- **Few close substitutes:** most cars, kombis and generators cannot run on anything else, so users cannot switch quickly.
- **Necessity:** many people need petrol to get to work, deliver goods or run a business.
- **Time:** in the short run, owners cannot change their vehicle or move house. Over a longer period some may buy more fuel-efficient vehicles, use public transport or switch to solar for generators, so demand becomes **more elastic** in the long run.
- **Share of income:** for a kombi operator petrol is a large share of costs, which pushes the other way (towards more elastic demand), but the lack of substitutes is the stronger influence in the short run.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing determinants without applying them"}
"Substitutes, income, time" earns little. Explain how the determinant works **for the good in the question**: "there are many brands of soap, so if one brand raises its price, consumers switch to another, making demand elastic".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying expensive goods always have elastic demand"}
It is the **proportion of income** spent that matters, together with substitutes. A costly medicine with no substitute can still have inelastic demand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the time period"}
Demand that is inelastic today may become elastic over months or years as consumers adjust. Say which time period you mean.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** name the determinant precisely ("availability of close substitutes", not just "substitutes").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** use the chain determinant → how consumers react to a price rise → elastic or inelastic. One fully explained determinant is usually worth two marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** when determinants point in different directions (as for petrol above), say which is stronger and why, and whether the answer changes over time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The availability of close substitutes is the most important determinant: more substitutes, more elastic demand.
- Goods that take a small share of income have inelastic demand; large shares make demand more elastic.
- Necessities and habit-forming goods have inelastic demand; luxuries have more elastic demand.
- Demand becomes more elastic over time as consumers find alternatives.
- The narrower the market definition (one brand rather than the whole product), the more elastic the demand.
:::
