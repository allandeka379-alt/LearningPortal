## Introduction

When a news report says that "the economy grew last year", it is quoting national income statistics. These figures guide big decisions: whether the government raises teachers' salaries, whether a firm builds a new factory in Gweru, and whether an international lender offers a loan. But a single number cannot capture everything that happens in an economy. In Zimbabwe a large share of people earn their living in the informal sector, as vendors, cross-border traders, small-scale farmers and artisans, much of which is never recorded.

This lesson builds on the lesson on GDP, GNP and the three methods of measurement. ZIMSEC asks you to *discuss* the uses of national income statistics by different groups and *explain* their limitations, so you need both sides: what the figures are good for, and why they may mislead.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nominal GDP"}
GDP measured at the prices of the year in which the output was produced (current prices). It rises if output rises **or** if prices rise.
:::

:::definition{term="Real GDP"}
GDP adjusted for changes in the price level, measured at the prices of a base year (constant prices). It changes only when the volume of output changes.
:::

:::definition{term="GDP per head (per capita)"}
GDP divided by the population. It shows the average output, and so the average income, per person.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Economic activity that is not registered, regulated or recorded by the authorities, such as street vending or unregistered small workshops.
:::

:::formula{name="Real GDP and GDP per head"}
Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ price index × 100

GDP per head = GDP ÷ population
:::

## Uses of national income statistics

| User | How the statistics are used |
| --- | --- |
| Government | Measures the growth rate of the economy; plans spending on schools, clinics and roads; sets the budget and estimates tax revenue; judges whether policies are working; spots a recession early |
| Firms | Judge whether incomes and demand are rising, so whether to invest, expand or enter a new market |
| Households and workers | Judge whether living standards are improving; trade unions use growth figures in wage negotiations |
| International bodies and investors | Compare countries' living standards (GDP per head); decide on loans, aid and investment |
| Economists and students | Study the structure of the economy (shares of agriculture, mining, manufacturing and services) and how it changes |

National income statistics are used to compare:

- **the same country over time**: is real GDP rising, and how fast (economic growth)?
- **different countries at the same time**: whose people are better off on average (GDP per head)?
- **sectors**: is the economy becoming less dependent on agriculture or mining?

## Limitations of national income statistics

1. **Double counting.** If statisticians add the sales of intermediate goods as well as final goods, output is overstated. Careful use of value added avoids it, but errors still happen.
2. **The informal sector.** Vendors, cross-border traders and small workshops often keep no records and pay little tax, so their output is missed or estimated. GDP is understated.
3. **Unrecorded (non-market) activities.** Subsistence farming, housework, caring for relatives and voluntary work produce real goods and services but are not sold, so they are left out.
4. **Inaccurate or incomplete data.** Surveys may be out of date, firms may under-report income to pay less tax, and statistics offices may lack money and staff. Figures are often revised later.
5. **Price changes.** Nominal GDP rises when prices rise, even if output does not. Comparisons over time need real GDP. In periods of very high inflation, such as Zimbabwe's hyperinflation of 2007-2008, nominal figures become almost meaningless.
6. **Population size.** Total GDP says nothing about how many people share it; GDP per head is needed.
7. **Distribution of income.** GDP per head is an average. It does not show whether income is shared fairly or held by a few.
8. **Quality of life and the environment.** GDP ignores leisure time, working conditions, pollution and the using up of natural resources such as forests and minerals. A country that cuts down its forests can show higher GDP while becoming poorer in the long run.
9. **Composition of output.** A rise in GDP from military spending or from repairing flood damage adds little to living standards.
10. **International comparisons.** Countries use different methods and currencies; converting at exchange rates ignores differences in the cost of living.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: nominal and real GDP]
A fictional country's nominal GDP rises from US\$6,000 million in the base year to US\$7,200 million the next year. Over the same period, the price index rises from 100 to 115.

| Year | Nominal GDP (US\$ million) | Price index (base year = 100) | Real GDP (US\$ million, base-year prices) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Base year | 6,000 | 100 | 6,000 |
| Next year | 7,200 | 115 | 6,260.9 |

1. Nominal growth = (7,200 − 6,000) ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **20.0%**.
2. Real GDP next year = 7,200 ÷ 115 × 100 = **US\$6,260.9 million** (to one decimal place).
3. Real growth = (6,260.9 − 6,000) ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **4.3%**.
4. Most of the 20% rise was caused by higher prices; output grew by only about 4.3%.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: total GDP and GDP per head]
Two fictional countries:

| Country | GDP (US\$ million) | Population (million) | GDP per head (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Country P | 18,000 | 12 | 1,500 |
| Country Q | 24,000 | 20 | 1,200 |

1. Country Q has the larger GDP, but GDP per head is 24,000 ÷ 20 = US\$1,200, compared with 18,000 ÷ 12 = US\$1,500 in Country P.
2. On average, people in Country P have more output per person.
3. Next year Country P's GDP grows by 3% (to US\$18,540 million) while its population grows by 4% (to 12.48 million). GDP per head becomes 18,540 ÷ 12.48 = US\$1,485.58, a **fall of about 1.0%**, even though GDP rose.
4. Even so, GDP per head says nothing about how income is shared, the size of the informal sector, or the quality of life in either country.
:::

:::example{title="Why a rise in GDP may not mean people are better off"}
Suppose the GDP of a fictional mining district rises after a new open-cast mine opens. Output and incomes are higher, but the mine's profits go mainly to its owners, nearby rivers are polluted, and farmland is lost. Recorded GDP rises, yet many households in the district may be worse off. A full judgement needs other information: how income is distributed, environmental damage, and indicators such as life expectancy and school enrolment.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating a rise in nominal GDP as economic growth"}
Nominal GDP rises with inflation. Growth means a rise in **real** GDP. Always ask whether the figures are at current or constant prices.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing total GDP instead of GDP per head"}
A country with a large population can have a large GDP and still be poor per person. Use GDP per head for living standards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing limitations without explaining their effect"}
Say **which way** each limitation biases the figure. For example, the informal sector is missed, so GDP **understates** the true output.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Discuss the uses:** organise your answer by user (government, firms, households, international bodies) and give a specific decision each one makes with the figures.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain limitations:** name the limitation, explain why it happens, and state its effect on the GDP total (overstates or understates). A Zimbabwean example, such as informal vending or subsistence farming, earns credit.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate real GDP:** divide nominal GDP by the price index and multiply by 100, then compare with the base year.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- National income statistics are used to measure growth, compare living standards, plan government policy and guide business and international decisions.
- Real GDP removes the effect of price changes; GDP per head allows for population.
- Limitations: double counting, the informal sector, unrecorded non-market activity, inaccurate data and price changes.
- GDP per head ignores income distribution, leisure, the environment and the kind of goods produced.
- Use GDP figures together with other indicators to judge living standards.
:::
