## Introduction

Every day, people in Zimbabwe produce goods and services: farmers in Mashonaland grow maize and tobacco, miners at Hwange dig coal, factories in Bulawayo make goods, kombi drivers carry passengers and teachers run lessons. **National income accounting** adds up the value of all this activity for a whole country over one year. The total tells us how large the economy is and whether it is growing or shrinking.

In Zimbabwe the official national accounts are compiled by ZIMSTAT, the national statistics agency, working with the Ministry of Finance and the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe. When you need a real figure, look up the latest ZIMSTAT publication and note its date; in this lesson every number is invented for practice.

National income accounting is a ZIMSEC topic examined in all three papers, including the Paper 2 data response, where you are often given a table of spending figures and asked to calculate GDP. You need three skills: define the measures, explain the three methods, and calculate.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gross Domestic Product (GDP)"}
The total market value of all final goods and services produced **within a country's borders** in one year, whoever owns the factors of production.
:::

:::definition{term="Net income from abroad"}
Income (wages, interest, profits and dividends) earned by a country's residents from their factors of production abroad, **minus** the same kinds of income paid to foreigners from production inside the country.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross National Product (GNP)"}
The total value of final goods and services produced by a country's **residents and their factors of production**, at home or abroad, in one year. GNP = GDP + net income from abroad.
:::

:::definition{term="Final goods and services"}
Goods and services bought by the final user (households, firms buying capital goods, government or foreign buyers), not used up in making something else. Goods used up in production are **intermediate goods**.
:::

:::definition{term="Value added"}
The value of a firm's output minus the value of the inputs it bought from other firms.
:::

:::formula{name="National income identities"}
GDP (expenditure method) = C + I + G + (X − M)

GNP = GDP + net income from abroad
:::

## GDP and GNP

GDP is about **where** production happens; GNP is about **who** owns the factors of production.

- A foreign-owned mine in Zimbabwe adds to Zimbabwe's GDP, but the profits sent back to its foreign owners are income paid abroad, so they are subtracted when moving to GNP.
- A Zimbabwean nurse working in another country earns income abroad. It is not part of Zimbabwe's GDP, but it is part of Zimbabwe's GNP if she remains a Zimbabwean resident.

If more income flows out to foreign owners than flows in, net income from abroad is **negative** and GNP is smaller than GDP. This is common in countries with many foreign-owned mines and firms.

## The three methods of measuring national income

Spending by one person is income to another, and income is earned by producing output. So, in principle, the three methods give the **same total**: output = income = expenditure. In practice the figures differ slightly because of errors and missing data, and statisticians record a "statistical discrepancy".

![Circular flow of income as a schematic: firms pay wages, rent, interest and profit to households for the factors of production (income, on one side), and households spend their income on the goods and services firms produce (spending, on the other), so output, income and expenditure measure the same flow and give the same total.](media:diagrams/circular-flow-schematic.png "Figure: schematic of the circular flow; output, income and expenditure are the same flow measured at different points")

### 1. The output (value added) method

Add up the value of output produced by every sector: agriculture, mining, manufacturing, construction and services. To avoid **double counting**, count only the **value added** at each stage, or only the value of final goods. If the value of a bale of cotton is counted when the farmer sells it and again inside the price of the shirt made from it, output is overstated.

### 2. The income method

Add up the incomes earned by the factors of production in producing the year's output: **wages and salaries** (labour), **rent** (land), **interest** (capital) and **profits** (enterprise). **Transfer payments** such as pensions, grants and gifts are left out, because no output is produced in return for them.

### 3. The expenditure method

Add up spending on final goods and services:

| Component | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumption (C) | Spending by households on goods and services | Food, transport fares, school uniforms |
| Investment (I) | Spending by firms on capital goods and additions to stocks | A new tractor, a factory building |
| Government spending (G) | Spending by government on goods and services | Teachers' salaries, building roads |
| Exports (X) | Spending by foreigners on the country's output | Tobacco sold to overseas buyers |
| Imports (M) | Spending by residents on foreign output (subtracted) | Imported cars and fuel |

Imports are subtracted because C, I and G include spending on foreign goods, which were not produced in the country. Government transfer payments, such as pensions, are not part of G.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: GDP and GNP by the expenditure method]
A fictional country, Country X, has the following spending in one year:

| Component | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Consumption (C) | 5,400 |
| Investment (I) | 1,250 |
| Government spending (G) | 1,600 |
| Exports (X) | 1,900 |
| Imports (M) | 2,150 |

Net income from abroad is −US\$320 million and the population is 16 million.

1. Net exports = X − M = 1,900 − 2,150 = −250 (imports are larger than exports).
2. GDP = C + I + G + (X − M) = 5,400 + 1,250 + 1,600 − 250 = **US\$8,000 million**.
3. GNP = GDP + net income from abroad = 8,000 + (−320) = **US\$7,680 million**. GNP is smaller because more income is paid to foreign owners than is received from abroad.
4. GDP per head = 8,000 million ÷ 16 million = **US\$500**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: value added and double counting]
A cotton-to-shirt chain in a fictional district (figures in US\$ thousand):

| Stage | Inputs bought (US\$ thousand) | Value of sales (US\$ thousand) | Value added (US\$ thousand) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cotton farmer (seed cotton) | 0 | 300 | 300 |
| Ginnery (cotton lint) | 300 | 450 | 150 |
| Clothing factory (shirts) | 450 | 900 | 450 |
| Retailer (shirts sold to households) | 900 | 1,200 | 300 |
| **Total** |  | **2,850** | **1,200** |

1. Value added at each stage = sales − inputs bought: 300, 150, 450 and 300.
2. Total value added = 300 + 150 + 450 + 300 = **US\$1,200 thousand**.
3. This equals the value of the final good (shirts sold to households for US\$1,200 thousand).
4. Adding all the sales gives US\$2,850 thousand, which overstates output by US\$1,650 thousand: this is double counting.
:::

:::example{title="Applying the income method"}
In Country X, wages and salaries are US\$3,900 million, rent US\$450 million, interest US\$550 million and profits US\$1,600 million. The government also paid US\$300 million in state pensions. National income by the income method is 3,900 + 450 + 550 + 1,600 = **US\$6,500 million**. The pensions are a transfer payment, not a reward for producing output, so including them (to get US\$6,800 million) would be wrong.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding imports instead of subtracting them"}
Imports are spending on goods produced abroad. They are **subtracted**: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). If imports exceed exports, net exports are negative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up GDP and GNP"}
GDP measures production **inside** the country. GNP adds net income from abroad. A negative net income from abroad makes GNP **smaller** than GDP.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting every sale in the output method"}
Only final goods, or the value added at each stage, are counted. Adding the sales of raw materials, intermediate goods and final goods counts the same output several times.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including transfer payments"}
Pensions, grants, gifts and the sale of second-hand goods are not payments for new output and are left out of national income.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- GDP is the value of final output produced within a country in a year; GNP = GDP + net income from abroad.
- Output = income = expenditure: the three methods should give the same total.
- Output method: add value added (or final goods only) to avoid double counting.
- Income method: add wages, rent, interest and profits; leave out transfer payments.
- Expenditure method: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M); imports are subtracted.
:::
