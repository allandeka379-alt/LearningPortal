## Introduction

Every Saturday Thandeka sells eggs at the Mbare market in Harare. If she sells 300 trays at US\$4 each, she takes in US\$1,200. That money is her **revenue**: the income a firm receives from selling its output. Revenue is not the same as profit, because Thandeka still has to pay for feed, vaccines and transport, but it is the other half of the profit calculation. A firm needs to know how its revenue changes when it sells more, when it raises or lowers its price, and when demand for its product changes.

Both boards ask you to define and calculate **total revenue** and **average revenue** and to explain how sales influence revenue. ZIMSEC also asks you to calculate **marginal revenue** (Forms 1 and 4), which you need for the profit-maximising rule in the next lesson. Revenue tables are common in ZIMSEC Paper 2 data-response questions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Revenue"}
The income a firm receives from selling its goods or services in a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Total revenue (TR)"}
The total income from sales: the price per unit multiplied by the quantity sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Average revenue (AR)"}
Revenue per unit sold: total revenue divided by the quantity sold. When every unit is sold at the same price, average revenue equals the price.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal revenue (MR)"}
The change in total revenue when one more unit is sold (ZIMSEC).
:::

## The formulas

$$TR = P \times Q \qquad AR = \frac{TR}{Q} = P \qquad MR = \frac{\Delta TR}{\Delta Q}$$

Here $P$ is the price, $Q$ the quantity sold and $\Delta$ means "change in". If sales change by more than one unit between two rows of a table, divide the change in TR by the change in quantity to get marginal revenue per unit.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Cambridge 0455 examines total revenue and average revenue only. ZIMSEC also examines marginal revenue and uses it with marginal cost to find the profit-maximising output.
:::

## Revenue for a firm that is a price taker

Some firms sell in markets with many sellers of an identical product, such as eggs, tomatoes or maize. Each seller is too small to influence the market price, so it takes the price as given: it is a **price taker**. Whatever it sells, it gets the same price for each unit.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: an egg producer selling at a fixed market price]
Thandeka's fictional poultry business sells eggs at the market price of US\$4 a tray.

| Quantity (units) | Price (US\$) | TR (US\$) | AR (US\$) | MR (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 4 | 0 | – | – |
| 100 | 4 | 400 | 4 | 4 |
| 200 | 4 | 800 | 4 | 4 |
| 300 | 4 | 1,200 | 4 | 4 |
| 400 | 4 | 1,600 | 4 | 4 |

1. TR for 300 trays: $4 \times 300 = 1{,}200$, so US\$1,200.
2. AR for 300 trays: $1{,}200 \div 300 = 4$, so US\$4, the same as the price.
3. MR between 200 and 300 trays (ZIMSEC): $(1{,}200 - 800) \div (300 - 200) = 400 \div 100 = 4$, so US\$4 per tray.
4. For a price taker, **price = AR = MR**, and TR rises by the same amount for every extra tray sold.
:::

If you describe these figures as a diagram with revenue on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the TR line is a straight line rising from the origin. On a separate diagram with price or revenue per unit on the vertical axis, AR and MR are the same horizontal line at US\$4.

## Revenue for a firm that must lower its price to sell more

Most firms face a downward-sloping demand curve: to sell more, they must lower the price. Because the lower price applies to all the units sold, not only the extra one, marginal revenue is **less** than the price.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a car wash that cuts its price to attract customers]
Tendai's fictional car wash in Bulawayo finds that it can wash more cars an hour only by lowering its price.

| Quantity (units) | Price (US\$) | TR (US\$) | AR (US\$) | MR (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 13 | 0 | – | – |
| 1 | 12 | 12 | 12 | 12 |
| 2 | 11 | 22 | 11 | 10 |
| 3 | 10 | 30 | 10 | 8 |
| 4 | 9 | 36 | 9 | 6 |
| 5 | 8 | 40 | 8 | 4 |
| 6 | 7 | 42 | 7 | 2 |

1. TR for 4 washes: $9 \times 4 = 36$, so US\$36.
2. AR for 4 washes: $36 \div 4 = 9$, so US\$9, equal to the price.
3. MR of the 4th wash (ZIMSEC): $36 - 30 = 6$, so US\$6. The 4th customer pays US\$9, but the price for the other three falls from US\$10 to US\$9, losing US\$3: $9 - 3 = 6$.
4. TR keeps rising but by less and less, because MR falls (12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2). If MR became negative, selling more would actually reduce TR.
5. AR (the price) falls as quantity rises, and MR is below AR after the first unit.
:::

Described as a diagram, the AR curve slopes downwards (it is the firm's demand curve), and the MR curve lies below it and falls more steeply.

## How sales affect revenue

Total revenue depends on two things: the price and the quantity sold. A change in either changes revenue.

- **More sales at the same price** raise TR. A firm can sell more through advertising, better quality, new markets (for example exporting to SADC neighbours) or because incomes rise.
- **A price change** affects TR in two opposite ways: a lower price earns less on each unit but may sell more units. Which effect wins depends on the **price elasticity of demand**. If demand is elastic, a price cut raises TR; if demand is inelastic, a price cut lowers TR.

:::example{title="Will a price cut raise revenue?"}
A fictional bakery in Masvingo cuts the price of its buns from US\$2.50 to US\$2.00 a pack, and weekly sales rise from 800 to 1,100 packs. TR before: $2.50 \times 800 = 2{,}000$, so US\$2,000. TR after: $2.00 \times 1{,}100 = 2{,}200$, so US\$2,200. Revenue rises by US\$200 because the percentage rise in sales (37.5%) is bigger than the percentage cut in price (20%): demand is price elastic. Higher revenue does not prove higher profit, though: the bakery now has to bake 300 more packs, which raises its costs.
:::

Revenue also changes when **demand** changes for other reasons. A rise in incomes, a successful advertising campaign or a rival closing shifts demand to the right, so the firm can sell more at each price. A new competitor, a fall in incomes or a change in tastes shifts demand to the left.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing revenue with profit"}
Revenue is income from sales. Profit is revenue minus costs. A firm can have high revenue and still make a loss.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to divide by the change in quantity"}
If quantity rises from 200 to 300 units, MR per unit is the change in TR divided by 100, not the change in TR itself.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a price cut always raises revenue"}
It raises revenue only if demand is price elastic. With inelastic demand, a price cut reduces total revenue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating AR and price as different things"}
When all units are sold at the same price, AR **is** the price. That is why the AR curve is the firm's demand curve.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate** questions: write the formula first ("TR = P × Q"), then the figures, then the answer with its unit. For MR, show the subtraction and the division by the change in quantity.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** how sales affect revenue: link price, quantity and price elasticity in a chain ("demand is elastic, so the percentage rise in sales is greater than the percentage fall in price, so TR rises").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a firm should cut its price to raise revenue: weigh the elasticity of demand, the reaction of rivals and the extra costs of producing more, and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Total revenue = price × quantity sold; average revenue = TR ÷ quantity = price.
- Marginal revenue (ZIMSEC) = change in TR ÷ change in quantity.
- For a price taker, price = AR = MR at every output, and TR rises in a straight line.
- A firm that must cut its price to sell more has a downward-sloping AR curve and an MR curve below it.
- Revenue rises with higher sales at a given price; the effect of a price change depends on price elasticity of demand.
- Revenue is not profit: costs must be subtracted.
:::
