## Introduction

Sipho makes solar lamps in a small workshop in Bulawayo. Selling one more lamp a day brings in extra revenue, but making it also adds to his costs. Should he make one more? The answer depends on comparing the **extra** revenue with the **extra** cost. This way of thinking "at the margin" is how economists explain the output a firm chooses, and it is the basis of the theory of market structures that follows.

This lesson is examined by ZIMSEC only (Form 4). You need to explain **marginal cost**, apply the profit-maximising rule **MR = MC**, describe **normal** and **abnormal** profit and the break-even condition **AR = AC**, and explain **economic efficiency**. Paper 2 data-response questions often give a table of output, revenue and cost and ask you to find the profit-maximising output.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marginal cost (MC)"}
The change in total cost when one more unit of output is produced: $MC = \Delta TC \div \Delta Q$. Because fixed cost does not change, MC is also the change in total variable cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
Total revenue minus total cost: $\text{profit} = TR - TC$. Per unit, profit is $AR - ATC$.
:::

:::definition{term="Normal profit"}
The minimum profit needed to keep the entrepreneur in this business, equal to what they could earn in the next best use of their time and capital. Economists count it as a cost, so a firm earning only normal profit has $TR = TC$.
:::

:::definition{term="Abnormal (supernormal) profit"}
Profit above normal profit: total revenue is greater than total cost, including normal profit ($AR > ATC$).
:::

:::definition{term="Break-even"}
The output at which total revenue equals total cost, so the firm makes normal profit only: $AR = AC$.
:::

## The profit-maximising rule: MR = MC

Think of each extra unit separately.

- If the **marginal revenue** of a unit is greater than its **marginal cost**, making it adds to profit (or reduces a loss). The firm should make it.
- If MR is less than MC, making it reduces profit. The firm should not make it.
- Profit is therefore at its maximum where **MR = MC**: the last unit made adds as much to revenue as to cost.

In a table, where MR and MC rarely match exactly, the rule means: keep producing every unit for which MR is greater than (or equal to) MC, and stop before the first unit for which MR is less than MC. Always check by working out $TR - TC$.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: finding the profit-maximising output]
Sipho's fictional lamp workshop must lower its price to sell more lamps a day.

| Output (lamps) | Price (US\$) | TR (US\$) | TC (US\$) | MR (US\$) | MC (US\$) | Profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 30 | 0 | 20 | – | – | −20 |
| 1 | 28 | 28 | 40 | 28 | 20 | −12 |
| 2 | 26 | 52 | 54 | 24 | 14 | −2 |
| 3 | 24 | 72 | 66 | 20 | 12 | 6 |
| 4 | 22 | 88 | 80 | 16 | 14 | 8 |
| 5 | 20 | 100 | 100 | 12 | 20 | 0 |
| 6 | 18 | 108 | 126 | 8 | 26 | −18 |

1. TR = price × output, for example $22 \times 4 = 88$.
2. MR of the 4th lamp $= 88 - 72 = 16$; MC of the 4th lamp $= 80 - 66 = 14$.
3. MR > MC for the 1st to the 4th lamp, so each of them adds to profit.
4. For the 5th lamp MR = 12 but MC = 20: it would **reduce** profit by US\$8.
5. So the profit-maximising output is **4 lamps**, where profit $= 88 - 80 = 8$, so US\$8 a day. MR = MC lies between the 4th and 5th lamps.
6. Note that the output with the highest revenue (6 lamps) is not the output with the highest profit.
:::

Described as a diagram, with costs and revenue per unit on the vertical axis and output on the horizontal axis, the MC curve falls and then rises steeply, the MR curve slopes downwards, and the profit-maximising output is where the rising MC curve cuts the MR curve.

## Normal profit, abnormal profit and losses

Comparing average revenue with average total cost at the chosen output tells you what kind of profit a firm makes. Remember that ATC includes normal profit.

- **AR > ATC**: abnormal (supernormal) profit. Total abnormal profit $= (AR - ATC) \times Q$.
- **AR = ATC**: normal profit only. The firm **breaks even**.
- **AR < ATC**: a loss (sub-normal profit). The owners would earn more elsewhere.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: three firms compared]
Three fictional firms each produce at their profit-maximising output.

| Firm | Output (units) | AR (US\$) | ATC (US\$) | TR (US\$) | TC (US\$) | Profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Firm X | 200 | 15 | 12 | 3,000 | 2,400 | 600 |
| Firm Y | 150 | 10 | 10 | 1,500 | 1,500 | 0 |
| Firm Z | 300 | 8 | 9 | 2,400 | 2,700 | −300 |

1. Firm X: $AR > ATC$. Abnormal profit $= (15 - 12) \times 200 = 600$, so US\$600.
2. Firm Y: $AR = ATC$. It breaks even and earns normal profit only. This is not a disaster: normal profit is already counted in its costs, so the owner is earning as much as in the next best use.
3. Firm Z: $AR < ATC$. Loss $= (9 - 8) \times 300 = 300$, so US\$300.
:::

Why do these differences matter? Abnormal profit acts as a **signal**. If firms can enter the market, abnormal profit attracts new firms, which increases supply and pushes the price down until only normal profit is left. Losses work the other way: firms leave the market in the long run, supply falls and the price rises. Whether entry is possible depends on the market structure, which you study next.

## Should a loss-making firm close at once?

In the short run a firm that makes a loss may still be better off producing. Its fixed costs (rent, loan interest) must be paid even if it closes. If its revenue covers its **variable** costs and something towards the fixed costs, producing loses less than closing. If revenue does not even cover variable costs ($AR < AVC$), it should stop production at once. In the long run every cost is variable, so a firm that cannot cover its total costs will leave the industry.

## Economic efficiency

**Productive efficiency** means producing at the lowest possible average total cost, so the fewest resources are used for each unit. In a cost table it is the output where ATC is at its minimum. In a diagram, it is where the MC curve cuts the ATC curve at the lowest point of ATC.

**Allocative efficiency** means producing the combination of goods that consumers want most, so that resources go where they are valued most. A firm is allocatively efficient when **price = marginal cost**: the value consumers place on the last unit (its price) equals the cost of the resources used to make it. If price is above MC, consumers would value more output than is being produced.

**Economic efficiency** is achieved when both are present. Market structures differ in how close they come: perfect competition achieves both in the long run, while a monopoly usually achieves neither.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing the output with the highest revenue or lowest cost"}
Profit is greatest where MR = MC, not where TR is greatest or ATC is lowest. In Worked example 1, revenue is highest at 6 lamps, but profit is highest at 4.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying normal profit means no profit"}
Normal profit is a real return to the entrepreneur and is counted as a cost. A firm earning normal profit has TR = TC in the economist's sense but is still rewarding its owner.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up MC and ATC"}
MC is the cost of one **more** unit; ATC is the cost **per unit** of all output. Compare MR with MC to choose output, and AR with ATC to judge the type of profit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate** MR and MC from a table by subtracting the row above, then **identify** the profit-maximising output and check with TR − TC.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** the rule MR = MC in steps: units with MR > MC add to profit; units with MR < MC reduce it; so profit is greatest where they are equal.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Distinguish** normal from abnormal profit by referring to AR and ATC, and explain the role of abnormal profit as a signal for entry.
:::

:::exam-tip
In **discuss** questions on efficiency, define productive and allocative efficiency precisely before judging a market structure.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- MC = change in TC from one more unit; MR = change in TR from one more unit.
- Profit is maximised where MR = MC: produce every unit with MR > MC and none with MR < MC.
- AR > ATC: abnormal profit; AR = ATC: normal profit (break-even); AR < ATC: a loss.
- Normal profit is the minimum return that keeps the entrepreneur in the industry and is counted as a cost.
- Productive efficiency: lowest ATC. Allocative efficiency: price = MC.
:::
