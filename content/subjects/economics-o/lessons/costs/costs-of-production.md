## Introduction

Chipo runs a small bakery in Gweru. Every week she pays rent for the shop, repayments on the loan for her oven and an insurance premium, whether she bakes one loaf or a thousand. She also buys flour, yeast, sugar and gas, and the more she bakes, the more of these she needs. To set a sensible price, and to know whether baking more will help or hurt her business, Chipo needs to understand her **costs of production**: which costs change with output, which do not, and what each loaf costs her on average.

Costs are the starting point for the whole theory of the firm. Both boards ask you to define and calculate fixed, variable, total and average costs from a table and to describe how they change as output rises. ZIMSEC also examines **semi-variable costs** (Form 2) and the difference between the **short run** and the **long run** (Form 3). Paper 2 data-response questions often give a cost table with gaps for you to fill.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fixed costs (total fixed cost, TFC)"}
Costs that do not change when the firm's output changes in the short run; they must be paid even when output is zero. Examples: rent, insurance, loan interest, salaries of permanent managers.
:::

:::definition{term="Variable costs (total variable cost, TVC)"}
Costs that change directly with output; they are zero when output is zero. Examples: raw materials, fuel used in production, packaging, wages paid per unit or per hour.
:::

:::definition{term="Semi-variable costs"}
Costs that have a fixed part and a variable part, such as an electricity or telephone bill made up of a fixed monthly charge plus a charge for each unit used (ZIMSEC).
:::

:::definition{term="Total cost (TC)"}
The cost of producing a given level of output: total fixed cost plus total variable cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Average total cost (ATC)"}
Total cost per unit of output: total cost divided by output. It is also called average cost (AC) or unit cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Average fixed cost (AFC) and average variable cost (AVC)"}
Fixed cost per unit (TFC divided by output) and variable cost per unit (TVC divided by output).
:::

## The formulas

$$TC = TFC + TVC$$

$$ATC = \frac{TC}{\text{output}} \qquad AFC = \frac{TFC}{\text{output}} \qquad AVC = \frac{TVC}{\text{output}}$$

It follows that $ATC = AFC + AVC$. This is useful for filling gaps in a table: if you know any two of the three averages, you can find the third.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards use total, fixed, variable, average fixed, average variable and average total cost. Average total cost is often shortened to "average cost". Semi-variable costs and marginal cost are examined by ZIMSEC; Cambridge does not ask for them.
:::

## Calculating costs from a table

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a bakery's weekly costs]
Chipo's fictional bakery has fixed costs of US\$600 a week. The table shows her variable costs at different weekly outputs of loaves.

| Output (units) | TFC (US\$) | TVC (US\$) | TC (US\$) | AFC (US\$) | AVC (US\$) | ATC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 600 | 0 | 600 | – | – | – |
| 200 | 600 | 300 | 900 | 3.00 | 1.50 | 4.50 |
| 400 | 600 | 500 | 1,100 | 1.50 | 1.25 | 2.75 |
| 600 | 600 | 750 | 1,350 | 1.00 | 1.25 | 2.25 |
| 800 | 600 | 1,080 | 1,680 | 0.75 | 1.35 | 2.10 |
| 1,000 | 600 | 1,600 | 2,200 | 0.60 | 1.60 | 2.20 |

1. Total cost at 600 loaves: $TC = 600 + 750 = 1{,}350$, so US\$1,350.
2. Average fixed cost at 600 loaves: $600 \div 600 = 1.00$, so US\$1.00 per loaf.
3. Average variable cost at 600 loaves: $750 \div 600 = 1.25$, so US\$1.25 per loaf.
4. Average total cost at 600 loaves: $1{,}350 \div 600 = 2.25$, so US\$2.25 per loaf. Check: $AFC + AVC = 1.00 + 1.25 = 2.25$.
5. At zero output total cost is US\$600: the fixed cost must still be paid. Averages cannot be calculated at zero output.
6. ATC is lowest (US\$2.10) at 800 loaves. This is the most efficient output of the bakery's present size.
:::

## How costs change as output rises

If you are asked to describe the cost curves, put **costs (US\$)** on the vertical axis and **output** on the horizontal axis.

- **TFC** is a horizontal straight line: it is the same at every output (US\$600 for Chipo).
- **TVC** starts at the origin (zero cost at zero output) and rises as output rises. It usually rises slowly at first, as workers and equipment are used more efficiently, and then more steeply.
- **TC** has the same shape as TVC but starts at the level of TFC on the vertical axis. The vertical gap between TC and TVC is always equal to TFC.
- **AFC** falls continuously as output rises, because the same fixed cost is spread over more units (US\$3.00, 1.50, 1.00, 0.75, 0.60). It gets closer and closer to the horizontal axis but never reaches it.
- **AVC** and **ATC** are usually **U-shaped**: they fall at first, reach a minimum and then rise. In the table ATC falls from US\$4.50 to US\$2.10 and then rises to US\$2.20.
- Because $ATC = AFC + AVC$, the gap between the ATC and AVC curves is AFC, so the two curves get closer together as output rises.

![Total cost curves of Chipo's bakery: total fixed cost is a horizontal line at US\$600; total variable cost starts at zero and reaches US\$1,600 at 1,000 loaves; total cost starts at US\$600 and reaches US\$2,200, always exactly US\$600 above total variable cost.](media:diagrams/bakery-total-cost-curves.png "Figure: TFC is flat, TVC starts at the origin and TC sits TFC above TVC")

Why does average cost eventually rise? In the short run some factors, such as the size of the bakery and the number of ovens, are fixed. As Chipo adds more workers and ingredients to the same ovens, each extra worker adds less output (diminishing returns), so the cost of each extra loaf rises, and eventually the average rises too.

## Semi-variable costs (ZIMSEC)

Many bills do not fit neatly into "fixed" or "variable". A mobile phone account, a water bill or an electricity bill often has a **standing charge** that is paid every month plus a charge for each unit used. A delivery van's costs include insurance (fixed) and fuel (variable). These are **semi-variable** costs.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a phone repair shop]
Farai runs a fictional phone repair shop in Mutare. Each month he pays rent of US\$300, an electricity bill of a US\$50 standing charge plus US\$0.50 for each phone repaired (the soldering and testing equipment), and US\$5 of parts per phone. In March he repairs 200 phones.

1. Fixed cost: rent = US\$300.
2. Semi-variable cost (electricity): $50 + 0.50 \times 200 = 150$, so US\$150.
3. Variable cost (parts): $5 \times 200 = 1{,}000$, so US\$1,000.
4. Total cost: $300 + 150 + 1{,}000 = 1{,}450$, so US\$1,450.
5. Average total cost: $1{,}450 \div 200 = 7.25$, so US\$7.25 per phone.
6. For a total, the fixed part of the electricity bill (US\$50) joins the fixed costs (US\$350 in all) and the part that varies joins the variable costs ($0.50 + 5 = 5.50$ per phone): $350 + 5.50 \times 200 = 1{,}450$, the same answer.
:::

## The short run and the long run

The **short run** is the period in which at least one factor of production is fixed. Chipo can hire more bakers and buy more flour this month, but she cannot quickly move to a larger shop or install a second oven. In the short run there are both fixed and variable costs.

The **long run** is the period in which **all** factors of production can be changed. Given enough time Chipo can rent a bigger building, buy more ovens or close the business. In the long run there are no fixed costs: every cost is variable. The length of the long run differs between industries: a street vendor can change scale in days, while a platinum mine may need years.

In the long run a firm chooses its scale. Growing larger may lower average cost (economies of scale) or eventually raise it (diseconomies of scale); you meet these in the lesson on economies of scale.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding the average costs to get total cost"}
Total cost is TFC + TVC. Averages are found by dividing totals by output. To go from an average back to a total, multiply by output: $TC = ATC \times \text{output}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fixed costs never change"}
Fixed costs do not change **with output** in the short run. The landlord can still raise the rent; that changes fixed cost at every output, but not because output changed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying average fixed cost is constant"}
Total fixed cost is constant; average fixed cost **falls** as output rises because the same amount is spread over more units.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all labour costs fixed or all labour costs variable"}
Wages paid per hour or per unit to production workers are variable; salaries of permanent managers are fixed. Read the context.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate** questions: write the formula, substitute, and give the unit ("ATC = 1,350 ÷ 600 = US\$2.25 per loaf"). Check with ATC = AFC + AVC.
:::

:::exam-tip
**State** or **identify** a fixed or variable cost: give one that fits the firm in the question (rent for a shop, seed and fertiliser for a farm).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** how average fixed cost changes with output: the same total is spread over more units, so each unit carries less fixed cost. **Describe** a cost diagram by naming the axes, each curve and its shape.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** questions on costs (for example whether a firm should expand output) need both sides: lower AFC and spreading of overheads against rising AVC once the fixed factors are crowded.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run; variable costs change with output and are zero at zero output; semi-variable costs have a fixed and a variable part (ZIMSEC).
- TC = TFC + TVC; ATC = TC ÷ output; AFC = TFC ÷ output; AVC = TVC ÷ output; ATC = AFC + AVC.
- TFC is a horizontal line; TVC starts at the origin; TC is TVC lifted up by TFC; AFC falls continuously; AVC and ATC are U-shaped.
- In the short run at least one factor is fixed; in the long run all factors, and so all costs, are variable.
:::
