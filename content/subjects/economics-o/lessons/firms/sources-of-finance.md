## Introduction

Nyasha's fictional transport business in Gweru has grown from one pick-up truck to three, and she now needs a larger delivery truck. A large manufacturing company in Harare wants to build a new factory. A small-scale farmer needs money to buy seed and fertiliser before the rains, but will not be paid until after the harvest. All three need **finance** (capital), but in different amounts, for different lengths of time and with different risks. The way a firm raises money affects how much it pays in interest, who controls the business and how risky its position becomes.

This lesson is examined by ZIMSEC (Form 4: methods of raising finance such as shares, debentures and leasing). Questions ask you to describe the sources of finance, give their advantages and disadvantages and recommend a suitable source for a firm in a given situation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal finance"}
Money raised from within the business, such as retained profits or the sale of assets the firm no longer needs.
:::

:::definition{term="External finance"}
Money raised from outside the business, such as loans, overdrafts, shares, debentures, leasing, hire purchase and grants.
:::

:::definition{term="Short-term finance"}
Finance needed for less than about one year, usually for working capital such as stock and wages (overdrafts, trade credit).
:::

:::definition{term="Long-term finance"}
Finance needed for several years, usually to buy fixed assets such as buildings and machinery (shares, debentures, long-term loans).
:::

:::definition{term="Collateral (security)"}
An asset the borrower pledges to the lender, which the lender can sell if the loan is not repaid.
:::

## Internal sources

- **Retained profits:** profit kept in the business instead of being paid to the owners. It has no interest cost and does not have to be repaid, and no control is given away. But it is available only to profitable firms, may not be enough for large projects, and the owners receive less now (lower dividends). There is still an opportunity cost: the money could have earned interest elsewhere.
- **Owner's savings** (for sole traders and partnerships) and the **sale of unwanted assets**, such as an old vehicle.

## External sources

### Shares

Only limited companies can issue shares. **Ordinary shares** carry a vote at shareholders' meetings and a dividend that depends on profit. **Preference shares** usually pay a fixed dividend, which is paid before ordinary shareholders receive anything, but usually carry no vote.

- **Advantages:** large sums can be raised (especially by a public company); the money never has to be repaid; no interest is payable and dividends can be cut in a bad year.
- **Disadvantages:** new shareholders share ownership, profits and (with ordinary shares) control; issuing shares is costly; a public company may be taken over if someone buys enough shares.

### Debentures

A **debenture** is a long-term loan to a company, usually from many investors, who receive a certificate. The company pays a **fixed rate of interest** every year, whether or not it makes a profit, and repays the debenture on a set date. Debentures are often secured on the company's assets. Debenture holders are **creditors**, not owners: they have no vote.

- **Advantages:** large, long-term sums; no loss of control; interest is fixed, so planning is easier.
- **Disadvantages:** interest must be paid even in a bad year; the loan must be repaid; if the company fails to pay, the debenture holders can claim its assets.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the cost of debentures]
A fictional company issues 2,000 debentures of US\$100 each at 9% interest a year.

1. Amount raised: $2{,}000 \times 100 = 200{,}000$, so US\$200,000.
2. Interest each year: $200{,}000 \times 9 \div 100 = 18{,}000$, so US\$18,000.
3. This US\$18,000 must be paid every year, even in a year when the company makes a loss, until the debentures are repaid. If instead it had issued shares and the year was poor, it could have paid a lower dividend or none at all, but the new shareholders would own part of the company and share its future profits.
:::

### Bank loans and overdrafts

A **bank loan** is a fixed sum borrowed for a fixed period and repaid in instalments with interest. It suits the purchase of equipment or buildings. The bank usually wants collateral, and interest must be paid whatever happens to profit. **Microfinance** institutions offer small loans to small businesses and individuals, often at high interest.

An **overdraft** lets a firm take out more money than it has in its current account, up to an agreed limit. Interest is paid only on the amount overdrawn and only for as long as it is overdrawn. It is flexible and good for short-term cash shortages (paying wages before customers pay), but interest rates are high and the bank can demand repayment at short notice.

### Trade credit

Suppliers allow the firm to receive goods now and pay later, for example after 30 days. It is cheap short-term finance, but the firm may lose discounts for prompt payment, and late payment damages its reputation.

### Hire purchase and leasing

**Hire purchase (HP):** the firm pays a deposit and then regular instalments; it uses the asset straight away and **owns it after the last instalment**. It avoids paying a large sum at once, but the total paid is higher than the cash price, and the asset can be repossessed if instalments are missed.

**Leasing:** the firm rents an asset (vehicles, machinery, office equipment) for an agreed period and **never owns it**. Repairs and maintenance are often the leasing company's responsibility, and equipment can be replaced with newer models. But over a long period leasing can cost more than buying, and the firm has no asset to show at the end.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: loan, hire purchase or leasing?]
Nyasha needs a delivery truck with a cash price of US\$18,000 for the next two years.

| Method | Total paid over 2 years (US\$) | Owns the truck at the end? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Bank loan (10% a year simple interest) | 21,600 | Yes |
| Hire purchase (deposit + 24 instalments) | 21,840 | Yes, after the last instalment |
| Leasing (24 monthly payments) | 14,400 | No |

1. Bank loan: interest $= 18{,}000 \times 10 \div 100 \times 2 = 3{,}600$; total $= 18{,}000 + 3{,}600 = 21{,}600$.
2. Hire purchase: deposit US\$3,600 plus 24 instalments of US\$760: $3{,}600 + 24 \times 760 = 21{,}840$. That is $21{,}840 - 18{,}000 = 3{,}840$ more than the cash price.
3. Leasing: $24 \times 600 = 14{,}400$, but she does not own the truck at the end.
4. Leasing has the lowest payments, but she would have no truck after two years. The loan is the cheapest way to own it, but needs collateral; hire purchase needs no collateral beyond the truck itself but costs a little more.
:::

### Grants

Governments, development agencies and non-governmental organisations sometimes give **grants** to firms, for example to start businesses in particular areas or sectors, train workers or adopt new technology. Grants usually do not have to be repaid, but they are limited, competitive and come with conditions.

## Choosing a source of finance

- **Purpose and time period:** long-term assets need long-term finance (shares, debentures, loans); short-term needs suit overdrafts and trade credit.
- **Amount needed:** large sums may require a share issue or debentures; small sums may come from retained profit or an overdraft.
- **Cost:** interest rates, the cost of issuing shares, the higher total paid under hire purchase.
- **Control:** issuing ordinary shares dilutes the owners' control; loans and debentures do not.
- **Risk:** loans and debentures must be repaid with interest even when profit falls; too much debt can threaten survival.
- **Legal form of the business:** sole traders and partnerships cannot issue shares; only public companies can sell shares to the public.
- **Security:** can the firm offer collateral?

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling debenture holders owners"}
Debenture holders are lenders (creditors). Shareholders are owners.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying leasing and hire purchase are the same"}
With hire purchase the firm owns the asset after the last payment; with leasing it never does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying retained profit is free"}
It has no interest cost, but it has an opportunity cost: the owners receive lower dividends now, and the money could have earned interest elsewhere.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe** a source by saying what it is, who provides it and whether it must be repaid with interest.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Recommend** a source for the firm in the question using the factors above: purpose, amount, cost, control, risk and legal form. A sole trader cannot issue shares.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** shares against debentures or loans: control and no repayment against fixed interest and keeping control, and conclude for the firm described.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Internal finance: retained profits, owner's savings, sale of assets. External finance: shares, debentures, loans, overdrafts, trade credit, hire purchase, leasing, grants.
- Shares are never repaid but share ownership and control; debentures and loans keep control but carry fixed interest and must be repaid.
- Overdrafts and trade credit are short-term; shares, debentures and long-term loans finance fixed assets.
- Hire purchase leads to ownership after the last instalment; leasing never does.
- The choice depends on purpose, amount, cost, control, risk, security and the legal form of the business.
:::
