## Introduction

When you switch on a light in Harare, catch a train in Bulawayo or pay a water bill in Mutare, you are probably dealing with a firm owned by the government. When you buy bread, a phone or a bus ticket on a privately owned kombi, you are dealing with a firm owned by private individuals. Every economy contains both kinds of firm. Zimbabwe, like most countries, has a large **private sector** and a number of **public corporations**, often called **parastatals**, such as ZESA Holdings (electricity) and the National Railways of Zimbabwe.

Both boards ask you to distinguish private-sector from public-sector firms. ZIMSEC (Form 3) also asks you to describe **public corporations** and their advantages and disadvantages; Cambridge asks you to classify firms as private or public sector. Questions often ask whether a service should be run by the government or by private firms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Private sector"}
The part of the economy owned and controlled by private individuals and groups, such as sole traders, partnerships, co-operatives and companies. Most private-sector firms aim to make a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Public sector"}
The part of the economy owned and controlled by the government (central or local), including government departments, local councils and public corporations.
:::

:::definition{term="Public corporation (parastatal)"}
A business owned by the state, set up by an Act of Parliament or under government authority, that sells goods or services and is run by a board appointed by the government.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalisation and privatisation"}
Nationalisation is the transfer of a firm from private ownership to state ownership. Privatisation is the transfer of a firm or activity from the public sector to the private sector.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC uses **public corporations** or **parastatals**; Cambridge 0455 uses **public sector firms**. They mean the same kind of organisation: a state-owned firm that sells its output.
:::

## Private-sector and public-sector firms compared

| Feature | Private-sector firm | Public-sector firm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owned by | Private individuals or shareholders | The government, on behalf of the people |
| Main aim | Usually profit | Usually social welfare: providing a service to all at a fair price |
| Controlled by | Owners or directors elected by shareholders | A board appointed by the responsible minister |
| Finance | Owners' capital, shares, loans, retained profit | Government funds, loans guaranteed by the state, revenue from sales |
| Profits or losses | Profits go to owners; losses borne by owners | Surpluses go to the government or are reinvested; losses are covered by the taxpayer |
| Accountable to | Owners and customers | The minister, Parliament and the public |

## Features of public corporations

- **State ownership:** the government owns the corporation on behalf of the public; there are no private shareholders (unless partly privatised).
- **Set up by law:** usually created by an Act of Parliament that states its purpose and powers.
- **Run by a board:** the minister appoints a board of directors and a chief executive to manage day-to-day affairs.
- **Separate legal entity:** it can own property, make contracts, sue and be sued in its own name.
- **Finance:** it earns revenue by selling its services (electricity, water, rail transport) and may receive grants or loans from the government.
- **Aims:** to provide essential services to everyone at reasonable prices, often in areas where private firms would not operate.

## Why governments own firms

- **Essential services:** water, electricity and rail transport are vital to households and industry; the government wants them supplied to everyone.
- **Natural monopolies:** in some industries, such as power transmission lines or railways, one supplier is cheapest. A private monopoly might charge high prices, so the state runs it in the public interest.
- **Strategic industries:** industries important for national security or development (energy, communications) may be kept under national control.
- **Social objectives:** serving rural or poor areas, keeping prices affordable and protecting jobs.
- **Large capital needs:** some projects, such as dams and railways, cost more than private investors are willing to risk.

:::worked-solution[Worked example: the cost of keeping fares low]
A fictional state-owned bus company in a southern African country carries 16 million passenger journeys a year. The government requires it to keep fares low so that workers on low incomes can travel.

| Item | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Fare revenue | 4.0 |
| Operating costs | 4.8 |
| Loss covered by the government | 0.8 |

1. Loss: $4.8 - 4.0 = 0.8$, so US\$0.8 million, paid from government revenue (taxpayers).
2. Average fare: $4.0 \div 16 = 0.25$, so US\$0.25 a journey.
3. Fare needed to cover costs: $4.8 \div 16 = 0.30$, so US\$0.30 a journey.
4. Subsidy per journey: $0.8 \div 16 = 0.05$, so US\$0.05.
5. The company could break even by raising fares by 5 US cents, but passengers on low incomes would pay more. The subsidy is the **opportunity cost** of the social objective: US\$0.8 million that could have been spent on schools or clinics.
:::

## Advantages of public corporations

- Essential services can be provided to all, including remote and poor areas that a profit-seeking firm might ignore.
- Prices can be kept affordable, and a natural monopoly cannot exploit consumers for private profit.
- Large, long-term investments (power stations, railways) can be financed.
- Any surplus goes to the government and can benefit the whole country.
- Jobs can be protected and strategic industries kept under national control.

## Disadvantages of public corporations

- **Inefficiency:** without the profit motive or competition, managers may have little incentive to cut costs or improve quality.
- **Losses** are covered by the government, which means higher taxes, more borrowing or less spending on other services.
- **Political interference:** decisions on prices, jobs or investment may be made for political rather than economic reasons.
- **Bureaucracy:** decisions can be slow because of rules and reporting to ministries.
- **Poor service:** with no competition, consumers have nowhere else to go.

## Advantages and disadvantages of private-sector firms

Private firms have a strong incentive to be efficient, innovate and respond to consumers, because profit depends on it, and they do not need taxpayers' money. However, they may ignore unprofitable customers, charge high prices where they have market power, and pay less attention to pollution or social goals. This is why many governments **privatise** some state firms to improve efficiency while **regulating** them to protect consumers.

:::example{title="Classifying firms"}
In a fictional town: a family bakery (private sector, sole trader or partnership), a mobile network company with shares listed on the stock exchange (private sector, public limited company), the municipal water department (public sector, local government) and the state-owned power utility (public sector, public corporation). Note that a **public limited company** is in the **private** sector: "public" refers only to the sale of its shares to the public.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a public limited company a public-sector firm"}
A public limited company is owned by private shareholders, so it is in the private sector. A public corporation is owned by the state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying public corporations never make profits"}
Many aim to break even or make a surplus; the difference is that social objectives come first and the government covers any losses.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
Questions on public ownership need both sides: access and affordability against inefficiency and the cost to taxpayers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe** public corporations by their features: ownership, legal basis, control, finance and aims.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an advantage or disadvantage with a consequence ("there is no competition, so managers have little incentive to reduce costs, so prices may be higher than necessary").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a service should be provided by the public or private sector: weigh access, prices and social goals against efficiency and cost to taxpayers, and consider regulation as a middle way.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Private-sector firms are owned by individuals or shareholders and usually aim for profit; public-sector firms are owned by the government and usually aim for social welfare.
- Public corporations (parastatals) are state-owned, set up by law, run by a board appointed by the minister and sell their output.
- Governments own firms to provide essential services, control natural monopolies and strategic industries, and meet social objectives.
- Advantages: access for all, affordable prices, large investment. Disadvantages: inefficiency, losses paid by taxpayers, political interference, bureaucracy.
- A public limited company is in the private sector.
:::
