## Introduction

Think of three organisations in Harare: a new food stall opened last month by a school leaver, a large private company that packs and sells cooking oil, and a water utility owned by the government. All three produce something people want, but they are not trying to achieve the same thing. The food stall's owner is worried about lasting until the end of the year. The cooking-oil company wants high profits for its shareholders and a bigger share of the market. The water utility is expected to supply safe water to as many households as possible, even in areas where that loses money.

What a firm is trying to achieve is its **objective**. Objectives matter because they shape the firm's decisions about prices, output, wages and investment. Cambridge 0455 asks you to explain four objectives: **survival**, **social welfare**, **profit maximisation** and **growth**, and to explain why objectives differ between firms and over time.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Objective of a firm"}
A goal that a firm aims to achieve, which guides its decisions on output, prices, costs and investment.
:::

:::definition{term="Survival"}
Staying in business: covering costs, or at least avoiding losses large enough to force the firm to close.
:::

:::definition{term="Social welfare"}
Aiming to benefit society, for example by providing affordable services, protecting the environment, creating jobs or treating workers and communities well, rather than only making profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit maximisation"}
Aiming to make the greatest possible difference between total revenue and total cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Growth"}
Aiming to increase the size of the firm, for example its sales, output, market share, number of outlets or workforce.
:::

## The four objectives

### Survival

For a new firm, or for any firm in a crisis, the first aim is simply to stay in business. A new business has few regular customers, high start-up costs and limited cash, and many new firms close in their first years. In a recession, when demand falls, even well-established firms may focus on survival: they cut costs, delay investment and may accept low prices to keep customers. A firm aiming to survive needs enough revenue to pay its bills as they fall due.

### Social welfare

Some firms aim mainly to benefit society. Public-sector firms, such as a state-owned electricity or water supplier, may be asked to keep prices low, supply remote areas and keep people in work. Social enterprises and co-operatives may use any surplus to support members or the community. Many private firms also take on social goals, such as paying fair wages, sourcing from local farmers or reducing pollution, partly because customers and workers value this and partly to protect their reputation. A firm aiming at social welfare may accept lower profit.

### Profit maximisation

Most private-sector firms are owned by people who have put their money at risk, and they expect a return. Profit pays the owners (as dividends to shareholders, for example), provides funds for investment, and is a sign that resources are being used in ways customers value. A firm that maximises profit chooses the price and output that give the largest gap between revenue and cost. In practice firms often lack the information to find the exact profit-maximising point, so they aim for **satisfactory** profits instead.

### Growth

Many firms aim to grow. A larger firm may benefit from economies of scale (lower average costs), have more power in the market, be safer from takeover and earn higher profits in the future. Managers may also prefer growth because their salaries, status and job security rise with the size of the firm. Growth can be measured by sales revenue, output, market share, the number of branches or the number of workers. A firm can grow internally, by selling more and opening new outlets, or externally, by merging with or taking over other firms.

:::worked-solution[Worked example: profit or growth?]
A fictional juice company in Mutare can set one of two prices for its cases of juice. Its market research gives these monthly estimates.

| Plan | Price (US\$) | Cases sold per month | ATC (US\$) | TR (US\$) | TC (US\$) | Profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Plan A: higher price | 10 | 5,000 | 7 | 50,000 | 35,000 | 15,000 |
| Plan B: lower price | 8 | 8,000 | 6.50 | 64,000 | 52,000 | 12,000 |

1. Plan A: TR $= 10 \times 5{,}000 = 50{,}000$; TC $= 7 \times 5{,}000 = 35{,}000$; profit $= 15{,}000$, so US\$15,000.
2. Plan B: TR $= 8 \times 8{,}000 = 64{,}000$; TC $= 6.50 \times 8{,}000 = 52{,}000$; profit $= 12{,}000$, so US\$12,000.
3. If the whole market is 40,000 cases a month, Plan A gives a market share of $5{,}000 \div 40{,}000 \times 100 = 12.5\%$ and Plan B gives $8{,}000 \div 40{,}000 \times 100 = 20\%$.
4. A **profit-maximising** firm chooses Plan A (US\$3,000 more profit). A firm aiming for **growth** chooses Plan B: 60% more cases sold, higher revenue and a bigger market share, at the cost of lower profit now.
5. The choice depends on the objective, and Plan B might also lead to higher profits later if the larger customer base allows further cost savings.
:::

## How objectives can conflict

Objectives often pull in different directions:

- **Profit versus growth:** cutting prices or spending heavily on advertising and new branches may raise sales and market share but reduce profit now.
- **Profit versus social welfare:** paying higher wages, buying from local suppliers at fair prices or installing pollution controls raises costs and reduces profit.
- **Growth versus survival:** expanding too quickly, especially with borrowed money, can leave a firm short of cash and threaten its survival.

Different **stakeholders** also want different things. Shareholders usually want profit; managers may want growth; workers want good pay and job security; local communities want jobs and a clean environment; the government may want social goals met by public-sector firms.

## Why objectives differ between firms

- **Size and age:** a new or small firm often aims to survive; a large established firm can aim for profit or growth.
- **Ownership:** private-sector firms usually aim for profit; public-sector firms usually aim for social welfare, such as universal access to water or power at affordable prices; co-operatives aim to benefit their members.
- **Who controls the firm:** in a large company, managers rather than owners make day-to-day decisions and may prefer growth.
- **Competition:** a firm facing many rivals may have to concentrate on survival; a firm with market power can choose higher profits.

:::example{title="Objectives that change over time"}
Rudo opens a fictional bakery in Masvingo. In her first year her aim is **survival**: she must build up regular customers and pay her rent each month. Once she is making a steady profit, she aims to **maximise profit** by adjusting her prices and cutting waste. After five years she opens two new branches and supplies local schools: her aim is now **growth**. When a drought pushes up the price of wheat flour and her customers' incomes fall, she returns to a survival focus. Later, as a well-known local business, she starts training young people from the community and donates unsold bread to a children's home, adding a **social welfare** objective.
:::

## Why objectives change over time

Objectives change as the firm's situation changes: as it grows from a start-up into an established business, when economic conditions worsen (a recession or a sharp rise in costs makes survival the priority), when ownership changes (a public-sector firm that is privatised will put more weight on profit), or when new competitors enter the market.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all firms aim to maximise profit"}
Many firms do, but new firms often focus on survival, public-sector firms on social welfare and many large firms on growth. Always consider who owns and runs the firm.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating growth and profit as the same thing"}
A firm can grow its sales while its profit falls, for example by cutting prices. Growth is about size; profit is the gap between revenue and cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing an objective without explaining it"}
"The firm wants to survive" is only identification. Explain why: for example, it is new, has few customers and must pay its fixed costs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** or **state** an objective with one word or phrase; **explain** it by saying why the firm in the question would choose it and how it affects decisions.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse** a conflict between objectives as a chain: "cutting prices raises sales and market share (growth), but lowers profit per unit, so total profit may fall".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a firm should aim for profit or another objective: consider the owners, the stage of the firm, competition and stakeholders, and end with a justified conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Firms' objectives include survival, social welfare, profit maximisation and growth.
- Objectives can conflict: profit against growth, profit against social welfare, growth against survival.
- They differ between firms because of size, age, ownership, who controls the firm and competition.
- They change over time as the firm grows, as economic conditions change and when ownership changes.
:::
