## Introduction

Walk along any street in Gweru and you will see many kinds of business: a woman selling vegetables from a stall, two brothers running a panel-beating workshop, a group of farmers who market their cotton together, and branches of large companies whose shares are traded on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange. They differ in who owns them, who makes decisions, how they raise money, who takes the profit and, very importantly, who has to pay if things go wrong.

These differences are the **forms of business organisation**. ZIMSEC examines them across several forms: sole traders and partnerships (Form 1), co-operatives and the documents used to set up limited companies (Form 2), and the comparison of private and public limited companies (Form 3). Questions ask you to describe features, give advantages and disadvantages and recommend a suitable form for a business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business. If the business cannot pay, the owners' personal possessions, such as a house or car, can be taken to pay its debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Limited liability"}
The owners (shareholders) can lose only the money they invested in the business; their personal possessions are safe.
:::

:::definition{term="Separate legal entity (incorporation)"}
A company exists in law separately from its owners: it can own property, make contracts, sue and be sued in its own name, and it continues even if its shareholders change.
:::

:::definition{term="Shareholder"}
A part-owner of a company who has bought shares in it and is entitled to a share of the profits, paid as a dividend.
:::

## Sole traders

A **sole trader** (sole proprietor) is a business owned by one person, who may employ others. It is the most common form in Zimbabwe, from vendors and hairdressers to small shops and farms.

- **Advantages:** easy and cheap to set up with few legal formalities; the owner keeps all the profit; quick decisions; close contact with customers; the owner's affairs stay private.
- **Disadvantages:** unlimited liability; limited capital (only the owner's savings and small loans); long hours and no one to share the work; the business may end if the owner falls ill or dies (no continuity); the owner may lack some skills.

## Partnerships

A **partnership** is owned by two or more people (partners) who share the capital, the work, the decisions and the profits. Common examples are law firms, doctors' practices, accountants and family businesses. Partners usually sign a **partnership agreement (deed)** setting out how much capital each puts in, how profit and losses are shared, each partner's duties and what happens if a partner leaves or dies. A **sleeping (dormant) partner** invests money but takes no part in running the business.

- **Advantages:** more capital than a sole trader; partners bring different skills; work, decisions and risk are shared; still fairly easy to set up.
- **Disadvantages:** usually unlimited liability (each partner can be liable for the debts of the whole firm); disagreements between partners; profit must be shared; the partnership may end when a partner dies or leaves; capital is still limited.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: sharing a partnership's profit]
Tendai, Rudo and Sipho run a fictional engineering partnership in Bulawayo. Their agreement says profit is shared in the ratio 3 : 2 : 1, according to the capital each invested. This year's profit is US\$36,000.

| Partner | Ratio | Share of profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Tendai | 3 | 18,000 |
| Rudo | 2 | 12,000 |
| Sipho | 1 | 6,000 |
| **Total** | **6** | **36,000** |

1. Total parts: $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$.
2. Value of one part: $36{,}000 \div 6 = 6{,}000$.
3. Tendai: $3 \times 6{,}000 = 18{,}000$; Rudo: $2 \times 6{,}000 = 12{,}000$; Sipho: $1 \times 6{,}000 = 6{,}000$.
4. Check: $18{,}000 + 12{,}000 + 6{,}000 = 36{,}000$.
:::

## Co-operatives

A **co-operative** is a business owned and controlled by its members, who join together for their mutual benefit. Each member usually has **one vote**, however much capital they put in, and any surplus is shared among members, often according to how much they bought from or sold through the co-operative. Types include:

- **Producer (marketing) co-operatives:** farmers who buy inputs in bulk and sell their crops or milk together, getting better prices.
- **Consumer (retail) co-operatives:** members own a shop and share the surplus.
- **Savings and credit co-operatives:** members save together and lend to one another at low interest.

- **Advantages:** bulk buying lowers costs; members gain bargaining power; democratic control; surplus stays with members; often has limited liability once registered; builds community skills.
- **Disadvantages:** capital is limited to members' contributions; one member, one vote can slow decisions; members may lack management skills; conflicts between members; little incentive for members who invest more.

## Limited companies

A **limited company** is owned by shareholders, has limited liability and is a separate legal entity. It is run by a **board of directors** elected by the shareholders. Profit is paid to shareholders as **dividends** or kept in the business.

| Feature | Private limited company | Public limited company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name ends in | (Private) Limited, (Pvt) Ltd | Limited, Ltd |
| Selling shares | Shares cannot be offered to the general public; transfer needs the agreement of other shareholders | Shares can be offered to the public and may be traded on the stock exchange |
| Size | Usually small or medium; often family businesses | Usually large |
| Capital | Limited to what the owners and their contacts can provide | Large sums can be raised by selling shares to the public |
| Control | Owners usually run the business | Ownership (many shareholders) is separated from control (directors and managers) |
| Publicity | Accounts are less widely published | Accounts must be published; affairs are public |

**Advantages of limited companies:** limited liability makes it easier to attract investors; more capital for expansion; continuity, because the company continues when shareholders change; easier to borrow because of their size and status.

**Disadvantages:** expensive and complex to set up; more legal rules and reporting; a public company can be taken over if someone buys enough of its shares; in a public company owners and managers may want different things; decision-making is slower.

## Documents for forming a company

To form a company, the promoters register it with the **Registrar of Companies**. The traditional documents in the syllabus are:

- **Memorandum of association:** the company's relationship with the outside world: its name, the address of its registered office, its objects (what it was formed to do), a statement that the members' liability is limited, and its share capital.
- **Articles of association:** the internal rules: the powers and duties of directors, how they are appointed, how meetings are held and voting works, how profits are shared as dividends, and how shares are transferred.
- **Certificate of incorporation:** issued by the Registrar once the documents are accepted. It gives the company its separate legal identity: it now exists in law.
- **Trading certificate (certificate to commence business):** needed by a public company before it can start trading, issued once it shows that it has raised the minimum share capital required.

:::example{title="Choosing a form of organisation"}
Chipo makes peanut butter at home and sells it to neighbours. She wants to supply supermarkets, which needs US\$15,000 for machinery. Staying a **sole trader** keeps control simple, but she has only US\$3,000 and would carry unlimited liability for a large loan. A **partnership** with her cousin, who has savings and sales experience, would bring capital and skills, but they could disagree and both would still have unlimited liability. Forming a **private limited company** would protect her house through limited liability and let her sell shares to family members, but costs more to set up and involves more paperwork. Because the loan is large and the business is growing, a private limited company is probably the best choice; a public company is not suitable for a business this small.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a sole trader works alone"}
A sole trader has one **owner**, but may employ many workers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the memorandum and the articles"}
The memorandum deals with the company's external relations (name, objects, capital, limited liability); the articles are the internal rules (directors, meetings, voting, dividends).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying limited liability means the company's debts are limited"}
The company must pay all its debts from its assets. Limited liability means the **shareholders** cannot lose more than their investment.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe** a form of organisation by giving its features: ownership, liability, capital, control, profit and continuity.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Compare** forms point by point ("a sole trader has unlimited liability, whereas a shareholder in a limited company can lose only the amount invested").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Recommend** or **discuss** the best form for a business in the question: use its size, capital needs, risk and the owners' wishes, and justify the choice.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sole trader: one owner, easy to set up, keeps all profit, unlimited liability, limited capital, no continuity.
- Partnership: two or more partners share capital, skills, work and profit; usually unlimited liability; disputes possible.
- Co-operative: owned by members with one vote each; surplus shared among members; bulk buying and bargaining power.
- Limited companies: owned by shareholders, limited liability, separate legal entity, continuity; private companies cannot sell shares to the public, public companies can.
- Forming a company: memorandum and articles of association, certificate of incorporation and, for a public company, a trading certificate.
:::
