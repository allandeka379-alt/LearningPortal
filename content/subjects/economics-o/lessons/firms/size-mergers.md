## Introduction

Zimbabwe's economy has room for firms of every size. A tailor in Masvingo works alone with one sewing machine; a supermarket chain has branches in every city; a mining group employs thousands of people. Some firms stay small for their whole life, while others grow, either by selling more or by joining with other firms. When two firms join, prices, jobs, choice and competition can all change.

Both boards ask for the advantages and disadvantages of **small and large firms**, the ways firms grow, and **mergers**. Cambridge 0455 asks you to define **horizontal, vertical and conglomerate mergers** and explain their advantages and disadvantages with examples. ZIMSEC (Form 4) calls this **integration** and asks you to explain the methods of growth and the effects of vertical and horizontal integration on **stakeholders**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal (organic) growth"}
A firm grows by expanding its own activities: selling more, opening new branches, building new factories or launching new products, financed from its own profits or borrowing.
:::

:::definition{term="External growth"}
A firm grows by joining with other firms through a merger or a takeover.
:::

:::definition{term="Merger"}
Two or more firms agree to join together to form a single firm.
:::

:::definition{term="Takeover (acquisition)"}
One firm buys enough shares in another firm to gain control of it, with or without the agreement of its managers.
:::

:::definition{term="Horizontal merger (integration)"}
A merger of firms at the **same stage** of production in the same industry, for example two bakeries.
:::

:::definition{term="Vertical merger (integration)"}
A merger of firms at **different stages** of production in the same industry. **Backward** vertical integration joins a firm with a supplier (a bakery buys a flour mill); **forward** vertical integration joins a firm with a business closer to the consumer (a flour mill buys a chain of bakeries).
:::

:::definition{term="Conglomerate merger (diversification)"}
A merger of firms in **unrelated** industries, for example a brewery joining with an insurance company.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Cambridge uses **mergers** (horizontal, vertical, conglomerate). ZIMSEC usually says **integration** (horizontal and vertical integration). Lateral integration (related but non-competing products) and conglomerate integration may also appear in ZIMSEC textbooks.
:::

## Measuring the size of a firm

There is no single measure. Common measures are the **number of employees**, **capital employed** (the value of buildings, machinery and other assets), **sales revenue** and **market share**. Different measures can rank firms differently.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: which firm is largest?]
Three fictional firms in the same country.

| Firm | Employees | Capital employed (US\$ million) | Annual sales revenue (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Firm P (supermarket chain) | 900 | 12 | 54 |
| Firm Q (mining company) | 400 | 85 | 70 |
| Firm R (software firm) | 60 | 3 | 9 |

1. By employees, P is largest (900).
2. By capital employed, Q is largest (US\$85 million): mining is capital-intensive.
3. By sales revenue, Q is largest (US\$70 million).
4. A supermarket is labour-intensive and a mine capital-intensive, so the measure chosen changes the answer. It is best to use more than one measure and to compare firms in the same industry.
:::

## Small firms

Small firms are very common: hairdressers, tailors, small shops, repair workshops, small-scale farmers and many informal-sector businesses.

**Advantages of small firms:** personal service and close contact with customers; flexibility and quick decisions; they can serve small or local markets and specialised needs; low start-up costs; owners are motivated because they keep the profit; good working relationships.

**Disadvantages:** they cannot benefit from economies of scale, so average costs are often higher; finance is hard to obtain and more expensive; limited skills and management; vulnerable to the loss of a major customer or a fall in demand.

**Why small firms survive:** the market may be small or local (a village shop); consumers want personal or specialised services (tailoring, repairs); low capital is needed to enter; they supply larger firms as subcontractors; some owners prefer to stay small to keep control; governments support small firms because they create jobs.

## Large firms

**Advantages:** economies of scale (bulk buying, specialist managers, cheaper finance, advanced machinery) give lower average costs; they can afford research and development; they can spread risk across many products and markets; they find it easier to raise finance; they can compete in export markets.

**Disadvantages:** diseconomies of scale, such as poor communication and slow decisions; less personal service; less flexibility; workers may feel undervalued; market power may be used to raise prices or reduce choice.

## How firms grow

**Internal growth** is slower but lower in risk: the firm grows at its own pace and keeps its culture. **External growth** through mergers and takeovers is fast: the firm gains customers, brands, skills and capacity at once, but it can be expensive and the two businesses may be hard to combine.

### Horizontal mergers

**Reasons and advantages:** economies of scale (bulk buying, closing duplicate offices), a larger market share and less competition, a wider customer base. **Disadvantages:** fewer competitors may mean higher prices and less choice for consumers; job losses where branches are duplicated; possible diseconomies of scale.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: market shares after a horizontal merger]
A fictional country has four cement producers. Their annual sales are shown below.

| Firm | Sales (US\$ million) | Market share (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Firm A | 63 | 35.0 |
| Firm B | 45 | 25.0 |
| Firm C | 40 | 22.2 |
| Firm D | 32 | 17.8 |
| **Total** | **180** | **100** |

1. Market share = firm's sales ÷ total sales × 100. For Firm C: $40 \div 180 \times 100 = 22.2\%$ (to one decimal place).
2. If B and C merge, the new firm has sales of $45 + 40 = 85$, so a share of $85 \div 180 \times 100 = 47.2\%$.
3. The merged firm becomes the market leader, bigger than A (35.0%), and the number of competitors falls from four to three.
4. Competition authorities may investigate a merger like this because consumers could face higher cement prices.
:::

### Vertical mergers

**Backward** integration secures supplies of raw materials and their quality, and removes the supplier's profit margin. **Forward** integration secures outlets for the product and gives control over how it is sold. In both cases the firm may cut costs by co-ordinating stages of production. **Disadvantages:** the firm may enter a stage it does not understand; it may refuse to supply rivals, reducing competition; a problem at one stage affects the whole business.

### Conglomerate mergers

**Advantages:** spreading risk (diversification), so a fall in demand in one industry is balanced by others; using spare funds profitably. **Disadvantages:** managers may lack knowledge of the new industry; few economies of scale, because the activities are unrelated; the firm can become too large to manage well.

## Effects on stakeholders

- **Consumers:** may gain from lower prices if costs fall, but may lose from higher prices and less choice if competition falls.
- **Workers:** some jobs may be lost where activities overlap, but a stronger firm may offer more security and promotion.
- **Shareholders:** may gain from higher profits and a higher share price, but some mergers fail to deliver promised savings.
- **Suppliers:** a larger firm has more bargaining power and may force down the prices it pays; after backward integration, independent suppliers may lose a customer.
- **Competitors:** face a stronger rival; some may be forced out, or be refused supplies after vertical integration.
- **The government:** may gain tax revenue from a more profitable firm, but must protect consumers from monopoly power.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing horizontal and vertical"}
Horizontal: the **same** stage (bank with bank). Vertical: **different** stages of the same production chain (cotton farm with textile mill).
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming bigger is always better"}
Large firms gain economies of scale but may suffer diseconomies of scale and lose flexibility. Small firms survive for good reasons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting backward and forward"}
In vertical integration, state the direction: towards the supplier (backward) or towards the consumer (forward).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define** each type of merger and give an example from the context of the question.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse** the effect of a merger on one stakeholder with a full chain: "a horizontal merger reduces competition, so the merged firm can raise prices, so consumers pay more and have less choice".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a merger benefits consumers: weigh economies of scale (lower costs, possibly lower prices) against market power (higher prices, less choice), and conclude on what is more likely in the case given.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Firm size can be measured by employees, capital employed, sales revenue or market share.
- Small firms offer flexibility and personal service but have higher costs and harder finance; they survive in small, local or specialised markets.
- Large firms gain economies of scale and spread risk but may suffer diseconomies and use market power.
- Firms grow internally (organic growth) or externally (mergers and takeovers).
- Horizontal: same stage; vertical: different stages (backward or forward); conglomerate: unrelated industries.
- Mergers affect consumers, workers, shareholders, suppliers, competitors and the government in different ways.
:::
