## Introduction

Why can a large supermarket chain sell a 2 kg bag of sugar more cheaply than a small tuck-shop and still make a profit? Part of the answer is **economies of scale**: as a firm grows, the cost of producing each unit often falls. The chain buys sugar by the truckload at a discount, uses large warehouses and lorries efficiently and employs specialist managers. But growth is not always good for costs. Very large organisations can become slow, wasteful and hard to manage, so their average costs rise: these are **diseconomies of scale**.

Both boards examine this topic. Cambridge 0455 asks you to explain internal and external economies and diseconomies of scale and to interpret average total cost diagrams that show them; ZIMSEC (Form 4) asks you to distinguish economies from diseconomies of scale. Exam questions often ask why a firm should or should not grow, and what happens to its costs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economies of scale"}
The fall in long-run average cost that results from an increase in the scale of production (the size of the firm).
:::

:::definition{term="Diseconomies of scale"}
The rise in long-run average cost that results from a firm growing beyond a certain size.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal economies of scale"}
Cost savings that come from the growth of the firm itself.
:::

:::definition{term="External economies of scale"}
Cost savings that a firm gains from the growth of the industry or of the area in which it is located, not from its own growth.
:::

## Internal economies of scale

- **Technical economies:** large firms can use bigger, more efficient machines that small firms cannot afford or fully use; they can apply the division of labour more widely; and some equipment costs less per unit when larger (a lorry twice the size does not cost twice as much to buy or run, and needs only one driver).
- **Purchasing (bulk-buying) economies:** suppliers give discounts on large orders, and delivery costs per unit are lower.
- **Marketing economies:** the cost of an advertising campaign or a sales team is spread over more units; large firms can run their own distribution networks.
- **Financial economies:** banks see large firms as less risky, so they lend more readily and at lower interest rates; large companies can also raise money by selling shares.
- **Risk-bearing economies:** a large firm can produce many products for many markets, so a fall in demand for one is balanced by others (diversification).
- **Managerial economies:** large firms can employ specialist managers (accountants, engineers, marketing experts) who work more efficiently than one owner trying to do everything.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a purchasing economy]
A fictional snack maker buys printed packaging bags. Its supplier charges US\$0.40 a bag on orders of 2,000 bags, but US\$0.32 a bag on orders of 10,000.

1. Cost of a small order: $2{,}000 \times 0.40 = 800$, so US\$800.
2. Cost of a large order: $10{,}000 \times 0.32 = 3{,}200$, so US\$3,200.
3. Change in cost per bag: $(0.32 - 0.40) \div 0.40 \times 100 = -20\%$.
4. A firm large enough to use 10,000 bags pays 20% less per bag, so its average cost falls. This is a purchasing (bulk-buying) economy of scale.
:::

## External economies of scale

When many firms in the same industry are located in one area, all of them can benefit:

- a **pool of skilled labour** trained by other firms, so recruitment and training costs fall;
- **specialist suppliers and services** (repairers, transporters, component makers) that set up nearby;
- better **infrastructure**: roads, railways, power and communications built to serve the industry;
- **training colleges and research** centred on the industry;
- **shared information**, trade associations and a reputation for the area's products.

For example, when many tobacco farms and processors operate in one region, there are firms that supply inputs, transport leaf and repair equipment, and workers already have the skills needed.

## Diseconomies of scale

**Internal diseconomies** arise inside a firm that has grown too large:

- **Communication problems:** messages pass through many layers of management, become slow or distorted.
- **Co-ordination and control problems:** it is harder to make sure that many departments and branches work together; decisions are slow and bureaucratic.
- **Poor motivation:** workers in a huge organisation may feel unimportant, leading to low morale, absenteeism and industrial disputes.

**External diseconomies** arise when an industry or area becomes too crowded: traffic congestion delays deliveries, land and rents become expensive, competition for skilled workers pushes up wages, and pressure on water, power and other services rises.

## The long-run average cost curve

In the long run a firm can change its scale by building a larger plant. The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve shows the lowest average cost of producing each output when the firm can choose its scale.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: average cost at different plant sizes]
A fictional manufacturer can choose between five sizes of plant.

| Plant size | Output (units per year) | Total cost (US\$) | Average cost (US\$ per unit) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Plant 1 | 1,000 | 20,000 | 20 |
| Plant 2 | 3,000 | 45,000 | 15 |
| Plant 3 | 6,000 | 66,000 | 11 |
| Plant 4 | 10,000 | 100,000 | 10 |
| Plant 5 | 12,000 | 132,000 | 11 |

1. Average cost = total cost ÷ output. Plant 3: $66{,}000 \div 6{,}000 = 11$, so US\$11 per unit.
2. From Plant 1 to Plant 4 average cost falls from US\$20 to US\$10: **economies of scale**.
3. From Plant 4 to Plant 5 average cost rises from US\$10 to US\$11: **diseconomies of scale**.
4. Plant 4 (10,000 units) has the lowest average cost: it is the most efficient scale in this example.
5. Note that total cost rises at every size; it is **average** cost that falls and then rises.
:::

To describe the diagram: put **average cost** on the vertical axis and **output** on the horizontal axis. The LRAC curve slopes downwards while the firm enjoys economies of scale, reaches a minimum at the most efficient scale, and slopes upwards when diseconomies of scale set in, giving a shallow U shape. In some industries the curve falls and then stays flat over a wide range of output (an L shape), because economies are exhausted but diseconomies do not appear until the firm is very large.

![Long-run average cost curve from the five plant sizes: average cost falls from US\$20 at 1,000 units to US\$10 at 10,000 units (economies of scale), then rises to US\$11 at 12,000 units (diseconomies of scale), so the most efficient scale is 10,000 units.](media:diagrams/lrac-plant-sizes.png "Figure: the long-run average cost curve falls, reaches a minimum, then rises")

External economies and diseconomies are shown differently: they shift the **whole** average cost curve. An external economy (for example, a new railway line serving the industry) shifts the firm's average cost curve **downwards**; an external diseconomy (for example, congestion or rising rents) shifts it **upwards**.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying total cost falls"}
Economies of scale reduce **average** cost. A larger firm almost always has a higher **total** cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing economies of scale with short-run falling costs"}
Economies of scale happen in the long run, when the firm changes its size. Spreading fixed costs over more output in an existing plant is a short-run fall in average cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up internal and external"}
Internal economies come from the growth of the firm; external economies come from the growth of the industry or area, and benefit every firm there.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing types without explaining them"}
"Financial economies" alone earns little credit. Explain why: "banks see large firms as less risky, so lend at lower interest, which lowers the cost of capital per unit".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an economy of scale with a chain that ends in lower average cost: "buying flour in bulk earns a discount, so the cost of flour per loaf falls, so average cost falls".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe** or interpret an LRAC diagram by naming the axes, the falling section (economies), the minimum point (most efficient scale) and the rising section (diseconomies).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a firm should grow: weigh economies of scale against diseconomies, and consider the size of the market (a firm can only gain economies if it can sell the extra output).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as the firm grows; diseconomies of scale: it rises when the firm grows too large.
- Internal economies: technical, purchasing, marketing, financial, risk-bearing and managerial.
- External economies come from the growth of the industry or area: skilled labour, suppliers, infrastructure, training and research.
- Internal diseconomies: poor communication, co-ordination and motivation; external diseconomies: congestion, higher rents and wages.
- The LRAC curve falls, reaches the most efficient scale and then rises; external economies shift the curve down, external diseconomies shift it up.
:::
