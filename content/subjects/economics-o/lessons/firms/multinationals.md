## Introduction

Many of the brands you see in Zimbabwe, from soft drinks and mobile phones to cars and fuel, belong to firms that operate in dozens of countries. Some of the country's mines, banks, hotels and food factories are owned wholly or partly by such firms. These are **multinational companies (MNCs)**: firms that produce goods or services in more than one country.

MNCs bring investment, jobs and technology, but they can also send large profits abroad, use their size to win favourable deals and damage the environment. Governments therefore both compete to attract them and try to regulate them. ZIMSEC asks you to describe the features of MNCs and outline their costs and benefits to host countries; Cambridge asks for their advantages and disadvantages to **host** and **home** countries. Expect these points in essays and Section B discussions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Multinational company (MNC)"}
A firm that has its headquarters in one country and produces goods or services (owns or controls production facilities) in more than one country.
:::

:::definition{term="Host country"}
The country in which an MNC sets up a branch, factory, mine or office.
:::

:::definition{term="Home country"}
The country where an MNC has its headquarters and is owned and controlled from.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign direct investment (FDI)"}
Investment by a firm in one country in productive assets, such as factories, mines and farms, in another country.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **multinational corporations**; Cambridge uses **multinational companies**. Both are abbreviated to **MNCs** and mean the same thing.
:::

## Features of multinational companies

- They **operate in several countries**, with headquarters in the home country and branches or subsidiaries abroad.
- They are usually **very large**, with huge sales, capital and workforces, and benefit from **economies of scale**.
- They often produce **well-known global brands** and spend heavily on advertising and research.
- They use **advanced technology** and modern management methods.
- They organise production in **global supply chains**, locating each stage where it is cheapest or most convenient.
- They can **move production and money between countries**, for example to lower-cost locations or to where taxes are lower.
- They are usually public limited companies whose shares are traded on stock exchanges.

## Why MNCs set up abroad

- To be **close to markets** and avoid transport costs and tariffs.
- To obtain **raw materials** such as minerals, timber or farm produce.
- To use **cheaper labour** or other lower costs.
- To benefit from **government incentives** such as tax holidays or special economic zones.
- To **spread risk** across several economies and gain economies of scale.

## Advantages for the host country

- **Investment (FDI)** in factories, mines and infrastructure, which raises output and growth.
- **Employment** and training: workers gain skills and management experience.
- **Technology transfer:** local firms learn new methods and may become suppliers.
- **Exports and foreign currency**, improving the current account.
- **Tax revenue** and royalties for the government.
- **More choice and competition** for consumers.
- Development of **local suppliers** and related businesses (such as transport and catering).

## Disadvantages for the host country

- **Profits sent abroad** (repatriated) to shareholders, so much of the income leaves the country (a primary income debit).
- **Local firms may be forced out** by competition from large, efficient MNCs.
- **Low wages or poor working conditions** in some cases; top management jobs often go to foreigners.
- **Environmental damage**, such as pollution and land degradation from mining.
- **Over-use of non-renewable resources**, which are taken out of the country.
- **Tax avoidance**, for example by moving profits to low-tax countries through transfer pricing.
- **Political influence:** large MNCs may pressure governments for favourable treatment.
- **Footloose behaviour:** an MNC may leave suddenly if costs rise elsewhere, causing job losses.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: how much of an MNC's income stays in the host country?]
A fictional mining MNC operates a mine in a southern African country. One year's figures:

| Item | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Sales revenue (exports) | 500 |
| Wages paid to local workers | 60 |
| Purchases from local suppliers | 90 |
| Imported equipment and inputs | 120 |
| Taxes and royalties paid to the host government | 70 |
| Profit | 160 |
| of which reinvested in the host country | 40 |
| of which sent to shareholders abroad | 120 |

1. Profit = 500 − (60 + 90 + 120 + 70) = **US\$160 million**.
2. Money that stays in the host country = local wages + local purchases + taxes + reinvested profit = 60 + 90 + 70 + 40 = **US\$260 million**.
3. Share of revenue that stays = 260 ÷ 500 × 100 = **52%**. The rest pays for imported inputs (120) and profits sent abroad (120).
4. The host government could raise the share by encouraging more local purchasing, training local managers and negotiating fair royalties, but if it pushes too hard the MNC may invest elsewhere.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages for the home country

**Advantages:** profits flow back from subsidiaries abroad, raising income and the home country's primary income credits; the MNC grows larger and more competitive; it gains access to raw materials and markets; head-office jobs in research, design and management stay at home.

**Disadvantages:** jobs in manufacturing may be lost when production moves abroad; less investment and tax revenue at home; the home country's current account may suffer if goods once made at home are now imported from subsidiaries.

:::example{title="Weighing an MNC's investment"}
A fictional food MNC builds a juice factory in Mutare. It buys oranges from local farmers, employs 400 workers and exports juice to the region. Farmers gain a reliable buyer and workers learn food-safety skills. But two small local juice makers close, the factory's managers come from abroad, and most profits are sent to the home country. Overall, the host gains if the jobs, local purchases, skills and taxes are worth more than the losses to local firms and the profits that leave the country; the government's policies on local content and training help decide which way the balance falls.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all of an MNC's revenue benefits the host"}
Imported inputs and repatriated profits leave the country. Count only what stays: local wages, local purchases, taxes and reinvested profits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the home country"}
Cambridge questions may ask about the home country: it gains profits and head-office jobs but may lose manufacturing jobs.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
MNCs are neither simply good nor simply bad. Give advantages and disadvantages and judge them in the context of the question.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe features:** size, several countries, headquarters at home, global brands, technology, ability to move production.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** develop each point into an effect, for example "MNCs transfer technology → local firms copy methods → productivity rises".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** weigh investment, jobs, skills, exports and taxes against repatriated profits, competition with local firms, environmental damage and the risk that the MNC leaves; conclude with the conditions under which the host gains (for example, local-content rules, training requirements and environmental regulation).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An MNC produces in more than one country; it has a home country (headquarters) and host countries.
- Features: large size, operations in several countries, global brands, advanced technology, global supply chains, mobility.
- Host country gains: investment, jobs, skills, technology, exports, taxes and choice.
- Host country costs: repatriated profits, closure of local firms, environmental damage, resource depletion, tax avoidance, political influence and footloose behaviour.
- Home country: gains profits and head-office jobs, but may lose manufacturing jobs and investment.
:::
