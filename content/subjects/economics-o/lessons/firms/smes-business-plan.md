## Introduction

Much of Zimbabwe's economic activity takes place in small businesses: welders and carpenters in Magaba and Siyaso in Harare, tailors and hairdressers in every town, small-scale poultry and horticulture producers, cross-border traders and the small factories in industrial areas. These are **small and medium enterprises (SMEs)**. They provide a living for a very large number of people, many of them in the informal sector. ZIMSTAT's labour force surveys are the official source if you want figures on how many people they employ.

Starting and running an SME is risky. A **business plan** helps the owner think the idea through, and a **cash-flow projection** shows whether the business will have enough money to pay its bills each month. Lenders usually ask to see both before they lend. This lesson is examined by ZIMSEC (Form 4): you must outline the features of SMEs, describe how they survive and draw up a business plan with a cash-flow projection.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small and medium enterprises (SMEs)"}
Businesses with a small number of workers, a small amount of capital and a relatively small turnover, usually owned and managed by one person or a few people. Official definitions set limits on employees, capital and turnover that differ between sectors and countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Business plan"}
A written document that describes a business idea, its market, how it will operate, who will run it and its expected finances, used to guide the owner and to persuade lenders or investors.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash-flow projection (forecast)"}
A month-by-month estimate of the money expected to come into a business (receipts) and go out of it (payments), showing the expected bank or cash balance at the end of each month.
:::

## Features of SMEs

- A small number of employees, often family members.
- Owner-managed: the owner makes all the decisions and often does much of the work.
- Small capital, usually from personal savings, family and microfinance.
- Serve local or niche markets; flexible and close to customers.
- Many operate informally, without full registration, records or tax returns.
- Often labour-intensive with simple equipment.

## Importance of SMEs in Zimbabwe

- **Employment and incomes:** they create jobs and self-employment, especially for young people and for workers who have lost formal jobs.
- **Use of local resources and skills:** they process local materials and develop entrepreneurial skills.
- **Goods and services at low cost** for local communities, often in areas large firms do not serve.
- **Linkages:** they supply larger firms and buy from local suppliers.
- **Competition and innovation:** they keep markets competitive and try new ideas.
- **Seedbed for growth:** some SMEs grow into large firms.
- **Government revenue** where they are registered and pay taxes and licence fees.

## Problems facing SMEs

- **Finance:** banks see them as risky; they lack collateral and records, so loans are hard to get and expensive.
- **Infrastructure:** power cuts, water shortages and poor roads raise costs.
- **Workspace:** lack of affordable, secure premises.
- **Markets:** small, crowded markets with many sellers of the same product; competition from imports and large firms.
- **Skills:** limited management, book-keeping and marketing skills.
- **Regulation:** licences, permits and taxes can be costly and slow to obtain for a small business.
- **Inflation and currency changes** that make planning difficult.

## How SMEs survive

- Finding a **niche**: specialised or personal products and services that large firms do not offer.
- Forming **co-operatives or associations** to buy in bulk, share equipment and market together.
- Working in **clusters** (for example, metal workers in one area), sharing suppliers, skills and customers.
- **Microfinance**, savings clubs and family support for finance.
- **Subcontracting** to larger firms.
- Keeping **costs low** and using **mobile money** and social media to reach customers.
- Taking part in **training** offered by government agencies, colleges and NGOs, and keeping proper records.

## The parts of a business plan

1. **Executive summary:** a short overview of the whole plan.
2. **Description of the business:** the product or service, its legal form, location and objectives.
3. **Market research and analysis:** the target customers, the size of the market and the competitors.
4. **Marketing plan:** price, promotion, place (distribution) and how the product differs from rivals'.
5. **Operations plan:** premises, equipment, suppliers and production methods.
6. **Management and staffing:** who runs the business, their skills and the workers needed.
7. **Financial plan:** start-up costs, sources of finance, projected sales and profit, and a **cash-flow projection**.
8. **Finance required:** how much money is needed, what it is for and how it will be repaid.

## Cash-flow projections

A cash-flow projection is set out month by month:

- **Opening balance:** cash at the start of the month (the closing balance of the previous month).
- **Receipts:** money coming in: cash sales, payments from credit customers, loans, the owner's capital.
- **Payments:** money going out: stock and raw materials, wages, rent, electricity, equipment, loan repayments.
- **Net cash flow** = total receipts − total payments.
- **Closing balance** = opening balance + net cash flow.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a poultry start-up]
Farai plans a fictional broiler business near Chinhoyi. He has US\$1,500 of savings and a US\$1,000 microfinance loan.

| (US\$) | Month 1 | Month 2 | Month 3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Opening balance | 1,500 | 250 | 680 |
| Sales of broilers | 0 | 1,400 | 1,600 |
| Loan from microfinance | 1,000 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total receipts** | 1,000 | 1,400 | 1,600 |
| Chicks and feed | 900 | 700 | 750 |
| Equipment | 1,200 | 0 | 0 |
| Rent of fowl run | 150 | 150 | 150 |
| Loan repayment | 0 | 120 | 120 |
| **Total payments** | 2,250 | 970 | 1,020 |
| **Net cash flow** | −1,250 | 430 | 580 |
| **Closing balance** | 250 | 680 | 1,260 |

1. Month 1 total payments: $900 + 1{,}200 + 150 + 0 = 2{,}250$.
2. Month 1 net cash flow: $1{,}000 - 2{,}250 = -1{,}250$.
3. Month 1 closing balance: $1{,}500 + (-1{,}250) = 250$, which becomes Month 2's opening balance.
4. Month 2: net cash flow $1{,}400 - 970 = 430$; closing balance $250 + 430 = 680$.
5. Month 3: net cash flow $1{,}600 - 1{,}020 = 580$; closing balance $680 + 580 = 1{,}260$.
6. There are no sales in Month 1 because the chicks take weeks to grow. The balance stays positive only because of the savings and the loan.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: spotting a cash-flow problem]
Rudo's fictional catering business has an opening balance of US\$300 in April.

| (US\$) | April | May | June |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Opening balance | 300 | −300 | 800 |
| Catering sales | 800 | 2,000 | 600 |
| **Total receipts** | 800 | 2,000 | 600 |
| Food and gas | 900 | 600 | 450 |
| Wages | 500 | 300 | 250 |
| **Total payments** | 1,400 | 900 | 700 |
| **Net cash flow** | −600 | 1,100 | −100 |
| **Closing balance** | −300 | 800 | 700 |

1. April: $800 - 1{,}400 = -600$; closing balance $300 - 600 = -300$. The business will be **US\$300 short** at the end of April.
2. May recovers strongly (a busy wedding month): $-300 + 1{,}100 = 800$.
3. Rudo can plan ahead: arrange an overdraft of at least US\$300 for April, ask customers for deposits, delay buying some stock, or negotiate trade credit. This is the main value of a cash-flow projection: it warns of shortages **before** they happen.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing cash flow with profit"}
A profitable business can run out of cash if customers pay late or it buys equipment; a business can have cash from a loan while making a loss.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to carry the balance forward"}
Each month's opening balance is the previous month's closing balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without context"}
Explain how a problem affects an SME: "without collateral it cannot get a bank loan, so it cannot buy equipment to expand".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Calculate** missing figures in a cash-flow projection by working down each column: total receipts, total payments, net cash flow, then closing balance.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Outline** the parts of a business plan in order, with a short phrase on what each contains.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** how SMEs can survive, or whether the government should support them: weigh their benefits (jobs, incomes) against their problems and the costs of support, and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SMEs are small, owner-managed businesses with few workers and little capital, often informal and serving local markets.
- They provide jobs and incomes, use local resources, supply larger firms and keep markets competitive.
- Problems include finance, infrastructure, workspace, crowded markets, skills and regulation.
- They survive through niches, co-operatives, clusters, microfinance, subcontracting, low costs and training.
- A business plan covers the business, market, marketing, operations, management and finances.
- In a cash-flow projection: net cash flow = receipts − payments; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
:::
