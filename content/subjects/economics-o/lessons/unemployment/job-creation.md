## Introduction

Walk through any Zimbabwean town and you will see people creating their own work: vendors selling vegetables and airtime, welders making gates at Siyaso in Mbare, carpenters, hairdressers, cross-border traders and mobile-money agents. For many school leavers, a formal job with a regular salary is hard to find, so making a living through **enterprise** has become the normal path rather than the exception.

The ZIMSEC syllabus (Form 4) asks you to **describe unemployment trends in Zimbabwe**, explain how **enterprise** and **indigenisation** can reduce unemployment, and **plan a small income-generating project**. This lesson covers all three. It describes general trends without quoting figures: if you need official numbers, look up the latest ZIMSTAT labour force survey and note its year and definitions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Formal sector"}
Businesses and jobs that are registered, taxed and regulated: government, large firms, banks, mines and registered companies, usually with contracts, regular wages and benefits.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Small-scale economic activities that are not registered or fully regulated, such as street vending, small workshops and backyard manufacturing, usually without contracts, pensions or social protection.
:::

:::definition{term="Enterprise (entrepreneurship)"}
The willingness and ability to start and run a business, taking risks to organise resources in the hope of making a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenisation"}
A policy of increasing the share of businesses and resources owned and controlled by indigenous (local) citizens.
:::

:::definition{term="Income-generating project"}
A small business activity, run by an individual, family or group, that produces goods or services for sale in order to earn an income.
:::

## Unemployment trends in Zimbabwe

Describe the general picture, and support it with reasons:

- **Loss of formal jobs:** during the economic crisis of the 2000s, which ended in the hyperinflation of 2007-2008, many factories closed or cut production (deindustrialisation), and formal employment shrank. Mining, manufacturing and commercial agriculture all shed workers at different times.
- **Growth of the informal sector:** people who lost formal jobs, and school leavers who could not find one, turned to informal work. A large share of Zimbabwean workers now earn their living informally.
- **Youth unemployment:** each year many young people leave school and college, more than the number of new formal jobs, so youth unemployment and underemployment are major concerns.
- **Underemployment:** many people work only part of the time, or in jobs far below their qualifications, such as graduates working as vendors.
- **Emigration:** many skilled and unskilled workers have moved to other countries, especially South Africa and overseas, in search of work. Their **remittances** support families at home, but the country loses skills (a brain drain).
- **Measurement problems:** because so much work is informal, the official unemployment rate can look lower than the true level of joblessness, since people with a little informal work are counted as employed.

## The informal sector: benefits and problems

| Benefits | Problems |
| --- | --- |
| Creates jobs and incomes for people who cannot find formal work | Low and irregular incomes; no contracts, pensions or sick pay |
| Easy to enter: little capital and few qualifications needed | Poor working conditions, health and safety risks |
| Provides cheap goods and services close to customers | Little tax is paid, so government revenue is lower |
| Develops business skills; some firms grow into formal businesses | Hard to obtain bank loans; firms stay small and unproductive |
| Uses local materials and recycled inputs | Conflict with councils over trading sites, licences and congestion |

## How enterprise creates jobs

- **Self-employment:** a person who starts a business employs himself or herself, removing one person from unemployment.
- **Employing others:** successful small and medium enterprises (SMEs) hire workers, often from the local community.
- **Linkages:** new businesses buy from suppliers (farmers, transporters, wholesalers), creating jobs there too.
- **Government support for enterprise:** training in business skills, access to credit through microfinance and youth or women's funds, workspace and market stalls, simpler registration and lower licence fees.
- **Limits:** many small firms fail in their first years; credit is expensive; markets are crowded with similar businesses (many vendors selling the same goods); and incomes are often low and uncertain.

## Indigenisation and job creation

Zimbabwe's **Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act (2007)** aimed to increase the share of the economy owned by indigenous Zimbabweans, including a requirement for local ownership of a majority share in many larger businesses. The requirements were later relaxed for most sectors. Like the fast-track land reform programme from 2000, it is a debated policy, so treat it in a balanced way.

- **Aims and possible benefits:** wider local ownership of businesses and resources; profits kept and reinvested in Zimbabwe; more local people in management; support for local suppliers and community share schemes; new local entrepreneurs who create jobs.
- **Recognised problems:** uncertainty about ownership rules can discourage foreign and domestic investment, which reduces job creation; transfers of ownership do not by themselves create new jobs; benefits may be concentrated among a few people; new owners may lack capital or experience.
- **Evaluation:** whether indigenisation creates jobs depends on how it is designed. Policies that help local people **start and grow new businesses** (training, credit, contracts for local suppliers) are more likely to add jobs than policies that only **transfer ownership** of existing firms.

:::example[Worked example 1: will this policy create jobs?]
A fictional town council offers a group of ten unemployed young people a free market stall, a six-week business course and a small loan to start a welding and furniture business.

1. **Direct jobs:** the ten members become self-employed (ten fewer unemployed).
2. **Linkages:** they buy steel, timber and paint from local suppliers and pay transporters, supporting jobs there.
3. **Growth:** if the business succeeds, it may hire apprentices.
4. **Risks:** the market may already have many welders, the loan may be hard to repay, and members may disagree about running the business.
5. **Judgement:** the scheme is likely to create some lasting jobs because it combines skills, capital and a workplace, but success depends on finding customers and on careful management.
:::

## Planning a small income-generating project

A good project plan answers these questions:

1. **Idea:** What will be produced or sold? Is there **demand** (who are the customers and how many)?
2. **Resources:** What land, equipment, materials and skills are needed? Where will the **capital** come from (savings, family, a loan, a group)?
3. **Costs:** list every cost (materials, equipment, transport, rent, licences).
4. **Price and revenue:** what price can be charged compared with competitors; how many units will be sold?
5. **Profit:** revenue − total cost; how many units must be sold to cover costs (break even)?
6. **Marketing:** where and how will the product be sold?
7. **Risks:** disease, drought, price changes, theft, competition, and how to reduce them.
8. **Records:** keep simple records of money in and money out.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: Chipo's broiler project]
Chipo plans to raise 100 broiler chickens at home in Masvingo for six weeks and sell them to neighbours and a local restaurant. Prices are invented for this example.

| Item | Cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: |
| 100 day-old chicks at US\$0.90 each | 90.00 |
| 8 bags of feed at US\$30 each | 240.00 |
| Vaccines and medicines | 15.00 |
| Transport to market | 20.00 |
| Drinkers, feeders and heat lamp | 60.00 |
| **Total cost** | 425.00 |

1. Chipo expects 5 chicks to die, so she sells $100 - 5 = 95$ birds at US\$5.50 each.
2. Revenue $= 95 \times 5.50 =$ US\$522.50.
3. Profit $= 522.50 - 425.00 =$ US\$97.50.
4. Break even: $425 \div 5.50 = 77.3$, so she must sell at least **78 birds** to cover her costs.
5. For a second batch she can reuse the equipment, so costs fall to $425 - 60 =$ US\$365 and profit rises to $522.50 - 365 =$ US\$157.50 if prices stay the same.
6. Risks: disease (vaccinate, keep the house clean), a fall in the selling price, and late payment by customers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Quoting statistics you cannot support"}
Do not invent unemployment rates. Describe trends in words ("formal employment fell; the informal sector grew") and explain why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating indigenisation one-sidedly"}
Examiners reward balance: give the aims and possible benefits, the recognised problems, and a conclusion about the conditions under which it creates jobs.
:::

:::mistake{title="A project plan without figures"}
A plan must show costs, price, expected sales and profit. Leaving out the cost of equipment or losses (such as birds that die) overstates profit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe trends:** use direction words (rose, fell, grew, shifted to the informal sector) and give a reason for each trend.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain how enterprise reduces unemployment:** self-employment, hiring workers, and linkages to suppliers; then give one limit.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Plan a project:** set it out under headings (idea, market, resources and capital, costs, price and revenue, profit, risks) and show the calculations.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Formal jobs in Zimbabwe fell during the crisis years; the informal sector grew and now provides a large share of work; youth unemployment, underemployment and emigration are major concerns.
- The informal sector creates jobs and incomes but offers low, insecure pay and little tax revenue.
- Enterprise creates jobs through self-employment, hiring and linkages; support includes training, credit and workspace.
- Indigenisation aims to widen local ownership; it creates jobs mainly when it helps new local businesses grow rather than only transferring ownership.
- A project plan shows the idea, market, resources, costs, revenue, profit and risks.
:::
