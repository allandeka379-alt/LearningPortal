## Introduction

Think of five people in Zimbabwe who are all out of work. Tatenda has just finished a diploma in Bulawayo and is going to interviews. Rudo picked tobacco on a farm near Chinhoyi until the season ended. Farai operated machines in a textile factory that closed because imported cloth became cheaper. Nyasha lost her job at a car dealer in Harare when people across the country stopped buying new cars during a downturn. Sipho was a bank clerk until his branch was replaced by mobile money and online banking.

All five are unemployed, but for very different reasons, and the cure for one is useless for another. Training will not help Rudo find work in the dry season, and a general rise in spending will not bring back Farai's factory. That is why examiners ask you to **identify** the type of unemployment in a case and **explain its cause**: the type tells the government which policy is likely to work.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Frictional unemployment"}
Short-term unemployment of people who are between jobs or searching for their first job, caused by the time it takes to match workers with vacancies.
:::

:::definition{term="Structural unemployment"}
Long-term unemployment caused by a change in the structure of the economy, such as the decline of an industry, so that the skills or location of the unemployed no longer match the jobs available.
:::

:::definition{term="Cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment"}
Unemployment caused by a fall in total (aggregate) demand in the economy during a downturn or recession, so that firms in many industries need fewer workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Seasonal unemployment"}
Unemployment that occurs at certain times of the year because the demand for some workers depends on the season, for example in farming, tourism or construction.
:::

:::definition{term="Regional unemployment"}
Unemployment that is concentrated in particular areas of a country, often because the main industry of the area has declined. It is a form of structural unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Technological unemployment"}
Unemployment caused when machines, computers or new methods replace workers. It is a form of structural unemployment.
:::

## Frictional unemployment

Even when there are plenty of jobs, some people are unemployed for a short time while they search: school leavers looking for their first job, workers who have resigned to find a better one, and people who have moved town. Finding the right vacancy takes time because job seekers do not know about every vacancy and employers take time to interview.

- **Cause:** imperfect information and the time it takes to search and match.
- **Length:** usually short (weeks or a few months).
- **Is it harmful?** Not very. Some frictional unemployment is a sign of a healthy labour market in which people move to jobs that suit them better. It exists even at full employment.

## Structural unemployment

Structural unemployment arises when the pattern of demand or production in the economy changes for good. An industry declines because of foreign competition, a fall in demand for its product, the exhaustion of a mine, or new technology. The workers who lose their jobs have skills that other industries do not need, or live where there are no new jobs.

- **Cause:** a **mismatch** of skills or location between the unemployed and the vacancies (**occupational** and **geographical immobility of labour**).
- **Length:** often long-term; older workers may never find similar work.
- **Examples:** the closure of a textile or clothing factory facing cheap imports; a mine that runs out of ore; a decline in demand for a crop.

**Technological unemployment** is structural unemployment caused by new technology: ticket sellers replaced by automatic machines, bank tellers by mobile money, typists by computers. Technology also creates new jobs, but often for workers with different skills.

**Regional unemployment** is structural unemployment concentrated in one area. When a town depends on one industry, such as a mine or a large factory, its closure leaves many people in the same place without work, and the area loses spending as well. Workers may be unable to move to where jobs are because of housing costs, family ties or lack of information.

## Cyclical unemployment

Economies go through a **business (trade) cycle** of booms and recessions. In a recession, households and firms spend less, so total demand for goods and services falls. Firms in **many industries at once** cut output and lay off workers, or stop hiring. This is cyclical or **demand-deficient** unemployment.

- **Cause:** a fall in aggregate demand (for example lower consumer confidence, higher interest rates, lower exports or cuts in government spending).
- **Length:** lasts as long as the downturn; falls again when demand recovers.
- **Sign in data:** unemployment rising across most industries and regions at the same time, together with falling output.

## Seasonal unemployment

Some jobs exist only at certain times of the year. In Zimbabwe, much farm work follows the rainy season, from planting and weeding to the harvest, and there is less work in the dry months. Tourism depends on holiday seasons, and some construction slows in the rains.

- **Cause:** regular, predictable changes in demand for workers during the year.
- **Length:** temporary; the same workers often return to work the next season.
- **Sign in data:** unemployment rises and falls at the same time each year.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: unemployment by type in a farming district]
The table shows estimated unemployment (in thousands) in a fictional farming district with a labour force of 200 thousand.

| Quarter | Frictional | Structural | Seasonal | Total unemployed | Unemployment rate (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| January-March | 6 | 14 | 4 | 24 | 12.0 |
| April-June | 6 | 14 | 2 | 22 | 11.0 |
| July-September | 6 | 14 | 15 | 35 | 17.5 |
| October-December | 6 | 14 | 9 | 29 | 14.5 |

1. The rate for July-September is $35 \div 200 \times 100 = 17.5\%$.
2. Only **seasonal** unemployment changes from quarter to quarter: it is highest in the dry months of July-September, after the harvest, and lowest at harvest time (April-June).
3. Frictional and structural unemployment stay at $6 + 14 = 20$ thousand all year, a rate of $20 \div 200 \times 100 = 10\%$. This part will not disappear when the rains return.
4. In July-September, seasonal unemployment is $15 \div 35 \times 100 \approx 42.9\%$ of the total.
5. Policy conclusion: irrigation or off-season work (for example processing crops) would reduce the seasonal part; retraining and new industries are needed for the structural part.
:::

:::example[Worked example 2: identifying the type]
Classify each case and explain the cause.

1. **Tatenda**, a new graduate attending interviews: **frictional**. He is searching for a suitable job; vacancies exist but matching takes time.
2. **Farai**, a textile machine operator whose factory closed because of cheap imports: **structural**. Demand has shifted away from the local industry for good, and his skills do not match the jobs available.
3. **Nyasha**, laid off by a car dealer when sales fell across the country in a recession: **cyclical**. The cause is a fall in total demand, which affects many industries at the same time.
4. **Rudo**, a tobacco picker out of work after the season: **seasonal**. Demand for her labour depends on the time of year.
5. **Sipho**, a bank clerk replaced by mobile money: **technological** (a form of structural). New technology has replaced his job.
6. **Former miners in a town whose only mine closed**: **regional** (structural). Many people in one area lost work at once, and they find it hard to move.
:::

## Why the type matters

| Type | Main cause | Typical length | Policy that targets it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frictional | Time to search and match | Short | Better information: job centres, online vacancy lists |
| Structural (incl. regional and technological) | Mismatch of skills or location | Long | Retraining, education, help to move, incentives for firms to locate in the area |
| Cyclical | Low aggregate demand | Length of the downturn | Raise demand: lower interest rates, more government spending, lower taxes |
| Seasonal | Seasonal demand for labour | Part of each year | Irrigation, year-round tourism, other work in the off-season |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every job loss structural"}
Ask *why* the job went. If demand fell across the whole economy in a recession, the unemployment is cyclical. It is structural only if the industry has declined for good or the skills are no longer needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing seasonal and cyclical unemployment"}
Seasonal unemployment follows the calendar and repeats every year. Cyclical unemployment follows the business cycle, which lasts several years and is not regular.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying frictional unemployment is a serious problem"}
Frictional unemployment is short-term and exists even at full employment. It becomes a concern only if search takes a long time because information is poor.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify / state:** name the type precisely ("structural unemployment") and, for ZIMSEC, use "regional" or "technological" where the case clearly fits.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** give the cause and link it to the case: "the factory closed because cheap imported cloth reduced demand for local cloth, so the workers' skills are no longer needed: structural unemployment."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse / discuss:** use the type to judge a policy. Retraining helps structural unemployment but not cyclical; extra spending helps cyclical but not structural. Say which type is most important in the case before concluding.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
Both boards examine frictional, structural, cyclical and seasonal unemployment. ZIMSEC also lists **regional** unemployment, and textbooks for both boards treat **technological** unemployment as a form of structural unemployment. "Demand-deficient" is another name for cyclical unemployment.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Frictional: short-term, between jobs; exists at full employment.
- Structural: long-term mismatch of skills or location after an industry declines; regional and technological unemployment are forms of it.
- Cyclical (demand-deficient): caused by a fall in aggregate demand in a downturn, affecting many industries.
- Seasonal: regular changes in demand for labour during the year.
- Identifying the type shows which policy will work.
:::
