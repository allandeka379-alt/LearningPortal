## Introduction

Ask ten school leavers in Chinhoyi what they are doing and you may hear: "I'm a bank teller", "I sell airtime and sweets at the bus stop", "I'm at college", "I help my mother at home", and "I've been looking for work for six months". Which of them are employed, which are unemployed, and which are not in the labour force at all? The answers matter, because a country's **unemployment rate** is one of the main signs of how well its economy is doing, and governments use it to decide on policy.

In Zimbabwe, official labour statistics come from **labour force surveys** carried out by ZIMSTAT, the national statistics agency. A large share of Zimbabwean workers earn their living in the **informal sector**, as vendors, cross-border traders, small-scale farmers and artisans, which makes the line between employment and unemployment harder to draw. If you need the latest official figures, look up ZIMSTAT's most recent labour force survey report and note its date and definitions.

Both syllabuses ask you to define employment, unemployment and the labour force, and to calculate the unemployment rate. This is the starting point for the lessons on the types, effects and policies of unemployment.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Working-age population"}
People of the age at which they could work, usually from about 15 years to retirement age (definitions differ between countries).
:::

:::definition{term="Employed"}
People of working age who did any work for pay or profit during the survey reference period (for example the past week), including part-time, self-employed and informal workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Unemployed"}
People of working age who are without work, are available to start work and are actively looking for work.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour force (working population)"}
All people who are employed plus all people who are unemployed: those who are working or willing and able to work. **Labour force = employed + unemployed.**
:::

:::definition{term="Economically inactive"}
People of working age who are neither working nor looking for work: for example full-time students, homemakers, the retired, the long-term sick and discouraged workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Full employment"}
A situation in which everyone who is willing and able to work at the going wage rate has a job, apart from a small amount of short-term (frictional and seasonal) unemployment. It does **not** mean an unemployment rate of zero.
:::

:::formula
Unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100
:::

## Who is in the labour force?

The working-age population divides into three groups:

| Group | Examples | In the labour force? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employed | Bank teller, teacher, self-employed carpenter, informal vendor, part-time worker | Yes |
| Unemployed | School leaver applying for jobs; retrenched miner looking for work | Yes |
| Economically inactive | Full-time student, homemaker, retired person, discouraged worker who has stopped looking | No |

The key test for unemployment is that the person is **without work**, **available** and **actively seeking** work. A person who has given up looking is not counted as unemployed, even though he or she would like a job. Such **discouraged workers** make measured unemployment lower than the true amount of joblessness.

## Measuring unemployment: the labour force survey

A **labour force survey** asks a large random sample of households about each member's activity in a recent reference week. Following international (ILO) guidelines, a person is classed as:

- **employed** if they did at least one hour of paid work or work for profit in the reference week;
- **unemployed** if they did no work, were available to start within about two weeks, and had looked for work in the past four weeks;
- **inactive** otherwise.

The sample results are scaled up to estimate the whole population.

- **Advantages:** uses the same definitions as other countries, so rates can be compared; includes people who do not register for or receive benefits; can collect information on age, sex, region and the informal sector.
- **Disadvantages:** costly and slow to carry out; based on a sample, so there is a margin of error; depends on honest answers; the "one hour of work" rule counts many people with very little work as employed, so it can hide **underemployment** (people working fewer hours than they want, or in jobs well below their skills).

Some countries also count the people who register for unemployment benefits (the **claimant count**). It is quick and cheap, but it misses people who are not entitled to, or do not claim, benefits. Where there are few unemployment benefits, as in Zimbabwe, it is of little use.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a labour force survey in Country X]
A fictional survey estimates (in thousands): working-age population 4,800; employed 2,950; unemployed 690.

1. Labour force = employed + unemployed = $2{,}950 + 690 = 3{,}640$ thousand.
2. Economically inactive = $4{,}800 - 3{,}640 = 1{,}160$ thousand.
3. Unemployment rate = $690 \div 3{,}640 \times 100 = 18.956...$, that is 19.0% (to one decimal place).
4. A common error is to divide by the working-age population: $690 \div 4{,}800 \times 100 = 14.4\%$, which understates the rate because it includes people who are not in the labour force.
5. The **participation rate** (labour force as a share of working-age population) is $3{,}640 \div 4{,}800 \times 100 \approx 75.8\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: classifying a small survey sample]
A survey in a fictional town questions 20 people of working age: 7 work full time; 2 work part time; 3 sell goods at a market (informal work); 3 have no work and are applying for jobs; 2 are full-time students; 1 is a homemaker; 1 is retired; and 1 has no job and has stopped looking because there seem to be no vacancies.

1. Employed = $7 + 2 + 3 = 12$ (part-time and informal workers count as employed).
2. Unemployed = 3 (without work, available, looking).
3. Labour force = $12 + 3 = 15$; the other 5 are inactive, including the discouraged worker.
4. Unemployment rate = $3 \div 15 \times 100 = 20\%$.
5. If the discouraged worker were counted as unemployed, the rate would be $4 \div 16 \times 100 = 25\%$. The definition used changes the figure.
:::

## Why measured unemployment may be misleading

- **Discouraged workers** are left out, so the rate understates joblessness.
- **Underemployment** is hidden: a vendor who sells for only a few hours a day counts as employed.
- **Informal and hidden work** may be missed or misreported.
- **Sampling errors** and changes in definitions make comparisons over time uncertain.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the whole population"}
The unemployment rate uses the **labour force** (employed + unemployed) as the base, not the total or working-age population.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting students and retired people as unemployed"}
They are not working but are **not looking** for work, so they are economically inactive, outside the labour force.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying full employment means zero unemployment"}
Even at full employment some people are between jobs or waiting for seasonal work. Full employment means that only this short-term unemployment remains.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** for "unemployed", include all three conditions: without work, available, actively seeking work.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** write the formula, find the labour force first, show the division and give the answer as a percentage with the rounding asked for.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / discuss measurement:** give the strengths of a labour force survey (international definition, includes non-claimants) and its weaknesses (cost, sampling error, hides underemployment and discouraged workers).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC asks you to explain unemployment and the **labour force** and to distinguish the employed from the unemployed. Cambridge asks you to define employment, unemployment and full employment, explain the **labour force survey** and calculate the unemployment rate. The claimant count is no longer in the Cambridge syllabus from 2027.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The employed work for pay or profit, including part-time and informal work; the unemployed are without work, available and seeking work.
- Labour force = employed + unemployed; students, homemakers, the retired and discouraged workers are inactive.
- Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
- A labour force survey uses a sample and international definitions; it is comparable but costly and can hide underemployment.
- Full employment allows for a small amount of frictional and seasonal unemployment.
:::
