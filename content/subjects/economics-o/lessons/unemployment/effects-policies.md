## Introduction

When a factory in Bulawayo lays off two hundred workers, the effect does not stop at their gates. The workers lose their wages, their families cut back on food and school fees, the vendors outside the factory lose customers, kombi operators carry fewer passengers, and the government collects less tax. Unemployment is one of the most serious problems an economy can face, and reducing it is one of the main aims of government policy.

Both boards ask you to explain the **consequences** of unemployment for different groups and to **propose and evaluate** policies to reduce it. High-scoring answers do two things: they trace the effects through the economy step by step, and they judge each policy by asking which **type** of unemployment it targets, how long it takes, how much it costs and what side effects it has.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost of unemployment"}
The output that the unemployed could have produced if they had been working: goods and services the economy has lost for ever.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand-side policy"}
A policy that raises aggregate (total) demand in the economy, such as more government spending, lower taxes or lower interest rates. It mainly reduces cyclical unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply-side policy"}
A policy that improves the quality, quantity or mobility of labour and the efficiency of markets, such as education, training, help to move and incentives for firms. It mainly reduces structural and frictional unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Multiplier effect (downward)"}
The way a fall in spending by the unemployed reduces the incomes of others, who then cut their own spending, so the total fall in income is larger than the first fall.
:::

## Consequences of unemployment

### For individuals and families

- **Loss of income:** lower living standards; difficulty paying for food, rent, school fees and health care. In countries with little or no unemployment benefit, families depend on relatives or on informal work.
- **Loss of skills and confidence:** the longer a person is unemployed, the more skills go out of date, and employers may be unwilling to hire them. This makes unemployment harder to escape.
- **Stress and poor health:** worry, depression and family breakdown are more common.
- **Social effects:** high youth unemployment is linked with crime, drug abuse and emigration of young people looking for work abroad.

### For firms

- **Lower demand:** unemployed people spend less, so firms selling to households, especially of normal and luxury goods, lose sales and profits.
- **Some benefits:** firms find it easier to recruit, and wage demands are weaker, which may keep costs down. Firms selling inferior goods may sell more.

### For the government

- **Lower tax revenue:** less income tax from workers and less VAT from spending.
- **Higher spending:** on benefits (where they exist), social welfare, health care and job programmes.
- So the **budget deficit** tends to widen, which may force cuts in other spending or more borrowing.

### For the economy

- **Lost output:** the economy produces inside its production possibility curve; the lost output is the opportunity cost of unemployment and can never be recovered.
- **Lower economic growth and living standards.**
- **Downward multiplier:** the fall in spending spreads to other industries and regions.
- **Brain drain:** skilled workers emigrate, reducing the country's future productive potential (although they may send home remittances).
- **Some argue a small benefit:** lower inflationary pressure, since demand and wage rises are lower.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the cost of unemployment in Country X]
In a fictional country, 150,000 people who would like to work are unemployed. If employed, each would earn an average of US\$300 a month and pay income tax of 15%.

1. Income lost in a year = $150{,}000 \times 300 \times 12 =$ US\$540,000,000 (US\$540 million).
2. Income tax lost by the government = $15\% \times 540 =$ US\$81 million a year.
3. After-tax income lost = $540 - 81 =$ US\$459 million. If workers would have spent 80% of this, local spending falls by $0.8 \times 459 =$ US\$367.2 million, which reduces the sales of shops, transport operators and other firms.
4. Conclusion: the cost of unemployment falls on the unemployed (lost income), on the government (lost tax and extra welfare spending) and on firms (lost sales), and through the multiplier on other workers too.
:::

## Policies to reduce unemployment

### Demand-side policies (for cyclical unemployment)

- **Expansionary fiscal policy:** more government spending (for example on roads, schools and clinics) creates jobs directly and raises demand; **lower taxes** leave households and firms more to spend.
- **Expansionary monetary policy:** **lower interest rates** make borrowing cheaper, so households buy more on credit and firms invest more; the central bank may also increase the money supply.
- **Limits:** they may cause **inflation** if the economy is near full capacity; extra spending may go on **imports**, creating jobs abroad; they increase the budget deficit and government debt; lower interest rates do little if confidence is low; and they do **not** cure structural unemployment.

### Supply-side policies (for structural and frictional unemployment)

- **Education and training / retraining:** give workers the skills employers need (occupational mobility).
- **Better job information:** job centres and online vacancy lists cut search time (frictional).
- **Help with moving:** relocation grants and affordable housing improve geographical mobility.
- **Incentives for firms:** tax breaks, grants or subsidised premises for firms that locate in high-unemployment regions or hire young workers; **wage subsidies** reduce the cost of hiring.
- **Support for enterprise:** cheap loans and advice for small businesses and the self-employed.
- **Limits:** they are often **slow** (training takes years), **costly**, and they work only if there are jobs to train for; trained workers may emigrate.

### Other measures

- **Protection** of domestic industries (tariffs, quotas) may save jobs in the short run but invites retaliation and raises prices.
- **Off-season work** and irrigation reduce seasonal unemployment.

:::example[Worked example 2: choosing between two programmes]
A fictional government compares two programmes.

| Programme | Cost (US\$ million) | Jobs created | Length of jobs |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Public works road programme | 24 | 8,000 | 1 year |
| Wage subsidy for apprentices | 9 | 2,500 | Permanent jobs |

1. Cost per job: roads $24{,}000{,}000 \div 8{,}000 =$ US\$3,000; apprenticeships $9{,}000{,}000 \div 2{,}500 =$ US\$3,600.
2. The road programme creates more jobs at a lower cost per job, quickly, and leaves useful roads. But the jobs end after a year, so it suits **cyclical** unemployment in a recession.
3. The apprenticeship subsidy costs more per job, but it gives young people skills and lasting jobs, so it tackles **structural** youth unemployment.
4. Judgement: if unemployment is mainly cyclical, the road programme is better value now; if it is mainly structural, the apprenticeship subsidy is better in the long run. Many governments use both.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing effects without explaining them"}
"Unemployment reduces tax revenue" earns little on its own. Explain the chain: fewer people earning, so less income tax, and less spending, so less VAT.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using one policy for every type"}
Lower interest rates do not reopen a closed mine, and retraining does not help when the whole economy is in recession. Always match the policy to the type.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the costs of a policy"}
Every policy has an opportunity cost and side effects: inflation, imports, budget deficits or time lags. A "discuss" answer must weigh them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain the effects:** state the group (individual, firm, government, economy), the effect, and why it happens. Two well-developed effects score more than six listed ones.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss a policy:** explain how it reduces unemployment (the chain of cause and effect), then give its limits (type of unemployment, time lag, cost, inflation, imports), and finish with a conclusion that depends on the situation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC asks you to *outline the effects of unemployment and propose solutions*; Cambridge asks you to *explain the consequences for individuals, firms, the government and the economy* and to *evaluate* policies. Both reward the same reasoning. Cambridge links the policies to its fiscal, monetary and supply-side policy topics.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Unemployment costs individuals income, skills and health; firms lose sales; government loses tax and spends more; the economy loses output for ever.
- Demand-side policies (more government spending, lower taxes, lower interest rates) reduce cyclical unemployment but risk inflation, imports and deficits.
- Supply-side policies (training, information, mobility, incentives for firms) reduce structural and frictional unemployment but are slow and costly.
- The best policy depends on the type of unemployment, cost, time and side effects.
:::
