## Introduction

In a season of good rains, farmers across Mashonaland deliver far more maize to buyers than in a drought year, even at the same price per tonne. When a cheaper source of fertiliser becomes available, or a new irrigation scheme starts, farmers can grow more at every price. When fuel prices rise, transport operators run fewer trips. In each case something **other than the good's own price** has changed, and the whole supply curve moves.

This lesson mirrors the one on shifts in demand. You need to know the **conditions of supply**, which way each one moves the curve, and how to tell a **change in supply** (a shift) from a **change in quantity supplied** (a movement along the curve). Examiners test it through events ("a drought hits the maize crop"), schedules and diagram descriptions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Change in quantity supplied"}
A movement along an existing supply curve caused only by a change in the good's own price: an **extension** (price rises, quantity supplied rises) or a **contraction** (price falls, quantity supplied falls).
:::

:::definition{term="Change in supply"}
A shift of the whole supply curve caused by a change in a factor other than the good's own price: an **increase** (shift to the right, more supplied at every price) or a **decrease** (shift to the left, less supplied at every price).
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidy"}
A payment by the government to producers, usually per unit produced, which lowers their costs and increases supply.
:::

:::definition{term="Indirect tax"}
A tax on goods and services, collected from producers, such as excise duty or value added tax (VAT). It raises producers' costs and decreases supply.
:::

## The conditions of supply

| Condition of supply | Change | Effect on supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Costs of production (wages, raw materials, fuel, rent) | Rise | Decrease: curve shifts left |
| Technology | Improves | Increase: curve shifts right |
| Weather and natural conditions | Good rains / drought, floods, pests | Increase / decrease |
| Number of firms | More firms enter | Increase |
| Indirect taxes | Rise | Decrease |
| Subsidies | Introduced or raised | Increase |
| Prices of other goods the firm could produce | Rise (e.g. tobacco price rises for a maize farmer) | Decrease in supply of maize as land switches to tobacco |
| Goods in joint supply | More of one is produced (e.g. more beef, so more hides) | Increase in supply of the by-product |
| Expectations | Producers expect higher prices later | Decrease now (they hold back stocks) |

Every change also works in reverse: lower costs increase supply, a removed subsidy decreases supply, and so on.

The reasoning always runs through **costs and profitability**: if producing becomes cheaper or easier, firms are willing and able to supply more at every price; if it becomes dearer or harder, they supply less at every price.

## Movements along versus shifts

On a diagram with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis:

- **Extension:** price rises, the point moves **up along** the curve S.
- **Contraction:** price falls, the point moves **down along** S.
- **Increase in supply:** the curve shifts **to the right**, from S1 to S2. More is supplied at every price.
- **Decrease in supply:** the curve shifts **to the left**, from S1 to S2. Less is supplied at every price.

Again, ask: "did the good's own price cause this?" If yes, it is a movement along the curve; if no, it is a shift.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a drought and the supply of maize]
The table shows the supply of maize to buyers from farmers in a fictional district in a normal season and in a drought season.

| Price of maize (US\$ per tonne) | S1: normal season (tonnes) | S2: drought season (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 200 | 800 | 500 |
| 250 | 1,000 | 700 |
| 300 | 1,200 | 900 |
| 350 | 1,400 | 1,100 |

1. Compare the columns at the same price: at US\$300, quantity supplied falls from 1,200 to 900 tonnes.
2. The fall is the same, 300 tonnes, at every price. Less is supplied at **every** price: a **decrease in supply**.
3. On the diagram the supply curve shifts to the **left**, from S1 to S2.
4. At US\$300 the fall is $\frac{300}{1{,}200} \times 100 = 25\%$ of the normal-season supply.
5. Contrast: if the price fell from US\$300 to US\$250 in a normal season, quantity supplied would fall from 1,200 to 1,000 tonnes. That is a contraction along S1, not a shift.
:::

:::example{title="Worked example 2: which way does the curve move?"}
Consider the supply of **cooking oil** made by a fictional factory in Harare.

- **The price of sunflower seed, its main raw material, rises.** Costs rise, so less oil is supplied at every price: **decrease**, shift left.
- **The factory installs a faster pressing machine.** Better technology lowers the cost per litre: **increase**, shift right.
- **The government removes VAT on cooking oil.** A tax on the product is removed, so the cost of supplying each litre falls: **increase**, shift right.
- **The price of cooking oil rises.** Own price: an **extension** along the existing curve, not a shift.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reading a leftward shift as more supply"}
A leftward shift of the supply curve looks "higher" on the diagram, but it means **less** is supplied at every price. Always describe supply shifts as to the left (decrease) or to the right (increase).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher price increases supply"}
A higher price causes an **extension** of supply (a movement along the curve). "Supply increases" means the curve has shifted right for another reason.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up taxes and subsidies"}
An indirect tax raises producers' costs and **decreases** supply. A subsidy lowers costs and **increases** supply.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** name the condition and give the direction: "a rise in the cost of fertiliser decreases the supply of maize; the supply curve shifts to the left".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** use the chain event → effect on costs or ability to produce → more or less supplied at every price → shift right or left.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe the diagram:** axes, original curve S1, new curve S2 to the right or left, and the quantities at one price taken from the data (for example 1,200 and 900 tonnes at US\$300).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of **increases and decreases in supply** versus **extensions and contractions**; Cambridge describes **shifts** of, and **movements along**, the supply curve. They are the same ideas.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the supply curve: an extension or a contraction.
- A change in any other condition of supply shifts the whole curve: right for an increase, left for a decrease.
- Conditions of supply include costs, technology, weather, the number of firms, taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods and expectations.
- Lower costs, better technology, good weather and subsidies increase supply; higher costs, drought and indirect taxes decrease it.
- Explain shifts through their effect on costs and profitability at every price.
:::
