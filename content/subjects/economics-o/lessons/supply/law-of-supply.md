## Introduction

When tomato prices at Mbare Musika are high, farmers in the Eastern Highlands send more crates to Harare. Some who usually sell locally load their produce onto lorries, and others pick fruit they would otherwise have left on the plants. When prices are low, fewer crates arrive. This is the **law of supply**: producers offer more for sale when the price is higher.

Supply is the producers' half of every market. Together with demand it decides prices, so you need it for equilibrium, price changes, elasticity of supply and government intervention. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge expect you to define supply precisely, read and build supply schedules, describe the supply curve in words and add individual supply into market supply.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Supply"}
The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each possible price over a given period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of supply"}
Other things being equal, as the price of a good rises the quantity supplied rises, and as the price falls the quantity supplied falls.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply schedule"}
A table showing the quantity supplied at each price over a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Market supply"}
The total quantity supplied by all the producers in a market at each price: the sum of the individual supplies.
:::

As with demand, supply is not a single number: it is a **quantity at each price** in a **time period**. "Sipho supplies 16 crates of tomatoes a week at US\$6 a crate" is a complete statement.

## Why does the law of supply hold?

The relationship between price and quantity supplied is **direct** (positive). There are three main reasons.

- **Profit motive:** at a higher price each unit sold earns more revenue, so producing more becomes more profitable and firms want to sell more.
- **Covering higher costs:** producing extra units often costs more per unit (overtime pay, less fertile land, older machines brought back into use). A higher price makes it worthwhile to cover these extra costs.
- **New producers:** at a higher price, producers who could not cover their costs before can now make a profit, so they enter the market. Existing firms may also switch resources from other products.

The phrase *other things being equal* means we hold constant everything else that affects supply, such as costs, technology and the weather. Only the good's own price changes.

## Supply schedules and the supply curve

The schedule below shows the weekly supply of tomatoes to a market by three fictional farmers.

| Price per crate (US\$) | Farmer Chipo | Farmer Sipho | Farmer Nyasha | Market supply (crates per week) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 20 | 10 | 0 | 30 |
| 6 | 30 | 16 | 5 | 51 |
| 8 | 38 | 22 | 10 | 70 |
| 10 | 45 | 28 | 14 | 87 |

Describe the **supply curve** from such a schedule like this:

- **Price** on the **vertical axis**, **quantity supplied** per period on the **horizontal axis**.
- Each row is a point; joining them gives the supply curve, labelled **S**.
- The curve **slopes upwards from left to right**: higher prices go with larger quantities supplied.
- A change in the good's own price causes a **movement along** the curve: an **extension** of supply when the price rises and a **contraction** when the price falls.

Notice Farmer Nyasha: at US\$4 she supplies nothing, because that price does not cover her costs. At US\$6 she enters the market. This is the "new producers" reason in action.

## Individual and market supply

Market supply is found by adding the quantities that all producers supply **at the same price**. At US\$8 the market supply of tomatoes is $38 + 22 + 10 = 70$ crates a week. The market supply curve slopes upwards and lies to the right of every individual curve.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: reading the tomato schedule]
Using the schedule above, describe the change in market supply when the price rises from US\$6 to US\$10 a crate.

1. At US\$6, market supply is $30 + 16 + 5 = 51$ crates a week.
2. At US\$10, market supply is $45 + 28 + 14 = 87$ crates a week.
3. Change: $87 - 51 = 36$ crates a week, a **rise** in quantity supplied.
4. This is an **extension of supply**, a movement up along the market supply curve from P = US\$6, Q = 51 to P = US\$10, Q = 87. The curve does not shift because only the price of tomatoes changed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: identical producers]
A fictional town has 60 small brickmakers with identical supply. Each supplies 800 bricks a week at US\$0.10 a brick, 1,000 at US\$0.12, 1,150 at US\$0.14 and 1,250 at US\$0.16.

1. Market supply = number of producers × quantity supplied by one producer, at each price.
2. At US\$0.10: $60 \times 800 = 48{,}000$ bricks; at US\$0.12: $60 \times 1{,}000 = 60{,}000$; at US\$0.14: $60 \times 1{,}150 = 69{,}000$; at US\$0.16: $60 \times 1{,}250 = 75{,}000$.
3. The market schedule therefore reads 48,000, 60,000, 69,000 and 75,000 bricks a week at the four prices.
4. Each rise of 2 cents adds fewer bricks than the last (12,000, then 9,000, then 6,000): the brickmakers are reaching the limit of their kilns, so it costs more and more to produce extra bricks. The supply curve slopes upwards but becomes steeper.
:::

:::example{title="Supply means willing and able"}
Tendai owns a small field near Masvingo. He would like to sell 100 bags of maize, but after a poor harvest he has only 40. His supply at today's price is 40 bags, not 100: supply counts only what producers are **able** to offer, as well as willing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing supply with the quantity produced or sold"}
Supply is what producers are willing and able to **offer for sale** at each price. A firm may produce goods it keeps in stock, and the quantity actually sold also depends on demand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing the supply curve sloping downwards"}
The normal supply curve slopes **upwards** from left to right. A downward slope is the demand curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher price shifts the supply curve"}
A change in the good's own price causes a movement **along** the supply curve (an extension or a contraction). Shifts are caused by other factors such as costs, technology and weather.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** include *willing and able*, *offer for sale*, *at each price* and *over a period of time*. For the law of supply say *other things being equal*.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain why the supply curve slopes upwards:** link the higher price to profit ("each unit now earns more revenue, so producing more is more profitable") and to new firms entering. One developed reason is worth more than three listed ones.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe the diagram:** axes, curve S sloping upwards, and the two points from the data with units. Say "movement along" or "extension", never "shift", for a change in own price.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC asks you to show the **price-supply relationship** on a supply curve; Cambridge asks you to link **individual and market supply**. Both use *extension* and *contraction* for movements along the curve.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price over a period of time.
- The law of supply: other things being equal, a higher price leads to a larger quantity supplied.
- Reasons: higher profit per unit, covering the rising cost of extra output, and new producers entering.
- The supply curve, with price on the vertical axis, slopes upwards; a change in own price moves along it (extension or contraction).
- Market supply adds every producer's quantity at each price.
:::
