## Introduction

Ask any farmer in Masvingo or Matabeleland South about the weather and you will hear the same story: the rains are harder to predict, dry spells in the middle of the season seem more common, and some years bring floods instead. Zimbabwe's economy depends heavily on rain-fed agriculture and on hydro-electric power from Lake Kariba, so changes in the climate affect production right across the economy.

ZIMSEC includes climate change in the topic on supply (Form 3). You need to explain what climate change is, describe its effects on production, and suggest measures to reduce it (**mitigation**) and to cope with it (**adaptation**). In economic terms, most of the effects are **decreases in supply**: the supply curve shifts to the left. Use the facts in this lesson with care: in the exam, describe effects clearly rather than quoting statistics you cannot be sure of.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate change"}
A long-term change in the average temperature and weather patterns of a region or of the whole Earth. Today's climate change is mainly caused by human activities, such as burning fossil fuels and clearing forests, which increase the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Greenhouse gases"}
Gases such as carbon dioxide and methane that trap heat in the atmosphere. More of them makes the Earth warmer.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions that reduce the causes of climate change, mainly by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or absorbing carbon dioxide (for example renewable energy and planting trees).
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
Actions that help people and producers cope with the effects of climate change (for example irrigation and drought-tolerant crops).
:::

Weather is what happens on a particular day or season; **climate** is the average pattern over many years. One dry season is not climate change, but a long-term trend towards hotter, drier and less predictable seasons is.

## Effects of climate change on production

### Crop farming

Most Zimbabwean crops, especially maize, depend on rainfall. Longer dry spells, late starts to the rainy season and higher temperatures reduce yields per hectare. Heavy storms can wash away soil and crops. Lower yields mean less is supplied at every price: the **supply curve shifts to the left**, the price of maize rises and the country may need to import grain.

### Livestock

Droughts dry up water points and pastures, so cattle, goats and sheep lose condition or die, and farmers may sell animals cheaply in a hurry. Supply of beef, milk and draught power falls, and so does the wealth that many rural households hold in their herds.

### Hydro-electricity and industry

Much of Zimbabwe's electricity comes from hydro-electric generation at Kariba. When rainfall in the Zambezi catchment is low, the water level in the lake falls and less electricity can be generated. Power cuts (load-shedding) then reduce output in mines, factories and businesses that depend on electricity, so their supply curves shift left too, and their costs rise if they must run diesel generators.

### Floods, cyclones and other effects

Extreme events damage roads, bridges, homes and crops. Cyclone Idai in March 2019 caused severe flooding and loss of life in Chimanimani and Chipinge in the eastern highlands, destroying farms and infrastructure. Climate change can also affect tourism (for example the flow over Victoria Falls in dry years), forestry and fisheries, and spread crop pests and diseases into new areas.

## Measures to mitigate and adapt

| Measure | Type | How it helps production |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar and other renewable energy | Mitigation (and adaptation) | Cuts emissions; reduces dependence on hydro power in dry years |
| Reforestation and stopping deforestation | Mitigation | Trees absorb carbon dioxide; protect soils and water catchments |
| Conservation agriculture (minimum tillage, mulching, planting basins) | Adaptation | Holds moisture and soil, so yields fall less in dry spells |
| Drought-tolerant and short-season crops (sorghum, millet, early-maturing maize) | Adaptation | Produce a harvest with less rain |
| Irrigation and water harvesting (dams, boreholes, drip systems) | Adaptation | Reduce dependence on rainfall |
| Weather forecasts and early-warning systems | Adaptation | Help farmers choose when and what to plant |
| Fodder storage and destocking before droughts | Adaptation | Protect the value of herds |
| Crop and livestock insurance | Adaptation | Protect farmers' incomes after a disaster, so they can plant again |

The government has promoted conservation agriculture through the Pfumvudza/Intwasa programme, in which farmers plant in small, mulched basins to save water and soil.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a drought on a small farm]
Farai grows maize on 5 hectares in a fictional ward. In a year of normal rains he harvests 4 tonnes per hectare; in a drought year only 1.5 tonnes per hectare. His neighbour plants sorghum, which yields 2.5 tonnes per hectare even in a drought.

| Crop and season | Yield (tonnes per hectare) | Output from 5 hectares (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Maize, normal rains | 4 | 20 |
| Maize, drought | 1.5 | 7.5 |
| Sorghum, drought | 2.5 | 12.5 |

1. Farai's maize output: $5 \times 4 = 20$ tonnes normally; $5 \times 1.5 = 7.5$ tonnes in a drought.
2. Percentage change: $\frac{7.5 - 20}{20} \times 100 = -62.5\%$, a fall of 62.5%.
3. Sorghum in the drought: $5 \times 2.5 = 12.5$ tonnes, which is 5 tonnes more than drought-hit maize.
4. Economic meaning: if many farmers lose output like this, the market supply of maize **decreases** (shift left). Switching some land to drought-tolerant crops is an **adaptation** that reduces the loss.
:::

:::example{title="Worked example 2: from low rainfall to a factory's output"}
Follow the chain for a fictional food factory in Bulawayo:

- Low rainfall in the Zambezi catchment → lower water level in Lake Kariba → less hydro-electricity generated.
- Less electricity → longer power cuts → the factory's machines stop for several hours a day.
- The factory produces less, or pays for diesel to run a generator → its costs rise.
- Higher costs and lower output → less is supplied at every price: the factory's **supply curve shifts to the left**, and the price of its products may rise.
- A solar installation on the factory roof would be both **mitigation** (less fossil fuel) and **adaptation** (less dependence on hydro power).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating one bad season as climate change"}
Climate change is a long-term trend in average conditions. A single drought is weather; more frequent and severe droughts over many years are evidence of climate change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mitigation and adaptation"}
Mitigation tackles the **cause** (fewer emissions, more trees). Adaptation copes with the **effects** (irrigation, drought-tolerant seed). Some measures, such as solar power, do both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the economics"}
An answer that only describes the weather misses marks. Link each effect to production: lower yields or higher costs → supply decreases → the supply curve shifts left → prices rise.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain an effect:** cause → effect on production (yield, output, costs) → effect on supply and prices. For example, "drought lowers maize yields, so less maize is supplied at every price and its price rises".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Suggest measures:** name the measure, say whether it mitigates or adapts, and explain how it protects production. Two developed measures are worth more than a list of six.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss / evaluate:** consider cost (dams and irrigation are expensive), who can afford it (small-scale farmers), and time (trees take years to grow), then conclude which measures are most practical.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Climate change is a long-term change in average temperatures and weather patterns, mainly caused by greenhouse gases from human activities.
- In Zimbabwe it means more erratic rainfall, droughts, higher temperatures and extreme events such as floods and cyclones.
- It reduces crop yields, harms livestock and lowers hydro-electricity output, so supply of many goods decreases (shift left) and prices rise.
- Mitigation reduces the causes (renewable energy, reforestation); adaptation helps producers cope (conservation agriculture, drought-tolerant crops, irrigation, insurance).
- Good answers link every effect or measure to production, costs and supply.
:::
