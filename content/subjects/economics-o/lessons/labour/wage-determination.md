## Introduction

Why does a qualified electrician in Harare earn more per day than a general hand on a building site, and why did the wages of builders rise when many new houses were being built around the city? A wage is a price: the price of labour. Like other prices, it is set by demand and supply, but in a **labour market**, where firms are the buyers and workers are the sellers.

This lesson applies the demand and supply model you already know to labour. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge examine it: you must explain why the demand for labour is derived, list what changes the demand for and supply of labour, find the equilibrium wage from a schedule, and describe what happens to the wage and employment when demand or supply changes. Trade unions and minimum wages, which interfere with this market, are covered in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Labour market"}
The market in which workers offer (supply) their labour and employers hire (demand) it. The price in this market is the wage rate.
:::

:::definition{term="Derived demand"}
Demand for something that comes from the demand for what it helps to produce. Firms want workers not for their own sake but because consumers want the goods and services the workers make.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand for labour"}
The number of workers (or hours of work) that employers are willing and able to hire at each wage rate in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply of labour"}
The number of workers (or hours of work) that people are willing and able to offer at each wage rate in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium wage"}
The wage rate at which the number of workers demanded equals the number of workers supplied, so there is neither a shortage nor a surplus of labour.
:::

## The demand for labour

The demand curve for labour slopes **downwards**: at a lower wage, firms can afford to hire more workers, and labour becomes cheaper compared with machines. A change in the wage causes a **movement along** the curve (an extension or contraction of demand). These factors **shift** the whole curve:

- **Demand for the product.** Because demand for labour is derived, more demand for houses raises the demand for bricklayers. This is the most important factor.
- **Productivity of labour.** If each worker produces more (better training, better tools), workers are worth more to the firm, so demand rises.
- **Price and productivity of capital.** If machines become cheaper or better and can replace workers, demand for labour may fall; if machines need operators, it may rise.
- **Other labour costs.** Taxes on employment, compulsory contributions and fringe benefits make each worker more expensive and can reduce demand.

## The supply of labour

The supply curve of labour to an occupation slopes **upwards**: a higher wage attracts more people into the job and encourages existing workers to offer more hours. These factors shift the supply curve:

- **Wages in other occupations.** If mining pays much more, some workers leave construction and supply to construction falls.
- **Non-wage factors.** Better working conditions, job security, holidays and status attract more workers.
- **Qualifications and training.** Long or costly training limits how many people can enter the job (doctors, pilots), so supply is low.
- **Size of the working population.** Population growth, immigration and changes in the school-leaving or retirement age change the number of people available. Emigration of skilled workers reduces supply.
- **Mobility of labour.** If workers can move easily between jobs and places, supply responds more quickly.

## Wage determination: the equilibrium

**Describing the diagram.** Put the wage rate on the vertical axis and the quantity of labour (number of workers) on the horizontal axis. Draw a downward-sloping demand curve for labour (DL) and an upward-sloping supply curve (SL). Where they cross, read off the equilibrium wage (W1) and the level of employment (Q1). Above W1 there is a surplus of labour (more people want the job than there are vacancies); below W1 there is a shortage of labour (unfilled vacancies), and employers raise wages to attract workers.

![Labour market for bricklayers: demand DL slopes down and supply SL slopes up and they cross at a wage of US\$14 a day with 480 workers; at US\$12 there is a shortage of 150 workers (540 wanted, 390 offered) and at US\$16 a surplus of 150 (570 offered, 420 wanted).](media:diagrams/bricklayers-wage-equilibrium.png "Figure: the equilibrium wage is where the demand for and supply of labour cross")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the market for bricklayers]
The table shows the daily market for bricklayers in a fictional town.

| Daily wage (US\$) | Bricklayers demanded | Bricklayers supplied |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 600 | 300 |
| 12 | 540 | 390 |
| 14 | 480 | 480 |
| 16 | 420 | 570 |
| 18 | 360 | 660 |

1. Find the row where demand equals supply: at US\$14 a day, 480 bricklayers are demanded and 480 supplied. The equilibrium wage is **US\$14** and employment is **480**.
2. At US\$12, 540 are demanded but only 390 supplied: a **shortage of labour** of $540 - 390 = 150$ bricklayers. Builders compete for workers and the wage rises.
3. At US\$16, 570 offer to work but only 420 are hired: a **surplus of labour** of $570 - 420 = 150$, so the wage falls.
4. The total daily wage bill at equilibrium is $14 \times 480 = 6{,}720$, that is US\$6,720.
:::

## Changes in demand and supply

When the demand curve shifts **right** (for example, a building boom), there is a shortage at the old wage; the wage rises and employment rises. When supply shifts **left** (for example, skilled workers emigrate), the wage rises but employment falls. The four basic cases are:

| Change | Shift | Wage | Employment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Demand for labour increases | DL right | rises | rises |
| Demand for labour decreases | DL left | falls | falls |
| Supply of labour increases | SL right | falls | rises |
| Supply of labour decreases | SL left | rises | falls |

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a building boom]
New housing projects raise the demand for bricklayers by 150 at every wage. Supply does not change.

| Daily wage (US\$) | Demanded before the boom | Supplied | Demanded after the boom |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 600 | 300 | 750 |
| 12 | 540 | 390 | 690 |
| 14 | 480 | 480 | 630 |
| 16 | 420 | 570 | 570 |
| 18 | 360 | 660 | 510 |

1. At the old wage of US\$14, demand is now 630 but supply is 480: a shortage of 150 bricklayers.
2. The new equilibrium is where 570 are demanded and 570 supplied: a wage of **US\$16**.
3. Employment rises from 480 to 570 (an extension of supply, a movement along SL).
4. The wage bill rises from US\$6,720 to $16 \times 570 = 9{,}120$, that is US\$9,120 a day.
5. In words for the diagram: the demand curve shifts right from DL1 to DL2; the equilibrium moves from W1/Q1 to a higher wage W2 and higher employment Q2.
:::

:::example[Applying the model: why some wages stay high]
Few people have the training to work as specialist doctors, and training takes many years, so the supply curve is far to the left and very inelastic. Demand for medical care is high. A high equilibrium wage results. For general farm labour, many people can do the work with little training, so supply is large and the wage is low, even though the work is hard.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting a curve when the wage changes"}
A change in the wage itself causes a movement **along** the demand or supply curve of labour (extension or contraction). Only other factors, such as demand for the product, shift the curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the buyers and sellers"}
In the labour market, **workers supply** labour and **firms demand** it. Households are the sellers here, the opposite of a product market.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting derived demand"}
"Demand for labour depends on the wage" is incomplete. Examiners expect you to link the demand for workers to the demand for the product they make.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify:** "Demand for labour is derived from the demand for the product" earns the mark; add an example (tobacco graders and demand for tobacco).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** follow the chain: cause → shift (which curve, which way) → shortage or surplus at the old wage → new wage and employment.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse with a diagram:** describe it fully: axes (wage, quantity of labour), DL and SL, the original W1/Q1, the shift, and the new W2/Q2.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** weigh up how far wages rise; for example, a rise in demand raises wages more when supply is inelastic (long training) than when many workers can enter easily.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A wage is the price of labour, set by the demand for and supply of labour in a labour market.
- Demand for labour is derived from demand for the product; it also depends on productivity, the cost of capital and other labour costs.
- Supply of labour depends on wages elsewhere, non-wage factors, training needed, the working population and mobility.
- The equilibrium wage is where labour demanded equals labour supplied; below it there is a shortage of labour, above it a surplus.
- More demand raises the wage and employment; less supply raises the wage but lowers employment.
:::
