## Introduction

A specialist surgeon may earn in a week what a farm worker earns in several months. A mine worker underground may earn more than a shop assistant with similar schooling. A nurse in a private clinic may earn more than one in a public hospital. These gaps are called **wage differentials**, and explaining them is a favourite exam question in both syllabuses.

The key idea is simple: a wage is the price of labour, so wages differ because the demand for and supply of different kinds of labour differ. Every other reason (training, danger, unions, discrimination, government policy) works through demand, supply or bargaining power. You should already know how a wage is set in a labour market.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Wage differential"}
A difference in wages between workers in different occupations, industries, regions, sectors or groups (for example men and women).
:::

:::definition{term="Discrimination (in the labour market)"}
Treating workers differently, for example paying them less or not promoting them, because of their sex, race, age, disability or other characteristic unrelated to their ability to do the job.
:::

:::definition{term="Compensating differential"}
Extra pay that an employer must offer to attract workers into a job with unattractive features, such as danger, dirt or unsocial hours.
:::

## Reasons why wages differ

### Demand and supply of labour

High wages occur where demand for labour is high and supply is low, as for experienced engineers when mining expands. Low wages occur where many people can do the work and demand is limited, as for general farm hands. Demand reflects how productive workers are and how much their output is worth; supply reflects how many people are able and willing to do the job.

### Skills, training and qualifications

Skilled jobs need long, costly training, so few people can enter (low supply), and skilled workers are more productive (high demand). People also expect to be paid back for years of study and lost earnings while training.

### Risk and working conditions

Dangerous or unpleasant jobs, such as underground mining or night security work, must offer higher pay (a compensating differential) to attract workers.

### Prestige and job satisfaction

Jobs with high status or satisfaction attract many applicants, which can keep their wages lower than the skill needed would suggest. Some respected jobs, such as clergy or teachers in some countries, are paid modestly for this reason.

### Bargaining strength

Workers in strong trade unions often win higher wages than similar workers without unions. Individual workers with rare skills also have strong bargaining power.

### Discrimination

Where employers pay women or other groups less for the same work, or keep them out of better-paid jobs, wages differ for reasons unrelated to productivity. Gender pay gaps also arise because women are more often in lower-paid occupations, work part-time, or take career breaks to care for children.

### Government policy

Minimum wages raise the lowest wages; equal pay laws aim to remove discrimination; the government sets public-sector pay scales; taxes and allowances change take-home pay.

### Culture and tradition

Custom can keep some groups out of certain jobs, and traditional views of "men's work" and "women's work" shape who trains for what.

## Patterns of differentials

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: reading a table of earnings]
The table shows average earnings in a fictional economy.

| Occupation | Sector | Skill level | Average monthly earnings (US\$) |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| General farm hand | Primary | Unskilled | 150 |
| Machine operator, food factory | Secondary | Semi-skilled | 320 |
| Electrician | Secondary | Skilled | 640 |
| Bank teller | Tertiary | Semi-skilled | 450 |
| Medical doctor (public hospital) | Tertiary | Skilled | 1,200 |

1. The doctor earns $1{,}200 \div 150 = 8$ times as much as the farm hand: the result of long training (low supply) and high demand for medical care.
2. Within the secondary sector, the electrician earns $(640 - 320) \div 320 \times 100 = 100\%$ more than the machine operator: skill and qualifications.
3. Skilled jobs earn the most in each sector; the lowest wage is in the primary sector, where many unskilled workers compete for jobs.
4. These are averages: some skilled farm managers earn more than some bank tellers.
:::

**By skill.** Skilled workers earn more than semi-skilled, who earn more than unskilled workers: training limits supply and raises productivity.

**By sector.** In many developing countries, primary-sector jobs (farming) pay the least because many unskilled workers compete for them and output per worker is low. Secondary and tertiary jobs often need more skills and capital and pay more, but the tertiary sector also includes low-paid jobs such as vending and cleaning.

**Private and public sectors.** Private firms may pay more for scarce skills (accountants, IT specialists) because they compete for them and can link pay to profit. Public-sector jobs often pay less at the top but offer more security, pensions and set pay scales; at the bottom, public-sector pay can be above private-sector pay because of minimum scales and union strength. Governments are limited by their budgets.

**By sex.** Women's average earnings are below men's in many countries, because of differences in occupations and hours, career breaks, and discrimination.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: measuring a gender pay gap]
In a fictional firm, men's average monthly pay is US\$540 and women's is US\$450.

1. The gap in dollars is $540 - 450 = 90$.
2. The gap as a percentage of men's pay is $90 \div 540 \times 100 = 16.7\%$ (to one decimal place).
3. Explanation needs evidence: if women do the same jobs for less pay, that suggests discrimination; if they are mostly in lower-paid grades, the cause may be fewer chances of promotion or training.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC lists the bases of differentials as training, risk, prestige, gender, government policy and culture. Cambridge asks why wages differ by skill, by sector (primary, secondary, tertiary), by sex, and between the private and public sectors. The same demand and supply reasons answer both.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hard work is always well paid"}
Effort alone does not set wages. Farm work is hard but low paid because supply is large; what matters is demand and supply of that labour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing reasons without explaining the link"}
"Doctors are well paid because of training" is incomplete. Add the link: long training means few doctors, so supply is low and the wage is high.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating every gender gap as discrimination"}
Part of a gap may come from differences in occupation and hours. Discrimination means unequal treatment for the same work or ability.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain** each differential in three steps: the feature (long training), its effect on demand or supply (fewer qualified workers), the result (higher wage).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Data questions:** calculate "how many times" with a division and a percentage difference with change ÷ the original (lower) value × 100, and say which value is the base.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a differential is fair or justified: consider training costs and productivity on one side, discrimination and bargaining power on the other.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wage differentials are differences in pay between occupations, sectors, regions and groups.
- They arise mainly from differences in the demand for and supply of different kinds of labour.
- Skills and training, risk, prestige, bargaining strength, discrimination, government policy and culture all affect demand, supply or bargaining power.
- Skilled workers earn more than unskilled; pay varies by sector, between private and public sectors, and by sex.
- Explain each reason through its effect on demand, supply or bargaining power.
:::
