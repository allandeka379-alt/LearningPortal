## Introduction

When a gold mine closes, some of its workers find jobs at another mine in a different province, some retrain as builders or drivers, and some remain unemployed for a long time. How easily workers can change jobs or move to where the jobs are is called the **mobility of labour**. It matters because economies are always changing: new industries grow, old ones decline, and new mines and factories open in some places while others close.

Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge examine mobility. You need to distinguish occupational from geographical mobility, explain what helps or hinders each, and explain the consequences for workers, firms and the whole economy. ZIMSEC links mobility to population and the labour force; Cambridge asks about the causes and consequences of **changes** in mobility.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mobility of labour"}
The ease with which workers can move between occupations or between places to work.
:::

:::definition{term="Occupational mobility"}
The ease with which workers can change from one type of job to another, for example from mining to construction.
:::

:::definition{term="Geographical mobility"}
The ease with which workers can move from one area to another to take up work, for example from Bulawayo to Harare or abroad.
:::

:::definition{term="Immobility of labour"}
A situation in which workers cannot easily change jobs or move to where jobs are, so unemployment and shortages of labour exist side by side.
:::

## Occupational mobility

Workers are occupationally mobile when their skills can be used in many jobs or they can retrain easily. Things that **reduce** occupational mobility:

- **Specific skills.** A tobacco grader's skill is of little use in a bank.
- **Cost and length of training.** Retraining as an electrician or nurse takes years and money.
- **Qualifications and licensing.** Many occupations require certificates or registration.
- **Lack of ability or education.** Some workers lack the basic education needed to retrain.
- **Age.** Older workers may find retraining harder and employers less willing to hire them.
- **Discrimination** that keeps some groups out of certain jobs.

Things that **increase** it: good general education (literacy, numeracy, digital skills), government retraining schemes, apprenticeships, and transferable skills such as driving or bookkeeping.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: is retraining worth it?]
Farai, a retrenched factory worker, can take a six-month welding course costing US\$600. Qualified welders earn about US\$75 a month more than he could earn without the course.

1. Extra earnings each month after qualifying: US\$75.
2. Months needed to recover the fee: $600 \div 75 = 8$ months.
3. So after the course and eight months of work, the training has paid for itself; after that every month adds US\$75.
4. If Farai cannot borrow the US\$600, or cannot live without income for six months, he may not retrain even though it pays. This is why the cost of training reduces occupational mobility and why government grants or loans can raise it.
:::

## Geographical mobility

Workers are geographically mobile when they can move to where jobs are. Things that **reduce** geographical mobility:

- **Housing costs and availability.** Rents in cities with many jobs are often much higher.
- **Family and social ties.** Children in school, a partner's job, caring for relatives, belonging to a community.
- **Cost of moving** and of travel.
- **Lack of information** about jobs elsewhere.
- **Differences in culture or language**, and, between countries, **immigration rules** and recognition of qualifications.

Things that **increase** it: better transport, affordable housing near jobs, job information online or through employment agencies, relocation allowances paid by employers, and regional agreements that make it easier to work in another country.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: why a higher wage may not be enough]
Thandeka works in Bulawayo and is offered a similar job in Harare.

| Monthly item (US\$) | Stay in Bulawayo | Move to Harare |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Wage | 430 | 520 |
| Rent | 90 | 170 |
| Transport to work | 25 | 35 |
| **Left after rent and transport** | **315** | **315** |

1. The Harare wage is US\$90 a month higher ($520 - 430$).
2. But rent is US\$80 higher and transport US\$10 higher: $80 + 10 = 90$.
3. After these costs she has US\$315 a month either way, so the higher wage gives no gain at all.
4. Add the cost of moving and leaving family and friends, and Thandeka is likely to stay. High housing costs reduce geographical mobility.
:::

## Changes in mobility and their consequences

Mobility changes over time. **Higher** mobility comes from better education and training, cheaper transport and communications, and better job information. **Lower** mobility can come from rising housing costs, stricter licensing or immigration rules, or skills becoming out of date as technology changes.

**Consequences for workers.**

- Mobile workers can move to better-paid jobs and escape unemployment.
- Immobile workers may stay unemployed or in low-paid work while vacancies exist elsewhere.
- Moving brings costs: housing, separation from family, the stress of change.

**Consequences for firms.**

- When labour is mobile, firms can fill vacancies more easily, at lower wage cost, and expand.
- When it is immobile, firms face skill shortages, must pay higher wages to attract workers, or must train them.
- Firms may lose staff they trained to competitors or to employers abroad.

**Consequences for the economy.**

- High mobility reduces **structural unemployment**, makes better use of resources and raises output.
- Immobility leaves unemployment in declining regions and shortages in growing ones.
- Geographical mobility between countries can cause a **brain drain**: skilled workers, such as doctors and engineers, leave for higher pay abroad, reducing the home country's skilled labour, although money they send home (remittances) supports families and adds to foreign currency inflows.

:::example[Mobility within southern Africa]
Many Zimbabwean workers have moved to neighbouring countries for work, and money sent home supports many families. For Zimbabwe this is high geographical mobility: individual workers gain higher wages and their families gain income, but the country loses some of the skills it paid to train. The receiving country gains workers for jobs that were hard to fill. Explain both sides when a question asks for consequences.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two types"}
Changing **job** is occupational mobility; changing **place** is geographical mobility. A teacher moving from Mutare to Gweru to teach is geographically mobile; a teacher becoming an accountant is occupationally mobile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming mobility is always good for a country"}
High international mobility can mean a loss of skilled workers. Consider who gains and who loses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing barriers without saying which type"}
Housing costs and family ties reduce **geographical** mobility; lack of skills and training costs reduce **occupational** mobility. Say which.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain** a barrier: name it, say which type of mobility it affects, and explain why ("high city rents mean a higher wage may leave a worker no better off, so fewer move").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse consequences** for three groups (workers, firms, the economy), using links to unemployment, wages and output.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** a policy to raise mobility (retraining grants, housing, job information): who benefits, cost, time to work, and a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mobility of labour is how easily workers change jobs (occupational) or places (geographical).
- Occupational mobility is limited by specific skills, training costs, qualifications, age and discrimination.
- Geographical mobility is limited by housing costs, family ties, moving costs, lack of information and immigration rules.
- Higher mobility reduces structural unemployment and skill shortages and raises output.
- Moving abroad can mean higher incomes and remittances, but also a brain drain of skilled workers.
:::
