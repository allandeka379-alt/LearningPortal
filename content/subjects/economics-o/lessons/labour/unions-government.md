## Introduction

In many workplaces wages are not set by one worker bargaining alone with an employer. Workers join **trade unions**, employers join **employers' associations**, and the two sides negotiate. The government may also step in, for example by setting a **minimum wage** below which no employer may pay. In Zimbabwe, sector-wide wage agreements are negotiated in National Employment Councils, where unions and employers' organisations meet, and national labour issues are discussed by government, business and labour together.

This lesson explains who the parties are, how collective bargaining works, what gives each side bargaining power, and how a minimum wage affects a labour market. Cambridge asks how union bargaining power and government policy, including a national minimum wage, affect wages. ZIMSEC also asks you to identify employers' and employees' associations and explain their roles. You should already know how the equilibrium wage is set by demand and supply.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade union"}
An organisation of workers that acts together to protect and improve its members' pay, working conditions and job security.
:::

:::definition{term="Employers' association"}
An organisation of employers in an industry or across the economy that represents their interests, for example in wage negotiations and in discussions with government.
:::

:::definition{term="Workers' committee"}
A group of employees elected at one workplace to represent the workers in dealings with management.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective bargaining"}
Negotiation over pay and conditions between a trade union, on behalf of its members, and an employer or employers' association.
:::

:::definition{term="National minimum wage"}
A legal lowest wage that employers must pay, set by the government. It is a minimum price (price floor) in the labour market.
:::

## The parties in wage setting

**Trade unions** have several roles:

- Negotiating wages and fringe benefits through collective bargaining.
- Improving working conditions: health and safety, hours, holidays.
- Protecting job security and representing members in disputes and disciplinary hearings.
- Providing services: legal advice, training, education, sometimes welfare benefits.
- Influencing government policy on labour laws, minimum wages and pensions.

**Workers' committees** do similar work at the level of a single workplace, raising workers' complaints with management and making sure agreements are applied.

**Employers' associations** negotiate with unions for the whole industry, advise members on labour law, train managers, and speak for business to the government.

## Collective bargaining and bargaining power

When the two sides disagree they may use **industrial action**. Workers may hold an overtime ban, a work-to-rule (doing only the minimum the contract requires), a go-slow or a strike. Employers may refuse to raise pay, threaten to lay off workers or, in some countries, lock workers out. Disputes may go to conciliation or arbitration, where an independent person helps the sides agree or decides for them.

A union's **bargaining power** is stronger when:

- A large share of the workers belong to it.
- Its members' skills are hard to replace, so a strike would be costly to the employer.
- The firm is profitable and demand for its product is strong.
- Labour costs are a small part of the firm's total costs.
- The law protects the right to strike and to bargain collectively.

Union power is weaker when unemployment is high (strikers can be replaced), when machines can replace workers, or when a firm faces strong competition and cannot pass on higher costs.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the cost of a negotiated pay rise]
A union representing 400 workers at a fictional food factory wins a 6% pay rise on a basic wage of US\$250 a month.

1. The rise per worker is $250 \times 0.06 = 15$ dollars, so the new wage is US\$265 a month.
2. The extra monthly wage bill is $15 \times 400 = 6{,}000$, that is US\$6,000.
3. Over a year this costs the firm $6{,}000 \times 12 = 72{,}000$, that is US\$72,000.
4. The firm may pay this from profit, raise its prices, or try to cut jobs. The higher the share of wages in its costs, the more likely it is to resist.
:::

## Government intervention in wage setting

Governments influence wages by:

- Setting **minimum wages**, nationally or for particular sectors.
- Passing **labour laws** on hours, leave, health and safety and dismissal.
- Acting as an **employer**: civil service pay affects wages elsewhere.
- Running **conciliation and arbitration** services, and bringing government, employers and unions together to agree on wages and prices.
- Sometimes restraining wage rises to fight inflation.

### A national minimum wage

A minimum wage set **below** the equilibrium wage has no effect. Set **above** it, it raises the pay of low-paid workers but creates a **surplus of labour**: more people want work at the higher wage, but employers hire fewer.

**Describing the diagram.** Wage on the vertical axis, quantity of labour on the horizontal axis. Downward-sloping DL and upward-sloping SL cross at W1 and Q1. Draw a horizontal line labelled "minimum wage" **above** W1. It meets DL at Qd (workers hired) and SL at Qs (workers who want jobs). The gap Qd to Qs is the surplus of labour (unemployment); employment falls from Q1 to Qd.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a minimum wage for farm workers]
The monthly market for general farm workers in a fictional district:

| Monthly wage (US\$) | Farm workers demanded | Farm workers supplied |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 100 | 1,600 | 800 |
| 120 | 1,400 | 1,000 |
| 140 | 1,200 | 1,200 |
| 160 | 1,000 | 1,400 |
| 180 | 800 | 1,600 |

1. Equilibrium: US\$140 a month, with 1,200 workers employed.
2. The government sets a minimum wage of US\$160. Farms hire only 1,000 workers (a contraction of demand), but 1,400 people want work (an extension of supply).
3. Surplus of labour: $1{,}400 - 1{,}000 = 400$ workers.
4. Employment falls by $1{,}200 - 1{,}000 = 200$. Those 1,000 still employed earn US\$20 a month more.
5. A minimum wage of US\$120 would have no effect, because it is below US\$140.
:::

**Advantages of a minimum wage:** it reduces poverty among low-paid workers, prevents exploitation where workers have little bargaining power, may raise motivation and productivity, and raises spending in the economy.

**Disadvantages:** it may cause unemployment (as above), especially among the young and unskilled; firms' costs and prices rise; some work moves into the informal sector where the law is not enforced; and firms may replace workers with machines. How large the job losses are depends on how far above equilibrium the minimum is set and how elastic the demand for labour is.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a minimum wage always causes unemployment"}
It only affects the market when set **above** the equilibrium wage, and the job losses are small if demand for labour is inelastic or if higher pay raises productivity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing employers' associations with trade unions"}
Unions represent **workers**; employers' associations represent **employers**. Both take part in collective bargaining, on opposite sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a union as only organising strikes"}
Strikes are a last resort. Most union work is negotiating, representing members and improving conditions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trade unions and workers' committees represent workers; employers' associations represent employers.
- Collective bargaining is negotiation between unions and employers over pay and conditions; industrial action is used when talks fail.
- Union bargaining power depends on membership, skills, the firm's profits, the share of wages in costs and the law.
- Government affects wages through minimum wages, labour laws, its own pay and arbitration.
- A minimum wage above equilibrium raises pay for those in work but creates a surplus of labour.
:::
