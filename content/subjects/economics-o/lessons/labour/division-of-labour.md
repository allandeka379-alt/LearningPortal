## Introduction

Walk through a busy clothing workshop in Bulawayo and you will see that nobody makes a whole shirt. One person cuts the cloth, another sews the collars, another the sleeves, another adds buttons, and someone else irons and packs. Each worker does one small part of the job, again and again. This is the **division of labour**, and it is one of the main reasons modern economies can produce so much.

Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge expect you to define specialisation and the division of labour, give examples, and explain the advantages and disadvantages. Questions often ask you to consider different groups: the workers themselves, the firm, consumers and the economy as a whole. ZIMSEC teaches this topic in Form 2 under Production; Cambridge places it under workers in the microeconomy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Specialisation"}
Concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services, or on one task, instead of trying to produce everything. People, firms, regions and countries can all specialise.
:::

:::definition{term="Division of labour"}
Breaking a production process into separate tasks, with each worker (or group of workers) concentrating on one task. It is specialisation by workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Interdependence"}
A situation in which workers, firms or countries rely on each other, because each produces only part of what is needed.
:::

## Specialisation at different levels

Specialisation happens at several levels:

- **By individuals (division of labour).** A doctor does not grow her own food or build her own house; she treats patients and buys what she needs with her income.
- **By firms.** A firm may produce only one product, such as bricks or school uniforms.
- **By regions.** Some areas specialise because of their resources, for example mining in areas with mineral deposits, or tourism around Victoria Falls.
- **By countries.** Countries specialise in what they produce relatively well and trade for the rest (this links to international trade).

### Examples you can use

- **At home:** one family member cooks, another fetches water, another cleans; the work is finished faster than if one person did everything.
- **At school:** subject teachers specialise in mathematics, science or languages; the head teacher manages; the bursar keeps the accounts; groundsmen look after the fields.
- **In industry:** on a vehicle assembly line, workers fit wheels, seats or wiring; in a bakery, one person mixes dough, another runs the ovens, another packs bread.

## Advantages of the division of labour

**For workers**

- Workers become **skilled and quick** at their task through repetition ("practice makes perfect").
- Training is **shorter and cheaper**, because each worker learns only one task.
- People can choose a task suited to their **talents**.

**For firms**

- **Higher output per worker** (higher productivity), so the cost per unit falls.
- **Time saved** because workers do not move between tasks and tools.
- It becomes worthwhile to use **specialised machinery**, because each task is simple and repeated.
- Easier to recruit, since simple tasks need less training.

**For consumers and the economy**

- More goods at **lower prices** and often of a more consistent quality.
- Higher total output, which can raise living standards.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: output before and after the division of labour]
A fictional furniture workshop in Gweru employs five carpenters. At first each carpenter makes whole chairs. The owner then divides the work into five tasks: cutting, shaping, joining, sanding and finishing.

| Method | Workers | Chairs per day | Chairs per worker per day |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Each carpenter makes whole chairs | 5 | 15 | 3 |
| Division of labour (cut, shape, join, sand, finish) | 5 | 24 | 4.80 |

1. Output per worker before: $15 \div 5 = 3$ chairs per day.
2. Output per worker after: $24 \div 5 = 4.8$ chairs per day.
3. Percentage increase in output: $(24 - 15) \div 15 \times 100 = 60\%$.
4. With the same five workers and the same wages, the wage cost of each chair falls, because the wages are spread over 24 chairs instead of 15. This is the main reason firms divide work.
:::

## Disadvantages of the division of labour

**For workers**

- **Boredom and monotony** from repeating the same task, which can lower morale and lead to mistakes or absence.
- **Loss of craftsmanship:** workers no longer have the skill or satisfaction of making a whole product.
- **Narrow skills reduce occupational mobility:** if the task disappears, the worker may struggle to find another job.
- Greater risk of **replacement by machines**, because simple repeated tasks are easy to mechanise.
- Repetitive strain and other health problems from doing the same movement all day.

**For firms**

- **Interdependence:** if one stage stops (a worker is absent, a machine breaks, a strike), the whole production line stops.
- Bored workers may have **lower motivation**, higher absence and higher staff turnover.

**For consumers and the economy**

- **Standardised products** with less variety.
- A region or country that specialises heavily is **vulnerable** if demand for its product falls or a new technique replaces it.

:::example[Worked example 2: a school uniform business]
Rudo and Chipo run a small business in Masvingo making school uniforms. Rudo is quicker at cutting and Chipo at sewing. When each made complete uniforms, they finished about ten a day between them. Now Rudo cuts all the cloth and Chipo does all the sewing, and they finish fourteen a day.

- **Advantage:** each uses her best skill, and neither wastes time changing tasks and tools, so output rises.
- **Disadvantage:** when Chipo was sick for three days, Rudo's cut cloth piled up and no uniforms were finished, showing interdependence.
- **Evaluation:** for a small business the gain in output is large, but the owners should make sure each of them can still do the other task in an emergency.
:::

## Limits to the division of labour

The division of labour is limited by:

- **The size of the market.** If a firm sells only a few products, dividing the work into many specialised tasks is not worthwhile; a village tailor does every task herself.
- **The nature of the product.** Hand-made crafts and personal services (such as hairdressing or counselling) are hard to divide.
- **The availability of capital** to equip each stage.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating specialisation and division of labour as the same in every case"}
Division of labour is specialisation **by workers** within a production process. Specialisation is wider: firms, regions and countries can specialise too.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'workers become more productive' without explaining why"}
Explain the reason: repetition builds skill and speed, no time is lost moving between tasks, and machines can be used for each simple task.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only the advantages"}
A discuss question needs both sides. Monotony, interdependence and loss of occupational mobility are the most commonly credited disadvantages.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State** or **identify** questions: one word or phrase is enough, for example "increased output per worker" or "boredom".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an advantage or disadvantage: name it, then give the reason and the effect ("workers repeat one task, so they become faster, which raises output per worker and lowers the cost per unit").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** whether a firm should introduce the division of labour: weigh higher output and lower costs against worker boredom, interdependence and the size of the market, and give a conclusion that depends on the firm's situation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Specialisation means concentrating on a limited range of goods, services or tasks; the division of labour is specialisation by workers within a production process.
- Examples occur at home, at school and in industry (assembly lines, bakeries, clothing workshops).
- Advantages: greater skill and speed, less time wasted, use of machinery, higher output per worker, lower costs and prices.
- Disadvantages: monotony, loss of craft skill, narrow skills that reduce occupational mobility, interdependence, standardised products.
- The division of labour is limited by the size of the market, the nature of the product and the capital available.
:::
