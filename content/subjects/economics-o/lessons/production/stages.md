## Introduction

A cotton farmer in the Gokwe area grows and picks seed cotton. A ginnery separates the fibre (lint) from the seed. A mill spins the lint into yarn and weaves cloth, a factory sews the cloth into shirts, and a shop in Harare sells the shirts. Every step turns something into a more valuable product, and every step depends on the others. Economists group these steps into three **stages of production**.

ZIMSEC asks you to outline the three stages with examples, describe how they depend on each other, explain **value addition** and why it matters to Zimbabwe, and distinguish **direct** from **indirect** production. Cambridge asks you to classify firms as primary, secondary or tertiary sector firms.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC speaks of the primary, secondary and tertiary **stages of production**; Cambridge classifies **primary, secondary and tertiary sector firms**. The three groups are the same.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production"}
Any activity that creates goods or services to satisfy wants, including making, moving, storing and selling them.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary production"}
Extracting or growing natural resources: farming, fishing, forestry and mining.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary production"}
Turning raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods: manufacturing, processing, construction, and the generation of electricity.
:::

:::definition{term="Tertiary production"}
Providing services, both commercial services that help trade (transport, banking, insurance, retailing, communication, advertising) and direct services to people (teaching, health care, hairdressing, policing).
:::

:::definition{term="Value addition"}
Increasing the value of a product by processing it further, so that it sells for more than the cost of the inputs bought to make it.
:::

## The three stages with examples

| Stage (sector) | What happens | Zimbabwean examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Natural resources are extracted or grown | Growing maize and tobacco, cattle ranching, gold and platinum mining, fishing at Kariba, timber in the Eastern Highlands |
| Secondary | Raw materials are processed or manufactured | Milling maize into mealie-meal, making cooking oil, smelting ores, brewing, building houses and roads |
| Tertiary | Services are provided | Kombi transport, banking and mobile money, retail shops and vendors, tourism at Victoria Falls, schools and clinics |

## How the stages depend on each other

The three stages are **interdependent**:

- **Secondary needs primary:** a mill cannot make mealie-meal without maize, and a smelter needs ore.
- **Primary needs secondary:** farmers need fertiliser, tools and tractors; mines need explosives and machinery.
- **Both need tertiary:** goods must be transported, stored, financed, insured, advertised and sold. Without banks and transport, farmers and factories cannot reach their customers.
- **Tertiary needs the others:** shops need goods to sell; transport firms need fuel and vehicles.

So a drought that cuts the maize harvest (primary) also reduces work for millers (secondary) and for transporters and retailers (tertiary).

## Value addition

Value is added at each stage when a firm sells its output for more than it paid for its inputs. **Value added = value of output − cost of inputs bought from other firms.** The total value added along a chain equals the price the final consumer pays.

![Value added along a cotton-to-shirt chain: the farmer adds 120, the ginnery 80, the mill 140, the clothing factory 220 and the retailer 140; each bar rises from the cost of inputs to the value of output and the total value added is 700, the price consumers pay for the shirts.](media:diagrams/value-added-cotton-chain.png "Figure: value is added at every stage, and the parts add up to the final price of US\$700")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: value added along a cotton chain]
The table follows one batch of cotton from a fictional farm to the shirts made from it.

| Stage | Sector | Cost of inputs bought (US\$) | Value of output sold (US\$) | Value added (US\$) |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Farmer grows seed cotton | Primary | 0 | 120 | 120 |
| Ginnery separates lint from seed | Secondary | 120 | 200 | 80 |
| Spinning mill makes yarn and cloth | Secondary | 200 | 340 | 140 |
| Clothing factory makes shirts | Secondary | 340 | 560 | 220 |
| Retailer sells shirts to consumers | Tertiary | 560 | 700 | 140 |
| **Total** |  |  |  | **700** |

1. Each stage's value added is its sales minus what it paid for inputs; for the clothing factory, $560 - 340 = 220$.
2. Adding all the stages: $120 + 80 + 140 + 220 + 140 = 700$, the price consumers pay for the shirts.
3. If the cotton is exported as seed cotton, the country earns only the first US\$120 of value. If it is processed into shirts at home, US\$700 of value is created in the country, together with the jobs and incomes at each stage. (For simplicity, the farmer's own inputs such as seed and fertiliser are ignored.)
:::

### Why value addition matters to Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has long exported many products in raw or semi-processed form: unmanufactured tobacco, seed cotton and lint, and mineral ores and concentrates. Government policy has encouraged **beneficiation** (processing minerals) and value addition in agriculture. The benefits include:

- **More jobs**, in factories and in the services that support them.
- **Higher export earnings**, because processed goods sell for more than raw materials, and so more foreign currency.
- **Less dependence on imports** of processed goods.
- **Growth of skills and technology**, and more tax revenue.
- **Less vulnerability** to swings in world prices of raw materials.

The obstacles include the high cost of machinery and power, unreliable electricity supply, shortages of skills and finance, and competition from established foreign processors.

:::example[Worked example 2: should a mine refine its ore at home?]
A fictional mining company in Zimbabwe sells a tonne of ore concentrate abroad for US\$900. If it built a refinery, the refined metal from the same tonne would sell for US\$2,600.

- **Value that could be added at home:** $2{,}600 - 900 = 1{,}700$ dollars a tonne, before the refinery's own costs of power, chemicals and labour.
- **For the economy:** refining creates skilled jobs and raises export earnings.
- **Against:** a refinery is expensive to build, needs a reliable power supply and skilled staff, and the extra value is only a gain if it exceeds those costs.
- **Conclusion:** value addition is worthwhile when the extra value is larger than the extra costs; government can help by improving power supply and training.
:::

## Direct and indirect production

- **Direct production** means producing goods for your own use, for example a family growing maize to eat or building its own hut. There is no exchange and no specialisation; living standards are usually low because each household must do everything.
- **Indirect production** means producing goods or services for sale or exchange, and using the income to buy what you need. It depends on specialisation, money and trade, and most modern production is indirect.

Rural subsistence farming in Zimbabwe is largely direct production, while a commercial farmer who sells her crop and buys her household goods takes part in indirect production.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting construction or electricity in the wrong sector"}
Building and electricity generation are **secondary** activities: they turn materials and fuel into a product. Mining and farming are primary; transport and banking are tertiary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding up sales instead of value added"}
Adding every firm's sales counts the same inputs several times. Only the value added at each stage adds up to the final price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating value addition as free"}
Processing raises the value of a product but also adds costs. Explain both when discussing it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Primary production extracts or grows natural resources; secondary production processes and manufactures; tertiary production provides services.
- The stages depend on each other for inputs, equipment and markets.
- Value added = value of output − cost of inputs bought; total value added along a chain equals the final price.
- Value addition creates jobs, raises export earnings and reduces dependence on raw-material prices, but needs capital, power and skills.
- Direct production is for one's own use; indirect production is for sale or exchange.
:::
