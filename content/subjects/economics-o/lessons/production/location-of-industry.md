## Introduction

Why are Zimbabwe's sugar mills in the Lowveld, close to the cane fields, while many clothing, food and printing firms are in Harare and Bulawayo? Why is a large thermal power station at Hwange? Firms choose locations that keep their costs low and give them access to customers, and governments try to influence those choices to spread jobs and development across the country.

ZIMSEC (Form 4) asks you to explain the factors affecting the location of industry, and how and why government influences location. Answers score well when they explain **why** a factor matters for a particular kind of firm, not just list factors.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Location of industry"}
The place where a firm or industry sets up its production, chosen to minimise costs and maximise access to markets and resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Weight-losing (bulk-reducing) industry"}
An industry whose raw materials are heavier or bulkier than its finished product, such as sugar milling or ore smelting. Such firms tend to locate near their raw materials.
:::

:::definition{term="Weight-gaining (bulk-increasing) industry"}
An industry whose finished product is heavier, bulkier or more fragile than its inputs, such as brewing, soft drinks or furniture. Such firms tend to locate near their markets.
:::

:::definition{term="Growth point"}
A selected small town or rural centre where government concentrates investment in infrastructure and services to attract firms and spread development away from the large cities.
:::

## Factors affecting the location of industry

### Raw materials

Firms that use heavy or bulky raw materials, or materials that perish quickly, locate close to them: sugar mills near cane estates, cotton ginneries in cotton-growing areas, smelters near mines, dairies near dairy farms.

### Markets

Firms whose products are bulky, fragile or perishable, or that provide services, locate close to their customers: bakeries, breweries, soft-drink bottlers, and most shops and banks. Large towns offer many customers within easy reach.

### Labour

Firms need workers with the right skills at an acceptable wage. Skilled labour is concentrated in large towns; plentiful cheaper labour may be found in rural areas or smaller towns.

### Power

Industries that use a great deal of energy, such as smelting and heavy engineering, locate where electricity or coal is reliable and affordable. In the past, coal-using industries located near coalfields; today the national grid allows wider choice, but reliability still matters.

### Transport and communications

Good roads, railways, airports and communications reduce the cost of bringing in inputs and delivering output. Firms value sites near main roads, railway lines and border posts.

### Water

Some industries use large volumes of water: breweries, paper mills, textile processing, food processing and power stations. They need a reliable supply.

### Other factors

- **Land:** cost and availability of suitable sites.
- **External economies of scale:** being near related firms, suppliers, banks and repair services.
- **Climate and personal preference** of owners.
- **Government policy** (see below).

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: where should a sugar mill be built?]
A fictional company plans a sugar mill. Every 100 tonnes of cane produce 10 tonnes of sugar. The cane fields are 200 km from the main town market, and transport costs US\$0.50 per tonne per kilometre for both cane and sugar.

| Site of the mill | Cost of carrying cane (US\$) | Cost of carrying sugar (US\$) | Total transport cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Next to the cane fields | 0 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| Next to the town market | 10,000 | 0 | 10,000 |

1. Mill at the fields: only the sugar travels. $10 \times 200 \times 0.50 = 1{,}000$ dollars.
2. Mill at the market: all the cane travels. $100 \times 200 \times 0.50 = 10{,}000$ dollars.
3. The mill should be built next to the cane fields, saving US\$9,000 for every 100 tonnes of cane. Sugar milling is a **weight-losing** industry, so it is pulled towards its raw material. Cane also deteriorates quickly once cut, which strengthens the case.
:::

:::example[Worked example 2: a soft-drinks bottling plant]
A fictional bottler uses water, sugar, flavouring and bottles. Water is available in most towns, and the finished product, full bottles of liquid, is much heavier and bulkier than the concentrate and sugar. The bottler should locate **near its market**, in or close to a large town, because carrying the finished drinks long distances would cost far more than carrying the concentrated ingredients. It also needs a reliable water supply and good roads for distribution. This is a **weight-gaining** industry.
:::

## How and why government influences location

### Why government intervenes

- **To reduce regional inequality:** spread jobs and incomes beyond the main cities and reduce rural poverty.
- **To reduce rural-urban migration** and the pressure it puts on housing, water and services in towns.
- **To reduce congestion and pollution** in crowded urban areas.
- **To develop natural resources** in remote areas and use them fully.
- **For strategic and environmental reasons**, such as keeping hazardous industries away from homes.

### How government influences location

1. **Incentives (the "carrot").** Tax holidays or lower tax rates, grants and cheap loans, subsidised land or factory buildings for firms that locate in chosen areas. Special economic zones offer such incentives in defined areas.
2. **Infrastructure.** Building roads, railways, power lines, water supplies, telecommunications and housing makes an area attractive to firms.
3. **Growth points.** Concentrating investment and services in selected rural centres so that they grow into business centres serving the surrounding area. Zimbabwe has used a growth-point policy since independence.
4. **Restrictions (the "stick").** Planning permission and zoning laws that keep industry out of residential areas or crowded city centres; environmental rules about where polluting firms may operate.
5. **Locating public-sector activities** (offices, colleges, state enterprises) in chosen areas.

### Limits of government policy

Incentives cost the government money (the opportunity cost of other spending), firms may leave when incentives end, and remote areas may still lack skilled workers and markets. A firm locating in an unsuitable place may have higher costs and be less competitive.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without explaining them"}
"Raw materials" is not enough. Say why: "sugar cane is bulky and perishable and loses most of its weight in milling, so the mill locates near the cane fields".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the type of firm"}
The pull of raw materials and markets depends on whether the industry is weight-losing or weight-gaining. Service firms locate near customers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing government policy as always successful"}
Incentives and growth points have costs and limits; a discuss question needs both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State** a factor: one word or phrase ("nearness to market"). **Explain**: give the reason and a suitable example of a firm.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain why** government influences location: name the aim (regional balance, less migration, less congestion) and how location policy achieves it.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** a policy such as growth points: benefits for jobs and development against cost, weak markets and lack of skills, ending with a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Firms choose locations that minimise costs and give access to markets and inputs.
- Factors: raw materials, markets, labour, power, transport, water, land, external economies and government policy.
- Weight-losing industries locate near raw materials; weight-gaining and service industries locate near markets.
- Government influences location to reduce regional inequality, migration and congestion and to develop resources.
- Tools: incentives, infrastructure, growth points, restrictions and the siting of public-sector activities; each has costs and limits.
:::
