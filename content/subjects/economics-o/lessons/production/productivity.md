## Introduction

Two brickworks near Masvingo both sell bricks to builders. One produces more bricks each week, but it also employs many more workers. Which one is more **productive**? Answering this needs a clear distinction between **production** (how much is made) and **productivity** (how much is made per unit of input). The difference matters for firms, because higher productivity lowers costs, and for the whole economy, because it is the main source of rising living standards.

Both boards examine the difference between production and productivity, the influences on productivity and the effect of investment. ZIMSEC (Form 4) also asks you to calculate **total, average and marginal product** from a table, a common Paper 2 task.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production"}
The total output of goods and services produced by a firm, industry or economy in a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
Output per unit of input in a period of time. **Labour productivity** is output per worker (or per worker-hour).
:::

:::definition{term="Total product (TP)"}
The total output produced by a given number of workers (ZIMSEC).
:::

:::definition{term="Average product (AP)"}
Output per worker: total product divided by the number of workers (ZIMSEC).
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal product (MP)"}
The change in total product when one more worker is employed (ZIMSEC).
:::

## Production versus productivity

Production can rise simply because more workers are employed, even if each one produces less. Productivity rises only when each unit of input produces more. A firm, or a country, can have high production but low productivity.

$$\text{Labour productivity} = \frac{\text{total output}}{\text{number of workers (or worker-hours)}}$$

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: comparing two brickworks]
Two fictional brickworks each run a 40-hour week.

| Firm | Bricks per week | Workers | Bricks per worker per week |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Brickworks A | 36,000 | 20 | 1,800 |
| Brickworks B | 45,000 | 30 | 1,500 |

1. Production: B makes more bricks ($45{,}000 > 36{,}000$).
2. Productivity of A: $36{,}000 \div 20 = 1{,}800$ bricks per worker per week.
3. Productivity of B: $45{,}000 \div 30 = 1{,}500$ bricks per worker per week.
4. Per worker-hour: A makes $1{,}800 \div 40 = 45$ bricks, B makes $1{,}500 \div 40 = 37.5$ bricks.
5. So B has the higher **production**, but A has the higher **productivity**. If wages are equal, A's labour cost per brick is lower.
:::

## Total, average and marginal product (ZIMSEC)

When a firm adds workers to a fixed amount of land or capital, output changes in a typical pattern. At first each extra worker adds more than the one before, because workers can specialise and use the fixed equipment better. Eventually each extra worker adds less, because the fixed factor becomes crowded. This is often called **diminishing returns**.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a market garden]
A fictional market garden near Mutare has a fixed plot of land and tools. The table shows weekly output (crates of vegetables) as workers are added.

| Workers | Total product (crates) | Average product | Marginal product |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | – | – |
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 20 |
| 2 | 50 | 25 | 30 |
| 3 | 90 | 30 | 40 |
| 4 | 120 | 30 | 30 |
| 5 | 140 | 28 | 20 |
| 6 | 150 | 25 | 10 |

1. **AP** with 5 workers: $140 \div 5 = 28$ crates per worker.
2. **MP** of the 4th worker: $120 - 90 = 30$ crates.
3. MP rises to a maximum of 40 (the 3rd worker), then falls: diminishing returns set in after the 3rd worker.
4. AP is highest (30) with 3 and 4 workers. When MP is above AP (up to the 3rd worker) AP rises; when MP is below AP (from the 5th worker) AP falls; when MP equals AP (the 4th worker, 30 = 30) AP stays the same.
5. TP keeps rising as long as MP is positive, but more and more slowly.
:::

If you describe these figures as a graph, put the number of workers on the horizontal axis and output on the vertical axis. The TP curve rises steeply at first, then more gently. The MP curve rises, peaks at the third worker and then falls, cutting the AP curve at the highest point of AP.

## Influences on productivity

- **Quantity and quality of capital.** Workers with modern machinery, tractors or computers produce more.
- **Education, training and skills** (human capital).
- **Motivation**: wages, bonuses, working conditions and job security.
- **Technology and methods of production**, including the division of labour.
- **Management and organisation**: planning, layout and maintenance.
- **Health and nutrition** of workers.
- **Quality of land and raw materials**, and of infrastructure such as reliable power, water, roads and communications.

## Ways of improving productivity

- **Investment** in new capital goods and technology.
- **Training** and education of workers.
- **Incentive schemes** such as piece rates and bonuses.
- **Better management**, maintenance and work organisation.
- **Government action**: spending on education, health and infrastructure, and support for research.

### The effect of investment

**Investment** means spending on capital goods: machinery, equipment, buildings and technology. It raises productivity because each worker has more and better capital to work with. For example, if a maize mill installs a faster milling machine and output per worker rises from 1,800 kg to 2,250 kg a day, labour productivity has risen by $(2{,}250 - 1{,}800) \div 1{,}800 \times 100 = 25\%$. Investment also raises the economy's capacity to produce, shifting the production possibility curve outwards over time. But it has costs: the money must be found, workers may need retraining, and some jobs may be replaced by machines.

## Why productivity matters

- **For firms:** lower cost per unit, higher profit, ability to cut prices and compete, including in export markets.
- **For workers:** more productive workers can be paid higher wages.
- **For the economy:** higher output per person raises living standards and supports economic growth; it also makes exports more competitive.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing production with productivity"}
More output is not the same as higher productivity. Always divide output by the input.
:::

:::mistake{title="Working out marginal product from the wrong rows"}
MP is the **change** in TP when one more worker is added, so subtract the previous row's TP from this row's TP. AP is TP divided by the number of workers in the same row.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying diminishing returns means output falls"}
Diminishing returns means each extra worker adds **less** than the one before. Total output still rises while MP is positive.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Production is total output; productivity is output per unit of input, such as output per worker or per worker-hour.
- TP is total output; AP = TP ÷ workers; MP = change in TP from one more worker (ZIMSEC).
- With a fixed factor, MP rises at first and then falls (diminishing returns); AP rises while MP is above it and falls when MP is below it.
- Productivity depends on capital, skills, motivation, technology, management, health and infrastructure.
- Investment in capital goods raises productivity and capacity, lowering costs and supporting growth.
:::
