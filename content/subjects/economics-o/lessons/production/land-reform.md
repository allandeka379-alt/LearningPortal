## Introduction

Land is the most important factor of production for a country where most people depend on farming. In Zimbabwe, who owns and uses land has been a central economic and political question since independence in 1980. From 2000 the government carried out the **fast-track land reform programme**, which transferred a large amount of commercial farmland to new farmers. It changed the structure of Zimbabwean agriculture, and economists still debate its effects.

ZIMSEC asks you to explain what land reform is, identify the **aims** of Zimbabwe's programme and outline its **economic effects**. Examiners reward balanced answers: state the aims, explain recognised benefits and recognised problems, and reach a reasoned conclusion. Avoid slogans and blame, and do not quote figures unless you know they come from an official source such as ZIMSTAT.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
A deliberate change by government in the ownership, distribution or use of land, usually to make access to land fairer and to use land more productively.
:::

:::definition{term="Land redistribution"}
Transferring land from some owners or users to others, for example from large estates to landless or land-short households.
:::

:::definition{term="Land tenure"}
The terms on which land is held: freehold title, leasehold, permits, or communal land held under traditional authority.
:::

## Background

At independence, a large share of the best-watered, most fertile farmland was held as large commercial farms by a relatively small number of owners, while most rural families farmed in crowded communal areas with poorer soils and lower rainfall. Land reform in the 1980s and 1990s resettled some families on land bought by the government on a "willing buyer, willing seller" basis. From 2000 the **fast-track** programme acquired land much more quickly and on a much larger scale, allocating it to small-scale (A1) and medium-to-large commercial (A2) farmers.

## Aims of land reform

The main aims of Zimbabwe's land reform programme were to:

1. **Correct the unequal distribution of land** inherited from the colonial period and broaden land ownership.
2. **Resettle landless and land-short families** and reduce overcrowding (decongest) in communal areas.
3. **Bring more land into productive use**, including land that was under-used.
4. **Create a larger class of indigenous commercial and small-scale farmers**, spreading incomes and opportunities more widely.
5. **Reduce rural poverty** and improve food security at household level.

## Economic effects

### Recognised positive effects

- **Wider access to land.** Many thousands of families gained land for the first time, giving them a means of earning a living and producing food.
- **More farmers and new enterprises.** A large number of new small and medium farmers entered agriculture.
- **Growth in smallholder production of some crops.** Small-scale farmers became important producers of some crops, notably tobacco, where contract-farming arrangements gave growers inputs and a buyer.
- **Reduced pressure on communal areas**, as some households moved to resettlement land.
- **Self-employment** for resettled families, who became producers rather than wage earners.

### Recognised negative effects

- **A fall in commercial agricultural output in the years after 2000.** Output of several crops and of commercial livestock fell, partly because of the speed of the changes, together with droughts and shortages of inputs and finance.
- **Loss of jobs for farm workers.** Many workers on former commercial farms lost their jobs and homes.
- **Lower export earnings and foreign currency**, because some export crops and products declined; the country imported more food in some years.
- **Effects on other industries.** Firms that processed farm produce or supplied farm inputs (agro-industries) lost business, showing the interdependence of the stages of production.
- **Finance and security of tenure.** Many new farmers held land under permits or leases rather than freehold title, which made it harder to use land as collateral for loans, so investment in irrigation and machinery was limited.
- **Skills, equipment and support.** Some new farmers lacked capital, equipment, training and extension support at first.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: interpreting district data]
The table shows **fictional** figures for one farming district before and after a land reform. Use it as you would a Paper 2 stimulus.

| Indicator (fictional district) | Year 1 | Year 6 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Households farming in the district | 1,200 | 3,000 |
| Maize output (tonnes) | 18,000 | 12,600 |
| Tobacco output (tonnes) | 900 | 1,080 |
| Permanent farm workers employed | 2,400 | 1,500 |

1. Households farming: $(3{,}000 - 1{,}200) \div 1{,}200 \times 100 = 150\%$ increase. Access to land widened greatly.
2. Maize output: $(12{,}600 - 18{,}000) \div 18{,}000 \times 100 = -30\%$, a 30% fall.
3. Tobacco output: $(1{,}080 - 900) \div 900 \times 100 = 20\%$ increase.
4. Permanent farm jobs: $(1{,}500 - 2{,}400) \div 2{,}400 \times 100 = -37.5\%$, a fall of 37.5%.
5. Interpretation: in this district, many more families farm and tobacco output has grown, but maize output and wage employment on farms have fallen. A full answer would ask what else changed: was there a drought, were inputs available, and how many of the new households are self-employed rather than counted as workers?
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: output per household]
Using the same fictional district, calculate maize output per farming household.

1. Year 1: $18{,}000 \div 1{,}200 = 15$ tonnes per household.
2. Year 6: $12{,}600 \div 3{,}000 = 4.2$ tonnes per household.
3. Output per household fell sharply, because the land is now shared by many more, smaller farms, many with less capital. But more households now produce food for themselves and earn an income, so the benefits are spread more widely. Which matters more depends on the aim: total output or a fairer distribution.
:::

## Evaluating land reform

A balanced evaluation considers:

- **Short run versus long run.** Disruption to output in the early years may be followed by recovery as new farmers gain experience, capital and secure tenure.
- **Equity versus efficiency.** Wider ownership meets the aim of fairness; whether output and exports recover depends on support for the new farmers.
- **Other causes.** Droughts, the economic crisis and hyperinflation of 2007-2008, shortages of inputs and finance also affected farming, so not every change can be blamed on land reform alone.
- **What would help.** Secure tenure that can be used as collateral, access to credit, irrigation, extension services and reliable markets.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing a one-sided answer"}
"Land reform was a total success" or "a total failure" scores poorly. Examiners want aims, benefits and problems, with a conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting statistics you cannot support"}
Do not invent figures. Describe changes in words ("output of several crops fell in the early 2000s") or use the figures given in the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the wider economy"}
Effects reach beyond farms: agro-processing firms, input suppliers, transport, exports and foreign currency all depend on agriculture.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State** or **identify** an aim: one clear phrase, such as "to resettle landless families".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an effect: say what changed and why, and link it to production, employment or exports ("new farmers lacked title deeds, so they could not borrow to invest, which limited output").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** the effects: give recognised benefits and recognised problems, consider other causes and the time period, and end with a justified conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land reform is a deliberate change in the ownership, distribution or use of land.
- Zimbabwe's fast-track land reform programme began in 2000.
- Aims: correct the unequal distribution of land, resettle the landless, decongest communal areas, broaden ownership and reduce rural poverty.
- Recognised benefits: wider access to land, many new farmers, growth of smallholder production of some crops such as tobacco.
- Recognised problems: falls in commercial output and exports in the early years, lost farm-worker jobs, effects on agro-industries, and limited finance where tenure could not be used as collateral.
- A balanced answer weighs equity against output, the short run against the long run, and other causes such as drought.
:::
