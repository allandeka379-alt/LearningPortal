## Introduction

A road contractor can build a road with hundreds of workers using picks, shovels and wheelbarrows, or with a small team operating graders, excavators and rollers. A brewery can fill bottles by hand or on an automated line. Every firm must decide **how many** workers, machines and other resources to use, and **in what combination**. These choices affect the firm's costs, the quality of its output and the number of jobs it creates.

Cambridge IGCSE asks you to explain the influences on the demand for factors of production, and why firms adopt **labour-intensive** or **capital-intensive** production, with the advantages and disadvantages of each. Questions often give a short case and ask you to analyse or discuss a firm's choice.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Demand for a factor of production"}
The quantity of a factor (land, labour, capital or enterprise) that firms are willing and able to employ at a given price of that factor.
:::

:::definition{term="Derived demand"}
Demand for a factor that comes from the demand for the goods and services it helps to produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour-intensive production"}
A method of production that uses a high proportion of labour relative to capital.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital-intensive production"}
A method of production that uses a high proportion of capital (machinery, equipment, technology) relative to labour.
:::

## Influences on the demand for factors of production

1. **Demand for the product.** Factor demand is **derived**: if demand for school furniture rises, furniture makers want more carpenters, more timber and more machines. If demand falls, they want fewer.
2. **The price of the factor.** A higher wage makes labour more expensive, so firms want fewer workers; a fall in the price of machinery makes capital more attractive.
3. **The price of other factors.** If machines become cheaper relative to labour, firms may replace workers with machines (substitution). If wages rise, capital becomes relatively cheaper.
4. **The productivity of the factor.** The more a worker or machine produces, the more it is worth to the firm, so the greater the demand for it.
5. **The availability of factors.** A shortage of skilled engineers, or of reliable electricity to run machines, limits what a firm can use.
6. **Other influences:** government policy (for example taxes on employment, subsidies for investment, or rules on dismissal), technology, and the firm's expectations about future demand.

## Why firms choose labour-intensive or capital-intensive methods

The main reason is **cost**: firms choose the combination that produces the output they want at the lowest cost. That depends on the relative prices of labour and capital, which differ between countries and industries.

Firms tend to choose **labour-intensive** methods when:

- labour is plentiful and wages are low compared with the cost of machines;
- the product is hand-made, customised or a personal service (tailoring, hairdressing, crafts, tourism);
- output is small, so expensive machines would stand idle;
- capital is expensive or hard to finance, or reliable power and maintenance are not available.

Firms tend to choose **capital-intensive** methods when:

- wages are high or skilled labour is scarce;
- output is large and standardised (bottling drinks, cement, car assembly), so the cost of machines is spread over many units;
- precision and consistent quality are needed;
- finance for capital is available and the technology is reliable.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: comparing the cost of two methods]
A fictional firm can produce 1,000 bags of cement a day by either method. Workers are paid US\$12 a day under both.

| Method | Workers | Daily wage bill (US\$) | Daily cost of machinery (US\$) | Total daily cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Labour-intensive | 60 | 720 | 200 | 920 |
| Capital-intensive | 15 | 180 | 680 | 860 |

1. Labour-intensive: $60 \times 12 = 720$ wage bill, plus 200 for machinery = US\$920 a day.
2. Capital-intensive: $15 \times 12 = 180$ wage bill, plus 680 for machinery = US\$860 a day.
3. Cost per bag: 920 ÷ 1,000 gives US\$0.92, against 860 ÷ 1,000, which gives US\$0.86. The capital-intensive method is cheaper at this wage.
4. Now suppose wages fall to US\$8 a day. Labour-intensive: $60 \times 8 + 200 = 680$. Capital-intensive: $15 \times 8 + 680 = 800$. The labour-intensive method becomes cheaper. **Relative factor prices decide the choice.**
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages

### Labour-intensive production

**Advantages**

- Lower set-up cost and less need to borrow.
- Flexible: easy to change the product or the amount produced by changing the number of workers or hours.
- Suits hand-made, customised and high-quality craft goods and personal services.
- Creates more jobs, which matters in an economy with high unemployment.
- Less dependent on reliable electricity and imported spare parts.

**Disadvantages**

- Higher cost per unit when output is large and wages are not low.
- Quality may be less consistent; more human error.
- Labour problems: absence, staff turnover, industrial action and the cost of training and managing many workers.
- Output per worker is usually lower.

### Capital-intensive production

**Advantages**

- Lower cost per unit at high output (economies of scale), so lower prices and greater competitiveness.
- High and consistent quality and precision.
- Machines can work long hours without breaks.
- Higher labour productivity, which can support higher wages for the remaining, more skilled, workers.

**Disadvantages**

- High initial cost; may need loans and interest.
- Breakdowns or power cuts stop production; spare parts may have to be imported.
- Less flexible: hard to change the product or to cut costs quickly if demand falls (machinery costs remain).
- Fewer jobs: workers may be replaced (technological unemployment), and remaining workers need training.

:::example[Worked example 2: a road project]
A fictional government must build 50 km of rural road. A contractor offers a capital-intensive plan using graders and excavators, and another a labour-intensive plan employing local people.

- **Capital-intensive:** faster, more consistent quality, lower cost per kilometre if machines are available, but creates few local jobs and depends on imported fuel and parts.
- **Labour-intensive:** slower, but creates hundreds of jobs and incomes in the area, uses local skills, and the money spent stays in the local economy.
- **Evaluation:** in a rural area with high unemployment and low wages, the labour-intensive plan may give greater overall benefit, even if it takes longer; for a major highway with heavy traffic, the quality and speed of the capital-intensive plan may matter more.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying capital-intensive is always better"}
It is cheaper only when output is large and capital is cheap relative to labour. In many developing economies, labour-intensive methods are the lower-cost choice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that factor demand is derived"}
Firms do not want workers or machines for their own sake. A fall in demand for the product reduces demand for every factor that makes it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing 'capital' with money"}
In this topic capital means machinery, tools, equipment and buildings, not money in a bank.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an influence on factor demand with a chain: "demand for houses rises, so builders need more bricklayers, so the demand for bricklayers rises".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Analyse** why a firm uses a method: link the firm's situation (wages, size of output, type of product, finance) to cost per unit and quality.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** a switch to capital-intensive production: lower unit costs and quality against the initial cost, inflexibility and job losses, and conclude for that firm.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Demand for factors is derived from demand for the product.
- It also depends on the price of the factor, the prices of other factors, productivity, availability and government policy.
- Labour-intensive production uses a high proportion of labour; capital-intensive production a high proportion of capital.
- Firms choose the method with the lowest cost for the output they want, so relative factor prices, the scale of output and the type of product decide the choice.
- Capital-intensive production lowers unit costs at high output but is costly to set up, inflexible and creates fewer jobs; labour-intensive production is flexible and creates jobs but often has higher unit costs.
:::
