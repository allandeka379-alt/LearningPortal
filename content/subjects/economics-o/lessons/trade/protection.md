## Introduction

Walk into a supermarket in Harare or Bulawayo and many products on the shelves are imported, often from South Africa. Local manufacturers of cooking oil, dairy products, furniture or clothing frequently complain that they cannot match the prices of large foreign producers. Governments everywhere, including Zimbabwe's, have at times responded by placing duties on imports or limiting the quantity that may come in, to give local firms a chance. This is **protectionism**: restricting imports (or helping local producers) to shield domestic industries from foreign competition.

Protection is popular with the firms and workers it shelters, but it has costs for consumers and can provoke other countries to retaliate. Both syllabuses ask you to describe the methods, explain the reasons, and weigh the advantages and disadvantages. It is a favourite "discuss" topic, so you need arguments on both sides.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Protectionism"}
Government measures that restrict international trade, usually by limiting imports or giving local producers an advantage, in order to protect domestic industries.
:::

:::definition{term="Tariff"}
A tax on imported goods. A specific tariff is a fixed amount per unit; an ad valorem tariff is a percentage of the value of the import.
:::

:::definition{term="Import quota"}
A legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported in a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidy to domestic producers"}
A payment by the government to local firms that lowers their costs, so that they can sell at lower prices and compete with imports or in export markets.
:::

:::definition{term="Embargo"}
A complete ban on trade in particular goods or with a particular country.
:::

:::definition{term="Exchange control"}
Government restriction on the amount of foreign currency that residents may buy, which limits their ability to pay for imports.
:::

:::definition{term="Dumping"}
Selling goods in a foreign market at a price below their cost of production (or below the price charged at home).
:::

## Methods of protection

- **Tariffs (customs duties)** raise the price of imports, so consumers buy fewer of them and switch to local goods. They also raise revenue; in Zimbabwe customs duty is collected by ZIMRA at the border.
- **Import quotas** limit the quantity of imports directly. Because supply is restricted, the price of the good rises, but the government earns no revenue unless it sells import licences.
- **Subsidies** to local producers lower their costs, letting them cut prices without the government taxing imports.
- **Embargoes** ban imports of a good completely (for example, dangerous goods) or ban all trade with a country, often for political reasons.
- **Exchange controls** ration foreign currency, so importers cannot obtain enough to buy as much as they would like (ZIMSEC).
- **Administrative barriers** such as strict product standards, licences and slow customs procedures also discourage imports.

## How a tariff works

Draw a diagram with **price** on the vertical axis and **quantity** on the horizontal axis. The domestic demand curve slopes down and the domestic supply curve slopes up. With free trade, the country can buy as much as it wants at the world price, drawn as a horizontal line below the domestic equilibrium. Imports are the gap between domestic demand and domestic supply at that price. A tariff shifts the world price line **up** by the amount of the tariff: domestic demand falls, domestic supply rises, and the gap (imports) shrinks.

![Cooking oil market: at the world price of US\$1.00 local firms supply 100 and consumers demand 900 thousand litres, so imports are 800; a US\$0.40 tariff lifts the price to US\$1.40, local supply rises to 300, demand falls to 700, imports fall to 400 thousand litres and the government collects US\$160,000 a week.](media:diagrams/tariff-cooking-oil.png "Figure: a tariff raises the price, cuts imports and raises local output and government revenue")

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: the effect of a tariff]
The table shows the domestic market for cooking oil in a fictional country. The world price is US\$1.00 a litre. The government then imposes a tariff of US\$0.40 a litre.

| Price (US\$ per litre) | Quantity demanded (thousand litres a week) | Quantity supplied by local producers (thousand litres a week) | Imports at this price (thousand litres) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.00 | 900 | 100 | 800 |
| 1.20 | 800 | 200 | 600 |
| 1.40 | 700 | 300 | 400 |
| 1.60 | 600 | 400 | 200 |
| 1.80 | 500 | 500 | 0 |

1. **Free trade:** price US\$1.00; consumers buy 900 thousand litres; local firms make 100; imports = 900 − 100 = **800 thousand litres**.
2. **With the tariff:** price = 1.00 + 0.40 = **US\$1.40**. Consumers buy 700; local firms make 300; imports = 700 − 300 = **400 thousand litres**.
3. The tariff is 0.40 ÷ 1.00 × 100 = **40%** of the world price (an ad valorem equivalent).
4. Tariff revenue = 0.40 × 400,000 litres = **US\$160,000 a week**.
5. Winners: local producers (output up from 100 to 300) and their workers; the government (revenue). Losers: consumers (pay 40 cents more and buy 200 thousand litres less) and foreign producers.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: a quota instead of a tariff]
Using the same table, the government instead sets an import quota of 200 thousand litres a week.

1. Imports cannot exceed 200, so the price rises until demand minus local supply equals 200.
2. At US\$1.60: demand 600 − local supply 400 = **200**. The price settles at **US\$1.60**.
3. The quota protects local producers even more (output 400) and raises the price more than the tariff did, but the government collects **no revenue**: the extra 60 cents on imported litres goes to whoever holds the right to import.
:::

![Cooking oil with an import quota of 200 thousand litres: the supply available to the market is local supply plus the quota, which meets demand at US\$1.60, where consumers buy 600, local firms supply 400 and imports fill the 200 gap; the price is higher than with the 0.40 tariff and the government collects no revenue.](media:diagrams/quota-cooking-oil.png "Figure: a quota limits imports to 200 thousand litres and the price rises to US\$1.60")

## Reasons for protection

- **Infant industries:** new firms need time to grow and gain economies of scale before they can compete with established foreign firms.
- **Declining industries:** protection slows the decline so that workers have time to retrain and move, avoiding sudden structural unemployment.
- **Strategic industries:** a country may wish to keep food, energy or defence production at home for security.
- **Preventing dumping:** goods sold below cost could drive efficient local firms out of business.
- **Current account deficit:** fewer imports reduce the deficit and save foreign currency.
- **Government revenue:** tariffs are easy to collect at the border, which matters where income tax is hard to collect.
- **Demerit goods and the environment:** high duties or bans on harmful goods (such as some drugs, or polluting second-hand vehicles) protect health and the environment.
- **Protecting jobs** in industries threatened by imports.

## Consequences, advantages and disadvantages

:::example{title="Weighing a ban on imported dairy products"}
A fictional government bans imports of milk powder to help local dairy farmers. **For:** local farmers expand, rural jobs rise, the country saves foreign currency and becomes less dependent on imports. **Against:** milk powder becomes scarcer and dearer, hurting poor households and food processors who use it as an input; local farmers face less pressure to become efficient; trading partners may retaliate by restricting the country's exports, such as its tobacco or citrus. Whether the ban is wise depends on whether local farmers can become competitive within a few years and on how much retaliation follows.
:::

**Disadvantages** of protection in general: higher prices and less choice for consumers; dearer imported inputs raise costs for other firms; protected firms may stay inefficient; **retaliation** reduces export opportunities; trading partners lose sales and jobs; and the world loses the gains from specialisation according to comparative advantage. Trade agreements such as those of SADC and COMESA also limit how far members may raise barriers against each other.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a quota raises revenue"}
A tariff raises revenue for the government; a quota does not, unless import licences are sold.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying protection creates jobs overall"}
Jobs may rise in the protected industry but fall elsewhere: in industries using the protected good as an input, and in export industries hit by retaliation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a subsidy a tax on imports"}
A subsidy is paid to local producers; it does not change the price of imports directly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe** each method precisely: what it is and how it reduces imports (price effect for tariffs; quantity limit for quotas).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / analyse:** link a reason to its argument, for example "an infant industry has high average costs because it is small; protection lets it grow and gain economies of scale until it can compete".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** give the gainers and losers, consider retaliation and efficiency, and conclude, for example that temporary, targeted protection for a genuine infant industry may be justified, but permanent protection is usually harmful.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Protectionism restricts trade to shield domestic industries.
- Methods: tariffs, import quotas, subsidies, embargoes, exchange controls and administrative barriers.
- A tariff raises the domestic price, increases local output, cuts imports and raises revenue; a quota cuts imports directly but raises no revenue.
- Reasons: infant and declining industries, strategic industries, dumping, the current account, revenue, demerit goods and the environment, jobs.
- Costs: higher prices, less choice, inefficiency, dearer inputs, retaliation and lost gains from trade.
:::
