## Introduction

Every day, maize meal milled in Harare reaches tuck-shops in Gokwe, tomatoes from Mutoko are sold at Mbare Musika, and a vendor at a Bulawayo kombi rank sells airtime, sweets and bananas. All of this is **domestic trade**: the buying and selling of goods and services within Zimbabwe's borders. Without it, producers could not reach consumers and specialisation would be impossible, because nobody could swap the goods they make for the goods they need.

ZIMSEC asks Form 1 learners to describe the characteristics and importance of domestic trade, the roles of retailers and wholesalers, and the difference between formal and informal trade, including the role of informal traders. These ideas appear in multiple-choice questions and short essays, and they connect to later topics such as specialisation, the informal sector and employment.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Domestic (home) trade"}
The buying and selling of goods and services within one country, using its own currency and laws.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of distribution"}
The route by which goods pass from the producer to the final consumer, usually producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer.
:::

:::definition{term="Wholesaler"}
A trader who buys goods in large quantities from producers and sells them in smaller quantities to retailers.
:::

:::definition{term="Retailer"}
A trader who buys goods from producers or wholesalers and sells them in small quantities to final consumers.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal trade"}
Trade carried out by businesses that are registered with the authorities, keep records, pay taxes such as those collected by ZIMRA and follow regulations.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal trade"}
Trade carried out by unregistered businesses, such as street vendors and many home-based traders, that operate largely outside official regulation and taxation.
:::

## Characteristics and importance of domestic trade

**Characteristics:** one currency is used, goods do not cross a national border, the same laws and customs apply, distances are usually shorter, and there are no customs duties or import documents.

**Importance:**

- It links producers with consumers, so goods reach the people who want them.
- It makes **specialisation** possible: farmers, factories and service providers can concentrate on what they do best and trade for the rest.
- It creates **employment** for traders, transporters, warehouse workers and shop assistants.
- It provides **income** and **tax revenue** (from formal traders).
- It spreads goods to all parts of the country, including rural areas, and widens consumer choice.
- It provides information to producers about what consumers want.

## The chain of distribution

### Functions of the wholesaler

- **Bulk breaking:** buys in large quantities and sells in smaller amounts that suit retailers.
- **Storage (warehousing):** holds stock so that producers do not need large warehouses and retailers can buy when they need.
- **Transport:** often delivers goods to retailers.
- **Credit:** may allow retailers to pay later, and pays producers promptly.
- **Risk bearing:** carries the risk of goods spoiling, being stolen or falling in price.
- **Information:** passes on information about prices and consumer demand between producers and retailers.
- **Some processing:** grading, packing and labelling.

### Functions of the retailer

- Sells goods in **small quantities** to consumers, close to where they live.
- Offers **variety and choice** from many producers.
- Provides **services**: advice, credit or lay-by, delivery, after-sales service, opening hours that suit customers.
- Passes on **information** about consumer tastes to wholesalers and producers.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: mark-ups along the chain]
A fictional soap manufacturer sells a carton of 24 bars to a wholesaler for US\$24. The wholesaler adds a mark-up of 25% and the retailer adds a mark-up of 20%.

1. Wholesaler's selling price = 24 × 1.25 = **US\$30** a carton.
2. Retailer's selling price = 30 × 1.20 = **US\$36** a carton.
3. Price to the consumer per bar = 36 ÷ 24 = **US\$1.50**, compared with US\$1.00 at the factory.
4. The extra 50 cents pays the wholesaler and retailer for storage, transport, bulk breaking, credit, risk and convenience. Consumers could save by buying directly, but only if they were willing to buy a whole carton and travel to the factory.
:::

### Types of retailer

- **Street vendors and hawkers:** sell small items from stalls, baskets or carts; low costs, convenient, often informal.
- **Market traders:** sell fresh produce and second-hand goods at markets such as Mbare Musika.
- **Tuck-shops and small general shops:** sell everyday goods near homes, often on credit to regular customers.
- **Supermarkets and chain stores:** large self-service stores with many branches, buying in bulk and charging lower prices.
- **Department stores:** large stores divided into departments selling a wide range of goods.
- **Online and mobile sellers:** sell through websites and social media, often with mobile money payments and delivery.

:::example{title="Can the wholesaler be cut out?"}
A large supermarket chain buys directly from manufacturers in very large quantities and has its own warehouses and lorries. It performs the wholesaler's functions itself, so it can cut out the wholesaler and keep the mark-up, which lets it charge lower prices. A small tuck-shop in a rural area cannot do this: it has little storage, needs small quantities and depends on the wholesaler's credit and deliveries. Whether the wholesaler is needed depends on the size of the retailer and the nature of the goods.
:::

## Formal and informal trade

| Feature | Formal trade | Informal trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration | Registered business | Not registered |
| Taxes | Pays taxes (VAT, income tax) | Pays little or no tax, though vendors may pay council fees |
| Records | Keeps accounts | Few records |
| Premises | Shops, offices, warehouses | Stalls, pavements, homes, markets |
| Workers' protection | Contracts, pensions, safety laws | Little protection |

## The role of informal traders in Zimbabwe

Informal trade is a very large part of economic life in Zimbabwe. Since formal-sector jobs became scarce, many people earn their living as vendors, cross-border traders, small-scale traders and artisans.

**Positive roles:**

- They provide **employment and incomes** for many people who would otherwise have none.
- They make goods **available in small quantities** and at convenient places and hours, for example single cigarettes or a cup of sugar.
- They often sell at **lower prices** because their costs are low.
- They distribute goods to areas that formal shops do not reach.
- They develop **enterprise skills**, and some grow into formal businesses.

**Problems:**

- The government collects little **tax** from them.
- Goods may be of poor quality, counterfeit or unhygienically handled, and consumers have little protection.
- Street trading can cause **congestion** and litter and can compete unfairly with tax-paying shops.
- Traders lack access to bank credit, insurance, pensions and legal protection, and their incomes are insecure.

Governments and councils try to balance these by providing designated market stalls, simplifying registration and encouraging informal traders to formalise.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying wholesalers only raise prices"}
The wholesaler's mark-up pays for real services: bulk breaking, storage, transport, credit and risk bearing. Without them, retailers would face higher costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing informal trade as illegal"}
Most informal trade involves legal goods; it is "informal" because the business is not registered or regulated, not because what is sold is against the law.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / describe:** list functions in pairs ("bulk breaking: buys in large quantities and sells in smaller lots").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the importance:** link domestic trade to specialisation, employment, choice and tax revenue.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss informal trade:** give its benefits (jobs, convenience, low prices) and its problems (lost tax, quality, congestion, insecurity), then conclude, for example that it should be supported and gradually formalised rather than banned.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Domestic trade is buying and selling within one country; it links producers and consumers and makes specialisation possible.
- The chain of distribution usually runs producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer.
- Wholesalers break bulk, store, transport, give credit, bear risk and pass on information; retailers sell small quantities conveniently and offer choice and services.
- Formal traders are registered and taxed; informal traders are not.
- Informal traders provide jobs, convenience and low prices in Zimbabwe, but bring problems of lost tax, poor consumer protection and insecure incomes.
:::
