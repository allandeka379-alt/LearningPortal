## Introduction

Zimbabwe earns much of its foreign currency from exports of minerals and farm products such as gold, platinum, chrome, lithium and tobacco, and it spends foreign currency on imports of fuel, machinery, vehicles, fertiliser and medicines. When world prices of gold or tobacco rise while the price of fuel stays the same, each tonne of exports pays for more imports. When the price of imported fuel rises sharply, the country must export more just to buy the same quantity of fuel.

The **terms of trade** measure exactly this: the price of a country's exports compared with the price of its imports. ZIMSEC asks you to describe, calculate and interpret the terms of trade. A typical Paper 2 question gives export and import price indices for several years and asks you to calculate the terms of trade, say whether a movement is favourable or unfavourable, and explain what it means for the country.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Terms of trade"}
The ratio of the average price of a country's exports to the average price of its imports, expressed as an index number.
:::

:::definition{term="Export price index"}
A weighted index of the prices of a country's exports compared with a base year (= 100).
:::

:::definition{term="Import price index"}
A weighted index of the prices of a country's imports compared with a base year (= 100).
:::

:::definition{term="Favourable movement in the terms of trade"}
A rise in the terms of trade index: export prices rise relative to import prices, so a given quantity of exports pays for more imports.
:::

:::definition{term="Unfavourable movement in the terms of trade"}
A fall in the terms of trade index: import prices rise relative to export prices, so more exports are needed to pay for the same imports.
:::

:::formula{name="Terms of trade"}
Terms of trade = index of export prices ÷ index of import prices × 100
:::

In the base year both price indices are 100, so the terms of trade are also 100. A figure above 100 means export prices have risen more (or fallen less) than import prices since the base year; a figure below 100 means the opposite.

## Calculating the terms of trade

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: a single year]
In a fictional country, the export price index is 132 and the import price index is 110 (base year = 100).

1. Terms of trade = 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = **120**.
2. Since the base year, export prices have risen by 32% and import prices by only 10%.
3. The terms of trade have moved **favourably**: each unit of exports now buys 20% more imports than in the base year.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: interpreting a series]
The table shows price indices for a fictional mineral-exporting country (2023 = 100).

| Year | Export price index | Import price index | Terms of trade |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | 100 | 100 | 100.0 |
| 2024 | 104 | 108 | 96.3 |
| 2025 | 98 | 112 | 87.5 |
| 2026 | 112 | 105 | 106.7 |

1. 2024: 104 ÷ 108 × 100 = 96.3. Export prices rose, but import prices rose faster: an **unfavourable** movement.
2. 2025: 98 ÷ 112 × 100 = 87.5. Export prices fell while import prices kept rising: a further, larger **unfavourable** movement. The country needed about 14% more exports than in 2023 to buy the same imports (100 ÷ 87.5 = 1.14).
3. 2026: 112 ÷ 105 × 100 = 106.7. Export prices recovered strongly and import prices fell: a **favourable** movement, and the terms of trade are now better than in the base year.
4. Notice that the terms of trade can worsen even when export prices rise (2024): it is the **relative** movement that matters.
:::

## Why the terms of trade change

- **World demand and supply of the country's exports.** Many developing countries export primary products (minerals, tobacco, cotton) whose prices can swing sharply with world demand, harvests and new sources of supply.
- **World prices of imports.** A rise in the world price of oil worsens the terms of trade of every oil-importing country.
- **Inflation at home and abroad.** If a country's costs and prices rise faster than those of its trading partners, its export prices rise relative to its import prices.
- **The exchange rate.** An appreciation of the currency usually raises export prices measured in foreign currency and lowers import prices measured in domestic currency, which tends to improve the terms of trade; a depreciation tends to worsen them.
- **Changes in productivity and quality.** Better-quality exports may command higher prices.

## Is a favourable movement always good?

The words "favourable" and "unfavourable" describe only the price ratio. Whether the change helps the country depends on **why** it happened and on what happens to the **quantities** traded.

:::example{title="Two causes of the same favourable movement"}
1. A drought in other tobacco-growing countries raises the world price of tobacco. A tobacco-exporting country sells the same quantity at a higher price: its export earnings rise, its current account improves and farm incomes rise. This favourable movement is good news.
2. A country's inflation is higher than its trading partners', so its export prices rise. Its terms of trade improve, but its goods become less competitive. If demand for its exports is price elastic, foreigners buy much less, export **earnings fall**, and jobs in export industries are lost. This "favourable" movement is bad news.
:::

Similarly, an unfavourable movement caused by a fall in export prices can be partly offset if the lower prices help the country sell a much larger quantity of exports, which happens when demand is price elastic.

### Effects of a sustained unfavourable movement

- More exports are needed to pay for the same imports, so the country's real income falls.
- The balance of trade and current account tend to worsen, which can lead to shortages of foreign currency.
- The cost of imported inputs such as fuel and fertiliser rises, adding to cost-push inflation.
- Governments of primary-exporting countries may respond by trying to **diversify** exports into processed and manufactured goods, whose prices are often more stable.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Dividing the import index by the export index"}
The terms of trade put **export** prices on top: export index ÷ import index × 100. Upside down, a favourable movement looks unfavourable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the terms of trade with the balance of trade"}
The terms of trade compare **prices** of exports and imports. The balance of trade compares the **values** of visible exports and imports. One can improve while the other worsens.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming 'favourable' always means 'beneficial'"}
A rise in the index means export prices have risen relative to import prices. If this makes exports uncompetitive and demand is elastic, export earnings may fall.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / describe:** give the formula and say what a figure above or below 100 means compared with the base year.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate:** show export index ÷ import index × 100 for each year, round as instructed (usually one decimal place) and state "favourable" or "unfavourable" by comparing with the previous year, not only with 100.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / discuss:** link the movement to its cause (world prices, inflation, exchange rate), then to quantities, export earnings and the current account. For "discuss", show that a favourable movement can help or harm depending on the cause and on price elasticity of demand for exports, and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100; the base year is 100.
- A rise is a favourable movement (exports buy more imports); a fall is unfavourable.
- They change with world commodity prices, import prices (especially fuel), relative inflation and the exchange rate.
- The terms of trade measure prices, not values; the balance of trade measures values.
- Whether a movement helps a country depends on its cause and on the price elasticity of demand for exports.
:::
