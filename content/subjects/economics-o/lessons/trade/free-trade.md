## Introduction

Over the past few decades many countries, including Zimbabwe through its membership of SADC and COMESA, have signed agreements to reduce tariffs and other barriers on trade with their partners. The idea behind these agreements is **free trade**: allowing goods and services to move between countries without government restrictions.

Supporters argue that free trade lowers prices, widens choice and makes the world richer. Critics argue that it exposes young industries in developing countries to competition they cannot survive and makes poorer countries dependent on a few exports. Both syllabuses ask you to define free trade and explain its advantages and disadvantages, and essays often ask you to weigh free trade against protection.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Free trade"}
International trade that takes place without government restrictions such as tariffs, import quotas, subsidies to domestic producers or embargoes.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade barrier"}
Any government measure that restricts international trade, such as a tariff, a quota or an embargo.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove tariffs and quotas on trade among themselves, while each keeps its own barriers against non-members.
:::

## Why countries move towards free trade

The case for free trade rests on **specialisation and comparative advantage**. When each country concentrates on the goods it produces at the lowest opportunity cost and trades freely, world output is higher than it would be if every country tried to make everything itself. Trade barriers stop this happening: they keep resources in industries where a country is relatively inefficient.

Since 1995 the World Trade Organization (WTO) has provided a forum where its member countries negotiate to reduce barriers and settle trade disputes. Regional groups such as SADC and COMESA have set up free trade areas so that members' goods can cross borders within the region with lower or no duties.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: who gains when a tariff is removed]
A fictional country charges a 20% tariff on imported refrigerators. A refrigerator costs US\$500 from the foreign producer.

1. With the tariff, the price in the shops is 500 × 1.20 = **US\$600**.
2. Under free trade the tariff is removed, so the price falls to **US\$500**: each buyer saves **US\$100**.
3. If 8,000 households bought refrigerators at the old price, they would together save 8,000 × 100 = **US\$800,000** on the same purchases, and some extra households can now afford one.
4. The government loses the tariff revenue on those imports: 100 × 8,000 = **US\$800,000**, which it may need to raise in other ways.
5. Local refrigerator makers now face cheaper competition and must cut costs or lose sales.
:::

## Advantages of free trade

- **Lower prices and greater choice** for consumers, because goods come from the cheapest producers in the world and there are no tariffs to pay.
- **Higher world output**: countries specialise according to comparative advantage, so resources are used more efficiently.
- **Larger markets** for exporters, so firms can grow and gain **economies of scale**.
- **Competition** forces domestic firms to become more efficient, innovate and improve quality.
- **Cheaper imported inputs** (fuel, machinery, fertiliser) lower costs for local producers.
- **Spread of technology and ideas**, and closer economic and political ties between countries.
- **No retaliation**: when barriers are low on all sides, countries avoid "trade wars" that harm everyone.

## Disadvantages of free trade

- **Infant industries** cannot grow behind protective barriers, so a developing country may stay dependent on primary exports.
- **Closure of uncompetitive firms** leads to job losses and structural unemployment, especially in towns that depend on one industry.
- **Dumping**: without barriers, foreign firms can sell below cost and drive local producers out of business.
- **Dependence** on imports of strategic goods such as food and fuel, and on a few exports whose prices can fall sharply.
- **Loss of tariff revenue**, which is an important source of government income in many developing countries.
- **Current account problems** if imports rise faster than exports.
- **Spread of harmful goods** and weaker control over imports that damage health or the environment.

:::example{title="Free trade seen from two sides"}
A fictional country in southern Africa joins a regional free trade area. Its citrus farmers and cement producers, which are efficient, gain access to neighbouring markets and expand, taking on more workers. At the same time, its small textile factories face cheaper clothing from a larger neighbour and some close. Consumers enjoy cheaper clothing and cement, and the country's exports rise, but the textile towns suffer. The overall gain is real, but it is unevenly shared: the government may need to help displaced workers retrain and invest in industries where the country can compete.
:::

## Free trade or protection?

In exams you are often asked to **discuss** whether a country should adopt free trade. A balanced answer:

1. explains the gains from specialisation, competition and lower prices;
2. explains the risks for infant industries, jobs, revenue and strategic goods;
3. considers the country's circumstances: Is it a developing country with young industries? Does it rely on tariff revenue? Are its trading partners also removing barriers?
4. reaches a conclusion, for example that gradual movement to free trade, with temporary support for genuine infant industries and help for displaced workers, gives most of the gains while limiting the costs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Defining free trade as trade without money"}
Free trade means trade without **government restrictions**, not trade without payment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming everyone gains"}
Free trade raises total output, but some groups (workers and owners in uncompetitive industries, governments that lose tariff revenue) can lose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a free trade area with a customs union"}
In a free trade area each member keeps its own tariffs against outsiders; in a customs union members also set a common external tariff.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** "trade between countries without restrictions such as tariffs, quotas, subsidies or embargoes".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** develop each advantage into a chain, for example "no tariffs → lower import prices → consumers' real incomes rise".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** consider consumers, producers, workers and the government, distinguish the short run (job losses) from the long run (efficiency, growth), and finish with a justified judgement for the country in the question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Free trade is trade between countries without government restrictions.
- It is based on specialisation according to comparative advantage, which raises world output.
- Advantages: lower prices, more choice, larger markets, economies of scale, competition, cheaper inputs and technology transfer.
- Disadvantages: harm to infant industries, job losses, dumping, dependence, lost tariff revenue and possible current account problems.
- A balanced judgement weighs total gains against how they are shared, often favouring gradual liberalisation with support for those who lose.
:::
