## Introduction

A mobile phone bought in Bulawayo may have been designed in one country, assembled in another from parts made in a dozen more, and paid for with a mobile money transfer. Its owner may watch football from Europe, buy clothes made in Asia and receive money from a relative working in the United Kingdom. This growing interconnection of the world's economies is called **globalisation**.

Globalisation has brought cheaper goods, new markets and faster growth to many countries, but it has also been blamed for job losses, pollution and widening inequality. The Cambridge syllabus asks you to define globalisation, explain what causes it to change, and explain its effects on trade, competition, the environment, migration, income distribution and development. Section B essays often end with "discuss whether globalisation benefits a developing economy".

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Globalisation"}
The process by which the world's economies become more closely connected through increased international trade in goods and services, flows of capital and investment, movement of people, and the spread of technology and information.
:::

:::definition{term="Multinational company (MNC)"}
A firm that produces goods or services in more than one country.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade openness"}
A measure of how important trade is to an economy, often calculated as (exports + imports) ÷ GDP × 100.
:::

## Causes of changes in globalisation

Globalisation is not a one-way process: it can speed up or slow down. Its main drivers are:

- **Trade restrictions.** When countries cut tariffs and quotas, through agreements in the World Trade Organization or regional groups such as SADC and COMESA, trade grows. When they raise barriers, as some countries have done in recent years, globalisation slows.
- **Transport costs.** Container ships, larger aircraft and better roads, railways and ports have greatly reduced the cost of moving goods. Cheaper transport makes it worthwhile to buy from distant suppliers and to split production across countries.
- **Communication costs.** The internet, mobile phones and cheap international calls let firms coordinate production worldwide, sell online and provide services such as call centres and software from anywhere.
- **The movement of multinational companies.** MNCs set up factories, mines, hotels and offices abroad to reach new markets, cheaper labour or raw materials, spreading capital, technology and management methods.
- **Other factors** include the freer movement of capital between countries, the spread of English and other common languages in business, and political events such as wars, pandemics and sanctions that can disrupt supply chains and slow globalisation.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: measuring increased integration]
A fictional country's trade figures (US\$ billion):

| Year | Exports | Imports | GDP | Trade openness (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2016 | 5 | 7 | 40 | 30.0 |
| 2026 | 12 | 15 | 60 | 45.0 |

1. Trade openness in 2016 = (5 + 7) ÷ 40 × 100 = **30%**.
2. Trade openness in 2026 = (12 + 15) ÷ 60 × 100 = **45%**.
3. Trade has grown faster than GDP: the economy has become more integrated with the rest of the world, so it gains more from trade but is also more exposed to events abroad.
:::

## Effects of globalisation

### Trade

Trade in goods and services grows, and countries specialise more according to comparative advantage. Production is split into **global supply chains**: a component might cross several borders before the final good is sold.

### Competition

Domestic firms face competition from foreign firms. Consumers gain lower prices, more choice and better quality, and efficient firms grow by selling abroad. Inefficient firms may close, and some industries in high-cost countries move to lower-cost ones.

### The environment

More production and transport raise carbon emissions and other pollution, and demand for raw materials can lead to deforestation and over-mining. On the other hand, globalisation spreads cleaner technologies such as solar panels, and international agreements can raise environmental standards.

### Migration

People move more freely in search of work and education. Host countries gain workers and skills; source countries receive **remittances** but may suffer a **brain drain** as skilled workers such as doctors, nurses and engineers leave. Zimbabwe, for example, receives significant remittances from citizens working abroad and has also lost many trained professionals.

### Income distribution

Globalisation has helped lift many people out of poverty in countries that became major exporters of manufactured goods. But gains are often unequal: owners of capital and skilled workers tend to gain more than unskilled workers, and some regions and countries have been left behind, so inequality **within** countries may widen.

### Development

Access to world markets, foreign investment and technology can speed up growth and development. However, countries that remain exporters of a few primary products may gain less, and they are exposed to world recessions and swings in commodity prices.

:::example{title="Globalisation and a developing economy"}
A fictional southern African country attracts a foreign car-parts maker, whose factory supplies a global supply chain. Jobs are created, workers learn new skills and exports rise. Cheaper imported phones and solar panels improve daily life. However, local shoe and clothing makers close in the face of imports, some of the profits of the factory go abroad, and when world demand for cars falls, the factory cuts its workforce. Meanwhile, many of the country's nurses take jobs abroad; the families they leave behind gain from remittances, but hospitals at home are short-staffed. The overall effect is positive for growth, but the benefits and costs fall on different people.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating globalisation as only about trade in goods"}
Globalisation also covers flows of capital, people, technology, services and information.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming globalisation always increases"}
Higher trade barriers, conflicts, pandemics and rising transport costs can slow or reverse it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving one-sided answers"}
For each effect there are gainers and losers; examiners reward answers that show both and judge which is more important in the context given.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define:** mention the increasing interconnection of economies through trade, capital, people and technology.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain causes:** link each cause to a lower cost or barrier ("cheaper communication lets firms coordinate production in several countries").
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss effects:** take each area named in the question (trade, competition, environment, migration, income distribution, development), give a benefit and a cost, and conclude on whether the country in the question gains overall.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Globalisation is the growing interconnection of economies through trade, capital, people and technology.
- It speeds up when trade restrictions, transport costs and communication costs fall and when MNCs expand abroad; it slows when barriers rise or supply chains are disrupted.
- Effects: more trade and specialisation; more competition; environmental costs and benefits; migration with remittances and brain drain; uneven income distribution; opportunities and risks for development.
- Gains are large overall but unevenly shared between and within countries.
:::
