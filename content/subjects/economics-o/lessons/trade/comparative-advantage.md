## Introduction

Zimbabwe exports tobacco, gold, platinum, chrome, lithium and other minerals, and it imports fuel, vehicles, machinery, medicines and many manufactured goods. Why does it not simply make everything it needs at home? Because every country has limited resources, and some countries can produce some goods far more cheaply than others. By concentrating on what it does best and trading for the rest, a country can enjoy more goods than it could make on its own.

This lesson explains the three reasons for international trade that both syllabuses name: **factor endowment**, **absolute advantage** and **comparative advantage**. Examiners like to give a small output table for two countries and two goods and ask you to identify which country has the advantage in each good. The method is short and mechanical once you understand opportunity cost, so it is a good place to pick up marks.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Factor endowment"}
The quantity and quality of the factors of production (land and natural resources, labour, capital and enterprise) that a country has.
:::

:::definition{term="Absolute advantage"}
A country has an absolute advantage in a good when it can produce more of that good than another country with the same quantity of resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Comparative advantage"}
A country has a comparative advantage in a good when it can produce that good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialisation by country"}
A country concentrating its resources on producing the goods and services in which it is most efficient (lowest cost) and trading to obtain other goods.
:::

:::formula{name="Opportunity cost from an output table"}
Opportunity cost of one unit of good X = output of good Y given up ÷ output of good X gained

(using the outputs the same resources could make of each good)
:::

## Factor endowment

Countries differ in their resources. Zimbabwe has mineral deposits such as gold, platinum and lithium, fertile land and a climate suited to tobacco and cotton, and natural attractions such as Victoria Falls and Hwange National Park. Other countries have oil fields, large stocks of capital and advanced technology, or a very large, low-cost labour force. A country tends to export goods that use intensively the factors it has in abundance, and to import goods that need factors it lacks. Some goods cannot be produced at all without a particular resource (a country with no oil cannot produce crude oil), so trade is the only way to obtain them.

## Absolute advantage

If one country is better at making one good and another country is better at making a second good, both gain when each specialises in the good it makes best and they trade.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: absolute advantage]
Two fictional countries, P and Q, each use the same quantity of resources. The table shows what those resources can produce of **either** good.

| Country | Tobacco (tonnes) | Wheat (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Country P | 40 | 20 |
| Country Q | 10 | 30 |

1. P makes more tobacco (40 against 10): **P has the absolute advantage in tobacco.**
2. Q makes more wheat (30 against 20): **Q has the absolute advantage in wheat.**
3. Without trade, suppose each country uses half its resources on each good:

|  | Tobacco (tonnes) | Wheat (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Country P (half and half) | 20 | 10 |
| Country Q (half and half) | 5 | 15 |
| **Total before** | 25 | 25 |
| **Total after specialising** | 40 | 30 |

4. If P makes only tobacco and Q only wheat, world output rises by 15 tonnes of tobacco and 5 tonnes of wheat. By trading, both countries can share these gains.
:::

## Comparative advantage

The more surprising idea is that trade is still worthwhile when one country is better at producing **both** goods. What matters is not who produces more but who gives up less. A country should specialise in the good in which its advantage is greatest, or its disadvantage smallest: the good with the **lower opportunity cost**.

To find the opportunity cost, ask: "To make one more unit of this good, how much of the other good must the country give up?" Divide the output of the good given up by the output of the good gained.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 2: comparative advantage]
Two fictional countries, A and B, use the same quantity of resources. The table shows what each could produce of **either** good.

| Country | Maize (tonnes) | Cloth (thousand metres) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Country A | 60 | 30 |
| Country B | 20 | 20 |

1. Country A produces more of both goods: it has the **absolute advantage in both**.
2. Opportunity costs:

| Country | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of maize | Opportunity cost of 1 thousand metres of cloth |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Country A | 30 ÷ 60 = 0.5 thousand metres of cloth | 60 ÷ 30 = 2 tonnes of maize |
| Country B | 20 ÷ 20 = 1 thousand metres of cloth | 20 ÷ 20 = 1 tonne of maize |

3. Maize costs A only 0.5 of cloth but costs B 1: **A has the comparative advantage in maize.**
4. Cloth costs B only 1 tonne of maize but costs A 2: **B has the comparative advantage in cloth.**
5. Check the gains. Before trade, each country splits its resources equally; after, B makes only cloth and A uses one-sixth of its resources on cloth, so that cloth output stays the same:

|  | Maize (tonnes) | Cloth (thousand metres) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Country A (half and half) | 30 | 15 |
| Country B (half and half) | 10 | 10 |
| **Total before specialising** | 40 | 25 |
| Country A (one-sixth of resources on cloth) | 50 | 5 |
| Country B (all resources on cloth) | 0 | 20 |
| **Total after specialising** | 50 | 25 |

6. The same resources now give 10 more tonnes of maize and the same amount of cloth. Both countries can gain if they trade at a rate between their opportunity costs: for example, 1 thousand metres of cloth for 1.5 tonnes of maize. A then pays less than the 2 tonnes it would give up to make cloth itself, and B receives more than the 1 tonne it would get by making maize.
:::

Notice the pattern: the two opportunity costs for one country are always the reciprocal of each other (0.5 and 2 for A). So a country can never have the comparative advantage in both goods. If it has the lower opportunity cost in one good, the other country must have it in the second good, unless the opportunity costs happen to be the same, in which case there is no gain from specialising.

## Specialisation on the basis of lowest cost

The Cambridge syllabus asks you to explain that specialisation by country is based on the **best allocation of resources** or the **lowest cost of production**. When each country uses its resources where their opportunity cost is lowest, the world produces more from the same resources, so the resources are allocated more efficiently. In practice, a country's comparative advantage is shaped by its factor endowment, the skills of its workers, its technology and infrastructure, and the cost of transport to its markets.

:::example{title="Comparative advantage in a real setting"}
A Zimbabwean farmer who grows high-quality flue-cured tobacco may also be able to grow vegetables well. But the land, skills and curing barns used for tobacco earn far more when used for tobacco. The opportunity cost of growing vegetables on that land is high, so the farmer sells tobacco and buys vegetables. Countries reason in the same way: they export goods with a low opportunity cost and import goods that would be costly for them to make.
:::

### Limits to the theory

- It assumes no transport costs; high transport costs, common for landlocked countries such as Zimbabwe, can wipe out the gains.
- It assumes resources can move easily from one industry to another; in reality, a cloth worker cannot become a farmer overnight.
- It assumes constant opportunity costs; as a country expands one industry, costs may rise.
- It ignores trade barriers, exchange rates and the risk of depending on a few exports whose prices can fall sharply.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing absolute and comparative advantage"}
Absolute advantage is about who produces **more** with the same resources. Comparative advantage is about who has the **lower opportunity cost**. A country can have the absolute advantage in both goods but the comparative advantage in only one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing the wrong way round"}
The opportunity cost of maize is cloth given up ÷ maize gained. Dividing maize by cloth gives the opportunity cost of cloth instead. Always write the units ("0.5 thousand metres of cloth per tonne of maize") to check.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a country has a comparative advantage in both goods"}
Because the two opportunity costs are reciprocals, each country can have the comparative advantage in only one of the two goods.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / define:** absolute advantage = more output from the same resources; comparative advantage = lower opportunity cost. Use the words "same resources" and "opportunity cost".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Calculate / identify:** draw up a small opportunity cost table, show the division for each country and each good, then state clearly which country specialises in which good.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain / analyse:** link specialisation on lowest opportunity cost to higher total output, then to the gains from trade (more goods, lower prices). **Discuss:** weigh the gains against transport costs, immobile resources, over-dependence on a few exports and protection by trading partners.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Countries trade because their factor endowments differ, and because they have absolute or comparative advantages.
- Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources.
- Comparative advantage: a lower opportunity cost; opportunity cost of X = output of Y given up ÷ output of X.
- A country can have the absolute advantage in both goods but the comparative advantage in only one.
- Specialising on comparative advantage raises total output, and trade at a rate between the opportunity costs lets both countries gain; transport costs, immobile resources and trade barriers limit the gains.
:::
