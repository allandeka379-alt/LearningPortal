## Introduction

Zimbabwe trades more with its neighbours, especially South Africa, Zambia, Mozambique and Botswana, than with almost any other part of the world. Goods cross Beitbridge, Chirundu, Plumtree and Forbes border posts every day. To make this trade easier and cheaper, Zimbabwe belongs to several regional organisations: the **Southern African Development Community (SADC)**, the **Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA)** and the **African Union (AU)**.

When countries agree to reduce the barriers between them and to cooperate on economic policy, they are taking part in **economic integration**. ZIMSEC asks you to give the reasons for integration, using SADC, COMESA and the African Union as examples. In the exam you should know the forms of integration, the aims of these organisations, and the benefits and problems of integration for a country like Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic integration"}
The process by which countries reduce or remove barriers to trade and the movement of factors of production between them and coordinate their economic policies.
:::

:::definition{term="Preferential trade area"}
A group of countries that charge lower (but not necessarily zero) tariffs on trade with each other than on trade with other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove tariffs and quotas on goods traded among themselves, while each member keeps its own tariffs against non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Customs union"}
A free trade area whose members also charge a **common external tariff** on imports from non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Common market"}
A customs union in which labour and capital, as well as goods and services, can move freely between members.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic union"}
A common market whose members also coordinate their economic policies; a monetary union goes further and adopts a single currency.
:::

## The forms of integration: a ladder

The forms are often shown as steps on a ladder, each including everything on the step below:

| Form | Tariffs between members | Common external tariff | Free movement of labour and capital | Common economic policies or currency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Preferential trade area | Reduced | No | No | No |
| Free trade area | Removed | No | No | No |
| Customs union | Removed | Yes | No | No |
| Common market | Removed | Yes | Yes | No |
| Economic (and monetary) union | Removed | Yes | Yes | Yes |

## Reasons for economic integration

- **A larger market:** firms can sell to the whole region without tariffs, so they can grow and gain **economies of scale**.
- **More trade and specialisation:** each member can specialise according to its comparative advantage.
- **Lower prices and more choice** for consumers.
- **More competition**, which encourages efficiency.
- **Attracting investment:** foreign firms are more willing to invest in a country that gives access to a regional market.
- **Joint infrastructure:** shared roads, railways, power lines and border posts (such as one-stop border posts, where officials of both countries work together) reduce transport costs and delays.
- **Bargaining power:** acting together gives small economies a stronger voice in world trade negotiations.
- **Peace and political cooperation:** countries that trade with each other have strong reasons to cooperate.

## SADC, COMESA and the African Union

:::keyfact{title="Southern African Development Community (SADC)"}
Formed in 1992 from the Southern African Development Coordination Conference (SADCC, set up in 1980); headquarters in Gaborone, Botswana. Its aims include promoting economic growth and development, reducing poverty, deepening regional integration and trade, developing shared infrastructure, and promoting peace and security. SADC launched a free trade area in 2008. Zimbabwe is a founding member.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA)"}
Formed in 1994, replacing the Preferential Trade Area for Eastern and Southern Africa (PTA, set up in 1981); headquarters in Lusaka, Zambia. Its aim is to build a large economic and trading unit by removing barriers to trade and investment among its members; it launched a free trade area in 2000 and works towards a customs union. Zimbabwe is a member.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The African Union (AU)"}
Launched in 2002, replacing the Organisation of African Unity (OAU, founded in 1963); headquarters in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Its aims include unity and cooperation among African states, peace and security, and economic integration and development across the continent. Its African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) agreement was signed in 2018, and trading under it began in 2021. Zimbabwe is a member of the AU.
:::

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: how a free trade area changes where a country buys]
A fictional SADC member imports cement. Cement costs US\$90 a tonne from a fellow member and US\$85 a tonne from a non-member overseas, before tariffs. The country charges a 20% tariff on imports.

1. **Before the free trade area:** member's cement = 90 × 1.20 = **US\$108**; non-member's cement = 85 × 1.20 = **US\$102**. The country buys from the non-member.
2. **After the free trade area:** the member's cement enters tariff-free at **US\$90**; the non-member's still costs **US\$102**. The country now buys from the member.
3. Builders pay US\$12 a tonne less than before (102 − 90), and regional trade grows. But the government loses the tariff revenue of 0.20 × 85 = **US\$17** a tonne it used to collect, and the cement now comes from a supplier that is not the world's cheapest (US\$90 against US\$85).
:::

## Benefits of integration for Zimbabwe

- **Wider markets** for Zimbabwean exports such as processed foods, fertiliser, cement, tobacco and manufactured goods within the region.
- **Cheaper imports** of goods and inputs from neighbours, lowering costs for firms and prices for consumers.
- **Investment and infrastructure**: regional projects on roads, rail, power and border posts; Zimbabwe's position at the centre of the region makes it a natural transport route.
- **Easier movement of people and capital** for work, study and business, and cooperation on matters such as disease control and drought.
- **Political and diplomatic support** and a stronger collective voice in negotiations with the rest of the world.

:::example{title="Integration seen from a Zimbabwean factory"}
A fictional Zimbabwean maker of cooking oil can now sell in Zambia and Malawi without paying tariffs, so it doubles its output and its average cost falls. At the same time, a fictional Zimbabwean biscuit maker faces tariff-free competition from a much larger South African firm and loses part of its home market. Integration rewards competitive firms and puts pressure on the rest, so the overall effect on jobs depends on which firms are stronger.
:::

## Problems of integration

- **Competition from stronger economies:** larger, more industrialised members (such as South Africa) may out-compete smaller members' firms, causing closures and job losses.
- **Loss of tariff revenue** on trade with members.
- **Unequal gains:** benefits may concentrate in the richest member or its industrial centres.
- **Overlapping memberships** (many countries belong to both SADC and COMESA) create conflicting rules and make a single customs union hard to achieve.
- **Poor infrastructure and slow border procedures** limit how much trade actually grows.
- **Similar exports:** many members export similar primary products and import manufactured goods from outside the region, so there is less to trade with each other.
- **Loss of policy independence** in deeper forms of integration, and political disagreements between members.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up a free trade area and a customs union"}
Both remove tariffs between members, but only a customs union has a **common external tariff**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing statistics about SADC or COMESA"}
Do not quote trade figures or member counts you are unsure of. Use the well-established facts (founding dates, headquarters, aims) and explain the economics.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing benefits only"}
Integration helps competitive firms and consumers, but uncompetitive firms and government revenue can suffer. Give both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / describe:** name the form of integration precisely and give its defining feature (tariffs, common external tariff, factor movement, common policies).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain reasons:** link each reason to an economic result: larger market → economies of scale → lower costs and prices.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss benefits and problems for Zimbabwe:** use the regional context (neighbours, transport routes, a strong neighbour's industries), weigh gains for exporters and consumers against losses for weaker firms and revenue, and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic integration means reducing barriers between countries and coordinating policies.
- Forms: preferential trade area → free trade area → customs union → common market → economic (monetary) union.
- Reasons: larger markets, economies of scale, specialisation, lower prices, investment, shared infrastructure, bargaining power and peace.
- Zimbabwe belongs to SADC (1992, Gaborone), COMESA (1994, Lusaka) and the African Union (2002, Addis Ababa).
- Benefits: wider markets, cheaper imports, investment, infrastructure. Problems: competition from stronger members, lost revenue, unequal gains, overlapping memberships and weak infrastructure.
:::
