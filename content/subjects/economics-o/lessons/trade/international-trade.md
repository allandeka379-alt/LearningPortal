## Introduction

A farmer in Mutare who sells tomatoes to a trader from Harare is taking part in **home trade**. When the same farmer's neighbour sells tobacco at the auction floors and the tobacco is shipped to a buyer in Asia or Europe, that is **foreign trade**. Both involve buying and selling, but crossing a border adds new problems: different currencies, customs duties, longer distances and different laws.

International trade lets countries obtain goods they cannot produce, and it lets them **specialise** in what they produce best. Zimbabwe, for example, specialises in minerals, tobacco and tourism, and it imports fuel, machinery and many manufactured goods. Both syllabuses ask you to compare home and foreign trade, define specialisation by country, and explain the advantages and disadvantages of international trade. These points appear in multiple-choice questions, short-answer questions and essays.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Home trade (domestic trade)"}
The buying and selling of goods and services within the borders of one country.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign trade (international trade)"}
The exchange of goods and services between countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Exports"}
Goods and services produced in a country and sold to buyers in other countries; they bring money into the country.
:::

:::definition{term="Imports"}
Goods and services produced abroad and bought by residents of a country; money flows out to pay for them.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialisation by country"}
A country concentrating its resources on producing the goods and services in which it is most efficient, and trading to obtain the rest.
:::

## Home trade and foreign trade compared

| Feature | Home trade | Foreign trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Currency | One currency is used | Currencies must be exchanged; exchange rates can change |
| Distance and transport | Usually shorter and cheaper | Longer; may need ports, rail and road links, which raise costs for a landlocked country |
| Laws and regulations | One set of laws | Customs procedures, import licences, product standards and tariffs |
| Documentation | Simple invoices and receipts | Export and import documents, certificates of origin, customs declarations |
| Language and culture | Usually shared | May differ, so marketing and contracts are harder |
| Risk | Lower | Higher: exchange rate changes, non-payment, political events |
| Market size | Limited to the home market | Access to much larger world markets |

The basic purpose of both is the same: to exchange goods and services so that people can obtain what they want and producers can sell what they make.

## Exports and imports

Exports and imports can be **visible** (goods such as gold, tobacco, fuel and vehicles) or **invisible** (services such as tourism, transport, insurance and education). When foreign tourists spend money at Victoria Falls, Zimbabwe is exporting a service, even though nothing leaves the country. When a Zimbabwean student pays fees to a university abroad, Zimbabwe is importing a service.

## Specialisation by country

Countries specialise because they have different resources (factor endowments) and because some can produce certain goods at a lower opportunity cost. A country with rich mineral deposits specialises in mining; one with a large, skilled workforce and plenty of capital may specialise in manufactured goods or financial services. Specialisation is the international version of the division of labour: just as a worker who concentrates on one task becomes skilled at it, a country that concentrates on certain industries can produce them on a large scale, gain economies of scale and build expertise.

:::worked-solution[Worked example 1: how specialised is a country's export trade?]
The table shows the exports of a fictional mineral-rich country in one year.

| Export | Value (US\$ million) | Share of total exports (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Gold | 1,200 | 40.0 |
| Tobacco | 900 | 30.0 |
| Other minerals | 600 | 20.0 |
| Manufactured goods | 300 | 10.0 |
| **Total** | **3,000** | **100.0** |

1. Each share = value ÷ total × 100; for gold, 1,200 ÷ 3,000 × 100 = 40%.
2. Gold and tobacco together make up 70% of exports: the country is **highly specialised** in primary products.
3. If the world price of gold fell by 25% with the same quantity sold, gold earnings would fall by 0.25 × 1,200 = US\$300 million, which is **10%** of total export earnings. This shows the main risk of narrow specialisation.
:::

## Advantages of international trade and specialisation

- **Access to goods a country cannot produce**, such as crude oil for a country without oil fields, or advanced medicines and machinery.
- **Lower costs and prices**: each country produces what it makes most efficiently, so total world output rises and goods are cheaper.
- **Greater choice** for consumers.
- **Larger markets** for producers, allowing **economies of scale**.
- **Foreign currency earnings** from exports, which pay for imports.
- **Competition** from imports pushes local firms to become more efficient and improve quality.
- **Transfer of technology and ideas** through trade and contact with foreign firms.
- **Employment** in export industries such as mining, farming and tourism.

## Disadvantages of international trade and specialisation

- **Over-dependence on a few exports**: if world prices or demand fall, export earnings, incomes and jobs fall sharply.
- **Dependence on imports** of essentials such as fuel and food, which can be disrupted by wars, sanctions or transport problems.
- **Competition from imports** can force local firms to close, causing structural unemployment.
- **Exchange rate risk** and the need for foreign currency.
- **Infant industries** find it hard to grow when cheaper imports are available.
- **Spread of harmful goods** and environmental damage from transport and from over-exploiting resources for export.

:::example{title="Specialisation in practice"}
A fictional country specialises in copper. In good years, high copper prices bring foreign currency, government revenue and jobs in mining towns, and it can import cheap consumer goods. When the world price of copper halves during a world recession, mines lay off workers, the government loses revenue, and the country struggles to pay for fuel imports. Its best response is to keep the benefits of specialisation while gradually **diversifying**, for example by processing copper into wire and cable at home and by developing agriculture and tourism.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting services"}
Exports and imports include services. Tourism by foreigners is an export, even though the tourists consume the service inside the country.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing only advantages"}
Specialisation brings risks as well as gains. A good answer explains both and judges which matters more for the country in the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing specialisation with self-sufficiency"}
A specialised country does **not** produce everything it needs; it relies on trade for the goods it does not make.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe / compare:** for home and foreign trade, set out the points as pairs (currency, distance, laws, documents, language, risk), one feature of each.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain:** link each advantage to its result, for example "larger markets allow economies of scale, so average costs and prices fall".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss:** weigh the gains from specialisation against dependence on a few exports, competition for local firms and exchange rate risk, then conclude with a judgement for the country described (often: specialise, but diversify gradually).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Home trade takes place within a country; foreign trade takes place between countries and involves currencies, distance, customs procedures and more risk.
- Exports bring money in and imports send money out; both can be goods (visible) or services (invisible).
- Specialisation by country means concentrating on the goods a country produces most efficiently and trading for the rest.
- Advantages: goods otherwise unavailable, lower costs, choice, economies of scale, foreign currency, competition and technology.
- Disadvantages: dependence on a few exports and on imports, closure of local firms, exchange rate risk and environmental costs.
:::
