## Introduction

Walk through a busy market such as Mbare Musika in Harare or a vegetable market in Bulawayo and you see a market system at work on a small scale. Nobody tells the traders how many tomatoes to bring or what price to charge. If tomatoes are scarce one week, prices rise and more farmers bring tomatoes the next week. If a trader charges too much, buyers go to the next stall.

A **market economic system** applies the same idea to a whole economy. This lesson explains what the system is and weighs up its advantages and disadvantages. Both ZIMSEC and Cambridge ask you to *explain* these and to *discuss* whether markets or governments should allocate resources, so you need balanced arguments and a clear conclusion.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC: market economy or free enterprise economy. Cambridge 0455: market economic system. They mean the same thing.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market economic system"}
An economic system in which resources are mainly privately owned and the price mechanism, through the decisions of consumers and producers, allocates resources, with a limited role for government.
:::

:::definition{term="Private sector"}
The part of the economy made up of firms owned by individuals and shareholders, run for profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumer sovereignty"}
The power of consumers to decide what is produced through the way they spend their money.
:::

:::definition{term="Market failure"}
A situation in which the market, left to itself, does not allocate resources efficiently or fairly, for example under-providing public goods or over-producing goods that cause pollution.
:::

## How a market economic system works

- **Private ownership:** individuals and firms own land, capital and businesses and can keep the income from them.
- **Self-interest:** consumers try to get the most satisfaction from their incomes; firms try to make the most profit; workers seek the best wages.
- **Price mechanism:** prices rise when goods are scarce and fall when they are plentiful. These price changes tell firms what to produce and tell consumers what to buy (the price mechanism is covered in its own lesson).
- **Competition:** many firms compete for customers, which keeps prices down and quality up.
- **Limited government:** the state provides defence, law and order, and enforces property rights and contracts.

## Advantages of a market economic system

1. **Choice.** Consumers can spend their income as they wish, and many firms compete to offer different products. Workers can choose their jobs and entrepreneurs can start businesses.
2. **Efficiency.** Competition and the profit motive push firms to cut costs and avoid waste. Firms that waste resources make losses and close, and their resources move to better uses.
3. **Incentives and innovation.** Because entrepreneurs keep their profits, they have a reason to develop new products and methods. Mobile money services grew quickly because firms saw profit in meeting a real need.
4. **Responsiveness.** Prices adjust quickly to changes in demand and supply, so resources move to where they are wanted without waiting for a government decision.
5. **No large planning bureaucracy.** The economy co-ordinates itself through prices, so there is no need for costly officials to plan every output.

:::example{title="Responsiveness: a rise in demand for solar lamps"}
After a period of long power cuts in a fictional town, households want solar lamps.

1. Shops run short and the price of solar lamps rises.
2. Higher profits encourage importers and local assemblers to supply more lamps; new traders enter the market.
3. As supply rises, the price falls back and more households can buy.

No official had to decide that the town needed more lamps: price signals and the profit motive moved resources to meet the new demand.
:::

## Disadvantages of a market economic system

1. **Inequality of income and wealth.** Goods go to those who can pay. People with valuable skills or property earn far more than others, while the sick, elderly and unemployed may have little income. Over time the gap can widen.
2. **Public goods are not provided.** Goods such as street lighting, national defence and flood barriers cannot easily be charged for, because people who do not pay cannot be stopped from benefiting. Private firms cannot make a profit, so they do not supply them.
3. **Merit goods are under-consumed.** Education and health care benefit society as well as the person using them, but if they are sold at market prices, poorer families buy too little.
4. **Demerit goods are over-consumed.** Harmful goods such as cigarettes and alcohol are produced and consumed in larger quantities than is good for society, because people may ignore or underestimate the harm.
5. **External costs are ignored.** A factory that pollutes a river does not pay the costs it imposes on farmers and fishermen downstream, so it produces more than is best for society.
6. **Monopoly power.** Firms may grow large or join together, reduce competition and charge high prices.
7. **Instability.** Market economies can go through booms and slumps, with periods of high unemployment.

:::example{title="Inequality: who gets the medicine?"}
In a fictional country with no government health service, a new medicine costs US\$40 a month.

1. A family with a monthly income of US\$1,500 can easily afford it.
2. A family earning US\$120 a month would have to give up a third of its income, so it may go without.
3. The market allocates the medicine by ability to pay, not by need: the poorer family, even if more ill, may not receive it.

This is why most countries' governments provide or subsidise health care: a reason for a mixed economy.
:::

## Weighing up the market system

The advantages are strongest for ordinary private goods such as food, clothing, phones and transport services, where many firms compete and consumers can judge what they are buying. The disadvantages are strongest for public goods, merit goods, goods with large external costs and markets dominated by one firm. This is why nearly every country uses a **mixed** economic system: markets for most goods, with government action where markets fail.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying markets are always efficient"}
Markets are efficient for most private goods, but they fail with public goods, merit and demerit goods, externalities and monopoly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages without explaining them"}
"Choice" alone earns little. Explain why: many competing firms and consumer sovereignty mean products match what people want.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling inequality a market failure in every answer"}
Inequality is a disadvantage of the market system; examiners usually treat it as a question of fairness (equity) rather than inefficiency. Say which you mean.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain, then develop"}
For *explain one advantage*, state the advantage, give the reason (competition, profit motive, price signals) and the result for consumers or the economy.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss questions"}
For *discuss whether a market economic system is the best way to allocate resources*, give at least two developed advantages, two developed disadvantages, and a conclusion that says "it depends on the type of good" with an example of each.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A market economic system relies on private ownership, self-interest, competition and the price mechanism, with limited government.
- Advantages: choice, efficiency, incentives to innovate, quick response to changes, no costly planning.
- Disadvantages: inequality, no public goods, too few merit goods, too many demerit goods, ignored external costs, monopoly power, instability.
- The system works best for ordinary private goods in competitive markets.
- These weaknesses explain why most countries have mixed economies.
:::
