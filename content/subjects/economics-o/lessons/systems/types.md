## Introduction

Every country faces the same basic economic problem: scarce resources and unlimited wants. Every country must therefore decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. But countries make these decisions in very different ways. In some, millions of buyers and sellers decide through prices. In others, a government office decides how much maize, cement or steel each farm and factory must produce. Most countries, including Zimbabwe, combine the two.

The way a country organises the ownership of resources and the making of these decisions is its **economic system**. This lesson sets out the features of the three systems you need for ZIMSEC (Form 1 and Form 2 objectives). The advantages and disadvantages of each system are in the next two lessons.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC uses market (free enterprise), planned (command) and mixed economy. Cambridge 0455 uses market economic system and mixed economic system, and does not examine the planned economy.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic system"}
The way a country organises the ownership of resources and the making of decisions about what, how and for whom to produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Market economy (free enterprise economy)"}
An economic system in which resources are mainly privately owned and decisions about production and consumption are made by individuals and firms through the price mechanism, with little government involvement.
:::

:::definition{term="Planned economy (command economy)"}
An economic system in which resources are owned by the state and the government decides what, how and for whom to produce, usually through a central planning authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixed economy"}
An economic system in which both the private sector and the public sector own resources and make decisions: some goods are allocated by the price mechanism and some by the government.
:::

## The market economy

In a market economy the key decision-makers are **households** (as consumers and as owners of the factors of production) and **private firms**.

- Ownership: land, capital and businesses are privately owned.
- Motive: firms aim to make profit; consumers aim to get the most satisfaction from their incomes.
- Co-ordination: the price mechanism (the interaction of demand and supply) signals what is wanted.
- Role of government: limited to defence, law and order, and protecting property rights and contracts.
- Consumer sovereignty: consumers' spending decisions determine what firms produce.

## The planned economy

In a planned economy the key decision-maker is the **state**.

- Ownership: the state owns land, factories, mines and most other capital.
- Motive: to meet targets set by the government for the benefit of society as a whole.
- Co-ordination: a central planning authority sets output targets, allocates resources to firms and often fixes prices and wages.
- Role of private enterprise: very small or none.
- Distribution: goods may be rationed or allocated according to the plan rather than sold to the highest bidder.

The former Soviet Union in the twentieth century is the best-known example. Today very few economies are close to fully planned.

## The mixed economy

A mixed economy has a **private sector** (firms owned by individuals and shareholders) and a **public sector** (government departments and state-owned enterprises, also called parastatals or public corporations).

- Many goods (food, clothing, phones, transport services) are produced by private firms for profit and allocated by prices.
- The government provides or pays for goods the market would under-provide, such as defence, roads, street lighting, basic education and health care.
- The government regulates markets, for example with laws on safety, consumer protection and the environment, taxes and subsidies, and sometimes price controls.

Almost every economy in the world today is mixed. They differ in the **balance**: some lean towards the market, others give the state a bigger role.

## How each system answers the three questions

| Question | Market economy | Planned economy | Mixed economy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What to produce? | Whatever consumers are willing and able to buy at a profitable price | What the planners decide society needs | Mostly by consumer demand; the state decides on public services |
| How to produce? | Firms choose the cheapest methods to maximise profit | Planners decide the methods and allocate resources | Private firms choose methods; the state decides for public services and sets rules |
| For whom to produce? | Those with the income to pay | Those the plan selects (often by need or equally) | Mostly those who can pay, with state help for those in need (free or subsidised services, welfare) |

:::example{title="Three ways to supply maize meal"}
Imagine three countries each deciding how much maize meal to produce.

1. **Market economy:** if households want more maize meal, its price rises, millers and farmers earn more profit, and they produce more. Families with low incomes may buy less if the price is high.
2. **Planned economy:** the planning ministry sets a target of a certain number of tonnes, orders state farms to grow the maize and state mills to grind it, fixes the price, and allocates bags to shops by region.
3. **Mixed economy:** private farmers and millers produce and sell most maize meal at market prices, but the government keeps a strategic grain reserve and may subsidise maize meal or give food aid to poor households in a drought.
:::

:::example{title="Recognising a system from its features"}
Country A: the state owns all large factories; a national plan sets five-year output targets; shop prices are fixed by a government board. This is a **planned** economy.

Country B: most firms are private, but the state runs the railways, the power utility and free primary schools, and collects income tax to pay for clinics. This is a **mixed** economy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a market economy has no government"}
Even a market economy needs a government to keep law and order, defend the country and enforce contracts. Its role is limited, not absent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a mixed economy is half and half"}
Mixed means both sectors play a part. The balance can lean heavily towards the market or the state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the public sector and the public"}
The public sector is the part of the economy owned or run by the government, not businesses owned by members of the public. A public limited company is in the private sector.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Features questions"}
For *state the features of a planned economy*, give ownership (state), decision-maker (central planners), co-ordination (targets, fixed prices) and motive (social goals). One clear feature per mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How each system answers the questions"}
For *explain how a market economy answers the three basic questions*, take what, how and for whom in turn and link each to prices, profit or income.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An economic system is how a country organises ownership and decisions about what, how and for whom to produce.
- Market economy: private ownership, decisions through the price mechanism, limited government.
- Planned economy: state ownership, decisions by central planners, fixed prices and targets.
- Mixed economy: private and public sectors both own resources and make decisions; almost all economies are mixed.
- Each system answers the three basic questions in its own way.
:::
