## Introduction

The market economic system gives choice and efficiency but leaves some people without health care and some goods unprovided. A planned economy tries to fix those problems by letting the state decide everything, but brings problems of its own. A **mixed economy** tries to combine the strengths of both.

This lesson weighs up planned and mixed economies, compares the three systems side by side and looks at Zimbabwe's own economic system. Expect *explain* and *discuss* questions: "Discuss whether a mixed economy is better than a market economy" is a typical essay or Section B question.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards examine the mixed economic system (definition, advantages, disadvantages). The planned (command) economy, the comparison of all three systems and Zimbabwe's economic system are ZIMSEC content.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Planned (command) economy"}
An economic system in which the state owns most resources and a central authority decides what, how and for whom to produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixed economic system"}
An economic system in which both the private sector and the public sector own resources and allocate them: the price mechanism allocates some resources and the government allocates others.
:::

:::definition{term="Parastatal (public corporation)"}
A business enterprise owned by the state and run by a board appointed by the government, such as a national railway or power utility. Cambridge calls these public sector firms.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Small-scale economic activities that are not registered or regulated by the state and usually pay little or no tax, such as street vending and small home-based workshops.
:::

## The planned economy: advantages and disadvantages

### Advantages

1. **Basic needs for all.** The state can make sure that everyone has food, housing, education and health care, whatever their income.
2. **Less inequality.** With no private ownership of land and capital, there are no large fortunes from rent, interest and profit.
3. **Public and merit goods provided.** Planners can choose to produce goods that markets under-provide.
4. **Fewer wasteful duplicates.** There is no need for ten firms to advertise competing brands of the same soap.
5. **Low unemployment.** The state can find a job for everyone.
6. **Long-term priorities.** The state can direct resources to targets such as building industries or infrastructure.

### Disadvantages

1. **Information problems.** Planners must decide output for millions of goods. Without prices to signal what people want, shortages of some goods and surpluses of others are common.
2. **Lack of incentives.** Workers and managers who cannot keep the reward for extra effort have little reason to work harder or cut costs.
3. **Little innovation.** Without the profit motive, there is little reason to develop new products or methods.
4. **Little choice.** Consumers buy what the plan provides, often a narrow range of poor-quality goods.
5. **Bureaucracy.** Planning needs many officials, which is costly and slow.
6. **Queues and black markets.** When fixed prices are below the level at which the market would clear, goods run short, people queue, and illegal markets develop.

## The mixed economy

In a mixed economy the market allocates most private goods, while the government:

- provides public goods (defence, street lighting, roads) and merit goods (education, health care);
- owns some enterprises, especially in strategic industries such as electricity, water and railways;
- regulates markets with laws on safety, competition and the environment;
- taxes and subsidises to change what is produced and to redistribute income (for example social welfare payments).

### Advantages of a mixed economy

1. **Choice and efficiency from the private sector.** Most goods are still produced by competing firms for profit.
2. **Market failures corrected.** The state provides public and merit goods, discourages demerit goods and controls pollution.
3. **Less inequality than a pure market economy.** Taxes, free services and welfare help people on low incomes.
4. **More stability.** Government spending and policy can reduce booms and slumps.

### Disadvantages of a mixed economy

1. **Government failure.** State intervention can make things worse: price controls can cause shortages, and state enterprises may be inefficient or loss-making.
2. **High taxes.** Paying for public services may need high taxes, which can reduce the incentive to work and invest.
3. **Bureaucracy and red tape.** Regulation adds costs for firms.
4. **Inefficient parastatals.** State firms that are protected from competition and rescued when they make losses may waste resources.
5. **Corruption risk.** The larger the state's role in licences and contracts, the greater the opportunity for corruption.

:::example{title="A mixed economy solving a market failure"}
In a fictional country, private clinics charge US\$25 for a consultation. Many rural families cannot afford this, so they wait until illnesses are serious.

1. The government opens public clinics funded by taxes, charging little or nothing.
2. More families seek treatment early: fewer working days are lost and infectious diseases spread less.
3. Private clinics still operate for those who want shorter waiting times.
4. The cost: taxes must rise or other spending must fall (the opportunity cost), and public clinics may suffer from long queues or medicine shortages if they are poorly funded.
:::

## Comparing the three systems

| Feature | Market economy | Planned economy | Mixed economy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership of resources | Private | State | Private and state |
| Who decides | Consumers and firms | Central planners | Firms, consumers and government |
| Allocation mechanism | Price mechanism | Plans, targets, rationing | Mainly prices, plus state provision |
| Main motive | Profit and self-interest | Social targets | Profit, plus social goals |
| Choice | Wide | Narrow | Wide |
| Inequality | High | Low | Moderate |
| Public goods | Not provided | Provided | Provided by the state |

**Similarities.** All three must answer the same three questions; all use money and some form of prices; all three have some government (even a market economy has courts and police) and, in practice, most planned economies allowed some small private trade.

## Zimbabwe's economic system

Zimbabwe has a **mixed economy**. Its main parts are:

- **The state.** Government ministries provide public services such as schools, hospitals, roads and the police; set policy; collect taxes through ZIMRA; and regulate business. The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe manages the currency and monetary policy.
- **Parastatals.** State-owned enterprises operate in areas such as electricity, railways and grain marketing.
- **Private firms.** Private businesses, from large mining, manufacturing and banking companies to small shops and farms, produce most goods and services for profit.
- **The informal sector.** A large informal sector of vendors, small traders, cross-border traders and home-based producers gives many people a livelihood.

The balance between the state and the market has changed over time. Since independence in 1980, governments have at different times increased the state's role (for example through price controls and state enterprises) and reduced it (for example through economic reform programmes that relied more on markets). Supporters of a larger state role stress basic needs, fairness and control of strategic industries; supporters of a larger market role stress efficiency, investment and choice. Economists continue to debate the right balance, and questions usually ask you to discuss both sides.

:::example{title="Identifying the sectors in a Zimbabwean town"}
In a town such as Gweru you might see:

1. A government school and a district hospital: the **public sector** providing merit goods.
2. A train run by the national railway company: a **parastatal**.
3. A supermarket, a bank and a cement factory: the **private sector** (formal).
4. Vendors selling vegetables and airtime by the roadside: the **informal sector**.

All four together make up the mixed economy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing features instead of advantages"}
"The state owns resources" is a feature. An advantage explains a benefit: "Because the state owns resources, it can make sure basic needs are met for everyone."
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided discussion"}
In a *discuss* question, give advantages and disadvantages of the system, then conclude. A list of advantages alone stays in the lowest level.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the informal sector as illegal goods"}
The informal sector is unregistered and untaxed activity, but most of what it sells (vegetables, clothing, repairs) is legal.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Compare systematically"}
For *compare the three systems*, use the same headings for each: ownership, decision-making, motive, allocation, choice and inequality. A table in your answer is acceptable.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Zimbabwe questions"}
When describing Zimbabwe's system, name the four parts (state, parastatals, private sector, informal sector) with a role for each. Do not invent statistics.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss with a conclusion"}
End with a judgement such as "A mixed economy is better for most goods because…, but its success depends on how efficient government intervention is."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Planned economies can meet basic needs and reduce inequality but suffer from information problems, weak incentives, little choice and shortages.
- A mixed economy combines a private sector using prices with a public sector that corrects market failures.
- Mixed economies risk government failure, high taxes, red tape and inefficient state firms.
- All three systems answer the same basic questions; they differ in ownership, decision-making and allocation.
- Zimbabwe is a mixed economy of the state, parastatals, private firms and a large informal sector; the right balance is debated.
:::
