## Introduction

A cattle farmer near Gwanda checks the market price on her phone, pays a supplier with mobile money and sends a photo of a sick animal to a vet in Bulawayo. A learner in Kariba follows a lesson on a tablet over the school's Wi-Fi. A family in a remote village watches television from a satellite dish. None of these people are connected by a cable: they rely on **mobile devices** and **wireless technologies**.

ZIMSEC Form 3 asks you to identify types of mobile technology and describe their role in communication, and to explain the applications of Wi-Fi, WiMAX, Bluetooth, infra-red, microwave and radio waves. Questions usually ask which technology suits a situation, and why.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mobile device"}
A portable computing device, powered by a battery, that can communicate without cables, such as a smartphone, tablet or personal digital assistant.
:::

:::definition{term="Wireless communication"}
Sending data between devices through the air using electromagnetic waves (radio waves, microwaves or infra-red) instead of cables.
:::

:::definition{term="Communication satellite"}
A satellite in orbit round the Earth that receives signals from one ground station and re-transmits them to another, allowing communication over very long distances.
:::

## Mobile technology

- **Mobile phones** (smartphones): voice calls, text messages, mobile data for internet, email and social media, mobile money, a camera and GPS. Phones connect to the mobile network through base stations (masts); each mast serves an area called a cell, and calls are handed from mast to mast as the user moves.
- **Tablets**: larger touch screens for reading, video calls, e-learning and presentations; they connect by Wi-Fi and often by mobile data.
- **PDAs** (personal digital assistants): handheld organisers for diaries, contacts and notes. They were the forerunners of the smartphone, and handheld devices of this kind are still used for data collection, for example by meter readers or census workers.
- **Satellite communication**: satellites relay telephone calls, television, internet access and navigation signals. **GPS** receivers work out their position from signals sent by several navigation satellites. Satellite links reach remote areas with no cables or masts, but they are expensive and the long distance to the satellite causes a delay.

Mobile technology lets people communicate from anywhere, supports mobile banking and e-learning, and helps emergency services. Its drawbacks include small screens, limited battery life, the cost of data, gaps in coverage in rural areas and the risk of losing a device that holds personal data.

## Wireless technologies

| Technology | Range (typical) | Speed | Typical uses |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **Infra-red** | A few metres, needs line of sight | Low | TV remote controls, some short transfers between devices |
| **Bluetooth** | About 10 m | Low to medium | Wireless earphones, keyboards and mice, car hands-free kits, sharing a file between two phones (a PAN) |
| **Wi-Fi** | Tens of metres from an access point | High | Wireless LANs in homes, schools, offices, cafés and hotels |
| **WiMAX** | Several kilometres | High | Wireless broadband to homes and businesses where laying cables is costly |
| **Microwave** | Tens of kilometres between towers in line of sight; also to satellites | High | Point-to-point links between masts on hilltops, satellite links |
| **Radio waves** | Long distances; pass through walls | Varies | Radio and TV broadcasting, two-way radios, mobile phone networks |

Several points are worth learning:

- **Infra-red** cannot pass through walls and needs a clear line of sight between sender and receiver, which also makes it hard to eavesdrop on from another room.
- **Bluetooth** uses little power and devices must be **paired** before they exchange data. It creates a personal area network.
- **Wi-Fi** connects devices to a **wireless access point** or router, which joins them to the wired LAN and the internet. Thick walls and distance weaken the signal, and the bandwidth is shared between users.
- **WiMAX** is like Wi-Fi with a much greater range: one base station can serve a whole suburb or village.
- **Microwaves** travel in straight lines, so the dishes on towers must be able to "see" each other; hills and tall buildings block them.
- **Radio waves** spread out and pass through walls, so one transmitter reaches many receivers at once.

:::example
**Choosing a technology for each task.** Sipho manages a lodge near Victoria Falls.

1. Guests want internet on their phones in the rooms and by the pool: **Wi-Fi** access points around the lodge, because Wi-Fi gives fast access within tens of metres to many devices.
2. The lodge is 15 km from the nearest town, with no cable: a **microwave** link from a dish on the lodge roof to a tower in town, or **WiMAX** from a provider's base station, because both carry broadband over kilometres without cables.
3. The guides need to talk to each other on game drives: **two-way radios** using radio waves, which reach several kilometres and need no network coverage.
4. The manager uses wireless earphones with her phone: **Bluetooth**, which is low-power and short-range.
:::

:::example
**Satellite communication for a remote school.** A rural school in Binga has no fibre and a weak mobile signal. A satellite dish on the roof sends and receives data via a satellite high above the equator. The school gets internet access anywhere under the satellite's coverage, without cables. The drawbacks are a high cost, a noticeable delay (the signal travels tens of thousands of kilometres up and back) and loss of signal in heavy storms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Wi-Fi and WiMAX"}
Both are wireless broadband, but Wi-Fi serves a building (tens of metres) while WiMAX serves a wide area (kilometres).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Bluetooth connects a home to the internet"}
Bluetooth is a short-range link between nearby devices (about 10 m). A home connects to the internet through an ISP, using Wi-Fi only inside the home.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting line of sight"}
Infra-red and microwave links both need a clear straight path between sender and receiver. Radio waves do not.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain an application", name the technology, the use and the reason: "Bluetooth for wireless earphones, **because** it is low-power and only needs a range of a few metres."
:::

:::exam-tip
When comparing two technologies, use the same features for both: range, speed, power use, line of sight and cost.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mobile phones, tablets, PDAs and satellites let people communicate and work from anywhere, but have limits of battery, screen, cost and coverage.
- Infra-red: a few metres, line of sight (remote controls). Bluetooth: about 10 m, low power, pairing (PANs).
- Wi-Fi: wireless LAN in a building. WiMAX: wireless broadband over kilometres.
- Microwave: point-to-point links between towers and to satellites, line of sight. Radio waves: broadcasting over long distances through walls.
- Satellites reach remote areas but are expensive and add delay.
:::
