## Introduction

The owners of a supermarket chain with branches in Harare and Gweru want point-of-sale terminals, bar code scanners and a central stock database. It sounds attractive, but before spending tens of thousands of dollars they need answers to some hard questions. Does the technology exist and will it work with the power and network supply at each branch? Will the savings repay the cost? Is it legal to store customer data this way? Will cashiers be able to use it? Can it be ready before the festive season?

A **feasibility study** answers these questions **before** the organisation commits to the project. ZIMSEC Form 2 asks you to explain the technical, economic, legal, operational and social areas of feasibility and to carry out a feasibility study. Paper 2 questions describe an organisation and ask you to explain one type of feasibility for it, or to judge whether a proposal is feasible.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Feasibility study"}
An investigation, carried out early in the systems development life cycle, to decide whether a proposed system is possible, practical and worthwhile, ending in a recommendation to go ahead or not.
:::

:::definition{term="Feasibility report"}
The document produced at the end of the feasibility study, summarising the findings for each area of feasibility and giving a recommendation to management.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-benefit analysis"}
A comparison of the costs of developing and running a new system with the benefits (savings and extra income) it is expected to bring.
:::

## The areas of feasibility

| Area | The key question | Things the analyst checks |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Technical | Can it be built and run with available technology? | Hardware and software availability; compatibility with existing systems; reliable power and internet; skills of the IT staff |
| Economic | Do the benefits outweigh the costs? | Development, hardware, software, training and running costs against savings and extra income; payback period |
| Legal | Does it obey the law? | Data protection (Cyber and Data Protection Act); copyright and software licences; tax, employment and health and safety rules |
| Operational | Will it work in practice for the people who use it? | Will staff accept it and be able to use it? Does it fit the way the organisation works? Will it meet users' needs? |
| Social | What is its effect on people and the community? | Job losses or new jobs; retraining; changes to working conditions; effect on customers and the community |
| Schedule | Can it be completed in the time available? | Deadlines, project length and the availability of staff and suppliers |

You may see the first five summarised as **TELOS** (technical, economic, legal, operational, schedule) in some books; ZIMSEC also names **social** feasibility, so learn all six.

## Costs and benefits

**Costs** can be one-off (development) or ongoing (running):

- *Development costs*: hardware, software and licences, the analyst's and programmers' time, installation, data conversion, initial training.
- *Running costs*: electricity, internet, maintenance contracts, consumables (paper, toner), support staff, licence renewals.

**Benefits** can be tangible (measurable in money) or intangible (harder to measure):

- *Tangible*: fewer staff hours, less stock wasted, fewer errors, more sales.
- *Intangible*: better customer service, faster decisions, improved reputation, better staff morale.

A simple measure is the **payback period**: how long the yearly net savings take to repay the development cost.

![Payback period graph: cumulative net savings of USD 6,000 a year (7,000 saving less 1,000 running cost) rise to meet the USD 12,000 development cost after 2 years, and reach USD 30,000 by year 5](media:diagrams/payback-period-graph.png "Figure: cumulative net savings against the 12,000 development cost, meeting at 2 years")

:::worked-solution[Payback period]
A pharmacy in Bulawayo plans a computerised stock system. Development costs USD 12,000. It will save USD 7,000 a year in wasted stock and staff time, but running costs are USD 1,000 a year. How long is the payback period?

1. Net saving per year = 7,000 − 1,000 = USD 6,000.
2. Payback period = development cost ÷ net saving per year = 12,000 ÷ 6,000 = 2 years.
3. If the system is expected to last five years, it pays for itself well within its life, so it is economically feasible (provided the estimates are realistic).
:::

## Carrying out a feasibility study

1. **Define the proposal**: the objectives and scope agreed in the preliminary investigation.
2. **Collect facts**: interviews with managers, quotations from suppliers, records of current costs, and the views of users.
3. **Identify alternatives**: for example buying an off-the-shelf package, having a bespoke system written, or improving the manual system.
4. **Assess each area of feasibility** for each alternative.
5. **Compare** costs, benefits and risks.
6. **Write the feasibility report** with a clear conclusion and recommendation.

## Writing the conclusion

The conclusion states whether the project is feasible, which alternative is recommended, and any conditions. It must be **justified** by the findings, not just stated.

:::example{title="A feasibility conclusion"}
**Proposal:** a computerised library system for Gweru Girls High (fictional).

- *Technical*: feasible. The school has a computer lab, a network and a generator; library software runs on its existing computers.
- *Economic*: feasible. The software costs less than the books lost each year through poor tracking; payback is under three years.
- *Legal*: feasible, provided learners' personal data is protected and the software is properly licensed.
- *Operational*: feasible. The librarian is keen; two student assistants will need training.
- *Social*: positive. No jobs are lost; learners can search the catalogue themselves.
- *Schedule*: data entry of 6,000 books will take a term; the system can start in Term 2.

**Conclusion:** the project is feasible. We recommend buying the off-the-shelf library package, training the librarian and assistants in Term 1, and starting the system in Term 2, on condition that a backup and data protection policy is in place.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying economic feasibility means 'it is cheap'"}
A system can be expensive and still economically feasible if its benefits are greater than its costs over its life. Compare costs **with** benefits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing operational and technical feasibility"}
Technical feasibility asks whether the **technology** can do it. Operational feasibility asks whether the **people and the organisation** can and will use it.
:::

:::mistake{title="A conclusion with no reasons"}
"It is feasible" alone earns little. Refer back to the findings: cost, technology, law, users and time.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain economic feasibility" give the question it answers **and** examples of costs and benefits from the scenario.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a scenario, make every point specific to the organisation: "the cashiers have never used computers, so operational feasibility depends on training them".
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The feasibility study is examined by ZIMSEC only (Form 2). Cambridge 0478 does not include it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A feasibility study decides, before development, whether a proposed system is possible, practical and worthwhile.
- Areas: technical (technology), economic (costs against benefits), legal (laws), operational (people will use it), social (effect on people) and schedule (time).
- Costs are development and running costs; benefits are tangible and intangible. Payback period = development cost ÷ yearly net saving.
- The study collects facts, considers alternatives, assesses each area and ends with a feasibility report.
- The conclusion recommends whether to proceed and which option, justified by the findings.
:::
