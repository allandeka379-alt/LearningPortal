## Introduction

A small hardware shop in Gweru needs software to keep track of its stock and sales. The owner has three choices. She can buy a ready-made stock-control package that thousands of other shops already use. She can pay a programmer to write a program designed for her shop alone. Or she can download a free program whose code anyone may study and change. Each choice has a different cost, a different waiting time and a different level of support.

The ZIMSEC Form 2 syllabus asks you to identify **off-the-shelf**, **customised** and **open-source** application software. Paper 2 questions usually give a scenario (a school, a clinic, a farm, a shop) and ask you to recommend one type and justify it with advantages and disadvantages. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the reasoning.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Off-the-shelf software"}
Ready-made (generic) application software written for a large market of users with similar needs, which is bought or licensed and installed straight away; for example a general word processor or an accounting package.
:::

:::definition{term="Customised (bespoke) software"}
Application software written, or adapted, specially to meet the exact requirements of one organisation, usually by its own programmers or by a software company it hires.
:::

:::definition{term="Open-source software"}
Software whose source code is made freely available so that anyone may study, change and share it, under the terms of an open-source licence.
:::

:::definition{term="Source code"}
The program as written by the programmer in a programming language, before it is translated into machine code.
:::

:::definition{term="Software licence"}
A legal agreement that states how a piece of software may be used, for example on how many computers it may be installed and whether it may be copied or changed.
:::

## Off-the-shelf software

Off-the-shelf packages are developed once and sold or licensed to many customers, so the development cost is shared among them.

**Advantages**

- Cheaper than customised software, because the cost of development is spread over many buyers.
- Available immediately: buy or download, install and use.
- Already tested by many users, so most errors have been found and fixed.
- Help is easy to find: manuals, online tutorials, user forums and trained staff who already know the package.
- Updates and support are provided by the developer.

**Disadvantages**

- It may include many features the organisation does not need, and may lack some it does need.
- The organisation may have to change the way it works to fit the software.
- It may not work well with the organisation's existing systems.
- Licence fees may have to be paid for every computer or every year.

## Customised (bespoke) software

**Advantages**

- It does exactly what the organisation needs, with no unnecessary features.
- It can be designed to fit existing working methods and other systems.
- The organisation can ask for changes as its needs grow.
- It may give a business an advantage over competitors who do not have the same system.

**Disadvantages**

- Expensive: the organisation pays the whole cost of analysis, programming and testing.
- Takes a long time to develop before it can be used.
- May contain more errors at first, because few people have used it.
- Help comes only from the developers; if they leave, maintaining the software becomes difficult.

## Open-source software

Open-source software is often free to download, and its source code is published. Well-known kinds include office suites, web browsers, operating systems and content management systems.

**Advantages**

- Usually free of licence fees.
- The source code can be changed to suit the organisation (by someone who can program).
- Large communities of volunteers find and fix errors and share help online.

**Disadvantages**

- Support may depend on community forums rather than a company with a duty to help.
- Changing the code needs programming skills.
- Some packages are less polished or less compatible with common file formats.
- Anyone can study the code, so weaknesses can be found by attackers as well as by helpers (although they are usually fixed quickly).

## Licences, freeware and shareware

Buying software usually means buying a **licence** to use it, not the program itself.

| Type | Cost | Source code available? | Typical conditions |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Commercial (proprietary) | Paid | No | Use on an agreed number of computers; copying and changing not allowed |
| Shareware | Free to try | No | Free trial for a limited time or with limited features, then payment is expected |
| Freeware | Free | No | May be used and shared free of charge, but not changed |
| Open-source | Usually free | Yes | May be studied, changed and shared, keeping the same licence terms |

Installing commercial software on more computers than the licence allows, or copying it for friends, is software piracy and breaks copyright law, including Zimbabwe's Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act.

:::example
**Recommending software for a clinic.** A rural clinic near Masvingo wants software to type letters and keep simple spreadsheets of stock. It has little money and two staff who have used office packages before.

1. The needs are **general** (letters, spreadsheets) and shared by millions of users, so customised software is not justified.
2. A **free open-source office suite** meets the needs with no licence fee, which suits the small budget.
3. A commercial off-the-shelf office package would also work and has company support, but costs licence fees.
4. Recommendation: open-source office suite, because it is free, already widely used and tested, and the staff can find help in online communities.
:::

:::example
**Recommending software for a mining company.** A mining company in the Midlands needs a system linking its ore-processing plant sensors, its shift rosters and its export paperwork. No package on the market does all three.

1. The needs are **specialised** and unique to this company, so off-the-shelf software would not fit without changing how the company works.
2. The company is large enough to pay for development and to wait for it.
3. Recommendation: **customised (bespoke)** software, because it can be designed around the company's exact processes and linked to its existing equipment. The company should make sure it receives full documentation, so that other programmers can maintain it later.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying open-source means 'free'"}
The defining feature is that the **source code is available** to study and change. Freeware is free but its code is closed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying bespoke software has no errors"}
Customised software is tested by few users before release, so it often has **more** errors at first than a widely used package.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-word advantages"}
"Cheaper" earns little on its own. Say why: "cheaper, because the development cost is shared among thousands of buyers".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**recommend** and justify", name the type, then give two reasons that are linked to the **scenario** (budget, how unusual the needs are, time available, staff skills).
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **compare** two types, write each point as a pair: "off-the-shelf is available immediately, **whereas** bespoke software takes months to develop".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Off-the-shelf software: ready-made for many users; cheap, immediate, well tested and supported, but may not fit exactly.
- Customised (bespoke) software: written for one organisation; fits exactly, but costly, slow to produce and supported only by its developers.
- Open-source software: source code available to study and change; usually free with community support, but needs skills to adapt.
- A licence states how software may be used; shareware is try-before-you-buy, freeware is free but closed.
- Recommend a type by linking its advantages to the scenario's budget, needs, time and skills.
:::
