## Introduction

Sipho, a Form 4 learner in Bulawayo, has an idea: a cheap sensor that sends a text message to a farmer when a borehole pump stops working. He can build a rough version with a microcontroller, but he has no workshop, no money for parts, no idea how to register a company and no contacts among farmers' organisations. Ideas like his succeed far more often when the young technopreneur is surrounded by organisations that provide space, equipment, advice, research and contacts. Together these form the **technopreneurship ecosystem**.

ZIMSEC Form 2 asks you to describe science parks, incubation centres, academic institutions and research and development centres as components of technopreneurship. Questions usually ask you to describe one component, explain how it helps a start-up, or apply design thinking to a local problem.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Technopreneurship ecosystem"}
The network of organisations, people and resources that together support the creation and growth of technology businesses: universities, research centres, incubators, science parks, investors, government and industry.
:::

:::definition{term="Innovation"}
Turning a new idea into a product, service or process that people actually use and that creates value.
:::

:::definition{term="Incubation centre (business incubator)"}
An organisation that helps new start-up businesses in their early stages by providing low-cost workspace, mentoring, training, shared equipment and links to investors, usually for a limited period.
:::

:::definition{term="Science park"}
An area of land, often linked to a university, with buildings and facilities where technology and research-based companies are located close together so that they can share resources and work with researchers.
:::

:::definition{term="Research and development (R&D)"}
Systematic work to discover new knowledge (research) and to use it to create or improve products, processes and services (development).
:::

## Components of the ecosystem

| Component | What it is | How it supports technopreneurs |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Academic institutions (universities, polytechnics, colleges, schools) | Places of teaching and research | Educate skilled graduates (human capital); research that can become products; laboratories and libraries; innovation competitions and hubs; staff as mentors |
| Research and development centres | Organisations (government, university or company) dedicated to research and to developing new technologies | Produce new knowledge and prototypes; test and improve products; share findings and specialist equipment; partner with start-ups to commercialise research |
| Incubation centres | Programmes and spaces for early-stage start-ups | Cheap office space, internet and power; mentoring and training in business skills; help with registration, legal issues and business plans; networking and introductions to investors |
| Science parks | Larger sites for growing technology firms | Purpose-built premises and infrastructure; firms, researchers and suppliers close together; sharing of ideas and facilities; prestige that attracts investors and partners |

Other parts of the ecosystem include **investors** (banks, angel investors, venture capital), **government** (policies, funding schemes, tax incentives, regulators), **industry** (large firms that buy from or partner with start-ups) and **innovation hubs and accelerators**, which offer short, intensive programmes to help start-ups grow fast.

In Zimbabwe, several universities have set up innovation hubs and industrial parks as part of the government's "Education 5.0" approach, which adds innovation and industrialisation to the traditional roles of teaching, research and community service. You do not need the names of particular hubs; you need to explain what each kind of component does.

:::example{title="Following a start-up through the ecosystem"}
Sipho's borehole sensor (from the introduction):

1. **Academic institution:** at a polytechnic he learns electronics and programming, and a lecturer helps him improve the circuit.
2. **R&D centre:** a university research centre tests the sensor in dusty, hot conditions and suggests a better casing.
3. **Incubation centre:** he joins an incubator for a year: a desk, internet, a mentor, a workshop on business plans and help registering his company.
4. **Investors:** at the incubator's demo day he pitches to investors and wins a small grant.
5. **Science park:** once he has employees and orders, his company rents a unit in a science park near the university, beside other electronics firms.
:::

## Technology innovation

Innovation can be classified by **how much** it changes:

- **Incremental innovation**: small improvements to an existing product or service, such as adding offline mode to an app.
- **Radical (breakthrough) innovation**: a completely new technology or way of doing things, such as mobile money compared with cash.
- **Disruptive innovation**: a simpler, cheaper product that starts in a small or neglected market and then replaces established products, such as smartphone cameras replacing many standalone cameras.

It can also be classified by **what** changes: a new **product**, a new **process** (a better way of producing or delivering), or a new **business model** (a new way of earning money, such as subscriptions instead of one-off sales).

## Design thinking

Design thinking is a **human-centred** approach to innovation: start with the people who have the problem, not with the technology. It usually has five stages, which are repeated as often as needed:

| Stage | What happens |
|:--|:--|
| 1. Empathise | Observe and talk to users to understand their needs, feelings and difficulties |
| 2. Define | State the real problem clearly from the user's point of view |
| 3. Ideate | Brainstorm many possible solutions without judging them too early |
| 4. Prototype | Build quick, cheap versions of the best ideas (sketches, paper screens, simple models) |
| 5. Test | Let users try the prototypes, gather feedback, and improve or go back to an earlier stage |

:::worked-solution[Applying design thinking]
Problem area: learners at a rural school in Masvingo province struggle to revise because they have little internet data.

1. **Empathise:** interview learners and teachers; observe that most families share one phone and buy small data bundles.
2. **Define:** "Learners need revision material they can use offline on a shared, basic phone."
3. **Ideate:** ideas include printed booklets, an offline app, revision by SMS, audio lessons on a community radio station, and a school Wi-Fi hotspot with downloadable content.
4. **Prototype:** make paper sketches of an offline quiz app and a sample SMS quiz for one topic.
5. **Test:** ten learners try both for a week. The SMS quiz works on every phone but is limited; the app is preferred by those with smartphones. The team improves the app to download content only at the school hotspot, and repeats testing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing an incubator with a science park"}
An **incubator** supports **new, small** start-ups with mentoring and cheap space for a limited time. A **science park** provides premises for **established or growing** technology firms near research institutions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying innovation is the same as invention"}
An **invention** is a new idea or device. **Innovation** is turning it into something people actually use and that creates value.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "describe how an incubation centre supports technopreneurs", give **specific** forms of support: workspace, mentoring, training, networking, help with funding and legal issues.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The ecosystem is the network that supports technology businesses: academic institutions, R&D centres, incubation centres, science parks, investors, government and industry.
- Academic institutions supply skills, research and mentors; R&D centres create and test new technology; incubators nurture early start-ups; science parks house growing firms near research.
- Innovation turns ideas into value: incremental, radical or disruptive; product, process or business model.
- Design thinking: empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test, repeated as needed.
:::
