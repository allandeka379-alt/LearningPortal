## Introduction

Thandeka wants to open a small computer repair and printing shop near a college in Masvingo. She needs about USD 3,000 for a laptop, a printer, tools, stock and the first two months' rent, but she has only USD 800 saved. Before she borrows money, she needs to know whether enough students and local businesses will actually pay for her services, what they would pay, and whether the shop should be near the college gate or in the town centre.

Every technopreneur faces three questions: **Where will the money come from? Will people buy? Where should the business be?** ZIMSEC Form 4 asks you to outline sources of finance and funding, to identify ideal conditions for a business location and to conduct market research. Questions often ask you to recommend a source of finance, to design market research, or to evaluate a location.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Start-up capital"}
The money needed to begin a business: equipment, premises, stock, registration and the costs of running until income starts.
:::

:::definition{term="Debt finance"}
Money borrowed that must be repaid, usually with interest, such as a bank loan; the owner keeps full ownership.
:::

:::definition{term="Equity finance"}
Money provided in exchange for a share of ownership in the business, such as from an angel investor or venture capitalist; it does not have to be repaid, but profits and control are shared.
:::

:::definition{term="Market research"}
Collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market to make better business decisions.
:::

## Sources of finance and funding

| Source | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| Personal savings | The owner's own money | No interest; no loss of control | Usually limited; the owner risks their own money |
| Family and friends | Loans or gifts from people the owner knows | Flexible, often low or no interest | Can damage relationships if the business fails |
| Bank loan | Borrowed for a fixed period with interest | Owner keeps control; planned repayments | Interest; needs collateral and a business plan; repayments even when sales are low |
| Microfinance | Small loans from microfinance institutions to small businesses | Accessible to those without much collateral | Small amounts; often high interest |
| Grants and competitions | Money from government, NGOs, development partners or innovation competitions | Does not have to be repaid; no loss of control | Competitive; strict conditions and reporting |
| Angel investors | Wealthy individuals who invest in early start-ups for a share | Bring money plus advice and contacts | Owner gives up part of the business and profits |
| Venture capital | Firms that invest large sums in high-growth businesses for a share | Large amounts for rapid growth | Lose a significant share and some control; only for high-growth ideas |
| Crowdfunding | Many people each contribute small amounts online, often for rewards or pre-orders | Tests demand; builds early customers | Needs good publicity; the idea is public; platform fees |
| Trade credit and leasing | Suppliers let the business pay later; equipment is rented instead of bought | Spreads costs; keeps cash available | Must pay on time; leasing can cost more overall |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business once it is trading | No interest or loss of control | Not available at the start |

**Choosing a source:** consider how much is needed, how soon, the cost (interest or shares), the risk, whether the owner wants to keep control, and what the business can offer as security. Many start-ups combine sources, for example savings plus a small loan.

:::worked-solution[How long will a loan take to repay?]
Thandeka borrows USD 2,200 from a microfinance institution. With interest, she must repay USD 2,640 in equal monthly instalments over 12 months. Her shop is expected to make USD 400 profit a month before repayments.

1. Interest charged = 2,640 − 2,200 = USD 440.
2. Monthly instalment = 2,640 ÷ 12 = USD 220.
3. Profit left each month after repayment = 400 − 220 = USD 180.
4. The repayments are affordable if the profit forecast is realistic, but she has only USD 180 a month of margin if sales are lower than expected.
:::

## Market research

### Primary research (field research)

New information collected first-hand for the business's own purpose.

- **Questionnaires and surveys** (paper or online): many people, easy to analyse.
- **Interviews**: detailed views of a few potential customers.
- **Focus groups**: a small group discusses the product with a leader.
- **Observation**: counting passers-by, watching competitors' queues.
- **Test marketing / prototypes**: offering the product to a small group first.

*Advantages:* up to date, specific to the business, private to the owner. *Disadvantages:* time-consuming and can be costly; small samples may be unrepresentative.

### Secondary research (desk research)

Information that already exists, collected by others.

- Internet searches, competitors' websites and price lists
- Government statistics, such as population and census data
- Reports from industry bodies, newspapers and trade magazines

*Advantages:* quick and cheap. *Disadvantages:* may be out of date, not specific to the business and available to competitors too.

### Steps in conducting market research

1. Decide the **objective** (what you need to know).
2. Choose **primary and/or secondary** methods.
3. Choose a **sample** that represents the target customers.
4. **Collect** the data.
5. **Analyse** it (totals, percentages, charts) and **draw conclusions** for the product, price, place and promotion.

:::example{title="Analysing survey results"}
Thandeka surveys 80 college students. 52 say they would use a printing service near the college gate, 18 prefer the town centre and 10 would not use either. 60 say they would pay no more than 10 cents a page.

- Near the gate: 52 ÷ 80 = 65%. Town centre: 18 ÷ 80 = 22.5%.
- Conclusion: locate near the college gate and price printing at or below 10 cents a page.
- Limitation: only students were asked; she should also ask local businesses before deciding.
:::

## Choosing a business location

Ideal conditions for a technology business location:

- **Close to customers** (the target market) and easy for them to reach.
- **Reliable electricity** (or the ability to install solar or a generator) and **good internet**.
- **Affordable rent** and other costs.
- **Security**: low crime, safe premises for equipment.
- **Transport and access**: roads, parking, public transport.
- **Skilled labour** available nearby.
- **Suppliers** and support services close by.
- **Competition**: not too many similar businesses nearby (or, sometimes, a cluster that draws customers).
- **Legal**: zoning and council permits for that type of business.

Online businesses still need a location for staff, equipment and deliveries, but power, internet and costs matter more than passing trade.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying equity finance must be repaid"}
Equity is exchanged for **ownership**, not repaid. Loans (debt) are repaid with interest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling any internet search primary research"}
Using information that others have already published is **secondary** research. Primary research is collected first-hand.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When recommending finance, match the source to the business: a small start-up with no track record might use savings, family or microfinance; a fast-growing tech firm might seek angel or venture capital.
:::

:::exam-tip
For location questions, name the factor **and** explain why it matters for that particular business.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Finance, funding, market research and business location are examined by ZIMSEC only. Use "US dollars" or "USD" in your answers rather than a symbol.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Start-up capital can come from savings, family, bank loans, microfinance, grants, angel investors, venture capital, crowdfunding, trade credit and leasing, and later retained profit.
- Debt must be repaid with interest; equity gives away part of the ownership.
- Primary research (questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing) is specific and up to date; secondary research (internet, statistics, reports) is quick and cheap.
- Market research follows: objective, method, sample, collection, analysis and conclusions.
- Good locations are near customers, with reliable power and internet, affordable, secure, accessible, with skilled staff and suppliers nearby.
:::
