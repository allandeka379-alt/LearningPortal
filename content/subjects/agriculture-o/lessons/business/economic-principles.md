## Introduction

The price of tomatoes at a busy town market can fall sharply when many gardens harvest at the same time; a few months later, when few farmers have tomatoes, the price is high again. Chipo, near Mutare, has noticed this and plans her plantings so that she harvests when others do not. She is using two of the most important ideas in agricultural economics: **supply and demand set the price**, and **every choice has a cost**, the value of the best alternative given up.

ZIMSEC asks you to *explain* the opportunities and choices facing farmers and **opportunity cost**, to *describe* the laws of demand and supply, to *interpret* schedules and curves and to *describe* what determines market price. Cambridge asks you to explain supply and demand and the opportunities and choices facing farmers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost"}
The value of the next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a product that buyers are willing and able to buy at each possible price in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply"}
The quantity of a product that sellers are willing and able to offer for sale at each possible price in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium price"}
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, so the market clears.
:::

## Opportunities in the agriculture industry

Agriculture offers far more than farming:

- **Production**: crops, livestock, horticulture, poultry, fish farming, forestry and beekeeping.
- **Input supply**: selling seed, fertiliser, feed, chemicals and equipment.
- **Processing and value addition**: milling, oil pressing, canning, dairy products, abattoirs, leather.
- **Marketing and transport**: buying, grading, storing, transporting and exporting produce.
- **Services**: extension, veterinary services, research, teaching, finance and insurance, machinery hire and repair, agro-tourism.

## Factors that influence a farmer's choice of enterprise

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Natural (physical) | Rainfall and its reliability, temperature, soil type and fertility, topography, water for irrigation, pests and diseases of the area |
| Economic | Capital and credit available, expected prices and costs, access to markets and transport, labour available, machinery and buildings |
| Personal and social | Farmer's skills and interests, family food needs, culture and customs, land tenure |
| Government and institutions | Policies, subsidies or support schemes, regulations, contract farming and buyers' requirements |
| Risk | How likely the enterprise is to fail through drought, disease or price falls |

## Opportunity cost

Land, labour, capital and the farmer's time are limited. Using them for one thing means **not** using them for something else. The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the **best** alternative given up, not of all alternatives added together.

:::worked-solution[Opportunity cost of a hectare]
Nyasha can use one hectare for maize, with an expected gross margin of US\$700, or for sugar beans, with an expected gross margin of US\$950. (Assume these figures.)

1. If she grows **maize**, the best alternative given up is sugar beans, so the opportunity cost of maize is **US\$950**.
2. If she grows **sugar beans**, the opportunity cost is the maize gross margin given up, **US\$700**.
3. Because maize's opportunity cost (US\$950) is greater than what maize earns (US\$700), on these figures sugar beans are the better economic choice, unless other reasons (food needs, rotation, risk) outweigh the money.
:::

## The law of demand

> Other things being equal, the **higher** the price of a product, the **lower** the quantity demanded; the lower the price, the higher the quantity demanded.

At a higher price some buyers cannot afford as much and others switch to substitutes (for example rice or potatoes instead of maize meal). A **demand schedule** is a table of prices and quantities demanded; plotted on a graph with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, it gives a **demand curve** that slopes **downwards** from left to right.

Demand also changes (the whole curve shifts) when something other than price changes: buyers' incomes, population, tastes and fashions, the prices of substitutes and complements, the season and festivals.

## The law of supply

> Other things being equal, the **higher** the price of a product, the **greater** the quantity supplied; the lower the price, the smaller the quantity supplied.

A higher price makes production more profitable, so farmers produce and sell more, release produce from store, or new producers start. The **supply curve** slopes **upwards** from left to right.

Supply changes (shifts) with the weather and season, the size of the harvest, pests and diseases, input costs, technology, the number of producers, imports and government policy. Farm supply responds slowly: a crop planted now is harvested months later.

## How the market price is set

Where the two schedules meet, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: this is the **equilibrium price**.

:::worked-solution[Reading a demand and supply schedule]
The schedule for crates of tomatoes at a town market in one week (example figures):

| Price per crate (US\$) | Crates demanded | Crates supplied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 900 | 100 |
| 4 | 700 | 300 |
| 6 | 500 | 500 |
| 8 | 300 | 700 |
| 10 | 100 | 900 |

1. Demand falls and supply rises as price rises, as the laws predict.
2. At US\$6, demand $=$ supply $= 500$ crates: the **equilibrium price is US\$6** and 500 crates are sold.
3. At US\$8, supply (700) exceeds demand (300): a **surplus** of $700 - 300 = 400$ crates. Sellers cut prices to clear their stock, so the price falls towards US\$6.
4. At US\$4, demand (700) exceeds supply (300): a **shortage** of 400 crates. Buyers compete and the price rises towards US\$6.
:::

### Why farm prices change so much

- **Seasonal supply**: at harvest supply floods the market and prices fall; in the off-season prices rise.
- **Weather**: a drought cuts supply and raises prices; a bumper harvest lowers them.
- **Perishability**: tomatoes and milk must be sold quickly, so sellers accept low prices; grain can be stored and sold later.
- **Demand changes**: incomes, population, festivals and school terms.
- **Government**: controlled prices, import rules and support schemes.

:::example{title="Using supply and demand to plan"}
Chipo knows most farmers near her plant tomatoes with the first rains, so supply peaks and prices fall at the same time. With a small irrigated plot she plants so that her harvest comes when supply is low and the price is higher. Her higher price comes from **lower market supply** at that time, not from better tomatoes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding up all the alternatives"}
Opportunity cost is the value of the **single best** alternative given up, not the total of every alternative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a movement with a shift"}
A change in the product's own price moves along the curve; a change in income, weather or other factors shifts the whole curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying supply is the amount produced"}
Supply is the amount offered for sale at each price; a farmer may store or eat part of what is produced.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State the laws precisely"}
Include "other things being equal" and the direction for both price and quantity. When describing a curve in words, say which way it slopes and why.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Interpreting a schedule"}
Find the price where the two quantities are equal; calculate a surplus or shortage as the difference between quantity supplied and quantity demanded, and explain which way the price will move.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Agriculture offers jobs in production, inputs, processing, marketing, transport and services.
- Farmers' choices depend on natural, economic, personal and social, government and risk factors.
- Opportunity cost = value of the next best alternative given up.
- Law of demand: higher price, lower quantity demanded. Law of supply: higher price, higher quantity supplied.
- Equilibrium price: quantity demanded = quantity supplied; surpluses push prices down, shortages push them up.
- Farm prices swing with seasons, weather, perishability, demand and policy.
:::
