## Introduction

At the end of the season Tendai's school poultry club was asked three simple questions by the head: how many eggs did the hens lay, how much did the feed cost, and did the project make money? Nobody knew. The eggs had been sold, the feed bought and the money spent, but nothing had been written down. Without records a farmer cannot tell which enterprise pays, cannot plan, cannot prove ownership and cannot convince a bank or buyer.

ZIMSEC asks you to *describe* the importance of farm records and to *differentiate* physical from financial records; Cambridge expects record keeping as part of producing a crop. Records are also the evidence in your coursework project portfolio.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Farm record"}
A written (or electronic) account of events, quantities and money on a farm, kept at the time they happen.
:::

:::definition{term="Physical record"}
A record of quantities, events and activities (numbers, masses, dates, work done) rather than money.
:::

:::definition{term="Financial record"}
A record of money received and paid out, and of what is owed to or by the farm.
:::

:::definition{term="Inventory"}
A list of everything the farm owns (land, buildings, machinery, livestock, stores) with quantities and values on a given date.
:::

## Why records are important

- **Measure performance**: show yields, growth rates, egg production and the profit or loss of each enterprise.
- **Plan and budget**: past figures are the best guide to next season's inputs, costs and income.
- **Make decisions**: compare enterprises, varieties, feeds or methods and choose the better one.
- **Detect problems early**: falling egg numbers or rising deaths show disease or poor feeding.
- **Control theft and loss**: stock counts and receipts show whether anything is missing.
- **Obtain credit**: banks and lenders want proof that the farm is well run and can repay.
- **Meet legal and tax requirements**, and settle disputes over ownership, wages and sales.
- **Breeding and health**: livestock records show parentage, service dates, vaccinations and treatments.

## Physical records

| Record | What it contains | Example of use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Farm diary | Daily events: weather, rainfall, planting, spraying, visitors, problems | Recalling when a field was sprayed |
| Inventory | Assets with quantities and values at a date (often the start and end of the year) | Valuing the farm; spotting losses |
| Crop records | Field, crop, variety, planting date, seed rate, fertiliser and chemicals with dates and rates, yield | Comparing fields and varieties |
| Livestock records | Identity of each animal, birth date, parents, weights, services and births, vaccinations and treatments, deaths and sales | Culling poor breeders; disease control |
| Production records | Output over time: eggs per day, milk per cow, kg harvested | Detecting falling production |
| Labour records | Workers, days or hours worked, tasks, wages paid | Paying wages; costing each enterprise |

## Financial records

| Record | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Receipts | Proof of money received or paid, with date, item and amount |
| Invoices | Bills showing money owed for goods supplied |
| Cash book (cash analysis book) | Every amount of money received and paid out, by date, so the balance can be found at any time |
| Petty cash record | Small day-to-day payments |
| Inventory valuation | Value of stock at the start and end of the year, used in a profit and loss account |

### Balancing a cash book

The **closing balance** is the money the farm should have at the end of a period:

$$\text{closing balance} = \text{opening balance} + \text{receipts} - \text{payments}$$

:::worked-solution[Balancing the poultry club's cash book]
In the first week of March the club's cash book shows an opening balance of US\$120. It received US\$85 and US\$64 from egg sales, and paid US\$96 for layers' mash and US\$12 for a vaccine.

1. Total receipts $=$ US\$85 $+$ US\$64 $=$ US\$149.
2. Total payments $=$ US\$96 $+$ US\$12 $=$ US\$108.
3. Closing balance $=$ US\$120 $+$ US\$149 $-$ US\$108 $=$ **US\$161**.
4. The treasurer counts the cash box: if it holds less than US\$161, money is missing or a payment was not recorded.
:::

## Records for a school project

For a school project (for example a vegetable plot, broilers or rabbits) keep:

1. A **project plan** with the aim, area or number of animals, and a timetable.
2. A **diary** of every operation with dates (land preparation, planting, watering, spraying, vaccination).
3. **Input records**: seed, fertiliser, feed and chemicals used, with quantities.
4. **Production records**: yields, weights, eggs, deaths.
5. **Financial records**: receipts and a cash book.
6. A final **profit and loss account** and an evaluation of what went well and what to change.

:::example{title="Using records to find a problem"}
Chipo's production record shows the club's 40 hens laying about 32 eggs a day for three weeks, then 24, then 20. The diary shows that the feed supplier changed the week production began to fall, and the labour record shows a new pupil on duty who filled the water troughs only once a day.

- The records point to two likely causes: a poorer feed and a shortage of water.
- Without the diary and production record the fall might not have been noticed until the money ran short.
:::

## Paper or electronic records?

Most small farms and school projects keep records in exercise books or printed record sheets, which need no power and are easy to check. More farmers now also use a spreadsheet or a mobile phone application, which adds up totals automatically, draws graphs of production and can be backed up. Whatever the method, the rules are the same: one record for each purpose, entries made on the day, and a regular check (for example every week) that the cash in hand matches the cash book and that the animals counted match the livestock register. Electronic records must be backed up, because a lost or broken phone can wipe out a whole season's information.

## Qualities of good records

- **Accurate** and **written at the time**, not from memory later.
- **Simple** and **complete**, using the same units every time.
- **Kept safely** and up to date, with receipts filed in date order.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up physical and financial records"}
A record of 600 eggs collected is physical; a record of US\$85 received for eggs is financial.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the cash balance as profit"}
The cash book shows money in hand. Profit also depends on stock values and costs not yet paid (see the profit and loss account).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Differentiate"}

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Importance"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Records measure performance, guide planning and decisions, reveal problems and theft, support loans and meet legal needs.
- Physical records: diary, inventory, crop, livestock, production and labour records.
- Financial records: receipts, invoices, cash book, petty cash, inventory valuation.
- Closing balance = opening balance + receipts − payments.
- Good records are accurate, kept at the time, simple, complete and safe.
:::
