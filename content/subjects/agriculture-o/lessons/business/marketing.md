## Introduction

Rudo grows tomatoes on an irrigated plot near Marondera. At harvest she can sell them at the farm gate to a trader, take them to a big produce market in Harare, or sign a contract to supply a supermarket that demands graded, packed fruit every week. Each choice gives a different price, different costs and different risks. Growing a good crop is only half the job: the farmer is paid only when the produce is **marketed**.

**Agricultural marketing** covers all the activities that move produce from the farm to the final consumer, in the form, place and time the consumer wants. This concept is in the **ZIMSEC** syllabus: Form 2 (controlled and uncontrolled, formal and informal markets), Form 3 (functions of marketing and factors affecting it) and Form 4 (marketing legislation).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Agricultural marketing"}
All the activities involved in moving farm produce from the producer to the consumer, including assembling, grading, processing, packaging, storing, transporting, financing and selling.
:::

:::definition{term="Controlled market"}
A market in which the government, through law or a statutory body, controls the price, the buyer or the way produce is sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Uncontrolled (free) market"}
A market in which prices are set by supply and demand between buyers and sellers, without government price or buyer control.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal market"}
An organised market with registered buyers, written contracts or receipts, grading standards and official records, such as an auction floor, an abattoir or a grain depot.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal market"}
An unregistered market with cash deals and few written records, such as roadside stalls, village sales and street vendors.
:::

:::definition{term="Contract farming"}
An arrangement in which a buyer agrees in advance to buy a farmer's crop or livestock on agreed terms, often supplying inputs, credit and advice that are repaid from the sale.
:::

## Functions of marketing

| Function | What it involves | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assembling | Collecting small amounts from many farmers into large lots | A co-operative collects milk from smallholders at a cooling centre |
| Grading and standardisation | Sorting produce into classes of quality and size | Maize graded by moisture and damage; tomatoes by size and ripeness |
| Processing and value addition | Changing produce into a more useful or durable form | Maize milled into meal; milk made into yoghurt; groundnuts into peanut butter |
| Packaging | Putting produce into containers that protect it and attract buyers | Crates for tomatoes, egg trays, labelled bags |
| Storage | Keeping produce until it is needed, spreading supply over the year | Grain in silos; potatoes in cool stores |
| Transport | Moving produce from farms to markets and consumers | Trucks taking cattle to auction |
| Financing | Providing money and credit between production and sale | Contract companies advancing seed and fertiliser |
| Risk bearing | Carrying the risk of loss from spoilage, theft or price falls | Traders who buy before prices are known |
| Market information | Collecting and spreading prices and demand | Price lists, radio and mobile phone messages |
| Selling (and buying) | Finding buyers, agreeing price, transferring ownership | Auctions, contracts, supermarket orders |

## Factors affecting the marketing of farm produce

- **Perishability**: tomatoes, milk and leafy vegetables spoil quickly and need fast transport, cooling or processing.
- **Bulk and weight**: bulky, low-value produce (hay, pumpkins) is costly to transport far.
- **Seasonality**: most crops are harvested at one time, so prices fall at harvest and rise later; storage spreads supply.
- **Quality and grading**: higher grades earn higher prices.
- **Distance, roads and transport**: remote farms on poor roads pay more to reach markets.
- **Supply and demand**: a bumper harvest lowers prices; shortages raise them.
- **Market information**: farmers who know prices in different markets bargain better.
- **Storage and processing facilities**.
- **Government policy and legislation**: controlled prices, licensing, levies, import and export rules, movement permits for livestock.
- **Number of buyers**: with only one or two buyers, farmers have little bargaining power.

:::worked-solution[Farm gate or town market?]
Rudo has 60 crates of tomatoes. Assume a trader offers US\$4.00 per crate at the farm gate. In Harare she expects US\$7.00 per crate, but transport costs US\$1.20 per crate and the market fee is US\$0.30 per crate.

1. Net price in town $= 7.00 - 1.20 - 0.30 =$ US\$5.50 per crate.
2. Extra per crate $= 5.50 - 4.00 =$ US\$1.50.
3. Extra for 60 crates $= 1.50 \times 60 =$ US\$90.
4. She must also weigh up her time, the risk of damage on the road and of an over-supplied market that day.
:::

## Markets for major crops and livestock in Zimbabwe

| Produce | Formal markets (examples) | Informal markets (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maize and wheat | Grain Marketing Board (GMB) depots, millers, contract buyers | Local sales to neighbours, small traders and millers |
| Tobacco | Registered auction floors and contract sales, regulated by the Tobacco Industry and Marketing Board (TIMB) | (Tobacco must be sold through the registered system) |
| Cotton | Licensed cotton buyers and contract companies | — |
| Cattle, goats, sheep | Auctions, abattoirs and butcheries (with movement permits from veterinary services) | Village sales, ceremonies, private sales |
| Milk | Dairy processors through collection centres | Selling fresh or sour milk to neighbours |
| Vegetables and fruit | Supermarkets, hotels, processors, contract supply | Open produce markets, roadside stalls, vendors |
| Poultry and eggs | Processors, supermarkets, hotels | Live birds and eggs sold locally |

:::worked-solution[Is grading worth it?]
Assume a buyer pays US\$290 per tonne for grade A maize and US\$250 for grade B, or US\$260 per tonne for ungraded maize. Tatenda has 2 t: 1.6 t grade A and 0.4 t grade B after sorting.

1. Graded value $= 1.6 \times 290 + 0.4 \times 250 = 464 + 100 =$ US\$564.
2. Ungraded value $= 2 \times 260 =$ US\$520.
3. Grading earns US\$44 more, which must cover the labour of sorting.
:::

## Marketing legislation

Zimbabwe has used laws and **statutory marketing bodies** (boards set up by an Act of Parliament) to organise the marketing of strategic produce. In general terms:

- Statutory bodies such as the **GMB** buy, store and sell grain and hold strategic grain reserves for food security; the **TIMB** registers tobacco growers, buyers and auction floors and regulates tobacco sales.
- **Controlled marketing** has at times meant fixed (gazetted) producer prices and single-channel selling, where a crop could be sold legally only to the statutory body.
- During the 1990s many markets were **liberalised**: some boards became commercial companies and private buyers were allowed to compete. Since then the rules for some crops, especially maize and wheat, have changed more than once between controlled and open marketing. Always check the **current** rules with AGRITEX or the statutory body.
- **Contract farming** is now common for tobacco, cotton, some horticultural crops and seed crops, and tobacco contracts are registered with the TIMB; the rules aim to discourage side-marketing (selling a contracted crop to another buyer without repaying the inputs).
- Other laws control grading standards, food safety, weights and measures and the movement of livestock to prevent disease.

**Controlled marketing** — recognised benefits: guaranteed buyer and price at planting time, food security reserves, protection from exploitation by traders. Recognised problems: late payment, prices below what an open market might pay, transport to distant depots.

**Free marketing** — recognised benefits: competition, quick cash payment, choice of buyer. Recognised problems: prices rise and fall sharply, and dishonest buyers may cheat farmers on weight or price.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing controlled with formal"}
A **formal** market is organised and registered; a **controlled** market has government control of price or buyer. A supermarket is formal but not controlled.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing functions without explanation"}
"Storage" alone earns little; add why: "storage spreads supply through the year, so prices are steadier and the farmer can sell when prices rise".
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing figures"}
Never quote real prices or production figures in an answer unless the question gives them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain a function"}
Name it, describe it and give its benefit to the farmer or consumer, with a Zimbabwean example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Legislation questions"}
Write neutrally: name the type of body (statutory marketing board), its role, and the recognised advantages and problems of controlled and free marketing.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Marketing moves produce from farm to consumer: assembling, grading, processing, packaging, storage, transport, financing, risk bearing, information and selling.
- Factors: perishability, bulk, seasonality, quality, distance and roads, supply and demand, information, storage, government policy, number of buyers.
- Controlled markets have government price or buyer control; uncontrolled markets use supply and demand.
- Formal markets are registered and organised (GMB, auction floors, abattoirs, supermarkets); informal markets are cash sales at roadsides and villages.
- Statutory bodies (GMB, TIMB) and contract farming operate under law; rules have changed over time.
- Net price = selling price − marketing costs (transport, fees).
:::
