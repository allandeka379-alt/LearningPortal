## Introduction

Twenty-five smallholder dairy farmers near Marondera each produce only a few litres of milk a day. On their own, none of them can afford a cooling tank or a truck, and a processor will not send a tanker for one farmer's bucket. Together they form a co-operative: members deliver milk twice a day to a shared cooling centre, the co-operative sells the milk in bulk, buys dairy meal by the truckload at a discount, and shares any surplus among members. What no single farmer could do, the group can.

ZIMSEC asks you to explain the principles of co-operatives, how they are formed and the types of agricultural co-operatives (Form 1), and to explain their benefits and identify their problems (Form 2).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Co-operative"}
An independent association of people who join voluntarily to meet shared economic and social needs through a business that they jointly own and democratically control.
:::

:::definition{term="Member"}
A person who has joined the co-operative, usually by buying at least one share, and who uses its services and has one vote.
:::

:::definition{term="Surplus"}
The money left after the co-operative has paid all its costs in a year (the co-operative equivalent of profit).
:::

:::definition{term="Patronage refund (dividend on trade)"}
The part of the surplus returned to members in proportion to the business each did with the co-operative, for example the litres of milk delivered or the value of inputs bought.
:::

## The co-operative principles

Co-operatives around the world follow a set of agreed principles:

1. **Voluntary and open membership**: anyone who can use the services and accepts the duties may join or leave, without discrimination.
2. **Democratic member control**: members control the co-operative, usually on the basis of **one member, one vote**, whatever the number of shares held; they elect a management committee at a general meeting.
3. **Member economic participation**: members contribute capital (shares); interest on shares is limited; the surplus is used to develop the co-operative, set aside as reserves, or returned to members **in proportion to their trade** with it.
4. **Autonomy and independence**: the co-operative is controlled by its members, not by outside bodies, even when it receives help.
5. **Education, training and information** for members, committee and staff.
6. **Co-operation among co-operatives**: working with other co-operatives locally and nationally (unions and federations).
7. **Concern for the community**: working for the sustainable development of the local community.

:::keyfact{title="Co-operative or company?"}
In a company, votes and profit follow the **number of shares** held. In a co-operative, each member has **one vote**, and the surplus is shared mainly according to **use** of the co-operative, not money invested.
:::

## How a co-operative is formed

1. A group of people with a **common need** (for example marketing milk or buying inputs) meets to discuss the idea, often with advice from an extension or co-operative development officer.
2. They hold an **inaugural (first) meeting**, agree to form the co-operative and elect an interim committee.
3. The committee studies whether the business is **viable** (costs, income, number of members, capital needed).
4. Members draw up **by-laws** (the rules): name, aims, membership conditions, share value, how the committee is elected, meetings, how the surplus is shared.
5. The co-operative applies to the government **registrar of co-operatives** for registration under co-operative law. Registration gives it legal status: it can own property, open a bank account, borrow money, sign contracts and sue or be sued in its own name.
6. Members buy **shares** to raise capital, hold the first **annual general meeting (AGM)** and elect a management committee (chairperson, secretary, treasurer and others), which may employ a manager.
7. The co-operative keeps proper **records and accounts**, which are audited and presented to members at each AGM.

## Types of agricultural co-operatives

| Type | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Producer co-operative | Members produce together, sharing land, machinery or labour | A group running a shared irrigation scheme or poultry unit |
| Marketing co-operative | Assembles, grades, stores, processes and sells members' produce | A dairy co-operative with a milk collection centre |
| Supply (input) co-operative | Buys seed, fertiliser, feed and chemicals in bulk and sells them to members | A farmers' co-operative shop |
| Savings and credit co-operative (SACCO) | Collects members' savings and lends to members at fair interest | Loans for inputs repaid after harvest |
| Multipurpose co-operative | Combines several of these services | Supplies inputs, markets produce and gives credit |

## Benefits of co-operatives to members

- **Bulk buying**: inputs bought in large amounts at lower prices, with shared transport.
- **Better selling prices**: produce assembled into large lots gives bargaining power with buyers, and grading and processing add value.
- **Shared facilities**: tractors, cooling tanks, storage sheds, dip tanks and trucks too costly for one farmer.
- **Credit**: savings and credit co-operatives lend to members who cannot get bank loans; lenders trust a registered group.
- **Training and information**: on production, prices and record keeping.
- **Democratic control and fairness**: members decide policy; the surplus returns to those who use the co-operative.
- **Social benefits**: employment, community projects and unity among farmers.

:::worked-solution[Saving by bulk buying]
Assume dairy meal costs US\$32 a bag in town. The co-operative buys a truckload at US\$28 a bag, plus US\$1 a bag for transport. A member needs 40 bags.

1. Co-operative price $= 28 + 1 =$ US\$29 a bag.
2. Saving per bag $= 32 - 29 =$ US\$3.
3. Saving for 40 bags $= 3 \times 40 =$ US\$120.
:::

:::worked-solution[Sharing the surplus by patronage]
At the AGM the members of a dairy co-operative decide to return US\$4,000 of the surplus to members in proportion to milk delivered. During the year 80,000 L were delivered; Nyasha delivered 2,500 L.

1. Refund per litre $= 4\,000 \div 80\,000 =$ US\$0.05.
2. Nyasha's refund $= 2\,500 \times 0.05 =$ US\$125.
3. A member who delivered more milk receives more, even with the same number of shares.
:::

## Problems faced by co-operatives

| Problem | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Poor management and lack of business skills | Wrong decisions, losses, poor records |
| Shortage of capital | Cannot buy equipment or pay members promptly |
| Dishonesty or misuse of funds by officials | Members lose money and trust |
| Low member commitment; members side-sell when a trader offers more | The co-operative cannot meet contracts or recover loans |
| Late payment to members | Members leave or lose interest |
| Conflicts and poor communication among members | Meetings fail; decisions delayed |
| Outside interference in decisions | The co-operative loses its independence |
| Poor transport and storage | Produce spoils; costs rise |

Solutions include training members and committees, transparent audited accounts, regular meetings, prompt payment, enforcing the by-laws fairly, and building reserves.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Votes by shares"}
In a co-operative it is **one member, one vote**, not one vote per share.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sharing the surplus by shares"}
The surplus is returned mainly in proportion to each member's **trade** with the co-operative; interest on shares is limited.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing types without functions"}
Name the type and say what it does for members, with an example.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain a principle"}
State the principle and what it means in practice: "democratic control: each member has one vote at the AGM, whatever the number of shares".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Benefits and problems"}
For each benefit or problem, add its effect on the farmer ("bulk buying lowers input costs, raising the farmer's profit").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A co-operative is a voluntary, jointly owned, democratically controlled business meeting members' shared needs.
- Principles: open membership, one member one vote, limited interest on shares and surplus shared by trade, independence, education, co-operation among co-operatives, concern for community.
- Formation: common need, first meeting, viability study, by-laws, registration with the registrar, shares, AGM and committee.
- Types: producer, marketing, supply, savings and credit, multipurpose.
- Benefits: bulk buying, bargaining power, shared facilities, credit, training.
- Problems: poor management, little capital, dishonesty, low commitment and side-selling, late payment, conflict, interference.
:::
