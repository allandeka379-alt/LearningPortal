## Introduction

Long before national parks existed, Sipho's great-grandparents in Matabeleland protected wildlife through their beliefs: a family never killed the animal that was its totem, certain pools and hills were sacred, and hunting was controlled by the chief. Today the same aims are pursued with laws, rangers, community programmes and international agreements.

Wildlife faces serious threats: **poaching**, loss of **habitats**, and **conflict** with people whose crops and livestock it damages. ZIMSEC Forms 3 and 4 ask you to **explain** how cultural values and the principles of conservation and preservation affect resource management, **describe** the effects of poaching, **discuss** biodiversity and habitats, **discuss** human-wildlife conflict, **explain** wildlife legislation and **discuss** the roles of different organisations. These are often "discuss" questions needing balanced, developed answers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
The wise management and sustainable use of natural resources so that they continue to benefit people now and in the future.
:::

:::definition{term="Preservation"}
Protecting a resource from any use or disturbance, keeping it in its natural state.
:::

:::definition{term="Poaching"}
The illegal hunting, capturing, killing or collecting of wild animals or plants.
:::

:::definition{term="Biodiversity"}
The variety of life in an area, at three levels: genetic, species and ecosystem diversity.
:::

:::definition{term="Habitat"}
The natural home of an organism, which provides its food, water, shelter and breeding sites.
:::

:::definition{term="Human-wildlife conflict"}
Any interaction in which wildlife harms people's lives, crops, livestock or property, or people harm wildlife in response.
:::

## Indigenous knowledge systems

Traditional beliefs and rules protected natural resources in several ways:

- **Totems:** each clan has a totem animal (for example shumba, the lion; mbizi, the zebra; nzou, the elephant) that its members do not kill or eat, so many species were protected by many people.
- **Taboos:** rules such as not cutting certain fruit or sacred trees, not killing pregnant or young animals, and not fishing or drawing water carelessly at sacred pools.
- **Sacred places:** forests, hills and springs where cutting, hunting and farming were forbidden.
- **Control by chiefs:** hunting seasons and the sharing of meat were controlled, and offenders were fined.

These systems worked because the whole community respected them. They are weaker today because of changing beliefs, population growth and commercial demand for wildlife products, but they are still valued and can support modern conservation.

## Conservation and preservation

| Conservation | Preservation |
| --- | --- |
| Allows **controlled, sustainable use** | Allows **no consumptive use** |
| Examples: quota hunting, game ranching, harvesting thatch grass or mopane worms with rules, tourism | Examples: strict protection of rhinos, pangolins and cycads; sacred forests; core zones of parks |
| Benefits people now and in the future | Keeps species and places untouched for the future |
| Gives communities a reason to protect wildlife | Can cause resentment if people lose access with no benefit |

:::example{title="Conservation or preservation?"}
A ward allows households to harvest thatch grass from a vlei only after seeding, and sells hunting rights for a few buffalo each year under a quota: this is **conservation**. The same ward fences a small sacred forest where no one may cut trees or hunt: this is **preservation**. Both have a place: conservation where resources can renew themselves under control, preservation where a species or site is too rare or sensitive for any use.
:::

## Poaching

**Causes:** high prices for ivory, rhino horn and pangolin scales on illegal markets; demand for bushmeat; poverty and unemployment; revenge for crop and livestock losses; and weak enforcement or corruption.

**Effects:**

1. Populations of targeted species, especially rhinos and elephants, **decline** and may become locally extinct.
2. **Wire snares** kill and maim many non-target animals.
3. **Tourism and hunting income** falls, and with it jobs and community revenue.
4. Ecosystems lose their balance when key species disappear.
5. Rangers and poachers are injured or killed in confrontations.

**Control:** anti-poaching patrols and intelligence, strict laws and penalties, dehorning and moving rhinos to safer areas, removing snares, sharing wildlife income with communities so they report poachers, education, and international bans on trade.

## Biodiversity and habitats

| Level | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genetic diversity | Variation in genes within one species | Differences between herds of indigenous cattle or between populations of sable |
| Species diversity | The number and variety of species in an area | A park with many mammals, birds, reptiles and plants |
| Ecosystem diversity | The variety of habitats and ecosystems | Miombo and mopane woodland, grassland, vleis, riverine forest, kopjes, lakes and rivers |

Biodiversity matters because varied ecosystems are **more stable**, supply **food, medicines and materials**, keep genes useful for **breeding** crops and livestock, and attract **tourists**. It is threatened by **habitat loss** (clearing, settlement, mining), **poaching**, **veld fires**, **pollution**, **invasive species** and **climate change**.

## Human-wildlife conflict

Conflict increases when **people settle and farm close to parks** or across **wildlife corridors**, when **droughts** force animals to search for food and water, and when **populations of people and animals grow**. Its effects include crop and livestock losses, injuries and deaths, and retaliatory killing or poisoning of wildlife. It is managed through **land-use planning**, **fencing and kraals**, **problem-animal control** by the wildlife authority, **early-warning** systems, and **benefit-sharing** so that communities gain from living with wildlife.

## Wildlife legislation in Zimbabwe

- The **Parks and Wild Life Act** is the main wildlife law. It provides for the national parks and other protected areas, lists **specially protected species**, controls hunting, capture and trade through **permits**, and allows landholders and rural district councils to be given **appropriate authority** to manage wildlife on their land.
- The **Environmental Management Act** created the **Environmental Management Agency (EMA)**, which protects the environment, including controlling veld fires and environmental damage.
- The **Forest Act** controls the cutting and moving of timber and protects state forests.

## Roles of organisations

- **Government:** the **Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority** manages parks, carries out anti-poaching patrols and problem-animal control, conducts research and game counts, and issues permits and hunting quotas; the police and courts enforce the law.
- **Community programmes:** **CAMPFIRE** (Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources), started in the late 1980s, lets rural district councils and communities manage wildlife and earn income from safari hunting and tourism, which pays for local projects such as schools, clinics, boreholes and grinding mills.
- **Voluntary (non-governmental) organisations:** groups such as Wildlife and Environment Zimbabwe and international bodies such as WWF fund research, education, anti-poaching work and community projects.
- **International conventions:** **CITES** (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) controls or bans international trade in threatened species and their products, such as ivory and rhino horn. **Transfrontier conservation areas**, such as the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park (which includes Gonarezhou) and the Kavango-Zambezi area (which includes Hwange), let animals move freely across borders.

:::worked-solution[Sharing wildlife income in a fictional CAMPFIRE district]
A fictional district earns US\$48,000 from safari hunting in one year. It agrees to share it: 60% to the producer wards, 25% to council administration and 15% to wildlife management.

1. Producer wards $= 0.60 \times 48\,000 =$ US\$28,800.
2. Council administration $= 0.25 \times 48\,000 =$ US\$12,000.
3. Wildlife management $= 0.15 \times 48\,000 =$ US\$7,200.
4. Check: US\$28,800 + US\$12,000 + US\$7,200 = US\$48,000.
5. If four wards share equally, each gets US\$28,800 ÷ 4 = US\$7,200. A ward that spends half on a borehole and shares the other half among 300 households gives each household US\$3,600 ÷ 300 = US\$12.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating conservation and preservation as the same"}
Conservation **allows sustainable use**; preservation **allows no use**. Give an example of each.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying biodiversity means only the number of animals"}
Biodiversity covers **genes, species and ecosystems**, plants as well as animals, and variety rather than total numbers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying CITES bans hunting in Zimbabwe"}
CITES controls **international trade** in threatened species and their products; national laws control hunting.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Discuss** the role of CAMPFIRE" needs what it does (devolves management, earns income from hunting and tourism), its benefits (community projects, less poaching, tolerance of wildlife) and its problems (unequal sharing, conflict losses still high, dependence on hunting income), with a conclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "**explain** the effects of poaching", link each effect to wildlife, the ecosystem **and** people (tourism income, jobs, community revenue).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Totems, taboos, sacred places and chiefs' rules traditionally protected wildlife and plants.
- Conservation is sustainable, controlled use; preservation protects resources from any use.
- Poaching, driven by demand and poverty, reduces populations, kills non-target animals and cuts income; patrols, laws, benefit-sharing and trade bans control it.
- Biodiversity exists at genetic, species and ecosystem levels; habitat loss is its main threat.
- The Parks and Wild Life Act, ZimParks, EMA, CAMPFIRE, voluntary organisations, CITES and transfrontier parks all play roles in managing wildlife.
:::
