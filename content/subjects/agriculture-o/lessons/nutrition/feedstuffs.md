## Introduction

In April, Farai's family near Marondera has plenty of green grass for their cattle. By September the veld is dry, brown and short, and the cattle lose weight. Farmers who cut grass for **hay** or chop green maize for **silage** while there is plenty, and who buy or grow **concentrates** for animals that need them, keep their animals productive through the dry season.

A **feedstuff** is any material that can be eaten by livestock and supplies nutrients. Choosing feedstuffs is a balance between what the animal can digest, what it needs to produce and what the feed costs. ZIMSEC (Form 3) asks you to classify feedstuffs and identify feeds for each class of livestock; Cambridge asks you to describe feeding practices and nutritional requirements. Hay and silage making are favourite *describe* questions on both papers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Roughage"}
A bulky feedstuff that is high in fibre (commonly taken as more than about 18% crude fibre in the dry matter) and relatively low in digestible energy, such as grazing, hay, silage and crop residues.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentrate"}
A feedstuff that is low in fibre and rich in digestible energy or protein, fed in smaller amounts, such as grains, oilseed cakes and fishmeal.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy concentrate"}
A concentrate valued mainly for its starch, sugar or oil and containing less than about 20% crude protein, for example maize grain or molasses.
:::

:::definition{term="Protein concentrate"}
A concentrate containing more than about 20% crude protein, for example soya bean cake or fishmeal.
:::

:::definition{term="Compound feed"}
A ready-mixed feed made from several ingredients and balanced for one class of animal, for example broiler starter or dairy meal; a **straight** feed is a single ingredient such as maize meal.
:::

## Classifying feedstuffs

| Class | Examples | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Succulent roughage | Grazing, fodder crops, silage, root crops | High water content; palatable; green material supplies vitamin A |
| Dry roughage | Hay, maize stover, groundnut haulms, straw | High fibre, low water; crop residues are cheap but often low in protein |
| Energy concentrate | Maize, sorghum and millet grain, wheat bran, molasses | High in starch or sugar; low fibre |
| Protein concentrate (plant) | Soya bean cake, cottonseed cake, groundnut cake, sunflower cake | By-products of oil extraction; high protein |
| Protein concentrate (animal) | Fishmeal, blood meal | Very high quality protein; used mainly for pigs and poultry |
| Supplements and additives | Mineral and salt licks, vitamin premixes, bone meal, limestone grit | Small amounts correct a shortage of one nutrient |

**Legume hay** such as lucerne is a roughage, but it is much richer in protein and calcium than grass hay, so it can replace part of the protein concentrate for dairy cows and rabbits.

## Feedstuffs for each class of livestock

- **Ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats)** have a rumen full of microbes that ferment fibre. Most of their diet is roughage (grazing, hay, silage, stover); concentrates are added for high producers such as dairy cows and fattening steers. Goats also browse leaves and pods of shrubs and trees.
- **Pigs and poultry** are non-ruminants with simple stomachs. They digest fibre poorly, so they are fed mainly concentrates as compound meals or pellets matched to their stage (starter, grower, finisher; layers' mash). Cottonseed cake contains a substance (gossypol) that is harmful to pigs and poultry in large amounts, so it is used mostly for ruminants.
- **Rabbits** are herbivores that digest fibre in a large caecum. They eat grass, legume hay and vegetable leaves, with pellets for growth and lactation.
- **Donkeys** do well on grazing and hay, with a little grain when working hard.

:::worked-solution[Which protein cake gives cheaper protein?]
Assume soya bean cake costs US\$0.66 per kg and contains 44% crude protein, while sunflower cake costs US\$0.36 per kg and contains 30% crude protein.

1. Cost per kg of protein from soya cake $= 0.66 \div 0.44 =$ US\$1.50.
2. Cost per kg of protein from sunflower cake $= 0.36 \div 0.30 =$ US\$1.20.
3. Sunflower cake supplies protein more cheaply, although more of it must be fed (and it contains more fibre, so it suits ruminants better than broilers).
:::

## Making hay

Hay is green fodder dried to a low moisture content so that it does not rot or go mouldy in store.

1. **Choose the crop**: a leafy grass (for example Rhodes grass) or a legume (lucerne, cowpea).
2. **Cut at the right stage**: at about early flowering, when yield and quality are balanced. Earlier gives little bulk; later gives stemmy, fibrous hay with less protein.
3. **Dry quickly**: cut in a dry spell, leave in rows (windrows), turn once or twice so it dries evenly. Legume leaves shatter if turned when very dry, so handle legumes in the morning.
4. **Bale or stack** when the hay is dry (about 15-20% moisture): stems should snap rather than bend.
5. **Store** under a roof or covered stacks raised off the ground, away from buildings because damp hay can heat up and catch fire.

Good hay is green, leafy, sweet-smelling and free of mould and dust. Hay that is rained on loses nutrients and colour.

## Making silage

Silage is green fodder preserved by **fermentation without air**. Lactic acid bacteria turn sugars into lactic acid, which makes the silage acidic (about pH 4) and stops rotting.

1. **Crop**: maize or forage sorghum is cut when the grain is at about the dough stage, when it has enough sugar and dry matter.
2. **Chop** into short pieces so that it packs tightly.
3. **Fill** a pit, trench or bunker quickly, in layers.
4. **Compact** each layer (for example by driving a tractor over it) to squeeze out air.
5. **Seal** with plastic sheeting weighted with soil or old tyres so that air and rain cannot enter. Molasses may be added to grass to provide extra sugar.
6. **Open** when needed in the dry season (fermentation is complete after a few weeks) and remove a thin face each day, resealing the rest.

Well-made silage is yellow-green or olive, smells pleasantly sharp and is moist. Black, slimy, bad-smelling silage has had air or too much water and must not be fed.

:::worked-solution[How long will the silage last?]
A trench of silage measures 12 m long, 4 m wide and 2 m deep. Assume well-packed silage has a density of about 650 kg per m³ and that 20 cows each eat 25 kg a day.

1. Volume $= 12 \times 4 \times 2 = 96$ m³.
2. Mass of silage $= 96 \times 650 = 62\,400$ kg (62.4 t).
3. Daily use $= 20 \times 25 = 500$ kg.
4. Days of feeding $= 62\,400 \div 500 = 124.8$, so about **124 full days** (round down: the 125th day would be short).
:::

### Hay and silage compared

| Feature | Hay | Silage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it is preserved | Drying (low moisture) | Acid fermentation without air |
| Weather needed | Dry, sunny days | Can be made in less sunny weather |
| Storage | Dry shed or covered stack | Sealed pit, trench or bunker |
| Losses | Leaf shatter, rain damage | Spoilage if air enters |

:::example{title="Choosing a dry-season feed"}
A farmer near Chinhoyi with a tractor, a trench and a field of maize, but frequent cloudy days in March, should make **silage**: it does not need long sunny spells for drying. A smallholder in Gokwe without machinery could instead cut and dry grass and cowpea haulms as **hay** and store the maize stover under a roof.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all grain-based feeds protein feeds"}
Maize and sorghum grain are **energy** concentrates (below about 20% protein). Oilseed cakes and fishmeal are protein concentrates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Letting air into silage"}
Silage stays good only while sealed. Loose packing or a torn sheet lets air in and the silage rots.
:::

:::mistake{title="Feeding roughage-based diets to broilers"}
Poultry and pigs cannot use much fibre; they need concentrates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Classify with a reason"}
When asked to classify a feed, give the class and the reason: "groundnut cake: protein concentrate, because it is low in fibre and contains more than about 20% protein".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the process in order"}
For hay and silage, write numbered steps from cutting to storage and explain each step ("compact to exclude air so that the bacteria work anaerobically").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Roughages are bulky and high in fibre (grazing, hay, silage, stover); concentrates are low in fibre and rich in energy or protein.
- Energy concentrates: grains, bran, molasses. Protein concentrates (over about 20% CP): oilseed cakes, fishmeal.
- Compound feeds are balanced mixtures; straight feeds are single ingredients; supplements correct shortages.
- Ruminants live mainly on roughage; pigs and poultry need concentrates; rabbits digest fibre in the caecum.
- Hay: cut at early flowering, dry, bale, store dry. Silage: chop, pack, compact, seal; lactic acid preserves it.
- Compare protein feeds by cost per kg of protein.
:::
