## Introduction

At a school farm near Gweru, Chipo notices that the piglets in one litter are pale, breathe fast and grow slowly, while the weaners in the next pen fed a complete pig ration are lively and pink. Across the yard, a cow that calved two days ago is lying down and cannot rise. Both problems come from the same cause: the animals are not receiving the right **nutrients** in the right amounts.

A **nutrient** is a substance in food that an animal uses for energy, growth, repair, production or keeping the body working. Feed is the largest single cost on most livestock farms, so knowing what each nutrient does, where it comes from and what happens when it is missing lets a farmer feed animals well without wasting money. ZIMSEC (Form 2) asks you to name the nutrients, state their functions and sources and describe deficiency symptoms; Cambridge asks you to describe nutritional requirements and deficiency signs and to explain why animals need clean water.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nutrient"}
A chemical substance in food that the animal digests, absorbs and uses for energy, growth, repair, production or the regulation of body processes.
:::

:::definition{term="Deficiency disease"}
An illness or abnormal condition caused by too little of one nutrient in the diet over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Dry matter (DM)"}
What is left of a feed when all its water has been removed by drying. Nutrients are compared on a dry-matter basis because feeds differ greatly in water content.
:::

:::definition{term="Crude protein (CP)"}
The protein content of a feed estimated from its nitrogen content and given as a percentage of the feed.
:::

## The six classes of nutrients

| Nutrient | Main functions | Good sources in livestock feeds | Effect of a shortage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates (starch, sugars, fibre) | Energy for movement, body heat and production; fibre keeps the rumen and gut working | Maize and sorghum grain, molasses, wheat bran, hay, grazing | Loss of weight and condition, low milk yield, slow growth |
| Fats and oils | Concentrated energy (about 2.25 times as much as carbohydrate per kilogram); carry fat-soluble vitamins | Oilseeds and oilseed cakes (soya, sunflower, cotton), fishmeal | Usually shows as energy shortage; poor coat |
| Proteins | Growth of muscle and organs, repair of tissues, production of milk, eggs, wool and young; enzymes and antibodies | Soya bean cake, cottonseed cake, groundnut cake, sunflower cake, fishmeal, legume hay such as lucerne | Slow growth, poor fertility, low milk and egg yields, weakness |
| Minerals | Bones and teeth, eggshells, blood, nerves and muscles, enzymes | Bone meal, limestone, salt and mineral licks, green feeds | Specific deficiency diseases (table below) |
| Vitamins | Small amounts regulate body processes, vision, bone formation, resistance to disease | Green grazing, yellow maize, fish liver oil, sunlight (vitamin D), premixes | Specific deficiency diseases (table below) |
| Water | Makes up most of the body; transports nutrients and wastes, controls body temperature, needed for digestion and milk | Drinking water, water in fresh feed | Reduced feed intake, low production, dehydration and death within days |

Proteins are built from **amino acids**. Pigs and poultry must receive certain essential amino acids (for example lysine and methionine) in their food, so their protein must be of good quality. Ruminants (cattle, sheep and goats) are less fussy: microbes in the rumen build protein from simpler nitrogen compounds and also make the B vitamins and vitamin K.

## Minerals and vitamins: deficiency signs

| Nutrient | Function | Deficiency sign (example) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium | Bones, teeth, eggshells, milk, muscle contraction | Rickets (soft, bent bones) in young animals; thin or soft eggshells; milk fever (low blood calcium) in dairy cows soon after calving |
| Phosphorus | Bones and teeth, energy use in cells | Pica (chewing bones, wood or soil), poor fertility, weak bones |
| Iron | Part of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood | Anaemia: pale skin and gums, fast breathing, slow growth (common in piglets kept indoors) |
| Iodine | Making the thyroid hormone | Goitre (swollen neck), weak or hairless newborn animals |
| Salt (sodium chloride) | Body fluids, nerves, appetite | Licking soil and objects, poor appetite |
| Vitamin A | Vision, healthy skin and linings, resistance to infection | Night blindness, watery eyes, poor growth, poor fertility |
| Vitamin D | Absorption of calcium and phosphorus | Rickets in young animals; thin eggshells |
| Vitamin K | Clotting of the blood | Bleeding that does not stop |

:::example{title="Diagnosing the pale piglets"}
Chipo's piglets are housed on a concrete floor and drink only sow's milk. Sow's milk contains little iron and piglets are born with small iron reserves; outdoors they would get some iron from soil, but on concrete they cannot. Pale skin and fast breathing point to **iron-deficiency anaemia**. The usual prevention is an iron injection in the first days of life (given by a trained person) or, in small units, a clean sod of soil placed in the pen.
:::

## Simple food tests

These tests are done in the laboratory on a small sample of feed ground and mixed with water.

| Nutrient tested | Reagent and method | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starch | Add a few drops of iodine solution | Blue-black colour |
| Reducing sugar | Add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath | Green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate |
| Protein | Add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper sulfate) | Purple or violet colour |
| Fat or oil | Shake with ethanol, then pour into water | Milky white emulsion (or a translucent grease spot on paper) |

Wear eye protection when heating Benedict's solution and keep ethanol away from flames.

## Dry matter: comparing feeds fairly

Fresh grass may be more than three-quarters water, while hay or grain has very little. To compare them, feeds are dried and their nutrients are given per kilogram of dry matter.

:::worked-solution[Dry matter of fresh grass]
A 250 g sample of fresh grass is dried in an oven until its mass stops changing. The dry sample weighs 60 g.

1. Dry matter % $= \dfrac{60}{250} \times 100 = 24\%$.
2. Water lost $= 250 - 60 = 190$ g, so moisture content $= 100 - 24 = 76\%$.
3. A cow that eats 20 kg of this fresh grass takes in $20 \times 0.24 = 4.8$ kg of dry matter.
4. If the dry matter is 10% crude protein, she receives $4.8 \times 0.10 = 0.48$ kg of crude protein.
:::

## Water: the forgotten nutrient

An animal can survive for weeks with little food but only a few days without water. Water:

- makes up most of the body and of milk and eggs;
- dissolves and carries nutrients, hormones and wastes (in blood and urine);
- is needed for digestion, including the fermentation in the rumen;
- cools the body through sweating and panting.

Animals drink more in hot weather, when lactating and when eating dry feed. Water must be **clean** and **always available**: dirty troughs and stagnant dams spread diseases and worms, and animals drink less of foul-tasting water, so they eat less and produce less.

:::worked-solution[Planning drinking water for a herd]
A farmer near Masvingo has 40 cows. Assume each drinks about 50 L a day in the hot season.

1. Daily need $= 40 \times 50 = 2\,000$ L.
2. A full 5,000 L storage tank lasts $5\,000 \div 2\,000 = 2.5$ days, so it must be refilled at least every two days.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling fibre useless"}
Fibre is a carbohydrate. Ruminants digest much of it with the help of rumen microbes, and it keeps the rumen working normally.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up iron and calcium"}
Iron shortage causes anaemia (pale animals). Calcium and vitamin D shortage cause rickets and soft eggshells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing fresh and dry feeds directly"}
Compare nutrients on a dry-matter basis; fresh grass is mostly water.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State, describe, explain"}
*State* a function in a few words ("protein: growth and repair"). *Describe* a deficiency sign as you would see it on the animal ("pale gums, rapid breathing"). *Explain* links the sign to the function ("iron is needed for haemoglobin, so less oxygen reaches the tissues").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Food tests"}
For a food test give the reagent, what you do (heat for Benedict's) and the colour of a positive result.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Six nutrient classes: carbohydrates, fats and oils, proteins, minerals, vitamins and water.
- Carbohydrates and fats give energy; proteins build and repair tissues and make milk, eggs and young.
- Calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D build bones and eggshells; iron forms haemoglobin.
- Deficiencies: rickets, pica, anaemia, goitre, night blindness, milk fever.
- Tests: iodine (starch), Benedict's (reducing sugar), biuret (protein), ethanol emulsion (fat).
- Dry matter % = dry mass ÷ fresh mass × 100; compare feeds on a dry-matter basis.
- Clean water must always be available.
:::
