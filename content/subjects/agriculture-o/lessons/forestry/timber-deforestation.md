## Introduction

Ten years ago, Tendai's village near Gokwe was surrounded by thick miombo woodland. Today the nearest firewood is a two-hour walk away, the stream that used to flow until July is dry by May, and gullies cut across fields that were cleared on the slopes. At the same time, a timber company in the Eastern Highlands fells its pine plantation, replants it the next season, and sells treated poles and sawn planks across the country.

The difference is **management**. Trees are a renewable resource only if harvesting is matched by replanting and natural regrowth. ZIMSEC Form 3 objectives ask you to **describe** methods of harvesting and treating timber, **identify** markets, and **describe** the causes and effects of deforestation with **solutions**, including afforestation and reforestation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Clear felling"}
Cutting down all the trees in an area at one time, usually in a plantation that is then replanted.
:::

:::definition{term="Selective felling"}
Cutting only chosen trees (mature, of the right species or size, or marked by a forester) and leaving the rest of the forest standing.
:::

:::definition{term="Seasoning"}
Drying freshly cut timber slowly, in air or in a kiln, to reduce its moisture content so it is lighter, stronger, more stable and less likely to rot.
:::

:::definition{term="Wood preservative"}
A chemical applied to timber to protect it from decay fungi, termites and wood-boring insects.
:::

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The permanent removal of forest or woodland so that the land is used for something else or left bare.
:::

:::definition{term="Afforestation"}
Planting trees on land that has not carried forest before, or not for a very long time.
:::

:::definition{term="Reforestation"}
Replanting trees, or allowing natural regrowth, on land where forest has recently been cut or destroyed.
:::

## Harvesting trees

### Methods

- **Clear felling** suits **plantations** of pine and eucalyptus: all trees are cut, the land is cleared and replanted. It is efficient but exposes soil until the new trees grow.
- **Selective felling** suits **indigenous forests** such as the teak forests: only mature, marked trees are cut, so the forest keeps its cover and regenerates.
- **Coppicing** (cutting eucalyptus near the ground and letting it regrow) and **thinning** also yield poles and firewood.

### Felling safely

1. Check the tree for dead branches and decide the **direction of fall** (away from people, buildings, power lines).
2. Clear an **escape route** at an angle behind the direction of fall, and keep everyone at least **two tree-lengths** away.
3. Cut a **notch** (wedge) on the side facing the direction of fall.
4. Make the **back cut** on the opposite side, slightly above the notch, leaving a strip of wood (the hinge) that guides the fall.
5. Move away along the escape route as the tree falls.
6. **Delimb** (remove branches), **cross-cut** into logs, and **extract** the logs by tractor, oxen or cable to the roadside for transport to the sawmill.

Workers wear **helmets, eye and ear protection, gloves and safety boots**, keep tools sharp, and never fell trees alone.

## Treating timber

Untreated timber, especially sapwood, is attacked by **decay fungi** (rot) in damp conditions and by **termites and wood borers**. Treatment has two stages.

### Seasoning

Logs are sawn and the planks are **stacked off the ground with spacers** (stickers) between layers, under a roof, so air moves around them. **Kiln drying** is faster and more controlled. Seasoning reduces moisture, so the timber is lighter, stronger, shrinks less after use and is less likely to rot.

:::worked-solution[How dry is the plank?]
The **moisture content** of timber is usually given as a percentage of its oven-dry mass:
$$
\text{moisture content} = \frac{\text{mass of water}}{\text{oven-dry mass}} \times 100
$$
A freshly sawn plank has a mass of 12.6 kg. After air drying its mass is 9.8 kg. Its oven-dry mass would be 8.4 kg.

1. Water in the fresh plank $= 12.6 - 8.4 = 4.2\ \text{kg}$; moisture content $= \dfrac{4.2}{8.4} \times 100 = 50\%$.
2. Water after air drying $= 9.8 - 8.4 = 1.4\ \text{kg}$; moisture content $= \dfrac{1.4}{8.4} \times 100 \approx 16.7\%$.
3. Air drying removed $4.2 - 1.4 = 2.8\ \text{kg}$ of water, so the plank is now far less likely to rot or warp.
:::

### Preservatives

| Type | Examples (by group) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oil-based (tar oils) | Creosote | Strong protection for poles and fence posts in the ground; dark and strong-smelling |
| Water-borne salts | Copper-based salts | Used in pressure treatment of poles and building timber; leaves timber clean and paintable |
| Organic-solvent based | Preservatives dissolved in a light solvent | Penetrate dry timber well; used for joinery |

**Methods of application**, from least to most effective: **brushing or spraying**, **dipping**, **soaking (steeping)** and **pressure treatment** (vacuum-pressure impregnation in a sealed cylinder, which forces preservative deep into the wood). Traditionally, the buried ends of poles were **charred** in a fire to resist rot and termites.

**Safety:** preservatives are poisonous. Wear gloves, overalls and eye protection, work in the open air, store chemicals locked away, and never burn treated offcuts on a cooking fire.

## Marketing timber and keeping records

**Markets** include sawmills, furniture and joinery makers, the building industry, mines (pit props and timber), electricity and telephone pole buyers, fencing contractors, local buyers of poles and firewood, and export buyers. Small growers can sell through timber companies, co-operatives or directly to local users.

**Production records** help the grower plan and prove ownership: species and area planted, planting date, operations (weeding, pruning, thinning) and their costs, number and size of trees felled, volume of timber sold, buyers, prices and income.

## Deforestation

### Causes

1. **Clearing land for farming**, including new fields on slopes and in resettlement areas.
2. **Firewood and charcoal** for cooking, heating, brick-making and **curing tobacco**.
3. **Veld fires** that kill young trees and prevent regeneration.
4. **Settlement, roads, dams and mining**.
5. **Uncontrolled logging** and cutting of poles.
6. **Overgrazing and browsing**, which prevent seedlings from establishing.

### Effects

- **Soil erosion** and gullies, and **siltation** of rivers and dams.
- Less infiltration, so streams and springs **dry up** earlier in the season.
- **Loss of habitats and biodiversity**; wild fruits, medicines and honey disappear.
- **Fuelwood shortages**: people, often women and children, walk further for firewood.
- Less carbon dioxide absorbed, contributing to **climate change**; local temperatures rise where shade is lost.

### Solutions

- **Afforestation** and **reforestation**: woodlots, community planting and replanting after felling; National Tree Planting Day each December encourages this.
- **Alternative energy**: solar, biogas and fuel-efficient stoves; curing tobacco with wood from planted woodlots rather than natural woodland.
- **Laws and enforcement**: permits for cutting and moving wood, fines for illegal cutting and starting fires, enforced by bodies such as the Environmental Management Agency (EMA) and the Forestry Commission.
- **Community management** of woodlands, **agroforestry**, **fire guards**, and **education** about the value of trees.

:::worked-solution[Measuring woodland loss and planning replanting]
A ward's woodland fell from 1,500 ha to 1,050 ha over twenty years. The community plans to replant 30 ha at a spacing of 2.5 m × 2.5 m.

1. Loss $= 1\,500 - 1\,050 = 450\ \text{ha}$.
2. Percentage loss $= \dfrac{450}{1\,500} \times 100 = 30\%$.
3. Area per tree $= 2.5 \times 2.5 = 6.25\ \text{m}^2$; trees per hectare $= 10\,000 \div 6.25 = 1\,600$.
4. Seedlings for 30 ha $= 1\,600 \times 30 = 48\,000$, before any extra for beating up.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up afforestation and reforestation"}
**Re**forestation puts trees **back** where forest was recently lost; **af**forestation creates forest on land that did not have it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating timber while it is wet"}
Most preservatives penetrate poorly into wet wood. Timber is **seasoned first**, then treated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Clear felling indigenous forest"}
Indigenous forests regenerate slowly; they are harvested by **selective felling**, keeping the forest cover.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**describe** how timber is treated", give seasoning (stacking, air flow, cover) and then a preservative method with an example group. For "**explain** the effects of deforestation", link each effect to its cause: "trees removed → no roots to bind soil → erosion → dams silt up".
:::

:::exam-tip
"**Suggest** solutions" questions reward practical, local measures with a reason: woodlots for firewood, efficient stoves, fire guards, community by-laws, replanting after felling.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Plantations are usually clear felled and replanted; indigenous forests are selectively felled; felling follows a safe notch-and-back-cut procedure with protective clothing.
- Timber is seasoned (air or kiln drying) and then treated with oil-based, water-borne or solvent preservatives by brushing, dipping, soaking or pressure treatment.
- Timber is sold to sawmills, builders, furniture makers, mines and local users; records track planting, operations, sales and income.
- Deforestation is caused by land clearing, firewood and tobacco curing, fires, settlement, mining and overgrazing; it brings erosion, siltation, dry streams and loss of biodiversity.
- Solutions include afforestation, reforestation, woodlots, alternative energy, laws, community management and education.
:::
