## Introduction

Look around a rural home in Zimbabwe and you will find trees everywhere: firewood stacked by the kitchen, poles holding up the granary roof, a mukwa stool carved by a grandfather, mazhanje (wild loquat) fruit gathered from the hills, and a sacred grove where the family's ancestors are remembered. Further afield are pine plantations in the Eastern Highlands and teak forests on the Kalahari sands of Matabeleland.

**Forests** give food, fuel, building materials, income and medicines, and they protect soil, water and wildlife. ZIMSEC Form 1 and 2 objectives ask you to **explain** the importance of forests, **identify** the main forest areas, **name** indigenous and exotic trees and **distinguish** softwood from hardwood. Expect short-answer and "explain" questions in Paper 1 and Paper 2.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Forest"}
A large area of land covered mainly by trees, either natural (indigenous) or planted.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous tree"}
A tree that grows naturally in Zimbabwe and has not been brought in from another country, for example msasa, mopane and mukwa.
:::

:::definition{term="Exotic tree"}
A tree that has been brought into Zimbabwe from another country, for example pine, eucalyptus and wattle.
:::

:::definition{term="Softwood"}
Timber from coniferous trees (cone-bearing trees with needle-like or scale-like leaves), such as pines and cypresses.
:::

:::definition{term="Hardwood"}
Timber from broad-leaved flowering trees, whose seeds are enclosed in fruits, such as mukwa, Zambezi teak and eucalyptus.
:::

## Why forests are important

| Type of importance | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Social | Firewood for cooking and heating; poles and thatch for building; shade; places for recreation and education |
| Economic | Timber for furniture, construction, poles and pulp; jobs in sawmills and plantations; non-timber products (honey, mushrooms, fruits, medicines, mopane worms); tourism; income from exports |
| Cultural | Sacred forests and trees for rituals and ancestral worship; trees linked to totems and taboos; wood for carvings and traditional tools |
| Ecological | Roots hold soil and prevent erosion; forests protect water catchments and keep rivers flowing; they provide habitats for wildlife; they act as windbreaks; leaf litter recycles nutrients; trees absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen |

The ecological role matters greatly to farmers: forested catchments release water slowly into rivers and dams, and trees on slopes stop soil from washing into fields and reservoirs.

## The main forest areas of Zimbabwe

- **Eastern Highlands (Nyanga, Chimanimani, Penhalonga and Chipinge areas):** large **plantations** of exotic **pines, eucalyptus and wattle**, in natural region I with high rainfall and cool temperatures. Small patches of moist evergreen forest, such as **Chirinda Forest** near Chipinge, also survive here.
- **Matabeleland North (Kalahari sands):** natural **teak forests** with **Zambezi teak** and **mukwa**, many of them **gazetted state forests** managed by the Forestry Commission.
- **The central plateau:** **miombo woodland** dominated by **msasa** and **munhondo**, the familiar woodland of much of the highveld.
- **Hot, low-lying areas (the Zambezi valley and the Lowveld):** **mopane woodland**, with baobabs in the hottest areas.

### Who looks after the forests?

The **Forestry Commission** manages the gazetted state forests, controls the harvesting and movement of timber and promotes tree planting. Timber companies own and manage most of the large plantations in the Eastern Highlands. The **Environmental Management Agency (EMA)** works to prevent veld fires and other damage to woodland. In communal areas, **rural district councils** and **traditional leaders** control the cutting of trees, and many communities protect sacred groves themselves.

### Forests and the farm

On a farm, trees are not just a source of wood. A **woodlot** of eucalyptus near the homestead supplies poles for kraals, granaries and tobacco barns, so the family does not have to cut the natural woodland. **Fruit trees** such as mango, guava and indigenous muhacha add vitamins to the diet and can be sold. **Fodder trees** feed goats and cattle in the dry season, when grass is scarce. Trees along field edges act as **windbreaks**, and their fallen leaves add organic matter to the soil. Keeping some indigenous trees in grazing land gives shade for livestock and browse for goats.

These benefits explain why examiners expect you to see forests as part of the farming system, not as something separate from it.

## Indigenous and exotic trees

| Indigenous trees | Uses |
| --- | --- |
| Mukwa | High-quality hardwood for furniture and carvings |
| Zambezi teak | Durable hardwood: furniture, flooring, railway sleepers in the past |
| Msasa | Firewood, poles; dominant tree of miombo woodland |
| Mopane | Hard, termite-resistant poles and firewood; leaves feed mopane worms and browsing animals |
| Muhacha | Shade, edible fruit; often a meeting place in villages |
| Baobab | Fruit, bark fibre, water storage in the trunk; important in culture |

| Exotic trees | Uses |
| --- | --- |
| Pines | Softwood for building timber, boards, pulp and paper |
| Eucalyptus (gum trees) | Fast-growing poles, firewood, building timber; woodlots |
| Black wattle | Tannin from the bark; poles and firewood |
| Jacaranda | Ornamental shade tree in towns |

Exotic trees are popular in plantations because they **grow fast** and give **straight, uniform timber**. However, some (such as eucalyptus) use a lot of water, and some (such as wattle) can spread and become **invasive**.

:::example{title="Classifying trees on a school field trip"}
On a trip from Mutare to Nyanga, a class recorded the trees they saw.

| Tree | Indigenous or exotic | Softwood or hardwood |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pine in a plantation | Exotic | Softwood (conifer with needles and cones) |
| Eucalyptus woodlot | Exotic | Hardwood (broad-leaved flowering tree) |
| Msasa on a hillside | Indigenous | Hardwood |
| Wattle along a road | Exotic | Hardwood |

Notice that "exotic" does not mean "softwood": eucalyptus and wattle are exotic **hardwoods**.
:::

## Softwood and hardwood

| Feature | Softwood | Hardwood |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Conifers (cone-bearing trees) | Broad-leaved flowering trees |
| Leaves | Needle-like or scale-like, mostly evergreen | Broad, flat leaves; some deciduous |
| Seeds | Borne in cones, not enclosed in a fruit | Enclosed in a fruit |
| Wood structure | No vessels (pores); water moves through tracheids | Has vessels (pores) visible on a cut end |
| Growth rate | Usually fast | Often slower |
| Typical uses | Building timber, boards, boxes, pulp and paper | Furniture, flooring, carvings, durable poles |

The names describe the **type of tree**, not exactly how hard the wood is. Most hardwoods are denser and harder than most softwoods, but there are exceptions.

:::example{title="Identifying a timber sample"}
Rudo is given two planks. Plank A is light, pale and has no visible pores on the cut end; it came from a tree with needles and cones. Plank B is heavy and dark, with tiny pores visible on the cut end, and it came from a tree with broad leaves and pods.

- **Plank A** is a **softwood** (conifer, no vessels), probably pine: suitable for roof timbers or boxes.
- **Plank B** is a **hardwood** (broad-leaved, vessels present), for example mukwa: suitable for furniture.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking all exotic trees are softwoods"}
Eucalyptus and wattle are exotic but are **hardwoods**. The softwood-hardwood difference is about conifers versus broad-leaved trees.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only economic importance"}
Questions on the importance of forests often ask for social, cultural **and** ecological points as well. Cover each type asked for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying forests make rain"}
Write precisely: forests protect water **catchments**, reduce runoff and keep rivers flowing; do not claim that a woodlot makes it rain.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**explain** the ecological importance of forests", give the function and its effect: "tree roots bind the soil, preventing erosion on slopes". For "**name** two exotic trees", one word each is enough: pine, eucalyptus.
:::

:::exam-tip
A **comparison table** (feature, softwood, hardwood) is the clearest way to answer "distinguish between softwood and hardwood". Give at least as many features as there are marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Forests have social (fuel, building, shade), economic (timber, jobs, non-timber products), cultural (sacred sites, totems) and ecological (soil, water, wildlife, climate) importance.
- Main forest areas: exotic plantations in the Eastern Highlands; teak forests in Matabeleland North; miombo woodland on the plateau; mopane in hot, low-lying areas.
- Indigenous trees include mukwa, Zambezi teak, msasa, mopane, muhacha and baobab; exotic trees include pine, eucalyptus, wattle and jacaranda.
- Softwoods come from conifers (cones, needles, no vessels); hardwoods from broad-leaved flowering trees (fruits, broad leaves, vessels).
- Exotic does not mean softwood: eucalyptus and wattle are exotic hardwoods.
:::
