## Introduction

Rudo's cattle keep escaping from a new paddock near Gweru. The posts are good and the wire is new, but the corner posts lean inwards and the wires sag; a cow simply pushes through. The fault is not the materials but the **construction**: the corners were not anchored, so the wire could never be strained tight.

This lesson covers **how** a wire fence is built: the specifications, the order of work, the **straining posts and anchors** that hold the wire tight, maintenance, and the **calculations** of materials and cost that examiners love. ZIMSEC asks you to *explain* the role of anchors, *discuss* their advantages and disadvantages and *determine* the materials for a given perimeter; Cambridge asks you to *describe* fence construction.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Straining post"}
A strong, deeply set post at a corner, end, gate or change of direction, from which the fence wires are strained (tightened) and to which they are tied off.
:::

:::definition{term="Line post"}
An intermediate post between straining posts that holds the wires at the correct height.
:::

:::definition{term="Anchor (brace or stay)"}
A structure that supports a straining post against the pull of the tightened wires so that it does not lean or lift.
:::

:::definition{term="Span"}
The distance between two neighbouring posts in a fence.
:::

## Fence specifications

A specification states the height, the number and spacing of strands, and the spacing of posts and droppers. Specifications depend on the animals and on local recommendations; the table gives **examples** only.

| Fence (example) | Height | Strands | Posts and droppers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cattle boundary | About 1.2-1.4 m | 4-5 strands of barbed wire, closer together near the ground | Line posts about 10 m apart, with 2-3 droppers in each span |
| Sheep and goats | About 1.0-1.2 m | Woven netting with 1-2 strands of barbed or plain wire above | Posts closer together, about 3-5 m |
| Internal electric paddock fence | About 0.8-1.0 m | 1-2 plain wires on insulators | Light posts or standards about 10-15 m apart |

Straining posts go at **every corner, end and gate**, at sharp **changes of direction or slope**, and at intervals along long straight runs, because wire cannot be strained evenly over a very long distance.

## Constructing a wire fence

1. **Plan and mark** the fence line with pegs and a string line; decide where gates and corners will be.
2. **Clear** a strip along the line of bushes, stumps and long grass.
3. **Set the straining posts** first, at corners, ends and gates. Dig deep holes, stand the posts upright, ram the soil firmly in thin layers (or set them in concrete) and **build the anchors**.
4. **Run a guide wire** tightly between the straining posts to keep the line posts straight.
5. **Set the line posts** at the specified spacing along the guide wire, firmly rammed and upright.
6. **Run out the wire** by unrolling the coil (pulling wire off a stationary coil makes kinks). Attach one end to a straining post.
7. **Strain the wire** with a **wire strainer** to the correct tension, then **tie it off** on the next straining post. Do not over-strain, or the wire may snap and anchors may move.
8. **Fix the wires** to the line posts at the specified heights with staples or tie wire.
9. **Fit droppers** evenly between posts to keep the wires spaced.
10. **Hang the gates** on the straining posts and check the whole fence.

Wear **gloves and eye protection** when straining wire: a wire that slips or snaps can whip back with great force.

## Anchors

The tight wires pull every straining post towards the next one. Without an anchor the post leans, the wires slacken and stock push through.

| Anchor | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Box (H) brace | A second post set about 2-3 m from the straining post, joined by a horizontal rail at the top; a diagonal wire is twisted tight between the base of one and the top of the other | Very strong; suits main corners and gates | Uses extra posts, wire and labour; costly |
| Diagonal stay (strut) | A stout pole runs from high on the straining post down to a block or stone set in the ground on the same side as the fence wires, so it pushes back against their pull | Simple; uses one pole | Can push the post upwards if badly set; may rot at ground level |
| Buried anchor ("dead man") | A heavy stone, log or block buried in the ground behind the post, tied to the post with strong wire | Cheap; little above ground to get in the way | Wire can rust underground; holds poorly in loose or wet soil; hard to inspect |
| Concrete footing | The straining post is set in a block of concrete | Firm and long-lasting | Costly; awkward on remote sites |

## Maintaining fences

- **Patrol** fences regularly, especially after storms and before animals are moved.
- **Re-strain** slack wires and repair broken wires with a proper join.
- **Replace** rotten, broken or termite-damaged posts and droppers; check anchors.
- **Clear vegetation** along the line, which also forms a firebreak and stops electric fences shorting out.
- **Repair gates**, hinges and latches; close gaps where animals have dug under.

## Calculating materials

On a **closed** fence (a loop), the number of posts equals the number of spans:

$$\text{posts} = \frac{\text{perimeter}}{\text{post spacing}}$$

Wire $=$ perimeter $\times$ number of strands; rolls are rounded **up**; droppers $=$ spans $\times$ droppers per span.

:::worked-solution[Materials for a paddock fence]
A rectangular paddock is $200\ \text{m}$ long and $150\ \text{m}$ wide. The fence has 4 strands of barbed wire, posts every $10\ \text{m}$ and 3 droppers in each span. Barbed wire is sold in $500\ \text{m}$ rolls.

1. Perimeter $= 2 \times (200 + 150) = 700\ \text{m}$.
2. Posts (closed loop) $= 700 \div 10 = 70$ posts, including the four corner straining posts.
3. Wire $= 700 \times 4 = 2\,800\ \text{m}$.
4. Rolls $= 2\,800 \div 500 = 5.6$, so **6 rolls** (round up).
5. Droppers $= 70 \text{ spans} \times 3 = 210$ droppers.
:::

![Plan of the 200 m by 150 m paddock fence with a post every 10 m: 70 posts in a closed loop with four corner straining posts, 2,800 m of wire (700 m times 4 strands, which is 5.6 rolls so 6 are bought), 210 droppers and a materials cost of US\$878.](media:diagrams/paddock-fence-plan-70-posts.png "Figure: the 70 posts of a closed paddock fence, and the quantities and cost worked out in the text")

:::worked-solution[Cost of the paddock fence]
Assume posts cost US\$5.00 each, a roll of barbed wire US\$60.00 and a dropper US\$0.80.

1. Posts: 70 × US\$5.00 = US\$350.
2. Wire: 6 × US\$60.00 = US\$360.
3. Droppers: 210 × US\$0.80 = US\$168.
4. Total cost of materials = US\$350 + US\$360 + US\$168 = **US\$878**, before staples, anchors, gates and labour.
:::

For a fence that is **not closed** (a straight boundary), there is one more post than spans: a 100 m straight fence with posts every 10 m has 10 spans and **11** posts.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Setting line posts before the straining posts"}
Straining posts and anchors come first; the line posts are lined up on a guide wire between them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding one post to a closed fence"}
On a closed loop the last span ends at the first post, so posts = spans. Only an open, straight fence needs one extra.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rounding rolls of wire down"}
5.6 rolls means buying 6. A part roll cannot finish the fence.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the role of anchors"}
Say what force acts and what the anchor prevents: "the strained wires pull the corner post towards the fence line; the anchor resists this pull so the post does not lean and the wires stay tight".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Materials questions"}
Lay out perimeter, posts, wire, rolls, droppers and cost on separate lines with units, and state your rounding. Read whether the fence is closed or open.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Specifications give height, strands and spacing of posts and droppers, chosen for the stock to be kept.
- Build in order: mark, clear, set and anchor straining posts, guide wire, line posts, run out and strain wire, fix to posts, droppers, gates.
- Anchors (box brace, diagonal stay, buried anchor, concrete) stop straining posts leaning under the wire's pull.
- Maintain fences by patrolling, re-straining, replacing posts and clearing vegetation.
- Closed fence: posts = perimeter ÷ spacing; wire = perimeter × strands; round rolls up; droppers = spans × droppers per span.
:::
