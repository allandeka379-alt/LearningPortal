## Introduction

On a farm near Marondera, a broken fence can cost more than the fence itself. Cattle that stray into a neighbour's maize cause a dispute and a bill; goats that get into the vegetable garden can strip it in an hour; and without paddock fences the farmer cannot rest grazing land or keep the bull away from young heifers. Good fences make good farming, and good neighbours.

This lesson covers **why** farms are fenced, the **types** of fence and their uses, fencing **materials and tools**, and the **treatment** of posts. Building a fence, anchors and fencing calculations are in the next lesson. Both boards examine fencing: expect *state*, *describe* and *explain* questions, and comparisons of materials.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fence"}
A barrier of posts and wire, mesh, rails or living plants that controls the movement of animals and people and marks out land.
:::

:::definition{term="Live fence (hedge)"}
A row of closely planted shrubs or trees, often thorny, grown and trimmed to form a barrier.
:::

:::definition{term="Windbreak"}
One or more rows of trees or tall shrubs planted across the direction of the prevailing wind to reduce wind speed on the land behind it.
:::

:::definition{term="Wood preservative"}
A chemical that protects timber from fungal rot and insect attack such as termites.
:::

## Why farms are fenced

1. **Control livestock**: keep animals in, and keep them out of crops, gardens and roads.
2. **Paddocks for grazing management**: divide grazing so that pastures can be rested and grazed in rotation, which prevents overgrazing.
3. **Separate classes of stock**: keep bulls and rams away from young females, wean young animals and isolate sick animals.
4. **Disease control**: reduce contact with neighbouring herds and wildlife that may carry disease.
5. **Protect crops** from livestock and wild animals.
6. **Mark boundaries** and reduce disputes.
7. **Security**: reduce theft of stock and produce.
8. **Shelter**: hedges and windbreaks reduce wind damage and soil erosion by wind.

## Types of fence

| Type | Description | Main uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barbed wire | Twisted strands of galvanised wire carrying sharp barbs | Boundary and paddock fences for cattle; deters animals from pushing |
| Plain (smooth) wire | Single strands, often high-tensile | Paddock fences; electric fences; less injury to animals |
| Woven or mesh | Wires woven or welded into a mesh: diamond mesh, sheep and goat netting, hexagonal poultry netting | Small stock (sheep, goats, pigs), poultry runs, gardens, security around homesteads |
| Electric | Insulated wires carrying short, high-voltage pulses from an energiser; an animal touching a wire gets a sharp but harmless shock through the ground | Rotational and strip grazing, temporary paddocks, keeping wildlife out; cheap per kilometre |
| Live hedge | Thorny shrubs planted close together (for example kei apple) | Boundaries and homestead gardens where cash is short |
| Windbreak | Rows of tall trees or shrubs across the wind | Shelter for crops, orchards and livestock |

An electric fence works only if it is well **earthed** (metal rods driven into moist ground) and the wires do not touch vegetation, which leaks the current to the ground. It is a **psychological barrier**: animals learn to respect it. Use only an approved energiser, never connect a fence directly to the mains, and hang **warning signs** where people pass.

## Fencing materials

| Material | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wooden posts (gum poles, hardwood) | Cheap and often grown on the farm; easy to cut and staple wire to | Rot and termite damage unless treated; can burn |
| Steel standards (for example Y-section) | Long-lasting; termite- and fire-proof; quick to drive in | Costly; can rust or be stolen; harder to fix wire to without clips |
| Concrete posts | Very long-lasting; termite- and fire-proof | Heavy, costly, may crack; awkward to transport |
| Droppers (thin wooden or steel stays) | Keep wires evenly spaced between posts; cheaper than extra posts | Do not support the fence on their own (they do not reach the ground) |
| Galvanised wire | Zinc coating resists rust; long life | Costs more than ungalvanised wire |
| Mesh | Holds small animals; difficult to climb through | Expensive per metre; heavy to handle |
| Live hedges | Cheap, long-lasting, give shelter and wildlife habitat | Slow to establish; need trimming; gaps form; may harbour pests and compete with crops |

**Accessories** include staples, binding (tying) wire, insulators for electric fences, gates and gate hinges, and earth rods.

## Fencing tools

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Wire strainer (fence strainer) | Pulls wire tight before it is tied off |
| Fencing pliers | Cut, twist and grip wire; pull and drive staples |
| Wire cutters | Cut wire cleanly |
| Post-hole digger or auger, spade | Dig holes for posts |
| Crowbar | Loosen hard ground and stones; tamp soil round posts |
| Sledgehammer or post driver | Drive standards and posts into the ground |
| Measuring tape, pegs and line | Mark out a straight fence line and equal spacing |
| Gloves | Protect hands from barbs and wire ends |

## Treating wooden posts

Untreated gum poles in the ground can be destroyed by **termites** and **fungal rot** within a few years. Treatment makes posts last many times longer.

1. **Prepare** the posts: remove all **bark** (preservative cannot pass through it and insects shelter under it) and **season** (dry) them, because preservatives enter dry wood far better than wet wood.
2. **Apply a preservative** by one of these methods:
   - **Pressure treatment**: posts are placed in a closed cylinder and a water-borne preservative such as copper chrome arsenate (CCA) is forced deep into the wood under pressure. This is done in factories and gives the longest life.
   - **Hot-and-cold open-tank method**: posts are heated in an oil-type preservative such as creosote and then left to cool in it, which draws preservative into the wood.
   - **Butt treatment (soaking)**: the lower part of each post, which goes into the ground, stands in preservative for several days.
   - **Brushing or spraying**: the cheapest but least effective, because the preservative stays near the surface.
   - **Charring** the butt in a fire: a traditional method that gives some protection but much less than chemical treatment.
3. **Safety**: wear gloves and overalls, avoid skin contact and fumes, keep preservatives away from children, animals and water sources, and never burn offcuts of treated timber or use them for feed troughs or cooking.

:::worked-solution[Is treating posts worth it?]
Assume a treated gum pole costs US\$4.00 and lasts about 15 years, while an untreated pole costs US\$1.50 and lasts about 3 years.

1. Cost per year of a treated pole: US\$4.00 ÷ 15 = US\$0.27 (to the nearest cent).
2. Cost per year of an untreated pole: US\$1.50 ÷ 3 = US\$0.50.
3. Over 15 years, 100 treated poles cost 100 × US\$4 = US\$400, while untreated poles must be bought five times: 100 × US\$1.50 × 5 = US\$750, plus the labour of rebuilding the fence four times.
4. Treated posts are the cheaper choice in the long run.
:::

## Protecting metal

- **Galvanising**: coating steel wire and standards with zinc, which resists rust.
- **Painting** with a rust-inhibiting primer and paint.
- **Tar or bitumen** on the part of a steel post that goes into the ground.
- Repair damaged coatings and avoid leaving wire coils lying in wet grass.

:::example{title="Choosing fences for a mixed farm"}
Tendai keeps cattle, goats and chickens. For his outer boundary he uses five strands of barbed wire on treated gum poles and steel droppers, which holds cattle and is affordable over a long distance. Around the homestead garden, where goats are the problem, he uses goat netting topped with one strand of barbed wire. The chickens' run has hexagonal netting buried a little way into the ground to keep out dogs. Inside the grazing land he uses a portable electric fence to move the cattle onto fresh grass every few days.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating posts without removing the bark"}
Bark blocks the preservative and hides insects. Debark and season posts first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking droppers are posts"}
Droppers hang on the wires to keep them spaced; they do not reach into the ground and carry no load.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fences control livestock, allow paddock grazing, separate stock, control disease, protect crops, mark boundaries and improve security.
- Types: barbed wire, plain wire, mesh, electric, live hedges and windbreaks, each suited to particular animals and uses.
- Materials: wooden, steel and concrete posts, droppers, galvanised wire and mesh; tools include the wire strainer, pliers, post-hole digger and crowbar.
- Debark and season wooden posts, then treat by pressure, hot-and-cold tank, butt soaking or brushing; handle preservatives safely.
- Protect metal by galvanising, painting and tarring.
:::
