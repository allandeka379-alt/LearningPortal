## Introduction

Tatenda wants to start a broiler project near Bulawayo. His first questions are practical ones: what should the house be built of, how big must it be for the birds he plans to keep, which way should it face, and what will it cost? A house that is too hot, damp or crowded will lose birds to disease and heat stress, however good the chicks and feed are. A roof that must be replaced every few years may cost more in the end than a dearer one that lasts.

Both boards expect you to *outline* the properties and uses of building materials. ZIMSEC also asks you to *explain* the requirements of livestock housing, *draw or describe* plans of livestock buildings, *calculate* construction costs and *determine* the cost effectiveness of materials.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Durability"}
How long a material lasts in use before it must be repaired or replaced.
:::

:::definition{term="Ventilation"}
The movement of fresh air into a building and stale, warm, moist or polluted air out of it.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost effectiveness"}
The value obtained for the money spent, often compared as the cost per year of useful life.
:::

:::definition{term="Stocking density"}
The number of animals kept per unit of floor area, for example broilers per square metre.
:::

## Building materials

| Material | Properties | Typical uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wood (poles, timber) | Cheap, light, easy to work; rots, burns and is eaten by termites unless treated | Roof trusses, rafters, poles, doors, pens, simple sheds |
| Concrete blocks | Strong, durable, fire- and termite-proof; cement is costly; heavy | Walls of piggeries, dairies, stores |
| Burnt (fired) bricks | Strong, durable, fire-proof, good at evening out temperature; firing uses much fuel wood | Walls of houses, poultry houses, stores |
| Stone | Very strong and durable, often free on the farm; heavy, needs skill and labour | Foundations, walls, kraals, dip tanks |
| Metal (steel frames, corrugated iron sheets) | Strong, quick to erect, fire- and termite-proof; hot in sun and noisy in rain; rusts unless galvanised or painted; costly | Roofs, frames, gates, feed and water equipment |
| Earth (pole and dagga, sun-dried bricks, rammed earth) | Very cheap and local; good insulation; weak, erodes in rain, attacked by termites; needs regular maintenance | Walls of simple shelters and granaries in dry areas |
| Thatch (grass) | Cheap, local, cool under the sun; burns easily, short life, harbours pests | Roofs of small poultry houses, shelters and traditional buildings |
| Concrete (cast) | Very strong, hard-wearing and easy to clean | Floors, foundations, troughs, drains, footbaths |

Choose materials for **durability, strength, resistance to fire, termites and weather, temperature control, cost and local availability**.

## Requirements of good livestock housing

1. **Ventilation**: removes heat, moisture, ammonia and dust. Poor ventilation causes respiratory diseases and heat stress. Open sides covered with wire mesh and curtains, ridge vents and windows provide it without draughts at animal level.
2. **Drainage**: a raised, well-drained site and floors that slope to drains keep the house dry; wet floors cause foot problems, parasites and disease.
3. **Space**: enough floor, feeder and drinker space for the number and size of animals; overcrowding causes stress, fighting, poor growth and disease.
4. **Light**: enough natural light for animals and workers, but walls protected from direct sun. In Zimbabwe a long, narrow house is usually built with its **long axis running east to west**, so the sun shines on the short ends rather than the long walls.
5. **Hygiene**: smooth, washable floors and walls, a footbath at the entrance, easy removal of manure and easy cleaning between batches.
6. **Security**: strong doors, locks and mesh to keep out thieves, predators (dogs, snakes, wild cats) and rodents.
7. **Protection**: roofs with overhangs and walls that keep out rain and cold wind, and temperature control for young animals.
8. **Convenience**: near water and feed stores, with good access for vehicles; downwind and at a distance from the homestead.

## Plans of livestock buildings

A plan is a scaled drawing seen from above; in an examination you may also be asked to describe one. A plan shows the outside dimensions, walls, doors, windows or open sides, internal divisions (pens, sleeping and dunging areas), feeders and drinkers, drains and the direction of north. The table describes a simple plan of a farrowing pen for a sow.

| Area (from the back wall to the front) | Feature | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Back of pen | Covered sleeping area with a creep corner for piglets, heated in cold weather | Warm, dry resting place; piglets kept away from the sow |
| Sides of sleeping area | Guard rails a little above the floor | Stop the sow lying on piglets |
| Middle | Concrete floor sloping to the front | Drains urine and washing water |
| Front | Open dunging area, feeding trough and drinker | Keeps the sleeping area clean; easy feeding |
| Front wall | Drain outside the pen, gate | Removes waste; access for the stockman |

:::worked-solution[Blocks and cost for a wall]
A wall of a pig house is $8\ \text{m}$ long and $2.4\ \text{m}$ high, with a door opening $2\ \text{m}$ high and $0.9\ \text{m}$ wide. Assume $12.5$ concrete blocks are needed per square metre and each block costs US\$0.80.

1. Area of the wall $= 8 \times 2.4 = 19.2\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Area of the door $= 2 \times 0.9 = 1.8\ \text{m}^2$.
3. Area to be built $= 19.2 - 1.8 = 17.4\ \text{m}^2$.
4. Blocks $= 17.4 \times 12.5 = 217.5$, so **218 blocks** (round up).
5. Cost: 218 × US\$0.80 = US\$174.40, before cement, sand and labour.
:::

:::worked-solution[Which roof is more cost-effective?]
Assume a thatch roof for a shed costs US\$300 and lasts about 5 years, while a corrugated iron roof costs US\$900 and lasts about 25 years.

1. Thatch: US\$300 ÷ 5 = US\$60 per year.
2. Corrugated iron: US\$900 ÷ 25 = US\$36 per year.
3. The iron roof costs three times as much to build but is more **cost-effective**: it costs less per year of use and needs less maintenance. Thatch remains useful where cash is short or a cool roof matters, and an iron roof can be insulated underneath.
:::

:::example{title="Planning a broiler house"}
Tatenda's house will be $10\ \text{m}$ by $6\ \text{m}$, a floor area of $60\ \text{m}^2$. Assuming a stocking density of about 10 broilers per square metre at slaughter weight, he can keep about $60 \times 10 = 600$ birds per batch. He orients the long axis east to west, builds low brick walls with wire mesh above and curtains for cold nights, puts a concrete floor under deep litter, slopes the floor to a drain for washing between batches and places a footbath at the door.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing the cheapest material to build"}
Compare the **cost per year of life** and the maintenance. The cheapest material to buy is often the most expensive over time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting openings when counting blocks"}
Subtract the area of doors and windows from the wall area before multiplying by blocks per square metre, and round **up**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Closing a house up tightly to keep animals warm"}
Warmth without ventilation traps moisture and ammonia and causes respiratory disease. Provide ventilation above the animals and avoid draughts at their level.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Properties and uses"}
Give a property and link it to a use: "concrete is hard-wearing and easy to clean, so it is used for floors of piggeries".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Housing requirements"}
State the requirement and explain the benefit: "good ventilation removes ammonia and moisture, reducing respiratory disease". Use the animal in the question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Materials differ in durability, strength, fire, termite and weather resistance, temperature control and cost: wood, blocks, bricks, stone, metal, earth, thatch and concrete.
- Good housing gives ventilation, drainage, space, light, hygiene, security, protection and convenience.
- Long, narrow houses in Zimbabwe usually run east to west to keep the sun off the long walls.
- Plans show dimensions, walls, openings, pens, equipment, drains and north.
- Blocks = (wall area − openings) × blocks per m², rounded up; compare materials by cost per year of life.
:::
