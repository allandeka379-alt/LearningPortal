## Introduction

Rudo's maize near Masvingo looked fine in December, yet by February many plants were short and yellow, as if short of water, although the rains had been good. At the base of the plants were small plants with bright red flowers: **witchweed**, which had been feeding on the maize roots for weeks before it even appeared above the ground. In another corner of the field couch grass had spread from a few pieces dragged in by the plough.

A **weed** is a plant growing where it is not wanted. Weeds cause more yield loss on many small farms than pests or diseases, and their control takes much of the labour of a growing season. Examiners ask you to *discuss* the harmful and beneficial effects of weeds, *classify* them, *name* local weeds and *describe* how they spread. Weed control is covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weed"}
Any plant growing where it is not wanted, especially one that competes with a crop or harms livestock or people.
:::

:::definition{term="Annual weed"}
A weed that completes its life cycle (germination, growth, flowering, seed) in one season and survives the dry season as seed.
:::

:::definition{term="Perennial weed"}
A weed that lives for more than two years, surviving the dry season by underground organs such as rhizomes or tubers, from which it regrows.
:::

:::definition{term="Parasitic weed"}
A weed that attaches to another plant and takes water and nutrients from it.
:::

:::definition{term="Seed dormancy"}
A resting state in which seeds stay alive in the soil but do not germinate, sometimes for many years.
:::

## Harmful effects of weeds

- **Competition** with the crop for water, nutrients (especially nitrogen), light and space, which lowers yield; competition is most damaging in the first weeks after the crop emerges.
- **Parasitism**: witchweed takes water and nutrients directly from crop roots.
- **Hosts of pests and diseases**: wild grasses can harbour the leafhoppers and virus of maize streak; nightshades and other weeds of the tomato family harbour pests and diseases of tomatoes and potatoes.
- **Lower quality**: weed seeds contaminate grain and seed lots; burrs and barbed seeds spoil wool; the seeds of thorn apple (*Datura*), a poisonous weed, can contaminate harvested grain.
- **Poisoning** of livestock that graze some weeds.
- **Higher costs** and more labour for weeding; weeds also hinder harvesting and block irrigation channels.
- Some weeds release chemicals that slow the growth of plants around them.

## Beneficial effects of weeds

- **Cover the soil** and reduce erosion where land would otherwise be bare.
- Add **organic matter** when slashed or ploughed in, or used as mulch or compost.
- Some are eaten as **vegetables** (young blackjack and pigweed leaves are eaten as relish in many homes) or used as **medicine**.
- **Fodder** for livestock, and nectar for bees.
- **Habitat** for natural enemies of pests.
- Some indicate soil conditions to an experienced farmer.

## Classifying weeds

### By life cycle

| Type | Life cycle | How it survives the dry season | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual | One season | Seed in the soil | Blackjack, pigweed, Mexican marigold, witchweed |
| Perennial | More than two years | Rhizomes, stolons, tubers or bulbs | Couch grass, nut grass, spear grass |

Annuals are controlled by stopping them from setting seed. Perennials regrow from underground parts, so cutting the tops alone does not kill them.

### By form

| Group | Leaves | Stem | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grasses | Long and narrow with parallel veins | Round, usually hollow, with nodes | Couch grass, spear grass |
| Sedges | Narrow, in three rows | Solid and triangular in cross-section | Nut grass |
| Broad-leaved | Wide with a network of veins | Varied | Blackjack, pigweed, Mexican marigold |

This grouping matters because many herbicides kill broad-leaved weeds but not grasses, or the other way round.

:::example{title="Naming the weed group from a description"}
Tatenda pulls up a weed with narrow leaves. He rolls the stem between his fingers and feels three sharp edges; the stem is solid when cut. Small round brown "nuts" are attached to the roots underground.

- Triangular solid stem and leaves in threes: a **sedge**, not a grass.
- Underground tubers: it survives as a **perennial** and regrows from the tubers.
- Conclusion: **nut grass**. Hoeing off the tops will not kill it; each tuber can sprout again.
:::

## Named local weeds

| Weed | Type | Main way of spreading | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Blackjack (*Bidens pilosa*) | Annual, broad-leaved | Seeds with barbed spines that stick to clothes and animal hair | Very many seeds; common in gardens and maize |
| Couch grass (*Cynodon dactylon*) | Perennial grass | Rhizomes underground and runners (stolons) on the surface; cut pieces regrow | Ploughing and hoeing spread the pieces |
| Nut grass (*Cyperus* species) | Perennial sedge | Underground tubers ("nuts") and rhizomes | One of the hardest weeds to eradicate |
| Witchweed (*Striga*) | Annual, parasitic | Huge numbers of dust-like seeds, carried by wind, water, soil, tools, animals and crop seed | Seeds stay dormant in soil for many years |

### Witchweed in more detail

Witchweed is a parasite of **maize, sorghum, millets** and wild grasses.

1. Its tiny seeds lie dormant in the soil until chemicals released by host roots nearby stimulate them to germinate.
2. The seedling attaches to a host root by a sucker (haustorium) and takes water and nutrients.
3. For several weeks it grows **underground**, while the crop becomes stunted, yellow and wilted, as if in drought.
4. It then emerges, flowers (often red in the common local species) and sets thousands of seeds, adding to the store in the soil.

The worst damage is done before the weed is seen, which is why witchweed is so serious on poor, sandy soils where cereals are grown year after year.

## How weeds spread

- **Seeds** carried by **wind** (light, hairy or winged seeds), **water** (runoff and irrigation), **animals** (hooked or barbed seeds on fur; seeds passing through the gut into manure) and **people** (clothes, boots, tools, vehicles).
- **Contaminated crop seed** and **poorly made compost or manure**.
- **Vegetative parts**: rhizomes, stolons and tubers, often cut and carried by ploughs, harrows and hoes.

Features that make weeds successful: many seeds, long seed dormancy, fast early growth, vegetative reproduction and efficient dispersal.

## Measuring weed density

A **quadrat** (a square frame, for example 1 m × 1 m) is thrown at random several times and the weeds inside are counted.

:::worked-solution[Weed density from quadrat counts]
Chipo throws a 1 m² quadrat five times in a bean field and counts 12, 7, 15, 9 and 11 weeds.

1. Total $= 12 + 7 + 15 + 9 + 11 = 54$ weeds.
2. Mean density $= 54 \div 5 = 10.8$ weeds per m².
3. Per hectare: $10.8 \times 10\,000 = 108\,000$ weeds per ha (1 ha $= 10\,000$ m²).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling nut grass a grass"}
Despite its name, nut grass is a **sedge**: triangular solid stem, leaves in three rows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Only listing harmful effects"}
"Discuss the effects of weeds" expects both harmful and beneficial effects.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying witchweed only harms the crop after it appears"}
Most of the damage is done while witchweed is still underground, attached to the crop roots.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Effects with reasons"}
For each effect say how it reduces yield or value: "weeds compete for nitrogen, so the maize leaves yellow and cobs are small".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Named weeds"}
Learn one annual and one perennial, one grass, one sedge and one broad-leaved weed, with how each spreads. Cambridge asks for one named local weed of a crop, its harmful effects and spread.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A weed is a plant growing where it is not wanted.
- Harm: competition, parasitism, hosts of pests and diseases, lower quality, poisoning, extra labour and cost.
- Benefits: soil cover, organic matter, food and medicine, fodder, habitat for natural enemies.
- Annuals survive as seed; perennials regrow from rhizomes, stolons or tubers.
- Grasses (round stems), sedges (triangular stems), broad-leaved (net veins).
- Blackjack (barbed seeds), couch grass (rhizomes, stolons), nut grass (tubers), witchweed (parasitic, dormant seeds).
:::
