## Introduction

In January Nyasha's tomatoes near Chinhoyi look healthy in the morning, yet by afternoon several plants have wilted, although the soil is moist. A week later they are dead. Her neighbour's tomatoes have dark spots with rings on the older leaves, and a maize field nearby has plants with broken yellow streaks along the leaves. All three are **diseases**, but one is caused by a bacterium, one by a fungus and one by a virus, and each needs a different response.

A **plant disease** is any harmful change in the normal working of a plant, usually caused by a micro-organism (a **pathogen**). ZIMSEC asks you to *classify* diseases by cause, *describe* symptoms and spread, and *describe* control and suitable chemicals; Cambridge asks you to describe the infection, harmful effects, prevention and control of one bacterial, one fungal and one viral disease.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pathogen"}
A micro-organism (fungus, bacterium or virus) that causes disease in a plant.
:::

:::definition{term="Symptom"}
A visible sign of disease in the plant, such as spots, wilting, mottling or rotting.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism, usually an insect, that carries a pathogen from a diseased plant to a healthy one.
:::

:::definition{term="Fungicide"}
A chemical that kills fungi or prevents them from infecting a plant.
:::

## Effects of diseases on crops

- Less **photosynthesis** where leaves are spotted, streaked or killed, so lower yield.
- **Wilting** and death when water-conducting tissue (xylem) is blocked.
- **Rotting** of roots, stems, fruits and stored produce.
- Lower **quality** and market value (spotted fruit, mouldy grain); some moulds make grain poisonous.
- Higher **costs** of control and, in bad cases, total crop loss.

## Classifying diseases by causal organism

| Group | Nature of the organism | General symptoms | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fungal | Threads (hyphae) that produce huge numbers of spores | Spots and lesions (often with rings), powdery or fluffy growth, orange rust pustules, rots, damping-off of seedlings | Grey leaf spot of maize, early blight of tomato, rusts |
| Bacterial | Single-celled organisms that multiply very fast in warm, wet conditions | Water-soaked spots, sudden wilting of green plants, slimy foul-smelling soft rots, bacterial ooze | Bacterial wilt of tomato and potato, black rot of cabbage |
| Viral | Extremely small particles that multiply only inside living cells | Mosaic (light and dark green mottling), yellow streaks, leaf curl and distortion, stunting | Maize streak virus, tobacco mosaic virus |

## How diseases enter and spread

**Entry**: fungal threads can grow straight through the leaf surface or enter through **stomata**; bacteria enter through **natural openings and wounds** (made by insects, hail, tools or pruning); viruses enter only through **wounds**, usually made by a feeding vector or by handling.

**Spread**:

- **Wind**: fungal spores (rusts, leaf spots) blow from field to field.
- **Water**: rain splash and irrigation water carry spores and bacteria; running water carries soil-borne bacteria.
- **Insect vectors**: leafhoppers carry maize streak virus; aphids carry many viruses.
- **Seed and planting material**: infected seed, seedlings and tubers carry pathogens to new farms.
- **Soil and crop residues**: pathogens survive in soil and old plant remains between seasons.
- **Tools, hands, clothing and machines**: tobacco mosaic virus spreads on hands and tools.

## Named diseases

### Fungal: grey leaf spot of maize

**Symptoms**: long, narrow, rectangular grey to tan lesions running between the leaf veins, starting on the lower leaves; badly affected leaves die early, so cobs fill poorly and stalks may lodge. **Favoured by** warm, humid weather and by maize residue left on the surface where maize follows maize. **Control**: resistant or tolerant varieties, **rotation** with a non-host crop such as soya beans, managing infected residue, and a fungicide when the disease appears early in a susceptible crop.

Early blight of tomato, another fungal disease, shows as dark brown spots with **concentric rings** on older leaves; it is controlled by removing infected leaves, avoiding overhead watering, rotation and protectant fungicide sprays.

### Bacterial: bacterial wilt of tomato, potato and other solanaceous crops

**Symptoms**: sudden wilting of young leaves, often while the plant is still green; the plant dies within days; the inside of the stem is brown. **Spread**: the bacteria live in the soil and enter through roots, especially wounded roots; they spread in water, soil on tools and boots, and infected tubers and seedlings. **Control**: there is **no chemical cure**. Use clean seedlings and certified seed potatoes; **rotate** with non-host crops such as maize or other cereals for several seasons; uproot and destroy wilted plants with the surrounding soil; avoid moving soil from infected land; improve drainage; use resistant varieties where available.

:::example{title="Bacterial wilt or water shortage?"}
Nyasha's tomatoes wilt although the soil is moist. To check for bacterial wilt she:

1. Cuts a wilted stem just above the soil.
2. Hangs the cut end in a clear glass of clean water.
3. Watches for a few minutes: milky threads of bacterial ooze stream down from the cut end.

Wilting in moist soil plus streaming ooze points to **bacterial wilt**, not drought. Watering more would not help; she uproots and destroys the plants and plans a rotation with maize.
:::

### Viral: maize streak virus and tobacco mosaic virus

**Maize streak virus**: narrow, broken, pale yellow streaks along the leaf veins; infected young plants are stunted and yield little. It is **spread by leafhoppers**, which pick up the virus from infected maize and grasses. Control: **resistant varieties**, planting at the recommended time and avoiding planting next to older infected maize, removing grass hosts, and controlling leafhoppers.

**Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)**: light and dark green **mosaic** mottling and distorted leaves on tobacco, tomatoes and peppers. It is spread **mechanically** by hands, tools and clothing, and survives well in dried plant material, including tobacco products. Control: wash hands with soap before handling plants, do not smoke or handle tobacco products while working with the crop, remove and destroy infected plants, disinfect tools, rotate crops and use resistant varieties.

No chemical cures a virus disease: control is by **resistant varieties, clean planting material, hygiene and controlling vectors**.

## Fungicides and other control methods

| Fungicide type | How it works | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protectant (contact) | Forms a layer on the plant surface that kills spores before they infect | Applied before infection and repeated after rain and on new growth; copper- and sulfur-based compounds are examples |
| Systemic | Absorbed and moved inside the plant | Can stop early infections and protects new growth |
| Seed dressing | Coats the seed | Protects the seed and seedling from soil-borne and seed-borne fungi |

Other methods apply to all groups: **resistant varieties**, **certified disease-free seed**, **crop rotation**, **field hygiene** (removing and destroying diseased plants and residues), correct **spacing** for air movement, avoiding wetting leaves, and controlling **vectors**.

:::worked-solution[Disease incidence]
Nyasha counts 200 tomato plants and finds 14 with bacterial wilt.

1. Disease incidence $= \dfrac{\text{diseased plants}}{\text{plants examined}} \times 100$.
2. $= \dfrac{14}{200} \times 100 = 7\%$.
3. Recording incidence each week shows whether the disease is spreading along the rows.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Spraying fungicide on a virus or a bacterial wilt"}
Fungicides kill fungi. They do not cure virus diseases or bacterial wilt; prevention and hygiene are the controls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the vector with the cause"}
Leafhoppers do not cause maize streak; they carry the virus that does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague symptoms"}
"The leaves go bad" earns nothing. Describe colour, shape and position: "narrow rectangular grey lesions between the veins of the lower leaves".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="One of each group"}
Learn one named fungal, one bacterial and one viral disease with cause, symptoms, spread and control. Questions often give symptoms and ask you to name the group of organism.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the control"}
Link each control to the spread: "rotate with maize for several seasons, because the wilt bacteria survive in the soil and maize is not a host".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diseases are caused by fungi, bacteria and viruses; they cut photosynthesis, block water flow, rot tissue and lower quality.
- Fungi: spots, rings, rusts, powdery growth; bacteria: water-soaked spots, sudden wilt, soft rots; viruses: mosaic, streaks, curl, stunting.
- Spread by wind, water, vectors, seed, soil and residues, tools and hands.
- Grey leaf spot (fungal), bacterial wilt (bacterial), maize streak (viral, leafhoppers) and TMV (viral, hands and tools).
- Control: resistant varieties, clean seed, rotation, hygiene, vector control and, for fungi, protectant and systemic fungicides.
:::
