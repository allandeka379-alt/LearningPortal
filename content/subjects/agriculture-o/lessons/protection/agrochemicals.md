## Introduction

At a school farm near Marondera a groundsman once mixed an insecticide in a cooking pot, sprayed in shorts and sandals on a windy afternoon, then rinsed the sprayer in the stream where cattle drink. Every one of those actions could have poisoned a person, an animal or the water. Pesticides and herbicides are useful tools, but they are designed to kill living things.

This lesson covers how to handle farm chemicals safely from the shop to the empty container, and how to set up a **knapsack sprayer** so that the right amount reaches the crop. ZIMSEC asks you to *explain* precautions, *describe* toxicity levels, *calculate* mixing ratios, *label* the parts of a knapsack sprayer and *calibrate* it; Cambridge asks you to *explain* the safe handling, dilution and storage of farm chemicals and fuels.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC calls them **agro-chemicals**; Cambridge calls them **farm chemicals**. Both mean pesticides (insecticides, fungicides, herbicides and others) and related products used on farms.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Agro-chemical (farm chemical)"}
A chemical product used in farming to control pests, diseases or weeds, or to regulate growth.
:::

:::definition{term="Active ingredient"}
The chemical in a product that actually kills or controls the pest; the rest of the product helps it mix, spread or stick.
:::

:::definition{term="Calibration"}
Measuring how much liquid a sprayer applies to a known area when used in the normal way, so that the correct amount of chemical can be mixed and applied.
:::

:::definition{term="Pre-harvest interval"}
The minimum time that must pass between the last application of a chemical and harvesting the crop, so that residues fall to a safe level.
:::

## Reading the label

The label is a legal document and the first safety tool. It gives:

- the product's use, the **active ingredient** and its concentration;
- the **rate** and how to mix and apply;
- **hazard symbols**, warning words and the **toxicity class** (often shown by a coloured band);
- **protective clothing** needed, **first aid** and advice for doctors;
- the **pre-harvest interval** and how long to keep people and animals out of a sprayed field;
- storage and disposal instructions, batch number and expiry date.

### Toxicity classes

Internationally, pesticides are placed in hazard classes according to how poisonous they are (the World Health Organization classes):

| Class | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Ia | Extremely hazardous |
| Ib | Highly hazardous |
| II | Moderately hazardous |
| III | Slightly hazardous |
| U | Unlikely to present acute hazard in normal use |

Labels show the class with symbols, warning words and a coloured band; learn the colour scheme used in your country from real labels. Even "slightly hazardous" products can poison if misused.

## Protective clothing

- **Overalls** with long sleeves and trousers (trousers outside the boots).
- **Rubber or nitrile gloves**, especially when handling the concentrate.
- **Rubber boots**.
- **Goggles or a face shield**, and a **mask or respirator** where the label says so.
- A **hat** and, for knapsack spraying, a waterproof apron or back cover.

## Precautions

**Before spraying**

1. Read the label; check that the chemical is right for the pest and crop.
2. Put on protective clothing; check the sprayer for leaks and that it has been **calibrated**.
3. Measure the concentrate with a dedicated measuring jug, never a kitchen utensil; mix outdoors, away from water sources, children and animals.
4. Warn neighbours and keep people and livestock away.

**During spraying**

1. Spray in **calm, cool** weather (early morning or late afternoon), never into the wind or when rain is expected.
2. Do **not eat, drink or smoke**; never blow through a blocked nozzle with your mouth.
3. Walk at a steady pace with the nozzle at a steady height; stop if you feel unwell.

**After spraying**

1. Clean the sprayer with water, spraying the rinsings onto the field just treated; never wash it in a river, dam or borehole area.
2. **Triple-rinse** empty containers, add the rinsings to the spray tank, **puncture** the containers so they cannot be reused, and dispose of them as the label or local authority directs. Never use them for food or water.
3. Wash protective clothing separately and wash your body with soap.
4. Put up warning signs; record the date, product, rate and field; observe the re-entry and pre-harvest intervals.

## Safe storage of chemicals and fuels

- A **locked**, well-ventilated store with a warning sign, away from houses, food, feed, wells and streams.
- Keep chemicals in their **original labelled containers**, on shelves off the floor, with liquids below powders.
- Store **fuels** (diesel, petrol, paraffin) separately from chemicals, away from heat, sparks and naked flames; no smoking near them.
- Keep **sand or sawdust** to soak up spills and a **fire extinguisher** by the door.
- Keep a list of the stock and use the oldest first; return or dispose of expired products safely.

## The knapsack sprayer

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Tank with lid and strainer | Holds the spray mixture (often about 15-20 L); the strainer at the opening stops debris entering |
| Pump (piston or diaphragm) and lever | The lever, moved up and down, works the pump, which builds pressure |
| Pressure chamber | Evens out the pressure so the spray is steady |
| Hose | Carries mixture from the pump to the lance |
| Trigger valve (cut-off) | Starts and stops the spray |
| Lance | A rigid tube that lets the operator direct the spray |
| Nozzle | Breaks the liquid into droplets of the right size and pattern: a hollow cone for insecticides and fungicides on leaves, a flat fan for even cover with herbicides |
| Straps | Carry the sprayer on the back |

**Maintenance**: rinse the sprayer inside and out after each use and spray clean water through the nozzle; clean the strainer and nozzle with a soft brush; oil the moving parts as the maker recommends; replace worn washers and leaking hoses; store empty, clean and dry, out of the sun.

## Calibration and mixing calculations

1. Mark out a known area, for example $10\ \text{m} \times 20\ \text{m} = 200\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Fill the sprayer with a measured volume of **water**.
3. Spray the area at your normal walking speed and pumping rate.
4. Measure the volume used (refill to the mark).
5. Work out the spray volume per hectare, then the chemical needed in each tank.

:::worked-solution[Calibrating a knapsack sprayer]
Tendai uses 4 L of water to spray 200 m². His tank holds 15 L.

1. Spray volume $= \dfrac{4}{200} \times 10\,000 = 200$ L/ha.
2. Tanks per hectare $= 200 \div 15 = 13.3$, so he needs **14 fills** (round up).
3. The label rate (for this example) is 1.5 L of product per hectare. Product per tank $= \dfrac{1.5 \times 1000 \times 15}{200} = 112.5$ mL.
:::

:::worked-solution[Mixing by ratio]
A product is to be mixed at 1 part concentrate to 49 parts water, and the mixture must fill a 20 L tank.

1. Total parts $= 1 + 49 = 50$.
2. Concentrate $= \dfrac{20\,000\ \text{mL}}{50} = 400$ mL.
3. Water $= 20\,000 - 400 = 19\,600$ mL $= 19.6$ L.
:::

A label may instead give a rate per litre: at 2.5 mL per litre, a 15 L tank needs $2.5 \times 15 = 37.5$ mL.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding the concentrate to a full tank"}
Half-fill with water, add the measured concentrate, then top up, so it mixes well and does not splash.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ratio confusion"}
"1 to 49" means 50 parts in total, so the concentrate is one fiftieth of the mixture, not one forty-ninth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rounding fills down"}
13.3 tank-loads means 14 fills; 13 leaves part of the field untreated.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read the label: use, active ingredient, rate, hazard class, protective clothing, first aid, pre-harvest interval.
- Wear overalls, gloves, boots, eye and face protection, and a mask where required.
- Before: calibrate, mix outdoors with dedicated measures. During: calm weather, no eating or smoking. After: rinse onto the field, triple-rinse and puncture containers, wash.
- Store chemicals locked, labelled and ventilated; fuels apart from chemicals and away from flames.
- Knapsack sprayer: tank, strainer, pump and lever, pressure chamber, hose, trigger valve, lance, nozzle.
- Calibration: L/ha $= \dfrac{\text{litres used}}{\text{area in m}^2} \times 10\,000$; product per tank from the label rate.
:::
