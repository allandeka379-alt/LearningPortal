## Introduction

When fall armyworm reached his area, Sipho sprayed his maize near Hwange every week with whatever insecticide the shop had. By February he had spent a lot of money, the caterpillars were still there, and he had seen no ladybirds or wasps in his field all season. His neighbour, guided by an AGRITEX officer, scouted twice a week, crushed egg masses, sprayed only when damage passed the advised level and destroyed crop residues after harvest. She spent less and harvested more.

The difference is **integrated pest management**. This lesson covers each group of control methods, how pesticides act and how methods are combined. Examiners ask you to *describe* a method, *explain* how it works, give its *advantages and disadvantages* and *discuss* IPM.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pest control"}
Any method that keeps pest numbers or damage low enough that the crop is not seriously harmed.
:::

:::definition{term="Trap crop"}
A plant grown near or within the main crop because the pest prefers it, so the pest gathers there and can be destroyed.
:::

:::definition{term="Biological control"}
Using living natural enemies (predators, parasites or disease organisms) to reduce pest numbers.
:::

:::definition{term="Integrated pest management (IPM)"}
Combining compatible control methods, guided by regular scouting, to keep pests below the level that causes economic loss, using chemicals only when needed and in the least harmful way.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic threshold"}
The pest level at which the cost of control is less than the value of the crop it saves, so control becomes worthwhile.
:::

## Cultural control

Cultural methods change normal farm practice so that conditions suit the crop and not the pest.

- **Crop rotation**: pests that attack one crop family die out or move away when a different crop follows.
- **Timely planting**: planting at the recommended time lets the crop pass its most sensitive stage before pest numbers peak; neighbours planting at about the same time avoids a "bridge" of young crops.
- **Field hygiene**: destroy or plough in old stalks and residues (where stalk borer larvae survive the dry season) and remove volunteer plants and weed hosts. In cotton, destroying all stalks after harvest gives a **closed season** in which bollworms have no food.
- **Trap crops and intercropping**: pests are drawn to a preferred plant. In the "push-pull" system a repellent legume (desmodium) between maize rows "pushes" stem borer moths away and an attractive grass (Napier grass) around the field "pulls" them out.
- **Deep ploughing** exposes pupae in the soil to sun and birds; **healthy, well-fed crops** tolerate attack better.

Advantages: cheap, part of normal work, no chemicals. Disadvantages: slow, rarely enough alone in an outbreak, and some (such as residue destruction) only work if neighbours do the same.

## Physical and mechanical control

- **Hand-picking** and crushing larvae and egg masses (practical on small plots).
- **Traps**: light traps, sticky traps, barriers and fine nets over seedbeds.
- **Storage hygiene**: dry grain well, sieve and winnow out insects, clean stores before filling, and use **airtight (hermetic) bags or metal silos**, in which insects die from lack of oxygen.

Advantages: safe, no residues. Disadvantages: labour-intensive and impractical on large areas.

## Biological control

Natural enemies kill pests:

- **Predators** eat pests: ladybird beetles and lacewing larvae eat aphids; birds eat caterpillars.
- **Parasitoids**: tiny wasps lay their eggs in or on pest eggs or larvae, and the wasp larvae kill the host.
- **Disease organisms**: a bacterium (*Bacillus thuringiensis*), fungi and viruses sold as biopesticides infect particular pests.

Advantages: no poisonous residues; often specific to the pest; can keep working season after season. Disadvantages: slow; never eradicates the pest; enemies are killed by broad-spectrum sprays; needs knowledge to use.

## Biotechnical control

- **Pheromone traps**: a lure releases the female's sex scent; males fly in and are caught. They are used mainly to **monitor** when moths arrive, and in large numbers to disrupt mating.
- **Resistant varieties**: bred (conventionally or by genetic modification) to resist or tolerate a pest; for example, crops carrying a gene from *Bacillus thuringiensis* make a protein toxic to caterpillars.
- **Sterile insect technique**: large numbers of sterilised males are released, so females lay infertile eggs.

## Chemical control

| Pesticide type | How it acts | Best against | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Kills the pest when the chemical touches its body | Pests exposed on the plant surface | Spray must cover the pest; washed off by rain; no effect on pests hidden inside stems or fruit |
| Systemic | Absorbed by the plant and carried in the sap to all parts | Sucking pests (aphids, whiteflies) and pests on new growth | Protects new growth; observe the waiting period before harvest |
| Stomach poison | Eaten with treated leaves or baits and kills from the gut | Biting and chewing pests (caterpillars, cutworm baits) | Must be on the part the pest eats |
| Fumigant | A poisonous gas that penetrates sealed stores and grain | Storage pests, including stages inside grains | Extremely toxic; for trained operators in sealed stores only |

Advantages: fast, effective in outbreaks, can treat large areas. Disadvantages: cost; poisoning of people, livestock and bees; residues on food; pollution of water; **resistance** when one chemical is used repeatedly; death of natural enemies, which can cause **secondary pest outbreaks**.

## Integrated pest management

1. **Prevent**: rotation, resistant varieties, timely planting, field hygiene, healthy crops.
2. **Monitor**: scout regularly and use pheromone traps.
3. **Decide**: act only when the pest passes the economic threshold.
4. **Act, least harmful first**: hand-picking, biological products, then a selective pesticide applied to the right place (for example into the maize funnel).
5. **Rotate pesticide groups** to slow resistance and protect natural enemies.
6. **Record and review** results for the next season.

:::worked-solution[Does spraying pay?]
Nyasha has 2 ha of maize expected to yield 4 t/ha. Scouting suggests she will lose about 15% of the crop if she does nothing. Assume maize sells at US\$300 per tonne and one spray costs US\$45 per hectare (chemical and labour), and assume the spray prevents the loss.

1. Expected crop $= 2 \times 4 = 8$ t.
2. Crop saved $= 8 \times \dfrac{15}{100} = 1.2$ t.
3. Value saved $= 1.2 \times 300 =$ US\$360.
4. Cost of spraying $= 2 \times 45 =$ US\$90.
5. Net benefit $=$ US\$360 $-$ US\$90 $=$ **US\$270**, so spraying pays at this pest level. At a much lower infestation the value saved could be less than the cost.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing the right weapon for the pest"}
Farai's cabbages have aphid colonies under the leaves, and his maize has stalk borer larvae already tunnelling in the stems.

- **Aphids** suck sap and hide under leaves: a **systemic** insecticide reaches them through the sap, but he first checks for ladybirds, which may control them alone.
- **Stalk borer** larvae inside stems are out of reach of a contact spray on the leaves. Control must target the young larvae in the funnel early, and he must **destroy the stalks after harvest** to stop the next generation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Spraying on a calendar"}
Spraying every week regardless of pest numbers wastes money, kills natural enemies and breeds resistance. Scout, then decide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up systemic and contact"}
Systemic pesticides move inside the plant; contact pesticides must touch the pest. A contact spray cannot reach a borer inside a stem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying IPM means no chemicals"}
IPM uses chemicals when they are justified, chosen and applied to do least harm.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Advantages and disadvantages"}
Give a matched pair for each method ("biological control leaves no residues, but it is slow"). ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 1 both reward specific examples linked to a named pest.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss IPM"}
Name methods from at least three groups, explain how scouting and thresholds decide when to spray, and finish with a judgement on why IPM is better than chemicals alone.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cultural: rotation, timely planting, field hygiene and closed seasons, trap crops and push-pull, deep ploughing.
- Physical and mechanical: hand-picking, traps, nets, clean dry storage and airtight bags.
- Biological: predators, parasitoid wasps and disease organisms; safe but slow.
- Biotechnical: pheromone traps, resistant (including Bt) varieties, sterile insects.
- Chemical: contact, systemic, stomach poisons and fumigants; fast but risky, costly and resistance-prone.
- IPM combines methods guided by scouting and the economic threshold.
:::
