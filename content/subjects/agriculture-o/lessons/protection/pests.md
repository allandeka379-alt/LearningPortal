## Introduction

In December Tatenda walks through his maize field near Gokwe and sees ragged holes in the young leaves and wet, sawdust-like droppings in the funnels. A neighbour's field has small "windows" in the leaves and some plants whose central shoot has died. Both fields have caterpillars, but they are **different pests** with different life cycles, and the right control depends on knowing which one is present.

A **pest** is any animal that damages crops or stored produce and so reduces yield, quality or profit. Most crop pests are **insects**, but mites, nematodes, birds, rodents and some larger animals are also pests. Examiners ask you to *classify* pests by how they feed, to *describe* the life cycle of a named pest, and to *explain* the damage it does. Control methods are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pest"}
An organism, usually an insect, that feeds on or damages crops or stored produce and so lowers yield, quality or income.
:::

:::definition{term="Metamorphosis"}
The change in body form that an insect goes through as it develops from egg to adult.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete metamorphosis"}
Development in four stages, egg → larva → pupa → adult, in which the young (larva) looks quite different from the adult.
:::

:::definition{term="Incomplete metamorphosis"}
Development in three stages, egg → nymph → adult, in which the young (nymph) looks like a small, wingless adult and grows by moulting.
:::

:::definition{term="Scouting"}
Walking through a crop at regular intervals and inspecting sample plants to find pests early and decide whether control is needed.
:::

## How pests harm crops

- **Reduce yield**: leaves eaten or sucked dry make less food; damaged roots and stems cause wilting and lodging.
- **Lower quality**: holes in fruit, bored grain and stained cotton lint fetch lower prices.
- **Spread diseases**: aphids and leafhoppers carry viruses from plant to plant; wounds let fungi and bacteria in.
- **Kill plants outright**: cutworms cut seedlings at ground level; stalk borers kill the growing point.
- **Raise costs**: money and labour are spent on scouting and control.
- **Destroy stored produce**: weevils and rats eat grain after all the work of growing it.

## Classifying pests by feeding habit

| Group | Mouthparts and feeding | Typical damage | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Biting and chewing | Strong jaws (mandibles) bite off and chew plant tissue | Holes and ragged edges in leaves, defoliation, cut stems and roots | Locusts, termites, cutworms, fall armyworm larvae, leaf-eating beetles |
| Piercing and sucking | A fine needle-like tube (stylet) pierces the plant and sucks sap | Curling, yellowing, wilting and stunting; sticky honeydew and black sooty mould; viruses spread | Aphids, whiteflies, stink bugs, cotton stainers; red spider mites (mites, not insects) |
| Boring | Larvae tunnel inside stems, fruits, pods or grain | Dead hearts, broken stems, rotten or holed fruit, hollow grain | Maize stalk borer, American bollworm in bolls and fruit, grain weevils |

The groups overlap: a boring larva also *chews*, but it is grouped by where it feeds. Knowing the group tells you which pesticide can reach the pest (next lesson).

## Complete and incomplete metamorphosis

| Feature | Complete | Incomplete |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stages | Egg, larva, pupa, adult | Egg, nymph, adult |
| Young stage | Larva (caterpillar, grub or maggot), unlike the adult | Nymph, like a small wingless adult |
| Resting stage | Pupa: no feeding, body rebuilt | None |
| Stage that does most damage | Usually the larva | Nymphs and adults (often the same food) |
| Examples | Moths (stalk borer, fall armyworm, bollworm), beetles and weevils | Locusts, grasshoppers, aphids, termites |

## Named pests

### Fall armyworm (Spodoptera frugiperda)

First reported in southern Africa in 2016-2017, it attacks maize most severely, as well as sorghum and other crops.

1. **Egg**: the moth lays eggs in masses on leaves, covered with greyish scales.
2. **Larva**: young larvae scrape the leaf surface; older larvae eat into the funnel, leaving ragged holes and wet, sawdust-like droppings (frass). A pale inverted "Y" on the head and four dark spots in a square near the tail help identify it. Larvae may also bore into cobs.
3. **Pupa**: the full-grown larva drops to the soil and pupates there.
4. **Adult**: the moth flies at night and can travel long distances, so several generations can build up in one season.

### Maize stalk borer (Busseola fusca)

1. The moth lays batches of eggs between the leaf sheath and the stem.
2. Young larvae feed in the funnel; when the leaves unfold they show rows of small holes or "windows". Older larvae **bore into the stem**. In young plants the growing point dies and the central leaves wither (**dead heart**); in older plants tunnels weaken the stem and the plant may break, and larvae may enter the cob.
3. At the end of the season larvae rest in the old dry stalks through the dry season, then pupate.
4. Moths emerge early in the next rains and attack the new crop. This is why destroying old stalks after harvest matters.

### Aphids

Small, soft-bodied, piercing and sucking insects that cluster on growing tips and the undersides of leaves. They develop by **incomplete metamorphosis**, and in warm weather females give birth to live young without mating, so colonies grow very fast; winged forms fly to new plants when crowded. Leaves curl and yellow, plants are stunted, and the sugary honeydew they excrete grows black sooty mould. Aphids also **transmit viruses**.

### American bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera)

A moth with complete metamorphosis. Eggs are laid singly on young leaves, buds and flowers of cotton, tomatoes, maize and other crops. The larvae bore into buds, flowers, cotton bolls and tomato fruits, which drop or rot. Larvae pupate in the soil.

### Termites, locusts and grain weevils

- **Termites** are social insects (queen, king, workers, soldiers) with incomplete metamorphosis. Workers chew roots and stems, especially in dry spells, so plants wilt and fall over.
- **Locusts** lay egg pods in the soil; the wingless nymphs (hoppers) moult several times; swarms of adults can strip whole fields.
- **Grain weevils** have complete metamorphosis that takes place **inside the grain**: the female chews a hole, lays an egg and seals it; the larva eats the inside and pupates there, and the adult chews out, leaving a round exit hole.

## How pests spread

- **Flight** of adults, often helped by wind (moths, locusts, winged aphids).
- **Infested seed, seedlings and planting material** moved to new farms.
- **Crop residues** that carry resting stages to the next season (stalk borer larvae in old stalks).
- **Transport and trade** of produce and bags (weevils in grain).
- **Alternative host plants** and weeds near fields.

## Scouting a field

1. Walk a set pattern (for example a W or zig-zag) so the whole field is sampled, not only the edges.
2. Inspect a fixed number of plants at several points, checking funnels, leaf undersides and stems.
3. Record the number of plants with pests or fresh damage and the growth stage of the crop.
4. Calculate the percentage of plants infested and compare it with the level at which control is recommended.

:::worked-solution[Percentage of plants infested]
Tatenda inspects 50 maize plants (10 at each of five points) and finds fresh fall armyworm damage on 12.

1. Percentage infested $= \dfrac{\text{plants infested}}{\text{plants inspected}} \times 100$.
2. $= \dfrac{12}{50} \times 100 = 24\%$.
3. He compares 24% with the action level advised by his extension officer and scouts again in a few days to see whether the infestation is rising.
:::

:::example{title="Diagnosing the pest from the damage"}
Chipo's young cabbages near Mutare have curled, yellowing leaves that feel sticky, with black patches on the upper leaves and no holes at all.

- No holes, but curling and yellowing: the pest is **sucking sap**, not chewing.
- Sticky leaves with black patches: **honeydew** with **sooty mould**.
- Conclusion: a piercing and sucking pest, most likely **aphids**. She should look under the leaves for colonies and watch for virus symptoms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every insect a pest"}
Ladybirds, bees and many wasps are useful insects. A pest is defined by the damage it does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nymph and larva"}
A **nymph** (incomplete metamorphosis) resembles the adult; a **larva** (complete metamorphosis) does not and is followed by a pupa.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing damage vaguely"}
"It eats the plant" earns little. Say which part is damaged and what happens: "larvae bore into the stem and kill the growing point, causing dead heart".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Life cycles"}
Name the stages in order, say where each happens (eggs between leaf sheath and stem; pupa in the soil) and which stage does the damage. ZIMSEC asks for one pest with complete and one with incomplete metamorphosis; Cambridge asks for one biting and chewing, one piercing and sucking and one boring pest.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="State, describe, explain"}
*State* the group or name; *describe* the damage you would see; *explain* why it lowers yield ("sap removed, so less sugar for growth").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pests lower yield and quality, spread diseases, kill plants, raise costs and destroy stored produce.
- Feeding groups: biting and chewing (locusts, termites, armyworm), piercing and sucking (aphids, whiteflies), boring (stalk borer, bollworm, weevils).
- Complete metamorphosis: egg, larva, pupa, adult (moths, weevils). Incomplete: egg, nymph, adult (locusts, aphids, termites).
- Stalk borer larvae survive the dry season in old stalks; fall armyworm larvae feed in the funnel and pupate in the soil.
- Pests spread by flight, wind, infested material, residues and trade. Scouting finds them early.
:::
