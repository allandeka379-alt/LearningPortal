## Introduction

Tendai grows maize on a field in a communal area near Masvingo that his grandfather was given by the village head. He cannot sell the field, and the bank will not accept it as security for a loan. His aunt Rudo farms a resettlement plot near Chinhoyi under a government permit, and her neighbour owns his farm outright with a title deed. All three farm land, but they hold it in **different ways**.

The way land is held decides who may use it, for how long, whether it can be sold or used to raise a loan, and how much a farmer is willing to invest in it. Land tenure and land reform are examined in ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2. Answers must be **factual and balanced**: describe each system, give its advantages and disadvantages, and state the aims, benefits and problems of land reform without slogans.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land tenure"}
The system of rights and rules under which land is held and used: who controls it, for how long, and what the holder may do with it.
:::

:::definition{term="Freehold tenure"}
Private ownership of land with a title deed, held permanently; the owner may sell, lease, mortgage or pass on the land.
:::

:::definition{term="Leasehold tenure"}
The right to use land owned by someone else (often the state) for a fixed period, in return for rent and on agreed conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Communal tenure"}
A system in which land is vested in the state and allocated to households by traditional leaders and local authorities; families have the right to use arable land, and grazing is shared.
:::

:::definition{term="Resettlement"}
Moving families onto land acquired by the state and allocating them plots under permits or leases.
:::

## Land tenure systems

| System | Who holds the land | Main advantages | Main disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Freehold | Private owner with a title deed | Secure; title deed can be used as collateral for loans; strong incentive to invest | Land can be concentrated in a few hands; land may be left idle; expensive to buy |
| Leasehold | Lessee rents from the owner (often the state) | Access to land without buying it; conditions can require productive use | Lease may not be renewed; lessee may invest less near the end of the lease; rent must be paid |
| Communal | Vested in the state; allocated by chiefs, headmen, village heads and rural district councils | Every family can get land; cheap; keeps communities and traditions together | No title deed, so it is hard to borrow; shared grazing is easily overgrazed; plots become fragmented |
| Resettlement | State land allocated to settlers under permits or leases | Gives land to people who had little or none; decongests communal areas | Permits are not always accepted as collateral; settlers may lack capital, equipment and skills |

:::example{title="Why tenure affects investment"}
Sipho wants to borrow money to drill a borehole and buy a pump.

- On **freehold** land he can offer his **title deed** as security, so a bank is more willing to lend.
- On **communal** land he has no title deed. He may have to rely on savings, a group loan or contract farming.
- On a **long lease** he may be able to use the lease, if the lender accepts it, and he will invest if the lease is long enough for the borehole to pay for itself.

The more **secure** and **transferable** the tenure, the easier it is to raise money and the more willing the farmer is to make long-term improvements such as boreholes, fences, orchards and conservation works.
:::

## Land as a national heritage

Land is more than a factor of production. It is described as a **national heritage** because:

1. it is the base of **food production**, jobs and the national economy;
2. it holds **ancestral graves, sacred sites and shrines** that link families to their history;
3. people's **identity** and sense of belonging are tied to their home areas;
4. its soils, forests, water and wildlife must be **conserved for future generations**.

## Land ownership through history

### Before colonial rule

Land belonged to the **community**. The **chief**, as custodian on behalf of the ancestors, allocated land to families through headmen. Families had the right to use land for homes and fields, grazing was shared, and land could not be sold. Shifting cultivation and long fallows were possible because land was plentiful.

### During colonial rule

Laws divided the land along racial lines:

- The **Land Apportionment Act (1930)** set aside separate areas for European and African occupation. Africans were moved into **native reserves**, later called Tribal Trust Lands, many of them in drier, less fertile regions.
- The **Native Land Husbandry Act (1951)** tried to control land use and stock numbers in the reserves.
- The **Land Tenure Act (1969)** again divided land between the races.

The result was crowded reserves with overgrazed, eroded land, alongside large commercial farms.

### After independence (1980)

1. **1980s:** resettlement on a "willing buyer, willing seller" basis, under which the government bought farms offered for sale.
2. **1990s:** the Land Acquisition Act (1992) allowed the state to acquire land compulsorily.
3. **From 2000:** the **Fast Track Land Reform Programme** acquired large areas of commercial farmland and resettled people under the **A1** and **A2** models.

## Reasons for land reform

- To correct the **unequal distribution** of land inherited from the colonial period.
- To **decongest** overcrowded communal areas and reduce land degradation there.
- To give land to the **landless** and to poor families.
- To create a class of **indigenous commercial farmers**.
- To bring **under-used** land into production.

## The resettlement models

| Feature | A1 model | A2 model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aim | Decongest communal areas; give land to the landless and poor | Create indigenous commercial farmers |
| Scale | Small-scale farming | Small-, medium- and large-scale commercial farming |
| Layout | Villagised (homesteads in a village, individual arable plots, shared grazing) or self-contained plots | Self-contained farms |
| Who qualifies | Families with little or no land | Applicants expected to show farming skills and access to capital |
| Tenure | Permits from the state | Leases from the state (long leases, for example 99 years) |

:::example{title="Matching a settler to a model"}
Farai has worked on farms for years, has saved money and owns a tractor; he wants to produce tobacco and soya beans for sale. Chipo's family has only a small, eroded field in a crowded communal area.

- Farai fits the **A2** model: a self-contained commercial farm for a settler with skills and capital.
- Chipo's family fits the **A1** model: a smaller plot that relieves congestion in the communal area.
:::

## Benefits and problems of land reform

**Recognised benefits**

- Many more families gained access to land, and congestion in some communal areas was reduced.
- Land ownership was spread more widely among Zimbabweans.
- New farmers entered crops such as tobacco and maize, many supported by contract farming.

**Recognised problems**

- Many new farmers lacked **capital, equipment, inputs and skills**, so production on some farms fell.
- Permits and leases have not always been accepted as **collateral**, which limits access to loans.
- Boundary disputes, multiple allocations and **tenure insecurity** discouraged investment.
- Some infrastructure (dams, irrigation, buildings) deteriorated, and some farm workers lost jobs and homes.
- In some areas tree cutting and poaching increased.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying communal land is owned by the family"}
Communal land is vested in the **state**. The family has the right to **use** it, not a title deed, so it cannot be sold or mortgaged.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up A1 and A2"}
**A1** is mainly for small-scale farmers from congested areas; **A2** is for commercial farming by settlers with skills and capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing one-sided answers"}
"Discuss land reform" needs aims, benefits **and** problems. Answers that only praise or only criticise lose marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**describe** a tenure system", state who holds the land, how it is allocated and what the holder can or cannot do. For "**compare**", use a table or "whereas": "a freehold owner holds a title deed, whereas a communal farmer has only the right to use the land".
:::

:::exam-tip
For "**explain** why tenure affects production", link security to investment: "without a title deed the farmer cannot borrow, so cannot buy inputs or build a borehole".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land tenure is the system of rights under which land is held: freehold, leasehold, communal and resettlement tenure.
- Secure, transferable tenure (title deeds) makes borrowing and long-term investment easier.
- Land is a national heritage: food, jobs, identity, ancestral sites and resources for future generations.
- Colonial laws such as the Land Apportionment Act (1930) divided land racially; after 1980 resettlement began, and fast-track land reform started in 2000.
- A1 settles small-scale farmers from congested areas; A2 creates commercial farms; reform brought wider access to land but also problems of capital, skills and tenure security.
:::
