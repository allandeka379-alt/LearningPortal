## Introduction

In a greenhouse outside Harare, lettuces grow in plastic channels with no soil at all: a thin stream of nutrient solution flows past their roots. On a farm near Mutare, a grower sells "certified organic" vegetables at a higher price to a supermarket, having used no synthetic fertiliser or pesticide. In other parts of the world, farmers plant maize that has been genetically modified to resist stem borers.

These three approaches, **organic production**, **hydroponics** and **genetically modified (GM) crops**, are very different answers to the same question: how can we produce more food, more profitably and with less harm? The Cambridge syllabus asks you to **describe** each one and to **discuss the arguments for and against** GM crops and organic production. A good discussion gives both sides and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organic production"}
Farming without synthetic (manufactured) fertilisers, pesticides or growth regulators, relying on manure, compost, crop rotation, legumes and biological pest control to keep soil fertile and crops healthy.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydroponics"}
Growing plants without soil, with their roots in a nutrient solution or in an inert material such as gravel, sand or coconut fibre that is supplied with nutrient solution.
:::

:::definition{term="Genetically modified (GM) crop"}
A crop whose DNA has been changed by genetic engineering, usually by inserting a gene from another organism, to give it a new characteristic.
:::

:::definition{term="Certification"}
Inspection and approval by a recognised body that a farm follows the rules of organic production, allowing its produce to be sold as organic.
:::

## Organic production

Organic farmers aim to keep the **soil** healthy so that it feeds the crop, rather than feeding the crop directly with soluble fertiliser. They use:

1. **Manure and compost** to supply nutrients and organic matter.
2. **Crop rotation** with **legumes** (groundnuts, beans, cowpeas) that fix nitrogen.
3. **Green manures and cover crops** that are dug in or left as mulch.
4. **Biological and cultural pest control**: natural enemies (ladybirds that eat aphids), resistant varieties, trap crops, hand-picking, and allowed natural sprays.
5. **Mechanical weed control**: hoeing, mulching and rotation instead of herbicides.

Before produce can be sold as organic, the land usually goes through a **conversion period** without synthetic inputs, and the farm must be **certified**.

:::worked-solution[Is organic production more profitable here?]
Chipo grows tomatoes on 0.1 ha. Compare one season of conventional and organic production (all prices assumed).

| Item | Conventional | Organic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yield (kg) | 6,000 | 4,800 |
| Price per kg | US\$0.50 | US\$0.75 |
| Variable costs | US\$950 | US\$1,150 |

1. Conventional income $= 6\,000 \times 0.50 =$ US\$3,000.
2. Conventional margin $=$ US\$3,000 − US\$950 = US\$2,050.
3. Organic income $= 4\,800 \times 0.75 =$ US\$3,600.
4. Organic margin $=$ US\$3,600 − US\$1,150 = US\$2,450.
5. The organic yield is $\dfrac{4\,800 - 6\,000}{6\,000} \times 100 = -20\%$, i.e. 20% lower, and costs are higher (more labour and certification), but the **premium price** makes the organic margin US\$400 higher. Without the premium, organic would be less profitable.
:::

## Hydroponics

In hydroponics, all the mineral nutrients a plant needs (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur and trace elements) are dissolved in water. Common systems:

- **Nutrient film technique (NFT):** a thin film of solution flows along sloping channels past the roots and is pumped back to a tank.
- **Aggregate (media) culture:** plants grow in gravel, sand or coconut fibre that is watered with the solution.
- **Deep water culture:** roots hang in an aerated tank of solution.

The grower must keep the **pH** and **nutrient concentration** of the solution correct and supply **oxygen** to the roots.

:::worked-solution[Mixing a hydroponic nutrient solution]
A grower's nutrient concentrate is used at 4 mL per litre of water. The tank holds 250 L.

1. Concentrate needed $= 4\ \text{mL/L} \times 250\ \text{L} = 1\,000\ \text{mL}$.
2. $1\,000\ \text{mL} = 1\ \text{L}$ of concentrate in the full tank.
3. The grower checks the pH and strength of the solution after mixing and tops up regularly, because plants remove water and nutrients at different rates.
:::

| Advantages of hydroponics | Disadvantages of hydroponics |
| --- | --- |
| No soil needed: can be used on poor land, rooftops or in towns | High cost of equipment, greenhouse and pumps |
| Exact control of nutrients gives fast growth and high yields | Needs technical skill to manage pH and nutrients |
| Recirculating systems use much less water than field irrigation | Depends on electricity: a pump failure can kill crops quickly |
| No soil-borne diseases, no weeds, little tillage | Water-borne diseases spread fast through shared solution |
| Clean produce, grown all year in a greenhouse | Suits high-value crops (lettuce, tomatoes, herbs), not staple grains |

## Genetically modified crops

Genetic engineering moves a specific **gene** into a crop, often from a completely unrelated organism. This is different from **selective breeding**, which only combines genes from plants that can cross naturally.

Examples of GM traits:

- **Insect resistance (Bt crops):** a gene from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* makes the plant produce a protein that kills certain caterpillars, such as stem borers in maize and bollworms in cotton.
- **Herbicide tolerance:** the crop survives a particular broad-spectrum herbicide that kills the weeds around it.
- **Improved nutrition:** for example rice engineered to produce beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A.
- Traits under development include **drought tolerance** and **disease resistance**.

### Arguments for and against GM crops

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| Higher yields from fewer losses to pests and weeds | Pests and weeds may evolve resistance to the trait |
| Less insecticide sprayed, which protects farm workers and natural enemies | Genes may spread by pollen to non-GM crops or wild relatives |
| Crops can be made more nutritious | Seed is expensive and often patented; farmers buy new seed each season |
| Possible tolerance of drought, heat or disease | Possible harm to non-target organisms and loss of crop diversity |
| Reduced tillage when herbicide-tolerant crops are used | Some consumers and importing countries reject GM food, which can limit exports |

Many countries regulate GM crops strictly, and some do not allow them to be grown commercially at all.

### Arguments for and against organic production

| For | Against |
| --- | --- |
| No synthetic pesticide residues in food | Yields are usually lower, so more land is needed for the same food |
| Builds soil organic matter and soil life | More labour (weeding, composting, hand pest control) |
| Less pollution of rivers by nitrate and pesticides | Pests and diseases are harder to control |
| Premium prices and growing markets | Certification and conversion cost time and money |
| Lower spending on bought inputs | Enough manure and compost may be hard to obtain |

:::example{title="Discussing GM maize for a smallholder"}
Tendai loses part of his maize to stem borers every season. A Bt maize variety would control the borers without spraying, so his yield and safety would improve. However, he would have to buy expensive seed every season, borers could become resistant if refuges of non-Bt maize are not planted, and if he sells to a market that rejects GM grain he could lose buyers. **Conclusion:** the variety would help only if seed is affordable, the market accepts it and resistance is managed; otherwise integrated control of stem borers may be the better choice.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying organic farming uses no fertiliser"}
Organic farming uses **organic** fertilisers (manure, compost, green manure). It avoids **synthetic** ones.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing GM with selective breeding or hybrids"}
Hybrids and selectively bred varieties come from crossing plants that breed naturally. GM inserts a gene in the laboratory, often from another species.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hydroponic plants need no minerals"}
Hydroponic plants need **all** the same mineral nutrients as plants in soil; they receive them dissolved in water.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Discuss** the arguments for and against" needs points on **both** sides, each explained, and a **conclusion** that weighs them for the situation given. A list of one-word points stays in the lowest level.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question gives figures (yields, prices, costs), calculate income and margins for each system and use them as evidence in your argument.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organic production avoids synthetic fertilisers and pesticides and relies on manure, compost, rotation, legumes and biological control; produce must be certified.
- Hydroponics grows plants without soil in a nutrient solution; it gives high yields and saves water but is costly, technical and power-dependent.
- GM crops carry genes inserted by genetic engineering, such as Bt insect resistance or herbicide tolerance.
- For GM: higher yields, less spraying, better nutrition. Against: resistance, gene flow, seed costs, market rejection.
- For organic: no residues, healthier soil, premium prices. Against: lower yields, more labour, harder pest control, certification costs.
:::
