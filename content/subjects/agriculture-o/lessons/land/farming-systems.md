## Introduction

On a smallholding near Gokwe, Nyasha grows maize with pumpkins and cowpeas between the rows, keeps six cattle that pull her plough, and spreads their manure on her fields. Twenty kilometres away, a commercial farm grows nothing but cotton on hundreds of hectares. Both are **farming systems**: ways of organising crops, animals, land, labour and money on a farm.

The choice of system affects yields, income, risk, soil fertility and the workload. You must be able to **describe** each system and **compare** their advantages and disadvantages, often for a named farm in a short scenario.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mixed farming"}
Keeping crops and livestock on the same farm so that each supports the other, for example crop residues fed to cattle and manure returned to the fields.
:::

:::definition{term="Monoculture"}
Growing one crop on the same land, often season after season, usually on a large scale.
:::

:::definition{term="Intercropping"}
Growing two or more crops at the same time on the same piece of land in a definite pattern, such as alternate rows.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixed cropping"}
Growing two or more crops together on the same land at the same time with no definite row pattern.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsistence farming"}
Farming mainly to feed the farmer's own family, with little produce for sale.
:::

:::definition{term="Commercial farming"}
Farming mainly to produce goods for sale at a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Intensive farming"}
Using high inputs of labour, capital, fertiliser, water or technology on a small area to obtain high output per hectare.
:::

:::definition{term="Extensive farming"}
Using large areas of land with low inputs per hectare, giving low output per hectare.
:::

## Mixed farming

Crops and livestock are linked:

- crop residues (maize stover, groundnut haulms) and fodder crops **feed the animals**;
- animals provide **manure**, **draught power** for ploughing and carting, and food;
- legumes in the crop rotation improve the soil for later crops and pasture.

**Advantages:** income from several enterprises spreads the **risk** if one fails; manure maintains soil fertility; labour is used throughout the year; waste from one enterprise feeds another.

**Disadvantages:** the farmer needs skills in both crops and livestock; more capital is needed (fences, pens, water points); attention is divided, so neither enterprise may be managed to the highest standard; animals may damage crops if fencing is poor.

## Monoculture

**Advantages:** the farmer **specialises** and becomes highly skilled; machinery, inputs and marketing are simpler and cheaper per unit (economies of scale); large uniform crops are easy to mechanise and suit contract farming.

**Disadvantages:** all income depends on one crop, so a drought, pest outbreak or price fall is very damaging; **pests and diseases build up** when the same crop is grown on the same land each year; the same nutrients are taken from the same depth, so **soil fertility falls**; soil structure may decline and erosion increase.

## Intercropping and mixed cropping

Common examples are maize with beans, cowpeas or pumpkins, and sorghum with cowpeas.

**Advantages:**

1. **Higher total output** from the same land, because the crops use space, light and nutrients differently.
2. A **legume** in the mixture fixes nitrogen that helps the cereal.
3. Better **ground cover** reduces erosion and weeds.
4. **Less risk**: if one crop fails, the other may still yield.
5. Some pests spread less easily through mixed crops.

**Disadvantages:** crops **compete** for water, light and nutrients, so each yields less than when grown alone; mechanisation, spraying and harvesting are harder; each crop may need different fertiliser or spraying.

Intercropping, with definite rows, is easier to manage than mixed cropping, where crops are scattered.

:::worked-solution[Comparing the output of maize alone and a maize-bean intercrop]
On 1 ha a farmer harvests 4,000 kg of maize grown alone. On another hectare an intercrop gives 3,200 kg of maize and 500 kg of sugar beans. Assume maize sells at US\$0.30 per kg and sugar beans at US\$1.00 per kg.

1. Maize alone: $4\,000 \times 0.30 =$ US\$1,200.
2. Intercrop maize: $3\,200 \times 0.30 =$ US\$960.
3. Intercrop beans: $500 \times 1.00 =$ US\$500.
4. Intercrop total: US\$960 + US\$500 = US\$1,460.
5. Difference: US\$1,460 − US\$1,200 = US\$260 more from the intercrop, although its maize yield is lower, because the beans add a second, valuable product.
:::

## Subsistence and commercial farming

| Feature | Subsistence | Commercial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main aim | Feed the family | Sell for profit |
| Scale | Small plots | Small to very large |
| Inputs | Family labour, few purchased inputs | Hired labour, machinery, purchased inputs |
| Enterprises | Many food crops and a few animals | Often one or a few specialised enterprises |
| Yields | Usually low | Usually higher |

Many Zimbabwean smallholders are **semi-commercial**: they feed the family and sell a surplus, or grow a cash crop such as cotton or tobacco.

## Intensive and extensive farming

**Intensive** systems include irrigated horticulture, broiler and layer houses, piggeries and dairy units. They give high output per hectare and suit small areas near markets, but need much capital, management skill and inputs, and they can pollute if wastes are not managed.

**Extensive** systems include cattle and game ranching on large areas of veld. They need little capital and labour per hectare and suit dry regions, but output per hectare is low and large areas of land are needed.

:::worked-solution[Output per hectare: intensive versus extensive]
Farm A irrigates 4 ha and harvests 36,000 kg of maize. Farm B grows rain-fed maize on 20 ha with few inputs and harvests 24,000 kg.

1. Farm A yield $= 36\,000 \div 4 = 9\,000\ \text{kg/ha}$ (9 t/ha).
2. Farm B yield $= 24\,000 \div 20 = 1\,200\ \text{kg/ha}$ (1.2 t/ha).
3. Farm B produces more maize **in total**, but Farm A produces $9\,000 \div 1\,200 = 7.5$ times as much **per hectare**: Farm A is intensive, Farm B extensive.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing a system for a small farm"}
Tatenda has 3 ha near Marondera, two oxen, a family that can work, and no money for a tractor. A **mixed farming** system suits him: maize intercropped with beans, a groundnut plot in rotation, and cattle that provide draught power and manure. It spreads his risk, keeps his soil fertile without much bought fertiliser and feeds the family, with a surplus to sell.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing intercropping with crop rotation"}
Intercropping grows crops **at the same time** on the same land; rotation grows different crops **one after another** in successive seasons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying intensive farming means a large farm"}
Intensive refers to **high inputs and output per hectare**, not to size. A 1 ha irrigated garden is intensive; a 5,000 ha ranch is extensive.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving advantages without explaining them"}
"Mixed farming is good" earns nothing. Say *why*: "manure from the cattle maintains soil fertility, reducing the cost of fertiliser".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**State** two advantages" needs two distinct points; "**explain**" needs the point and its reason. In "**compare**" questions, cover the same feature for both systems in one sentence.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question describes a farm, identify the system from the clues: crops and animals linked (mixed), one crop only (monoculture), crops in alternate rows (intercropping), mainly for the family (subsistence), high inputs on a small area (intensive).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mixed farming links crops and livestock: residues feed animals, manure and draught power serve crops, and risk is spread.
- Monoculture allows specialisation and mechanisation but builds up pests and diseases, lowers fertility and concentrates risk.
- Intercropping and mixed cropping raise total output and cover, and legumes add nitrogen, but crops compete and mechanisation is harder.
- Subsistence farming feeds the family; commercial farming sells for profit.
- Intensive farming uses high inputs for high output per hectare; extensive farming uses large areas with low inputs.
:::
