## Introduction

Farai's grandfather grew maize on the same field near Murehwa for twenty years. Each season the yield fell, witchweed spread and more fertiliser was needed to get a crop at all. His neighbour, who moved maize, groundnuts and sunflower around her fields in turn and rested one field under grass, kept her yields up with less fertiliser.

Growing the same crop on the same land year after year is called **monoculture**. **Crop rotation** is the planned alternative. ZIMSEC asks you in Form 3 to outline the principles, design a four-course rotation and discuss its advantages; Cambridge includes rotations among the agricultural systems and practices of land use.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Crop rotation"}
Growing different crops one after another on the same piece of land in a planned, repeated sequence.
:::

:::definition{term="Course"}
One of the crops (or the ley or fallow) in a rotation, and the piece of land on which it is grown in a given year. A four-course rotation has four courses and takes four years to complete.
:::

:::definition{term="Ley"}
Land sown to grass (sometimes with legumes) for one or more years as part of a rotation, usually grazed or cut for hay, before it is ploughed for crops again.
:::

:::definition{term="Heavy feeder"}
A crop that removes large amounts of nutrients, especially nitrogen, from the soil, for example maize, tobacco and cabbage.
:::

## Principles of crop rotation

1. **Alternate deep-rooted and shallow-rooted crops.** Crops with deep taproots (cotton, sunflower, legumes such as soya beans) take water and nutrients from deeper layers and open up the subsoil; crops with shallow fibrous roots (cereals such as maize and wheat) feed mainly in the topsoil. Alternating them uses the whole soil profile and improves structure.
2. **Follow a heavy feeder with a legume, and plant the heavy feeder after the legume.** Legumes fix nitrogen with *Rhizobium*; their residues release it to the next crop. Maize or tobacco following a legume needs less nitrogen fertiliser.
3. **Grow crops of different families in turn.** Many pests, diseases and parasitic weeds attack only one family. A crop from another family gives them no host, so their numbers fall. For example, witchweed attacks maize, sorghum and millets (the grass family) but not groundnuts.
4. **Include a ley or fallow.** A grass ley rests the land, builds organic matter and soil structure through its dense fibrous roots, protects the soil from erosion and provides grazing.
5. **Give manure to the most demanding crop.** Manure is applied before the heavy feeder that benefits most.
6. **Alternate crops that are hard to keep clean with those that suppress weeds** or allow easy weeding.
7. **Suit the farm**: choose crops with a market, that fit the climate and natural region, and that spread labour and income through the year.

## Designing a four-course rotation

Divide the arable land into four equal fields (courses). In year 1 each field carries a different crop; each year every crop moves one field along, so after four years each field has carried every crop once.

| Field | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Maize (manure + fertiliser) | Soya beans | Sunflower | Grass ley |
| B | Grass ley | Maize (manure + fertiliser) | Soya beans | Sunflower |
| C | Sunflower | Grass ley | Maize (manure + fertiliser) | Soya beans |
| D | Soya beans | Sunflower | Grass ley | Maize (manure + fertiliser) |

![Four-course rotation on 12 ha: four fields of 3 ha carry maize, soya beans, sunflower and grass ley, shown for year 1 and year 2 as each crop moves one field along; on every field the order is maize, soya beans, sunflower, ley, and the maize field gets 30 t of manure a year.](media:diagrams/four-course-rotation-plan.png "Figure: a four-course rotation on four 3 ha fields, years 1 and 2, and the order of crops on each field")

How this plan follows the principles:

| Course | Family | Roots | Reason for its place |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maize | Grass (cereal) | Fibrous, mainly in topsoil | Heavy feeder; gets the manure and fertiliser; follows the ley, which leaves organic matter and good structure |
| Soya beans | Legume | Taproot, deeper | Fixes nitrogen; breaks cereal pest and disease cycles |
| Sunflower | Daisy family | Deep taproot | Uses nutrients from the subsoil; another family breaks pest cycles |
| Grass ley | Grass | Dense fibrous mat | Rests the soil, adds organic matter, controls erosion; grazed by cattle, which add manure |

Some farmers replace the ley with a bare or weedy **fallow**, which rests the land but gives less protection and no grazing, or use groundnuts instead of soya beans and cotton instead of sunflower. Note that maize follows a grass ley: grass-family pests can carry over, so a legume before maize is also a sound design.

:::worked-solution[Areas, manure and nitrogen in Tendai's rotation]
Tendai has 12 ha of arable land in a four-course rotation. He applies manure at 10 t/ha to the maize course. Assume the maize after soya needs 40 kg/ha less nitrogen than continuous maize and that ammonium nitrate contains 34.5% N, in 50 kg bags.

1. Area per course = 12 ÷ 4 = **3 ha**.
2. Manure for the maize course = 10 t/ha × 3 ha = **30 t** each year.
3. Ammonium nitrate saved per ha = 40 ÷ 0.345 = 115.9 kg/ha.
4. For 3 ha: 115.9 × 3 = 347.8 kg, which is 347.8 ÷ 50 = 6.96, almost **7 bags** saved every year.
:::

## Advantages and limitations

| Advantages | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Maintains soil fertility; legumes add nitrogen, so less fertiliser is bought | Needs careful planning, records and knowledge of each crop |
| Breaks pest, disease and parasitic-weed cycles | The farm must be divided into several fields of similar size and soil |
| Improves soil structure and organic matter; leys and cover reduce erosion | Some courses (ley, legumes) may earn less than the main cash crop |
| Spreads labour and income through the year | Different crops need different implements, inputs and skills |
| Several crops reduce the risk of total failure from drought, pests or low prices | A market is needed for every crop in the rotation |
| Uses nutrients and water from different depths | Less suited to very small farms or where land is scarce |

:::example{title="Fixing a monoculture problem"}
Nyasha's maize fields near Masvingo have heavy witchweed and grey leaf spot and her yields are falling. Her first plan, rotating maize with groundnuts and sorghum, is not ideal, because sorghum, like maize, is a host of witchweed. Her extension officer suggests maize, groundnuts, sunflower and cotton instead. Groundnuts, sunflower and cotton are non-hosts: they cannot support witchweed, so its seed bank in the soil declines, grey leaf spot spores on old maize residues have no host for three years, and the groundnuts supply nitrogen for the next maize crop.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting two cereals one after the other"}
Maize followed by sorghum or wheat keeps the same family in the field, so stalk borer, witchweed and cereal diseases carry over.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying rotation adds nitrogen by itself"}
Only the legume course fixes nitrogen. Rotation without a legume does not add nitrogen, although it still helps with pests and structure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Design a rotation"}
Draw a table of fields against years, move each crop one field along each year and label the families and root types. Then justify each position with a principle.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss rotation"}
"Discuss" needs both sides: give advantages with reasons and at least two limitations, then conclude whether rotation suits the farm described.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Crop rotation grows different crops in a planned sequence on the same land; monoculture repeats one crop.
- Principles: alternate deep and shallow roots, put legumes with heavy feeders, change families, include a ley or fallow, manure the heavy feeder, and suit markets and labour.
- A four-course rotation divides the land into four equal fields; each crop moves one field along each year.
- Advantages: fertility, pest and disease control, structure, less erosion, spread of labour, income and risk.
- Limitations: planning, divided land, lower-value courses, varied inputs and skills, and markets for every crop.
:::
