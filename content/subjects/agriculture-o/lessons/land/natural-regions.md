## Introduction

Farai farms near Marondera and plants maize, tobacco and soya beans every season. His cousin Sipho runs cattle on a large ranch south of Masvingo and grows only a small plot of sorghum. Both are good farmers. They farm differently because the **rain, temperature and soils** of their areas are different.

Zimbabwe is divided into **five natural farming regions** (I to V, with region II split into IIa and IIb). Each region has a typical amount and reliability of rainfall, and each suits a particular **farming system**. Knowing the regions lets you explain why tea grows at Chipinge but not at Chiredzi, why small grains are advised in dry areas, and why ranching dominates the Lowveld.

This topic is examined in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (objective items) and in Paper 2, often as a short table of rainfall data or a farm situation followed by "state", "describe" and "suggest" parts.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural farming region"}
An area of the country with broadly similar rainfall amount and reliability, temperature and soils, and therefore suited to a similar type of farming.
:::

:::definition{term="Farming system"}
The way a farm is organised: which crops and animals are kept, how intensively the land is used and what the produce is for.
:::

:::definition{term="Intensive farming"}
Farming that uses a lot of inputs (fertiliser, labour, machinery, irrigation, improved seed) on a small area to get high yields per hectare.
:::

:::definition{term="Extensive farming"}
Farming that uses large areas of land with few inputs per hectare, so output per hectare is low; ranching cattle or game on large areas of natural veld is an example.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainfall reliability"}
How likely an area is to receive enough rain, at the right time, season after season. Reliable rain varies little from year to year and has few long dry spells.
:::

## Why the country is divided into regions

The regions were drawn mainly from **rainfall** (how much falls and how reliable it is), helped by **temperature** (which depends largely on altitude) and **soils**. Rainfall in Zimbabwe generally decreases from the north-east and east towards the south, south-east and west, and it becomes less reliable as the total falls.

Dividing the country into regions is useful because it:

1. **guides farmers** to choose crops and livestock that suit their area, reducing the risk of crop failure;
2. helps **AGRITEX extension officers** give advice that fits the local conditions;
3. helps government and planners decide on **land use, resettlement and irrigation** projects;
4. helps **banks and insurers** judge the risk of lending for, or insuring, a crop;
5. helps **researchers and seed breeders** develop varieties and practices for each region.

## The five regions

| Region | Approximate mean annual rainfall | Location (examples) | Climate notes | Farming system |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | More than about 1,000 mm | Eastern Highlands (Nyanga, Chimanimani, Chipinge highlands) | High altitude, cool; some rain falls in most months | Specialised and diversified: plantation forestry, tea, coffee, deciduous fruit, intensive dairy and beef |
| IIa | About 750-1,000 mm | Northern and north-eastern highveld (Mashonaland) | Reliable rain; dry spells uncommon | Intensive crop and livestock farming |
| IIb | About 750-1,000 mm | Northern and north-eastern highveld | Less reliable; more severe dry spells in the rainy season | Intensive farming, with more risk than IIa |
| III | About 650-800 mm | Much of the Midlands | Fairly high temperatures; mid-season dry spells are common | Semi-intensive: livestock with fodder crops, plus drought-tolerant cash crops |
| IV | About 450-650 mm | Large parts of Matabeleland and Masvingo | Low rain, periodic seasonal droughts and severe dry spells | Semi-extensive: livestock and drought-tolerant crops (sorghum, millets) |
| V | Lower and more erratic than region IV | The Lowveld (south-east) and the Zambezi valley | Hot; too dry and unreliable for most rain-fed crops | Extensive cattle and game ranching; irrigated sugar cane in the Lowveld |

:::note{title="About the numbers"}
Textbooks give slightly different rainfall boundaries for the regions, and the ranges overlap. Learn the order (I wettest, V driest) and the approximate values; examiners credit reasonable figures.
:::

### Region I: specialised and diversified farming

High altitude keeps temperatures low and rain falls even outside the main season. This suits crops that need a cool, moist climate all year: **tea and coffee** (around Chipinge), **pine and eucalyptus plantations** (Nyanga and Chimanimani), and **apples and other deciduous fruit**. Lush pastures support **dairy** cattle. Steep slopes mean soil conservation is very important.

### Region II: intensive crop and livestock farming

Region II is the country's main **crop-growing** area. Good rain and fertile soils allow **maize, tobacco, cotton, soya beans** and **groundnuts** in summer, and **wheat** in winter under irrigation. Farmers also keep **beef and dairy cattle, pigs and poultry**, often fed on crop products. IIa has more reliable rain than IIb, so IIb farmers face more yield losses from dry spells.

### Region III: semi-intensive farming

Rain is moderate but **mid-season dry spells** often damage maize at critical stages such as tasselling. Farming is based mainly on **livestock**, supported by fodder crops, with cash crops that tolerate dry spells (for example **cotton**, **sorghum** and early-maturing maize varieties).

### Region IV: semi-extensive farming

Rainfall is low and droughts are frequent, so rain-fed maize often fails. Farmers should rely on **livestock** (cattle, goats) and on **drought-tolerant crops** such as **sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet**. Water harvesting and conservation tillage reduce risk.

### Region V: extensive farming

Rain is too low and erratic for reliable crop production. The land is best used for **extensive cattle ranching and game ranching** (wildlife tourism and hunting). Where rivers and dams supply water, **irrigated sugar cane** is grown in the Lowveld.

:::example{title="Advising a farmer in region IV"}
Chipo farms 6 ha in a dry part of Masvingo province, in region IV. For three seasons her rain-fed maize has wilted during January dry spells.

**Advice:** replace most of the maize with **sorghum or pearl millet**, which tolerate drought; keep **goats and cattle**, which turn veld grazing into income; harvest water with **tied ridges or potholes**; and grow a small plot of maize only on the best, moisture-holding soil. This matches the semi-extensive system of region IV.
:::

:::worked-solution[Placing a farm in a region from rainfall records]
A farm on the Mashonaland highveld recorded this rainfall over six seasons.

| Season | Rainfall (mm) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 812 |
| 2 | 905 |
| 3 | 768 |
| 4 | 870 |
| 5 | 694 |
| 6 | 931 |

1. Add the six totals: $812 + 905 + 768 + 870 + 694 + 931 = 4\,980\ \text{mm}$.
2. Mean seasonal rainfall $= 4\,980 \div 6 = 830\ \text{mm}$.
3. 830 mm lies within the region II range (about 750-1,000 mm).
4. Five of the six seasons had at least 750 mm; one season (694 mm) fell short. Occasional dry seasons like this suggest the less reliable sub-region, **IIb**, rather than IIa.
5. Suitable system: intensive crop and livestock farming, with drought-tolerant varieties or supplementary irrigation to reduce the risk in dry seasons.
:::

## Matching enterprises to regions

A useful rule: **the drier the region, the more the farm should depend on livestock and drought-tolerant crops, and the less on rain-fed maize.** Crops can be grown outside their "best" region only if the farmer changes the conditions, for example by irrigating (wheat in winter, sugar cane in the Lowveld).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the order of the regions"}
Region I is the wettest and region V the driest. Rainfall decreases as the region number increases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting sugar cane in region I or II"}
Sugar cane is grown mainly in the hot **Lowveld (region V)**, and only under **irrigation**. Rain-fed crops in region V usually fail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying region IV is 'no good for farming'"}
Region IV suits **livestock and small grains**. It is unsuited to intensive rain-fed maize, not to farming in general.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting reliability"}
Two areas with the same average rainfall can differ in reliability. Region IIb has a similar total to IIa but more severe dry spells.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**state**" or "**name**", one word or phrase per mark is enough: "region V: extensive ranching". For "**describe**" the farming system, give the enterprises and how intensive the farming is. For "**explain**", link the climate to the choice: "sorghum is grown in region IV *because* it tolerates drought and low rainfall".
:::

:::exam-tip
In a "**suggest**" question about a farm in a dry region, give practical choices (small grains, goats, water harvesting) and justify each with the region's rainfall. Use approximate rainfall figures; do not argue over exact boundaries.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe has five natural farming regions (I to V, with IIa and IIb), drawn mainly from rainfall amount and reliability, with temperature and soils.
- Rainfall and reliability fall from region I (more than about 1,000 mm, Eastern Highlands) to region V (low, erratic rain in the Lowveld and Zambezi valley).
- Systems: I specialised and diversified; II intensive crop and livestock; III semi-intensive; IV semi-extensive; V extensive ranching.
- The regions guide farmers, extension officers, planners, lenders and researchers.
- Crops can be grown outside their best region only by changing conditions, for example by irrigation.
:::
