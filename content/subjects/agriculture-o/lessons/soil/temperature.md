## Introduction

In October the surface of a bare, dark seedbed near Masvingo can become too hot to touch by early afternoon, yet in June a vlei garden near Marondera may be white with frost at dawn. Both extremes harm crops. Seeds in a cold soil lie for weeks without germinating; seedlings in a scorching soil can be killed where their stems meet the ground; and frost can blacken a field of tomatoes overnight.

Farmers cannot control the weather, but they can change how hot or cold their soil becomes. This topic explains why soil temperature matters, what controls it and how to reduce its extremes. Examination questions ask you to *explain* the effect of soil temperature on germination or on soil organisms, to *interpret* a table of soil temperatures and to *describe* methods such as mulching and shading.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil temperature"}
How hot or cold the soil is at a stated depth, measured with a soil thermometer. It changes through the day and the year, most strongly near the surface.
:::

:::definition{term="Optimum temperature"}
The temperature at which a process such as germination or root growth happens fastest. Above or below it the process slows down.
:::

:::definition{term="Frost"}
Ice crystals that form on plants and the soil surface when the temperature at ground level falls to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ or below, usually on clear, still winter nights.
:::

:::definition{term="Mulch"}
A layer of material, such as dry grass, crop residues or plastic sheeting, spread on the soil surface to protect it.
:::

## How soil temperature affects crops and soil life

### Germination

Germination is controlled by enzymes, which work fastest at an optimum temperature. Each crop has a minimum below which its seeds hardly germinate, an optimum, and a maximum above which the embryo is damaged. Warm-season crops such as maize, groundnuts and pumpkins germinate poorly in cold soil (maize slows sharply below about $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$), which is one reason why they are planted when the rains start in warm soil. Wheat and peas, grown in the cool dry season, germinate at lower temperatures.

### Root growth and nutrient uptake

Roots grow and absorb water and nutrients fastest in warm, moist soil. In cold soil, root respiration is slow, so active uptake of mineral salts slows down; young plants may show nutrient deficiency signs even when nutrients are present. Very hot soil damages young roots near the surface.

### Soil organisms

Bacteria, fungi and earthworms are most active in warm, moist soil. Decomposition of organic matter, release of nutrients and nitrification all speed up as the soil warms and almost stop in cold soil. Extreme heat at the surface kills organisms or drives earthworms deeper.

### Dangers of extreme temperatures

- **Excessive heat:** a bare, dry surface can become much hotter than the air. Young seedlings can be scorched at soil level (the stem is damaged where it touches the hot soil), and the soil dries out quickly, so germinating seeds die.
- **Frost:** water inside plant cells freezes, ice crystals damage the cells and the leaves blacken and die. Tender crops such as tomatoes, potatoes, beans, pumpkins and bananas are at risk in winter, especially in low-lying areas where cold air settles (frost pockets).

## What makes a soil warm or cool?

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Colour | Dark soils absorb more of the sun's energy and warm faster than pale soils |
| Moisture | Wet soils warm slowly, because water needs a lot of heat to warm up and evaporation cools the surface |
| Texture | Sandy soils hold less water and warm quickly; clay soils warm slowly |
| Cover | Vegetation and mulch shade the surface and reduce both heating by day and cooling at night |
| Slope and aspect | In the southern hemisphere, north-facing slopes receive more sun and are warmer than south-facing slopes |
| Organic matter | Darkens the soil and holds water, so its effect depends on which dominates |

## Modifying soil temperature

1. **Mulching.** Grass, stover or leaves shade the surface by day and slow heat loss at night, so the soil stays cooler in hot weather and warmer on cold nights. Mulch also saves water. Black plastic sheeting warms soil; pale or reflective materials keep it cooler.
2. **Shading.** Nursery beds and young seedlings are covered with grass shades, shade cloth or branches on a frame to protect them from scorching sun.
3. **Watering (irrigation).** Moist soil heats more slowly, and evaporation cools the surface. Watering before a frosty night helps, because moist soil stores more heat and releases it during the night.
4. **Ridging.** Ridges expose more surface to the sun and drain better, so they warm faster after cold or wet periods; seeds on ridges germinate earlier.
5. **Choosing the time and place.** Plant warm-season crops when the soil has warmed, avoid frost pockets for tender crops in winter, and use windbreaks and north-facing slopes for early crops.

:::worked-solution[Interpreting soil temperature readings]
Thandeka measured soil temperature at $5\ \text{cm}$ depth under bare soil and under a grass mulch on a hot October day.

| Time | 06:00 | 10:00 | 14:00 | 18:00 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bare soil (°C) | 12 | 24 | 41 | 27 |
| Mulched soil (°C) | 15 | 21 | 29 | 24 |

1. Range under bare soil $= 41 - 12 = 29\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. Range under mulch $= 29 - 15 = 14\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. The mulch reduced the daily range by $29 - 14 = 15\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
4. At 14:00 the mulched soil was $41 - 29 = 12\ ^\circ\text{C}$ cooler; at 06:00 it was $15 - 12 = 3\ ^\circ\text{C}$ warmer.
5. Conclusion: the mulch shaded the soil by day and reduced heat loss at night, so it **reduced the extremes**.
:::

:::example{title="Protecting a winter tomato crop"}
Sipho grows tomatoes in a garden at the bottom of a valley near Chinhoyi. Frost is forecast in July.

1. Cold air drains downhill and collects in the valley, so his garden is a frost pocket; next season he should plant on higher, sloping ground.
2. He waters the beds in the afternoon so the moist soil stores heat and releases it during the night.
3. He covers the young plants with grass or a light cover overnight to trap heat near the ground.
4. In future he can time planting so that tender crops are not in the field during the coldest weeks.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying mulch always warms the soil"}
An organic mulch **reduces extremes**: cooler in the heat of the day, warmer at night. Only dark plastic mulch is used mainly to warm soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the organisms"}
Soil temperature affects more than the crop. Decomposition, nitrification and nutrient release all depend on soil organisms, which slow down in cold soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Muddling aspect"}
In Zimbabwe (southern hemisphere) the sun is to the north for most of the year, so **north**-facing slopes are warmer.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain questions"}
Link temperature to a process: "Warm soil speeds up enzyme activity, so seeds germinate faster." A bare statement such as "warm soil is good" earns little.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Data questions"}
When you compare two treatments, quote figures with units, calculate the range (maximum − minimum) and state which treatment reduced the extremes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Soil temperature controls germination, root growth, nutrient uptake and the activity of soil organisms.
- Excessive heat scorches seedlings and dries the soil; frost kills tender crops, especially in frost pockets.
- Dark, dry, sandy, bare and north-facing soils warm fastest.
- Mulching, shading, watering, ridging and good timing reduce temperature extremes.
- In data questions, compare ranges (maximum − minimum) and quote figures.
:::
