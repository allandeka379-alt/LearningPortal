## Introduction

Ask a farmer in Gokwe about her fields and she will tell you which ones are "heavy" and which are "light". The light, sandy field near the homestead is easy to plough and warms up early, but the maize wilts a few days after the rain stops. The heavy, dark clay in the vlei holds water for weeks, but it is too sticky to plough after a storm and too hard to dig in the dry season. Between them, a brown loam field gives good crops in most seasons.

Knowing the soil type helps a farmer choose crops, decide how often to irrigate, plan fertiliser use and time cultivation. In the examination you may be asked to *compare* the properties of the three soils, to *describe* an experiment that compares them, to interpret its results, and to *suggest* how to improve a sandy or a clay soil.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sandy soil"}
A soil in which sand particles make up most of the mineral matter (roughly 70% or more), with little clay. It feels gritty and does not stick together when moist.
:::

:::definition{term="Clay soil"}
A soil with a high proportion of clay particles (roughly 40% or more). It is sticky and plastic when wet and hard when dry.
:::

:::definition{term="Loam soil"}
A soil with a balanced mixture of sand, silt and clay, usually with a good amount of humus, so that it combines the good properties of sandy and clay soils.
:::

:::definition{term="Water-holding capacity"}
The amount of water a soil can hold against the pull of gravity after excess water has drained away.
:::

:::definition{term="Aeration"}
The supply of air (especially oxygen) to the pore spaces of a soil, which roots and soil organisms need for respiration.
:::

## How the three soils compare

The differences come from particle size. Sand particles are large, so the pores between them are large but few in number and the total surface area is small. Clay particles are tiny, so the pores are very small, the total surface area is enormous and the particles hold water and nutrients on their surfaces.

| Property | Sandy soil | Loam soil | Clay soil |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Feel | Gritty, loose | Slightly gritty, crumbly | Sticky and plastic when wet |
| Pore size | Large | Mixed large and small | Very small |
| Drainage | Very fast | Good | Slow; waterlogs easily |
| Aeration | Very good | Good | Poor when wet |
| Water-holding capacity | Low | Medium to high | High (but some held too tightly for plants) |
| Capillary rise | Low, fast | Medium | Highest, but slow |
| Nutrient content and retention | Low; nutrients leach easily | Good | High; nutrients held on clay surfaces |
| Workability | Easy ("light") at most moisture levels | Easy over a wide range of moisture | Difficult ("heavy"): sticky when wet, hard and cracked when dry |
| Warming in spring | Warms quickly | Medium | Warms slowly (holds much water) |
| Erosion | Wind erosion when dry; low cohesion | Moderate | Resists wind but suffers sheet erosion and runoff when sealed |

Loam is the best all-round soil for most crops because its mixture of pore sizes gives both drainage and water storage, and it is fertile and easy to work.

## Comparing the soils by experiment

### Drainage and water-holding

1. Place a plug of cotton wool in the neck of three equal funnels and stand each funnel on a measuring cylinder.
2. Put the same mass of dry sandy, loam and clay soil into the funnels.
3. Pour the same volume of water onto each soil at the same time.
4. After a fixed time (for example 30 minutes), read the volume of water collected in each cylinder.
5. Water retained = water poured − water collected. Note also how fast water first drips through.

Keep everything except the soil type the same (mass of soil, volume of water, time, funnel size) so that the test is fair.

:::worked-solution[Interpreting a drainage experiment]
Farai pours $200\ \text{mL}$ of water onto each soil and records the water collected after 30 minutes.

| Soil | Water poured (mL) | Water collected (mL) | Water retained (mL) | Retained (%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sandy soil | 200 | 150 | 50 | 25 |
| Loam soil | 200 | 110 | 90 | 45 |
| Clay soil | 200 | 64 | 136 | 68 |

1. Water retained by the sandy soil: $200 - 150 = 50\ \text{mL}$; as a percentage, $\frac{50}{200} \times 100 = 25\%$.
2. Clay soil: $200 - 64 = 136\ \text{mL}$, which is $\frac{136}{200} \times 100 = 68\%$.
3. Conclusion: the sandy soil drains fastest and holds least water; the clay soil drains slowest and holds most.
4. Explanation: sand has large pores and a small surface area; clay has tiny pores and a very large surface area that holds water.
:::

### Capillary rise

Fill three long glass tubes, each closed at the bottom with muslin, with the three soils and stand them in a tray of water. Mark the height of the wet soil at intervals. Water rises fastest at first in sand, but it eventually rises **highest in clay**, because the narrow pores between clay particles act as very fine capillary tubes.

### Porosity and air content

Add a measured volume of water slowly to a measured volume of dry soil in a cylinder. The water that fits in without the level rising above the soil shows the air space. Sandy soils have large individual pores, but clay soils have more total pore space in many tiny pores; when wet, clay pores are full of water, which is why clay soils are poorly aerated.

## Improving sandy and clay soils

:::example{title="Improving a sandy garden at a school in Hwange"}
The garden soil is pale and sandy, and vegetables wilt between waterings.

1. Dig in **well-rotted manure or compost** every season: humus holds water and nutrients and binds sand grains into crumbs.
2. Cover beds with a **mulch** of dry grass to cut evaporation and keep the surface cool.
3. Mix in **anthill (termite-mound) soil**, which is richer in clay, to raise water and nutrient holding.
4. Apply fertiliser in **small, split doses** so less is lost by leaching.
5. Grow **green manure** or cover crops and return the residues.
:::

:::example{title="Improving a clay field near Gweru"}
The field is sticky and waterlogged after rain and sets hard in the dry season.

1. Add **organic matter** (manure, compost, crop residues) to open up the soil into crumbs and improve drainage and aeration.
2. Apply **lime** where the soil is acid: calcium helps clay particles to flocculate into crumbs.
3. Improve **drainage** with ridges, beds or drains so roots get oxygen.
4. **Cultivate at the right moisture**, never when wet, to avoid smearing and clods.
5. Grow deep-rooted crops or grass leys whose roots open channels.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying clay has no air"}
Clay soils have a large total pore space, but the pores are tiny and stay full of water, so aeration is poor **when wet**. When they dry, they crack.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a clay soil gives plants the most water"}
Clay holds the most water, but some of it is held so tightly that roots cannot take it up. The water **available** to plants is often greatest in a loam.
:::

:::mistake{title="An unfair experiment"}
If the soils have different masses, or water is poured at different times, the comparison is not valid. Name the variables you keep the same.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Comparing soils"}
When asked to *compare*, write matched statements: "Sandy soil drains quickly, **whereas** clay soil drains slowly." A list of sandy properties followed by a separate list of clay properties may lose marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Experiment questions"}
Give numbered steps, the variables kept constant, what is measured, and the expected result with an explanation linked to particle and pore size. Always subtract to find water retained: retained = poured − collected.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sandy soils: large pores, fast drainage, good aeration, low water and nutrient holding, light to work, warm quickly.
- Clay soils: tiny pores, slow drainage, poor aeration when wet, high water and nutrient holding, heavy to work, warm slowly.
- Loam: a balance of sand, silt, clay and humus; the best all-round soil for crops.
- Funnel, capillary-tube and porosity experiments compare the soils; keep all other variables the same.
- Improve sand with organic matter, mulch and anthill soil; improve clay with organic matter, lime and drainage.
:::
