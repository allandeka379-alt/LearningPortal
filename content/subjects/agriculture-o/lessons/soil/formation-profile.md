## Introduction

Walk from the granite hills near Marondera to the dark plains south of Gweru and the soil under your feet changes colour, feel and depth. On the granite you find pale, sandy soils that dry out quickly; on the plains formed from basalt or dolerite you find heavier red or black clays. The difference starts with the rock underneath and with the slow processes that turn hard rock into a loose material in which crops can grow.

Every soil question in the examination builds on this topic: texture, soil types, drainage and erosion all depend on how soil forms and how deep it is. Examiners often ask you to *describe* a type of weathering with an example, to *state* the factors of soil formation, or to *describe* a soil profile and *explain* its importance to a crop.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil"}
The loose upper layer of the earth's surface, made of weathered rock particles, organic matter, water, air and living organisms, in which plants can grow.
:::

:::definition{term="Weathering"}
The breaking down of rock into smaller particles where it lies, by physical, chemical or biological processes. Unlike erosion, weathering does not carry the particles away.
:::

:::definition{term="Parent material"}
The rock or deposited material from which a soil develops. It strongly influences the soil's texture, colour and mineral content.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil profile"}
A vertical section through the soil from the surface down to the parent rock, showing the different layers (horizons).
:::

:::definition{term="Horizon"}
A layer in the soil profile that differs from the layers above and below it in colour, texture, structure or organic matter.
:::

## How rock becomes soil: weathering

Weathering acts on the rock surface for hundreds or thousands of years. Three groups of processes work together.

### Physical (mechanical) weathering

Physical weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces **without changing its chemical make-up**.

- **Temperature changes.** In much of Zimbabwe, rock surfaces heat strongly by day and cool at night. The outer layer expands and contracts more than the inside, so thin sheets peel off (**exfoliation**), as seen on granite domes (dwalas).
- **Unequal expansion of minerals.** Granite contains several minerals that expand by different amounts when heated, so the grains loosen and the rock crumbles into coarse sand.
- **Water and wind.** Running water rolls stones together and wears them down (abrasion); wind carrying sand blasts rock surfaces.
- **Freezing and thawing.** Water in cracks expands when it freezes and widens the cracks. This matters on high, cold ground but is less important in most of Zimbabwe.

### Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering **changes the minerals** in the rock into new substances, often softer or soluble. Warmth and moisture speed it up.

- **Hydrolysis.** Water reacts with minerals such as feldspar, slowly changing them into clay.
- **Oxidation.** Oxygen reacts with iron in minerals, forming iron oxides that give many soils their red or yellow-brown colour and weaken the rock.
- **Carbonation.** Rainwater dissolves carbon dioxide and forms a weak acid, carbonic acid, which dissolves limestone and other carbonate rocks.
- **Solution and hydration.** Some minerals dissolve in water; others absorb water, swell and break apart.

### Biological weathering

Living things break rock down physically and chemically.

- **Plant roots** grow into cracks and widen them as they thicken.
- **Lichens and mosses** on bare rock produce weak acids that attack the surface; when they die, their remains add the first organic matter.
- **Animals** such as termites, earthworms and burrowing rodents mix particles, bring material to the surface and open channels for air and water.
- **Micro-organisms** decompose dead material, releasing carbon dioxide and organic acids that speed up chemical weathering.
- **People** break rock by quarrying, road building and cultivation.

:::example{title="Weathering on a granite kopje"}
On a granite kopje near Masvingo, Tatenda's class finds: (1) curved sheets of rock peeling off the bare dome, (2) a fig tree whose roots have split a boulder, (3) grey-green lichen patches on shaded rock, and (4) reddish crumbly material where the rock meets the soil.

1. The peeling sheets are **physical** weathering by daily heating and cooling (exfoliation).
2. The split boulder is **biological** weathering: roots widen the crack as they grow.
3. The lichen is **biological** weathering, mainly through the weak acids it produces (a chemical effect started by a living thing).
4. The reddish, crumbly material shows **chemical** weathering: iron has been oxidised and feldspar is changing to clay.
:::

## Factors that decide the kind of soil

Five factors control which soil forms in a place.

| Factor | How it affects the soil |
| --- | --- |
| Parent material | Sets the texture and minerals: granite gives coarse, sandy soils low in nutrients; basalt and dolerite give finer, red or dark clay soils richer in bases |
| Climate | Rainfall and temperature control the speed of chemical weathering and leaching; hot, wet areas form deep, leached soils |
| Living organisms | Vegetation supplies organic matter; organisms mix the soil and decompose residues |
| Topography (relief) | Steep slopes lose soil by erosion and have shallow soils; valley bottoms collect deep, often poorly drained soils |
| Time | Older soils are deeper and have clearer horizons; young soils are shallow and close to the parent material |

:::mistake{title="Confusing weathering with erosion"}
Weathering breaks rock down **where it is**. Erosion **removes and carries** the particles away by water or wind. A question about how soil *forms* wants weathering, not erosion.
:::

## The soil profile

When a pit is dug about one to two metres deep, its side wall shows layers called **horizons**. A simple profile has three.

| Horizon | Name | Appearance | Importance to crops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Topsoil | Usually darkest, because of humus; crumbly; many roots and organisms | Holds most of the nutrients and organic matter; where most feeding roots grow and where seeds germinate |
| B | Subsoil | Lighter or redder than topsoil; little humus; may be more clayey or compact, sometimes with a hardpan | Stores water and some nutrients for deep roots; a hardpan here restricts roots and drainage |
| C | Parent material | Partly weathered rock fragments | Source of new mineral particles; roots rarely reach it |

Below the C horizon lies solid **bedrock**. On undisturbed land a thin layer of fallen leaves and decaying litter may cover the topsoil.

### Reading a profile

- **Depth.** A deep profile gives roots more room and stores more water, so crops survive dry spells better.
- **Colour.** Dark topsoil shows humus. Red or brown subsoil shows good drainage and aeration (oxidised iron). Grey or mottled (spotted) subsoil shows that water stands in the soil for long periods: poor drainage.
- **Hard layers.** A plough pan or hardpan stops roots and water; it can be broken by deep ripping.
- **Stones.** Many stones near the surface mean a shallow, young soil, poor for root crops.

:::example{title="Choosing a site for a school orchard"}
Two pits are dug at a school near Mutare.

- **Pit 1:** dark topsoil 25 cm thick, red-brown subsoil to 120 cm, weathered rock below.
- **Pit 2:** topsoil 10 cm thick, grey subsoil with orange mottles from 30 cm, water seeping into the pit at 70 cm.

Pit 1 is the better site. Fruit trees are deep-rooted and need a deep, well-drained profile; the red-brown subsoil shows good aeration. The grey, mottled subsoil and seeping water in Pit 2 show waterlogging, which would starve tree roots of oxygen.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC uses topsoil, subsoil and parent material (horizons A, B and C). Cambridge describes the profile as topsoil, subsoil and underlying material. They mean the same layers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying physical weathering changes the minerals"}
Physical weathering only makes pieces smaller. Changing minerals into new substances (feldspar to clay, iron to iron oxide) is chemical weathering.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing roots only as physical weathering"}
Root action is classed as **biological** weathering because a living thing causes it, even though the effect is mechanical. Say so clearly if a question asks you to classify it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking all soils have the same horizons"}
Profiles differ: young soils on slopes may have almost no B horizon, and valley soils may be deep and grey. Describe the profile you are given.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe a type of weathering"}
For each type, *name* the process and give an *example* of how it acts: "Physical weathering: daily heating and cooling makes the outer rock expand and contract, so sheets peel off (exfoliation)." One mark is usually for the process and one for the example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Profile questions"}
When asked to *describe* a soil profile, go from the surface downwards and give each horizon's name, appearance and importance. When asked to *explain* why a profile suits a crop, link depth, colour and drainage to root growth, water supply and aeration.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Soil forms from parent material by physical (no chemical change), chemical (new minerals) and biological (living things) weathering.
- Hydrolysis, oxidation and carbonation are the main chemical processes; heating and cooling cause exfoliation.
- Parent material, climate, organisms, topography and time decide the kind of soil that forms.
- A profile has topsoil (A), subsoil (B) and parent material (C) above the bedrock.
- Deep, dark topsoil over red-brown subsoil suits crops; grey or mottled subsoil shows poor drainage.
:::
