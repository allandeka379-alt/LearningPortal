## Introduction

On many communal-area farms near Masvingo, the cattle kraal is the most valuable "fertiliser factory" on the homestead. Each dry season the dung that has built up in the kraal is carted to the fields, and many families also keep a compost heap beside the vegetable garden. These **organic manures** cost little cash, and they do something no bag of fertiliser can do: they add **organic matter** that improves the soil itself.

Both boards expect you to *describe* types of organic manure, *describe* how compost is made, *explain* how organic matter improves soil structure and fertility, *explain* the importance of legumes, and *compare* organic with inorganic fertilisers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organic manure"}
Material of plant or animal origin, such as animal dung, compost or ploughed-in green plants, added to the soil to supply nutrients and organic matter.
:::

:::definition{term="Farmyard (kraal) manure"}
A mixture of animal dung, urine and bedding (such as grass or stover) collected from livestock pens and kraals.
:::

:::definition{term="Compost"}
Dark, crumbly organic material produced when plant remains and other organic wastes are decomposed by micro-organisms in a heap or pit.
:::

:::definition{term="Green manure"}
A crop, usually a fast-growing legume, grown in order to be ploughed into the soil while still green, to add organic matter and nutrients.
:::

:::definition{term="Humus"}
The dark, stable, well-decomposed part of soil organic matter that holds water and nutrients.
:::

## Types of organic manure

- **Farmyard and kraal manure.** Cattle, goat, pig and poultry manure. Poultry manure is the richest in nitrogen; cattle manure is bulkier. Manure keeps its nutrients best when heaped, compacted and covered, because sun and rain cause nitrogen to be lost as ammonia gas and by leaching.
- **Compost.** Made from crop residues, weeds, kitchen waste, dung and ash.
- **Green manure.** Legumes such as sunn hemp and velvet bean are grown and ploughed in at about flowering, when they are succulent and rich in nutrients, so they rot quickly.
- **Crop residues.** Maize stover, groundnut haulms and other residues left as a mulch or worked into the soil. Woody, high-carbon residues such as maize stalks rot slowly and can **lock up** soil nitrogen for a while, because the microbes breaking them down use nitrogen themselves.

## How compost is made (heap method)

1. **Choose a site**: level, well drained, shaded and near water, close to where the compost will be used.
2. **Lay a base** of coarse material such as maize stalks or twigs, so air can enter from below.
3. **Build layers**: about 15-20 cm of plant material, then a thin layer of manure (to supply nitrogen and micro-organisms), then a sprinkling of topsoil (and wood ash if available). Repeat the layers to a height of about 1-1.5 m.
4. **Water each layer** so the heap is damp like a squeezed sponge, not soaking.
5. **Insert a stick or pole** through the heap to test it, and cover the heap with grass or soil to keep in moisture and heat.
6. **Check the temperature**: the pulled-out stick should feel hot after a few days. The heat (up to about 60 °C) comes from the respiration of micro-organisms and kills many weed seeds and pathogens.
7. **Turn the heap** after about three weeks, and again later, moving the outside material to the centre to let in air and give even decomposition.
8. Compost is **ready** in about two to three months, when it is dark, crumbly and smells earthy and the original materials can no longer be recognised.

In dry areas, compost is often made in a **pit**, which holds moisture better. Diseased plants, plastics, glass and metal are left out.

:::worked-solution[Manure needed for a school garden]
A school garden has six beds, each $10\ \text{m}$ long and $1.2\ \text{m}$ wide. Compost is applied at $5\ \text{kg/m}^2$ and carried in a wheelbarrow that holds about $60\ \text{kg}$.

1. Area of one bed $= 10 \times 1.2 = 12\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Total area $= 6 \times 12 = 72\ \text{m}^2$.
3. Compost needed $= 72 \times 5 = 360\ \text{kg}$.
4. Wheelbarrow loads $= 360 \div 60 = 6$ loads.
:::

## How organic matter improves soil

| Effect | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Better structure | Humus and microbial gums bind particles into crumbs; sandy soils hold together and clay soils become more open and easier to work |
| More water held | Humus holds several times its own mass of water, so crops survive dry spells longer |
| Nutrients held and supplied | Humus holds nutrient ions against leaching and releases N, P, S and trace elements as it decomposes |
| More soil life | Food for earthworms, bacteria and fungi, which aerate the soil and recycle nutrients |
| Steadier pH | Humus resists sudden changes in soil pH |
| Less erosion | Crumbs and surface residues resist rain splash and runoff |

## Legumes and soil fertility

Legumes such as groundnuts, soya beans, cowpeas, sunn hemp and velvet bean have **root nodules** containing *Rhizobium* bacteria. The bacteria **fix** nitrogen gas from the soil air into compounds the plant can use. When the roots, leaves and haulms rot, some of this nitrogen is left in the soil for the next crop. That is why a cereal such as maize often yields better after a legume in a **rotation**, and why legumes are the main green manure crops.

:::worked-solution[Nitrogen in a load of manure]
Assume kraal manure contains about 1.5% nitrogen and ammonium nitrate 34.5% N. A farmer applies $10\ \text{t}$ (that is $10\,000\ \text{kg}$) of manure to a hectare.

1. Nitrogen in the manure $= 10\,000 \times \frac{1.5}{100} = 150\ \text{kg N}$.
2. Mass of ammonium nitrate holding the same nitrogen $= 150 \div 0.345 = 434.8\ \text{kg}$.
3. Interpretation: the manure contains as much nitrogen as about 434.8 kg of ammonium nitrate, but it is released **slowly** as the manure decomposes, so only part of it is available to the first crop. The manure also adds organic matter, which the fertiliser cannot.
:::

## Organic compared with inorganic fertilisers

| | Organic manures | Inorganic fertilisers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nutrient content | Low and variable | High and stated on the bag |
| Speed of release | Slow, over several seasons | Fast (most are soluble) |
| Soil structure and organic matter | Improved | Not improved |
| Range of nutrients | Many, including trace elements | Only those in the product |
| Bulk, labour and transport | Bulky; much labour to make and spread | Concentrated; easy to transport and apply |
| Cost | Cheap or free on the farm | Must be bought |
| Risks | Weed seeds and pathogens if poorly made | Fertiliser burn, acidification, leaching and pollution if misused |

Most farmers get the best results by **combining** the two: manure or compost to build the soil, with a small, well-timed dose of fertiliser to give the crop a fast boost.

:::example{title="Choosing for a smallholding"}
Nyasha has two cattle, a sandy soil and little cash. She builds a compost heap with her kraal manure and maize stover, plants groundnuts in rotation with maize, and buys only a little ammonium nitrate for top dressing. The compost improves the water-holding of her sandy soil, the groundnuts add nitrogen, and the small cash input is used where it gives the biggest return.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving manure in the sun"}
Manure left spread out in the sun and rain loses much of its nitrogen as ammonia gas and by leaching. Heap, compact and cover it until it is used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying compost heats up because of the sun"}
The heat comes from the **respiration of micro-organisms** breaking down the material, not from sunlight.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ploughing in green manure too late"}
Ploughed in after it has set seed, a green manure is woody and rots slowly. Plough it in at about flowering.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe how compost is made"}
Give the steps in order (site, base, layers, water, cover, turn, ready), and add a reason for key steps: coarse base for aeration, manure for nitrogen and microbes, turning for air.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Advantages and disadvantages"}
Write in pairs that compare: "organic manure improves soil structure, whereas inorganic fertiliser does not". One point with a reason earns more than a list of single words.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organic manures include kraal and farmyard manure, compost, green manure and crop residues.
- Compost is built in layers of plant material, manure and soil, kept moist, covered and turned; it is ready in about two to three months.
- Organic matter improves crumb structure, water-holding, nutrient supply and soil life, and reduces erosion.
- Legumes fix nitrogen with *Rhizobium* and enrich the soil for the next crop.
- Organic manures are cheap and improve the soil but are bulky and low in nutrients; combine them with inorganic fertilisers.
:::
