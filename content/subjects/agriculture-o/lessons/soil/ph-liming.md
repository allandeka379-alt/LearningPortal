## Introduction

On many sandy granite soils of Zimbabwe, years of cropping with nitrogen fertiliser and heavy summer rain have made the soil **acid**. The farmer keeps adding fertiliser, but yields still fall: in very acid soil, phosphorus is locked up, aluminium poisons the roots and the bacteria in legume nodules work poorly. A simple soil test and a few tonnes of lime can do more for the crop than another bag of fertiliser.

Both boards expect you to *explain* why soil is sampled and *describe* a sampling method, *describe* how pH is tested and *interpret* the result, *explain* how pH affects crops, *explain* how fertilising and liming change pH, and *distinguish* lime from fertiliser. ZIMSEC may also ask you to *calculate* lime for a given area.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil pH"}
A measure of how acid or alkaline a soil is, on a scale from 0 to 14: 7 is neutral, below 7 is acid and above 7 is alkaline.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil sample"}
A small quantity of soil collected so that it represents a larger area of land for testing.
:::

:::definition{term="Composite sample"}
A sample made by thoroughly mixing many small subsamples (cores) taken across a uniform area.
:::

:::definition{term="Liming"}
Adding calcium (or calcium and magnesium) compounds to an acid soil to raise its pH.
:::

## Soil sampling

### Why sample?

- To find out the **pH** and **nutrient** status before deciding on lime and fertiliser.
- To avoid **wasting money** on fertiliser that is not needed, or under-fertilising.
- To detect problems such as acidity, salinity or a nutrient shortage before they cut yields.
- To keep **records** of how the soil changes over the years.

### Principles

1. The sample must be **representative**: one composite sample for each area that has the same soil, slope, crop history and management.
2. Avoid **unusual spots**: anthills, old kraals, manure heaps, fertiliser bands, wet patches, field edges, roads and paths, and burnt areas.
3. Take **many subsamples** (for example 15-20) to the same depth, usually the topsoil (about 0-20 cm), and mix them well.
4. Use **clean tools** and a clean plastic bucket; avoid metal or containers that held fertiliser.
5. **Label** each bag (farm, field, depth, date) and keep a field map.

### Method (zig-zag pattern)

1. Walk across the field in a **zig-zag** (W) pattern.
2. At each point, clear surface litter and take a core with a **soil auger**, or dig a V-shaped hole with a spade and cut a thin slice from one side.
3. Put each subsample into the bucket.
4. Mix all the subsamples thoroughly, break up clods and remove stones and roots.
5. Take about half a kilogram of the mixture, air-dry it in the shade, bag and label it, and send it to the laboratory.

A **grid** method (sampling at the corners of equal squares) is also used, especially on large, uniform fields.

## Testing soil pH

**Universal indicator (field kit):** put a little soil in a test tube, add a pinch of barium sulfate (which makes the clay settle so the liquid is clear), distilled water and a few drops of universal indicator. Shake, allow to settle and compare the colour of the liquid with the **colour chart**: red and orange are acid, green neutral and blue to purple alkaline.

**pH meter:** mix soil with distilled water (or a salt solution, as many laboratories do), stir and leave to stand, calibrate the meter with buffer solutions, then dip the electrode in the suspension and read the pH. A meter is more precise than an indicator.

:::note
Laboratories may report pH measured in water or in a calcium chloride solution. The calcium chloride reading is usually somewhat lower, so always compare a result with guidelines for the same method.
:::

## How pH affects crops

| Soil condition | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| Strongly acid | Aluminium and manganese become soluble and **toxic** to roots; phosphorus is locked up by iron and aluminium; calcium, magnesium and molybdenum are short; nitrifying bacteria and *Rhizobium* work poorly |
| About neutral to slightly acid | Most nutrients are readily available; most crops grow well |
| Alkaline | Iron, manganese, zinc and boron become less available (for example iron chlorosis); phosphorus is again less available |

Most field crops grow best in slightly acid to neutral soils. Some crops, such as tea and potatoes, tolerate fairly acid soil, while lucerne and many legumes need soil close to neutral.

The pH scale is **logarithmic**: each whole unit is a tenfold change. A soil at pH 4.5 is $10^{6.5-4.5} = 10^{2} = 100$ times more acid (in hydrogen ion concentration) than a soil at pH 6.5.

## Why soils become acid

- **Ammonium fertilisers**: ammonium nitrate, urea and especially ammonium sulfate release acid when their ammonium is changed to nitrate by bacteria.
- **Leaching** of calcium, magnesium and potassium by heavy rain, leaving acid elements behind.
- **Crop removal** of calcium and magnesium in harvested produce.
- Decomposition of organic matter, which releases acids.
- Acid **parent rock** such as granite.

## Liming

| Liming material | Chemical | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agricultural (calcitic) lime | Calcium carbonate (ground limestone) | Safe, slow-acting, most common |
| Dolomitic lime | Calcium magnesium carbonate | Also supplies magnesium |
| Burnt lime (quicklime) | Calcium oxide | Fast, caustic; burns skin |
| Slaked (hydrated) lime | Calcium hydroxide | Fast, caustic |

**How to lime:** spread lime evenly by broadcasting and work it into the soil **well before planting** (lime acts slowly and must be mixed with the soil). Finely ground lime acts faster. Do not apply lime together with ammonium fertilisers or fresh manure, because ammonia gas is lost. Re-test every few years; liming is not done every season.

**Over-liming** raises pH too far and causes shortages of iron, manganese, zinc and boron, so always lime according to a soil test.

### Lime and fertiliser are different

- **Lime** corrects soil **acidity** (raises pH); it supplies calcium (and magnesium from dolomitic lime) but is applied mainly to change the soil's reaction.
- **Fertiliser** supplies **nutrients** to the crop; many nitrogen fertilisers actually **lower** pH.
- Lime is applied in tonnes per hectare every few years; fertiliser in kilograms per hectare every season.
- **Gypsum** (calcium sulfate) supplies calcium and sulfur but does **not** correct acidity, so it is not a liming material.

:::worked-solution[Lime needed for a field]
A laboratory recommends $2\ \text{t/ha}$ of dolomitic lime for a $0.6\ \text{ha}$ field. Lime is sold in $50\ \text{kg}$ bags.

1. Convert the rate: $2\ \text{t/ha} = 2\,000\ \text{kg/ha}$.
2. Lime needed $= 2\,000 \times 0.6 = 1\,200\ \text{kg}$.
3. Bags $= 1\,200 \div 50 = 24$ bags.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a soil test"}
Sipho's soil test for a maize field near Gweru reports a low pH, low phosphorus and low magnesium. He reasons that the acidity is locking up phosphorus and that the soil lacks magnesium, so he chooses **dolomitic** lime rather than calcitic lime, spreads it in the dry season and discs it in, and plants maize with a basal compound at the start of the rains.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling lime a fertiliser"}
Lime changes the soil's **pH**; fertiliser supplies **nutrients**. Lime does add calcium, but that is not why it is used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking pH 5 is a little more acid than pH 6"}
pH 5 is **ten times** more acid than pH 6, because the scale is logarithmic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sampling from the easiest spot"}
One sample from beside the kraal or under a tree is not representative. Mix many subsamples from across the uniform area.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe a sampling method"}
Give numbered steps (pattern, tool, depth, number of subsamples, mixing, drying, labelling) and at least one "avoid" point. The principles (representative, clean, labelled) earn separate marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain pH effects"}
Name the nutrient or element and its effect: "In acid soil, aluminium becomes soluble and damages roots, and phosphorus is fixed by iron and aluminium, so the crop shows phosphorus deficiency."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sample soil to decide lime and fertiliser needs; make composite samples of 15-20 cores from each uniform area, avoiding unusual spots.
- Test pH with universal indicator and a colour chart, or with a calibrated pH meter.
- Acid soils lock up phosphorus and release toxic aluminium and manganese; alkaline soils make trace elements less available.
- Ammonium fertilisers, leaching and crop removal acidify soils.
- Lime (calcitic or dolomitic) raises pH; it is not a fertiliser; apply by soil test, well before planting.
:::
