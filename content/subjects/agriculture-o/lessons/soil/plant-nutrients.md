## Introduction

Walk through a maize field near Gokwe in January and you can often read the soil from the leaves. Pale plants with yellow lower leaves, purple-tinged seedlings in a cold wet spell, brown scorched leaf edges on a sandy patch: each pattern points to a nutrient the crop is short of. A farmer who can read these signs buys the right fertiliser instead of guessing.

Plants make their own sugars by photosynthesis, but they must take **mineral nutrients** from the soil as ions dissolved in the soil water. Examiners ask you to *state* the functions of nutrients, *describe* deficiency symptoms, *explain* why a symptom appears where it does, and *suggest* what happens when a nutrient is oversupplied. This lesson covers the nutrients themselves; the fertilisers and manures that supply them have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plant nutrient"}
A chemical element that a plant needs for normal growth and that it cannot complete its life cycle without.
:::

:::definition{term="Major nutrients (macronutrients)"}
Nutrients needed in relatively large amounts: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg) and sulfur (S). N, P and K are sometimes called the primary nutrients.
:::

:::definition{term="Minor nutrients (trace elements, micronutrients)"}
Nutrients needed in very small amounts, such as iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and molybdenum. They are just as essential as the major nutrients.
:::

:::definition{term="Deficiency"}
A shortage of a nutrient in the plant, which shows as visible symptoms (for example chlorosis, purpling or scorching) and lowers growth and yield.
:::

:::definition{term="Chlorosis"}
Yellowing of leaf tissue because too little chlorophyll is made.
:::

## The major nutrients

| Nutrient | Main functions | Deficiency signs | Effects of oversupply |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen (N) | Part of proteins, enzymes and chlorophyll; vigorous leafy growth | Pale green to yellow plants; yellowing starts on older (lower) leaves; in maize a yellow V from the leaf tip down the midrib; stunting | Soft, lush growth that lodges and attracts pests and diseases; delayed maturity; in tobacco, poor leaf quality |
| Phosphorus (P) | Energy transfer (ATP); root growth; flowering, seed and fruit formation; early maturity | Purple or reddish leaves (clear in young maize); poor roots; stunted plants; late maturity | Can make zinc and some other trace elements less available |
| Potassium (K) | Controls opening of stomata and water balance; activates enzymes; moves sugars; strong stems; disease resistance; quality of fruit and tubers | Scorching (browning) of the margins and tips of older leaves; weak stalks that lodge; small fruit and tubers | Can reduce the uptake of magnesium and calcium |
| Calcium (Ca) | Part of cell walls; growth of root tips and shoots; pod filling in groundnuts | Young leaves and growing points distorted or die; blossom-end rot of tomatoes; empty groundnut pods ("pops") | Usually raises soil pH (from too much lime), making some trace elements less available |
| Magnesium (Mg) | Central atom of the chlorophyll molecule; activates enzymes | Interveinal chlorosis of older leaves (yellow between green veins; striped maize leaves) | Rare; can reduce potassium or calcium uptake |
| Sulfur (S) | Part of some amino acids and proteins; oil formation in oilseeds | General yellowing, often first on young leaves; small, thin plants | Rare in crops; too much elemental sulfur makes the soil more acid |

## Why symptoms appear on old or young leaves

Some nutrients are **mobile** in the plant: when supply is short, the plant moves them out of old leaves into new growth. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and magnesium are mobile, so their deficiency shows **first on the older, lower leaves**.

Other nutrients are **immobile**: once built into a leaf they stay there. Calcium, iron, boron and (largely) sulfur are immobile, so a shortage shows **first on young leaves and growing points**. This is the single most useful rule when you diagnose a deficiency.

:::example{title="Diagnosing a problem in a maize field"}
Farai's maize is knee-high. The lower leaves are yellow, with the yellow running from the leaf tip down the centre of the leaf in a V; the youngest leaves are still green. Heavy rain fell two weeks after planting on his sandy soil.

1. The symptom is on **old** leaves, so the nutrient is mobile: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium or magnesium.
2. A **V-shaped yellowing along the midrib** fits nitrogen; potassium would scorch the leaf edges, magnesium would give stripes between veins and phosphorus would give purpling.
3. Heavy rain on a sandy soil **leaches nitrate**, which supports the diagnosis.
4. Action: apply a nitrogen top dressing such as ammonium nitrate beside the plants and split future applications.
:::

## Minor nutrients (trace elements)

| Trace element | Main function | Example of a deficiency sign |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron (Fe) | Needed to make chlorophyll; enzymes | Interveinal chlorosis of young leaves, common on alkaline soils |
| Manganese (Mn) | Enzymes; photosynthesis | Yellowing between the veins with small dead spots |
| Zinc (Zn) | Enzymes and growth hormones | White or pale bands either side of the midrib in young maize leaves ("white bud"); short internodes |
| Boron (B) | Cell division, pollen and seed set | Death of growing points; hollow stems in brassicas; poor seed set |
| Molybdenum (Mo) | Nitrogen fixation in legume nodules; using nitrate in the plant | Legumes short of nitrogen; "whiptail" (narrow, distorted leaves) in cauliflower |
| Copper (Cu) | Enzymes | Wilting and dieback of young shoots |

Trace elements are needed in tiny amounts, and the gap between "enough" and "too much" is small. **Toxicity** is a real risk: on very acid soils, aluminium and manganese become so soluble that they damage roots. Over-applying a trace element, for example boron, can scorch and kill a crop.

## Measuring the effect of a nutrient

Deficiency symptoms are best learned by seeing them. A classic school experiment grows seedlings in **culture solutions**: a complete solution with every nutrient, and solutions each missing one nutrient. Everything else (light, temperature, water, seedling size) is kept the same.

:::worked-solution[Percentage reduction in growth in a culture-solution trial]
Pupils at a school near Masvingo grew maize seedlings for five weeks and then dried and weighed them.

| Solution | Mean dry mass per pot (g) | Reduction compared with complete (%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Complete solution | 24.0 | — |
| No nitrogen | 9.6 | 60 |
| No phosphorus | 13.2 | 45 |
| No potassium | 18.0 | 25 |

1. Reduction for the no-nitrogen pots $= 24.0 - 9.6 = 14.4\ \text{g}$.
2. Percentage reduction $= \frac{14.4}{24.0} \times 100 = 60\%$.
3. In the same way, no phosphorus gives $\frac{24.0 - 13.2}{24.0} \times 100 = 45\%$ and no potassium $\frac{24.0 - 18.0}{24.0} \times 100 = 25\%$.
4. Conclusion: leaving out nitrogen reduced growth most, because nitrogen is needed in the largest amount for proteins and chlorophyll.
:::

:::worked-solution[Replacing the nitrogen removed by a harvest]
Assume that every tonne of maize grain removes about $15\ \text{kg}$ of nitrogen from the field, and that ammonium nitrate contains 34.5% N. A farmer harvests $6\ \text{t/ha}$.

1. Nitrogen removed $= 6 \times 15 = 90\ \text{kg N/ha}$.
2. Ammonium nitrate needed to replace it $= 90 \div \frac{34.5}{100} = 260.9\ \text{kg/ha}$ (to one decimal place).
3. In $50\ \text{kg}$ bags: $260.9 \div 50 = 5.2$, so the farmer buys **6 bags** per hectare (always round bags up).
:::

## Oversupply and balance

More is not always better. Each extra kilogram of a nutrient gives a smaller increase in yield, and beyond a point it does harm:

- **Too much nitrogen** gives tall, soft, dark green plants with weak stems that **lodge** (fall over); they are attacked more by aphids and fungal diseases and may mature late. Fruit crops may grow leaves instead of fruit.
- **Fertiliser burn**: a heavy dose of a soluble fertiliser placed against seeds or roots draws water out of them by osmosis, so seedlings wilt and die.
- **Imbalance**: an excess of one nutrient can block another. High phosphorus can induce zinc deficiency; high potassium can reduce magnesium uptake.
- **Pollution and waste**: nitrate that the crop cannot use is leached to groundwater and rivers, where it encourages algal growth, and money is wasted.

The aim is a **balanced** supply matched to the crop, the soil test and the expected yield.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying every deficiency makes leaves yellow"}
Name the **specific** sign: nitrogen gives general yellowing of old leaves, phosphorus purpling, potassium marginal scorch, magnesium interveinal chlorosis. "Yellow leaves" alone rarely earns the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up old and young leaves"}
Mobile nutrients (N, P, K, Mg) show on **older** leaves; immobile ones (Ca, Fe, B, mostly S) show on **young** leaves and growing points. Learn the rule, not a list of exceptions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking trace elements are less important"}
"Minor" refers only to the **amount** needed. A crop short of zinc or boron can fail completely.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Functions and symptoms"}
For a *state* question give one precise function ("nitrogen is part of chlorophyll and proteins"). For *describe the symptoms* say what, where and colour: "yellowing of the older, lower leaves, starting at the tip".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain questions"}
Link the function to the symptom: magnesium is part of chlorophyll, so without it less chlorophyll is made and the leaf yellows between the veins. For oversupply, give the effect and its consequence: lush growth, so the plant lodges and yield falls.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Major nutrients are N, P, K, Ca, Mg and S; trace elements include Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B and Mo.
- N builds proteins and chlorophyll; P roots, energy and seed; K water balance, strength and quality; Ca cell walls; Mg chlorophyll; S proteins.
- Key signs: N yellow old leaves, P purpling, K scorched margins, Mg interveinal chlorosis, Ca dead growing points.
- Mobile nutrients show deficiency on old leaves; immobile ones on young leaves.
- Oversupply causes lodging, pests, fertiliser burn, nutrient imbalance, waste and pollution.
:::
