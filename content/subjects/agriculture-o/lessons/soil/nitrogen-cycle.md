## Introduction

Nearly four-fifths of the air above every field near Harare is nitrogen gas, yet nitrogen is the nutrient that most often limits maize yields. The reason is that crops cannot use nitrogen gas. It must first be changed into ammonium or nitrate, and in the soil these forms are constantly being made, used, moved and lost. The **nitrogen cycle** describes these changes. Understanding it explains why farmers grow legumes, why they split their top dressing, why waterlogged fields turn yellow and why ploughing in fresh stalks can starve a crop.

You may be asked to *describe* the nitrogen cycle in words or from a described diagram, to *name* the organisms involved, to *explain* how particular practices add or lose nitrogen, and to *explain* the cycle's importance to soil fertility.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nitrogen fixation"}
The conversion of nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) from the air into nitrogen compounds that plants can use, such as ammonium.
:::

:::definition{term="Ammonification"}
The release of ammonium from the proteins and other nitrogen compounds in dead organisms and wastes, by decomposers (bacteria and fungi).
:::

:::definition{term="Nitrification"}
The oxidation of ammonium to nitrite and then to nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in well-aerated soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Denitrification"}
The conversion of nitrate back to nitrogen gas by denitrifying bacteria, mainly in waterlogged soil short of oxygen.
:::

## The stages of the cycle

Think of the cycle as a set of **pools** (air, soil organic matter, ammonium, nitrate, plants, animals) joined by **processes**.

### 1. Fixation: getting nitrogen out of the air

- **Lightning** provides the energy to combine nitrogen and oxygen into nitrogen oxides; these dissolve in rain and reach the soil as dilute nitric acid, giving nitrate.
- **Symbiotic bacteria**: *Rhizobium* lives in **nodules** on the roots of legumes such as soya beans, groundnuts, cowpeas and clover. The plant gives the bacteria sugars; the bacteria fix nitrogen into ammonium compounds that the plant uses to make proteins. Healthy, active nodules are pink inside.
- **Free-living bacteria** in the soil, such as *Azotobacter* (aerobic) and *Clostridium* (anaerobic), and **blue-green bacteria** (cyanobacteria) in wet soils, fix smaller amounts.
- **Industrial fixation**: factories combine nitrogen from the air with hydrogen at high temperature and pressure to make ammonia (the Haber process). Ammonia is the starting point for ammonium nitrate, urea and other nitrogen fertilisers.

$$\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NH}_3$$

### 2. Uptake and feeding

Plants take up **nitrate** (and some ammonium) through their roots and use it to make **amino acids and proteins**. Animals get their nitrogen by **eating** plants or other animals.

### 3. Decomposition and ammonification

When plants and animals die, and when animals excrete dung and urine, **decomposers** (saprophytic bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins and release **ammonium** ($\text{NH}_4^+$).

### 4. Nitrification

Two groups of **nitrifying bacteria**, which need oxygen, change ammonium to nitrate in two steps:

1. ***Nitrosomonas*** oxidises ammonium to **nitrite** ($\text{NO}_2^-$).
2. ***Nitrobacter*** oxidises nitrite to **nitrate** ($\text{NO}_3^-$), the main form taken up by crops.

Nitrification is fastest in warm, moist, well-aerated soil that is not too acid. It also releases acid, which is one reason ammonium fertilisers lower soil pH.

### 5. Losses

- **Denitrification**: in waterlogged, airless soil, denitrifying bacteria use nitrate instead of oxygen and release **nitrogen gas** (and some nitrous oxide) to the air.
- **Leaching**: nitrate is soluble and not held by clay or humus, so heavy rain washes it below the roots, especially in sandy soils.
- **Volatilisation**: ammonia gas escapes from urea or manure left on the soil surface, especially on alkaline or limed soil.
- **Crop removal**: nitrogen leaves the farm in grain, hay, meat and milk.
- **Erosion and burning** remove nitrogen in topsoil and organic matter.

| Process | Organisms or agent | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symbiotic fixation | *Rhizobium* in legume nodules | $\text{N}_2 \rightarrow$ ammonium compounds |
| Free-living fixation | *Azotobacter*, *Clostridium*, blue-green bacteria | $\text{N}_2 \rightarrow$ nitrogen compounds |
| Atmospheric fixation | Lightning | $\text{N}_2 \rightarrow$ nitrogen oxides $\rightarrow$ nitrate |
| Ammonification | Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) | Proteins $\rightarrow$ ammonium |
| Nitrification | *Nitrosomonas*, then *Nitrobacter* | Ammonium $\rightarrow$ nitrite $\rightarrow$ nitrate |
| Denitrification | Denitrifying bacteria (no oxygen) | Nitrate $\rightarrow \text{N}_2$ gas |

## Importance to soil fertility

The cycle decides how much **available** nitrogen a crop can find. Farmers manage it by:

- **Growing legumes** in rotation and as green manures, and **inoculating** legume seed with the right *Rhizobium* strain.
- **Adding organic matter** (manure, compost, residues), which is ammonified and nitrified to release nitrogen slowly.
- **Draining** waterlogged land and keeping soils aerated to favour nitrification and avoid denitrification.
- **Liming** very acid soils, because nitrifying bacteria and *Rhizobium* work poorly at low pH.
- **Splitting** nitrogen fertiliser and avoiding over-irrigation, to reduce leaching.
- **Covering** urea with soil, to reduce volatilisation.

:::worked-solution[Nitrogen left by a legume]
Assume a soya bean crop leaves about $60\ \text{kg}$ of nitrogen per hectare in its roots and residues for the next crop, and that ammonium nitrate contains 34.5% N.

1. Ammonium nitrate with the same nitrogen $= 60 \div 0.345 = 173.9\ \text{kg/ha}$.
2. In $50\ \text{kg}$ bags: $173.9 \div 50 = 3.5$, so the legume saves the equivalent of about 3.5 bags (just under 4 bags) per hectare.
3. The saving depends on the legume, the season and how much residue is left; harvesting all the haulms for feed removes much of it.
:::

:::worked-solution[A simple nitrogen budget]
Assume a farmer applies $120\ \text{kg N/ha}$ to maize on sandy soil. The crop takes up $78\ \text{kg}$, $24\ \text{kg}$ is leached and $6\ \text{kg}$ is lost by denitrification.

1. Nitrogen left in the soil $= 120 - 78 - 24 - 6 = 12\ \text{kg N/ha}$.
2. Percentage taken up by the crop $= \frac{78}{120} \times 100 = 65\%$.
3. Percentage lost $= \frac{24 + 6}{120} \times 100 = 25\%$.
4. Splitting the dose and adding organic matter would reduce the leaching loss, raising the share used by the crop.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying plants take in nitrogen from the air"}
Only legumes **with** *Rhizobium* benefit from nitrogen gas, and it is the bacteria that fix it. Plants absorb nitrogen as nitrate and ammonium through their roots.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing nitrification with nitrogen fixation"}
**Fixation** starts from nitrogen **gas**. **Nitrification** starts from **ammonium** and makes nitrate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nitrifying and denitrifying bacteria"}
Nitrifying bacteria need **oxygen** and make nitrate. Denitrifying bacteria work **without oxygen** and destroy nitrate.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describing the cycle"}
Write it as a sequence with the organism named at each step: fixation (*Rhizobium*, lightning), uptake into proteins, death and excretion, ammonification by decomposers, nitrification (*Nitrosomonas* then *Nitrobacter*), and losses (denitrification, leaching). Each correctly named step usually earns a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Applying the cycle"}
For "explain why" questions, link the practice to a process: waterlogging → denitrification; legumes → fixation; liming → more active nitrifiers and *Rhizobium*; split dressing → less leaching.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Plants use nitrate and ammonium, not nitrogen gas.
- Fixation by lightning, *Rhizobium*, free-living bacteria and industry brings nitrogen from the air into the soil.
- Decomposers release ammonium (ammonification); *Nitrosomonas* and *Nitrobacter* turn it into nitrate (nitrification).
- Nitrogen is lost by denitrification, leaching, volatilisation, crop removal, erosion and burning.
- Legumes, organic matter, drainage, liming and split fertiliser keep more nitrogen available to crops.
:::
