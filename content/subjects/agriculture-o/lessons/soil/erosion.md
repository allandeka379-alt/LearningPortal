## Introduction

After a summer thunderstorm, the streams below many communal fields in Zimbabwe run the colour of strong tea. That brown water is carrying topsoil: the darkest, most fertile layer that took hundreds of years to form. Some of it ends up in the bed of the nearest dam, which slowly fills with silt. Higher up, the fields left behind show stones standing on little columns of soil, roots exposed at the base of trees and channels cut across the land.

Soil erosion is one of the most serious problems facing farmers, because lost topsoil cannot be replaced within a lifetime. This lesson covers the **types, agents, causes, signs and consequences** of erosion; prevention and control are in the lesson on soil conservation. Examination questions ask you to *name* and *describe* the types of erosion, to *explain* how a farming practice causes erosion and to *state* its effects on and off the farm.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil erosion"}
The removal of soil, especially topsoil, from one place to another by agents such as running water and wind, faster than new soil can form.
:::

:::definition{term="Agent of erosion"}
The force that detaches and carries soil particles away: mainly water and wind, helped by gravity, animals and people.
:::

:::definition{term="Runoff"}
Rainwater that flows over the soil surface instead of soaking in. It carries detached soil downslope.
:::

:::definition{term="Siltation"}
The deposition of eroded soil particles in rivers, dams and irrigation canals, making them shallower.
:::

## How water erodes soil

Water erosion has two stages: **detachment** (particles are knocked loose) and **transport** (they are carried away). Bare soil, intense rain and slopes increase both.

### Types of water erosion

| Type | What happens | How to recognise it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Splash (raindrop) erosion | Heavy raindrops hit bare soil, break up crumbs and throw particles into the air; they land further downslope | Soil splashed onto stems and walls; surface caps; small pedestals |
| Sheet erosion | A thin, even layer of topsoil is washed off a wide area by runoff | Hard to see: paler patches, exposed roots, stones standing on soil columns (pedestals), lower yields |
| Rill erosion | Runoff gathers into small channels a few centimetres deep | Small channels running downslope that can be filled in by cultivation |
| Gully erosion | Rills join and deepen into large channels that cannot be crossed by implements | Deep, wide channels with steep sides; they grow back upslope at their head |

The types usually follow one another: splash detaches particles, sheet erosion removes them in a thin layer, rills form where water concentrates, and neglected rills become gullies.

### Wind erosion

On dry, bare, loose soils, especially fine sands in the drier south and west of Zimbabwe, strong winds lift and carry particles. Fine particles are blown long distances as dust (**suspension**); sand grains bounce along the surface (**saltation**) and knock other grains loose; larger grains roll (**surface creep**). Signs are dust storms, ripples of sand, exposed roots and sand piled against fences and bushes.

## Causes of erosion

Erosion is natural, but human activity speeds it up greatly by removing the plant cover that protects the soil.

- **Overgrazing:** too many animals for the grazing land eat the grass to the ground and trample the soil, so bare, compacted soil sheds runoff. Cattle paths to water become channels that turn into gullies.
- **Deforestation:** cutting trees for firewood, curing tobacco or clearing land removes the canopy and roots that hold soil.
- **Cultivating slopes and stream banks:** ploughing steep land, especially up and down the slope, lets runoff flow straight down the furrows. Cultivation next to rivers removes the vegetation that protects the banks.
- **Veld fires:** burning removes grass and litter, leaving bare soil exposed to the first storms of the season.
- **Poor tillage:** overcultivation breaks down structure, burning residues leaves the surface bare and repeated ploughing forms pans that reduce infiltration.
- **Roads, paths and mining:** these concentrate runoff and expose bare soil.

### Factors that affect the amount of erosion

| Factor | More erosion when... |
| --- | --- |
| Rainfall | Storms are intense, with large drops (common in Zimbabwe's summer thunderstorms) |
| Slope | The slope is steep and long, so runoff gains speed |
| Soil | Structure is weak, organic matter is low and the surface caps; fine sands and silts detach easily |
| Cover | The soil is bare at the start of the rains, before the crop canopy closes |
| Management | Ploughing runs up and down the slope and residues are burned |

:::worked-solution[Comparing soil loss from runoff plots]
At a research site, two plots each $20\ \text{m} \times 5\ \text{m}$ are set on the same slope. Soil washed off each is caught in a trough. In one season the bare plot loses $6\ \text{kg}$ of soil and the grass plot $0.9\ \text{kg}$.

1. Plot area $= 20 \times 5 = 100\ \text{m}^2 = \frac{100}{10\,000} = 0.01\ \text{ha}$.
2. Bare plot: $\frac{6}{0.01} = 600\ \text{kg/ha}$, which is $0.6\ \text{t/ha}$.
3. Grass plot: $\frac{0.9}{0.01} = 90\ \text{kg/ha}$.
4. Reduction $= \frac{600 - 90}{600} \times 100 = 85\%$.
5. Conclusion: grass cover reduced soil loss by 85%, because it intercepts raindrops, slows runoff and its roots bind the soil.
:::

## Consequences of erosion

### On the farm

- Loss of the fertile **topsoil**, with its humus and nutrients; fertiliser and seed may be washed away too.
- **Lower yields**, and more fertiliser is needed for the same crop.
- Lower **water-holding capacity** and shallower rooting depth, so crops suffer sooner in dry spells.
- **Gullies** cut fields into pieces, block tractors and ox-drawn implements, and lower the water table near them.
- Grazing land loses grass cover, so its carrying capacity falls.

### Off the farm

- **Siltation** of rivers, dams and irrigation canals reduces their storage and raises the cost of clearing them.
- Rivers become shallower and **flood** more easily; roads and bridges are damaged.
- Sediment carries fertiliser and pesticide residues into water, harming fish and drinking water (**pollution** and **eutrophication**).
- Wind-blown **dust** damages crops, reduces visibility and causes breathing problems.

:::example{title="Reading the signs on a hillside field"}
Farai's grandmother has ploughed a sloping field up and down the slope for many years. Farai notices: stones perched on small soil columns, pale subsoil showing on the upper part, small channels running straight downhill after storms, and a deep channel starting where the cattle path meets the field.

1. Pedestals and pale subsoil are signs of **sheet erosion** removing the topsoil.
2. The straight channels are **rills**, formed because furrows run downslope.
3. The deep channel at the cattle path is a **gully** forming where trampling and runoff are concentrated.
4. Causes: cultivating the slope up and down, bare soil at the start of the rains and an eroded cattle track.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all erosion 'gully erosion'"}
Name the type that fits the evidence. Sheet erosion is the most widespread and least visible; it does the most damage to fertility before anyone notices.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing causes and agents"}
Water and wind are **agents**. Overgrazing, deforestation and burning are **causes** that make the soil vulnerable to the agents.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting off-farm effects"}
Many questions ask for effects "on and off the farm". Siltation of dams and rivers is the most important off-farm effect.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining a cause"}
Use a chain: practice → effect on cover or soil → effect on runoff or wind → erosion. For example: "Overgrazing removes grass cover and compacts the soil, so less rain infiltrates and more runs off, carrying topsoil away."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Soil loss figures from small plots must be scaled to a hectare: divide the mass by the plot area in hectares (1 ha = 10,000 m²).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Erosion removes topsoil faster than it forms; water and wind are the main agents.
- Water erosion: splash → sheet → rill → gully. Wind erosion moves soil by suspension, saltation and creep.
- Overgrazing, deforestation, cultivating slopes and stream banks, veld fires and poor tillage are the main causes.
- Signs include pedestals, exposed roots, rills, gullies, siltation and dust.
- Effects: loss of fertility and yield on the farm; siltation, flooding and pollution downstream.
:::
