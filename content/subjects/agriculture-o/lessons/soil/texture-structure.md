## Introduction

Rudo's family farms near Chipinge. After the first heavy storm of the season, the bare field next to the homestead is covered by a hard, shiny crust, and the maize seedlings struggle to push through. In the neighbouring field, where maize stover was left on the surface, the soil stays loose and crumbly. Both fields have the same soil particles, the same **texture**, but very different **structure**.

Texture and structure decide how much water a soil holds, how easily roots and air move through it and how hard it is to cultivate. Examination questions ask you to put particles in order of size, to find a textural class from data, to tell structure from texture and to explain how farmers build and protect a good crumb structure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil texture"}
The relative proportions of sand, silt and clay particles in a soil. It describes how coarse or fine the soil feels and is almost impossible for a farmer to change.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil structure"}
The way individual soil particles are grouped together into larger units called aggregates (peds), and the pore spaces between them. A farmer can improve or destroy it.
:::

:::definition{term="Crumb structure"}
Small, rounded, porous aggregates, a few millimetres across, held together by humus and clay. It is the ideal structure for topsoil.
:::

:::definition{term="Textural class"}
The name given to a soil according to its percentages of sand, silt and clay, for example sandy loam, loam or clay loam.
:::

:::definition{term="Capping"}
A hard, thin crust formed on the surface of a bare soil when raindrops break down aggregates and the fine particles set as they dry.
:::

## Soil particles in order of size

| Particle | Approximate diameter | Feel when moist | Main property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gravel and stones | larger than 2 mm | Hard pieces | Not true soil; reduce working |
| Sand (coarse and fine) | 2 mm down to about 0.02 mm | Gritty | Large pores: drains fast, holds little water and few nutrients |
| Silt | about 0.02 mm down to 0.002 mm | Smooth and silky, like flour | Holds water fairly well; easily capped and eroded |
| Clay | smaller than 0.002 mm | Sticky and plastic; can be rolled into a thread | Tiny pores: holds much water and many nutrients; drains slowly |

The sand–silt boundary is 0.02 mm in the international system used in many textbooks and 0.05 mm in the American (USDA) system. The order **gravel > sand > silt > clay** is the same in both.

## Finding the texture of a soil

### The feel method (in the field)

Moisten a little soil in your palm and rub it between finger and thumb. A gritty feel shows sand; a smooth, soapy feel shows silt; a sticky soil that can be pressed into a long, thin ribbon or rolled into a thread shows clay. Loam feels slightly gritty, slightly smooth and only a little sticky.

### Sieving

A dry sample is shaken through a stack of sieves with smaller and smaller holes. The mass left on each sieve is weighed, giving the proportions of gravel, coarse sand and fine sand. Silt and clay pass through the finest sieve, so they are separated by sedimentation.

### Sedimentation (the jar test)

1. Put soil in a tall, straight-sided glass jar until it is about one third full.
2. Add water (with a pinch of salt or washing soda to separate the clay particles) until the jar is nearly full.
3. Shake hard for a few minutes, then stand the jar on a level surface.
4. The heaviest particles settle first: gravel and coarse sand at the bottom, then fine sand, then silt, then clay on top. The finest clay keeps the water cloudy for a day or more, and organic matter floats.
5. When the water clears, measure the height of each layer and work out each as a percentage of the total.

:::worked-solution[Percentages from a jar test]
A jar test on soil from Rudo's field gives layers of sand $4.8\ \text{cm}$, silt $2.0\ \text{cm}$ and clay $1.2\ \text{cm}$. Find the percentage of each particle.

1. Total height of settled soil: $4.8 + 2.0 + 1.2 = 8.0\ \text{cm}$.
2. Sand: $\frac{4.8}{8.0} \times 100 = 60\%$.
3. Silt: $\frac{2.0}{8.0} \times 100 = 25\%$.
4. Clay: $\frac{1.2}{8.0} \times 100 = 15\%$.
5. Check: $60 + 25 + 15 = 100\%$. On the textural triangle this soil is a **sandy loam**.
:::

### The textural triangle

The textural triangle has three sides marked from 0 to 100%: one for sand, one for silt and one for clay. Read the clay percentage across, the sand and silt percentages along their sides, and find the point where the lines meet; the area it falls in gives the class. Because the three percentages always add up to 100, two of them are enough to place the soil.

Typical classes run from **sand** and **loamy sand** (mostly sand), through **sandy loam**, **loam**, **sandy clay loam** and **clay loam**, to **sandy clay**, **silty clay** and **clay** (40% clay or more). A loam has a balanced mixture, for example about 40% sand, 40% silt and 20% clay.

:::note{title="Which triangle?"}
Class boundaries differ slightly between versions of the triangle. In the examination, read the class from the triangle printed on the paper.
:::

:::worked-solution[Percentages from sieving]
Tendai sieves $200\ \text{g}$ of dry soil. Gravel $14\ \text{g}$, coarse sand $62\ \text{g}$ and fine sand $48\ \text{g}$ stay on the sieves; $76\ \text{g}$ (silt and clay) passes through.

1. Gravel: $\frac{14}{200} \times 100 = 7\%$.
2. Coarse sand: $\frac{62}{200} \times 100 = 31\%$.
3. Fine sand: $\frac{48}{200} \times 100 = 24\%$.
4. Silt and clay: $\frac{76}{200} \times 100 = 38\%$; a jar test is needed to split it.
5. Check: $7 + 31 + 24 + 38 = 100\%$.
:::

## Soil structure

| Structure | Description | Where found and effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crumb (granular) | Small, rounded, porous aggregates | Good topsoils rich in humus; ideal for seedbeds, air and water movement |
| Single-grain | Particles not joined at all | Sandy soils; drains fast, holds little water, erodes easily |
| Blocky | Cube-like blocks with sharp or rounded edges | Subsoils; moderate drainage |
| Platy | Thin, flat plates lying horizontally | Compacted layers and pans; blocks roots and water |
| Prismatic or columnar | Tall vertical columns | Some clay subsoils; cracks when dry |

### Why a good crumb structure matters

- Large pores between crumbs let **air** reach roots and let **excess water drain**, while small pores inside crumbs **hold water** for the crop.
- Roots and seedlings push through easily, and the soil makes a fine **seedbed**.
- Rain soaks in instead of running off, so there is less **erosion**.
- The soil is easier to work and warms better in spring.

### What holds crumbs together

**Humus** is the key. It coats particles and binds them with clay. Sticky substances made by bacteria, fungal threads, fine grass roots and **earthworm casts** add to this. **Calcium** (from lime) helps clay particles group together (flocculate) instead of dispersing.

### What destroys structure

- **Oxidation of organic matter.** Each time soil is ploughed, air enters and micro-organisms break humus down faster. With less humus, crumbs fall apart; fine particles then wash into the pores and set hard as a **cap** on the surface, or are washed down to form a dense layer.
- **Raindrop impact on bare soil** shatters crumbs and causes capping.
- **Overcultivation**, especially of wet soil, smears and breaks crumbs.
- **Ploughing to the same depth every year** and heavy machinery form a compacted **plough pan** below the surface.
- **Burning crop residues** and **overgrazing** remove the organic matter that feeds the soil.

:::example{title="Explaining capping in Rudo's bare field"}
Rudo's family has ploughed the bare field every year and burned the stover. Humus has been oxidised and not replaced, so the crumbs are weak. The heavy storm shattered the crumbs; silt and clay filled the surface pores and dried into a hard cap. The seedlings could not break through, and later rain ran off instead of soaking in. In the mulched field, the stover absorbed raindrop impact and added organic matter, so the crumbs survived.
:::

### Improving and maintaining structure

- Add **organic matter**: kraal manure, compost, crop residues and green manure.
- Keep the surface **covered with mulch** to stop raindrop impact and capping.
- Use **minimum tillage** and cultivate only when the soil is moist, not wet or very dry.
- Grow **grass leys** or deep-rooted crops in the **rotation**.
- **Lime** acid clay soils to add calcium.
- Break pans by **ripping** or by varying the depth of ploughing; avoid driving on wet soil.

:::keyfact{title="Texture versus structure"}
Texture is the **size** mixture of particles and cannot practically be changed. Structure is the **arrangement** of particles into aggregates and can be improved or destroyed by management.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up texture and structure"}
"Adding manure improves texture" is wrong. Manure improves **structure** (and water-holding); it does not change the proportion of sand, silt and clay.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the layers of a jar test in the wrong order"}
Sand settles first at the bottom, clay last on top. Organic matter floats; it is not part of the texture percentages.
:::

:::mistake{title="Percentages that do not add up"}
Divide each layer by the **total** height of settled soil, not by the height of the water or the jar. The three percentages must add up to 100.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* the particles in order of size from largest to smallest unless the question says otherwise. *Describe* a test as numbered steps, including what you observe. *Explain* the importance of crumb structure by linking pores to air, drainage, water holding and root growth.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Show the total, then each percentage with the fraction written out, and check that the three add to 100. Name the textural class only after the percentages are found.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Particles from largest to smallest: gravel, sand, silt, clay (below 0.002 mm).
- Texture (the proportions of sand, silt and clay) is found by feel, sieving, the jar test and the textural triangle.
- Structure is the grouping of particles into aggregates; crumb structure is ideal for topsoil.
- Humus, roots, earthworms and calcium build crumbs; oxidation of organic matter, bare soil, overcultivation and pans destroy them.
- Organic matter, mulch, minimum tillage, leys and liming improve structure.
:::
