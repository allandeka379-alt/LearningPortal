## Introduction

Pick up a handful of good topsoil from a school garden in Marondera and you are holding far more than crushed rock. There are mineral particles, dark humus, water clinging to the grains, air in the spaces between them and millions of living organisms, from earthworms you can see to bacteria you cannot. Each of these **constituents** has a job in feeding and supporting a crop.

This topic explains what a good soil is made of, how water is held and moves in it, and why soil organisms matter. Examination questions often ask you to *state* the constituents, to *describe* an experiment that shows one of them, to *distinguish* the forms of soil water and to *explain* the roles of earthworms or bacteria.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil constituents"}
The components that make up a soil: mineral matter, organic matter (including humus), soil water, soil air and living organisms.
:::

:::definition{term="Humus"}
Dark, well-decomposed organic matter in the soil that no longer shows the structure of the plants or animals it came from. It holds water and nutrients and binds soil crumbs.
:::

:::definition{term="Field capacity"}
The amount of water a soil holds after it has been saturated and all the gravitational water has drained away, usually one to three days after heavy rain or irrigation.
:::

:::definition{term="Permanent wilting point"}
The soil water content at which plants can no longer take up water and wilt permanently, not recovering even when the air is cool and humid.
:::

:::definition{term="Available water"}
The water held between field capacity and the permanent wilting point; this is the water crops can actually use.
:::

## The constituents of a good agricultural soil

A good loam topsoil, by volume, is roughly half solid and half pore space. A typical textbook figure is about 45% mineral matter, 5% organic matter, 25% water and 25% air, though real soils vary widely and the water and air shares change after every rain.

| Constituent | What it is | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mineral matter | Sand, silt and clay from weathered rock | Supports and anchors plants; supplies mineral nutrients such as potassium, calcium and magnesium; decides texture |
| Organic matter and humus | Plant and animal remains at all stages of decay | Releases nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur) as it decomposes; holds water and nutrients; binds crumbs; food for organisms; darkens and warms soil |
| Soil water | Water in the pores and around particles | Dissolves nutrients so roots can absorb them; needed for germination, photosynthesis, transpiration and cooling |
| Soil air | Air in the pores not filled by water | Supplies oxygen for root and organism respiration; nitrogen for nitrogen-fixing bacteria |
| Living organisms | Macro-organisms (earthworms, termites) and micro-organisms (bacteria, fungi) | Decompose residues and recycle nutrients; mix and aerate soil; fix nitrogen; some cause disease |

Soil air usually contains more carbon dioxide and less oxygen than the atmosphere, because roots and organisms respire. When the soil is waterlogged, water fills the pores and roots run short of oxygen.

## Showing the constituents by experiment

- **Water:** weigh a fresh soil sample, dry it in an oven at about $105\ ^\circ\text{C}$ until its mass stops changing, and weigh again. The loss in mass is water.
- **Organic matter:** heat the oven-dried soil strongly in a crucible until it stops glowing. The further loss in mass is organic matter that has burnt away.
- **Air:** add a measured volume of dry, loose soil (for example $50\ \text{cm}^3$) to a measured volume of water ($50\ \text{cm}^3$) in a cylinder and stir. The final volume is less than $100\ \text{cm}^3$ because water has filled the air spaces; the shortfall is the volume of air. Bubbles rising from soil dropped into water also show air.
- **Living organisms:** set up two flasks of lime water, one connected to fresh soil and one to soil that has been heated to kill the organisms; the lime water turns milky faster beside the fresh soil because its organisms respire and give off carbon dioxide.
- **Mineral matter:** the jar (sedimentation) test separates sand, silt and clay.

:::worked-solution[Moisture and organic matter content]
Nyasha weighs an empty tin ($20.0\ \text{g}$), the tin with fresh soil ($70.0\ \text{g}$), the tin after oven drying ($62.0\ \text{g}$) and the tin after strong heating ($59.6\ \text{g}$).

1. Mass of fresh soil $= 70.0 - 20.0 = 50.0\ \text{g}$.
2. Mass of oven-dry soil $= 62.0 - 20.0 = 42.0\ \text{g}$.
3. Water lost $= 50.0 - 42.0 = 8.0\ \text{g}$.
4. Moisture content (as a percentage of the dry soil) $= \frac{8.0}{42.0} \times 100 = 19.0\%$ (to one decimal place).
5. Organic matter burnt away $= 62.0 - 59.6 = 2.4\ \text{g}$, which is $\frac{2.4}{42.0} \times 100 = 5.7\%$ of the dry soil.
:::

## Soil water

### Three forms of soil water

| Form | Where it is held | Available to plants? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravitational water | Fills the large pores just after heavy rain; drains downward under gravity | Only briefly; it drains away within a day or two and carries nutrients with it (leaching) |
| Capillary water | Held in the small pores and as films around particles by surface tension | Yes: the main source of water for crops |
| Hygroscopic water | A very thin film held extremely tightly on the surface of each particle | No: held too strongly for roots to take up; removed only by oven drying |

### Field capacity and wilting point

After a soaking rain the soil is **saturated**: every pore is full. Gravitational water then drains away and the soil reaches **field capacity**, holding only capillary and hygroscopic water. As the crop uses water, the soil dries towards the **permanent wilting point**, when the remaining water is held too tightly. The **available water** lies between the two.

:::worked-solution[Available water]
A loam holds 32% water (by mass) at field capacity and 18% at the permanent wilting point.

1. Available water $= 32 - 18 = 14\%$ of the dry soil mass.
2. Water above 32% is gravitational and drains away; water below 18% is unavailable.
3. Clay soils hold more water at field capacity than loams, but much of it is below the wilting point, so loams often supply the most **available** water.
:::

### How water moves in soil

- **Infiltration:** rain enters the soil surface.
- **Percolation (drainage):** gravitational water moves down through large pores.
- **Capillary movement:** water moves through small pores from wetter to drier soil, in any direction, including upward towards a drying surface.
- **Vapour movement:** a little water moves as vapour from warm to cool soil.

## Soil organisms

### Macro-organisms

- **Earthworms** burrow, mix and aerate the soil, improve drainage, drag leaves underground and produce casts that build crumb structure.
- **Termites** break down dead wood and grass and mix soil, but some species damage crops, seedlings and timber.
- **Ants, millipedes, beetles and rodents** mix soil and break up residues. Some insect larvae, such as cutworms and white grubs, eat roots and stems.

### Micro-organisms

- **Bacteria** decompose organic matter and release nutrients; *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules and some free-living bacteria **fix nitrogen**; other bacteria change ammonium to nitrate (**nitrification**). Harmful: denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soil release nitrogen gas, and some cause disease (bacterial wilt).
- **Fungi** decompose tough residues and form mycorrhizae that help roots absorb phosphorus; others cause damping-off and root rots.
- **Nematodes** are tiny roundworms; some feed on bacteria, but root-knot nematodes damage tomatoes, tobacco and other crops.

:::example{title="Why a long-cropped field has few earthworms"}
Tatenda finds many earthworms in the school's grass paddock but almost none in a field cropped with maize for years and burned after harvest. Burning and continuous cultivation removed the organic matter earthworms feed on and exposed them to heat and drying. Returning residues, adding manure and reducing tillage would bring them back, improving aeration and structure.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling gravitational water the main water for crops"}
Gravitational water drains away quickly. Crops depend on **capillary** water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wet mass in the moisture calculation"}
State which basis you use. Most agriculture textbooks express moisture as a percentage of the **dry** soil mass: water lost ÷ dry mass × 100.
:::

:::mistake{title="All soil organisms are pests"}
Most soil organisms are useful decomposers. Name both useful and harmful roles when asked.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Constituents questions"}
For *state the importance*, give one clear role per constituent, such as "Air supplies oxygen for root respiration." For an experiment, give numbered steps, the measurement and the calculation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Soil water"}
Use the three terms exactly and say whether each is available to plants. Field capacity is reached **after** gravitational water has drained, not when the soil is saturated.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A good soil contains mineral matter, organic matter, water, air and living organisms (roughly 45 : 5 : 25 : 25 by volume in a loam).
- Gravitational water drains away, capillary water is available to crops, hygroscopic water is not.
- Field capacity is the water held after drainage; available water lies between field capacity and the permanent wilting point.
- Water moves by infiltration, percolation, capillarity and as vapour.
- Earthworms, bacteria and fungi are mostly useful; some termites, larvae, fungi, bacteria and nematodes are harmful.
:::
