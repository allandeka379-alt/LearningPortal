## Introduction

Zimbabwe's rain falls mainly between November and March, and even in that season a two- or three-week **dry spell** can wilt maize at tasselling. From April to October there is almost no rain at all. A farmer near Chinhoyi with a small dam can grow wheat or vegetables through the winter and save the summer crop in a dry spell; a farmer without water must wait for the rains. That difference is why irrigation matters.

Before a farmer chooses an irrigation method, two questions must be answered: **where** will the water come from, and **is it good enough** for crops? Examiners ask you to *explain* the need for irrigation, *list* and *compare* sources of water and *describe* how the suitability of water is judged.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Irrigation"}
The artificial application of water to land or crops to supply the moisture needed for growth when rainfall is not enough.
:::

:::definition{term="Groundwater"}
Water stored underground in the pores and cracks of rocks (aquifers), reached by wells and boreholes.
:::

:::definition{term="Surface water"}
Water in rivers, streams, dams and lakes.
:::

:::definition{term="Salinity"}
The concentration of dissolved salts in water or soil; high salinity makes it harder for roots to take up water.
:::

## Why irrigation is needed

1. **Seasonal rainfall**: a long dry season from about April to October, when no crop can grow without water.
2. **Unreliable rainfall**: late starts, early ends and mid-season dry spells reduce yields, especially in natural regions III to V.
3. **More crops per year**: winter crops such as wheat, potatoes and vegetables, and two or even three crops a year on the same land.
4. **Higher and more reliable yields** and better **quality**: larger fruit, even grading and fewer cracked or misshapen products.
5. **Timing**: crops can be planted early, before the rains, and produce can be sold when prices are high.
6. **Special uses**: establishing nurseries and orchards, and leaching salts out of the root zone.

Irrigation has costs and risks too: equipment, pumping and labour are expensive; poorly managed irrigation causes **waterlogging** and **salinisation**; and still water in canals can breed mosquitoes and the snails that carry bilharzia.

## Sources of irrigation water

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perennial rivers and streams | Large volumes; cheap to take where the river flows all year | Many rivers dry up in the dry season; may carry silt and pollution; permits needed |
| Dams (large and small) | Store summer floods for the dry season; can supply by gravity | Costly to build; lose water by evaporation and seepage; silt up |
| Boreholes | Reliable, clean groundwater close to fields; little evaporation loss | Costly to drill and pump; yields vary; water may be hard or salty; water table can fall |
| Shallow (hand-dug) wells | Cheap, built with local labour | Small yields; may dry up; easily polluted if not lined and covered |
| Springs | Clean, steady flow, often by gravity | Few farms have one; flow may be small |
| Stored rainwater (roof and ground catchment, tanks) | Free, clean water near the homestead | Small volumes; only during and soon after the rains |
| Treated waste water | Available all year near towns | Must be properly treated; risk of pathogens on food crops |

In Zimbabwe, the **Zimbabwe National Water Authority (ZINWA)** manages water resources, and taking river or dam water for commercial irrigation generally needs a **water permit** obtained through the catchment council.

:::worked-solution[How long will the dam last?]
A crop on a $100\ \text{m} \times 50\ \text{m}$ plot uses about $6\ \text{mm}$ of water a day in hot weather. A small farm dam holds $4\,500\ \text{m}^3$ of usable water. Assume the only use is the crop and ignore evaporation.

1. Area $= 100 \times 50 = 5\,000\ \text{m}^2$.
2. One millimetre over one square metre is one litre, so the daily use is $5\,000 \times 6 = 30\,000\ \text{L} = 30\ \text{m}^3$.
3. Days the dam lasts $= 4\,500 \div 30 = 150$ days, about five months: enough for most of the dry season.
4. In reality, evaporation and seepage from the dam and losses in the irrigation system shorten this time.
:::

### Protecting the source

A source is only useful while it lasts and stays clean, so farmers protect it:

- Keep the **catchment** above a dam under grass or trees, so less silt washes in and the dam does not fill up.
- Fence dams and river banks and water livestock at **troughs**, so animals do not trample the banks or foul the water.
- Line and cover wells, and site them uphill and well away from pit latrines, kraals and fuel or chemical stores.
- Do not mix or wash out pesticide sprayers near rivers, wells or dams.
- Do not pump more from a borehole than it can safely yield, or the water level falls and the borehole may fail.

## Is the water suitable?

Good irrigation water must not harm the crop, the soil, the equipment or the people who eat the produce.

| Quality factor | Why it matters | Simple test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dissolved salts (salinity) | Salts build up in the soil, make water harder for roots to absorb and scorch leaves | Electrical conductivity (EC) meter; evaporate a known volume and weigh the residue |
| Sodium | Too much sodium compared with calcium and magnesium breaks down soil structure, so the soil seals and drains poorly | Laboratory analysis |
| Silt and suspended matter | Blocks pipes, sprinklers and drip emitters; fills canals and dams | Let a jar of water stand and look at the sediment |
| pH | Very acid or alkaline water damages crops and equipment and affects nutrient availability | pH meter or indicator paper |
| Pollution (sewage, mine effluent, chemicals) | Pathogens on vegetables eaten raw; toxic metals; residues of farm chemicals | Laboratory tests for bacteria and chemicals |
| Specific toxic elements (for example boron) | Small excesses damage sensitive crops | Laboratory analysis |

:::worked-solution[A simple salt test]
Nyasha evaporates $250\ \text{mL}$ of borehole water to dryness in a clean, weighed dish and finds $0.45\ \text{g}$ of solid left behind.

1. Convert to litres: $250\ \text{mL} = 0.25\ \text{L}$.
2. Dissolved solids $= 0.45 \div 0.25 = 1.8\ \text{g/L}$.
3. She compares this with the laboratory's guideline for her crop. A high value means she should send a sample for full analysis and choose salt-tolerant crops or another source.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing a source"}
Chipo's farm near Masvingo has a seasonal river that stops flowing in July, a hand-dug well near the house and a site where a small dam could be built. For a winter vegetable garden she needs water from May to September. The river fails halfway; the well gives too little for more than a small garden. She plans a small dam to store summer floods, applies for a permit, and keeps the well for drinking water, which she protects with a lined, covered top.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking more water always means a higher yield"}
Over-irrigation causes waterlogging, leaching and salinisation. Irrigation is about the **right amount at the right time**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Judging water by appearance alone"}
Clear water can still be salty or polluted. Test salts with an EC meter or by evaporation, and test for bacteria and chemicals in a laboratory.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining the need for irrigation"}
Link each point to the local climate: "rain falls only from November to March, so winter crops such as wheat need irrigation". Two linked points earn more than four bare ones.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Comparing sources"}
Give an advantage **and** a disadvantage for each source named, and use the words reliable, volume, cost, quality and distance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Irrigation is needed because rainfall is seasonal and unreliable, and it allows winter crops, more crops a year, higher yields and better quality.
- Sources are rivers, dams, boreholes, wells, springs, stored rainwater and treated waste water, each with advantages and drawbacks.
- Water quality depends on salts, sodium, silt, pH, pollution and toxic elements.
- Test salts with an EC meter or by evaporation, silt by settling, pH with a meter, and pathogens and chemicals in a laboratory.
- Poor irrigation causes waterlogging, salinisation and health risks.
:::
