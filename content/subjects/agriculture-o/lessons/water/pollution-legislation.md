## Introduction

A farmer who irrigates vegetables from a river near a town may find the pump inlet choked with water weeds, the water green with algae and the fish gone. Further upstream, the same river collects fertiliser washed from fields, dung from cattle crossings, silt from eroded land, untreated sewage and waste from mining. Polluted water harms crops, livestock, fish and people, and cleaning it is expensive.

Because water is shared by everyone in a river basin, its use is controlled by law. This Form 4 topic covers the **causes and effects of water pollution**, **ways of reducing it** and how water is **managed in Zimbabwe**. ZIMSEC questions ask you to *explain* how a farming practice pollutes water, to *describe* eutrophication, to *discuss* ways of reducing pollution and to *describe* the roles of ZINWA, catchment councils and EMA.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water pollution"}
The contamination of water by substances or organisms that make it harmful or less useful to people, animals, crops or aquatic life.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
The over-enrichment of a water body with nutrients, especially nitrates and phosphates, causing excessive growth of algae and water plants, followed by a fall in dissolved oxygen when they die and decompose.
:::

:::definition{term="Siltation"}
The deposition of eroded soil particles in rivers, dams and canals.
:::

:::definition{term="Water permit"}
Written permission, issued through the catchment council, to abstract and use water for purposes other than primary (household) use, for example irrigation.
:::

## Causes of water pollution

### From agriculture

- **Fertilisers:** nitrates and phosphates wash off fields in runoff or leach down to groundwater, especially when applied in excess or just before heavy rain.
- **Pesticides:** spray drift, runoff, washing sprayers in rivers and careless disposal of containers put poisons into water.
- **Livestock wastes:** dung and urine from kraals, feedlots, piggeries and cattle crossings add nutrients and disease organisms.
- **Dip chemicals:** spills or poorly managed dip tanks can contaminate nearby streams.
- **Soil erosion:** silt makes water muddy and fills dams.

### From other sources

- **Sewage:** untreated or poorly treated sewage from towns adds nutrients and disease organisms (for example the bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid).
- **Industry:** factory effluents may contain chemicals, oils and heavy metals.
- **Mining:** waste rock and tailings, acid drainage, and mercury used in small-scale gold processing poison water; alluvial gold panning in riverbeds causes severe siltation.
- **Solid waste and oil:** litter, plastics and fuel spills from vehicles and machinery.

## Eutrophication step by step

1. Nitrates and phosphates from fertiliser runoff and sewage enter a river, dam or lake.
2. Algae and water plants (for example water hyacinth) grow very fast, covering the surface (an **algal bloom**).
3. The cover blocks light, so plants below the surface die.
4. **Bacteria** decompose the dead plants and algae, using up the dissolved oxygen in the water.
5. **Fish and other animals die** from lack of oxygen, and the water may smell bad and become toxic.

Lakes near cities in Zimbabwe, such as Lake Chivero, which supplies water to Harare, have suffered from water hyacinth and algal blooms linked to urban sewage and runoff.

:::worked-solution[Interpreting river samples]
Rudo's class tested a river above and below a vegetable-growing area. Nitrate rose from $2.0\ \text{mg/L}$ upstream to $9.5\ \text{mg/L}$ downstream, and dissolved oxygen fell from $8.0\ \text{mg/L}$ to $2.4\ \text{mg/L}$.

1. Increase in nitrate $= 9.5 - 2.0 = 7.5\ \text{mg/L}$.
2. Percentage increase $= \frac{7.5}{2.0} \times 100 = 375\%$.
3. Fall in dissolved oxygen $= 8.0 - 2.4 = 5.6\ \text{mg/L}$, which is $\frac{5.6}{8.0} \times 100 = 70\%$.
4. Interpretation: fertiliser from the gardens has enriched the river; algae grew and decomposing material used up oxygen, typical of **eutrophication**.
:::

## Effects of water pollution on agricultural production

- **Livestock:** polluted water causes disease (from sewage organisms) and poisoning (from pesticides, mining chemicals or toxic algae).
- **Irrigation:** water weeds and algae block pumps, pipes and canals; silt wears pumps and fills dams, reducing the water available for irrigation.
- **Crops:** vegetables irrigated with sewage-polluted water can carry disease organisms to consumers; chemicals in water can damage crops or build up in them.
- **Fish farming:** low oxygen and toxins kill fish.
- **Farm workers and families:** water-borne diseases reduce the health and working days of farm labour.
- **Costs:** treating water, clearing weeds and de-silting dams all cost money.

:::worked-solution[Siltation of a farm dam]
A farm dam held $400\,000\ \text{m}^3$ when built. A survey years later shows it now holds $340\,000\ \text{m}^3$.

1. Capacity lost $= 400\,000 - 340\,000 = 60\,000\ \text{m}^3$.
2. Percentage lost $= \frac{60\,000}{400\,000} \times 100 = 15\%$.
3. Effect: 15% less water for irrigation in the dry season. Controlling erosion in the catchment would slow further loss.
:::

## Reducing water pollution

| Source | Ways of reducing it |
| --- | --- |
| Fertilisers | Apply the right amount at the right time, split nitrogen doses, never apply just before heavy rain or close to water |
| Pesticides | Follow label instructions, never wash sprayers or containers in rivers, dispose of containers safely, avoid spraying in wind |
| Erosion and silt | Soil conservation, keep vegetation along river banks, no cultivation of stream banks |
| Livestock | Site kraals, piggeries and dip tanks away from water; provide troughs instead of letting animals stand in rivers |
| Sewage | Proper treatment works; latrines sited away from and downhill of wells and rivers |
| Mining and industry | Treat effluent before discharge; contain tailings; enforce environmental laws |

A strip of natural vegetation along a river (a **riverbank buffer**) traps silt and absorbs nutrients from runoff before they reach the water.

## Water management in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe's water law, in general terms, works like this:

- **Water belongs to the State.** No one owns the water in a river or under the ground; people are given permission to use it.
- **Water permits** replaced the older system of water rights. A permit is needed to abstract water for commercial uses such as irrigation, but **primary use** (for example water for drinking, cooking, washing and watering livestock at home) does not need one.
<!-- Reviewer: the summary of the Water Act (1998), the ZINWA Act and the Environmental Management Act is deliberately general; check the description of primary use and permits against the current Acts. -->

- **Catchment councils** are set up for the main river basins (for example the Manyame, Save and Runde catchments), with **sub-catchment councils** below them. They include representatives of water users, plan water use in their area and deal with permit applications.
- **ZINWA** (the Zimbabwe National Water Authority) manages and develops water resources: it runs many large dams, supplies raw water to farmers and towns, monitors rivers and groundwater and collects charges for water use.
- **EMA** (the Environmental Management Agency) protects the environment: it controls the discharge of effluents, licenses polluters, carries out environmental impact assessments and enforces rules against stream-bank cultivation and other damage.

:::example{title="Applying for irrigation water"}
Nyasha wants to pump water from a river to irrigate 2 ha of wheat in winter. She contacts the sub-catchment council for her area, which advises her on how much water is available. She applies for a water permit, stating the amount she needs and the period of use, and pays the charges set for that water. Her permit lets her use the water within the limits set, so that farmers downstream also receive their share. If her pump station needs works next to the river, EMA may require an assessment of their environmental impact.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing eutrophication as 'fertiliser kills the fish'"}
The fertiliser does not poison the fish directly. Nutrients cause algal growth; when the algae die, **bacterial decomposition uses up oxygen** and the fish suffocate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up ZINWA and EMA"}
ZINWA manages and supplies **water**; EMA protects the **environment** and controls pollution. Catchment councils plan and allocate water locally.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying farmers own the river on their land"}
Under Zimbabwe's water law, water belongs to the State; farmers need a permit for commercial use.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain a cause"}
Give the source, the pollutant and the effect: "Fertiliser applied before heavy rain is washed into a dam; the nitrates and phosphates cause algal blooms."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss water management"}
Describe what each body does and how it helps farmers share water fairly and keep it clean. Keep your answer factual and balanced.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water is polluted by fertilisers, pesticides, livestock waste, silt, sewage, industry and mining.
- Eutrophication: nutrients → algal bloom → plants die → bacteria use oxygen → fish die.
- Pollution harms livestock, irrigation equipment and supply, crops, fish farming and farm workers' health.
- Reduce it by careful use of chemicals, soil conservation, river-bank buffers, good siting of kraals and latrines, and treating effluent.
- Water belongs to the State; permits, catchment councils, ZINWA and EMA manage and protect it.
:::
