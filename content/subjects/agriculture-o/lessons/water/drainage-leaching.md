## Introduction

In a wet January, the low end of a maize field near Marondera turns yellow. The plants there are short, their lower leaves are pale and the soil smells sour; water lies in the furrows for days after each storm. Higher up the same field, on a free-draining sandy soil, the maize looks pale for a different reason: the nitrogen fertiliser applied at planting has been washed below the roots by the heavy rains.

Both problems are about water moving, or failing to move, through the soil. Too little drainage gives **waterlogging**; too much water passing through a light soil gives **leaching**. Examination questions ask you to *state* how water is lost from soil, to *explain* the effects of waterlogging on roots and soil organisms, to *describe* drainage methods and to *explain* how leaching can be reduced.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Drainage"}
The downward movement of excess (gravitational) water through the soil under the force of gravity; also the removal of excess water from land by drains.
:::

:::definition{term="Waterlogging"}
The condition in which all or nearly all the pore spaces of a soil are filled with water for a long time, leaving little or no air.
:::

:::definition{term="Leaching"}
The washing of soluble plant nutrients, such as nitrate, potassium, calcium and magnesium, down through the soil and out of the root zone by drainage water.
:::

:::definition{term="Water table"}
The upper surface of the zone in which the soil or rock is saturated with water. Where it is close to the surface, land becomes waterlogged.
:::

## How water is lost from the soil

1. **Evaporation** from the soil surface, fastest from bare, hot, windy surfaces.
2. **Transpiration** by crops and weeds, which take water up through their roots and lose it from their leaves.
3. **Surface runoff**, when rain falls faster than it can soak in.
4. **Drainage (percolation)** of gravitational water downwards, below the reach of roots.

:::worked-solution[A soil water balance after a storm]
A storm drops $60\ \text{mm}$ of rain on a field. Runoff is $12\ \text{mm}$, evaporation in the following days is $6\ \text{mm}$, and the soil stores $22\ \text{mm}$ as it is refilled to field capacity. How much water drained below the root zone?

1. Water entering the soil $= 60 - 12 = 48\ \text{mm}$.
2. Water not lost by evaporation $= 48 - 6 = 42\ \text{mm}$.
3. Drainage $= 42 - 22 = 20\ \text{mm}$.
4. Check: $12 + 6 + 22 + 20 = 60\ \text{mm}$. On a sandy soil, which stores less, drainage would be larger, and so would leaching.
:::

## Waterlogging

### Causes

- An **impermeable layer** in the subsoil: a clay pan, a hardpan or a plough pan stops water draining down.
- A **high water table**, for example in vleis (dambos) and valley bottoms.
- **Low-lying land** that receives runoff and seepage from the slopes above.
- **Heavy, prolonged rain** or **over-irrigation**.
- **Compaction** by machines or animals and a fine-textured soil with poor structure.

### Effects

- **Root respiration fails.** Water fills the pores, so roots run short of oxygen. They respire anaerobically, release little energy and may die, so the crop takes up less water and fewer mineral salts. Waterlogged plants often wilt and turn yellow even though the soil is full of water.
- **Soil organisms suffer.** Aerobic bacteria, fungi and earthworms die or leave. Decomposition and **nitrification** slow down, and **denitrifying bacteria** turn nitrate into nitrogen gas, so nitrogen is lost.
- **Toxic substances** such as reduced forms of iron and manganese build up and the soil becomes grey and mottled.
- **Seeds rot** instead of germinating; root diseases increase.
- Wet soil **warms slowly** and cannot be **cultivated** or entered by machinery until it dries; working it wet ruins its structure.

Signs of waterlogging include standing water, grey or mottled soil, reeds and sedges, a sour smell and yellow, stunted crops.

## Improving drainage

| Method | Description | Advantages and drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open ditches | Channels dug along and across the field lead water to a main drain and an outlet | Cheap and easy to inspect; take up land, hinder machinery and need regular clearing |
| Tile drains | Perforated clay or plastic pipes laid in trenches about a metre deep and covered with gravel and soil | No land lost, work for many years; costly to install |
| Mole drains | A bullet-shaped mole pulled through a clay subsoil by a tractor leaves an unlined channel | Cheap for clay soils; last only a few years; need a stable clay subsoil |
| Rubble (French) drains | Trenches filled with stones or brushwood | Use local materials; may silt up |
| Ridging and raised beds | Crops are grown on ridges or beds above the wet furrows | Simple for smallholders; does not lower the water table |
| Breaking pans | Subsoiling or ripping breaks impermeable layers | Lets water drain down; needs power |

Field drains are often laid in a **herringbone** pattern, with side drains joining a main drain at an angle, or a **gridiron** pattern of parallel drains. Deep-rooted, high-water-use trees are sometimes planted on wet sites to lower the water table, and **organic matter** improves structure so water moves through the soil more easily. Above all, avoid over-irrigation.

## Leaching

### Causes

- **Heavy rainfall** or **over-irrigation**, which sends much water down through the soil.
- **Sandy soils** with large pores, little clay and little humus, which hold few nutrients.
- **Bare soil** with no roots to take up nutrients.
- Applying all of a **soluble fertiliser at once**, especially nitrogen just before heavy rain.

### Effects

- Loss of nutrients, especially **nitrate**, potassium, calcium and magnesium, so crops show **deficiency** symptoms and yields fall.
- Money wasted on fertiliser.
- **Soil acidity** increases as calcium and magnesium are removed.
- Nitrate and other chemicals may **pollute groundwater** and streams.
- Leached materials such as clay and iron can collect in the subsoil and, over long periods, help to form a hardpan.

### Control

1. **Split** nitrogen applications: a little at planting and the rest as top dressing when the crop can use it.
2. **Time** applications to avoid heavy storms, and irrigate only as much as the soil can hold.
3. Add **organic matter** to raise the soil's capacity to hold nutrients.
4. Keep the soil **covered** with crops, cover crops or mulch, so roots take nutrients up.
5. Use **deep-rooted crops** in the rotation to recover nutrients from deeper layers.
6. **Lime** acid soils to replace leached calcium and magnesium.

:::worked-solution[The value of splitting nitrogen]
Assume a farmer applies $300\ \text{kg}$ of ammonium nitrate (34.5% N) to a hectare of maize on sandy soil. Assume 30% of the nitrogen is leached if it is all applied at planting, but only 10% if the same amount is split.

1. Nitrogen applied $= 300 \times \frac{34.5}{100} = 103.5\ \text{kg N}$.
2. Lost with one application $= 30\% \times 103.5 = 31.05\ \text{kg N}$.
3. Lost with split applications $= 10\% \times 103.5 = 10.35\ \text{kg N}$.
4. Nitrogen saved $= 31.05 - 10.35 = 20.7\ \text{kg N}$ per hectare, which stays available to the crop.
:::

:::example{title="Solving both problems on one farm"}
Tatenda's farm has a vlei at the bottom and sandy land on the slopes. In the vlei, he digs an open main drain to the stream and grows vegetables on raised beds in winter, when the water table is lower. On the sandy slopes, he adds kraal manure, splits his top dressing into two doses and keeps a mulch, so less nitrate is leached.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying waterlogged plants wilt from too much water"}
They wilt because roots short of **oxygen** cannot respire well and take up water. Explain the oxygen link.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing leaching with erosion"}
Leaching moves **dissolved** nutrients **down** through the soil. Erosion moves **soil particles** away over the surface.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drains with no outlet"}
A drainage system must lead water to a lower outlet, such as a stream or main drain. A ditch that ends in the field only moves the problem.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Effects of waterlogging"}
Use a chain: pores full of water → no oxygen → poor root respiration → less water and nutrient uptake → yellowing, wilting, low yield. Add the effect on organisms: nitrification stops, denitrification causes nitrogen loss.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Leaching questions"}
Name the nutrients lost (nitrate especially), the soils most at risk (sandy, low in organic matter) and give control methods with reasons.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water leaves the soil by evaporation, transpiration, runoff and drainage.
- Waterlogging comes from impermeable layers, a high water table, low-lying land, heavy rain or over-irrigation.
- It starves roots and aerobic organisms of oxygen, stops nitrification, causes denitrification and delays cultivation.
- Drain wet land with open ditches, tile, mole and rubble drains, ridges and beds, and by breaking pans.
- Leaching washes soluble nutrients below the roots; reduce it by split applications, organic matter, cover and liming.
:::
