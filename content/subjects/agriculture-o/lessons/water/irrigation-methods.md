## Introduction

Drive past irrigated land in Zimbabwe and you will see very different systems. Sugar cane in the Lowveld is watered by furrows and by pivots; a commercial wheat field near Chinhoyi turns slowly under a centre pivot; a tomato grower near Mutare runs thin black drip lines along the beds; and a school garden is watered by pupils with buckets and watering cans. Each method suits a particular crop, soil, water supply and budget.

Both boards ask you to *describe* how each method works, give its *advantages and disadvantages*, *explain* its effect on yield and quality, *discuss* the factors in choosing a system and carry out simple *calculations* of water quantity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Surface irrigation"}
Irrigation in which water flows over the soil surface by gravity, as in flood, basin, border and furrow irrigation.
:::

:::definition{term="Overhead irrigation"}
Irrigation in which water is sprayed over the crop like rain, by sprinklers or a centre pivot.
:::

:::definition{term="Drip (trickle) irrigation"}
Irrigation in which water is delivered slowly, drop by drop, through emitters on pipes laid along the rows, directly to the root zone of each plant.
:::

:::definition{term="Irrigation efficiency"}
The percentage of the water supplied that is actually stored in the root zone and available to the crop.
:::

## Surface irrigation

- **Flood irrigation**: water is let onto a field and spreads over it. Simple but wasteful and uneven.
- **Basin irrigation**: level plots surrounded by low earth walls (bunds) are filled with water, which soaks in. Suits rice, orchards (a basin round each tree) and pastures on flat land.
- **Border (strip) irrigation**: long, gently sloping strips between parallel bunds; water flows down each strip as a sheet.
- **Furrow irrigation**: water runs along furrows between ridges on which the crop grows. Water is fed into each furrow from a supply canal by **siphons** or from **gated pipes**. Suits row crops such as maize, sugar cane, cotton and vegetables.

**Advantages**: cheap to run, no pumping where water comes by gravity, simple equipment, little energy. **Disadvantages**: needs level or gently sloping land and land levelling; large losses by deep drainage and runoff, so lower efficiency (typically about 50-70%); uneven watering on sandy soils; risk of waterlogging and salinisation; much labour to manage the flow.

## Overhead irrigation

- **Sprinklers**: water under pressure from a pump is carried by main and lateral pipes to sprinklers on risers; rotating sprinklers spread it in circles that overlap. Systems may be portable, semi-portable or permanent.
- **Centre pivot**: a long pipe on wheeled towers turns slowly around a fixed pivot at the centre of a circular field, spraying water from nozzles along its length.

**Advantages**: suits uneven land and sandy soils; even application; higher efficiency (typically about 70-80%); can apply small amounts often; little land lost to canals; pivots need little labour. **Disadvantages**: high cost of pipes, pumps and energy; wind blows the spray and causes uneven watering; evaporation losses in hot weather; wet leaves encourage fungal diseases and wash off sprays; water must be clean enough not to block nozzles.

## Drip irrigation

Water passes through a **filter** and along plastic pipes to **drip lines** with emitters beside each plant, at low pressure. Fertiliser can be dissolved in the water (**fertigation**).

**Advantages**: highest efficiency (typically about 90%), because only the root zone is wetted; leaves stay dry, so fewer leaf diseases; fewer weeds between rows; works on slopes and with small water supplies; saves labour; even, high-quality produce. **Disadvantages**: expensive to buy; emitters block with silt, algae and salts, so filtering and flushing are essential; lines are damaged by rodents, sun and implements; needs skill to manage.

## Hand watering

Watering cans, buckets and hosepipes are used in **nurseries**, small gardens and for transplants. Cheap and precise for small areas, but very labour-intensive and impossible on a large scale.

## Effects on yield and quality

Irrigation that keeps the soil between field capacity and the point where the crop is stressed gives **higher yields** and **better quality**: larger, evenly sized fruit and tubers, fewer cracked tomatoes and less blossom-end rot. Uneven watering lowers both. Overhead watering in the evening can increase leaf diseases, and water splashing soil onto fruit can spoil it, while drip keeps produce clean. Over-irrigation leaches nutrients and causes waterlogging.

## Water calculations

One millimetre of water spread over one square metre is **one litre**. So:

$$\text{litres} = \text{area in m}^2 \times \text{depth in mm}$$

:::worked-solution[Water for one irrigation]
A farmer wants to apply $25\ \text{mm}$ of water to $0.5\ \text{ha}$.

1. Area $= 0.5 \times 10\,000 = 5\,000\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Water $= 5\,000 \times 25 = 125\,000\ \text{L}$.
3. In cubic metres: $125\,000 \div 1\,000 = 125\ \text{m}^3$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Allowing for efficiency]
A crop on $2\,000\ \text{m}^2$ needs $30\ \text{mm}$ stored in the root zone. Assume furrow irrigation is 60% efficient and drip irrigation 90% efficient.

1. Water needed by the crop $= 2\,000 \times 30 = 60\,000\ \text{L}$.
2. Furrow: water to supply $= 60\,000 \div 0.60 = 100\,000\ \text{L}$.
3. Drip: water to supply $= 60\,000 \div 0.90 = 66\,667\ \text{L}$ (to the nearest litre).
4. Drip saves $100\,000 - 66\,667 = 33\,333\ \text{L}$ for every irrigation.
:::

For hand watering: a bed $10\ \text{m} \times 1\ \text{m}$ given $10\ \text{mm}$ needs $10 \times 10 = 100\ \text{L}$, or ten $10\ \text{L}$ cans.

## Choosing a system

| Factor | How it affects the choice |
| --- | --- |
| Water supply | Little water, or expensive water, favours drip; plentiful gravity water favours furrows |
| Water quality | Silty or dirty water blocks drip and sprinklers unless filtered |
| Soil | Sandy soils lose furrow water by deep drainage, so sprinklers or drip suit them; clay loams suit furrows |
| Slope | Surface methods need level or gently sloping land; sprinklers and drip suit uneven land |
| Crop | High-value vegetables and fruit justify drip; closely spaced wheat suits sprinklers or pivots; row crops suit furrows |
| Cost and energy | Surface is cheap to install and run; pivots and drip need capital and pumping |
| Labour and skills | Furrows need much labour; pivots and drip need little labour but technical skill |
| Climate | Strong wind makes sprinkling uneven; hot, dry weather raises evaporation losses |

:::example{title="Matching methods to farms"}
Farai has a sandy soil, a borehole with a small yield and a market for tomatoes: drip suits him, because it saves water and keeps leaves dry. His uncle in a valley has a clay loam soil, level land and a canal fed by gravity from a dam, and grows maize and sugar beans: furrow irrigation suits him, because it is cheap to run and his soil holds water well.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to convert hectares to square metres"}
$0.5\ \text{ha}$ is $5\,000\ \text{m}^2$. Using 0.5 in the formula gives a tiny, impossible answer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling drip irrigation cheap"}
Drip **saves water and labour**, but it is expensive to buy and needs filtering and care.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring disease"}
Overhead irrigation wets leaves, which favours fungal diseases. This is a key disadvantage examiners expect.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe and explain"}
To *describe* a method, say how the water gets to the plant. To *explain* an advantage, give the reason: "drip has high efficiency **because** only the root zone is wetted, so little water is lost by evaporation and drainage".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Factors in choosing"}
Use the situation in the question. If it mentions a slope, sandy soil, scarce water or a high-value crop, use those words in your answer.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Surface methods (flood, basin, border, furrow) are cheap but need level land and waste more water.
- Sprinklers and centre pivots suit uneven land and sandy soils but cost more, are affected by wind and wet the leaves.
- Drip is the most efficient and keeps leaves dry, but is costly and needs clean, filtered water.
- Litres = area (m²) × depth (mm); divide by the efficiency to find the water to supply.
- Choose by water supply and quality, soil, slope, crop, cost, labour and climate.
:::
