## Introduction

Most of Zimbabwe receives its rain in a few months, from about November to March, often in heavy storms separated by dry spells of two or three weeks. In natural regions IV and V the total is low as well as unreliable. Yet much of the rain that does fall is lost: it runs off bare fields into rivers, or evaporates from the soil surface within days. A farmer who can keep more of that water in the soil, or catch it and store it, can carry a crop through a dry spell, water a garden in the dry season and give livestock a drink close to home.

This topic covers why water conservation matters, how to conserve water on arable land, how to harvest and store rainwater, and the main groundwater sources. ZIMSEC questions ask you to *describe* methods, to *explain* how they conserve water, to *calculate* the water collected from a roof and to *suggest* suitable methods for a given farm.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water conservation"}
Using water carefully and reducing its loss by runoff, evaporation, seepage and waste, so that more is available when and where it is needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainwater harvesting"}
Collecting and storing rainwater that runs off roofs, rocks, roads or land surfaces, or trapping it in the field where it falls.
:::

:::definition{term="Tied ridges"}
Ridges along the contour joined at intervals by small cross-ridges (ties), forming basins that hold rainwater in the field until it soaks in.
:::

:::definition{term="Runoff coefficient"}
The fraction of rain falling on a surface that runs off and can be collected, for example about 0.8 for a metal roof (some water is lost by splashing and evaporation).
:::

## Why conserve water?

- Rainfall is **seasonal, unreliable and low** in much of the country, with frequent dry spells and droughts.
- Water is needed for **crops, livestock, people and industry**, and demand keeps growing.
- Lost runoff also carries away **soil**; keeping water in the field reduces erosion.
- Over-use of groundwater lowers the **water table**, so wells and boreholes dry up.
- Stored water allows **dry-season production**, such as vegetable gardens, which improves food supply and income.

## Conserving water on arable land

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Mulching | Covers the soil, reducing evaporation and runoff and keeping the soil cool |
| Tied ridges | Basins between ridges and ties hold rain where it falls, so it infiltrates instead of running off |
| Potholes and planting pits | Small pits around plants collect and hold runoff close to the roots |
| Infiltration pits | Pits dug at intervals in contour ridge channels hold runoff so it soaks into the soil |
| Contour cultivation and conservation tillage | Rows across the slope slow runoff; undisturbed soil with good structure lets water in |
| Weed control | Weeds compete with the crop and transpire soil water; removing them early saves water |
| Adding organic matter | Humus increases the water-holding capacity of the soil |
| Windbreaks and shading | Reduce wind speed and temperature, lowering evaporation and transpiration |
| Efficient irrigation | Watering early in the morning or late afternoon, drip irrigation and watering only when needed reduce waste |
| Suitable crops and timely planting | Drought-tolerant crops such as sorghum and millets, and planting with the first effective rains, make the best use of the water available |

## Harvesting rainwater

### From roofs

Gutters along the edges of a roof lead water through a downpipe into a storage tank. A **first-flush device** or a simple diverter discards the first few litres of each storm, which carry dust and bird droppings. Tanks should be covered to keep out light, mosquitoes and dirt, and fitted with a tap above the bottom so sediment stays behind.

The amount collected depends on the roof area, the rainfall and the runoff coefficient:

$$
\text{Volume (L)} = \text{roof area (m}^2) \times \text{rainfall (mm)} \times \text{runoff coefficient}
$$

:::worked-solution[Water from a classroom roof]
A school roof measures $10\ \text{m} \times 6\ \text{m}$. In January $150\ \text{mm}$ of rain falls. Assume a runoff coefficient of 0.8.

1. Roof area $= 10 \times 6 = 60\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Rain falling on the roof $= 60 \times 150 = 9\,000\ \text{L}$ (since $1\ \text{mm}$ on $1\ \text{m}^2 = 1\ \text{L}$).
3. Water collected $= 9\,000 \times 0.8 = 7\,200\ \text{L}$.
4. A cylindrical tank $2\ \text{m}$ in diameter and $2\ \text{m}$ high holds $\pi \times 1^2 \times 2 = 6.28\ \text{m}^3$, about $6\,283\ \text{L}$, so it would overflow in January: the school needs a larger tank, or a second one.
:::

### From land surfaces

- **Rock catchments:** low walls on bare granite outcrops (dwalas) lead runoff into a tank or reservoir.
- **Road and yard runoff:** water from roads, paths and compacted yards is led into ponds or pits beside gardens.
- **In-field harvesting:** tied ridges, basins and pits trap rain where it falls.
- **Ponds and pans:** shallow excavations, sometimes lined with clay or plastic, store runoff for livestock and gardens.

## Storing water

| Store | Description | Uses and problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tanks | Brick, ferro-cement or plastic tanks fed by roofs or rock catchments | Clean water for people and gardens; limited volume; must be covered |
| Weirs | A low wall built across a river to raise the water level and hold water behind it | Supplies water for pumping and livestock; may silt up |
| Small earth dams | An earth wall across a valley stores runoff from the catchment | Large volumes for irrigation and livestock; evaporation and siltation losses; spillway needed |
| Ponds | Small excavated or embanked stores | Cheap; high evaporation; mosquito breeding |

Evaporation from open water is high in the hot season, so deep, narrow stores lose less water than wide, shallow ones. Protecting the catchment with grass cover reduces **siltation**.

## Groundwater sources

- **Springs:** groundwater emerges naturally; fence off and protect the eye of the spring and pipe water to a trough.
- **Wells:** hand-dug to below the water table and lined; shallow wells may dry up late in the dry season and must be covered.
- **Boreholes:** drilled deep into an aquifer, cased and fitted with a pump; reliable but expensive.

Site wells and boreholes uphill of and away from latrines, kraals and dip tanks.

:::example{title="Choosing methods for a farm in natural region IV"}
Sipho farms near Masvingo on sandy soil with about 500 mm of erratic rain. He has a homestead with an iron roof and a granite outcrop near his garden.

1. In his fields he uses **tied ridges** and **mulch**, so storm water soaks in and evaporation falls.
2. He grows **sorghum** as well as maize, because it tolerates dry spells better.
3. He fits **gutters and a tank** to the roof for household water.
4. He builds a low wall on the **rock outcrop** to lead runoff into a covered tank for a dry-season garden, watered with drip lines early in the morning.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the runoff coefficient"}
Not all rain on a roof reaches the tank. If a coefficient is given, multiply by it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing units"}
$1\ \text{mm}$ of rain on $1\ \text{m}^2$ gives $1\ \text{L}$; $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1\,000\ \text{L}$. Check whether the answer is wanted in litres or cubic metres.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking weeds only compete for nutrients"}
Weeds also use a great deal of soil water. Early weeding is a water-conservation method.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe and explain methods"}
For each method, say what it is and **how it saves water**: "Tied ridges form basins that hold rain until it infiltrates, so less runs off."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Suggest questions"}
Match the methods to the situation in the question: rainfall, soil, slope, available roofs or rocks, and the purpose (field crops, garden or livestock).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water must be conserved because rainfall is seasonal, erratic and often low, and demand is high.
- Field methods: mulch, tied ridges, potholes, infiltration pits, contour cultivation, weed control, organic matter, windbreaks and efficient irrigation.
- Roof harvest (L) = roof area (m²) × rainfall (mm) × runoff coefficient.
- Store water in covered tanks, weirs, small dams and ponds; reduce evaporation and siltation.
- Springs, wells and boreholes supply groundwater; protect them from pollution.
:::
