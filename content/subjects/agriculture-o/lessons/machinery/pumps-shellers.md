## Introduction

**Appropriate technology** means tools and machines that suit the people who use them: affordable, simple to repair with local skills and parts, and matched to the scale of the farm. On a smallholding near Gokwe, a treadle pump worked by foot can irrigate a vegetable garden from a shallow well, and a hand-cranked sheller can shell a family's maize in a fraction of the time it takes to rub cobs by hand. Larger farms use engine-driven centrifugal pumps and power shellers.

ZIMSEC expects you to *identify* the parts of hand- and power-operated irrigation pumps, *explain* how they work, *describe* their routine maintenance, and *identify* the parts of maize and groundnut shellers and *explain* their functions and operating principles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Appropriate technology"}
Technology that is affordable, simple to operate and maintain with local skills and materials, and suited to the scale and needs of the farm.
:::

:::definition{term="Suction lift"}
The vertical height from the water surface up to a pump that draws water by suction. Air pressure pushes the water up, so the lift is limited.
:::

:::definition{term="Priming"}
Filling the casing and suction pipe of a pump with water before starting, so that it can draw water instead of air.
:::

:::definition{term="Shelling"}
Removing grains from maize cobs, or kernels from groundnut pods.
:::

## The treadle pump

**Parts:** two **cylinders**, each with a **piston** fitted with a leather or rubber seal; two **treadles** (foot pedals) linked by a rope or rocker so that one rises as the other falls; **inlet (suction) valves** and **outlet valves** (simple flap valves); a **suction pipe** with a **strainer** (and often a foot valve) in the water; an **outlet** or delivery pipe; and a frame.

**How it works:**

1. The operator steps from one treadle to the other, as if walking on the spot.
2. As a piston **rises**, it lowers the pressure in its cylinder. **Atmospheric pressure** on the water surface pushes water up the suction pipe, through the inlet valve and into the cylinder.
3. As that piston **falls**, the inlet valve closes and the water is forced out through the outlet valve.
4. The two cylinders work alternately, so the flow is almost continuous.

Because air pressure does the lifting, a suction pump can raise water only a limited height: in theory under about 10 m, in practice a treadle pump is used where the water is only a few metres below the pump. It can also push water a short distance uphill or along a hose.

:::worked-solution[Output of a treadle pump]
Assume each stroke of a treadle pump delivers about $1.5\ \text{L}$ and the operator makes 50 strokes a minute.

1. Output per minute $= 1.5 \times 50 = 75\ \text{L/min}$.
2. Output per hour $= 75 \times 60 = 4\,500\ \text{L/h}$.
3. Time to fill a $2\,000\ \text{L}$ tank $= 2\,000 \div 75 = 26.7$ minutes (to one decimal place).
:::

## The piston (lift) hand pump

Many rural boreholes in Zimbabwe are fitted with a lever-operated **piston pump** such as the bush pump. A **handle** (lever) moves a **pump rod** up and down; at the bottom of the rod, inside a **cylinder** placed **below** the water level in the borehole, a **plunger** with a valve and a **foot (non-return) valve** lift water up the **rising main** to the spout. Because the cylinder sits under water, the water is **lifted**, not sucked, so these pumps work from depths far beyond the suction limit.

## The centrifugal pump

**Parts:** an **impeller** (a wheel with curved vanes) mounted on a **shaft**, turning inside a spiral **casing (volute)**; an **inlet** at the centre (the eye); an **outlet**; **bearings** and a **seal or gland** where the shaft leaves the casing; a **priming plug**; a **suction pipe** with a **foot valve and strainer**; and a driving **engine or electric motor**.

**How it works:**

1. The pump is **primed**: the casing and suction pipe are filled with water.
2. The engine spins the impeller fast. The vanes fling water outwards by **centrifugal force** into the casing.
3. The spiral casing slows the water down and turns its speed into **pressure**, so it leaves the outlet under pressure for sprinklers or a long pipe.
4. Water leaving the centre creates low pressure at the eye, so more water is pushed in up the suction pipe. The foot valve keeps the pipe full when the pump stops.

Centrifugal pumps deliver large volumes smoothly, but they cannot pump air: without priming they simply spin.

## Routine maintenance of pumps

| Pump | Routine maintenance |
| --- | --- |
| Treadle and piston pumps | Replace worn piston seals and valve flaps; oil the pulley or pivot; tighten bolts; clean the strainer; keep the suction pipe end off the mud; store in the shade |
| Centrifugal pump and engine | Check engine oil and fuel daily and change oil as the manual says; clean the air filter; grease bearings; check the gland for leaks; check belt tension; clean the strainer and foot valve; prime before starting; drain and store dry when not in use |

## Maize shellers

**Hand-held tube sheller:** a short metal or wooden tube with ridges inside. Twisting it along a dry cob rubs the grains off. Cheap but slow.

**Hand-operated (crank) disc sheller:** a **hopper or feed throat**; a **toothed (spiked) disc** turned by a **handle (crank)** and **flywheel**; a **spring-loaded pressure plate** that holds the cob against the teeth; a **grain outlet** below; and a **cob outlet**. The teeth catch the grains and knock them off as the cob is drawn through and turned; the spring adjusts to different cob sizes.

**Power-driven drum sheller:** a **hopper**; a **rotating drum or cylinder** fitted with beaters or spikes inside a **concave** (a curved screen); a **fan (blower)**; and separate outlets for grain and cobs. Cobs are beaten and rubbed against the concave; grains fall through the screen; the fan blows away chaff and husk pieces. It is driven by an engine, electric motor or tractor power take-off.

:::worked-solution[Hand or power sheller?]
Assume a hand-cranked sheller shells about $60\ \text{kg}$ of grain an hour and a hired power sheller about $600\ \text{kg}$ an hour. Tendai has $1.2\ \text{t}$ of maize to shell.

1. Convert: $1.2\ \text{t} = 1\,200\ \text{kg}$.
2. Hand sheller: $1\,200 \div 60 = 20$ hours of work.
3. Power sheller: $1\,200 \div 600 = 2$ hours.
4. Tendai weighs the cost of hiring against 20 hours of family labour; for seed maize, the gentler hand sheller may be preferred to reduce damaged grain.
:::

## Groundnut shellers

A hand-operated groundnut sheller has a **hopper**, a curved **perforated screen (grate)** at the bottom, and a **handle** that rocks a **beater (shoe)** back and forth over the screen. The pods are pressed and rubbed against the screen until they crack; **kernels and shell pieces fall through** the holes and are then **winnowed** to blow away the shells. The screen size is chosen to suit the size of the kernels, so they pass through without splitting. Power groundnut shellers work on the same rubbing principle with a rotor and a blower.

Groundnuts for **seed** are usually shelled by hand, or very carefully, shortly before planting, because damaged or split kernels germinate poorly.

## Correct and safe use of shellers

1. Shell only well-**dried** crop; damp grain smears and is damaged.
2. Remove stones, wire and other objects that can break teeth or screens.
3. Adjust the spring, clearance or screen to the cob or kernel size to reduce broken grain.
4. Feed steadily; do not overload or force the machine.
5. Keep **hands, loose clothing and hair** away from moving parts; keep guards in place; stop the engine before clearing a blockage.
6. Wear a **dust mask** where dust is heavy, and keep children away.
7. After use, clean the machine, oil moving parts and store it under cover.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the pump 'sucks' water up"}
The pump lowers the pressure; **atmospheric pressure pushes** the water up. That is why suction lift is limited.
:::

:::mistake{title="Starting a centrifugal pump dry"}
Without priming the impeller only spins in air, and running dry can damage the seal.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Treadle pumps use two pistons worked by foot; atmospheric pressure pushes water up, so suction lift is limited to a few metres.
- Piston hand pumps on boreholes have the cylinder below water level and lift water from depth.
- Centrifugal pumps use a spinning impeller in a volute casing; they must be primed and give large flows under pressure.
- Maintain pumps by replacing seals and valves, oiling and greasing, cleaning strainers and checking engines.
- Maize shellers rub or beat grains off cobs; groundnut shellers rub pods against a screen; use them dry, adjusted, steadily fed and guarded.
:::
