## Introduction

In the communal areas around Masvingo, Gokwe and Bulawayo, much of the ploughing, planting, weeding and carting is done by oxen and donkeys. The animal can only turn its strength into useful work through the **yoke or harness** that connects it to the plough or cart. A well-made, well-fitted harness lets the animal pull hard all day; a rough or badly fitted one causes sores, makes the animal unwilling to work and wastes its power.

ZIMSEC asks you, in Form 1, to list and describe types of yokes, label their parts and describe the wood used, and in Form 2 to tell breast-band from collar harnesses, label their parts, describe materials and harness specific animals.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Yoke"}
A wooden beam fitted to the necks or heads of draught cattle, usually in pairs, through which they pull an implement or cart.
:::

:::definition{term="Harness"}
The set of straps, bands and fittings by which an animal (especially a donkey or horse) is attached to and pulls an implement or cart.
:::

:::definition{term="Span"}
A team of draught animals working together, for example two, four or six oxen yoked in pairs one behind the other.
:::

:::definition{term="Trace"}
A strap, rope or chain on each side of a harnessed animal that carries the pull from the breast-band or collar back to the load.
:::

## Types of yokes

| Feature | Withers (neck) yoke | Head yoke |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position | Rests on top of the neck, just in front of the withers (the hump of zebu cattle) | Tied to the head behind the horns, or to the horns themselves |
| How the pull is taken | Through the shoulders and hump against the beam | Through the head and neck muscles |
| Advantages | Simple to make and fit; the animal moves its head freely; suits humped cattle; the most common yoke in Zimbabwe | Firm control; animals can hold back (brake) a load such as a cart going downhill |
| Disadvantages | Can rub sores if rough or badly fitted; less able to hold back a load | Restricts head movement; needs strong horns; must be shaped for the animals' heads |

Yokes may be **single** (for one animal) or **double** (for a pair). In a span, pairs are yoked one behind the other along a **trek chain**.

### Parts of a double withers yoke

Described from top to bottom, as a labelled drawing would show them:

| Part | Description and function |
| --- | --- |
| Yoke beam | The main wooden bar lying across both necks; takes the pull from the animals |
| Neck rests (saddles) | Smooth, curved hollows shaped on the underside of the beam where each neck sits, so that pressure is spread |
| Yoke skeys (keys) | Two upright wooden or metal pegs passing through holes in the beam on either side of each animal's neck; keep the yoke in place |
| Strop (yoke strap) | A strap of leather, rope or chain joining the lower ends of each pair of skeys under the animal's throat; stops the yoke lifting off |
| Centre ring or hook | Fixed at the middle of the beam; the trek chain is attached here |

## The wood used for yokes

Good yoke wood should be:

1. **Strong and tough**, so that it does not break or split under the pull.
2. **Light**, so that the animals do not carry unnecessary weight on their necks.
3. **Durable**, resistant to rot and termites.
4. **Straight-grained** and easy to shape, drill and smooth.
5. **Seasoned (dry)**, so that it does not crack or warp after it is made.
6. **Smooth when finished**, with no splinters, knots or sharp edges where it touches the animal.

Many farmers use local hardwoods; the choice depends on what grows nearby. The finished yoke is smoothed and rubbed with oil to keep it from cracking.

:::example{title="Making a simple withers yoke"}
Farai chooses a straight, seasoned log of tough, light wood. He cuts it to length so that his two oxen can walk side by side without crowding, and shapes the beam with an axe and a spokeshave or knife, carving smooth neck rests on the underside. He measures the width of each ox's neck, marks and drills the skey holes on either side of each rest, and fits skeys that are smooth and rounded at the top. He fixes a strong ring at the centre of the beam for the trek chain and threads rope strops through the lower holes of the skeys. Finally he sands every surface, rubs the yoke with oil, tries it on the oxen and adjusts the strops so that they do not press on the throat.
:::

## Harnesses

Donkeys have no hump and a different neck shape from cattle, so a cattle yoke does not sit well on them. They are usually worked in a **breast-band** or **collar** harness.

| Feature | Breast-band harness | Collar harness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Description | A wide, padded band across the chest, held up by a neck strap, with traces on each side | A rigid, padded collar fitting round the neck and resting on the shoulders, with hames to which the traces attach |
| Advantages | Simple and cheap; easy to make from local materials; adjustable to fit different animals | Spreads the load over the shoulders, so the animal can pull heavier loads efficiently; does not press on the windpipe when fitted well |
| Disadvantages | If set too high it presses on the windpipe; less efficient for heavy pulling | Must be made to fit each animal; costly and harder to make |
| Typical use | Donkeys pulling carts, light ploughs and cultivators | Heavier work; horses and some donkeys |

**Other parts** of a harness:

- **Neck strap**: holds the breast-band at the right height.
- **Back band (saddle)** and **belly band (girth)**: hold the traces at the right height and keep the harness in place.
- **Breeching**: a strap round the hindquarters that lets the animal hold back a cart going downhill.
- **Swingletree (spreader bar)**: a wooden or metal bar behind the animal to which the traces are hooked; keeps the traces apart so they do not rub the legs.
- **Bridle or halter**: for leading and controlling the animal.

**Materials**: leather (strong and comfortable, but needs oiling), canvas or nylon webbing, strips of old car tyres or conveyor belting (strong, durable and cheap), sisal rope, padding of sacking, foam or old blankets, metal rings and buckles, and wood for swingletrees.

:::example{title="Harnessing a donkey for a cart"}
Chipo catches her donkey calmly and puts on the halter. She places the padded breast-band across the chest at the point of the shoulders, below the windpipe, and adjusts the neck strap so it stays there. She fits the back band and belly band, then hooks the traces to the swingletree, checking that both traces are the same length so that the pull is even. She runs her hand under every strap to make sure nothing rubs or pinches, then leads the donkey off quietly. After work she checks for rubbed skin and washes and oils the harness.
:::

## Harnessing cattle correctly

1. Pair animals of similar size, strength and pace, and train them young and patiently.
2. Approach calmly from the side, speaking quietly; never beat or shout at the animals.
3. Lift the yoke onto the necks, insert the skeys on either side of each neck and fasten the strops so that they are secure but do not press on the throat.
4. Attach the trek chain to the centre ring and check that the chain is long enough for the animals to walk freely.
5. Check for rubbing and sores before and after work; rest animals in the heat of the day and give them water.
6. Clean, repair and oil yokes and harnesses, and store them under cover.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using a cattle yoke on donkeys"}
Donkeys lack a hump and the yoke slips or presses on the windpipe. Use a breast-band or collar harness.
:::

:::mistake{title="Tight strops and high breast-bands"}
Both press on the windpipe and choke the animal. Set breast-bands at the point of the shoulders and leave strops just firm.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Label a yoke"}
Use the exact names: yoke beam, neck rest, skeys, strop and centre ring. Add a function for each when asked to "explain".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Compare harnesses"}
Compare breast-band and collar harnesses point by point: construction, efficiency, cost, fitting and suitable animals.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Withers (neck) yokes rest in front of the hump and are the most common; head yokes are tied behind the horns and help animals hold back loads.
- A double withers yoke has a beam, neck rests, skeys, strops and a centre ring for the trek chain.
- Yoke wood must be strong, light, durable, straight-grained, seasoned and smooth.
- Breast-band harnesses are cheap and simple; collar harnesses spread the load and suit heavy work; both use traces, back band, belly band and a swingletree.
- Harness animals calmly, fit everything so it does not rub or choke, check for sores and maintain the equipment.
:::
