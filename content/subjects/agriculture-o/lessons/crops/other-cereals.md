## Introduction

In Hwange, Masvingo and the south of Zimbabwe, maize fails in many seasons because the rains are low and unreliable. Farmers there have long grown **small grains**: sorghum (mapfunde), pearl millet (mhunga) and finger millet (rapoko or zviyo). These crops survive dry spells that kill maize, store well and make good food, malt and stock feed. At the other extreme, **wheat** is grown in winter, when almost no rain falls, on irrigated land in natural region II.

Examiners ask you to explain why small grains suit regions IV and V, to describe how they and wheat are grown, and to compare them with maize. This lesson builds on the maize lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small grains"}
Cereals with small seeds, mainly sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet, that are adapted to hot, dry conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Drought tolerance"}
The ability of a crop to survive and still yield when water is short, through features that reduce water loss or improve water uptake.
:::

:::definition{term="Tillering"}
The production of side shoots (tillers) from the base of a cereal plant; each tiller can carry an ear or head.
:::

:::definition{term="Winter cereal"}
A cereal such as wheat that is planted in late autumn, grows through the cool dry months under irrigation and is harvested before the rains.
:::

## Why small grains suit regions IV and V

Sorghum and the millets have several features that help them in dry areas:

1. **Extensive fibrous root systems** that explore a large volume of soil for water.
2. **A waxy coating (bloom)** on leaves and stems that cuts water loss.
3. **Leaves that roll** in hot weather, reducing the area exposed to the sun.
4. **The ability to stop growing during drought** and recover when rain returns; maize that wilts at tasselling usually cannot recover.
5. **Short seasons** in many cultivars, so the crop finishes before the soil dries out.
6. Pearl millet in particular tolerates **sandy, infertile soils** and high temperatures.

Small grains also **store well** (finger millet can keep for years with little insect damage), and they are used for porridge, traditional opaque beer and malt, and livestock and poultry feed. The stover is fed to cattle.

| Crop | Best conditions | Main uses | Special points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sorghum | Regions III-V; tolerates heavier soils and short waterlogging | Food, malt and beer, stock feed | Red and brown (high-tannin) grain resists birds; white grain is preferred for food |
| Pearl millet | Regions IV-V; hot, dry, sandy soils | Food, beer, poultry feed | Most drought- and heat-tolerant; candle-like heads |
| Finger millet | Regions II-IV; needs a little more rain than pearl millet | Porridge, malt | Small seeds need a fine seedbed; stores very well |

## Growing small grains

**Land preparation.** Small seeds need a **fine, firm seedbed** so that they make good contact with moist soil.

**Planting.** Plant with the first effective rains. Sorghum is drilled in rows (for example 75-90 cm apart) and thinned to about 10-15 cm in the row. Finger millet is often broadcast or drilled thinly and thinned. Seed is sown shallowly, about 2-3 cm deep.

:::worked-solution[Sorghum seed for one hectare]
Sorghum is planted in rows 75 cm apart and thinned to 15 cm in the row. Assume 1,000 seeds weigh 25 g and 80% germinate.

1. Area per plant = 0.75 m × 0.15 m = 0.1125 m².
2. Plants per hectare = 10,000 ÷ 0.1125 = 88,889 plants.
3. Seeds needed = 88,889 ÷ 0.80 = 111,111 seeds.
4. Mass = 111,111 × 25 g ÷ 1,000 = 2,778 g, about **2.8 kg/ha**.
5. Because the seed is small and drilled continuously, then thinned, farmers sow more than this minimum.
:::

**Fertiliser.** Small grains respond to fertiliser and manure, but in dry areas modest rates are used because the risk of crop failure is high. Manure, a small basal dressing and a light nitrogen top dressing are typical.

**Weeds.** Weed early. **Witchweed** (*Striga*) is a serious parasite of sorghum and millets on poor sandy soils. Control it by rotation with non-host crops (for example groundnuts or cotton, which make witchweed seed germinate without supporting it), pulling witchweed before it flowers and sets seed, adding manure and nitrogen, and growing tolerant cultivars.

**Birds.** Flocks of birds, especially the red-billed quelea, eat ripening grain. Farmers scare birds, plant early and at the same time as neighbours, grow brown high-tannin sorghum that birds find less palatable, and harvest promptly.

**Pests and diseases.** Stalk borers, aphids and grain weevils attack sorghum; smut diseases damage the heads.

**Harvesting and storage.** Heads are cut when the grain is hard and dry, dried further on clean floors or racks, threshed by beating or with a thresher and stored dry.

:::note{title="Livestock safety"}
Young sorghum plants and fresh regrowth can contain substances that release prussic acid (hydrogen cyanide) in the animal's stomach. Do not graze very young or drought-stressed sorghum.
:::

## Wheat: an irrigated winter crop

Wheat needs **cool weather** for tillering and grain filling, but it would be damaged by rain at harvest. In Zimbabwe it is therefore planted in **late April to May**, grown through the dry winter on **irrigated** land, and harvested in **September to October**, before the rains.

1. **Land**: level, well-drained, fertile soil close to a reliable water source (a dam or river), usually in region II.
2. **Seedbed**: fine and level so that irrigation water spreads evenly.
3. **Planting**: drilled in close rows (for example 15-25 cm apart) at a high seed rate of about 100-120 kg/ha, because wheat is grown at a high plant population.
4. **Fertiliser**: a heavy basal compound and nitrogen top dressing during tillering.
5. **Irrigation**: frequent, especially at tillering, booting, flowering and grain filling; stop as the grain hardens.
6. **Pests and diseases**: aphids, birds, and fungal diseases such as rusts.
7. **Harvesting**: usually by combine harvester when the grain is hard and dry.

:::worked-solution[Seed, water and nitrogen for 3 ha of wheat]
Tendai plants 3 ha of wheat near Chinhoyi at 110 kg/ha of seed. Each irrigation applies 40 mm of water. He applies 450 kg/ha of a compound containing 7% N and 300 kg/ha of ammonium nitrate (34.5% N).

1. Seed = 110 × 3 = 330 kg; in 50 kg bags 330 ÷ 50 = 6.6, so **7 bags**.
2. Area = 3 ha = 30,000 m². Water per irrigation = 30,000 m² × 40 mm = 1,200,000 L = **1,200 m³** (1 mm over 1 m² = 1 L).
3. Nitrogen per ha = 450 × 7 ÷ 100 + 300 × 34.5 ÷ 100 = 31.5 + 103.5 = **135 kg N/ha**.
:::

## Comparing the cereals

| Feature | Maize | Sorghum and millets | Wheat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Season | Summer, rain-fed | Summer, rain-fed | Winter, irrigated |
| Drought tolerance | Low at flowering | High | Depends on irrigation |
| Natural regions | II-III | III-V | II (with water) |
| Bird damage | Low | High | Moderate |
| Storage | Weevils, larger grain borer | Good (finger millet excellent) | Good if dry |

:::example{title="Choosing a cereal for a farm near Hwange"}
Sipho farms sandy soil in natural region V, where rainfall is low and dry spells are common. Maize would often fail. Pearl millet tolerates the heat, the sandy soil and dry spells; sorghum is a second choice on his heavier patches. He plants a brown sorghum to reduce bird losses, pulls witchweed before it flowers and rotates with groundnuts. He does not grow wheat because there is no water for winter irrigation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling wheat a summer crop"}
In Zimbabwe wheat is a winter crop grown under irrigation; rain at harvest causes grain to sprout and spoils quality.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying small grains need no fertiliser"}
Small grains tolerate poor soils better than maize, but they still yield more with manure and fertiliser.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain drought tolerance"}
Link each feature to water: "a waxy bloom reduces water loss by transpiration", "an extensive root system absorbs water from a larger volume of soil".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Comparisons"}
In "compare" questions, give both crops for each point: "maize is badly damaged by drought at tasselling, whereas sorghum can stop growing and recover when it rains". Use the table above as a checklist.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet suit hot, dry regions IV and V because of deep fibrous roots, waxy leaves, leaf rolling, short seasons and recovery after drought.
- They need a fine seedbed, early planting, weeding and protection from birds and witchweed; they store well.
- Wheat is an irrigated winter crop planted in late April to May and harvested before the rains, at about 100-120 kg/ha of seed.
- Water to apply = area in m² × depth in mm, in litres.
- Choose the cereal to suit the region, the water supply and the market.
:::
