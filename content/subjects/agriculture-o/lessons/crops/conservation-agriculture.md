## Introduction

In the dry months before the rains, families across rural Zimbabwe can be seen digging rows of small square holes in their fields and carrying grass and maize stalks to cover the ground between them. This is **conservation agriculture**, promoted since 2020 through the government's **Pfumvudza** programme (called **Intwasa** in isiNdebele). Instead of ploughing the whole field, the farmer disturbs only the small planting stations, keeps the soil covered and rotates crops.

Conservation agriculture brings together much of what you learn about soil structure, water and erosion, and applies it to crop production. Examination questions ask you to *state* the three principles, to *describe* how planting basins are prepared, to *explain* how the system conserves soil and water, and to *discuss* its benefits and limitations for smallholder farmers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conservation agriculture"}
A farming system based on three principles applied together: minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover and crop rotation or association, so that soil, water and nutrients are conserved while yields are maintained or improved.
:::

:::definition{term="Minimum tillage"}
Disturbing the soil only where the seed and fertiliser are placed (in planting basins or rip lines) instead of ploughing the whole field.
:::

:::definition{term="Planting basin"}
A small hole dug by hand at each planting station, in which seed, manure and fertiliser are placed and rainwater collects.
:::

:::definition{term="Pfumvudza/Intwasa"}
Zimbabwe's conservation-agriculture programme for smallholder farmers, introduced in 2020, using planting basins, mulch, crop rotation and precise application of inputs on a standard plot.
:::

## The three principles

| Principle | What the farmer does | Why it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum soil disturbance | Digs planting basins or rips narrow lines; does not plough the whole field | Organic matter is not oxidised so fast, crumbs and earthworm channels survive, no plough pan forms, less erosion |
| Permanent soil cover | Keeps crop residues, grass or a cover crop on the surface, ideally covering at least about 30% of the ground | Mulch absorbs raindrop impact, stops capping, slows runoff, reduces evaporation, keeps soil cooler and feeds soil organisms |
| Crop rotation or association | Rotates cereals with legumes (for example maize then groundnuts or soya beans) or intercrops them | Legumes fix nitrogen, rotation breaks pest, disease and weed cycles, different roots use different depths |

All three must be used together. Basins without mulch, for example, still lose water by evaporation and still suffer capping between the basins.

## Preparing and using planting basins

1. **Mark out** the field in straight rows, for example with a string line, using the spacing recommended for the crop (one example used for maize is $75\ \text{cm}$ between rows and $60\ \text{cm}$ between basins).
2. **Dig the basins** in the dry season, before the rains, so that the farmer is ready to plant on time. Each basin is small, for example about $15\ \text{cm}$ long, wide and deep.
3. **Apply manure precisely:** put a measured amount of well-rotted manure or compost (for example one handful) in each basin, not scattered over the whole field.
4. **Apply basal fertiliser precisely:** a measured small container of basal fertiliser per basin, covered with a little soil so the seed does not touch it.
5. **Spread mulch** between the rows; keep it away from the planting hole itself.
6. **Plant with the first effective rains:** sow seed in the basins and cover lightly; thin to the recommended number of plants.
7. **Top-dress** each plant with a measured amount of nitrogen fertiliser when the crop needs it, placed near the plant.
8. **Weed early and often**, before weeds compete with the crop or set seed.

<!-- Reviewer: Pfumvudza basin size, spacing, plot size and input measures are given as examples only; check them against current AGRITEX guidance. -->

:::worked-solution[Basins on a plot]
A family prepares a plot $39\ \text{m} \times 16\ \text{m}$ with basins $75\ \text{cm}$ between rows and $60\ \text{cm}$ within the row.

1. Plot area $= 39 \times 16 = 624\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Area served by one basin $= 0.75 \times 0.60 = 0.45\ \text{m}^2$.
3. Number of basins $\approx \frac{624}{0.45} = 1\,386.7$, so about $1\,387$ basins (the exact count depends on how the rows start and end at the edges).
4. Per hectare: $\frac{10\,000}{0.45} \approx 22\,222$ basins.
:::

:::worked-solution[Fertiliser per basin]
Assume the basal fertiliser rate is $300\ \text{kg/ha}$ and there are $22\,222$ basins per hectare (the spacing above).

1. Fertiliser per hectare in grams $= 300 \times 1\,000 = 300\,000\ \text{g}$.
2. Per basin $= \frac{300\,000}{22\,222} = 13.5\ \text{g}$.
3. For the $624\ \text{m}^2$ plot: $300 \times \frac{624}{10\,000} = 18.72\ \text{kg}$, less than one $50\ \text{kg}$ bag.
4. Placing a small, measured amount in each basin puts the nutrients next to the roots, instead of spreading them where no crop grows.
:::

## Timeliness

Because the basins are dug in the dry season, the crop can be planted as soon as the first effective rains fall, instead of waiting for draught animals or a tractor to plough. Early planting uses the whole rainy season and gives a crop canopy before the heaviest storms. Timely weeding matters just as much: without ploughing, weeds must be controlled by hand hoeing, slashing or mulch, and they must be removed while small.

## Benefits

- **Water conservation:** basins collect rainwater around the plant and mulch reduces evaporation, so crops survive dry spells better.
- **Less erosion:** mulch protects the surface; undisturbed soil keeps its structure.
- **Better soil over time:** more organic matter, more earthworms and better structure.
- **Efficient use of inputs:** manure and fertiliser go exactly where the roots are.
- **Less need for draught power:** households without oxen can still plant on time.
- **More reliable yields**, especially in seasons with poor rainfall distribution.

## Limitations

- **Labour:** digging basins and frequent hand weeding take much work, especially in the first seasons.
- **Competition for residues:** in many communal areas livestock graze crop residues in the dry season, so keeping mulch is difficult.
- **Termites** may eat the mulch.
- **Weeds** increase without ploughing unless weeding is timely.
- **Waterlogging** can occur in basins on heavy clay soils in very wet seasons.
- **Inputs:** precise application only helps if seed, fertiliser and manure are available on time.

:::example{title="Planning a Pfumvudza plot with rotation"}
Rudo's family has two Pfumvudza plots. This season Plot A grows maize and Plot B grows groundnuts. Next season they swap: maize follows groundnuts on Plot B, benefiting from the nitrogen the legume left behind, and groundnuts follow maize on Plot A, breaking the cycle of maize stalk borer and leaf diseases. The maize stover from Plot A is kept as mulch, so the family fences the plots or agrees with neighbours to keep cattle out after harvest.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling any no-plough field 'conservation agriculture'"}
All three principles are needed. A field that is not ploughed but is left bare, or is planted to maize every year, is not practising conservation agriculture.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing fertiliser and seed together"}
Basal fertiliser is covered with a little soil before the seed goes in; direct contact can damage the germinating seed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying conservation agriculture means less work"}
It saves ploughing, but basin digging and weeding often need **more** labour, at least at first.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State the principles exactly"}
Give the three principles in full: minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover (mulch) and crop rotation or association. Then explain each by linking it to soil, water or nutrients.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss questions"}
When asked to *discuss* the programme, give benefits **and** limitations, applied to smallholders (labour, livestock and residues, draught power, input supply), and end with a balanced judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conservation agriculture rests on minimum soil disturbance, permanent soil cover and crop rotation or association.
- Planting basins are dug in the dry season; manure and fertiliser are measured into each basin.
- Timely planting with the first effective rains and early weeding are essential.
- Benefits: water conservation, less erosion, better soil, efficient inputs, less draught power, more reliable yields.
- Limitations: labour, residues grazed by livestock, termites, weeds, waterlogging on heavy soils, input supply.
:::
