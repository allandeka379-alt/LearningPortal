## Introduction

Drive through Gokwe or Sanyati in May and you will see families picking white cotton into bags; drive past Marondera or Karoi in February and you will see tobacco barns with smoke rising as the leaf is cured. Cotton and flue-cured tobacco are Zimbabwe's best-known **cash crops**: they are grown mainly for sale, not for food, and they bring money into rural areas.

Cambridge asks you to describe in detail the cultivation of **one crop of local importance**, and either of these crops is a good choice. This lesson gives you the whole season for both, so that you can answer on either. You should already know about land preparation, fertilisers and pest control.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cash crop"}
A crop grown mainly to be sold for money rather than eaten by the farm family.
:::

:::definition{term="Topping"}
Removing the flower head of a tobacco plant so that its food goes into the leaves instead of flowers and seed.
:::

:::definition{term="Suckering"}
Removing the side shoots (suckers) that grow from leaf axils after topping.
:::

:::definition{term="Curing"}
The controlled drying of harvested tobacco leaf in a barn, during which the leaf turns yellow and then dries so that its colour and quality are fixed.
:::

:::definition{term="Contract farming"}
An arrangement in which a company supplies inputs such as seed, fertiliser and chemicals on credit and agrees to buy the crop, deducting the cost of the inputs from the payment.
:::

## Cotton

### Requirements

- A **long, warm, frost-free season**, moderate rainfall during growth and **dry weather when the bolls open**, because rain stains the lint and lowers its grade.
- **Deep, well-drained soils**; cotton grows well on heavier, fertile soils.
- In Zimbabwe it is grown mainly by smallholders in the warmer, drier parts of regions III and IV.

### Planting and management

1. Prepare a clean seedbed and plant with the first effective rains, usually in November, because a late crop meets cold weather and pests before the bolls mature.
2. Plant several seeds per station in rows about 1 m apart and thin to one or two strong plants.
3. Apply a basal compound fertiliser and a nitrogen top dressing; too much nitrogen gives leafy plants that shed squares and bolls.
4. Weed early by hoe or cultivator.
5. **Scout** the crop at least weekly and spray only when pest numbers reach the threshold advised by the extension service.

:::worked-solution[Plant population of cotton]
Farai plants 2 ha of cotton at 100 cm × 30 cm, one plant per station after thinning.

1. Area per station = 1.0 m × 0.3 m = 0.3 m².
2. Plants per ha = 10,000 ÷ 0.3 = 33,333 plants.
3. For the field: 33,333 × 2 = 66,667 plants.
:::

### Main pests of cotton

| Pest | Damage | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| American bollworm (*Helicoverpa armigera*) | Larvae bore into squares (flower buds), flowers and bolls; damaged squares drop | Also attacks tomatoes, maize and other crops; scout for eggs and young larvae on the growing points |
| Red bollworm | Larvae feed inside green bolls | Survives the dry season in old stalks |
| Aphids | Suck sap; honeydew makes lint sticky and grows sooty mould | Natural enemies (ladybirds) help control them |
| Jassids | Leaf edges curl and turn red or brown | Hairy-leaved cultivars resist them |
| Red spider mite | Leaves turn bronze or red with fine webbing, mainly in hot, dry spells | Late in the season |

After harvest, cotton stalks are **uprooted and destroyed** so that there is a **closed season** without living cotton plants, which breaks the life cycle of bollworms that survive in old plants. In Zimbabwe this is required by regulation.

### Picking and marketing

1. Pick bolls as they open fully, after the dew has dried.
2. Keep cotton **clean and dry**: pick into cotton bags, never into polypropylene sacks, whose fibres contaminate the lint.
3. **Grade** as you pick: clean white cotton apart from stained, trashy or immature cotton.
4. Deliver to the buyer or ginnery, where **ginning** separates the **lint** (fibre for textiles) from the **seed** (used for oil and stock-feed cake).

:::worked-solution[Lint from seed cotton]
Assume the ginning out-turn is 40%. Farai delivers 1,800 kg of seed cotton.

1. Lint = 1,800 × 40 ÷ 100 = **720 kg**.
2. Seed and waste = 1,800 − 720 = 1,080 kg.
:::

## Flue-cured tobacco

### Requirements

- Warm weather and a frost-free season; flue-cured tobacco is grown mainly in region II.
- **Light, sandy, well-drained soils** of low natural fertility, so that the farmer controls the nitrogen supply and therefore leaf quality.
- A rotation that does not follow tobacco with tobacco, because **root-knot nematodes** build up; a grass ley is a common rotation crop.

### Seedlings and transplanting

1. Seedlings are raised in **sterilised seedbeds or float trays** (trays of growing medium floating on water with dissolved nutrients), protected from pests and diseases.
2. After about eight to ten weeks, when seedlings are about pencil-thick and 15-20 cm tall, they are **hardened off** by reducing water.
3. They are **transplanted** into ridges, early under irrigation or later with the rains.

**Fertiliser.** Tobacco fertilisers are low in nitrogen and high in potassium, and potassium is supplied as **potassium sulfate**, not muriate of potash, because **chloride** spoils the burning quality of the leaf.

### Topping and suckering

When the plants flower they are **topped**: the flower head is broken off, leaving a set number of leaves. The plant then puts its food into the leaves, which become **larger, thicker and heavier**, with more nicotine. Topping makes **suckers** grow in the leaf axils; these are removed by hand or controlled with an approved sucker-control chemical, otherwise they use the food that topping saved.

### Pests and diseases

Aphids (which also spread viruses), budworms, cutworms in young plants and root-knot nematodes (galls on roots) are the main pests. **Tobacco mosaic virus** spreads on hands and tools: workers wash their hands with soap and do not use tobacco products while handling plants.

### Reaping, curing and grading

1. **Reaping**: leaves ripen from the bottom up. A few ripe, lemon-yellow leaves are reaped from each plant every week or so.
2. **Curing** in a flue-cured barn takes about a week and has three stages:
   - **Yellowing** at about 35 °C with high humidity, while the leaf is still alive and turns yellow.
   - **Leaf drying** (colour fixing): temperature is raised gradually and ventilators opened, drying the leaf blade.
   - **Midrib (stem) drying** at the highest temperature, up to about 70 °C, until the thick midribs are dry.
3. **Conditioning**: the dry, brittle leaf is allowed to take up a little moisture so that it can be handled without breaking.
4. **Grading** by leaf position, colour, size and quality, then **baling**.
5. **Marketing**: bales are sold at **auction floors** or delivered under **contract** to merchants, who pay according to grade.

Curing barns burn large amounts of fuel. Where wood is used, farmers should plant their own woodlots (for example of gum trees) and use efficient barns to reduce deforestation. Workers handling wet green leaf should wear long sleeves and gloves, because nicotine absorbed through the skin causes **green tobacco sickness**.

:::example{title="Choosing between the two crops"}
Rudo farms sandy soil near Marondera with a small dam; Sipho farms heavier soil in a hot, drier area near Gokwe with no irrigation. Tobacco suits Rudo: her light soil lets her control nitrogen, the dam allows early transplanting, and she has labour for reaping and curing. Cotton suits Sipho: it tolerates the heat and drier conditions, needs no curing barn, and a contract company in his area supplies inputs on credit. Both farmers must plan a rotation: tobacco should not follow tobacco, and cotton stalks must be destroyed after harvest.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Topping makes the plant grow taller"}
Topping stops upward growth and diverts food to the leaves; it makes leaves bigger and heavier, not the plant taller.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using any potash fertiliser on tobacco"}
Muriate of potash contains chloride, which spoils the burning quality of tobacco leaf. Use potassium sulfate.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="One crop in detail"}
For "describe the cultivation of one crop of local importance", state the crop first, then go through requirements, land preparation, planting, spacing, fertiliser, weed, pest and disease control, maturity, harvesting, yield, storage or curing, marketing and records, with a reason for each step.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain topping"}
Two marks usually need the action and the effect: "removing the flower head diverts food (assimilates) to the leaves, so leaves are larger and heavier and yield and quality rise".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cotton needs a long, warm, frost-free season and dry weather at boll opening; it is planted with the first rains, scouted for bollworms, aphids, jassids and mites, and its stalks are destroyed after harvest.
- Cotton is picked clean into cotton bags, graded and ginned into lint and seed.
- Flue-cured tobacco is raised in seedbeds or float trays, transplanted, fertilised with low-nitrogen, chloride-free fertiliser, topped and suckered.
- Tobacco is reaped from the bottom up, cured in three stages (yellowing, leaf drying, midrib drying), conditioned, graded and baled.
- Both crops are sold through contracts or auction systems and are paid for by grade.
:::
