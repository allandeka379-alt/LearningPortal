## Introduction

An orchard is a long-term investment. A farmer near Mutare who plants citrus or mangoes this year will wait three or more years for the first real crop, but a well-planned orchard can then bear fruit for decades. Mistakes made at planting, such as a frost pocket, a poorly dug hole or trees too close together, cannot easily be corrected later.

ZIMSEC teaches orchards in Form 2: site selection, land preparation, planting patterns, preparing holes, planting, fertiliser calculations, irrigation scheduling, pruning, fire guards, signs of maturity and marketing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Orchard"}
An area of land planted with fruit trees or other perennial fruit crops, managed for fruit production.
:::

:::definition{term="Deciduous fruit"}
Fruit from trees that shed their leaves in winter and need a period of winter cold (chilling) to flower well, such as apples, peaches, plums and grapes.
:::

:::definition{term="Pruning"}
The planned removal of parts of a plant, usually branches and shoots, to shape it, keep it healthy and improve the yield and quality of fruit.
:::

:::definition{term="Fire guard (fire break)"}
A strip of land cleared of grass and other fuel around an orchard or field so that a veld fire cannot cross into it.
:::

## Groups of fruit

| Group | Examples | Climate needs | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Deciduous | Apples, peaches, plums, grapes | Cold winters for chilling, e.g. the Eastern Highlands | Pruned in winter while dormant |
| Tropical and subtropical | Bananas, mangoes, guavas, pawpaws | Warm, frost-free areas such as low-lying valleys | Bananas need plenty of water; mangoes tolerate a dry season |
| Citrus | Oranges, naartjies, lemons, grapefruit | Warm, frost-free, with water for irrigation | Evergreen; propagated by budding onto rootstocks |

## Choosing the site

1. **Climate** suited to the fruit: frost-free for tropical and citrus fruit; enough winter cold for deciduous fruit.
2. **Avoid frost pockets**: cold air drains downhill and collects in valley bottoms, so plant on gentle slopes.
3. **Soil**: deep (trees root deeply), well drained and fertile, with no hardpan or waterlogging.
4. **Water** for irrigation close by, because young trees and most fruit need water in the dry season.
5. **Shelter** from strong wind (plant windbreaks first), and **access** to roads and markets for perishable fruit.
6. **Security** from livestock, wild animals and theft.

## Land preparation and planting patterns

Clear trees, stumps and rocks; plough or rip deeply to break any hardpan; build contours on slopes; and plant windbreaks a year or more before the trees if possible.

| Pattern | Layout | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Square | Trees at the corners of squares | Simple to mark out; cultivation in two directions | Wastes some space |
| Rectangular | Rows farther apart than trees in the row | Room for machines and intercrops between rows | Slightly fewer trees than square at the same in-row spacing |
| Triangular (equilateral) | Each tree equally distant from six neighbours | About 15% more trees per hectare than square at the same spacing | Harder to mark out |
| Quincunx | Square with an extra tree in the centre of each square | Fills space while main trees are young (the centre tree is removed later) | Needs thinning later |
| Contour | Rows follow the contours | Controls erosion on slopes | Rows are uneven in length |

:::worked-solution[Trees for an orchard block]
Rudo plans a citrus block 120 m × 60 m on the square system at 6 m × 6 m.

1. Area = 120 × 60 = 7,200 m².
2. Area per tree = 6 × 6 = 36 m².
3. Trees = 7,200 ÷ 36 = **200 trees** (20 trees per row × 10 rows).
4. Per hectare: 10,000 ÷ 36 = about 278 trees. On the triangular system at the same spacing, each tree occupies 0.866 × 36 = 31.2 m², so about 321 trees fit, roughly 15% more.
:::

## Pegging, holes and planting

**Pegging** marks the exact position of every tree with a peg, using a measuring tape, ranging rods and right angles (a 3-4-5 triangle) so that rows are straight.

A **planting board** keeps the tree exactly where the peg was: place the centre notch of the board against the peg and put small pegs in the notches at each end; remove the board and centre peg, dig the hole, then replace the board on the end pegs. The tree is planted at the centre notch.

**Planting holes** are dug some weeks before planting, typically about 1 m wide and deep for citrus and mangoes:

1. Put the **topsoil** on one side and the **subsoil** on the other.
2. Mix the topsoil with well-rotted manure or compost and a phosphate fertiliser.
3. Refill the hole with the mixture (topsoil goes back at the bottom where the roots will be) and let it settle, ideally after rain or watering.

**Planting**:

1. Plant at the start of the rains or, with irrigation, in cool weather.
2. Dig a small hole in the settled mixture, remove the container and set the tree at the depth it grew in the nursery, with the **bud or graft union well above the soil** (soil against it lets rootstock-type diseases such as collar rot enter and roots form above the union).
3. Firm the soil, make a **basin** round the tree, water thoroughly, tie the tree to a stake, mulch (keeping mulch away from the stem) and shade it from hot sun at first.

## Managing the orchard

**Fertiliser.** Young trees receive increasing amounts as they grow. A common way to state rates is a mass per tree per year of age.

:::worked-solution[Fertiliser and water for 200 citrus trees]
Assume Rudo's trees are 4 years old and receive 100 g of ammonium nitrate per tree for each year of age, in 50 kg bags. Each tree has a basin of radius 1 m that is given 40 mm of water at each irrigation.

1. AN per tree = 100 g × 4 = 400 g.
2. For 200 trees = 200 × 400 g = 80,000 g = 80 kg = 80 ÷ 50 = 1.6, so **2 bags**.
3. Basin area = π × 1² = 3.14 m².
4. Water per tree = 3.14 m² × 40 mm = **126 L** (1 mm on 1 m² = 1 L).
5. For 200 trees = 200 × 125.7 L = about **25,100 L** per irrigation.
:::

**Irrigation scheduling** means deciding how much water to give and how often. Young trees and sandy soils need small amounts often; mature trees on clay soils need larger amounts less often. Fruit trees need most water at flowering, fruit set and fruit growth, and in the hot, dry months before the rains. Deciduous trees need little water while dormant in winter.

**Pruning** is done for different purposes:

1. **Training (formative) pruning** of young trees to build a strong framework, for example an **open centre (vase)** for peaches or a **central leader** for apples.
2. **Maintenance pruning** to remove dead, diseased, damaged and crossing branches and **water shoots**, and to let light and air into the tree.
3. **Fruiting pruning** of deciduous trees and grapes in winter to balance new growth with fruiting wood.
4. Removing **suckers** that grow from the rootstock below the bud union of citrus.
5. **Desuckering bananas**: keep one mother plant, one daughter and one granddaughter (follower) per stool, and remove the other suckers.

Use sharp, clean tools, and cut just above a bud or just outside the branch collar.

**Fire guards.** Before the dry season, slash and clear (or burn under control) a strip several metres wide around the orchard, keep grass short under the trees and keep firefighting tools ready.

**Pests and diseases** include fruit flies (maggots in ripening fruit), scale insects and aphids on citrus, and fungal diseases such as anthracnose of mangoes. Collect and destroy fallen fruit, use traps and spray when needed.

## Signs of maturity and marketing

| Fruit | Signs of maturity |
| --- | --- |
| Orange or naartjie | Full size, colour change (although warm-area fruit may stay greenish), juicy with a good sugar to acid balance (taste test) |
| Mango | Shoulders rise above the stalk end, skin colour changes, flesh yellow near the stone |
| Banana | Fingers fill out and become rounded (the angles disappear); bunches are cut green and ripened off the plant |
| Guava | Skin turns from dark green to light or yellow-green and softens slightly |
| Apple | Seeds turn brown and the fruit parts easily from the spur when lifted and twisted |

Fruit is **graded** by size and appearance, **packed** in crates or cartons, kept cool and sold fresh to markets, supermarkets and schools or **processed** into juice, jam and dried fruit to reduce waste.

:::example{title="Planning a mango orchard near Chipinge"}
Chipo has a gentle north-facing slope and a frost-prone valley floor. She plants mangoes on the slope, where cold air drains away, using contour rows to control erosion. She digs holes a month ahead, mixing topsoil with manure and phosphate, and plants grafted trees at the start of the rains with the graft union well above the soil. She clears a fire guard each May and harvests when the shoulders rise and the flesh near the stone turns yellow.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Burying the bud union"}
The union must be above the soil. Buried, it lets disease into the scion and can make the scion root, losing the benefits of the rootstock.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting subsoil back on top"}
Topsoil is fertile and full of organisms; it goes back round the roots, not underneath the infertile subsoil.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Site selection"}
Give the factor and why it matters: "avoid valley bottoms because cold air collects there and frost damages tropical fruit".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
For trees: area ÷ (spacing × spacing). For fertiliser: rate per tree × trees, then convert g to kg and bags. For water: basin area (π r²) × depth in mm gives litres.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Choose a frost-free (or, for deciduous fruit, cold-winter) site with deep, well-drained soil, water, shelter, access and security.
- Patterns: square, rectangular, triangular (about 15% more trees), quincunx and contour; peg accurately with a planting board.
- Dig holes early, keep topsoil and subsoil apart, refill with topsoil, manure and phosphate, and plant with the bud union above the soil.
- Fertilise by tree age, schedule irrigation by tree age, soil and season, prune for shape, health and fruiting, and clear fire guards.
- Harvest at the right signs of maturity, grade, pack, keep cool and market or process the fruit.
:::
