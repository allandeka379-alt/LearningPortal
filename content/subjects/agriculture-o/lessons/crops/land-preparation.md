## Introduction

Before the rains, farmers all over Zimbabwe prepare their land. On a smallholding near Masvingo this may mean a pair of oxen pulling a mouldboard plough; on a commercial farm near Chinhoyi, a tractor with a disc harrow; under Pfumvudza, a hoe digging planting basins through the mulch. Each method aims to give the seed a good start while protecting the soil.

Both boards examine land preparation: ZIMSEC in Forms 1 and 3 (reasons, steps, primary and secondary tillage, minimum tillage) and Cambridge in topic 4 (stumping and clearing, primary and secondary cultivation by hand or machine). The implements themselves are described in detail in the lesson on tillage implements.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tillage (cultivation)"}
The working of the soil with tools or implements to prepare a seedbed, control weeds and improve the soil's physical condition.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary tillage"}
The first, deep working of the soil, which breaks and usually turns it, for example ploughing or digging.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary tillage"}
Shallower working after primary tillage to break clods, level the soil and produce a seedbed, for example harrowing, raking and ridging.
:::

:::definition{term="Seedbed"}
Soil prepared to a suitable tilth, moisture and level for sowing seed or transplanting seedlings.
:::

:::definition{term="Minimum (conservation) tillage"}
Disturbing the soil as little as possible, only along planting lines or in planting basins, and leaving crop residues on the surface as a mulch. **Zero tillage** plants directly into untilled soil.
:::

## Reasons for land preparation

1. **Control weeds** by burying, cutting or uprooting them before the crop is planted.
2. **Loosen the soil** so that roots can penetrate and air and water can enter (better aeration and infiltration).
3. **Bury crop residues, manure and fertiliser** so that they decompose and release nutrients.
4. **Produce a seedbed** of the right tilth so that seed makes good contact with moist soil.
5. **Control pests and diseases** by exposing soil insects and their eggs to the sun and birds and burying diseased residues.
6. **Level the land** for even planting and irrigation, or **shape it** into ridges and beds.
7. **Conserve soil and water** when tillage follows the contours or forms ridges and basins that hold rain.

## Stumping and clearing new land

Land that has never been cultivated must first be cleared.

1. **Survey** the land and mark any trees to be kept for shade, windbreaks or soil protection, and any steep slopes or stream banks that must not be cleared.
2. **Slash** grass and bush with slashers and axes.
3. **Fell** the trees with axes or saws, or with a chainsaw or bulldozer on large farms.
4. **Stump**: dig out the stumps and main roots with mattocks, picks and axes, or pull them with oxen, a tractor or a bulldozer. Stumps left in the ground break implements and send up new shoots.
5. **Remove stones** and pile them on contours or field edges.
6. **Dispose of the debris**: use timber for poles and firewood, pile brush along contours, or burn it carefully with a fire guard and in line with local rules.
7. Construct **contour ridges** and waterways before the first ploughing so that the newly bare soil is protected from erosion.

Clearing exposes the soil, so it should be done in the dry season and followed quickly by soil conservation works and a crop.

## Primary tillage

Primary tillage breaks and turns the soil to a depth of roughly 15-25 cm.

| Method | How | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand digging | Hoe, spade or fork | Small areas, gardens and basins; slow and tiring |
| Mouldboard plough | Ox- or tractor-drawn; cuts, lifts and inverts a furrow slice | Buries weeds and residues well |
| Disc plough | Tractor-drawn rotating discs | Suits hard, stony or root-filled soils |
| Ripper or chisel plough | Narrow tines loosen soil without turning it | Breaks a plough pan; used in conservation farming |

**Timing.** Many farmers plough soon after harvest, while the soil is still moist (often called winter or autumn ploughing). This buries residues so that they rot before planting, kills weeds before they seed and exposes pests. Others plough just before planting. Plough when the soil is **moist but not wet**: wet soil smears and forms hard clods and compacted layers; dry soil is too hard to work.

:::worked-solution[How far does an ox plough walk?]
Sipho's ox plough cuts a furrow 20 cm wide. He ploughs 1 ha at an average speed of 2.5 km/h.

1. 20 cm = 0.2 m.
2. Distance = area ÷ width = 10,000 m² ÷ 0.2 m = 50,000 m = 50 km.
3. Time = 50 km ÷ 2.5 km/h = **20 hours** of ploughing, not counting turns and rests, which is why ox ploughing takes several days per hectare.
:::

## Secondary tillage and seedbeds

After ploughing, the soil is cloddy and uneven. Secondary tillage prepares it for planting:

- **Harrowing** with a spike-tooth or disc harrow breaks clods, levels the surface, kills young weeds and can cover broadcast seed.
- **Raking** by hand in gardens produces a fine tilth and removes stones and trash.
- **Rolling** firms loose soil so that small seeds touch moist soil.
- **Ridging** with a ridger forms ridges for potatoes, sweet potatoes and some vegetables, or **tied ridges** (ridges joined by small cross-ties) to hold rainwater in dry areas.

The **tilth** needed depends on the seed:

| Crop | Seedbed |
| --- | --- |
| Small seeds (tobacco, onions, finger millet, many vegetables) | Fine, firm and level |
| Large seeds (maize, beans, groundnuts) | Medium tilth; some small clods are fine and reduce crusting |
| Root and tuber crops (potatoes, sweet potatoes) | Deep, loose soil, often ridged |
| Irrigated wheat | Fine and level so that water spreads evenly |

:::worked-solution[Hours needed to harrow a field]
A tractor pulls a disc harrow 1.2 m wide at 6 km/h. Because of turning and overlapping, it works at 75% field efficiency. How long will 8 ha take?

1. Theoretical rate = width × speed = 1.2 m × 6,000 m/h = 7,200 m²/h = 0.72 ha/h.
2. Effective rate = 0.72 × 0.75 = 0.54 ha/h.
3. Time = 8 ÷ 0.54 = **14.8 hours**.
:::

## Conventional and minimum tillage compared

| Feature | Conventional tillage (plough and harrow) | Minimum or zero tillage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soil disturbance | The whole field is turned | Only planting lines or basins |
| Erosion | High risk: bare, loose soil | Low: residues cover and protect the soil |
| Soil moisture | Lost from turned soil | Conserved under mulch |
| Organic matter and soil life | Broken down faster | Built up over time |
| Labour, fuel and draught power | High | Lower once established |
| Weed control | Ploughing buries weeds | Relies on mulch, hand weeding or herbicides |
| Pests and diseases | Residues buried | Some pests and diseases survive in surface residues |
| Plough pan | Can form from ploughing at the same depth | Avoided; rippers can break an existing pan |

:::example{title="Choosing a method on Thandeka's farm"}
Thandeka farms sandy soil on a slope near Gweru. Ploughing every year has left bare soil that washes away in storms and a hard layer at about 20 cm. She switches part of the land to basins under Pfumvudza, keeping a grass mulch, and uses an ox-drawn ripper on the rest to break the pan without turning the soil. Weeds now need more hand weeding, but runoff and erosion fall and the soil stays moist for longer in dry spells.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing primary and secondary tillage"}
Ploughing and digging are primary; harrowing, raking, rolling and ridging are secondary. Harrowing does not replace ploughing on hard, weedy land.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ploughing wet soil"}
Ploughing when the soil is wet smears and compacts it. Wait until it is moist but crumbly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State and explain"}
For "state reasons for land preparation", one word per reason may do. For "explain", add the effect: "ploughing buries weeds, so they do not compete with the crop for water and nutrients".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Compare tillage systems"}
Compare point by point with "whereas": "conventional tillage leaves the soil bare, whereas minimum tillage keeps a mulch that reduces erosion".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land is prepared to control weeds, loosen and aerate the soil, bury residues and manure, make a seedbed, expose pests and conserve soil and water.
- New land is surveyed, slashed, felled, stumped, cleared of stones and protected with contours before ploughing.
- Primary tillage (digging, mouldboard, disc or chisel ploughing) is deep; secondary tillage (harrowing, raking, rolling, ridging) produces the seedbed.
- Small seeds need a fine, firm tilth; large seeds a medium tilth; roots and tubers deep, loose soil or ridges.
- Minimum tillage reduces erosion, moisture loss, labour and fuel, but needs more weed control.
:::
