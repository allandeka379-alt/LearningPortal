## Introduction

Almost every rural homestead and school in Zimbabwe has a vegetable garden. A well-kept garden of rape (covo), tomatoes and onions feeds the family every day, and the surplus sells quickly at the local market, at Mbare Musika in Harare or to a boarding school. Because vegetables are worth a lot per square metre, a small irrigated garden can earn more than a large field of maize.

ZIMSEC teaches vegetable production in Form 1: you are expected to identify the branches of horticulture, prepare seedbeds and establish, manage and market one vegetable from each group (leaf, root, legume, bulb, tuber and fruit) at the recommended spacing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Horticulture"}
The intensive growing of vegetables, fruit, flowers and ornamental plants, usually on small areas with high inputs of labour and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Olericulture"}
The branch of horticulture that deals with growing vegetables.
:::

:::definition{term="Pomology"}
The branch of horticulture that deals with growing fruit.
:::

:::definition{term="Floriculture"}
The branch of horticulture that deals with growing flowers and ornamental plants, for example for cut flowers.
:::

:::definition{term="Hardening off"}
Gradually exposing nursery seedlings to field conditions, by reducing watering and removing shade, so that they survive transplanting.
:::

## Importance of horticulture

1. **Nutrition**: vegetables and fruit supply vitamins (such as A and C), minerals and fibre that a diet of sadza alone lacks.
2. **Income**: high value per hectare; quick returns because many vegetables mature in two to four months.
3. **Employment**: labour-intensive work in gardens, packing and marketing.
4. **Exports**: cut flowers, fruit and vegetables earn foreign currency.
5. **Beauty and environment**: ornamental plants and lawns improve homes, schools and towns.

## The nursery seedbed

Many vegetables (cabbage, rape, tomatoes, onions) have small seeds and are raised in a **nursery** and then transplanted. This gives the young plants care and protection, saves seed and water, and lets the main garden carry another crop while the seedlings grow.

1. **Site**: near water, sheltered from strong wind, well drained, in full sun with some shade available, and not where tomatoes, potatoes or related crops grew recently (soil-borne diseases).
2. **Preparation**: dig deeply, remove stones, roots and weeds, add well-rotted compost or manure, and rake to a **fine tilth**. Make a **raised bed** about 1 m wide so that you can reach the middle without stepping on it.
3. **Sterilising** the soil reduces damping-off fungi, nematodes and weed seeds: for example burn dry grass on the bed or cover moist soil with clear plastic in hot sun (solarisation).
4. **Sowing**: make shallow drills (about 1 cm deep, 10-15 cm apart), sow thinly, cover lightly and firm.
5. **Mulch** with dry grass to keep the surface moist until the seedlings emerge, then remove it and provide light shade.
6. **Water** gently with a watering can with a fine rose, morning and evening; thin crowded seedlings.
7. **Harden off** for one to two weeks before transplanting.

## Transplanting

Seedlings are usually ready after about four to six weeks, when they have four to six true leaves and are sturdy.

1. Water the nursery bed a few hours before lifting so that soil clings to the roots.
2. Transplant in the **late afternoon or on a cloudy day** to reduce wilting.
3. Lift seedlings with a trowel, keeping a ball of soil round the roots; hold them by the leaves, not the stem.
4. Plant at the same depth as in the nursery (tomatoes may be set deeper), firm the soil and water immediately.
5. Give temporary shade if the weather is very hot, and replace (gap) any plants that die.

Crops with large seeds or tubers, and root crops that would be damaged by moving, are sown **directly**: beans, peas, carrots and potatoes.

| Group | Crop | Planting | Typical spacing | Key management | Harvest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Leaf | Rape (covo) | Transplanted | 45 cm × 30 cm | Nitrogen top dressing; control aphids | Pick lower leaves regularly over many weeks |
| Leaf | Cabbage | Transplanted | 60 cm × 45 cm | Nitrogen top dressing; control diamondback moth, aphids and cutworms | When heads are firm |
| Root | Carrot | Sown directly in rows | Rows 20-30 cm, thinned to about 5 cm | Loose, stone-free soil; no fresh manure (forked roots) | When roots reach market size |
| Legume | Beans | Sown directly | 45 cm × 10 cm | Little nitrogen; support climbing types | Green pods while tender, or dry beans |
| Bulb | Onion | Transplanted | 30 cm × 10 cm | Shallow roots: frequent light watering, careful weeding | When the necks soften and the tops fall over, then cure |
| Tuber | Potato | Seed tubers planted | 90 cm × 30 cm | Earth up to cover tubers; control late blight | When the tops die back and the skins are set |
| Fruit | Tomato | Transplanted | 90 cm × 45 cm | Stake and prune; even watering; control mites, bollworm and blights | At the colour-break stage for distant markets, ripe for local sale |

Spacings are typical values; they differ with cultivar, soil and season.

:::worked-solution[Seedlings and fertiliser for a cabbage plot]
Tendai's cabbage plot is 18 m × 15 m, planted at 60 cm × 45 cm. He raises 10% extra seedlings for gapping. His nursery bed is 10 m × 1.2 m and he applies compound fertiliser at 80 g/m² before sowing.

1. Plot area = 18 × 15 = 270 m².
2. Area per plant = 0.60 m × 0.45 m = 0.27 m².
3. Plants = 270 ÷ 0.27 = **1,000** cabbages.
4. Seedlings to raise = 1,000 × 1.1 = **1,100**.
5. Nursery bed area = 10 × 1.2 = 12 m²; fertiliser = 12 × 80 = **960 g**.
:::

## Managing the growing crop

- **Watering**: regular and even, especially for tomatoes (uneven watering causes blossom-end rot and cracked fruit) and onions (shallow roots).
- **Weeding** by hand or hoe, shallowly near shallow-rooted crops; mulch to reduce weeds and save water.
- **Top dressing** leafy crops with a nitrogen fertiliser such as ammonium nitrate, watered in.
- **Staking and pruning** tomatoes to keep fruit off the soil and let in air and light, which reduces disease.
- **Earthing up** potatoes so that tubers are not exposed to light; exposed tubers turn green and become poisonous.
- **Pest and disease control**: scout often; remove badly affected plants; rotate crops of different families; use approved chemicals only when needed and observe the waiting period before harvest.

:::example{title="Problems in Chipo's garden"}
Chipo's tomatoes have fruit with dark, sunken patches at the end opposite the stalk; she waters only when she remembers. This is **blossom-end rot**, caused by a shortage of calcium in the fruit when watering is irregular, so she must water evenly and mulch. Her cabbage seedlings are being cut off at ground level at night: **cutworms** are hiding in the soil, so she should dig them out near damaged plants and protect new transplants with a collar of paper or cardboard round the stem. Small green caterpillars making holes in cabbage leaves are **diamondback moth** larvae.
:::

## Harvesting and marketing vegetables

Vegetables are **perishable**: they lose water and quality within days.

1. Harvest at the right stage and in the **cool of the morning**.
2. Keep produce in the shade and handle it gently to avoid bruising.
3. **Grade** by size and quality and remove damaged produce.
4. **Pack** in clean crates, bags or bundles suited to the crop (onions and potatoes in net or mesh bags, tomatoes in crates).
5. Transport quickly to the market: a local market, Mbare Musika, a supermarket, a school or hospital, or a roadside stall.
6. **Stagger planting** (sow small amounts every few weeks) so that produce is available over a long period instead of all at once when prices are low.
7. Keep records of costs, sales and prices to judge which crops pay best.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Transplanting at midday"}
Seedlings lose water faster than their damaged roots can take it up and wilt badly. Transplant in the late afternoon and water at once.
:::

:::mistake{title="Fresh manure for carrots"}
Fresh manure makes carrot roots fork and split. Use well-rotted manure applied for an earlier crop.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Horticulture has three main branches: olericulture (vegetables), pomology (fruit) and floriculture (flowers); it provides nutrition, income, jobs and exports.
- Small-seeded vegetables are raised in a fine, sterilised, mulched and shaded nursery bed, hardened off and transplanted in the late afternoon.
- Learn one crop of each group: rape or cabbage, carrot, beans, onion, potato and tomato, with spacing and management.
- Water evenly, weed, top dress, stake tomatoes, earth up potatoes and control pests such as aphids, diamondback moth and cutworms.
- Vegetables are perishable: harvest in the cool, grade, pack, transport quickly and stagger planting.
:::
