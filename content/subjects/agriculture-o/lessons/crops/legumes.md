## Introduction

On many smallholdings near Murehwa or Chipinge, a family groundnut plot supplies food and peanut butter sold at the local market. On larger farms, soya beans are grown for oil and for the protein cake fed to chickens and pigs, and sugar beans are a valuable source of protein for families and a good cash crop.

These crops are **legumes**: their roots carry nodules full of *Rhizobium* bacteria that fix nitrogen from the air. That makes them cheap to fertilise and valuable in rotations. ZIMSEC asks you to grow one legume in Form 3; Cambridge may choose a legume as the crop of local importance. You should already know the nitrogen cycle.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Legume"}
A plant of the pea and bean family whose seeds form in pods and whose roots have nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
:::

:::definition{term="Inoculation"}
Coating legume seed with a culture of the correct *Rhizobium* bacteria before planting so that the roots form effective nodules.
:::

:::definition{term="Pegging"}
In groundnuts, the growth of the stalk (peg) from a fertilised flower down into the soil, where the pod develops underground.
:::

:::definition{term="Shelling percentage"}
The mass of kernels obtained from a mass of unshelled pods, expressed as a percentage of the pod mass.
:::

## Requirements of the three crops

| | Groundnuts | Soya beans | Field (sugar) beans |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soil | Light, well-drained sandy loams, so pegs enter easily and pods lift cleanly | Well-drained, fertile loams | Well-drained, fertile soils |
| Climate | Warm; regions II-IV | Warm; good rainfall, regions II-III | Short season (about three months); killed by frost |
| Key nutrient | Calcium in the pegging zone (gypsum) | Phosphorus and potassium | Phosphorus |
| Nitrogen | Little needed | Little needed if well nodulated | Small starter dose often given |
| Main use | Food, peanut butter, oil, cake | Oil, protein cake for stock feed, food | Food (dry beans) |

## Seed and planting

**Groundnuts** are shelled just before planting. Choose whole kernels with an unbroken skin (testa), treat them with a fungicidal seed dressing, and plant about 5 cm deep. Two groups of cultivars are grown: erect **Spanish** types that mature early and have little seed dormancy (so they may sprout in the ground if harvest is late), and spreading **Virginia** types that need a longer season and have dormant seed.

**Soya beans** must be **inoculated** where the crop has not been grown before, because the right bacteria are not present in the soil. Mix the inoculant with the seed in the **shade**, plant the same day, and keep inoculated seed away from sunlight, fertiliser and some seed dressings, which kill the bacteria.

**Field beans** need disease-free seed, because several bean diseases are carried in seed.

All three are planted in rows when the soil is moist, usually at the start of the rains (beans may also be planted later in the season or in winter in frost-free areas). Because legume seeds are large, seed rates are high, often around 100 kg/ha.

:::worked-solution[Groundnut population and seed rate]
Groundnuts are planted 45 cm between rows and 10 cm within the row. Assume 1,000 kernels weigh 450 g and 85% germinate.

1. Area per plant = 0.45 m × 0.10 m = 0.045 m².
2. Plants per ha = 10,000 ÷ 0.045 = 222,222 plants.
3. Kernels needed = 222,222 ÷ 0.85 = 261,438 kernels.
4. Mass = 261,438 × 450 g ÷ 1,000 = 117,647 g, about **118 kg/ha** of kernels.
:::

## Fertiliser and gypsum

Well-nodulated legumes supply most of their own nitrogen, so heavy nitrogen fertiliser is wasted and can even reduce nodulation. They do need **phosphorus** (for root and nodule growth) and **potassium**, given as a basal dressing such as single superphosphate or a compound low in nitrogen.

**Groundnuts** need **calcium** in the soil where the pods form, or many pods are empty ("pops"). Gypsum (calcium sulfate) is spread over the rows at **early flowering**, before pegging, so that calcium is in the pegging zone when the pods fill.

:::worked-solution[Gypsum and inoculant for two fields]
Chipo spreads gypsum at 250 kg/ha on 0.6 ha of groundnuts. She also plants 2 ha of soya at 90 kg/ha. Assume gypsum comes in 50 kg bags and one sachet of inoculant treats 50 kg of soya seed.

1. Gypsum = 250 × 0.6 = 150 kg = 150 ÷ 50 = **3 bags**.
2. Soya seed = 90 × 2 = 180 kg.
3. Inoculant = 180 ÷ 50 = 3.6, so she buys **4 sachets** (round up so that all the seed is treated).
:::

## Weeds, pests and diseases

Legumes grow slowly at first and are poor competitors, so weed early by hand, with a hoe or with a suitable herbicide. In groundnuts, finish weeding before pegging: hoeing later damages pegs and young pods.

| Crop | Pest or disease | Notes and control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Groundnuts | Groundnut rosette (virus spread by aphids) | Plant early at a full, even stand; remove infected plants |
| Groundnuts | Early and late leaf spot (fungal) | Rotation, resistant cultivars, fungicide sprays |
| Soya beans | Soya bean rust (fungal) | Scout, fungicide sprays, plant on time |
| Soya beans | Pod-sucking bugs, red spider mite | Scout and spray approved insecticides when needed |
| Field beans | Bean stem maggot (bean fly) | Seed dressing, early planting, earthing up |
| Field beans | Bacterial blight, rust, anthracnose | Clean seed, rotation, avoid working in wet crops |
| All, in store | Bruchids (bean weevils) and other storage insects | Dry well, clean store, approved protectant or airtight storage |

## Maturity, harvesting and storage

**Groundnuts** are mature when the leaves turn yellow and most pods have a darkened inside shell and well-coloured kernels; pull up a few plants to check. Lift the plants by hand or with a lifter when the soil is moist, shake off soil, and dry them with the **pods upwards** in windrows, then in stacks, so that pods dry quickly and do not touch damp soil. Damp groundnuts grow a mould that produces **aflatoxin**, a poison that harms people and livestock. Strip the pods when dry and store them in the shell in a dry, well-ventilated store.

**Soya beans** are ready when the leaves have dropped and the pods are dry and rattle. Harvest promptly: dry pods **shatter** and drop seed. Cut and thresh, and store at a safe moisture content.

**Field beans** are pulled when most pods are dry, dried, then threshed by beating and cleaned.

:::worked-solution[Shelling percentage]
Rudo shells 1,000 kg of dry pods and obtains 700 kg of kernels.

1. Shelling % = 700 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **70%**.
2. A low shelling percentage would suggest many empty pods ("pops"), for example from calcium shortage.
:::

## Marketing and rotation

Legumes are sold to processors, through contracts, at local markets and as value-added products such as peanut butter or roasted nuts. Grade the produce, keep it clean and free of mould, and record yields and costs.

:::example{title="Legumes in Tendai's rotation"}
Tendai grows maize on the same land every year and his yields are falling. His AGRITEX officer suggests soya beans every second year. The soya fixes nitrogen and leaves residues that release it as they decompose, so the next maize needs less top dressing. Because soya belongs to a different family, maize pests and diseases such as grey leaf spot have no host for a season, and the soya's taproot opens the soil. The change also spreads his income across two crops.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Inoculating in the sun"}
*Rhizobium* bacteria are living organisms killed by sunlight, heat and contact with fertiliser. Inoculate in the shade and plant at once.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying gypsum at planting"}
The calcium is needed in the pegging zone when the pods form, so gypsum goes on at early flowering, not at planting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drying groundnuts on wet soil"}
Pods touching damp soil grow mould and aflatoxin. Dry them pods-up and off the ground.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Groundnuts, soya beans and field beans are legumes that fix nitrogen through *Rhizobium* in root nodules.
- Groundnuts need light, well-drained soils and gypsum at early flowering; soya must be inoculated in the shade where it is new.
- Seed rates are high (often about 100 kg/ha); fertiliser supplies phosphorus and potassium with little nitrogen.
- Weed early; control rosette, leaf spots, rust, bean fly and storage bruchids.
- Harvest at maturity, dry quickly (groundnuts pods-up to avoid aflatoxin), store dry, and use legumes in rotations.
:::
