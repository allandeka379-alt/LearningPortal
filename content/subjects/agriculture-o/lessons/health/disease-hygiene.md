## Introduction

When Chipo's school started a broiler project near Mutare, the agriculture teacher insisted on a footbath of disinfectant at the poultry house door, a rule that no visitors enter, and a full clean-out between batches. Students grumbled at the extra work until a neighbour who ignored these rules lost a large part of his flock to disease in one week.

Most livestock losses on small farms come from diseases that could have been **prevented**. Prevention is cheaper than treatment: drugs, vets' visits, deaths and lost production all cost money, and some diseases can spread to people. ZIMSEC (Form 2) asks you to explain the causes of disease and how diseases are transmitted, justify hygiene and discuss methods of control; Cambridge asks you to explain how infectious and contagious diseases spread and why hygiene and isolating sick animals matter.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pathogen"}
A micro-organism that causes disease, such as a bacterium, virus, protozoan or fungus.
:::

:::definition{term="Infectious disease"}
A disease caused by a pathogen that invades the body and multiplies; it can be passed from one animal to another by some route.
:::

:::definition{term="Contagious disease"}
An infectious disease that spreads readily by direct or close contact between animals, for example foot-and-mouth disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
A living organism, usually an arthropod such as a tick or a biting fly, that carries a pathogen from one animal to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Quarantine"}
Keeping newly bought or exposed animals apart from the rest of the stock for a period, to see whether they develop disease before they join the herd.
:::

:::definition{term="Isolation"}
Separating an animal that is already sick from healthy animals, to stop the disease spreading.
:::

Every contagious disease is infectious, but not every infectious disease is contagious. **Tetanus**, for example, is caused by bacteria entering a wound from the soil; it is infectious but does not spread from animal to animal.

## Causes of disease

| Cause | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Bacteria | Anthrax, mastitis (often), blackleg, tetanus |
| Viruses | Foot-and-mouth disease, Newcastle disease, lumpy skin disease |
| Protozoa | Trypanosomiasis, redwater (babesiosis), coccidiosis |
| Fungi | Ringworm (skin), poisons from mouldy feed |
| Parasites | Ticks, lice, mites, roundworms, tapeworms, liver fluke |
| Poor nutrition | Rickets, anaemia, milk fever, pica, general weakness |
| Injury | Wounds, broken bones, foot injuries from rough floors or wire |
| Poisoning | Poisonous plants, mouldy feed, farm chemicals stored carelessly, polluted water |

Disease seldom has a single cause. Poor feeding, overcrowding, cold or wet housing and stress such as transport **lower an animal's resistance**, so pathogens and parasites that are always present cause illness more easily.

## How infectious diseases spread

1. **Direct contact**: animals touching, licking, mating or suckling (e.g. foot-and-mouth disease, some skin diseases).
2. **Air (droplets and dust)**: coughing and sneezing spread respiratory diseases, especially in crowded, poorly ventilated houses (e.g. Newcastle disease in poultry).
3. **Contaminated feed and water**: dung or discharges in troughs and dams spread scours and worm eggs.
4. **Vectors**: ticks carry redwater, gallsickness and heartwater; tsetse flies carry trypanosomiasis.
5. **Equipment, vehicles, clothing and footwear** (sometimes called fomites): pathogens are carried on boots, crates, egg trays, feed bags and lorry wheels from farm to farm.
6. **Carriers**: animals that look healthy but carry and shed a pathogen, including wild animals and birds.
7. **Animal products**: meat, hides, bones and milk from infected animals.
8. **From mother to young**: before birth or in milk.

:::example{title="Tracing an outbreak"}
Farai buys three goats at a market near Gokwe and puts them straight in with his herd. Two weeks later several of his goats have scabby, itchy skin, and the new goats were the first affected. The disease came in with the purchased animals, and spread by **direct contact** and by rubbing on the same posts. A **quarantine** pen for the new goats, with checking and treatment before mixing, would have stopped it.
:::

## Prevention and control

**Hygiene and sanitation**

- Clean pens, houses, feeders and water troughs regularly; remove dung and wet litter.
- Provide clean water and fresh feed stored away from rats and birds.
- Ventilate houses well but keep out cold draughts; avoid overcrowding.
- Wash hands, boots and equipment; keep separate overalls and boots for each house.
- Dispose of dead animals safely by deep burial with lime or by burning, away from water sources; never leave carcasses for dogs and scavengers.

**Biosecurity at the farm gate**

- Keep visitors and vehicles away from animal houses; use a **footbath** with disinfectant at each door.
- Buy stock from known, healthy sources and **quarantine** new animals.
- Use **all-in, all-out** systems for poultry and pigs: the house is emptied, cleaned, disinfected and left empty for a period before new stock arrive.

**When disease appears**

- **Isolate** sick animals in a sick pen, downwind and away from the herd, with their own feeder, water and equipment.
- Call a veterinary or extension officer; report suspected notifiable diseases by law.
- Treat as advised, complete the full course and observe the withdrawal period before selling milk, eggs or meat.

**Building resistance**

- Feed balanced rations and give clean water.
- Vaccinate against common diseases; control ticks, flies and worms.
- Choose breeds adapted to local conditions, such as indigenous cattle and chickens.

:::worked-solution[Making up a footbath]
The instructions for a farm disinfectant (assume) say: mix 1 part concentrate with 39 parts water. The footbath holds 40 L.

1. Total parts $= 1 + 39 = 40$.
2. Concentrate $= \dfrac{40 \times 1\,000}{40} = 1\,000$ mL (1 L).
3. Water $= 40\,000 - 1\,000 = 39\,000$ mL (39 L).
4. Replace the footbath when it becomes dirty; dung and soil inactivate many disinfectants.
:::

:::worked-solution[Judging a batch by mortality]
A school starts a batch of 400 day-old broiler chicks and 12 die before slaughter.

1. Mortality $= \dfrac{12}{400} \times 100 = 3\%$.
2. Recording mortality batch after batch shows whether hygiene is working: a sudden rise is a warning sign of disease or poor management.
:::

Disinfectants are farm chemicals: wear gloves and eye protection when handling the concentrate, measure accurately, and store them locked away from feed, children and animals.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing quarantine and isolation"}
**Quarantine** holds new or exposed animals that look healthy; **isolation** separates animals that are already sick.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating 'infectious' and 'contagious' as the same"}
Contagious diseases spread by contact; some infectious diseases (tetanus) do not.
:::

:::mistake{title="Disinfecting a dirty floor"}
Remove dung and litter and wash first: disinfectant does not work well on organic matter.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Justify hygiene"}
A *justify* or *explain* question needs the practice **and** the reason: "a footbath at the door kills pathogens on boots, so visitors do not carry disease into the house".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss methods of control"}
For a *discuss* question, group your points (hygiene, biosecurity, isolation, vaccination, vector control, nutrition) and say why each works and any drawback, such as cost or labour.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Causes: pathogens (bacteria, viruses, protozoa, fungi), parasites, poor nutrition, injury and poisoning; stress lowers resistance.
- Contagious diseases are infectious diseases spread by contact; tetanus is infectious but not contagious.
- Spread: contact, air, feed and water, vectors, equipment and footwear, carriers, products, mother to young.
- Prevention: hygiene, biosecurity, quarantine of new stock, isolation of sick stock, all-in all-out, safe carcass disposal, vaccination, vector control, good feeding.
- Disinfect only after cleaning; handle disinfectants safely.
:::
