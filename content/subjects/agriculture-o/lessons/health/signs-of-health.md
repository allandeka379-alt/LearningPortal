## Introduction

Every morning before school, Tatenda walks through his family's cattle kraal near Masvingo. Most of the cows get up as he approaches, stretch, and move off to graze. One cow stays lying down with her head turned to her side, her nose dry, and she ignores the hay put out for her. Tatenda calls his father, who takes her temperature and phones the veterinary officer. Because the problem was spotted early, the cow is treated before she loses her calf.

Recognising ill-health early is the most valuable skill a stockman has: treatment works best and costs least when a disease is caught early, and infectious diseases can be isolated before they spread. ZIMSEC (Form 1) asks you to define animal health and distinguish healthy from unhealthy livestock; Cambridge asks you to recognise signs of health and ill-health.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Animal health"}
The state of an animal whose body and behaviour function normally, which is free from disease, injury and parasites, and which grows, reproduces and produces as expected for its breed and age.
:::

:::definition{term="Disease"}
Any condition that disturbs the normal working of an animal's body, shown by signs of ill-health and usually a fall in production.
:::

:::definition{term="Vital signs"}
Measurements of body functions that change in illness: body temperature, pulse rate and respiration (breathing) rate.
:::

## Signs of health and ill-health

| Feature | Healthy animal | Unhealthy animal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behaviour | Alert, active, stays with the group, responds to sounds and people | Dull, isolated from the group, restless or lies down for long periods; may grind its teeth or kick at its belly |
| Appetite and rumination | Eats and drinks normally; ruminants chew the cud for long periods | Off feed, not drinking or drinking excessively; cud-chewing stops |
| Coat, skin and feathers | Smooth, glossy coat; clean, bright feathers; supple skin | Rough, dull, staring coat; ruffled or missing feathers; wounds, lumps or ticks |
| Eyes | Bright, clear, alert | Dull, sunken, watery, discharging or with pale membranes |
| Nose and mouth | Moist, clean muzzle in cattle; no discharge | Dry muzzle; discharge from the nose; excess saliva; blisters |
| Droppings and urine | Normal colour and consistency for the species; passed without strain | Diarrhoea (scours), very hard dung, blood or worms in dung, discoloured urine, straining |
| Posture and movement | Stands evenly on all four legs; walks freely | Lame, hunched back, head hanging low, stiff or staggering gait |
| Body condition | Well fleshed for its age and stage | Thin, ribs and hip bones showing, pot belly or swelling |
| Production | Normal growth, milk yield, egg laying | Sudden drop in milk or eggs, slow growth, abortion |
| Mucous membranes | Pink gums and inner eyelids | Pale (anaemia), yellow (jaundice) or blue-tinged |

A single sign may have several causes; the stockman looks for **a pattern of signs** and compares the animal with its healthy pen-mates.

## Vital signs and how to measure them

The values below are **approximate normal values** for resting adult animals. Textbooks differ slightly; young animals and animals that are excited, hot or have just been driven give higher readings.

| Animal | Rectal temperature (about) |
| --- | --- |
| Cattle | 38.5 °C |
| Sheep and goats | 39-40 °C |
| Pigs | 39 °C |
| Chickens | 41-42 °C |

In resting adult cattle the pulse is about 40-80 beats per minute and the breathing rate about 10-30 breaths per minute. Smaller animals generally have faster pulse and breathing rates than cattle.

**Temperature**: shake a clinical thermometer down, lubricate it, insert it gently into the rectum and hold it against the rectal wall for about one minute (or until a digital thermometer beeps). A temperature well above normal is a **fever**, usually a sign of infection; a temperature below normal may mean shock or a very weak animal.

**Pulse**: feel an artery where it passes over a bone: under the base of the tail (cattle), inside the thigh (sheep, goats, pigs) or under the jaw. Count for 15 or 30 seconds and multiply up to beats per minute.

**Respiration**: stand back and count the rise and fall of the flank, without disturbing the animal.

:::worked-solution[Calculating pulse and respiration]
Rudo counts 16 pulse beats in 15 seconds under a cow's tail, and 11 movements of the flank in 30 seconds.

1. Pulse rate $= 16 \times 4 = 64$ beats per minute (60 s ÷ 15 s = 4).
2. Respiration rate $= 11 \times 2 = 22$ breaths per minute.
3. Both lie within the approximate normal ranges for cattle, so they give no sign of illness on their own.
:::

:::worked-solution[Spotting the sick cow]
Five cows have rectal temperatures of 38.4, 38.7, 40.9, 38.5 and 38.6 °C.

1. Cow C (40.9 °C) is about $40.9 - 38.5 = 2.4$ °C above the approximate normal temperature: she has a **fever**.
2. Mean of the other four $= (38.4 + 38.7 + 38.5 + 38.6) \div 4 = 38.55$ °C, close to normal.
3. Including cow C would raise the mean to 39.02 °C and hide which cow is sick: always look at individuals as well as averages.
:::

## Observing and comparing animals

Good stockmanship means watching the animals **every day**, ideally at the same times:

1. **From a distance first**: notice which animals stand apart, lie down, or do not come to feed.
2. **At feeding**: note appetite, and in ruminants whether they are chewing the cud.
3. **Close up**: check the eyes, nose, coat, udder, feet and back end; look at the droppings.
4. **Handle calmly**: catch and restrain the animal quietly (in a crush for cattle) to take temperature and pulse, so that excitement does not raise the readings.
5. **Record**: write down what you see, the readings and the date, and compare with earlier records and with healthy pen-mates.
6. **Act**: isolate an animal that may have an infectious disease and call the veterinary or extension officer.

:::example{title="Reading the signs in a poultry house"}
Nyasha finds that egg collection in her layer house has dropped from the usual number to about two-thirds in three days. Several hens sit hunched with ruffled feathers and closed eyes, some have watery, greenish droppings, and two are gasping. The **pattern** (sudden fall in production, dullness, abnormal droppings, breathing difficulty in several birds at once) suggests an infectious disease, not a feeding problem in one hen. She should keep visitors out, separate sick birds and report to a veterinary officer straight away.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Relying on one sign"}
A dry muzzle or a slightly high temperature alone may mean the cow has just walked in the sun. Look for several signs together and compare with healthy animals.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring after chasing the animal"}
Running and excitement raise temperature, pulse and breathing. Let the animal rest and handle it calmly first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating exact normal values"}
Normal values are approximate ranges. Write "about 38.5 °C" for cattle, not an exact figure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Healthy versus unhealthy"}
In a "distinguish" question, set your answer out in pairs: "a healthy cow chews the cud; a sick cow stops ruminating". One mark is often given for each matched pair.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Multiply a pulse count by 60 ÷ (seconds counted). Give units: beats per minute, breaths per minute, °C.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A healthy animal is alert, eats and ruminates normally, has a glossy coat, bright eyes, normal droppings and normal production.
- Signs of ill-health include dullness, isolation, loss of appetite, rough coat, discharges, abnormal droppings, lameness and a fall in production.
- Vital signs: temperature (cattle about 38.5 °C, chickens about 41-42 °C), pulse and respiration, measured on a calm, rested animal.
- Pulse per minute = count × (60 ÷ seconds counted).
- Observe daily, compare with healthy animals, record, isolate and report.
:::
