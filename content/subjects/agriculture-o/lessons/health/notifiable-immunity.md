## Introduction

When foot-and-mouth disease is confirmed in a district, the effects reach far beyond the farm where it started. Veterinary officers stop cattle movements, sales and auctions in the area, roadblocks check vehicles, and beef exports from the whole country may be stopped. A single farmer who hides sick animals can cost a whole region its markets.

That is why certain diseases are **notifiable**: the law requires anyone who suspects them to report them. This lesson covers four named examples, how veterinary services control them, and how animals become **immune**. ZIMSEC asks you to discuss methods of disease control; Cambridge asks you to explain notifiable (scheduled) diseases.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
ZIMSEC usually says **notifiable diseases**; Cambridge says **notifiable (scheduled) diseases**. Both mean diseases listed in animal health law that must be reported to the government veterinary services.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Notifiable (scheduled) disease"}
A disease named in animal health law that must, by law, be reported to the government veterinary services as soon as it is suspected, because it spreads quickly, causes great losses, can infect people, or threatens trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Immunity"}
The ability of an animal's body to resist a particular disease, usually because it has antibodies or memory cells against the pathogen.
:::

:::definition{term="Vaccine"}
A preparation of killed or weakened pathogens, or parts of them, given to an animal to make it produce its own antibodies without causing the disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Colostrum"}
The first milk produced by a female after giving birth: thick, yellowish and rich in antibodies, protein, vitamins and minerals.
:::

## Why report notifiable diseases?

- They **spread rapidly** over large areas (foot-and-mouth disease, Newcastle disease).
- They can **infect people** (anthrax, rabies).
- They cause **heavy losses** of animals and production.
- They **close markets**: other countries refuse meat and live animals from infected areas.
- Only government veterinary services can impose the control measures needed: **quarantine** of farms and districts, **movement control** (permits, roadblocks, stopping sales), **compulsory vaccination**, **slaughter and safe disposal**, and disinfection.

The farmer's duty is to report suspected cases, keep the animals confined, allow inspection and follow the veterinary officers' instructions. Other diseases that are commonly notifiable include rabies, African swine fever and lumpy skin disease; the list is set by each country's law.

## Four named examples

| | Anthrax | Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) | Newcastle disease | Trypanosomiasis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Bacterium (*Bacillus anthracis*), forms long-lived spores in soil | Virus | Virus | Protozoan (*Trypanosoma*) in the blood |
| Animals | Cattle, sheep, goats and others; **people** | Cloven-hoofed animals: cattle, pigs, sheep, goats; buffalo can carry it | Chickens and other poultry | Cattle and other livestock |
| Spread | Spores in soil and on grazing; infected carcasses | Contact, air, animal products, vehicles, people; carriers | Air, droppings, contaminated equipment, people, wild birds | Bite of the **tsetse fly** (vector) |
| Main signs | Sudden death; dark, tarry blood that does not clot oozing from nose, mouth and anus; no stiffening of the carcass | Fever; blisters on the mouth, tongue, feet and teats; drooling; lameness; sharp drop in milk | Gasping, coughing, twisted neck, paralysis, greenish diarrhoea, sharp drop in eggs, many deaths | Anaemia, fever, weight loss, weakness, swollen lymph nodes, abortion; a slow, wasting disease |
| Control | **Never open a suspected carcass**: air makes the bacteria form spores. Report; burn or bury deep with lime. Annual vaccination in risk areas | Report; quarantine and movement control; vaccination; separating cattle from buffalo by fencing | Report; vaccination of chicks and repeat doses; biosecurity; no cure | Tsetse control (traps and insecticide-treated targets, spraying); trypanocidal drugs given by trained people; avoiding tsetse areas |

:::example{title="A sudden death in the paddock"}
Tendai finds a cow dead in the morning with no sign of struggle. Dark blood is oozing from her nostrils and does not clot. These signs suggest **anthrax**. He must not cut the carcass open or let dogs reach it. He should keep people and animals away, report at once to the veterinary officer, and follow instructions for burning or deep burial with lime. Anthrax can infect people who handle or eat meat from such a carcass.
:::

## Immunity

Animals resist disease in two main ways. **Active** immunity: the animal makes its own antibodies after meeting the antigen; it takes some days to develop but lasts a long time. **Passive** immunity: ready-made antibodies are received from outside; it acts at once but lasts only a few weeks.

| | Active | Passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Recovering from an infection | Antibodies from the mother in colostrum (and across the placenta in some species) |
| Artificial | Vaccination | Injection of antiserum (ready-made antibodies), for example after a high-risk wound |

### Colostrum

Newborn calves, lambs, kids and piglets are born with very little protection. Their gut can absorb whole antibodies from colostrum only for a short time after birth, and this ability falls quickly during the first day. The young must therefore **suck within the first few hours**. Colostrum also gives energy, vitamin A and a laxative effect that clears the first dung. A calf that receives no colostrum is far more likely to die of scours or pneumonia. Farmers keep frozen colostrum to feed newborns whose mother has died or has no milk.

### Vaccination

Vaccines are given by injection, in drinking water, by eye or nose drop or by spray, depending on the disease and the animal. Good practice:

1. Keep vaccines cool (in a refrigerator or cool box) and out of sunlight; check the expiry date.
2. Vaccinate only healthy animals, at the age and interval on the label, with booster doses where needed.
3. Use clean equipment; for drinking-water vaccines use clean water without disinfectant, and withhold water for a short time first so the birds drink it quickly.
4. Mix only what will be used at once and dispose of leftovers safely.
5. Record the date, vaccine, batch number and animals treated.

:::worked-solution[How many vials of Newcastle vaccine?]
A farmer has 1,250 chickens. Assume the vaccine comes in vials of 500 doses and each bird needs one dose.

1. Vials needed $= 1\,250 \div 500 = 2.5$, so buy **3 vials** (vials cannot be split between days).
2. Doses left over $= 3 \times 500 - 1\,250 = 250$; mixed vaccine cannot be kept, so plan vaccination days to reduce waste.
:::

:::worked-solution[Vaccination coverage]
A dip-tank area has 240 cattle and 180 are brought for vaccination.

1. Coverage $= \dfrac{180}{240} \times 100 = 75\%$.
2. A quarter of the cattle stay unprotected and can keep a disease circulating, which is why veterinary services aim to vaccinate as many animals as possible.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Opening an anthrax carcass to check"}
Never open it. Spores form in air, contaminate the soil for many years and can infect people.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling colostrum active immunity"}
Antibodies in colostrum are made by the mother: the young receive **passive** immunity that lasts only weeks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying vaccines cure disease"}
Vaccines prevent disease in healthy animals; they do not cure animals that are already sick.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Named diseases"}
For each named disease learn four things: the cause (type of organism), the animals affected, two signs, and two control measures. Exam questions often give signs and ask you to name the disease.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain why notifiable"}
Give reasons, not just a definition: rapid spread, danger to people, heavy losses and loss of export markets, so that government action is needed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Notifiable (scheduled) diseases must be reported by law to the veterinary services.
- Anthrax (bacterium; sudden death, unclotted blood; never open the carcass), FMD (virus; blisters, cloven-hoofed animals), Newcastle disease (virus; poultry; vaccinate), trypanosomiasis (protozoan; tsetse fly).
- Control: quarantine, movement control, vaccination, vector control, slaughter and safe disposal.
- Active immunity (infection, vaccination) is made by the animal and lasts; passive immunity (colostrum, antiserum) is borrowed and short.
- Young animals need colostrum within the first few hours of life.
:::
