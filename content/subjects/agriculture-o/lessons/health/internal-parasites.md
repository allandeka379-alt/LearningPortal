## Introduction

Nyasha's goats near Chinhoyi look well fed in December, but by February, after weeks of warm rain, several kids are thin with rough coats, pale inner eyelids and a soft swelling under the jaw. One dies suddenly. The goats have been grazing the same small, wet paddock near the homestead all season. The cause is not lack of food but **worms**: blood-sucking roundworms that build up on wet, heavily grazed pasture.

**Internal parasites** (endoparasites) live inside the host's body, usually in the gut, liver or lungs. They seldom kill quickly, but they quietly reduce growth, milk and fertility, so they are among the costliest problems in livestock farming. ZIMSEC and Cambridge both ask you to explain the problems caused by parasites; you should be able to describe a roundworm life cycle and explain control.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal parasite (endoparasite)"}
A parasite that lives inside its host's body, for example in the stomach, intestines, liver or lungs.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct life cycle"}
A life cycle completed with one host only: the infective stage develops outside the host (on pasture) and is swallowed by the same kind of animal.
:::

:::definition{term="Intermediate host"}
An animal in which a parasite spends part of its life cycle (often multiplying or forming cysts) before it reaches its final host, for example the water snail for liver fluke.
:::

:::definition{term="Anthelmintic"}
A drug (dewormer or dose) that kills internal parasitic worms; it is given by mouth, injection or pour-on.
:::

## The main groups

| Group | Examples and where they live | Life cycle | Main damage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roundworms (nematodes): round, unsegmented | Wireworm in the abomasum of sheep and goats; large roundworm in the intestine of pigs; roundworms of poultry | Direct: eggs in dung, larvae develop on pasture or in pens | Blood loss (wireworm), poor digestion, diarrhoea, poor growth; larvae migrating through the liver and lungs of pigs |
| Tapeworms (cestodes): flat, made of segments | Tapeworms in the intestine of sheep, goats and poultry; beef and pork tapeworms in people, with cysts in cattle or pig muscle | Indirect: need an intermediate host | Poor growth in young stock; cysts ("measles") make meat unfit to sell and can infect people |
| Liver fluke (trematode): flat, leaf-shaped | Bile ducts of the liver of cattle, sheep and goats | Indirect: water snail is the intermediate host | Liver damage, anaemia, weight loss, swelling under the jaw; livers condemned at slaughter |

## Life cycle of a roundworm (wireworm of sheep and goats)

1. Adult worms live in the **abomasum**, where they suck blood. Females lay large numbers of eggs.
2. The eggs pass out in the **dung** onto the pasture.
3. In warm, moist weather each egg hatches into a first-stage **larva**, which feeds on bacteria in the dung and moults twice.
4. The third-stage larva is the **infective** stage. It moves out of the dung and up grass blades in films of moisture, especially after rain and in the early morning dew. In warm, wet weather the cycle from egg to infective larva can take as little as a week or two.
5. A grazing animal swallows the infective larvae with the grass.
6. The larvae develop into adults in the abomasum in about three weeks and start laying eggs, so the cycle repeats.

The life cycle is **direct**: no intermediate host. The more animals graze one paddock, the more dung and the more larvae, so heavy stocking of small, wet paddocks gives the worst worm burdens.

## Tapeworms and liver fluke

**Beef tapeworm**: the adult lives in the human gut and sheds egg-filled segments in human faeces. Cattle swallow eggs on grass or in water contaminated by human faeces; the larvae form cysts (beef measles) in the muscles. People are infected by eating undercooked infected beef. Control: use toilets (never defecate on grazing land), meat inspection at abattoirs, and cooking meat thoroughly.

**Liver fluke**:

1. Adult flukes in the bile ducts lay eggs that pass out in the dung.
2. In water, eggs hatch into a swimming larva that enters a **water snail**.
3. Inside the snail the larvae multiply; many free-swimming larvae leave it.
4. They form cysts on grass at the water's edge.
5. Cattle, sheep or goats swallow the cysts; young flukes burrow through the liver to the bile ducts.

Control: fence off or drain swampy areas, provide water from troughs, control snails, and dose with a drug that kills flukes.

## Signs of internal parasites

- Loss of weight and condition despite good feed; poor growth in young stock.
- Rough, dull, staring coat.
- Pale membranes of the eye and gums (anaemia), especially with wireworm and liver fluke.
- Swelling under the jaw ("bottle jaw") from fluid collecting when blood protein is lost.
- Diarrhoea and dirty hindquarters (with many gut worms).
- Pot belly in young animals; worms or tapeworm segments (like rice grains) in the dung.
- Coughing (lungworms; migrating larvae in pigs); sudden deaths in severe wireworm infestations.

## Prevention and control

1. **Dosing**: give an anthelmintic at strategic times (for example during the rainy season, when infection is highest) and when animals show signs. Weigh animals or dose by the weight of the **heaviest** in the group, because under-dosing leaves worms alive and encourages **resistance**. Change the drug group as advised, not every dose.
2. **Rotational grazing**: move animals on before larvae build up and rest paddocks so many larvae die; avoid grazing the same small paddock all season.
3. **Avoid overstocking** and keep young animals on the cleanest pasture.
4. **Avoid wet areas**: drain or fence off swamps; do not graze wet, low-lying land in the early morning when larvae are high on the grass.
5. **Hygiene**: clean pens and water troughs, raise feeders so feed is not soiled with dung, and remove dung from pig and poultry houses.
6. **Control intermediate hosts**: control snails (for fluke) and prevent people from contaminating grazing (for beef and pork tapeworms).
7. **Check and record**: an eyelid colour chart (pale = anaemic) lets farmers dose only the goats and sheep that need it; dung samples can be checked for worm eggs; dose and quarantine new animals before they join the herd.

:::worked-solution[Dosing a group of goats]
Assume a dewormer label says 1 mL per 10 kg of body weight. Three sample goats weigh 28, 32 and 41 kg.

1. Dose for the heaviest goat $= 41 \div 10 = 4.1$ mL.
2. Give each goat in the group 4.1 mL so that none is under-dosed.
3. For 20 goats: $4.1 \times 20 = 82$ mL of dewormer.
:::

:::worked-solution[Using an eyelid colour chart]
Tendai checks the inner eyelids of 60 goats and finds 12 very pale.

1. Percentage anaemic $= \dfrac{12}{60} \times 100 = 20\%$.
2. Only these 12 are dosed for wireworm; the others are left untreated, which saves money and slows the build-up of resistance.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the roundworm cycle indirect"}
Roundworms of livestock have a **direct** cycle; tapeworms and liver fluke need intermediate hosts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Under-dosing to save money"}
Guessing weights or using too little drug leaves worms alive and leads to resistant worms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dosing without changing management"}
If animals return to the same wet, overstocked paddock they are reinfected within weeks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the roundworm life cycle"}
Use numbered stages: adult in gut, eggs in dung, larvae on pasture, infective larvae on grass, swallowed, adult. State that it is direct.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link control to the life cycle"}
Explain **why** each method works: "rotational grazing rests paddocks so infective larvae die before animals return".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Internal parasites: roundworms (direct cycle), tapeworms and liver fluke (need intermediate hosts).
- Roundworm cycle: adult in gut → eggs in dung → larvae on pasture → infective larvae on grass → swallowed → adult.
- Signs: weight loss, rough coat, anaemia, bottle jaw, diarrhoea, pot belly, worms in dung.
- Liver fluke needs water snails; beef tapeworm needs people and cattle.
- Control: correct dosing by weight, rotational grazing, avoid overstocking and wet areas, hygiene, snail control, toilets and meat inspection.
:::
