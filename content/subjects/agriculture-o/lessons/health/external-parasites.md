## Introduction

On dipping day at a communal dip tank near Gokwe, the veterinary assistant checks the dip wash and the herders drive the cattle through one by one. Without regular dipping, ticks would cover the cattle's ears, udders and the soft skin under the tail in the hot, wet months, and the diseases they carry (redwater, gallsickness, heartwater and January disease) would kill many animals. Ticks are the most important external parasites of cattle in southern Africa.

An **external parasite** (ectoparasite) lives on the outside of its host, feeding on blood, skin or hair. ZIMSEC and Cambridge both ask you to explain the problems caused by parasites, describe their control, and describe the life cycle of one tick.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Parasite"}
An organism that lives on or in another living organism (the host), obtaining food from it and harming it.
:::

:::definition{term="External parasite (ectoparasite)"}
A parasite that lives on the skin, hair or feathers of its host, for example ticks, lice, mites, fleas and biting flies.
:::

:::definition{term="One-host tick"}
A tick that spends its larval, nymphal and adult feeding stages on the same host animal, dropping off only as an engorged adult female to lay eggs.
:::

:::definition{term="Three-host tick"}
A tick whose larva, nymph and adult each feed on a different host, dropping to the ground to moult after each meal.
:::

:::definition{term="Acaricide"}
A chemical that kills ticks and mites, used in dips, sprays, pour-ons and tick grease.
:::

## Damage caused by external parasites

- **Blood loss**: heavy infestations cause anaemia, weakness and poor growth.
- **Irritation**: animals rub, scratch and bite themselves instead of feeding and resting, so they lose condition and produce less.
- **Wounds and damaged hides**: bites leave holes that lower the value of hides and skins; wounds become infected or attract blowflies.
- **Damage to teats and udders**: ticks with long mouthparts cause abscesses, blind teats and mastitis.
- **Transmission of disease**: ticks and biting flies are vectors of serious diseases.
- **Production losses**: less milk, meat, eggs and wool, and more deaths among young stock.

## Ticks

Ticks are arachnids (eight legs as adults and nymphs; six as larvae). Every stage feeds on blood. The main types differ in the number of hosts in their life cycle.

| Tick | Hosts in its life cycle | Where it attaches (typical) | Disease spread (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Blue tick | One host | Body, neck, shoulders, belly | Redwater (babesiosis) and gallsickness (anaplasmosis) |
| Red-legged tick | Two hosts | Under the tail, around the anus | Mainly damage and irritation |
| Brown ear tick | Three hosts | Inside the ears | Theileriosis (January disease) |
| Bont tick | Three hosts | Udder, scrotum, feet, under the tail | Heartwater; long mouthparts cause deep wounds |

### Life cycle of the blue tick (a one-host tick)

1. An engorged adult female drops off the host onto the ground and hides in grass or under litter.
2. She lays thousands of eggs in one mass over a period of days and then dies.
3. The eggs hatch into tiny six-legged **larvae**, which climb grass stems and wait for a host to brush past.
4. The larvae attach to a passing animal and feed on blood.
5. On the **same** host the larva moults into an eight-legged **nymph**, which feeds and moults into an **adult**.
6. Adults mate on the host; the female feeds until she is swollen with blood (about three weeks after the larva attached) and drops off to lay eggs.

Because the blue tick stays on one animal for about three weeks, regular dipping at short intervals kills it before the females drop off to lay eggs.

### Two-host and three-host ticks

In a **two-host tick** (for example the red-legged tick) the larva and nymph feed on the first host; the engorged nymph drops, moults on the ground, and the adult finds a second host. In a **three-host tick** (brown ear tick, bont tick) each stage drops off after feeding and moults on the ground, so the tick spends much of its life off the host. Three-host ticks are harder to control because dipping only reaches the stages that happen to be on the animal; they can also survive long periods on the veld without feeding.

## Other external parasites

| Parasite | Hosts | Signs and damage | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lice (biting and sucking) | Cattle, pigs, goats, poultry | Itching, rubbing, rough coat, hair loss, anaemia; eggs (nits) glued to hairs | Dips, sprays, dusts; clean housing; treat all animals in the group |
| Mites | Pigs, goats, sheep, poultry, rabbits | Mange: thick, crusty, hairless skin and intense itching; scaly leg in poultry | Repeated acaricide treatment; isolate and treat affected animals; clean housing |
| Fleas | Pigs, poultry, dogs | Irritation, anaemia in young animals | Insecticidal dusts; clean, dry bedding |
| Biting flies (tsetse, stable flies, horse flies) | Cattle, donkeys | Painful bites, restlessness, blood loss; tsetse spread trypanosomiasis | Traps and insecticide-treated targets, sprays, clearing breeding sites |
| Blowflies | Sheep, cattle, any animal with a wound | Maggots in wounds (fly strike) | Treat wounds promptly; insecticidal wound dressing |

## Prevention and control

1. **Plunge dipping**: animals swim through a long tank of acaricide wash so the whole body, including the ears, is wetted.
2. **Spray race**: animals walk through a race fitted with spray nozzles; uses less wash, but wetting must be thorough.
3. **Hand spraying** with a knapsack sprayer: for small herds, goats and sheep.
4. **Pour-on** (back-line) products: poured along the back; convenient for small numbers.
5. **Hand dressing**: tick grease applied to ears, tail brush, udder and between the legs where ticks hide.
6. **Pasture management**: rotational grazing and **spelling** (resting) paddocks starve some tick stages, and short grass gives larvae fewer places to wait.
7. **Breeds**: indigenous breeds such as the Mashona and Nguni carry fewer ticks than many exotic breeds.

**Safe and effective dipping**: keep the wash at the correct strength (measure and replenish it as instructed), dip at regular intervals (more often in the hot, wet season when ticks are most numerous), do not dip animals that are hot, thirsty or exhausted, keep young calves from swallowing wash, wear protective clothing when handling acaricide concentrate, and never let wash drain into rivers. Using a weak wash helps ticks become **resistant** to the acaricide.

:::worked-solution[Making up a dip tank]
A dip tank holds 15,000 L of wash. Assume the acaricide instructions say 2 mL of concentrate per litre of water.

1. Concentrate $= 2 \times 15\,000 = 30\,000$ mL.
2. $30\,000 \div 1\,000 = 30$ L of concentrate.
:::

:::worked-solution[Did the dipping work?]
Before dipping, Sipho counts an average of 120 ticks per cow on one side of each animal. A week after starting regular dipping the average is 18.

1. Change $= \dfrac{18 - 120}{120} \times 100 = -85\%$.
2. Tick numbers fell by **85%**, showing the dipping is effective; a count that hardly falls would suggest a weak wash or acaricide resistance.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling ticks insects"}
Ticks and mites are arachnids: adults have eight legs. Lice, fleas and flies are insects with six legs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up one-host and three-host"}
One-host: all stages feed on one animal. Three-host: larva, nymph and adult each drop off and find a new host.
:::

:::mistake{title="Diluting the dip to save money"}
A weak wash does not kill all the ticks and encourages acaricide resistance.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe a life cycle"}
Write the stages in order as numbered steps: egg, larva, nymph, adult; say where each feeds and where it moults (on the host or on the ground). Name the tick.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Damage questions"}
For "explain the problems caused by ticks", link each problem to its effect: "blood loss leads to anaemia and poor growth; bites damage hides, lowering their value".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- External parasites: ticks, lice, mites, fleas, biting flies and blowflies.
- Damage: blood loss, irritation, damaged hides and teats, disease transmission, lower production.
- Blue tick (one-host) spreads redwater and gallsickness; brown ear tick (three-host) spreads theileriosis; bont tick (three-host) spreads heartwater.
- Control: dipping, spray races, hand spraying, pour-ons, tick grease, pasture spelling, resistant breeds.
- Keep dip wash at the correct strength to kill ticks and slow resistance.
:::
