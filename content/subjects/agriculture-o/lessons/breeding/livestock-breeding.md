## Introduction

Sipho runs a herd of indigenous Mashona cows near Masvingo. They survive the dry season on poor grazing, resist ticks well and calve regularly, but their calves grow slowly and fetch modest prices. A neighbour suggests buying an exotic beef bull; an AGRITEX officer suggests artificial insemination with semen from a proven bull; an uncle warns him not to keep breeding from the same family of bulls. Each suggestion is a **breeding decision**, and each has costs, benefits and risks.

Livestock breeding means deciding which animals become parents so that the next generation is more productive. Examination questions ask why animals are bred, how the environment affects breeding, how to select breeding stock, how the main breeding systems work with their advantages and disadvantages, and why artificial insemination is useful.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Breed"}
A group of animals of one species with similar appearance and characteristics that, when mated together, produce offspring with the same characteristics.
:::

:::definition{term="Inbreeding"}
Mating closely related animals, such as father and daughter, mother and son, or full brother and sister.
:::

:::definition{term="Line breeding"}
A mild form of inbreeding in which animals are mated so that their offspring stay closely related to one outstanding ancestor.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-breeding"}
Mating animals of two different breeds to combine their useful characteristics and obtain hybrid vigour in the offspring.
:::

:::definition{term="Upgrading (grading up)"}
Mating local or non-descript females, and then their female offspring in each generation, to purebred males of an improved breed, so that the herd becomes almost purebred after several generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Artificial insemination (AI)"}
Placing semen collected from a selected male into the reproductive tract of a female on heat, using an instrument, instead of natural mating.
:::

## Why livestock are bred

- **Higher productivity**: faster growth, more milk, more eggs, better feed conversion, larger litters.
- **Better quality** products: carcass, milk fat, wool or skins.
- **Adaptation** to local conditions: heat tolerance, ability to use poor grazing, resistance to ticks and tick-borne diseases.
- **Fertility and mothering ability**: animals that conceive easily, give birth without help and rear their young.
- **Good temperament**: docile animals are easier and safer to handle.

## Effects of the environment on breeding

Genes set what an animal can achieve; the environment decides what it does achieve.

- **Climate**: exotic European breeds suffer **heat stress** in hot areas; they eat less, grow and milk less and their fertility falls. Zebu and Sanga breeds, including the indigenous Mashona, Tuli and Nkone, tolerate heat.
- **Nutrition**: poorly fed heifers reach puberty late, cows on poor grazing may not come on heat, and underfed males produce less semen. Good feeding is needed for animals to show their genetic potential.
- **Disease and parasites**: ticks, tick-borne diseases and worms reduce growth and fertility; exotic breeds are often less resistant.
- **Management**: housing, water supply, health care and handling all affect performance, so improved breeds need improved management.

## Selecting animals for breeding

Breeders choose parents using three sources of information.

| Basis | What is checked | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Performance** | How well the animal itself produces | Growth rate and weaning weight, milk yield, number and weight of young weaned, regular calving, feed conversion |
| **Records** | Pedigree (ancestors' performance) and **progeny** (offspring's performance) | A bull whose daughters give high milk yields; a sow from a line with large litters |
| **Conformation** | Body shape and soundness | Straight back, deep body, strong legs and feet, sound teeth and eyes, well-formed udder and teats, normal reproductive organs, breed characteristics |

Animals with defects, poor fertility, bad temperament or poor mothering are **culled** (removed from breeding). Because a male mates many females, choosing the right male has the biggest effect on the herd, so males are selected most strictly; **progeny testing** (judging a bull by the performance of his offspring) is the most reliable method, although it takes years.

## Artificial selection

Artificial selection is the repeated choosing of the best animals as parents, generation after generation. Over time the useful alleles become more common and the average performance of the herd rises. All the breeds kept today, from dairy breeds to broiler strains, were produced by artificial selection.

## Systems of breeding

| System | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inbreeding** | Fixes desirable characteristics; produces uniform, true-breeding stock; exposes hidden recessive defects so carriers can be culled | Increases homozygosity, so harmful recessive alleles show up: lower fertility, weak young, defects and slower growth (**inbreeding depression**) |
| **Line breeding** | Keeps the qualities of an outstanding ancestor with less risk than close inbreeding | Still some risk of inbreeding depression; slow; progress limited to that line |
| **Cross-breeding** | **Hybrid vigour** (faster growth, better survival and fertility); combines characteristics of two breeds, e.g. heat and tick tolerance of an indigenous breed with fast growth of an exotic breed | Offspring are not true-breeding; crossbred females mated among themselves give variable offspring; both parent breeds must be maintained |
| **Upgrading** | Cheap way of improving a local herd, because only purebred males (or semen) are bought; adaptation is gained gradually | Slow (several generations); upgraded animals may lose the hardiness of local stock and need better feeding and health care |

:::worked-solution[Upgrading a local herd]
Farai mates his local cows to a purebred exotic bull, and in every generation mates the female offspring to another purebred bull of the same breed.

1. Generation 1: offspring have $\frac{0 + 100}{2} = 50\%$ exotic genes.
2. Generation 2: $\frac{50 + 100}{2} = 75\%$.
3. Generation 3: $\frac{75 + 100}{2} = 87.5\%$.
4. Generation 4: $93.75\%$; generation 5: $96.875\%$.

| Generation | Exotic breed (%) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 |
| 2 | 75 |
| 3 | 87.5 |
| 4 | 93.75 |
| 5 | 96.875 |

After about five generations the animals are almost purebred; with cattle that is many years of work.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing a system for a communal-area herd"}
Sipho's Mashona cows are hardy but slow-growing. Upgrading to an exotic breed would, after several generations, give heavy animals that may not survive the dry season and ticks without supplementary feed and regular dipping, which he cannot afford. Cross-breeding his cows with a bull of a larger, heat-tolerant breed gives first-cross calves with hybrid vigour, which grow faster but keep much of their mothers' hardiness. He should sell these crossbred calves for beef and keep breeding replacement heifers from his best Mashona cows, so the hardy base herd is not lost. He must avoid using one bull for so long that it mates its own daughters, which would cause inbreeding.
:::

## Artificial insemination

Semen is collected from a selected male, checked under a microscope, diluted (extended) and packed in thin plastic **straws**, then frozen and stored in **liquid nitrogen** at about −196 °C. A trained inseminator thaws a straw and deposits the semen in the reproductive tract of a female that is **on heat**, at the right time in the oestrous cycle.

### Benefits

- One outstanding, progeny-tested male can breed thousands of females, even in other countries, and after his death.
- No need to buy, feed and keep a bull or boar, which is costly and dangerous.
- Reduces the spread of **venereal (sexually transmitted) diseases**.
- Avoids injury when a heavy male mates small females, and avoids inbreeding when semen of unrelated sires is used.
- Gives access to many breeds, so farmers can cross-breed or upgrade cheaply.
- Accurate breeding records are kept.

### Limitations

- Needs **skilled inseminators**, equipment (liquid nitrogen containers, guns) and a reliable supply of semen.
- **Heat detection** must be accurate; inseminating at the wrong time gives low conception rates.
- Semen of a bull carrying a hidden defect can spread it widely and fast.
- Difficult in remote areas, and costs per conception rise when conception rates are low.

:::worked-solution[Conception rate after AI]
An extension officer inseminates 40 cows in a district; 26 are confirmed pregnant.

1. Conception rate = $\frac{26}{40} \times 100 = 65\%$.
2. The 14 cows that did not conceive must be watched for their next heat about 21 days later and inseminated again.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all cross-breeding upgrading"}
Cross-breeding mates two breeds; upgrading **repeatedly** uses purebred males of one breed on each generation of females.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying inbreeding is always bad"}
Inbreeding fixes characteristics and exposes carriers, which breeders use in stud herds. Its danger is inbreeding depression when it is uncontrolled.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the environment"}
An improved breed will not perform without the feeding, water and health care it needs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* reasons for breeding briefly. *Distinguish* breeding systems by what animals are mated (closely related, different breeds, purebred males on each generation). *Explain* advantages and disadvantages with a reason, and *evaluate* AI by weighing benefits against the skills and costs it needs.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Selection questions"}
Name the three bases of selection (performance, records, conformation) and give one example of each for the named animal: milk yield for a dairy cow, litter size for a sow, growth rate for a beef bull.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Livestock are bred for productivity, quality, adaptation, fertility and temperament.
- Climate, nutrition, disease and management affect how well genes are expressed.
- Select by performance, records (pedigree, progeny) and conformation; cull poor animals; select males most strictly.
- Inbreeding and line breeding fix characteristics but risk inbreeding depression; cross-breeding gives hybrid vigour; upgrading slowly converts a herd to an improved breed.
- AI spreads the genes of superior males widely, saves keeping males and reduces venereal disease, but needs skill, equipment and accurate heat detection.
:::
