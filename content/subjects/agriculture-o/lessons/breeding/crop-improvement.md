## Introduction

Every year before the rains, Thandeka's family near Gokwe buys new bags of hybrid maize seed from an agro-dealer, although they have plenty of grain left from last season. Her grandfather remembers planting his own grain from the previous harvest, but his yields were much lower. Her uncle, who lives in a drier area, still saves seed of an open-pollinated variety and of sorghum. These choices are the result of **crop improvement**: the work of plant breeders, who have produced varieties that yield more, resist diseases, survive drought and give better quality.

Examination questions ask why crops are bred, what heterosis is, how open and controlled pollination differ, how the three types of maize hybrid are produced, why hybrid seed must be bought every season, and how conventional breeding differs from genetic modification.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Crop improvement (plant breeding)"}
Changing the genetic make-up of a crop by selection and controlled crossing to produce varieties with more useful characteristics.
:::

:::definition{term="Artificial selection"}
Choosing, by people, of the plants or animals with the most desirable characteristics to be the parents of the next generation, so that those characteristics become more common.
:::

:::definition{term="Open pollination"}
Pollination that is left to natural agents (wind or insects), so the pollen parent is not controlled.
:::

:::definition{term="Controlled pollination"}
Pollination in which the breeder decides which plant supplies the pollen, for example by bagging flowers, removing tassels or anthers and dusting chosen pollen onto the stigmas.
:::

:::definition{term="Inbred line"}
A line of plants made nearly homozygous by self-pollinating for several generations. Inbred lines are uniform but weak and low-yielding.
:::

:::definition{term="Heterosis (hybrid vigour)"}
The greater vigour, growth or yield of the first-generation (F1) offspring of a cross between two genetically different parents, compared with the parents.
:::

## Why crops are bred

- **Higher yield** per hectare and per plant.
- **Resistance to diseases and pests**, for example to maize streak virus or grey leaf spot, reducing the need for chemicals.
- **Hardiness**: tolerance of drought, heat or poor soils; **early maturity** so the crop finishes before the rains end, which is important in regions IV and V.
- **Quality**: better taste, nutritional value, protein or oil content, storage life and grain colour preferred by consumers.
- **Uniformity** in height and maturity, which makes management, mechanised harvesting and marketing easier.

## Artificial selection

The oldest method is **mass selection**: at harvest the farmer chooses the best plants or cobs (large, healthy, true to the variety, from plants that stood well) and keeps their seed. Repeated over many generations, it slowly raises the frequency of useful alleles. Farmers who save seed of open-pollinated maize, sorghum, millets and beans use this method. It is cheap but slow, and in a cross-pollinated crop the pollen parent is not controlled, so progress is limited.

## Open and controlled pollination

| Open pollination | Controlled pollination |
| --- | --- |
| Pollen comes from any nearby plant | The breeder chooses the pollen parent |
| Cheap and needs no special skill | Needs labour, skill and equipment (bags, labels) |
| Offspring vary; good plants may be crossed with poor ones | Offspring of known parents; crosses can be repeated |
| Used for open-pollinated varieties (OPVs) | Used to make inbred lines and hybrids |

In maize, controlled pollination is done by covering the young ear with a bag before the silks appear and the tassel with a paper bag to collect pollen; the chosen pollen is dusted onto the silks and the ear is bagged again. In seed fields it is done on a large scale by **detasselling**: the tassels of the female rows are pulled out before they shed pollen, so all their silks receive pollen from the male rows.

## Heterosis and inbred lines

1. The breeder self-pollinates selected maize plants for several generations. Each generation becomes more **homozygous**. The resulting **inbred lines** are uniform but small and low-yielding: this loss of vigour is called **inbreeding depression**.
2. Two unrelated inbred lines are crossed. The F1 plants are **heterozygous** at many genes, and they show **heterosis**: they are taller, more vigorous and much higher-yielding than either parent, and they are uniform because both parents were uniform.

:::worked-solution[Measuring heterosis in a trial]
In a fictional trial, inbred line A yields 3.2 t/ha, inbred line B yields 2.8 t/ha and their single-cross hybrid yields 7.5 t/ha.

1. Mean of the parents (mid-parent value) = $(3.2 + 2.8) \div 2 = 3.0$ t/ha.
2. Increase of the hybrid over the mid-parent = $7.5 - 3.0 = 4.5$ t/ha.
3. Heterosis as a percentage = $\frac{4.5}{3.0} \times 100 = 150\%$ above the mid-parent.
:::

## Producing the three types of maize hybrid

Letters A, B, C and D stand for four different inbred lines.

| Hybrid | How it is produced | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Single cross** | Inbred A (female, detasselled) × inbred B (male): A × B | Most uniform and highest yield potential; seed is the most expensive, because it is harvested from a weak inbred female that yields little seed |
| **Three-way cross** | Single cross (A × B) used as the female × inbred C as the male: (A × B) × C | Seed cheaper to produce because the female is a vigorous single cross; yield and uniformity between the other two |
| **Double cross** | Single cross (A × B) × single cross (C × D) | Cheapest seed (both parents vigorous); least uniform of the three, as four lines are involved, but more adaptable |

Producing hybrid seed every season involves: maintaining the inbred lines by self-pollination, planting female and male rows in a set pattern in an **isolated** field (by distance or planting time, so no foreign pollen arrives), **detasselling** the female rows before pollen is shed, removing off-type plants (rogueing), harvesting only the female rows for seed, and then drying, grading, treating and bagging the certified seed.

## Why hybrid seed must be bought every season

The grain harvested from a hybrid crop is the **F2 generation**. When F1 plants are pollinated by each other, their alleles **segregate**: the F2 plants are a mixture of many genotypes, some resembling the weak inbred parents. The F2 crop is **not uniform**, loses much of the hybrid vigour and **yields less**. The farmer must therefore buy fresh F1 seed every season. Open-pollinated varieties, by contrast, can be saved and replanted for a few seasons, if they are kept away from other maize, because they are not relying on heterosis.

:::worked-solution[Yield loss from replanting hybrid grain]
A farmer's hybrid crop yielded 7.5 t/ha. Next season he planted grain from that crop (F2) and harvested 5.4 t/ha under similar conditions.

1. Loss = $7.5 - 5.4 = 2.1$ t/ha.
2. Percentage change = $\frac{5.4 - 7.5}{7.5} \times 100 = -28\%$, a fall of 28%.
3. The saving on seed was small compared with the value of 2.1 t of grain lost per hectare.
:::

## Selective breeding and genetic modification

| Selective (conventional) breeding | Genetic modification (GM) |
| --- | --- |
| Crosses plants of the same or closely related species | A gene can be inserted from a different species, even a bacterium |
| Whole sets of genes are mixed; many generations of selection are needed | One or a few chosen genes are transferred directly in the laboratory |
| Slow (often ten years or more for a new variety) | Faster once the method works |
| Long history of acceptance | Raises concerns about safety, gene flow to wild relatives, seed costs and control by companies; strictly regulated |

For example, a gene from a soil bacterium has been inserted into some maize and cotton varieties elsewhere in the world so that the plants make a protein toxic to certain caterpillar pests.

:::example{title="Choosing seed for a dry region"}
Rudo farms in a drier area where rains often stop early. A long-season hybrid with a high yield potential may not finish before the soil dries out. She chooses an early-maturing, drought-tolerant hybrid for most of her land and keeps seed of an open-pollinated sorghum, which she can save each year, for the driest fields. Plant breeding has given her a choice; she matches the variety to her conditions and resources.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hybrid grain is poor quality"}
The **grain** of a hybrid is good to eat or sell. The problem is only when it is **planted** as seed, because the F2 plants vary and yield less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the hybrid types"}
Single cross: two inbreds. Three-way: single cross × inbred. Double cross: single cross × single cross.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all improved seed GM"}
Hybrids and improved open-pollinated varieties are made by conventional breeding, not genetic modification.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Hybrid production"}
*Describe* hybrid production as numbered steps: produce inbreds by selfing, plant female and male rows in isolation, detassel the female rows, harvest the female rows. State the parent lines of each type of hybrid with letters.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain and compare"}
*Explain* heterosis in terms of crossing unrelated inbred lines; *explain* why seed must be bought in terms of segregation in the F2. In comparisons, give matched points for both sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Crops are bred for yield, disease and pest resistance, hardiness, early maturity, quality and uniformity.
- Artificial (mass) selection is simple and slow; controlled pollination lets the breeder choose both parents.
- Inbred lines are uniform but weak; crossing unrelated inbreds gives F1 hybrids with heterosis.
- Single cross A × B; three-way (A × B) × C; double cross (A × B) × (C × D). Seed fields are isolated and the female rows detasselled.
- F2 grain from hybrids segregates, losing uniformity and vigour, so hybrid seed is bought each season.
- GM transfers chosen genes, even between species; selective breeding crosses related plants and selects over generations.
:::
