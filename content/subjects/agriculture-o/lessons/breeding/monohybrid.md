## Introduction

Farai keeps a small beef herd near Masvingo. Horns cause injuries when cattle are crowded in pens and trucks, so he would like a herd without horns (a **polled** herd). He buys a polled bull, but when the calves arrive some of them have horns. Why? The answer lies in how a single characteristic is passed from parents to offspring: **monohybrid inheritance**. With a simple genetic diagram, Farai can predict what proportion of calves will be polled and can find out whether a polled bull carries a hidden gene for horns.

Both boards expect you to define the genetic terms precisely, to draw genetic diagrams for crosses that give 3:1 and 1:1 ratios, to use a test cross and to explain why the difference between genotype and phenotype matters to breeders.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Allele"}
One of two or more alternative forms of a gene, found at the same position on a pair of chromosomes; for example, the allele for polled and the allele for horned.
:::

:::definition{term="Dominant allele"}
An allele that shows its effect in the phenotype whenever it is present, even when only one copy is present. It is written with a capital letter (P).
:::

:::definition{term="Recessive allele"}
An allele that shows its effect only when no dominant allele is present, that is, when there are two copies. It is written with a small letter (p).
:::

:::definition{term="Homozygous"}
Having two identical alleles of a gene (PP or pp). A homozygous organism is true-breeding for that characteristic.
:::

:::definition{term="Heterozygous"}
Having two different alleles of a gene (Pp).
:::

:::definition{term="Genotype"}
The genetic make-up of an organism: the alleles it carries, for example Pp.
:::

:::definition{term="Phenotype"}
The observable characteristics of an organism, such as polled or horned, which result from its genotype and the environment.
:::

A **chromosome** is a thread of DNA in the nucleus carrying many genes; a **gene** is a length of DNA controlling one characteristic. Each body cell has two copies of each chromosome, so it has **two alleles** of every gene; each gamete has only **one**.

## Writing a genetic diagram

A complete genetic diagram always shows, in order:

1. the **parental phenotypes**;
2. the **parental genotypes**;
3. the **gametes** (one allele each, circled or listed);
4. a **Punnett square** (or lines) showing fertilisation;
5. the **offspring genotypes** and **phenotypes**, with the **ratio**.

Always state the key: for example, P = allele for polled (dominant), p = allele for horned (recessive). In cattle, polled is dominant to horned, and black coat is dominant to red.

## A cross giving a 3:1 ratio

Farai first crosses a pure-breeding (homozygous) polled bull with horned cows.

- Parents: polled bull **PP** × horned cows **pp**.
- Gametes: bull **P** only; cows **p** only.
- First generation (F1): all **Pp**, all polled.

He then mates F1 polled animals with each other (Pp × Pp).

| Gametes | P | p |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P** | PP (polled) | Pp (polled) |
| **p** | Pp (polled) | pp (horned) |

- Genotypes: 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp.
- Phenotypes: 3 polled : 1 horned, a **3:1 ratio**.

:::worked-solution[Predicting numbers from a 3:1 cross]
Polled heterozygous cows (Pp) are mated with a heterozygous polled bull (Pp) and 120 calves are born.

1. Gametes from each parent: P and p.
2. Punnett square: PP, Pp, Pp, pp, so 3 polled : 1 horned.
3. Expected polled calves = $\frac{3}{4} \times 120 = 90$.
4. Expected horned calves = $\frac{1}{4} \times 120 = 30$.
5. Of the 90 polled calves, only $\frac{1}{3}$ (about 30) are expected to be PP; the rest are Pp and look identical. The actual numbers will be close to, but rarely exactly, 90 and 30, because fertilisation is random.
:::

## A cross giving a 1:1 ratio and the test cross

When a heterozygous animal is crossed with a homozygous recessive one:

- Parents: polled **Pp** × horned **pp**.
- Gametes: P and p; p only.
- Offspring: 2 Pp (polled) : 2 pp (horned), a **1:1 ratio**.

This is the basis of the **test cross**. An animal showing the dominant phenotype may be homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp); you cannot tell by looking. Mate it with a **homozygous recessive** animal, which can only give recessive gametes, so every offspring's phenotype reveals the allele it received from the unknown parent.

| Unknown parent | Cross | Expected offspring |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homozygous dominant (BB) | BB × bb | All Bb: all show the dominant phenotype |
| Heterozygous (Bb) | Bb × bb | 1 Bb : 1 bb, half dominant and half recessive phenotype |

:::example{title="Testing a black bull"}
Chipo wants to sell a black bull as pure-breeding for black coat (B, dominant to red b). She mates him with several red cows (bb). If any calf is red, the bull must have given a **b** allele, so he is **Bb** and not pure-breeding. If all the calves are black, he is probably **BB**; the more calves born without a red one, the more certain the result, which is why breeders test a male on a fair number of females.
:::

## Why genotype and phenotype matter in breeding

- **Hidden recessive alleles**: heterozygous animals or plants look like homozygous dominant ones but can pass on an unwanted recessive characteristic, such as horns or a genetic defect. Test crosses and records reveal carriers.
- **True-breeding stock**: to produce uniform offspring, breeders need parents that are homozygous for the wanted characteristics; selling "pure-breeding" stock depends on knowing the genotype.
- **Plant breeding**: inbred lines of maize are made homozygous by repeated self-pollination before they are crossed to make hybrids. Crossing pure lines gives uniform F1 plants.
- **Selection**: selecting only by phenotype may keep carriers of undesirable recessive alleles, while the environment can make a poor genotype look good (or a good one look poor), so breeders use records of performance and progeny as well as appearance.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing gametes with two letters"}
A gamete carries **one** allele of each gene: P or p, never Pp.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading ratios as certainties"}
3:1 is a **probability** for each offspring. In a litter of four, there may be no recessive offspring at all; the ratio appears only in large numbers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Choosing letters that look alike"}
Use a letter whose capital and small forms differ (B/b, P/p). Avoid C/c, O/o, S/s and W/w, which are easily confused in handwriting.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Full marks for a genetic diagram"}
Show the key, parental phenotypes and genotypes, gametes, a Punnett square, offspring genotypes, phenotypes and the ratio. Marks are given for each step, so lay them out clearly even when the answer seems obvious.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
To *calculate* expected numbers, multiply the total by the fraction from the ratio (3/4 and 1/4, or 1/2 and 1/2). *Explain* a test cross by naming the homozygous recessive partner and both possible results.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A gene has alternative forms called alleles; dominant alleles (capital letters) show whenever present, recessive ones only when homozygous.
- Genotype is the alleles carried; phenotype is the observable result of genotype and environment.
- Heterozygous × heterozygous gives 3:1 (dominant : recessive); heterozygous × homozygous recessive gives 1:1.
- A test cross with a homozygous recessive reveals whether a dominant-looking animal or plant is homozygous or heterozygous.
- Breeders need to know genotypes to produce true-breeding stock and to remove carriers of unwanted recessive alleles.
:::
