## Introduction

A calf born on a farm near Bulawayo weighs perhaps 30 kg. Two years later it may weigh ten times as much. The extra mass is made of new cells, and every one of them was produced by a cell dividing in two. When that animal later breeds, its testes or ovaries make sperm or eggs by a different kind of division, which halves the number of chromosomes, so that the calf it fathers or bears has the right number again.

Breeders of crops and livestock need to understand these two kinds of cell division, **mitosis** and **meiosis**, because they explain how organisms grow, how vegetative propagation produces identical plants, how gametes are made and why offspring of sexual reproduction vary. ZIMSEC examines the stages of each in outline, the differences between them and how the environment affects the way genes show themselves.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chromosome"}
A thread-like structure in the nucleus made of DNA and protein, carrying many genes in a line. Chromosomes become visible when a cell divides.
:::

:::definition{term="Gene"}
A length of DNA on a chromosome that controls a particular characteristic, such as coat colour or plant height.
:::

:::definition{term="Diploid"}
Describes a cell with two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent (2n). Body cells are diploid.
:::

:::definition{term="Haploid"}
Describes a cell with one set of chromosomes (n). Gametes (sperm, eggs and the male nuclei of pollen) are haploid.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitosis"}
Cell division that produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and kind of chromosomes as the parent cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Meiosis"}
Cell division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid cells that are genetically different from each other; it produces gametes.
:::

## Chromosomes in the nucleus

In a body cell the chromosomes occur in **pairs** (homologous pairs): one of each pair came from the father and one from the mother, and both carry genes for the same characteristics in the same positions. The number is constant for each species: for example, cattle body cells have 60 chromosomes (30 pairs) and maize cells have 20 (10 pairs). Before a cell divides, each chromosome copies itself, so it then consists of two identical **chromatids** joined at a point called the **centromere**.

## Mitosis

Before division, during **interphase**, the cell grows and copies its DNA. Mitosis is then described in four stages:

1. **Prophase**: the chromosomes coil up, become shorter and thicker and visible, each as two chromatids. The nuclear membrane breaks down and a **spindle** of fibres forms.
2. **Metaphase**: the chromosomes line up singly across the middle (the **equator**) of the spindle, attached by their centromeres.
3. **Anaphase**: the centromeres split and the two chromatids of each chromosome are pulled to **opposite poles** of the cell.
4. **Telophase**: a nuclear membrane forms round each group of chromosomes, which uncoil. The cytoplasm divides (in plants a new cell wall forms across the middle), giving **two identical daughter cells**.

Each daughter cell receives an exact copy of every chromosome, so it is genetically identical to the parent cell and is diploid if the parent was diploid.

### Where mitosis happens and why it matters

- **Growth**: at root and shoot tips (meristems), in the cambium of stems and in growing young animals.
- **Repair and replacement** of damaged tissues, such as healing wounds and replacing skin and blood cells.
- **Asexual reproduction**: tubers, cuttings, grafts and suckers all grow by mitosis, so the new plants are clones of the parent.
- **Tissue culture**, used to multiply disease-free plantlets, depends on mitosis.

:::worked-solution[How fast cells multiply by mitosis]
One cell in a root tip divides by mitosis, and each new cell divides again, four times in all.

1. After 1 division: $2$ cells; after 2: $2 \times 2 = 4$; after 3: $8$.
2. After 4 divisions: $2^4 = 16$ cells.
3. If the parent cell is a maize cell with 20 chromosomes, every one of the 16 cells has 20 chromosomes, identical to the parent.
:::

## Meiosis

Meiosis has **two divisions** one after the other.

- **First division**: in prophase the members of each **homologous pair** come together and may exchange pieces (**crossing over**). The pairs line up on the equator, and then **one chromosome of each pair** goes to each pole. Each new cell now has only one chromosome of every pair: it is haploid.
- **Second division**: this is like mitosis. The chromatids of each chromosome separate to opposite poles.
- The result is **four haploid cells**, each with half the parent's chromosome number, and each **genetically different**, because the pairs separate in many different combinations (independent assortment) and crossing over mixes genes.

### Where meiosis happens and why it matters

- In animals, in the **testes** (sperm) and **ovaries** (eggs); in flowering plants, in the **anthers** (pollen) and **ovules** (egg cells).
- It **keeps the chromosome number constant** from generation to generation: two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation to give a diploid zygote.
- It produces **variation** among offspring, which breeders select from to improve crops and livestock.

:::worked-solution[Chromosome numbers in cattle]
Cattle body cells have 60 chromosomes.

1. Meiosis halves the number, so each sperm and egg has $60 \div 2 = 30$ chromosomes.
2. At fertilisation, $30 + 30 = 60$: the zygote is diploid again.
3. The zygote grows into a calf by mitosis, so every body cell of the calf has 60 chromosomes.
4. Each cell that enters meiosis gives 4 gametes, so 3 such cells in a testis give $3 \times 4 = 12$ sperm.
:::

## Mitosis and meiosis compared

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of divisions | One | Two |
| Number of cells produced | Two | Four |
| Chromosome number of new cells | Same as parent (diploid stays diploid) | Halved (haploid) |
| Genetic make-up of new cells | Identical to each other and the parent | Different from each other and the parent |
| Pairing of homologous chromosomes and crossing over | Does not happen | Happens in the first division |
| Where | Body cells: meristems, growing tissues, wounds | Reproductive organs: testes, ovaries, anthers, ovules |
| Purpose | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Gamete production, variation |

## The environment and the expression of genes

Genes decide what an organism **could** become; the environment decides how far that potential is reached. This is often summarised as:

$$
\text{phenotype} = \text{genotype} + \text{environment}
$$

- A **hybrid maize** variety with genes for high yield gives a poor crop in a drought, in poor soil or when weeds are not controlled.
- A **dairy cow** with genes for high milk production gives much less milk if she is underfed, heat-stressed or diseased.
- Seedlings grown in darkness are pale and spindly (etiolated) although they have the genes for chlorophyll: those genes need **light** to be expressed.
- In **Himalayan rabbits**, the fur grows dark only on the cooler parts of the body (ears, nose, feet, tail); the gene for dark fur is expressed only at lower temperatures.

:::example{title="Why the same bull gives different calves on two farms"}
Two farmers near Gweru use semen from the same high-quality beef bull. On Sipho's farm the calves are well fed, dipped and dewormed, and they grow fast. On the neighbouring farm the calves graze an overstocked veld and carry heavy tick loads, and they grow slowly. The calves have similar genes for growth from their sire, but the poor environment stops the genes from being fully expressed. Good breeding must therefore go with good feeding, health care and management.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying meiosis makes body cells"}
Body cells are made by **mitosis**. Meiosis happens only in reproductive organs to make gametes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Muddling chromosome numbers"}
Mitosis keeps the number the same; meiosis **halves** it. A cattle sperm has 30, not 60, chromosomes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the environment"}
An animal or plant with good genes will not perform well without good feeding, water and health care.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Stages"}
*Explain the stages* of mitosis in order (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) with what the chromosomes do in each. For meiosis, say that there are two divisions and four haploid, different cells.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Comparisons and applications"}
*Distinguish* mitosis from meiosis with matched pairs of points. When asked how the environment affects genes, give a farm example: the genotype sets the potential and feeding, climate and disease decide the phenotype.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chromosomes in the nucleus carry genes; body cells are diploid (pairs), gametes haploid.
- Mitosis: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase; two identical diploid cells; for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
- Meiosis: two divisions; four haploid, genetically different cells; in testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules; keeps chromosome numbers constant and creates variation.
- Phenotype = genotype + environment: good genes need good management to be expressed.
:::
