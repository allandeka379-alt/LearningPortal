## Introduction

Two neighbouring farms near Bulawayo have the same rainfall and the same veld. On one, the cattle graze freely over the whole farm all year; by the end of each dry season the ground is bare around the water points, gullies are spreading, and thorn bushes are taking over. On the other, the farm is fenced into paddocks, the herd is moved from one to the next, the number of cattle is matched to the grass available, and the veld is still covered in grass in October. The difference is **grazing management**.

Grass is the cheapest feed a livestock farmer has, but only if it is used without being destroyed. This lesson is part of **Cambridge** topic 8: you must explain grazing systems and describe the extensive management of grazing land, including **stocking rate** and **carrying capacity** calculations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Livestock unit (LU)"}
A standard used to compare animals of different sizes by the amount of grazing they need. One LU is commonly taken as one mature cow of about 450 kg; smaller animals count as a fraction of an LU. The factors vary between sources, so questions give them.
:::

:::definition{term="Stocking rate"}
The number of livestock units actually kept on a given area of grazing land, given as LU per hectare or as hectares per LU.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The number of livestock units an area of grazing can support over a long period without damaging the vegetation or the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Overstocking"}
Keeping more animals on the land than its carrying capacity: the stocking rate is higher than the land can support.
:::

## Grazing systems

| System | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous (set-stocked), unenclosed | Animals graze the whole area all year, often on open communal land | Cheap; little fencing or labour | Favourite grasses are grazed out; bare patches and erosion near water and homes; worms build up |
| Rotational grazing | The herd moves between areas so each is grazed and then rested | Grass recovers; more even grazing; breaks parasite cycles | Needs fencing or herding, water in each area, planning |
| Paddock grazing | A fenced form of rotational grazing: the farm is divided into paddocks, each grazed for a few days then rested for weeks | Precise control of grazing and rest; can be intensive | Fencing and water points are costly |
| Strip grazing | A movable electric fence gives a fresh strip of pasture each day | Very efficient use of improved pasture | Needs daily labour and electric fencing |
| Zero grazing (cut and carry) | Animals are kept in pens and fodder is cut and brought to them | Animals do not trample pasture; manure is collected; suits small farms and dairy | High labour; fodder must be grown and carried every day |
| Extensive grazing | Large areas of natural veld, low stocking rate, little input | Low cost per animal; suits dry regions | Low output per hectare |
| Intensive grazing | Small areas of improved, fertilised and often irrigated pasture, high stocking rate | High output per hectare (dairy) | High costs and skill needed |

**Enclosed** systems are fenced (paddocks, camps); **unenclosed** systems rely on herders or none at all.

### Rest periods in paddock grazing

With $n$ paddocks grazed in turn for $d$ days each, each paddock is rested for $d \times (n - 1)$ days.

:::worked-solution[Rest period with six paddocks]
A herd grazes each of 6 paddocks for 5 days.

1. Rest period $= 5 \times (6 - 1) = 25$ days.
2. The herd returns to the first paddock after $5 \times 6 = 30$ days.
3. In fast summer growth 25 days may be enough rest; in slower growth the farmer adds paddocks or shortens grazing periods.
:::

## Stocking rate and carrying capacity

:::worked-solution[Stocking rate in livestock units]
Assume a cow counts as 1 LU and a weaned calf as 0.5 LU. A farm has 40 cows and 30 weaned calves on 220 ha.

1. Livestock units $= 40 \times 1 + 30 \times 0.5 = 55$ LU.
2. Stocking rate $= 55 \div 220 = 0.25$ LU/ha.
3. Equivalently $220 \div 55 = 4$ ha per LU.
:::

:::worked-solution[Carrying capacity and overstocking]
A ranch of 600 ha of veld needs about 5 ha per LU (assume). The owner has 150 LU.

1. Carrying capacity $= 600 \div 5 = 120$ LU.
2. The ranch is overstocked by $150 - 120 = 30$ LU.
3. He should sell or move 30 LU, or improve the grazing (for example with paddocks, bush clearing or a fodder bank), before the veld is damaged.
:::

Carrying capacity is higher where rainfall and soil fertility are higher and on improved pastures; it falls in drought years, so a careful farmer reduces stock numbers early in a drought rather than waiting for animals to die.

## The dangers of overstocking

- Palatable perennial grasses are grazed repeatedly before they recover and die out; unpalatable plants and weeds take their place.
- Bare ground increases runoff and **soil erosion**; gullies form along paths to water.
- Less grass cover means less fuel and seed, and bush seedlings grow without competition (**bush encroachment**).
- Animals are underfed, lose condition and produce less; more die in drought.
- Parasites build up because animals graze close to dung.
- Over many years the land can be degraded so badly that it carries fewer animals than before.

## Bush control

Bush encroachment occurs when shrubs and trees thicken and shade out grass. Control methods:

- **Mechanical**: cutting, stumping or uprooting bush by hand or machine; cut stumps may need treatment to stop regrowth.
- **Chemical**: selective herbicides for woody plants applied to stumps or soil, used with care and according to the label.
- **Biological**: keeping goats (browsers) with cattle (grazers), so both grass and bush are used.
- **Fire**: hot fires kill bush seedlings and top growth when there is enough dry grass to carry them.
- **Prevention**: correct stocking so grass stays vigorous and competes with bush seedlings.

## Use and misuse of fire

**Correct use** (a planned, controlled burn):

- Burn only where there is plenty of old, dead grass, usually late in the dry season or just after the first rains when the grass is dormant and the soil is moist, and in calm weather.
- Prepare **fireguards** (cleared or burnt strips) to stop the fire spreading to neighbours, buildings, crops and forest.
- Purposes: remove old, unpalatable grass so animals graze the fresh regrowth, control bush and some ticks.
- **Rest** the burnt area from grazing until the grass has regrown.

**Misuse**:

- Burning too often, every year, or where there is little grass, weakens perennial grasses and exposes the soil to erosion.
- Uncontrolled fires destroy grazing that was needed for the dry season, kill trees, wildlife and livestock, and damage fences and homes.
- Grazing burnt veld immediately damages the new shoots.

:::example{title="Planning a ranch near Hwange"}
A rancher with 800 ha of dry veld, frequent drought and thickening bush wants better grazing. A good plan: fence the ranch into several camps with water in each and rotate the herd; set the stocking rate at or below carrying capacity and sell stock early in droughts; keep goats to browse bush and clear the worst thickets; and use an occasional controlled burn with fireguards where enough old grass has built up, resting the burnt camp afterwards.
:::

## How fencing and rotation improve pasture use

- Grazing pressure is controlled: each area is grazed evenly and then rested.
- Grass recovers, sets seed and builds root reserves, so the better perennial grasses survive.
- Animals can be separated by class (young stock on the best grazing, dry cows on poorer areas).
- Grazing can be kept in reserve for the dry season (a standing hay reserve).
- Worm larvae on rested paddocks die off, and animals are easier to observe and handle.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing stocking rate and carrying capacity"}
Stocking rate is how many animals **are** on the land; carrying capacity is how many it **can** support without damage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inverting the units"}
LU/ha and ha/LU are reciprocals. 0.25 LU/ha is the same as 4 ha per LU, not 0.25 ha per LU.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating fire as always good or always bad"}
Planned burning has uses; frequent or uncontrolled burning degrades veld. Give both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show the LU calculation"}
Multiply each class of animal by its LU factor, add them up, then divide by the area. State the units (LU/ha or ha/LU).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss questions"}
For "discuss the use of fire" or "discuss overstocking", give advantages and dangers and finish with a judgement on when the practice is justified.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Systems: continuous, rotational, paddock, strip and zero grazing; enclosed or unenclosed; extensive or intensive.
- Rest period with $n$ paddocks of $d$ days each $= d \times (n - 1)$.
- Stocking rate = LU ÷ area (or area ÷ LU); carrying capacity = area ÷ ha needed per LU.
- Overstocking kills good grasses, causes erosion and bush encroachment, and lowers production.
- Bush control: mechanical, chemical, browsers, fire and correct stocking.
- Fire: planned, controlled burns with fireguards and rest are useful; frequent or uncontrolled fires degrade veld.
- Fencing and rotation give even grazing, rest, parasite control and dry-season reserves.
:::
