## Introduction

Most cattle, sheep and goats in Zimbabwe live on **natural grazing (veld)**: a mixture of grasses, a few legumes, shrubs and trees that nobody planted. Veld is cheap, but its quality falls in the dry season, and on many farms it can carry only a few animals per hectare. A dairy farmer near Marondera who needs more milk from the same land may plough part of the farm and sow an **improved pasture** of productive grass and legume species, fertilised and managed carefully. An improved pasture costs money to establish, but it can feed far more animals of higher production.

This concept is in the **Cambridge** syllabus (topic 8, pasture management): you must describe the vegetation of grazing lands and how improved pastures are established.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pasture"}
Land covered with grasses, legumes or other plants that are grazed by livestock.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural grazing (veld, rangeland)"}
Grazing land whose plants grew naturally and were not sown, usually grazed extensively.
:::

:::definition{term="Improved pasture"}
A pasture sown or planted with selected, productive grass and legume species and managed with fertiliser, weed control and controlled grazing.
:::

:::definition{term="Browse"}
Leaves, young shoots and pods of shrubs and trees eaten by livestock, especially goats.
:::

## The vegetation of grazing lands

| Type | Examples | Value to livestock | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grasses | Rhodes grass, star grass, Kikuyu grass, Guinea grass; veld grasses such as red grass and thatching grass | Main source of energy and fibre; grow fast in the rains; tolerate grazing because their growing points are low down | Protein falls and fibre rises as they mature; poor quality in the dry season |
| Legumes | Lucerne, silverleaf desmodium, stylo, cowpea, clovers in cool areas | High protein and calcium; fix nitrogen with *Rhizobium* bacteria in root nodules, improving the grass growing with them | Some are hard to establish; need phosphorus and often lime; large amounts of some fresh legumes can cause bloat in cattle |
| Browse | Leaves and pods of acacias, mopane and other trees and shrubs | Stays green and protein-rich into the dry season; vital for goats and in dry areas | Hard to manage; bush can thicken and choke out grass (bush encroachment) |

**Sweet veld and sour veld**: in drier areas with fertile soils, grasses keep much of their feeding value when they dry off (**sweet veld**), so animals can graze them through the dry season. In higher-rainfall areas with leached soils, grasses are palatable only when young and green (**sour veld**); in the dry season animals on sour veld lose condition unless given supplements.

## Why establish an improved pasture?

- It produces much more feed per hectare, so the **carrying capacity** rises.
- Feed is of higher quality (especially with legumes), giving more milk and faster growth.
- A grass-legume mixture needs less nitrogen fertiliser because the legume fixes nitrogen.
- Pastures protect the soil from erosion and add organic matter, and can be part of a crop rotation (a **ley**).
- Surplus growth can be cut for hay or silage.

The costs are seed, fertiliser, lime, fencing, machinery and labour, so improved pastures are mostly used for dairy cows and other high-value stock on good soils, often with irrigation.

## Steps in establishing an improved pasture

1. **Choose the site**: deep, well-drained soil, near water and the homestead or dairy, protected from erosion.
2. **Test the soil** for pH and nutrients; apply **lime** to acid soils some months before sowing (legumes such as lucerne need near-neutral soil).
3. **Clear and prepare the land**: remove bush and stumps, plough, then harrow to a **fine, firm seedbed**. Pasture seeds are very small and need close contact with moist soil.
4. **Choose species and seed**: suited to the rainfall, temperature and soil, and to the use (grazing, hay). A grass-legume mixture is common. Buy certified seed; **inoculate** legume seed with the correct *Rhizobium* just before sowing.
5. **Fertilise at sowing**: phosphorus (for example single superphosphate) helps root growth, especially for legumes; grass-only pastures need nitrogen top dressing later.
6. **Sow** at the start of the rains, or under irrigation, either in rows with a drill or broadcast; cover lightly (seed about 1-2 cm deep at most) and roll to firm the soil. Grasses such as star grass and Kikuyu are often **planted vegetatively** from runners or root splits in moist soil.
7. **Control weeds**: young pasture grows slowly and weeds compete strongly; mow or slash weeds before they seed, hand-pull, or use a suitable selective herbicide.
8. **First grazing**: graze lightly and briefly once the plants are well rooted (they are not pulled out of the ground when tugged), then rest; this encourages tillering and spreading. Do not graze young pasture when the soil is wet.
9. **Maintain**: top dress, control grazing with paddocks, remove weeds and reseed bare patches.

1. Area $= 250 \times 100 = 25\,000$ m² $= 25\,000 \div 10\,000 = 2.5$ ha.
2. Grass seed $= 6 \times 2.5 = 15$ kg; legume seed $= 4 \times 2.5 = 10$ kg.
3. Superphosphate $= 300 \times 2.5 = 750$ kg $= 750 \div 50 = 15$ bags.

:::example{title="Choosing species for two farms"}
On a high-rainfall farm in the Eastern Highlands near Chipinge, with cool nights and moist soils, Kikuyu grass planted from runners, with a clover, would suit dairy cows. On a drier farm near Masvingo with sandy soil, a drought-tolerant grass such as Rhodes grass sown with stylo, a legume that tolerates poor sandy soils, would be a better choice. In each case the species are matched to rainfall, temperature and soil.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Sowing small seeds deep in a rough seedbed"}
Pasture seeds have little food store. Sown deep or in cloddy soil, the seedlings die before reaching the light.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to inoculate legumes"}
Without the right *Rhizobium*, the legume forms few nodules and fixes little nitrogen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Grazing too early and too hard"}
Animals pull out young plants or graze them to the ground; graze lightly only when the plants are firmly rooted.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Grazing land vegetation: grasses (energy and bulk), legumes (protein, nitrogen fixation) and browse (dry-season protein, especially for goats).
- Sweet veld keeps its value when dry; sour veld is palatable only when young.
- Improved pastures raise carrying capacity and feed quality but cost money to establish.
- Steps: site, soil test and lime, fine firm seedbed, suitable certified species and inoculated legumes, phosphorus at sowing, shallow sowing and rolling, weed control, light first grazing, maintenance.
- Seed or fertiliser needed = rate (kg/ha) × area (ha).
:::
