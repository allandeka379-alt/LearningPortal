## Introduction

On a farm near Hwange a heifer calving for the first time strains for hours in a far corner of the paddock. If nobody notices that one leg is folded back, both cow and calf may die. A few kilometres away a healthy calf is born but never manages to suck; by the third day it has diarrhoea and is very weak. Most losses of young stock happen around birth and in the first weeks of life, and nearly all of them can be prevented by good stockmanship.

Cambridge asks you to describe birth in a named farm mammal, define weaning and lactation, explain the importance of colostrum and describe the care and rearing of young stock. ZIMSEC (Form 4) includes the care of young stock with the management of cattle, sheep and goats. The cow is the named animal here; the same principles apply to goats, sheep and pigs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Parturition"}
The process of giving birth; in cattle it is called calving, in pigs farrowing, in sheep lambing and in goats kidding.
:::

:::definition{term="Colostrum"}
The thick, yellowish first milk produced for the first few days after birth, rich in antibodies, protein, vitamin A and minerals, and mildly laxative.
:::

:::definition{term="Lactation"}
The production and secretion of milk by the mammary glands, starting at birth and continuing until the animal is dried off or the young is weaned.
:::

:::definition{term="Weaning"}
Permanently separating the young animal from its mother's milk so that it lives on solid feed.
:::

## Signs that birth is near

In the last days of pregnancy a cow shows:

1. A large, firm udder ("bagging up"); the teats fill and may leak.
2. A swollen, soft and relaxed vulva, sometimes with a string of clear mucus.
3. Slackened ligaments on either side of the tail head, leaving hollows.
4. Restlessness, leaving the herd, lying down and getting up often, raising the tail.

Move her a few days before the expected date to a clean, dry, sheltered **calving paddock or pen** close to the homestead, where she can be watched.

## The stages of birth

| Stage | What happens | Typical duration (cow) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Opening | Contractions of the uterus begin and the cervix opens; the cow is restless | A few hours |
| 2. Delivery | The water bag appears, then the two front feet (soles down) with the nose lying on them; strong straining pushes the calf out | Usually under about two hours; longer in heifers |
| 3. Afterbirth | The placenta (afterbirth) is expelled | Usually within a few hours |

**When to help.** Call an experienced stockman or the veterinarian if the cow strains strongly for a long time without progress, if only one leg or the head alone appears, if the tail or hind legs come first, or if the afterbirth has not come away within about a day. When assisting: wash hands and arms with soap and use a lubricant; check that the presentation is normal; attach clean calving ropes above the fetlocks; pull only when the cow strains, gently outwards and then down towards her hocks. Never use force with a vehicle or many people.

:::example{title="Deciding whether to help a calving heifer"}
Tatenda finds a heifer that has been straining for over an hour. One front foot and the nose are visible, but the second foot is missing. This is an **abnormal presentation**: the other leg is folded back. Pulling now would jam the calf's shoulder in the pelvis. He should keep the heifer quiet and call the veterinarian, who can push the calf back gently between contractions and bring the leg forward before delivery.
:::

## Care of the newborn

1. **Breathing.** Clear mucus from the nose and mouth; rub the calf briskly with dry grass if it is slow to breathe. The mother normally licks the calf, which dries and stimulates it.
2. **Navel.** Dip the navel in an iodine solution to dry it and keep out germs that cause navel ill.
3. **Colostrum.** Make sure the calf sucks within the first hour or two of birth.
4. **Shelter.** Keep the calf in a clean, dry, draught-free place; wet bedding and draughts cause pneumonia and diarrhoea (scours).

## Why colostrum matters

A calf is born with almost no antibodies, because they do not cross the placenta of the cow. Colostrum supplies them, giving **passive immunity** until the calf's own immune system develops. The calf's gut can absorb these large antibody molecules whole only for a short time after birth; the ability falls quickly over the first day, so early feeding is vital. Colostrum is also rich in protein, energy and vitamin A, and its laxative effect clears the first dark dung (meconium) from the gut.

:::worked-solution[Colostrum for a dairy calf]
Rudo's Friesian calf weighs 36 kg at birth. Assume the farm guideline is colostrum equal to 10% of body mass in the first 12 hours, split into two feeds, and that 1 kg of colostrum is about 1 L.

1. Colostrum needed = 10% of 36 kg = 36 × 10 ÷ 100 = **3.6 kg**, about 3.6 L.
2. Per feed = 3.6 ÷ 2 = **1.8 L**, the first within about two hours of birth.
3. Spare colostrum from cows with plenty can be frozen for calves whose mothers have none.
:::

## Rearing young stock

**Feeding.** Beef calves, kids and lambs stay with their mothers. Dairy calves are usually removed after colostrum and bucket-fed whole milk or milk replacer at body temperature, at the same times each day, from clean buckets. Offer clean water, good hay and a **calf starter** from the first week so that the rumen develops; calves can be weaned when they eat enough starter, often at about 8-12 weeks.

**Weaning ages (typical).** Beef calves about 6-8 months; bucket-reared dairy calves about 8-12 weeks; kids and lambs about 3-4 months; piglets about 4-8 weeks.

:::worked-solution[Growth of a beef calf to weaning]
A Mashona calf weighed 36 kg at birth and 186 kg when weaned at 210 days.

1. Live-weight gain = 186 − 36 = 150 kg.
2. Average daily gain = 150 ÷ 210 = **0.71 kg/day** (to two decimal places).
3. Comparing this figure between calves helps the farmer choose the best cows and bulls to keep.
:::

**Management practices at the right age:**

| Practice | Method | Best age and reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identification | Ear tags, ear notches (piglets), tattoos, branding (cattle) | Soon after birth, so records start at once |
| Castration of males not kept for breeding | Rubber ring (elastrator), bloodless castrator (burdizzo) or knife | Young (rubber ring within the first week): less stress, bleeding and setback in growth |
| Dehorning (disbudding) | Hot iron or caustic paste on the horn buds | Calves and kids at a few days to a few weeks old, before the horn grows from the skull |
| Vaccination and dosing | As advised by the veterinarian | Protects against diseases and worms before they cause losses |

Castrated males are calmer, cannot mate unwanted females and fatten well. Dehorned animals injure each other and their handlers less and bruise less in transport.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pulling a calf whenever the cow strains"}
Help only when there is a clear problem, pull with the contractions and never with force. Correct an abnormal presentation first or call the vet.
:::

:::mistake{title="Waiting to give colostrum"}
The gut absorbs antibodies whole only for a short time after birth. Colostrum given late protects far less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing lactation with weaning"}
Lactation is milk production by the mother; weaning is taking the young off milk.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe and explain"}
For *describe the care of a newborn calf*, give the steps in order (breathing, navel, colostrum, shelter). To *explain* the importance of colostrum, link antibodies to passive immunity and early absorption by the gut.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Age matters"}
When asked about castration or dehorning, always state *when* it is done and *why* that age is best.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Signs of approaching birth: bagging up, relaxed vulva and mucus, slack tail-head ligaments, restlessness.
- Birth has three stages: opening of the cervix, delivery (front feet and nose first) and expulsion of the afterbirth; help only for clear problems, hygienically and with the contractions.
- Newborns need clear airways, an iodine-dipped navel, colostrum within the first hours and a dry, draught-free shelter.
- Colostrum gives antibodies (passive immunity), nutrients and a laxative effect; lactation is milk production; weaning is removing the young from milk.
- Identify, castrate and dehorn young stock early to reduce stress and losses.
:::
