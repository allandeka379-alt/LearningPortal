## Introduction

Walk through a communal area near Masvingo and you will see cattle grazing on the veld, goats browsing the bushes, a few chickens scratching around the homestead and perhaps a donkey pulling a cart of water drums. On a commercial farm near Marondera you may also find a piggery, a broiler house and a fish pond. Each of these animals is **livestock**: animals kept by people for food, income, work or other uses.

In Form 1 ZIMSEC asks you to name the types of livestock, explain why they are important and list their products and by-products. In Form 2 you must sort animals into **ruminants** and **non-ruminants** and explain the difference. These ideas run through the whole of animal husbandry, because the type of stomach an animal has decides what it can eat, how it is housed and how it is managed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Livestock"}
Domesticated animals kept on a farm for food, income, work, manure or other uses, for example cattle, goats, sheep, pigs, poultry, rabbits, donkeys and farmed fish.
:::

:::definition{term="Ruminant"}
A cud-chewing animal with a stomach of four compartments (rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum) in which microbes ferment fibrous plant material. Cattle, sheep and goats are ruminants.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-ruminant (monogastric animal)"}
An animal with a simple, single-compartment stomach that does not chew the cud, for example the pig, the rabbit, the horse, the donkey and poultry.
:::

:::definition{term="Livestock product"}
The main thing an animal is kept to produce, such as meat, milk, eggs, wool or draught power.
:::

:::definition{term="By-product"}
A useful material obtained in addition to the main product, often at slaughter, such as hides, skins, bones, blood, feathers, horns, hooves and manure.
:::

## Types of livestock

| Group | Examples | Main uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large ruminants | Cattle | Beef, milk, draught power, hides, manure |
| Small ruminants | Goats, sheep | Meat, milk (goats), wool and mohair, skins |
| Non-ruminant mammals | Pigs, rabbits | Pork and bacon, rabbit meat, fur |
| Working animals (non-ruminants) | Donkeys, horses | Draught, carting, pack transport |
| Poultry | Chickens, ducks, turkeys, guinea fowl, geese | Meat, eggs, feathers, manure |
| Fish | Tilapia (bream) in ponds and dams | Fish for food and sale |

**Poultry** are domesticated birds. They are kept by almost every rural household because they are cheap to buy, need little space and give quick returns. **Fish farming** (aquaculture) keeps fish such as tilapia in ponds, dams or cages and is growing on farms with a reliable water supply.

## Ruminants and non-ruminants

The difference lies in the digestive system.

A **ruminant** swallows grass quickly, then later brings the food back up as a soft ball called the **cud**, chews it again and swallows it. This is **rumination** (chewing the cud). Its large stomach has four compartments. Billions of bacteria and protozoa in the **rumen** break down **cellulose**, the tough fibre of grass and leaves, so ruminants live well on roughage that people cannot eat. Ruminants have no upper front teeth; instead they have a hard **dental pad** against which the lower incisors cut grass.

A **non-ruminant** has one simple stomach. Pigs and poultry digest starch, protein and fat well but digest fibre poorly, so they need concentrated feeds such as maize, soya bean meal and fishmeal. This makes them compete with people for grain. Rabbits, horses and donkeys are non-ruminant **herbivores**: they ferment fibre in a large **caecum** and colon at the end of the gut, so they can still live on forage, although less efficiently than ruminants.

| Feature | Ruminant (e.g. cow, goat) | Non-ruminant (e.g. pig, fowl) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stomach | Four compartments | One simple stomach |
| Chews the cud | Yes | No |
| Upper incisors | Absent (dental pad) | Present in pigs; poultry have a beak |
| Use of fibre | Good: microbes in the rumen digest cellulose | Poor (pigs, poultry); fair in rabbits and donkeys through the caecum |
| Main feeds | Grass, browse, hay, silage, crop residues | Grain-based concentrates, kitchen waste (boiled), some green feed |

:::example{title="Sorting the animals at a school farm"}
A school farm near Gweru keeps dairy cows, Boer goats, a pen of Large White pigs, broilers, rabbits and two donkeys. The class is asked to sort them.

1. Look for cud chewing and a four-compartment stomach: the **cows** and **goats** are ruminants.
2. All the others have a single stomach: **pigs**, **broilers**, **rabbits** and **donkeys** are non-ruminants.
3. Among the non-ruminants, the rabbits and donkeys are herbivores that ferment fibre in the caecum, while the pigs and broilers need concentrate feeds.

The farm manager can feed the cows and goats mainly on grazing, browse and hay, but the pigs and broilers depend on bought meal, which is the biggest cost of those enterprises.
:::

## Importance of livestock

Livestock matter to the family, the farm and the nation.

1. **Food**: meat, milk and eggs supply high-quality protein, minerals such as iron and calcium, and vitamins.
2. **Income and employment**: animals and their products are sold at sales pens, auctions, butcheries and to processors; abattoirs, dairies and tanneries employ many people.
3. **Draught power**: oxen and donkeys plough, cultivate and pull carts on farms that cannot afford tractors.
4. **Manure**: dung and poultry litter return nutrients and organic matter to the soil.
5. **Savings and security**: a herd is a store of wealth that can be sold when cash is needed for school fees or in a drought year.
6. **Social and cultural value**: cattle and goats are used for bride wealth (*roora* or *lobola*), ceremonies and gifts.
7. **Use of land**: ruminants turn veld grass on land too dry or rocky for crops (natural regions IV and V) into food.
8. **Raw materials for industry**: hides for leather, wool and mohair for textiles, bones and blood for animal feeds and fertiliser.

## Products and by-products

| Animal | Main products | By-products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beef cattle | Beef | Hides, bones (bone meal), blood (blood meal), horns, hooves, offal, dung |
| Dairy cattle | Milk (and butter, cheese, yoghurt made from it) | Calves, cull cows for beef, manure |
| Goats | Meat, milk | Skins, mohair (Angora goats), manure |
| Sheep | Mutton and lamb, wool | Skins, manure |
| Pigs | Pork, bacon, sausages | Lard, skin, manure (also for biogas) |
| Poultry | Meat, eggs | Feathers, manure (litter), feather meal |
| Rabbits | Meat | Fur pelts, manure |

:::example{title="Is it a product or a by-product?"}
Farai keeps Saanen goats for milk. Each year he sells the male kids and old does for meat and dries their skins.

- His **main product** is milk, because that is why he keeps the herd.
- The meat from culls and male kids, the skins and the manure are **by-products** of his milk enterprise.

The same item can be a main product on one farm and a by-product on another: on a meat-goat farm with Boer goats, meat is the main product.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the pig a ruminant because it eats plants"}
Eating plants does not make an animal a ruminant. A ruminant chews the cud and has a four-compartment stomach. Pigs, rabbits and donkeys have a single stomach.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that poultry are non-ruminants"}
Chickens have a crop and a gizzard, but they do not chew the cud. They are non-ruminants (monogastric).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing products when by-products are asked for"}
If a question asks for by-products of beef cattle, beef is wrong. Give hides, bones, blood, horns, hooves, offal or dung.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="State, explain and distinguish"}
*State* or *name* needs only a word or two (for example "goats"). *Explain the importance* needs a use and why it matters ("oxen provide draught power, so farmers without tractors can plough"). *Distinguish* needs both sides of the same feature, ideally in a table: "ruminants have four stomach compartments; non-ruminants have one".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 objective items"}
Expect items that ask which animal is a ruminant, which item is a by-product, or which feature (cud chewing, dental pad) identifies a ruminant. Read every option before choosing.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Livestock are domesticated animals kept for food, income, draught power, manure and social reasons; they include cattle, goats, sheep, pigs, rabbits, donkeys, poultry and farmed fish.
- Ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats) chew the cud, have four stomach compartments and use microbes to digest fibre.
- Non-ruminants (pigs, poultry, rabbits, horses, donkeys) have one simple stomach; pigs and poultry need concentrate feeds, while rabbits and donkeys ferment fibre in the caecum.
- Products are what an animal is kept for (meat, milk, eggs, wool); by-products are extra useful materials (hides, bones, blood, feathers, manure).
:::
