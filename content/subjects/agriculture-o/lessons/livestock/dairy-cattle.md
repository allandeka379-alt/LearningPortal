## Introduction

At a smallholder dairy scheme near Marondera, farmers deliver milk to a collection centre twice a day, where it is tested, cooled and sent to a processor. Milk that is dirty, sour or contains antibiotics is rejected, and the farmer earns nothing for it. A dairy cow is the most demanding animal on the farm: she must be fed for high production, milked at the same times every day, kept free of udder infection and got back in calf soon after calving.

Cambridge examines one named ruminant for housing, nutrition and health, and dairy cattle are a common choice. ZIMSEC (Form 4) asks for the breeds of cattle in Zimbabwe, their characteristics and their management. Questions often give milk records to interpret.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lactation"}
The period during which a cow produces milk, from calving until she is dried off; a standard lactation is taken as 305 days.
:::

:::definition{term="Dry period"}
The rest period of about six to eight weeks before calving during which the cow is not milked.
:::

:::definition{term="Mastitis"}
Inflammation of the udder, usually caused by bacteria entering through the teat canal.
:::

:::definition{term="Clean milk"}
Milk from healthy cows, produced and handled hygienically, with a low bacterial count, a normal smell and taste, and no dirt, added water or drug residues.
:::

:::definition{term="Steaming up"}
Feeding extra concentrate to a cow in the last few weeks before calving to prepare her for lactation.
:::

## Breeds

| Breed | Appearance | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holstein-Friesian | Large, black and white | Highest milk volume; lower butterfat (often about 3.5%); needs good feed; suffers in heat |
| Jersey | Small, fawn to brown | Highest butterfat (often about 5%); efficient; more heat tolerant; matures early |
| Guernsey | Fawn and white | Rich, golden-coloured milk |
| Ayrshire | Red-brown and white | Hardy, good grazer, strong udder |

Indigenous cattle give little milk. Smallholders often keep crossbreds (for example a Friesian or Jersey crossed with a local breed) that combine higher yields with hardiness.

**A good dairy cow** is wedge-shaped when seen from the side, lean and angular rather than fleshy, with a deep body, a thin neck, a large, well-attached udder with four evenly spaced teats and prominent milk veins.

## Housing

- **Milking parlour or shed:** concrete floor sloping to a drain, water for washing, good light, feed troughs, easy to clean.
- **Dairy (milk room):** separate from the parlour, for filtering, cooling and storing milk away from dust, flies and smells.
- **Cow shed or loose housing** with shade, feeding and water troughs and dry lying areas.
- **Calf pens** and a **calving pen**.
- On small farms, **zero grazing**: cows kept in a roofed pen and fed cut forage, which saves land and collects manure.

## Feeding for milk production

Milk is mostly water, with fat, protein, lactose and minerals, so a dairy cow needs plenty of clean water, energy, protein and minerals.

1. **Roughage** is the base: good pasture, silage and hay keep the rumen working and the butterfat up.
2. **Concentrates** (dairy meal) are fed according to milk yield, usually at milking.
3. **Minerals**, especially calcium and phosphorus, and salt.
4. **Water** at all times; a cow drinks much more when milking.

:::worked-solution[Concentrate ration from milk yield]
Assume a farm rule: the roughage supports about 5 kg of milk a day, and each extra 2.5 kg of milk needs 1 kg of dairy meal. Chipo's cow gives 20 kg of milk a day.

1. Milk above what roughage supports = 20 − 5 = 15 kg.
2. Dairy meal = 15 ÷ 2.5 = **6 kg a day**, split between the two milkings.
3. As her yield falls later in lactation, the meal is reduced in the same way.
:::

## The milking routine and clean milk

1. Milk at the **same times every day**, about 12 hours apart, by a calm, healthy milker with clean hands, short nails and clean clothes.
2. Bring the cow quietly into the clean parlour; fear stops milk let-down.
3. **Wash the teats** with clean warm water using a separate cloth or paper towel for each cow, then **dry** them. This also stimulates **let-down**, which lasts only a few minutes, so start milking promptly.
4. Squirt the first few streams from each quarter into a **strip cup** with a dark plate to check for clots or watery milk (signs of mastitis).
5. Milk quickly and completely with the **full-hand** method (or a well-maintained machine).
6. **Dip each teat** in disinfectant after milking; give feed so that the cow stands while the teat canals close.
7. Weigh and **record** each cow's milk, filter it and **cool it quickly** to about 4 °C.
8. Wash equipment at once: rinse in cold water, wash in hot water with detergent, rinse, sanitise and dry. Seamless stainless-steel or aluminium utensils are easiest to clean.

Milk from cows treated with antibiotics must be **discarded** for the withdrawal period stated by the veterinarian or on the label.

## Mastitis

**Signs:** a hot, swollen, painful quarter; clots, flakes or watery or bloody milk; lower yield; sometimes fever. In **subclinical** mastitis there are no visible signs, but a simple test of the milk (such as the California mastitis test) or a high cell count shows infection.

**Prevention:** clean housing and bedding, hygienic milking, the strip cup, teat dipping, well-maintained milking machines, milking infected cows last, dry-cow treatment and culling cows that are infected again and again. Treat early on veterinary advice.

## The dry cow and replacement heifers

Dry the cow off about **six to eight weeks** before she is due to calve, so that the udder tissue can rest and renew itself, the calf can grow in the last months of pregnancy and she can rebuild body reserves. Many farmers infuse each quarter with a dry-cow antibiotic at drying off on veterinary advice. Give good roughage, then **steam up** in the last weeks.

:::worked-solution[Planning the dry period]
Rudo's Jersey cow is due to calve on 10 December 2026. She will be dried off 60 days before.

1. Drying-off date = 10 December 2026 − 60 days = **11 October 2026**.
2. Steaming up begins about two to three weeks before calving, in late November.
:::

**Replacement heifers** are reared well so that they are served when they reach a good size and calve at about two years old. Underfed heifers calve late and produce less milk.

## Milk records and the lactation curve

Weigh and record every cow's milk at each milking. After calving, yield rises to a **peak** about six to eight weeks into lactation and then falls slowly until drying off.

:::worked-solution[Lactation yield and butterfat]
A Jersey cow averaged 18 kg of milk a day over a 305-day lactation, with 4.5% butterfat.

1. Lactation yield = 18 × 305 = **5,490 kg** of milk.
2. Butterfat = 5,490 × 4.5 ÷ 100 = **247.05 kg**.
3. Records like these show which cows to keep, how much meal to feed each cow, and when a sudden fall warns of illness.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Washing teats and leaving them wet"}
Wet teats let dirty water run into the milk. Wash, then dry each teat with its own towel.
:::

:::mistake{title="Selling milk from treated cows"}
Antibiotic residues spoil milk for processing and are a health risk. Discard milk for the whole withdrawal period.
:::

:::mistake{title="Milking right up to calving"}
Without a dry period the udder cannot renew itself and the next lactation is lower.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe a milking routine"}
Number the steps in order from preparing the milker and cow to cleaning equipment, and give a reason for at least some steps (washing stimulates let-down; teat dipping prevents mastitis).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Interpret milk records"}
Describe the trend with numbers (peak yield and when it occurred), explain it (peak lactation, then decline) and suggest causes of any sudden change (mastitis, heat, feed or water shortage).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Friesians give the most milk; Jerseys the richest milk; Guernseys and Ayrshires are other dairy breeds; dairy cows are wedge-shaped with a large udder.
- Feed roughage as the base, concentrates by yield, minerals and plenty of water.
- Milk at regular times; wash and dry teats, use a strip cup, milk fully, dip teats, cool milk fast and clean equipment.
- Prevent mastitis by hygiene, teat dipping, dry-cow therapy and culling chronic cases.
- Dry off six to eight weeks before calving and steam up; record yields: the lactation peaks about six to eight weeks after calving.
:::
