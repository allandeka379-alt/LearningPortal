## Introduction

At a dairy near Marondera the cows spend about a third of the day lying quietly, jaws moving steadily although they are not eating. In the pig unit next door the pigs eat meal from a trough, and in the poultry house the broilers peck at crumbs and swallow small stones. These three animals deal with food in very different ways, and the differences explain why cows live on grass and silage while pigs and chickens need concentrated feeds.

Both ZIMSEC (Form 3) and Cambridge expect you to name the parts of the digestive system of a ruminant and a non-ruminant, state what each part does, describe digestion and absorption (without enzyme names) and compare the systems. Questions often give a described diagram and ask you to identify a part from its function.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Digestion"}
The breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed, by mechanical action (chewing, grinding, churning) and chemical action (digestive juices and microbes).
:::

:::definition{term="Absorption"}
The passage of digested food from the gut into the blood or lymph, mainly through the villi of the small intestine.
:::

:::definition{term="Rumination"}
Bringing a soft ball of partly digested food (the cud) back up from the rumen to the mouth, chewing it again and swallowing it.
:::

:::definition{term="Microbial fermentation"}
The breakdown of cellulose and other food by bacteria and protozoa living in the rumen (or the caecum), producing volatile fatty acids that the animal absorbs, together with methane and carbon dioxide.
:::

## The ruminant digestive system

Follow a mouthful of grass through a cow.

1. **Mouth.** The tongue wraps around the grass and the lower incisors cut it against the **dental pad** (there are no upper incisors). Large amounts of **saliva** moisten the food and buffer the rumen, keeping it from becoming too acid. Food is chewed only briefly at first.
2. **Oesophagus.** Carries food to the stomach and, during rumination, carries the cud back up.
3. **Rumen.** The largest compartment, filling most of the left side of the abdomen. It is a fermentation vat: bacteria and protozoa break down cellulose to **volatile fatty acids**, which are absorbed through the rumen wall and supply most of the animal's energy. The microbes also make protein from simple nitrogen sources and make B vitamins and vitamin K. Gases (methane and carbon dioxide) are belched out (**eructation**).
4. **Reticulum.** A small compartment with a honeycomb lining, joined closely to the rumen. It moves food into the omasum or back up for rumination and traps heavy objects such as wire and nails, which can pierce the wall ("hardware disease").
5. **Omasum.** Has many folded leaves ("manyplies") that squeeze the food and absorb water.
6. **Abomasum.** The **true stomach**, with glands that secrete acidic gastric juice. It digests protein, including the microbes that flow from the rumen.
7. **Small intestine.** Juices from the pancreas and bile from the liver complete digestion; nutrients are absorbed through the **villi**.
8. **Caecum and large intestine.** Some further fermentation; water is absorbed and faeces form. They leave through the **rectum** and **anus**.

:::worked-solution[Share of the stomach taken by the rumen]
1. Total capacity = 160 + 10 + 15 + 15 = 200 L.
2. Rumen share = 160 ÷ 200 × 100 = **80%**.
3. Reticulum share = 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5%; omasum and abomasum are 7.5% each.
4. Conclusion: in an adult ruminant the rumen is by far the largest compartment, because fermenting fibre needs a large volume and a long time.
:::

**The young calf.** At birth the rumen is small and not working, and the abomasum is the largest compartment. When the calf sucks, a fold called the **oesophageal groove** closes to form a tube that carries milk straight to the abomasum, bypassing the rumen. As the calf starts eating hay and calf starter, microbes colonise the rumen and it grows quickly. This is why calves are offered good hay and starter from the first weeks.

## The pig: a simple stomach

The pig is an omnivore with a **monogastric** (single-stomach) system: mouth with a full set of teeth → oesophagus → simple **stomach** (acid gastric juice, digestion of protein) → **small intestine** (most digestion and absorption) → small **caecum** → **colon** (large intestine; water absorption and a little fermentation) → rectum → anus. Because so little fibre is fermented, pigs need feeds rich in starch and protein (maize meal, soya meal, fishmeal) and only limited fibre.

## The fowl: crop and gizzard

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Beak | Picks up food; there are no teeth |
| Oesophagus | Carries food to the crop |
| Crop | A pouch in the oesophagus that stores and softens food with water |
| Proventriculus | The glandular stomach; secretes acid gastric juice |
| Gizzard | A thick, muscular organ that grinds food with grit, doing the job of teeth |
| Small intestine | Completes digestion; absorbs nutrients |
| Two caeca | Some fermentation and water absorption |
| Large intestine (short) | Absorbs water |
| Cloaca and vent | Common opening for faeces, urine (as white uric acid) and eggs |

Free-range chickens pick up small stones as grit. Birds in houses should be offered insoluble grit if they eat whole grain.

:::example{title="Diagnosing a problem from the anatomy"}
A cow on a farm near Chinhoyi stops eating, stands with an arched back and is reluctant to move. The paddock was recently fenced and pieces of wire were left lying in the grass. The most likely cause is a wire swallowed with grass that has collected in the **reticulum** and pierced its wall, because the reticulum traps heavy objects and contracts strongly. The farmer should call the veterinarian and, in future, clear wire and nails from paddocks and feed troughs.
:::

## Differences between ruminant and non-ruminant digestion

| Feature | Ruminant (cow) | Pig | Fowl |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stomach | Four compartments | One simple stomach | Proventriculus and gizzard |
| Teeth | No upper incisors; dental pad | Full set of teeth | None; beak and gizzard |
| Food storage before the stomach | Rumen | None | Crop |
| Main site of fibre digestion | Rumen (microbes) | Little (colon) | Little (caeca) |
| Rumination | Yes | No | No |
| Suited diet | Roughage (grass, hay, silage) | Concentrates | Concentrates and grain |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the rumen the true stomach"}
The **abomasum** is the true stomach because it has gastric glands, like the stomach of a pig. The rumen is a fermentation chamber.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up crop and gizzard"}
The **crop** stores and softens food; the **gizzard** grinds it. The crop comes first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the cow digests cellulose itself"}
The cow makes no enzyme that digests cellulose. Microbes in the rumen do it, and the cow absorbs the products.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify and state functions"}
When a part is described ("a compartment lined with folds like the pages of a book"), name it (omasum) and give its function (absorbs water). Use the exact names: rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum, crop, proventriculus, gizzard.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Distinguish or compare"}
Write matched pairs: "a ruminant has four stomach compartments, whereas a pig has one". A table earns marks quickly. Do not name enzymes; Cambridge says digestion is studied without them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Digestion breaks food into small soluble molecules; absorption takes them into the blood, mainly through the villi of the small intestine.
- Ruminants have a rumen (microbial fermentation of cellulose to volatile fatty acids), reticulum (honeycomb; traps heavy objects), omasum (absorbs water) and abomasum (true stomach).
- Rumination means regurgitating, re-chewing and re-swallowing the cud; the calf's oesophageal groove sends milk to the abomasum.
- The pig has one simple stomach and needs concentrates; the fowl has a crop (stores), proventriculus (gastric juice) and gizzard (grinds with grit).
:::
