## Introduction

In the dry south-west of Zimbabwe, around Gwanda and Beitbridge, the donkey cart is a common sight: carrying water drums from the borehole, firewood from the bush and maize to the grinding mill. Many families who lost oxen in droughts now plough with a span of donkeys. Donkeys are cheap to buy, hardy and easy to handle, but they are often overworked, poorly fed and hurt by rough harnesses. A healthy, well-treated donkey works better and for many more years.

ZIMSEC (Form 3) lets schools study pigs or donkeys: identify the breeds, describe housing and manage the animal to maturity. The design and fitting of harnesses are covered in the lesson on harnesses; here we deal with the animal itself.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Jack, jenny and foal"}
A jack is a male donkey, a jenny (or jennet) is a female donkey and a foal is a young donkey.
:::

:::definition{term="Mule"}
The offspring of a jack (male donkey) and a mare (female horse); mules are strong and hardy but are almost always sterile. A hinny is the offspring of a stallion and a jenny.
:::

:::definition{term="Draught animal"}
An animal used to pull loads, such as ploughs, cultivators and carts.
:::

:::definition{term="Animal welfare"}
The physical and mental well-being of an animal: enough feed and water, shelter, freedom from pain, injury and disease, freedom to behave normally and freedom from fear and distress.
:::

## Uses of donkeys

1. **Draught power:** ploughing, ridging and weeding with a span of donkeys.
2. **Transport:** pulling scotch carts and carrying packs of water, firewood, crops and goods to market.
3. **Income:** hiring out the animals and cart for ploughing or transport.
4. **Manure** for the fields and gardens.
5. In some countries donkeys are also kept for milk.

Compared with oxen, donkeys are cheaper, need less feed and water, survive drought better and are easier for women and children to handle. However, a donkey is weaker than an ox, so a team of donkeys is needed for ploughing and work is slower.

## Breeds

Most donkeys in Zimbabwe are **local (indigenous) donkeys**: small, usually grey to brown with a dark stripe along the back and across the shoulders, hardy and well adapted to heat, poor grazing and little water. Larger **exotic breeds** have been developed elsewhere, such as the Poitou of France and the Catalan of Spain; they are taller and stronger but need better feeding and are rarely kept here. Jacks of large breeds are also used to breed mules from horse mares.

## Housing

A donkey's coat is not as waterproof as a horse's, so donkeys need shelter from rain and cold as well as shade from the sun.

- A simple shed or roofed corner of a kraal, open on the side away from the wind.
- A dry, well-drained floor (wet ground softens hooves and causes hoof rot and thrush).
- Secure fencing or walls against predators and theft.
- Clean water and a feed trough or hay rack; remove dung regularly.

## Feeding

Donkeys are grazers and browsers that digest fibre in a large caecum and colon. They live well on veld grass, browse, hay and crop residues such as maize stover and groundnut haulms.

- Allow plenty of time to graze; give hay or stover in the dry season.
- Give a little grain or concentrate when the donkey is working hard, pregnant or nursing a foal.
- Provide a salt lick and **clean water at least twice a day**, and especially after work.
- Avoid sudden changes of diet and too much rich green feed or grain, which can cause colic and laminitis (painful inflammation in the hooves); donkeys also become fat easily if overfed and underworked.

## Breeding and care of foals

Jennies are usually first bred at about three years old. They come on heat about every three to four weeks, and heat lasts several days. Gestation is long, **about 12 months** (often a little more), and usually a single foal is born.

**Care of the foal:**

1. Make sure it suckles colostrum soon after birth; dip the navel in iodine.
2. Keep mother and foal in a clean, sheltered place and do not work the jenny hard in late pregnancy or the first weeks after foaling.
3. Handle the foal gently every day so it becomes used to people, a head collar and having its feet lifted.
4. Wean at about six months or later, when it eats grass and hay well.

**Training.** Begin gently with a halter and leading. Introduce a harness and light loads at about two to three years; full work only when the donkey is mature, at about four years. Train with patience and reward, never by beating.

:::example{title="Choosing between oxen and donkeys"}
Rudo's family near Hwange has lost two oxen in a drought and must decide what to buy. Grazing is poor and water is far away. Donkeys are the better choice: they cost less, need less feed and water, recover quickly after drought and can be handled by Rudo and her younger brother. She should buy four donkeys for a plough span, budget for a good, padded harness and plan for slower ploughing than with oxen.
:::

## Managing working donkeys

**Hoof care.** Pick out the hooves regularly and check for stones and cracks. Hooves grow continuously; trim them about every two to three months, or whenever they are overgrown, because long, misshapen hooves cause lameness. Keep standing areas dry.

**Harness sores.** Sores form where a hard, dirty or badly fitting harness, rope or yoke rubs the skin, especially on the chest, withers, back and under the tail. Prevent them with a well-fitting, padded harness (never bare wire or thin rope), clean harness pads, checks of the skin after every working day, and rest until any wound has healed.

**Workload.** Do not overload carts or packs; work in the cooler parts of the day; give rest breaks, water and shade; and do not work very young, old, sick, lame or heavily pregnant donkeys.

:::worked-solution[Planning the ploughing]
A span of four donkeys ploughs about 0.25 ha a day when worked for about five hours with rest breaks (assume this rate). Tendai must plough 1.5 ha.

1. Days needed = area ÷ rate = 1.5 ÷ 0.25 = **6 days**.
2. To protect the donkeys he works mornings only, gives water and rests at midday, and checks harness contact points every evening.
3. If one donkey develops a harness sore, it must rest; with a spare animal (a span of five) the work can continue without delay.
:::

**Health and welfare.** Deworm regularly, control ticks, treat wounds promptly, vaccinate (for example against tetanus) where advised, and have the teeth checked, because sharp edges stop a donkey eating properly. Signs of a well-cared-for donkey: alert, good body condition, clean healthy hooves, no sores, and willing to work.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking donkeys need no shelter"}
Donkeys are hardy, but their coats are not waterproof. They need shelter from rain and cold as well as shade.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using wire or thin rope as harness"}
Thin, hard materials concentrate pressure and cause harness sores. Use broad, padded straps that fit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Working donkeys too young"}
Heavy work before the donkey is mature (about four years) damages joints and shortens its working life.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe management"}
Organise answers under headings (housing, feeding, breeding, foal care, training, hooves, harness, workload, health) and give a reason with each practice.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Welfare questions"}
When asked to *suggest* how to improve welfare, link each action to a problem: padded harness → fewer sores; rest and water → less exhaustion; hoof trimming → less lameness.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Donkeys provide draught power, transport, income from hire and manure; they are cheap, hardy and drought tolerant but weaker than oxen.
- Zimbabwe's donkeys are mostly local, hardy animals; large exotic breeds include the Poitou and Catalan.
- House donkeys in a dry, sheltered shed; feed grazing, hay and residues with extra feed for hard work, salt and clean water at least twice a day.
- Gestation is about 12 months; handle foals early, wean at about six months, start light work at two to three years and full work at about four.
- Trim hooves regularly, prevent harness sores with padded, well-fitting harnesses, avoid overloading and protect welfare.
:::
