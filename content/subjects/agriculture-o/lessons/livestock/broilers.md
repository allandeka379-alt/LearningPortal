## Introduction

Broiler production is one of the most popular projects for schools, women's clubs and young farmers in Zimbabwe. A batch of chicks bought on a Monday in Chinhoyi can be sold as table birds about six weeks later, so money comes back quickly. But broilers grow so fast that small mistakes, such as a cold brooder, wet litter or an empty drinker, quickly turn a profit into a loss.

ZIMSEC teaches broilers in Forms 1 and 2: their importance, breeds, housing, feeding, growth records, slaughtering, dressing percentage and marketing. Cambridge uses a named non-ruminant to examine housing, nutrition and health, and broilers are a good choice. Expect calculations of feed conversion ratio, mortality, dressing percentage and profit.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Broiler"}
A chicken of a fast-growing meat strain, reared for slaughter at a young age (usually about 5-7 weeks).
:::

:::definition{term="Brooding"}
Providing day-old chicks with warmth, feed, water and protection during their first weeks, before they can control their own body temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Feed conversion ratio (FCR)"}
The mass of feed eaten divided by the live-weight gain; the lower the FCR, the more efficiently feed is turned into meat.
:::

:::definition{term="Dressing (killing-out) percentage"}
The mass of the dressed carcass divided by the live mass of the bird, multiplied by 100.
:::

## Importance of broilers

- A quick return on money: a batch is ready in about six weeks, so several batches can be produced in a year.
- A cheap source of high-quality protein.
- Little land is needed, and the house can be built from local materials.
- Income and employment for smallholders, schools and youth projects.
- The litter is a valuable manure for gardens.

## Breeds and strains

Modern broilers are **hybrid strains** developed by breeding companies from meat breeds such as the White Cornish and the White Plymouth Rock. They have white feathers (so the plucked carcass looks clean), broad breasts, very fast growth and an efficient FCR. Day-old chicks are bought from registered hatcheries, which vaccinate many of them before sale.

## Housing and brooding

**The house.** Build the long axis **east-west** so that the sun does not shine directly into the house. Use a concrete floor, low walls with wire mesh above and curtains that can be raised or lowered, a well-insulated roof with an overhang, and a **footbath** of disinfectant at the door. The house must be rat-proof and well ventilated but free of draughts. Allow, for example, about 10 birds per m² of floor at slaughter weight.

**Before the chicks arrive:** clean, wash and disinfect the house and equipment, rest it for at least two weeks, spread clean, dry litter (wood shavings or chopped grass) about 5-10 cm deep, set up a **chick guard** (a ring of hardboard or cardboard that keeps chicks near the heat) and switch the brooder on several hours early.

**Heat.** Gas or paraffin brooders, infrared lamps or charcoal burners provide heat. Charcoal gives off poisonous carbon monoxide, so ventilation is essential. A typical programme starts at about 32-35 °C at chick level and drops by about 3 °C a week until the birds are fully feathered. The chicks' behaviour is the best thermometer.

| Behaviour of the chicks | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crowded under the heater, cheeping loudly | Too cold | Lower the heater or add heat |
| Far from the heater, panting with wings out | Too hot | Raise the heater or open curtains |
| Huddled on one side of the guard | Draught from that side | Block the draught |
| Evenly spread, eating, drinking and resting | Correct | No change |

## Feeding and water

| Feed | Form | Typical period (for example) | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Broiler starter | Crumbs | First 1-2 weeks | Highest protein, for fast early growth; often contains a coccidiostat |
| Broiler grower | Crumbs or pellets | About weeks 3-4 | Slightly less protein |
| Broiler finisher | Pellets | Until slaughter | More energy for fattening |

Feed and clean water must always be available. Start chicks on feed spread on trays or paper, then use tube or trough feeders and drinkers raised as the birds grow, so that the lip is level with their backs; this reduces spilt feed and wet litter.

## Health

Vaccinate against **Newcastle disease** and **Gumboro disease** (infectious bursal disease) on the schedule given by the hatchery or veterinarian. Keep litter dry to prevent **coccidiosis**. Practise **all-in, all-out**: one batch of the same age in the house at a time, then clean and rest the house. Keep visitors out, use the footbath and remove dead birds at once.

## Records and growth

**Physical records:** chicks placed, daily deaths, feed delivered and used, weekly average weights (weigh a sample of about 10% of the birds), vaccinations and temperatures. **Financial records:** every cost and every sale. Plotting average live mass against age gives a **growth curve** that should rise steeply; a flattening curve warns of disease or underfeeding.

:::worked-solution[Rudo's batch: mortality and FCR]
Rudo placed 200 day-old chicks of about 0.04 kg each. Six died. At six weeks the 194 survivors averaged 2.2 kg, and the batch ate 680 kg of feed.

1. Mortality = 6 ÷ 200 × 100 = **3%**.
2. Final live mass = 194 × 2.2 = 426.8 kg; mass of chicks placed = 200 × 0.04 = 8 kg.
3. Live-weight gain = 426.8 − 8 = 418.8 kg.
4. FCR = 680 ÷ 418.8 = **1.62** (two decimal places): about 1.6 kg of feed for every 1 kg of gain.
5. Floor space needed at 10 birds per m² = 200 ÷ 10 = 20 m².
:::

## Slaughtering and dressing

1. Withdraw feed for about 8-12 hours before slaughter (keep water available) so that the gut is nearly empty.
2. Catch birds calmly in dim light and handle them by the legs and body, never by the wings or neck.
3. Kill humanely and quickly (for example, stunning followed by cutting the neck blood vessels), then hang the bird to **bleed** completely.
4. **Scald** in hot, not boiling, water and **pluck** the feathers.
5. Remove the head and feet, then **eviscerate**: cut around the vent and remove the intestines, crop and windpipe. Keep and clean the giblets (heart, liver, gizzard).
6. Wash and **chill** the carcass quickly, pack, label and keep it cold or frozen.

Work on clean surfaces in protective clothing and dispose of feathers and offal by burying or composting. A well-finished broiler typically dresses at about 70-75%.

:::worked-solution[Dressing percentage and gross margin]
A sample bird from Rudo's batch weighed 2.2 kg live and 1.6 kg dressed. She sold all 194 dressed birds at US\$5 each (assume these prices).

1. Dressing percentage = 1.6 ÷ 2.2 × 100 = **72.7%**.
2. Income = 194 × US\$5 = US\$970.
3. Variable costs: chicks 200 × US\$0.80 = US\$160; feed 680 × US\$0.60 = US\$408; vaccines and medicines US\$18; litter and disinfectant US\$20; heating fuel US\$33; packaging US\$16; total **US\$655**.
4. Gross margin = US\$970 − US\$655 = **US\$315**, about US\$1.62 per bird sold.
:::

**Marketing.** Birds can be sold live to households and traders, or dressed to butcheries, schools, hotels and supermarkets. Plan batches to be ready at month-end and before holidays, when demand is high, and agree on buyers before the chicks are ordered.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Turning the dressing percentage upside down"}
Dressing percentage = carcass ÷ live mass × 100. Live ÷ carcass gives more than 100%, which is impossible.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a high FCR is good"}
FCR is feed per kilogram of gain. An FCR of 1.6 is better than 2.2 because less feed is needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the chicks' behaviour"}
A thermometer reading is useless if the chicks are crowding. Adjust the heat to how the chicks behave.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Write the formula first, show the numbers with units and round as the question says. Mortality, FCR and dressing percentage are each one division; profit is income minus costs.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the management of broilers"}
Work in time order: prepare the house, brooding, feeding, water, health, records, slaughter and marketing. Add a reason to each step when the question says *explain*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Broilers are fast-growing meat chickens (hybrid strains) slaughtered at about 5-7 weeks; they give quick returns from little land.
- Houses run east-west, are well ventilated but draught-free, with dry litter, a footbath and all-in, all-out management.
- Brooding starts at about 32-35 °C, falling about 3 °C a week; read the chicks' behaviour.
- Feed starter, grower and finisher with clean water at all times; vaccinate against Newcastle and Gumboro disease; keep litter dry against coccidiosis.
- FCR = feed ÷ live-weight gain; mortality = deaths ÷ placed × 100; dressing % = carcass ÷ live mass × 100; gross margin = income − variable costs.
:::
