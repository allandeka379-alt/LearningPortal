## Introduction

Thandeka runs a laying unit of 400 hens behind her house near Bulawayo and sells trays of eggs to tuck shops every morning. Her aunt in a communal area keeps about thirty indigenous chickens that roam the homestead, hatch their own chicks and are sold live at Christmas. Both are poultry farmers, but they manage their birds in very different ways and for different products.

ZIMSEC (Forms 3 and 4) asks you to identify breeds, choose a site and design housing, describe nutrition and the management of young birds, and then to slaughter off-layers or indigenous chickens, prepare eggs for market and keep production and financial records. The egg itself and how it forms are covered in the poultry reproduction lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Layer"}
A hen of a breed or strain kept mainly to produce table eggs.
:::

:::definition{term="Point of lay"}
The age at which a pullet lays her first egg, usually about 18-22 weeks.
:::

:::definition{term="Off-layer"}
A hen that has stopped laying or lays too few eggs to pay for her feed, and is culled and sold for meat.
:::

:::definition{term="Broodiness"}
The urge of a hen to stop laying and sit on a clutch of eggs to incubate them and then care for the chicks.
:::

:::definition{term="Hen-day laying percentage"}
The number of eggs laid divided by the number of hen-days (hens × days), multiplied by 100.
:::

## Layer breeds and strains

| Type | Examples | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light breeds | White Leghorn | Small body, white eggs, eat less feed, rarely broody |
| Dual-purpose (heavy) breeds | Rhode Island Red, Black Australorp, Light Sussex | Brown eggs, heavier carcass when culled, more likely to go broody |
| Commercial hybrids | Brown-egg and white-egg strains from breeding companies | High egg numbers, efficient feed use, broodiness bred out |
| Indigenous chickens | Local village chickens of many colours | Hardy, good mothers, scavenge well, fewer eggs |

## Housing layers

**Siting:** on well-drained, slightly raised ground, away from the dwelling but close enough for security, with the long axis east-west, shade trees nearby and access for feed deliveries.

**Deep-litter system.** Birds run loose on a thick layer of dry litter. The house needs **nest boxes** (roughly one for every four or five hens), placed in a dim part of the house, **perches** for roosting, feeders, drinkers, a footbath and good ventilation. It is cheaper to build and the birds can move freely, but eggs can be dirty or laid on the floor, birds can peck one another, and diseases spread through the litter.

**Cage (battery) system.** Two to four hens share a sloping wire cage; eggs roll forward to a tray and droppings fall below. More birds fit in a house, eggs stay clean, feeding and record-keeping per cage are easy and poor layers are spotted quickly. However, cages are expensive, birds cannot move freely, and many people have welfare concerns about keeping hens in small cages.

## Feeding and lighting

| Age | Feed | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day-old to about 6-8 weeks | Chick mash (starter) | High protein for early growth |
| About 8 weeks to point of lay | Grower mash | Steady growth without getting fat |
| From point of lay | Layer mash | Balanced for egg production; high in calcium for shells |

Layers also need clean water at all times (an egg is mostly water), and many farmers offer limestone or oyster-shell grit for extra calcium.

**Lighting.** Laying is stimulated by day length. Laying hens are given about **16 hours of light a day** using electric lights in the early morning or evening, and the day length must **never be reduced** while the birds are laying, or production falls.

## Egg collection, grading and marketing

1. Collect eggs at least two or three times a day, so they stay clean, are not broken or eaten and are cooled quickly.
2. Do not wash eggs: that removes the cuticle. Clean slightly soiled eggs with a dry cloth or fine sandpaper.
3. **Candle** eggs over a light to remove cracked eggs and those with blood spots.
4. **Grade** eggs by size (mass) and quality, and pack them in trays of 30 with the **broad end up** so the air cell stays at the top and the yolk stays central.
5. Store them in a cool, dry place and sell them while fresh, keeping a record of trays sold.

:::worked-solution[Laying percentage and feed per dozen eggs]
Thandeka's 400 hens laid 2,520 eggs in 7 days and ate about 46 kg of layer mash a day.

1. Hen-days = 400 × 7 = 2,800.
2. Hen-day laying percentage = 2,520 ÷ 2,800 × 100 = **90%**.
3. Trays of 30 = 2,520 ÷ 30 = **84 trays**; dozens = 2,520 ÷ 12 = 210 dozen.
4. Feed for the week = 46 × 7 = 322 kg.
5. Feed per dozen eggs = 322 ÷ 210 = **1.53 kg** (two decimal places). Tracking this figure shows whether the flock is still paying for its feed.
:::

## Culling

A flock's production peaks a few weeks after point of lay and then slowly falls. Hens that stop laying are culled. Signs:

| Feature | Laying hen | Non-laying hen (cull) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Comb and wattles | Large, red, waxy | Small, pale, shrunken |
| Vent | Large, moist, oval | Small, dry, puckered |
| Distance between the pelvic bones | Wide (about two to three fingers) | Narrow (about one finger) |
| Abdomen | Soft and deep | Hard or shallow |
| Yellow skin colour (yellow-skinned breeds) | Bleached from beak, vent and shanks | Yellow colour returns |

:::example{title="Handling a flock near the end of lay"}
Farai's commercial layers are 74 weeks old and the laying percentage has fallen to about 60%. Feed per dozen eggs is rising, so each hen now earns less than she eats. He checks the flock and culls hens with pale combs and narrow pelvic bones, then plans to sell the whole flock as off-layers and restock with point-of-lay pullets after cleaning and resting the house.
:::

## Indigenous chickens

Indigenous chickens are kept by most rural households. They are **hardy**: they tolerate heat, poor feed and many diseases better than exotic birds, scavenge for much of their food, and their meat is popular. They are excellent **brooders** and mothers, so flocks replace themselves. However, they grow slowly and lay fewer eggs, in clutches, because the hen stops laying to brood.

**Systems:**

- **Free-range (extensive):** birds roam freely, find their own food and roost in trees or a simple shelter. Cheap, but losses to predators, theft and Newcastle disease can be high.
- **Semi-intensive:** birds are housed at night in a simple, secure fowl run and allowed out during the day, with supplementary feed (grain, kitchen scraps) and water.
- **Intensive:** fully housed and fed like exotic birds; rarely used for indigenous chickens.

Simple improvements raise output: vaccinating against **Newcastle disease**, a secure house at night, supplementary feed and water, creep-feeding chicks in a small pen the hen cannot enter, and removing chicks from the hen after a few weeks so she starts laying again sooner.

## Slaughtering off-layers and indigenous chickens

The method is the same as for broilers: withdraw feed for 8-12 hours (give water), kill humanely, bleed, scald, pluck, eviscerate, wash, chill and pack. Off-layers are tougher than broilers and are often sold live or as stewing birds.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Dividing eggs by hens only"}
The hen-day laying percentage uses hens × days. 2,520 eggs from 400 hens in a week is 90%, not 630%.
:::

:::mistake{title="Packing eggs pointed end up"}
Pack eggs **broad end up** so the air cell stays at the top and the yolk stays central.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shortening the day while hens lay"}
A falling day length signals the end of the season and reduces laying. Keep the light at about 16 hours.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Compare systems"}
For deep litter versus cages, or free-range versus semi-intensive, write advantages and disadvantages in pairs and finish with a judgement for the farmer described.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Records"}
Production records (eggs per day, deaths, feed) and financial records (feed, pullets, sales) let you calculate laying percentage, feed per dozen eggs and profit. Show every step.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Layer breeds: light (White Leghorn), dual-purpose (Rhode Island Red, Black Australorp, Light Sussex) and commercial hybrids; point of lay is about 18-22 weeks.
- Deep litter is cheaper and freer; cages give clean eggs and easy culling but cost more and raise welfare concerns.
- Feed chick mash, grower mash, then layer mash high in calcium; give about 16 hours of light, never reducing it during lay.
- Collect eggs often, do not wash them, candle, grade and pack broad end up; cull hens with pale combs, dry vents and close pelvic bones.
- Indigenous chickens are hardy, good brooders and lay fewer eggs; semi-intensive housing, Newcastle vaccination and extra feed improve output.
:::
