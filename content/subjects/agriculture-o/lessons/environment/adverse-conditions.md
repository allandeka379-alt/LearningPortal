## Introduction

The school garden at a secondary school in Gweru faces every problem the climate can bring: hot October afternoons that scorch seedlings, a dry spell in January, strong winds that flatten the maize, heavy storms that wash soil from the beds, and a June frost. The agriculture club cannot change the weather, but it can change **how the weather affects its crops and animals**.

An **adverse condition** is any environmental condition that harms production: too little or too much water, too much heat or cold, strong wind or intense rain. This lesson describes the practical measures you are expected to know for ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2. For each measure, learn **what it is**, **how it is done** and **why it works**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Adverse environmental condition"}
A condition of climate or soil, such as drought, high temperature, strong wind, frost or intense rain, that reduces the growth or production of crops and livestock.
:::

:::definition{term="Mulching"}
Covering the soil surface with a layer of material such as dry grass, crop residues or leaves to reduce evaporation, keep the soil cool and protect it from raindrop impact.
:::

:::definition{term="Potholing"}
Digging small basins (pits) in the field, usually at planting stations, to collect and hold rainwater around the plants.
:::

:::definition{term="Tied ridging"}
Making ridges along the contour and joining them at intervals with small cross-ties, so that rainwater is held in basins between the ridges instead of running off.
:::

:::definition{term="Windbreak"}
A row or belt of trees, shrubs or tall grass planted at right angles to the prevailing wind to reduce wind speed over crops, gardens or livestock pens.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation tillage"}
Growing crops with minimum disturbance of the soil (no ploughing or only planting holes or rip lines) and leaving crop residues on the surface.
:::

## Measures for crops

### Conserving water in the soil (against drought and dry spells)

- **Mulching** cuts evaporation, keeps the soil cooler, reduces weed growth and protects the surface from crusting. Organic mulch also adds organic matter as it rots.
- **Potholing** and **planting basins** catch rain at each planting station, so water soaks in where the roots are.
- **Tied ridges** hold water in small basins across the whole field; on heavy soils in high-rainfall periods, ties can be broken to avoid waterlogging.
- **Manuring** with compost or animal manure adds organic matter, which improves soil structure and increases the **water-holding capacity** of sandy soils.
- **Conservation tillage** keeps residues on the surface as a mulch and leaves soil pores intact, so more rain infiltrates.
- **Cover crops** (for example cowpeas or velvet beans) shade the soil, reduce evaporation and runoff, and add organic matter.

:::worked-solution[How much water a tied-ridge basin holds]
A basin between two tied ridges measures 2.0 m by 0.9 m and fills to a depth of 60 mm in a storm.

1. Area of the basin $= 2.0 \times 0.9 = 1.8\ \text{m}^2$.
2. 1 mm of water over 1 m² is 1 L, so volume $= 1.8 \times 60 = 108\ \text{L}$.
3. Without the ties, much of this 108 L would have run off the field during the storm. Held in the basin, it soaks in and supports the crop through the next dry spell.
:::

### Supplying water

- **Watering and irrigation** replace missing rain. Water early in the morning or in the evening, when evaporation is lowest, and soak the root zone rather than wetting the surface lightly.

:::worked-solution[Watering a nursery bed]
A seedbed measures 8 m by 1.5 m and needs about 20 mm of water. The club uses 20 L watering cans.

1. Area $= 8 \times 1.5 = 12\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Volume $= 12\ \text{m}^2 \times 20\ \text{mm} = 240\ \text{L}$ (1 mm on 1 m² = 1 L).
3. Number of cans $= 240 \div 20 = 12$ cans.
:::

### Choosing suitable crops

- **Drought-tolerant crops** such as sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, cowpeas and cassava survive dry spells better than maize. **Early-maturing varieties** complete their growth before the rains end.
- Changing the **planting date** so that the most sensitive stage (for maize, tasselling and silking) falls in the wettest weeks reduces risk.

### Protecting from heat, sun and wind

- **Shading** seedlings and transplants with shade cloth, grass or branches lowers temperature and water loss until roots establish. Nurseries are often built under shade netting.
- **Windbreaks** of trees or tall grass across the prevailing wind reduce wind speed, which lowers evapotranspiration, prevents lodging and reduces wind erosion. They should be dense but not completely solid, so wind is slowed rather than forced over the top in strong eddies.

### Controlling the whole environment

- **Greenhouses** and **tunnels** of plastic or shade net protect high-value crops (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, seedlings) from frost, heavy rain, hail, wind and some pests, and allow production out of season. They are expensive and need good ventilation, because temperatures inside can rise very high.

## Measures for livestock

| Adverse condition | Measure | How it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat and strong sun | Shade trees, shelters, well-ventilated housing | Lowers heat stress, so animals keep eating and producing |
| Drought | Store hay and silage; provide water points; reduce stock numbers early | Animals have feed and water through the dry season |
| Cold and wind | Draught-free housing, bedding, heat for chicks and piglets | Young animals keep warm and survive |
| Harsh climate generally | Keep hardy indigenous breeds (for example Mashona, Tuli and Nkone cattle; indigenous goats and chickens) | Adapted to heat, drought and local diseases |

:::example{title="Planning for a dry region garden"}
A school in a dry part of Masvingo province loses its vegetables every October. The club decides to:

1. put up **shade netting** over the seedbeds (cooler, less water loss);
2. **mulch** every bed with dry grass (less evaporation, cooler soil);
3. dig compost into the beds (**manuring** raises water-holding capacity);
4. plant a **windbreak** of indigenous shrubs on the side of the prevailing wind;
5. water early in the morning, soaking the root zone.

Each step attacks a different cause of water loss, so together they let the club grow vegetables with less water.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying mulch adds water to the soil"}
Mulch does not add water. It **reduces the loss** of water by evaporation and helps rain soak in.
:::

:::mistake{title="Planting windbreaks parallel to the wind"}
Windbreaks must be **at right angles** to the prevailing wind, on the side it comes from.
:::

:::mistake{title="Watering at midday"}
Much of the water evaporates in the heat of the day. Water in the **early morning or evening**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**describe** how tied ridges are made", give the steps (ridges on the contour, cross-ties at intervals, basins between them). For "**explain** how mulching reduces the effect of drought", give the mechanism (shades the soil, reduces evaporation, keeps soil moist for longer).
:::

:::exam-tip
In a "**suggest**" question about a named condition, match each measure to the cause: drought → conserve and supply water; heat → shade; wind → windbreaks; frost → greenhouses, covers, timing. Two well-explained measures beat a list of six names.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Adverse conditions include drought, heat, wind, frost and intense rain; measures reduce their effect rather than change the weather.
- Water is conserved by mulching, potholing, tied ridging, manuring, cover crops and conservation tillage, and supplied by watering or irrigation.
- Drought-tolerant and early-maturing crops and better planting dates reduce the risk of dry spells.
- Shading, windbreaks and greenhouses protect crops from heat, wind, frost and heavy rain.
- Livestock are protected with shade, housing, stored feed, water and hardy indigenous breeds.
:::
