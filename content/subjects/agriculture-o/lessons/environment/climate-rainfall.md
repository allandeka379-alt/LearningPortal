## Introduction

In January, Tatenda's maize near Masvingo looks healthy in the morning, droops by two o'clock and recovers by evening. In February a three-week dry spell arrives and the leaves stay rolled even at dawn. In June, a clear, still night leaves the tomato seedlings in a low corner of the garden blackened by frost. All of these are effects of **climate** on a farm.

Climatic factors decide which crops and animals can be kept, when to plant, how much water is needed and which risks to prepare for. This topic appears in ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 and in Cambridge Paper 1 (how climate limits land use). Expect definitions, rainfall data to interpret and "explain" questions on wilting and frost.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Evaporation"}
The change of liquid water into water vapour from the surface of soil, dams and other open water.
:::

:::definition{term="Evapotranspiration"}
The total water lost from an area as vapour: evaporation from the soil surface plus transpiration from the plants growing on it.
:::

:::definition{term="Temporary wilting"}
Drooping of a plant when it loses water faster than its roots can absorb it, usually in the heat of the day; the plant recovers when the rate of water loss falls.
:::

:::definition{term="Permanent wilting"}
Wilting from which a plant does not recover, even when water loss falls at night, because the soil has dried to the point where roots cannot extract water; the plant dies unless it is watered.
:::

:::definition{term="Frost"}
Ice that forms when the temperature at or near the ground falls to 0 °C or below; ice crystals in plant tissues damage the cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainfall effectiveness"}
The proportion of the rain that falls which actually enters the soil and becomes available to plants, after losses by runoff and evaporation.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainfall reliability"}
How much the amount and timing of rainfall vary from one season to the next; reliable rainfall varies little.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainfall intensity"}
The amount of rain that falls in a given time, for example millimetres per hour.
:::

## The climatic factors

| Factor | Effects on crops | Effects on livestock |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wind | Spreads pollen and seeds; increases transpiration and drying; lodges (flattens) tall crops; spreads pests and diseases; causes wind erosion of bare soil | Cools animals in hot weather; cold winds chill young stock; carries dust and disease |
| Light | Needed for photosynthesis; intensity and day length affect growth and flowering; too little light gives weak, pale (etiolated) seedlings | Day length affects breeding and egg laying; long hours of light stimulate hens to lay |
| Temperature | Controls germination and growth rate; very high temperatures increase water loss and can damage flowers; low temperatures slow growth; frost kills tender tissue | Heat stress reduces feeding, growth, milk and egg production; cold increases feed needs for warmth |
| Rainfall | Main source of soil water in rain-fed farming; too little causes wilting and crop failure, too much causes waterlogging, leaching and erosion | Supplies drinking water and grass growth; long droughts cause feed and water shortages |
| Humidity | High humidity lowers transpiration but favours fungal diseases; low humidity increases water loss | High humidity with heat makes heat stress worse, because animals cannot lose heat by sweating or panting as easily |

## Evaporation, evapotranspiration and wilting

Water is lost from a field in two ways: **evaporation** from the soil surface and **transpiration** through the stomata of leaves. Together they are **evapotranspiration**. It is highest when it is **hot, sunny, windy and dry**, and lowest when it is cool, cloudy, calm and humid.

When evapotranspiration is faster than water uptake, cells lose turgor and the plant wilts:

1. **Temporary wilting** happens on hot afternoons even when the soil has water; the plant recovers in the evening. It slows photosynthesis but does little lasting harm.
2. **Permanent wilting** happens when the soil reaches its **permanent wilting point**. The plant cannot recover without water and will die.

Farmers reduce water loss by **mulching** (cuts evaporation from the soil), **windbreaks** (reduce wind speed), **weeding** (weeds also transpire), **shading** seedlings and **planting at the right time** so that the crop's critical stages fall in the wettest part of the season.

:::worked-solution[A monthly water balance]
A farm in a dry area records rainfall and estimated evapotranspiration (ET) for a maize crop.

| Month | Rainfall (mm) | ET (mm) | Rainfall − ET (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| November | 95 | 150 | −55 |
| December | 170 | 140 | +30 |
| January | 200 | 135 | +65 |
| February | 60 | 130 | −70 |
| March | 85 | 125 | −40 |

1. For each month, subtract ET from rainfall, e.g. February: $60 - 130 = -70\ \text{mm}$.
2. Negative values are **deficits**: the crop uses more water than falls, so it draws on soil water.
3. The largest deficit is in **February** ($-70\ \text{mm}$), when maize is often tasselling and silking, the stage most sensitive to water stress.
4. **Interpretation:** a mid-season dry spell in February is the main risk; mulching, water harvesting, an earlier planting date or a drought-tolerant crop would reduce it.
:::

## Frost

Frost forms on **clear, calm winter nights**, when heat radiates from the ground into a cloudless sky. Cold air is dense and drains downhill, so **valley bottoms and hollows** (frost pockets) are coldest. In Zimbabwe frost is a risk mainly on the highveld in June and July.

**Damage:** water in plant cells freezes, ice crystals rupture the cells, and leaves turn black and die. Tender crops such as tomatoes, potatoes, beans and young bananas are most at risk; cabbages, peas and other hardy winter vegetables tolerate light frost.

**Protection:**

- avoid planting tender crops in **frost hollows**; plant on slopes where cold air drains away;
- **time planting** so that tender crops grow after the frost season;
- **irrigate** the day before a frost is expected: moist soil stores and releases more heat;
- **cover** seedlings with grass, plastic or shade cloth, and **mulch** around plants;
- grow **frost-tolerant** crops in winter.

## Shade

Shade lowers light intensity and temperature. **Useful shade** protects seedlings in nurseries, reduces wilting of transplants and gives livestock relief from heat. **Too much shade** under trees or between tall crops reduces photosynthesis and gives weak, low-yielding plants, so trees near fields must be placed carefully.

## Rainfall in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has **one rainy season**, roughly November to March, followed by a long dry season from about April to October. Four features of rainfall matter to farmers:

- **Distribution:** how rain is spread **through the season** and **across the country**. Rain falls mainly in summer; amounts are higher in the north and east (especially the Eastern Highlands) and lower in the south and west. Uneven distribution within the season brings **mid-season dry spells**.
- **Effectiveness:** how much of the rain is **useful to plants**. Rain on hot, bare, crusted soil, or in heavy storms, is lost by runoff and evaporation and is less effective.
- **Reliability:** how much totals and timing **vary between seasons**. Reliability is generally **lower where rainfall is lower**, which makes dry areas doubly risky.
- **Intensity:** how **heavily** rain falls. Most rain comes in short, intense **thunderstorms**, which exceed the soil's infiltration rate, causing runoff and **erosion**.

:::worked-solution[How intensity affects effectiveness]
Two storms each drop 40 mm on the same field. Storm A falls gently over 8 hours; storm B falls in 30 minutes on bare, crusted soil.

| Storm | Rain (mm) | Runoff (mm) | Evaporation (mm) | Water entering the soil (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 40 | 6 | 4 | 30 |
| B | 40 | 22 | 2 | 16 |

1. Intensity of A $= 40 \div 8 = 5\ \text{mm per hour}$; intensity of B $= 40 \div 0.5 = 80\ \text{mm per hour}$.
2. Effective rain, A $= 40 - 6 - 4 = 30\ \text{mm}$, which is $\dfrac{30}{40} \times 100 = 75\%$ of the rain.
3. Effective rain, B $= 40 - 22 - 2 = 16\ \text{mm}$, which is $\dfrac{16}{40} \times 100 = 40\%$.
4. The intense storm gave the same total but less than half as much useful water, and its runoff carried away topsoil. Mulch and ground cover would slow runoff and raise effectiveness.
:::

## How climate limits land use

Climate decides which enterprises a region can support: cool, wet highlands suit tea, timber and dairy; warm areas with moderate, fairly reliable rain suit maize, tobacco and soya; hot, dry, unreliable areas suit livestock, wildlife and drought-tolerant small grains, or irrigated crops where water is available.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all wilting permanent"}
A plant that droops at midday and recovers by evening shows **temporary** wilting. Permanent wilting means it does **not** recover without watering.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing reliability with amount"}
Reliability is about **variation** between seasons, not the total. An area can have a high average and still be unreliable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying frost forms on cloudy nights"}
Clouds trap heat. Frost forms on **clear, calm** nights when heat escapes from the ground.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "**explain** the effect of a climatic factor", name the factor, the process and the result: "high temperature → faster evapotranspiration → soil dries → crop wilts". When given rainfall data, quote figures with units in your answer.
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the four rainfall terms as a set: **distribution** (spread), **effectiveness** (usefulness), **reliability** (variation), **intensity** (rate). Many questions ask you to define two and give an effect of each on farming.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wind, light, temperature, rainfall and humidity affect crop growth, pests, diseases and livestock performance.
- Evapotranspiration is evaporation plus transpiration; it is highest when hot, sunny, windy and dry.
- Temporary wilting recovers when water loss falls; permanent wilting does not.
- Frost forms on clear, calm nights in hollows; protect crops by siting, timing, irrigation, covers and tolerant crops.
- Zimbabwe's rain falls mainly from November to March; its distribution, effectiveness, reliability and intensity limit farming, and reliability is lowest in the driest areas.
:::
