## Introduction

At a farm near Mutare, Nyasha's father plants Irish potatoes from small tubers kept from last season, cuts sweet potato vines into short pieces for a new field, and pushes lengths of cassava stem into ridges. Down the road a citrus nursery joins buds of a good orange variety onto young rootstock seedlings. None of these farmers is using true seed. They are using **asexual reproduction**, also called **vegetative propagation**, in which a new plant grows from a part of one parent.

Many of the most important food and cash crops in southern Africa are propagated this way, including potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava, sugar cane, bananas, citrus and mangoes. In examinations you will describe methods of propagation step by step, calculate how much planting material is needed, and compare the advantages and disadvantages of sexual and asexual reproduction.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Asexual reproduction"}
Reproduction from one parent without the fusion of gametes. The offspring are genetically identical to the parent and to each other (a clone).
:::

:::definition{term="Vegetative propagation"}
Producing new plants from vegetative parts (stems, roots, leaves or buds) of a parent plant rather than from seed.
:::

:::definition{term="Scion and rootstock"}
In grafting and budding, the **scion** is the bud or shoot taken from the variety wanted for its fruit; the **rootstock** (stock) is the rooted plant onto which it is joined, chosen for vigour, disease resistance or tolerance of the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Cambium"}
A thin layer of dividing cells between the xylem and the phloem. In grafting and budding the cambium of the scion must touch that of the stock so that the two grow together.
:::

## Methods of vegetative propagation

### Stem tubers: Irish potato and yam

The Irish potato tuber is a swollen stem with **eyes** (buds). Healthy, disease-free **seed potatoes** of about egg size are stored in diffuse light until short, strong green **sprouts** form (chitting). They are planted in furrows with the sprouts upwards, and each sprout grows into a stem. Large tubers can be cut into pieces, each with at least one or two eyes, and the cut surfaces left to dry before planting. Yams are grown from tubers or pieces of tuber in the same way.

### Stem cuttings: sweet potato, cassava and sugar cane

- **Sweet potato**: pieces of vine about 30 cm long, cut from the tips of healthy plants, are planted on ridges with about two-thirds of the length, including several nodes, buried. Roots grow from the buried nodes.
- **Cassava**: mature, woody stem pieces about 20–30 cm long with several nodes are planted upright, slanting or flat in the soil. Roots grow from the base and shoots from the buds.
- **Sugar cane**: stem pieces called **setts**, each with two or three nodes, are laid in furrows and covered. Each node carries a bud that grows into a shoot, and roots form at the node.

### Runners

A runner is a stem that grows along the soil surface from the parent and forms roots and a new plant at its nodes, as in strawberries. The young plants are cut from the parent once rooted.

### Bulbs

An onion or garlic bulb is a short stem with fleshy leaves around a bud. Garlic is planted as separate **cloves**; onions can be grown from small bulbs called **sets** (although onions are more often raised from seed in a nursery).

### Layering

A low branch is bent down, wounded on the underside and pegged into the soil with its tip above ground. Roots form at the wound. When the layer has rooted it is cut from the parent and planted out. In **air layering** the wounded stem is wrapped in moist material and plastic while still on the plant.

### Budding

Budding is used for citrus and other fruit trees.

1. Raise a vigorous rootstock seedling (for example rough lemon for citrus) in the nursery.
2. Make a T-shaped cut through the bark of the stock a little above the ground.
3. Slice a single bud, with a shield of bark, from a budwood stick of the wanted variety.
4. Slide the bud under the flaps of bark so that its cambium touches the cambium of the stock.
5. Tie firmly with budding tape, leaving the bud exposed.
6. When the bud has joined and starts to grow, cut off the top of the stock above the bud.

### Grafting

Grafting joins a **scion** with several buds (a short shoot) onto a stock, as in mangoes and avocados. In a **cleft graft** the stock is cut across and split, the base of the scion is cut into a wedge and pushed into the split so that the cambium layers meet on at least one side, and the union is tied and sealed. Cleanliness, a sharp knife and good cambium contact are essential.

:::worked-solution[Sweet potato cuttings for a field]
Rudo will plant sweet potato vine cuttings on ridges 90 cm apart, with cuttings 30 cm apart along each ridge, on 0.25 ha.

1. Area for each cutting = $0.90 \times 0.30 = 0.27\ \text{m}^2$.
2. Cuttings per hectare = $10\,000 \div 0.27 = 37\,037$ (to the nearest whole cutting).
3. For 0.25 ha: $37\,037.04 \times 0.25 = 9\,259$ cuttings.
4. She should prepare some extra cuttings to replace any that fail to root.
:::

:::worked-solution[Seed potatoes needed]
Tatenda will plant Irish potatoes at 75 cm × 30 cm on 0.1 ha, using seed tubers of about 60 g each.

1. Plants per hectare = $10\,000 \div (0.75 \times 0.30) = 44\,444$.
2. Plants for 0.1 ha = $44\,444.4 \times 0.1 = 4\,444$.
3. Mass of seed = $4\,444.4 \times 60 \div 1\,000 = 267$ kg (to the nearest kg).
:::

## Comparing sexual and asexual reproduction

| | Sexual (seed) | Asexual (vegetative) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parents | Two gametes, usually from two plants | One parent |
| Offspring | Varied; differ from parents | Identical to the parent (true to type) |
| Planting material | Small, light, easy to store and transport | Bulky, heavy, perishes quickly |
| Diseases | Most viruses are not carried in seed | Viruses and other diseases pass to the new plant |
| Time to harvest | Often slower; fruit trees from seed take years to bear | Usually faster; budded trees bear earlier |
| New varieties | Possible through variation and breeding | No new varieties, except by mutation |

### Advantages of asexual reproduction in crop production

- Offspring are **true to type**: a good orange, mango or potato variety keeps its quality, yield and taste.
- Crops **mature faster**: budded and grafted trees bear fruit years earlier than seedlings; cuttings establish quickly because they use stored food.
- **Uniform** crops make management, harvesting and marketing easier.
- Crops that produce little or no viable seed (cultivated bananas, garlic), or whose seedlings vary widely (cassava, sugar cane, potatoes), can be multiplied true to type.
- A **rootstock** can add disease resistance or tolerance of a poor soil to a good fruiting variety.

### Disadvantages of asexual reproduction

- **Diseases pass on**, especially viruses (for example in potatoes, sweet potatoes and cassava), so clean, certified planting material is needed.
- **No variation**, so the whole crop is equally open to a new pest, disease or change in climate.
- Planting material is **bulky**, costly to transport and cannot be stored for long.
- Plants may become **overcrowded** around the parent and compete with each other.
- Budding and grafting need skill.

### Advantages and disadvantages of sexual reproduction

Seed gives **variation**, which plant breeders use to produce new, improved varieties, and seed is easy to store and transport and is usually free of viruses. On the other hand the offspring of cross-pollinated crops are **not true to type**, growth to maturity is slower, and seed of some crops has low viability or dormancy.

:::example{title="Choosing a method for a new citrus orchard"}
Farai wants an orchard of a sweet orange variety on a farm where the soil has a root disease problem. Seedlings grown from orange pips would vary, many would not resemble the parent, and they would take years longer to bear. By buying trees budded onto a rootstock that tolerates the root disease, he gets uniform trees that are true to type and bear earlier, while the rootstock protects the roots. He should buy from a certified nursery so the budwood is free of virus diseases.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking clones resist disease better"}
Clones carry the parent's diseases and are all equally susceptible. Clean planting material is essential.
:::

:::mistake{title="Joining bark instead of cambium"}
A graft or bud only takes when the **cambium** of scion and stock touch; joining bark to bark fails.
:::

:::mistake{title="Planting sweet potato roots"}
Sweet potatoes are normally planted from **vine (stem) cuttings**, not from the storage roots, which are eaten or sold.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Asexual reproduction uses one parent and gives identical offspring (clones).
- Methods: stem tubers (Irish potato, yam), stem cuttings (sweet potato, cassava, sugar cane setts), runners, bulbs and cloves, layering, budding and grafting (cambium to cambium).
- Asexual: true to type, uniform, faster, uses rootstocks; but spreads disease, no variation, bulky material.
- Sexual: variation for breeding, light and easily stored seed, few viruses; but variable offspring and slower growth.
:::
