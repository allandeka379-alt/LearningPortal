## Introduction

In January, Chipo walks through her maize field near Marondera. At the top of each plant the tassels are shedding clouds of yellow pollen, and lower down, from the tip of each young cob, a bunch of fine silks is hanging out. In the next field her sugar beans are flowering with small white and purple flowers. Within weeks the silks will dry and turn brown and the bean flowers will drop their petals, leaving young pods. Every grain and every bean she harvests depends on what happens at flowering: **pollination** and **fertilisation**.

Flowering is also when crops are most sensitive. A drought or heatwave at tasselling and silking, or poor pollination, can leave cobs with many missing grains. Weeds that are allowed to flower and set seed spread thousands of seeds over the field. In examinations you will describe maize and bean flowers part by part, compare self- and cross-pollination, describe fertilisation and seed formation and explain how dispersal affects weed control.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sexual reproduction"}
Reproduction in which a male gamete and a female gamete fuse (fertilisation) to form a zygote, which grows into a new individual. The offspring differ from their parents and from each other.
:::

:::definition{term="Pollination"}
The transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-pollination"}
The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-pollination"}
The transfer of pollen from an anther of one plant to a stigma of a different plant of the same kind.
:::

:::definition{term="Fertilisation"}
The fusion of a male gamete nucleus (from the pollen) with a female gamete (egg cell) nucleus in the ovule to form a zygote.
:::

## The maize flower

Maize is **monoecious**: one plant carries separate male and female flowers.

- **Tassel (male inflorescence)** at the top of the stem. It is a branched head of many small flowers (spikelets). Each male flower has three **stamens**; each stamen has a long **filament** that lets the **anther** hang out in the wind. The anthers release huge numbers of light, dry, smooth pollen grains.
- **Ear or cob (female inflorescence)** in the axil of a leaf about halfway up the stem, wrapped in **husk leaves**. Along the cob are rows of **ovaries**, each containing one ovule. From each ovary a long thread, the **silk** (style and stigma), grows out of the tip of the husks. The silk is long and sticky or feathery to catch pollen from the air. Each silk that receives pollen can lead to one grain.

Maize has no showy petals, scent or nectar, because it is **wind-pollinated** and does not need to attract insects.

## The bean flower

The bean flower is **bisexual** (hermaphrodite): each flower has both male and female parts.

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calyx (sepals) | Small green leaf-like parts, joined at the base | Protect the flower in the bud |
| Standard | The large upright back petal | Attracts insects |
| Wings | Two side petals | Attract insects; give a landing place |
| Keel | Two lower petals joined to form a boat shape | Encloses and protects the stamens and pistil |
| Stamens (10) | Nine filaments joined into a tube and one free, each with an anther | Produce pollen (male gametes) |
| Pistil (carpel) | Stigma, style and a long ovary with a row of ovules | Receives pollen; the ovules become seeds and the ovary the pod |

Because the anthers and stigma are enclosed together in the keel, the anthers shed pollen onto the stigma of the same flower, often before the flower opens. Beans are therefore mainly **self-pollinated**, although visiting bees may carry some pollen between plants.

## Self- and cross-pollination in maize and bean

| Feature | Maize | Bean |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flowers | Separate male (tassel) and female (silk) on one plant | Bisexual flowers |
| Main type of pollination | Cross-pollination by wind | Self-pollination within the keel |
| Why | Tassels usually shed pollen a few days before the silks of the same plant are ready (protandry); pollen blows between plants | Stamens and stigma are enclosed together in the keel |
| Effect for the farmer | Varieties mix if grown close together; hybrids and seed fields need isolation | Varieties stay true to type; farmers can save seed of open varieties |

### Features of wind- and insect-pollinated flowers

| Wind-pollinated (maize, sorghum, wheat) | Insect-pollinated (bean, pumpkin, sunflower) |
| --- | --- |
| Small, dull flowers without nectar or scent | Large, brightly coloured petals, often scented with nectar |
| Anthers hang outside on long filaments | Anthers inside the flower |
| Large amounts of light, smooth pollen | Less pollen, sticky or rough to cling to insects |
| Long feathery or sticky stigmas outside the flower | Small sticky stigmas inside the flower |

## Fertilisation, seed and fruit formation

1. A pollen grain lands on a ripe stigma (in maize, on a silk).
2. The pollen grain absorbs sugary fluid and grows a **pollen tube**, which grows down through the style (the whole length of the silk in maize).
3. The tube enters the ovule through a small pore, the **micropyle**.
4. A **male nucleus** fuses with the **egg cell nucleus**: this is fertilisation, forming a **zygote**. A second male nucleus fuses with nuclei in the centre of the ovule to form the **endosperm**, a food store.
5. The zygote divides to form the **embryo** (plumule, radicle and cotyledons).

After fertilisation:

- the **ovule** becomes the **seed**, and its outer layers (integuments) become the **testa** (seed coat);
- the **ovary** becomes the **fruit**, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (**pericarp**): in beans, the pod;
- the petals, stamens and stigma wither and fall.

A maize grain is really a one-seeded fruit, because the ovary wall is fused to the seed coat.

:::worked-solution[Grain set on a maize cob]
Tendai counts a cob from a field that suffered a dry spell at silking. It has 16 rows, each with room for 40 grains, but only 576 grains have formed.

1. Number of ovules = $16 \times 40 = 640$.
2. Percentage of ovules that became grains = $\frac{576}{640} \times 100 = 90\%$.
3. Unfilled positions = $640 - 576 = 64$. Each gap is an ovule that was not fertilised, often because the silks dried out or emerged after the pollen had been shed.
:::

## Dispersal of seeds and fruits

Dispersal spreads seeds away from the parent so that seedlings do not compete with it or with each other, and the species can colonise new places.

| Method | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wind | Light seeds, with wings or hairs | Winged seeds of jacaranda; hairy seeds of cotton and of many weeds |
| Animals (hooks) | Hooks, barbs or bristles that catch on fur and clothing | Blackjack (*Bidens*), burrs of many weeds |
| Animals (eaten) | Fleshy, coloured fruits; seeds pass through the gut unharmed | Tomato, guava, wild fruits; weed seeds in manure |
| Self (explosive) | Pods dry and split suddenly, flinging seeds out | Beans, soya beans, cowpeas |
| Water | Fruits that float | Seeds of water weeds carried by irrigation water and floods |

### Importance in weed spread and control

Many weeds are successful because they produce **very large numbers of seeds** that are **dispersed widely** and can lie **dormant** in the soil for years. A single weed that is allowed to set seed adds to the "seed bank" for many seasons.

:::example{title="Planning weed control around dispersal"}
Sipho's field is full of blackjack. Each plant's barbed fruits cling to clothing, animals' coats and bags, so they spread to clean fields too. He decides to: weed before the blackjack flowers so no seed is set; remove and burn any plants that have already set seed; brush down clothes, animals and implements before entering clean fields; use only certified, clean crop seed; and use well-rotted manure, because weed seeds eaten by cattle can survive the gut and grow from fresh manure. Over several seasons the seed bank in the soil will fall. If even 5% of the seeds from one plant carrying 2,400 seeds produced seedlings, that one plant would leave $2400 \times 0.05 = 120$ new weeds.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing pollination with fertilisation"}
Pollination is the **transfer of pollen** to the stigma. Fertilisation is the **fusion of nuclei** inside the ovule, which happens later.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the silk a flower"}
Each silk is the **style and stigma** of one female flower; the ovary is on the cob.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the ovary becomes the seed"}
The **ovule** becomes the seed; the **ovary** becomes the fruit.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Maize has male tassels and female ears with silks; it is wind- and mainly cross-pollinated.
- The bean flower is bisexual, with standard, wings and keel enclosing 10 stamens and the pistil; it is mainly self-pollinated.
- Pollination is the transfer of pollen; fertilisation is the fusion of a male nucleus with the egg nucleus after a pollen tube grows to the ovule.
- The ovule becomes the seed and the ovary the fruit.
- Seeds and fruits are dispersed by wind, animals, explosion and water; weeds spread by dispersal, so weed before they seed and keep seed, implements and manure clean.
:::
