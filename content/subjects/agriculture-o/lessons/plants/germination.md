## Introduction

Every November, Tendai's family near Chinhoyi watches the sky and waits for the first good rains before planting maize. Planting into dry soil wastes seed; planting into cold, waterlogged soil makes it rot. When the seed has been saved from last season, Tendai first tests a sample on wet newspaper to see how many seeds will grow. The result tells the family whether the seed is worth planting and how much extra to sow.

A good stand of seedlings is the first step to a good yield, and gaps in the rows cannot be made up later. In the examination you will label maize and bean seeds from a description, state and explain the conditions for germination, describe experiments that show them, tell germination from emergence, compare epigeal and hypogeal germination and calculate percentage germination and seed rates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Germination"}
The process in which the embryo of a seed resumes growth and develops into a seedling. It starts when the seed takes up water and is usually counted from the moment the radicle breaks through the seed coat.
:::

:::definition{term="Emergence"}
The appearance of the shoot (plumule) of the seedling above the soil surface. Emergence comes after germination.
:::

:::definition{term="Epigeal germination"}
Germination in which the cotyledons are carried above the soil surface, because the part of the stem below them (the hypocotyl) lengthens. The common bean germinates in this way.
:::

:::definition{term="Hypogeal germination"}
Germination in which the cotyledon(s) stay below the soil surface, because the part of the stem above them (the epicotyl, or the mesocotyl in maize) lengthens. Maize germinates in this way.
:::

:::definition{term="Percentage germination"}
The number of seeds that germinate in a test, divided by the number of seeds tested, multiplied by 100.
:::

## Structure of a bean seed

Externally a bean seed has a tough seed coat, the **testa**, which protects the embryo. On the concave side is the **hilum**, the scar where the seed was attached to the pod wall, and next to it a tiny pore, the **micropyle**, through which water enters first.

Inside the testa is the **embryo**:

- two large **cotyledons** (seed leaves), which store the food (starch and protein) for germination;
- the **radicle**, the embryonic root, which grows out first;
- the **plumule**, the embryonic shoot with tiny first leaves, lying between the cotyledons.

A bean seed has **no endosperm**: the cotyledons have absorbed the food store.

## Structure of a maize grain

A maize grain is a one-seeded fruit. Its outer layer is the **fruit wall (pericarp) fused with the testa**. Most of the grain is the **endosperm**, a starchy food store. On one flat face lies the **embryo**:

- one **cotyledon** (the **scutellum**), a shield-shaped structure pressed against the endosperm, which absorbs digested food from it and passes it to the growing embryo;
- the **plumule**, protected by a sheath called the **coleoptile**;
- the **radicle**, protected by a sheath called the **coleorhiza**.

| Feature | Bean seed (dicotyledon) | Maize grain (monocotyledon) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outer covering | Testa only | Pericarp fused with testa |
| Cotyledons | Two, large, store food | One (scutellum), absorbs food |
| Endosperm | Absent | Large, starchy |
| Protective sheaths | None | Coleoptile over plumule, coleorhiza over radicle |
| Type of germination | Epigeal | Hypogeal |

## Conditions for germination

A seed needs three conditions, plus a living embryo that is not dormant:

1. **Water**. The seed absorbs water by imbibition, then osmosis. Water softens the testa, makes the seed swell and burst the coat, activates **enzymes** that digest the stored starch and protein into soluble sugars and amino acids, and carries these to the growing embryo.
2. **Oxygen**. The embryo respires rapidly to release energy for growth, and **aerobic respiration** needs oxygen. Seeds in waterlogged or compacted soil, or planted too deep, may die.
3. **A suitable temperature**. Germination is controlled by enzymes, which work slowly in the cold and are damaged at very high temperatures. Each crop has a range in which it germinates well; maize, for example, germinates slowly in cold soil.

Most crop seeds do **not** need light to germinate, because they are buried and live on stored food until the shoot reaches the light.

### Experiments to show the conditions

Set up four test tubes, each with a few soaked bean or maize seeds on cotton wool:

| Tube | Conditions | Result | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Moist cotton wool, air, room temperature | Germinate | Control |
| B | Dry cotton wool, air, room temperature | No germination | Water is needed |
| C | Seeds under boiled (oxygen-free) water with a layer of oil on top, room temperature | No germination | Oxygen is needed |
| D | Moist cotton wool, air, in a refrigerator | No germination (or very slow) | A suitable temperature is needed |

In a fair test only one condition differs from the control in each tube. Instead of boiled water, tube C can contain moist cotton wool and a chemical that absorbs oxygen, such as alkaline pyrogallol, in a sealed tube.

## Germination and emergence

Germination happens inside the soil and is complete when the radicle and plumule are growing from the seed. **Emergence** is when the shoot appears above ground. A seed lot may germinate well in a test but emerge poorly in the field because of a hard surface cap, deep planting, cold or waterlogged soil, soil pests or fungal rots. Good seedbed preparation, planting at the right depth into moist soil and seed dressing improve emergence.

## Epigeal and hypogeal germination

- In the **bean (epigeal)** the hypocotyl grows into a hook that pushes up through the soil and then straightens, pulling the cotyledons above ground, where they turn green for a short time before shrivelling. The hook protects the plumule from damage.
- In **maize (hypogeal)** the grain stays below ground. The coleoptile, a pointed sheath, pushes straight up through the soil and protects the plumule; when it reaches the light the first leaves break out of it. The radicle is soon followed by other roots that form the fibrous root system.

Because the bean must drag its large cotyledons through the soil, a crusted soil surface harms bean emergence more than maize emergence.

## Germination tests and seed rates

A simple **rag-doll** or **tray test**: count out 100 seeds (or four lots of 25), place them on moist newspaper or cloth, roll it up or cover the tray, keep it moist and warm, and count the seeds that have produced a normal radicle and plumule after about 7 days.

:::worked-solution[Percentage germination]
Tendai tests four lots of 25 maize seeds. The numbers that germinate are 22, 21, 23 and 19.

1. Seeds tested = $4 \times 25 = 100$.
2. Seeds germinated = $22 + 21 + 23 + 19 = 85$.
3. Percentage germination = $\frac{85}{100} \times 100 = 85\%$.
4. Good seed usually germinates at about 80–90% or more, so this lot is acceptable, but extra seed is needed to make up for the 15% that fail.
:::

:::worked-solution[Adjusting the seed rate]
For example, maize is planted at 75 cm × 30 cm with one plant per station. The mass of 1,000 seeds is 320 g and the germination is 85%.

1. Plant population = $10\,000 \div (0.75 \times 0.30) = 44\,444$ plants/ha.
2. Seeds needed = $44\,444 \div 0.85 = 52\,288$ seeds/ha.
3. Mass = $52\,288 \times 320 \div 1\,000 = 16\,732$ g = **16.7 kg/ha** (to one decimal place).
4. With 100% germination it would be 14.2 kg/ha. In practice farmers often sow more, about 25 kg/ha, to allow for losses to soil pests, birds and poor emergence.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying seeds need light or soil to germinate"}
The conditions are **water, oxygen and a suitable temperature**. Seeds germinate on wet cotton wool without soil, and most germinate in the dark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up epigeal and hypogeal"}
Epi- means "above": the cotyledons come **above** the ground (bean). Hypo- means "below": they stay **below** (maize).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using germination and emergence as the same word"}
Germination happens in the soil; emergence is the shoot appearing above the soil.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain each condition"}
Do not just list water, oxygen and temperature: *explain* each one (water activates enzymes and softens the testa; oxygen for respiration to release energy; temperature for enzyme activity).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Percentage germination = germinated ÷ tested × 100. To correct a seed rate, divide the seeds needed by the germination as a decimal, not multiply.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bean seed: testa, hilum, micropyle, two food-storing cotyledons, plumule, radicle, no endosperm.
- Maize grain: pericarp fused with testa, starchy endosperm, scutellum, plumule in a coleoptile, radicle in a coleorhiza.
- Germination needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature (and a viable, non-dormant embryo).
- Bean germination is epigeal; maize germination is hypogeal. Emergence is the shoot appearing above the soil.
- Percentage germination = germinated ÷ tested × 100; low germination means a higher seed rate.
:::
