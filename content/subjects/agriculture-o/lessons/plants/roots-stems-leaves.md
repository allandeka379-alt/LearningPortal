## Introduction

Farai uproots a young bean plant and a young maize plant from his family's field near Gokwe and lays them side by side. The bean has one thick root going straight down with thinner roots branching from it; the maize has a mass of thin roots of similar size. Both have stems, leaves and buds, and both will later carry flowers. Each part has a job, and inside each part different **tissues** do different work: some carry water up, some carry food, some make food and some protect the plant.

Knowing the structure of roots, stems and leaves helps a farmer understand why transplanted seedlings wilt when their root hairs are damaged, why ring-barking a tree kills it, why stalk borers that tunnel into maize stems reduce yield, and why leaves need plenty of light, water and carbon dioxide. In examinations you will be asked to name parts and tissues from a description, state their functions and explain how a leaf is suited to photosynthesis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tissue"}
A group of similar cells that work together to carry out one function, for example xylem, which carries water.
:::

:::definition{term="Xylem"}
The tissue that carries water and dissolved mineral salts upwards from the roots to the stem and leaves. Its vessels are dead, hollow tubes with strong walls that also help to support the plant.
:::

:::definition{term="Phloem"}
The tissue that carries dissolved food made in the leaves (mainly sugar) to other parts of the plant, such as growing points, roots, fruits and storage organs.
:::

:::definition{term="Root hair"}
A long, thin outgrowth of a single cell of the root's outer layer, found just behind the root tip. Root hairs give a very large surface area for absorbing water and mineral salts.
:::

:::definition{term="Stoma (plural stomata)"}
A small pore in the epidermis of a leaf, surrounded by two guard cells that open and close it. Gases are exchanged and water vapour is lost through the stomata.
:::

## External parts of a flowering plant

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Roots | Anchor the plant; absorb water and mineral salts; some store food (carrot, cassava) |
| Stem | Holds leaves up to the light and flowers up for pollination; carries water and food between roots and leaves; some store food (sugar cane) |
| Leaves | Make food by photosynthesis; exchange gases; lose water vapour in transpiration |
| Buds | Grow into new shoots, leaves or flowers; the terminal bud makes the stem grow longer |
| Nodes and internodes | Nodes are where leaves and buds join the stem; internodes are the stem lengths between them |
| Flowers | Carry out sexual reproduction to form seeds and fruits |
| Fruits and seeds | Protect and disperse the seeds; seeds grow into new plants |

Dicotyledons such as beans have a **tap root**: one main root with lateral roots branching from it, reaching deep water. Monocotyledons such as maize have **fibrous roots** of similar size near the surface, which hold soil well. Maize also forms **prop (brace) roots** from the lower nodes, which help to hold the tall stem upright.

## Internal structure of a root

Imagine cutting across a young dicotyledonous root and looking at the cut surface from the outside inwards:

1. **Epidermis with root hairs**: one layer of cells on the outside. Behind the tip many of these cells grow root hairs, which push between soil particles and absorb water and mineral salts.
2. **Cortex**: a wide zone of large, thin-walled cells. Water passes across it to the centre; in storage roots the cortex is packed with starch.
3. **Endodermis**: a single ring of cells around the centre that controls what enters the vascular tissue.
4. **Vascular tissue in the centre**: the xylem forms a star shape in the middle, with phloem lying in the spaces between the arms of the star.

The central position of the vascular tissue makes the root strong against pulling, like a rope, which suits a part that anchors the plant. The **root cap** covers and protects the tip as it pushes through the soil.

:::example{title="Why transplants wilt"}
Chipo pulls tomato seedlings out of a dry nursery bed by hand and plants them in the garden on a hot afternoon. Next day they are wilting. Pulling the seedlings from dry soil tore off most of the fine roots and root hairs, which do almost all of the absorbing. The leaves went on losing water but the damaged roots could not replace it. She should water the nursery bed well before lifting, lift the seedlings with soil around the roots, transplant in the late afternoon or on a cloudy day, water at once and shade the plants for a few days while new root hairs grow.
:::

## Internal structure of a dicotyledonous stem

From the outside inwards, a young bean or sunflower stem has:

1. **Epidermis**: a protective outer layer, often with a waxy cuticle.
2. **Cortex**: several layers of cells; the outer ones may be green and photosynthesise, and some have thickened walls for support.
3. **Vascular bundles arranged in a ring**: each bundle has **phloem on the outside** and **xylem on the inside**, with a thin layer of **cambium** between them. Cambium cells divide to make new xylem and phloem, so the stem grows thicker.
4. **Pith**: soft cells in the centre that store food and water.

The ring of bundles near the outside makes the stem stiff against bending by wind. In a monocotyledon such as maize the bundles are **scattered** through the stem instead of forming a ring.

Because the phloem lies on the outside of the xylem, removing a ring of bark from a tree trunk removes the phloem but leaves the xylem. This is the basis of the ring-barking experiment on translocation.

## Internal structure of a leaf

A leaf is thin and flat. Its parts, from the top surface to the bottom, are:

| Layer or part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waxy cuticle | A thin waterproof layer on the outer surface | Reduces water loss; transparent, so light passes through |
| Upper epidermis | One layer of transparent cells | Protects the leaf and lets light through |
| Palisade mesophyll | Long cells packed closely side by side, full of chloroplasts | Main site of photosynthesis, near the light |
| Spongy mesophyll | Rounded cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces | Gas exchange: carbon dioxide diffuses to the cells and oxygen and water vapour away |
| Vein (vascular bundle) | Xylem on the upper side, phloem on the lower side | Xylem brings water and salts; phloem takes sugar away; veins also support the blade |
| Lower epidermis | A layer of cells with many stomata | Protection |
| Stomata and guard cells | Pores, each between two guard cells | Let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapour out; close to save water |

In most dicotyledons the stomata are mainly on the lower surface, where it is cooler and shaded, so less water is lost.

![Cross-section of a dicotyledonous leaf from top to bottom: waxy cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll full of chloroplasts, spongy mesophyll with air spaces and a vein with xylem above phloem, then the lower epidermis with a stoma between two guard cells that lets carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapour out.](media:diagrams/leaf-cross-section.png "Figure: a section through a leaf, from the waxy cuticle to the lower epidermis")

### How leaf structure suits photosynthesis

- A **broad, flat blade** gives a large surface area to absorb light.
- The leaf is **thin**, so carbon dioxide has only a short distance to diffuse to the cells.
- The **palisade cells** are packed with chloroplasts and lie just below the transparent upper surface, where light is strongest.
- **Air spaces** in the spongy layer let gases move freely to and from the cells.
- **Stomata** let carbon dioxide in and oxygen out, and guard cells close them when water is short.
- A **network of veins** brings water to every cell and carries the sugar away.

:::worked-solution[Counting stomata]
Thandeka paints nail varnish on the lower surface of a bean leaf, peels it off and counts stomata in five fields of view of a microscope: 32, 28, 35, 30 and 30.

1. Total = $32 + 28 + 35 + 30 + 30 = 155$.
2. Mean = $155 \div 5 = 31$ stomata per field of view.
3. The upper surface of the same leaf gives a mean of 9. The lower surface has far more stomata, which reduces water loss because it is shaded and cooler.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Swapping xylem and phloem"}
Xylem carries **water and mineral salts up**; phloem carries **food (sugar)** in both directions. In a stem bundle, phloem is on the **outside**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying root hairs are separate roots"}
A root hair is part of **one epidermal cell**, not a branch root. It lives only a short time and is easily torn off when a plant is pulled up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the palisade layer at the bottom"}
The palisade mesophyll is **just below the upper epidermis**, nearest the light. The spongy layer with air spaces is below it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Name* or *identify* a tissue from its description (for example "long cells full of chloroplasts" is palisade mesophyll). *State the function* in a few words. *Explain how a leaf is adapted* by giving a feature **and** what it does for photosynthesis, for example "thin, so carbon dioxide diffuses a short distance to the palisade cells".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing a diagram in words"}
If you are asked to describe a section, go in order from the outside inwards (root and stem) or from the top surface down (leaf), naming each tissue and its job.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Roots anchor and absorb; stems support and transport; leaves make food; flowers reproduce.
- Root (outside inwards): epidermis with root hairs, cortex, endodermis, central star of xylem with phloem between the arms.
- Dicotyledonous stem: epidermis, cortex, vascular bundles in a ring (phloem outside, cambium, xylem inside), pith.
- Leaf: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll with air spaces, veins, lower epidermis with stomata.
- A leaf is broad, thin, rich in chloroplasts near the top, full of air spaces and veins, and has stomata: all suit photosynthesis.
:::
