## Introduction

Every bag of maize that leaves a farm near Chinhoyi for the Grain Marketing Board began as carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. The maize leaves trapped sunlight and used its energy to join these simple substances into sugar, which the plant then turned into starch in the grain, cellulose in the stalks and protein in the seed. This process is **photosynthesis**, and it is the source of almost all the food that crops and livestock produce.

A farmer cannot control the sun, but many farm practices, such as correct plant spacing, weeding, irrigation, fertilising and growing crops in tunnels, work by helping the crop photosynthesise faster. In the examination you must state the word and chemical equations, describe experiments that show what photosynthesis needs, explain the factors that affect its rate and apply them to crop production.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Photosynthesis"}
The process by which green plants use light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll, to make carbohydrates (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen as a by-product.
:::

:::definition{term="Chlorophyll"}
The green pigment, found in chloroplasts, that absorbs light energy for photosynthesis.
:::

:::definition{term="Limiting factor"}
The factor that is in shortest supply and therefore controls the rate of a process, such as light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature in photosynthesis.
:::

:::definition{term="Destarching"}
Keeping a plant in complete darkness for about two days so that it uses up the starch in its leaves before an experiment.
:::

## The equations

The **word equation** is:

$$
\text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} \xrightarrow[\text{chlorophyll}]{\text{light}} \text{glucose} + \text{oxygen}
$$

The **balanced chemical equation** is:

$$
\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O ->[light][chlorophyll] C6H12O6 + 6O2}
$$

Six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water make one molecule of glucose and six of oxygen. Light and chlorophyll are written over and under the arrow because they are needed but are not raw materials: light provides the energy and chlorophyll absorbs it.

### What happens to the glucose

- It is used in **respiration** to release energy for the plant's life processes.
- It is changed to **starch** for storage in leaves, seeds and tubers; starch is insoluble, so it does not affect osmosis.
- It is changed to **sucrose** for transport in the phloem.
- It is used to make **cellulose** for cell walls.
- Combined with nitrogen from nitrates it forms **amino acids and proteins**; it is also used to make **oils** in seeds such as groundnuts and soya beans.

## Where photosynthesis happens and gas exchange

Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the **palisade mesophyll** of the leaf, in the chloroplasts. The raw materials arrive by two routes: **water** comes from the soil through the xylem, and **carbon dioxide** enters from the air through the **stomata**, diffusing through the air spaces of the spongy layer to the cells. **Oxygen** diffuses out the same way.

In bright light a leaf photosynthesises faster than it respires, so overall it takes in carbon dioxide and gives out oxygen. In darkness only respiration continues, so the leaf takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide.

## Experiments on photosynthesis

All three classic experiments use the **starch test** on a leaf, because glucose made in photosynthesis is quickly stored as starch.

### The starch test

1. Boil the leaf in water for about a minute to kill it and break the cell membranes.
2. Turn off any flame. Place the leaf in a test tube of ethanol standing in the hot water; the ethanol removes the chlorophyll. **Ethanol is flammable: never heat it over a naked flame.**
3. Dip the leaf in hot water to soften it.
4. Spread the leaf on a white tile and add iodine solution. Parts that contain starch turn **blue-black**; parts without starch stay **brown** (the colour of the iodine).

### Showing that light is needed

Destarch a potted plant, then cover part of one leaf on both sides with black paper or aluminium foil. Leave the plant in sunlight for several hours and test the leaf for starch. Only the uncovered part turns blue-black, so light is needed to make starch.

### Showing that chlorophyll is needed

Use a **variegated** leaf, which has green and white (or yellow) areas. Draw the pattern before testing. After a few hours in light, only the parts that were green turn blue-black; the white parts, which lack chlorophyll, stay brown.

### Showing that carbon dioxide is needed

Destarch two plants. Enclose one leaf (or plant) in a clear flask or bag containing **soda lime** or **potassium hydroxide solution**, which absorbs carbon dioxide. Enclose a similar leaf in a flask without the absorber (or with water) as the **control**. After some hours in light, test both leaves. The leaf without carbon dioxide stays brown; the control turns blue-black.

### Showing that oxygen is produced

Place pondweed under a funnel in a beaker of water, with an upturned test tube full of water over the funnel stem. In light, bubbles of gas collect. A **glowing splint relights** in the gas, showing that it is rich in oxygen.

## Factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis

| Factor | Effect | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light intensity | Rate rises as light increases, then levels off | Light supplies the energy; once something else is limiting, more light does not help |
| Carbon dioxide concentration | Rate rises as carbon dioxide increases, then levels off | Carbon dioxide is a raw material |
| Temperature | Rate rises to an optimum, then falls sharply | Photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes, which work faster when warmer but are damaged at high temperatures |
| Water | Shortage reduces the rate | Stomata close to save water, so carbon dioxide cannot enter |
| Chlorophyll (leaf health) | Less chlorophyll lowers the rate | Yellow leaves from nitrogen or magnesium shortage, disease or pests absorb less light |

At any moment the rate is controlled by whichever factor is in shortest supply: the **limiting factor**. On a bright, warm afternoon in a closed tunnel, carbon dioxide is often limiting; at dawn, light is limiting.

:::worked-solution[Light intensity and the rate of photosynthesis]
Nyasha places pondweed at different distances from a lamp and counts the oxygen bubbles given off in 2 minutes.

| Distance of lamp (cm) | Bubbles in 2 min | Bubbles per min |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 64 | 32 |
| 20 | 40 | 20 |
| 30 | 22 | 11 |
| 40 | 12 | 6 |

1. Rate = bubbles ÷ time; at 10 cm, $64 \div 2 = 32$ bubbles per minute.
2. As the lamp moves away, light intensity falls and so does the rate.
3. From 10 cm to 40 cm the number of bubbles falls from 64 to 12, a decrease of $\frac{64 - 12}{64} \times 100 = 81.3\%$.
4. Conclusion: in this range light intensity is the limiting factor. A beaker of water between the lamp and the plant keeps the temperature constant, so that only light changes.
:::

## Using photosynthesis in crop production

- **Correct spacing and plant population** so leaves are not shaded by neighbours, while the ground is still covered to capture most of the light.
- **Weed control**, because weeds shade the crop and compete for water and nutrients.
- **Irrigation**, so stomata stay open and carbon dioxide can enter.
- **Fertilisers**: nitrogen and magnesium are needed to make chlorophyll; pest and disease control keeps the leaf area healthy.
- **Planting at the right time**, so the crop has its full leaf area during the sunniest, warmest part of the season.
- **Greenhouses and tunnels** keep temperatures near the optimum; in large commercial greenhouses extra carbon dioxide and lighting may be supplied.

:::example{title="Diagnosing a slow-growing tunnel crop"}
Thandeka grows tomatoes in a closed plastic tunnel near Harare. On sunny mornings the plants grow well, but by midday, with the tunnel closed, growth slows even though it is bright and warm. The plants have used up much of the carbon dioxide in the still air of the closed tunnel, so carbon dioxide has become the limiting factor; later, the temperature may also rise above the optimum. Opening the tunnel sides lets fresh air in, restoring carbon dioxide and cooling the crop, and keeping the soil moist lets the stomata stay open.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling light a raw material"}
Light is the **energy source**; the raw materials are carbon dioxide and water. That is why light is written over the arrow, not on the left of the equation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Testing a leaf for glucose with iodine"}
Iodine tests for **starch**. The glucose made in photosynthesis is stored as starch, which is why the starch test is used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to destarch"}
Without destarching, a leaf may already contain starch, and the experiment proves nothing. Always say the plant was kept in the dark for about two days first.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Equations"}
Learn the balanced equation exactly: $\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}$, with light and chlorophyll over and under the arrow. Marks are lost for unbalanced equations or for putting light among the reactants.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing an experiment"}
Give numbered steps: destarch, set up the test and the **control**, leave in light, starch test, results and conclusion. Name the safety point about ethanol. *Explain* a factor in terms of limiting factors and enzymes; *suggest* a farm practice by linking it to a factor.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll.
- Balanced: $\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}$.
- Carbon dioxide enters and oxygen leaves through the stomata; water arrives in the xylem.
- Destarched leaves and the iodine starch test show that light, chlorophyll and carbon dioxide are needed; pondweed shows oxygen is given off.
- Light, carbon dioxide, temperature and water limit the rate; spacing, weeding, irrigation, fertilisers and tunnels raise it.
:::
