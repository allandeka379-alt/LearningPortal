## Introduction

On a hot, windy October afternoon in Masvingo, Sipho notices that the leaves of his irrigated cabbages are drooping, although the soil was watered that morning. By evening, when the wind has dropped and the air is cooler, the leaves are firm again. Behind this everyday sight are the processes in this lesson: water enters the roots by osmosis, travels up the xylem and evaporates from the leaves faster than it can be replaced when the weather is hot, dry and windy.

A maize crop moves a very large amount of water from the soil to the air during a season. Understanding how water and mineral salts are taken up and lost explains irrigation scheduling, why fertiliser placed too close to seed can "burn" it, why seedlings are transplanted in the evening and why windbreaks help crops. In examinations you will define the processes, describe the pathway of water, interpret potometer or weighing results and explain each factor that affects transpiration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diffusion"}
The net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, without the use of energy from the cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Osmosis"}
The movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (high water concentration) to a more concentrated solution (low water concentration) through a partially permeable membrane.
:::

:::definition{term="Active uptake (active transport)"}
The movement of ions into a cell against a concentration gradient, from a lower to a higher concentration, using energy released by respiration.
:::

:::definition{term="Imbibition"}
The uptake of water by dry materials such as the cell walls, starch and proteins of a seed, which swell as they absorb it. It is the first stage of water uptake in a germinating seed.
:::

:::definition{term="Transpiration"}
The loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly by evaporation from the mesophyll cells and diffusion of water vapour out through the stomata.
:::

:::definition{term="Wilting"}
The drooping of leaves and soft stems when cells lose water faster than it is replaced and so lose their turgor (firmness).
:::

## Absorption of water by root hairs

Root hairs are in close contact with the thin film of water around soil particles. The **soil water is a dilute solution**, while the **cell sap of a root hair is more concentrated**. The cell membrane is partially permeable, so water moves **into the root hair by osmosis**. The root hair cell now has more water than the cell next to it, so water passes on across the cortex from cell to cell by osmosis (and along cell walls) until it reaches the **xylem** in the centre of the root.

Root hairs make this efficient because they give a **very large surface area**, are **thin-walled** and grow between soil particles where the water is.

:::example{title="Why too much fertiliser near the seed causes damage"}
Rudo places a handful of fertiliser right against her maize seedlings. Around the roots the soil solution becomes **more concentrated** than the cell sap. Water now moves **out of** the root hairs by osmosis, the seedlings wilt and their leaves look scorched, even though the soil is moist. This is called fertiliser "burn". Banding the fertiliser a few centimetres away from the seed and applying top dressing beside the row avoids it.
:::

## Uptake of mineral salts

Plants need mineral salts such as nitrate, phosphate and potassium ions. These are usually at a **lower concentration in the soil water than inside the root cells**, so diffusion alone would move them the wrong way. Root cells take them in by **active uptake**, using energy from respiration. This is why roots need **oxygen**: in a waterlogged soil the roots cannot respire properly, mineral uptake falls and crops turn yellow. Some ions also enter by diffusion when their concentration in the soil is high. Once inside, the dissolved salts travel up in the xylem with the water.

## The transpiration stream

Water moves through the plant in a continuous column called the **transpiration stream**:

1. Water enters root hairs by osmosis.
2. It crosses the cortex to the xylem.
3. It rises in the xylem vessels of the root and stem to the veins of the leaves.
4. It passes out of the xylem into the mesophyll cells.
5. Water evaporates from the wet surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
6. Water vapour diffuses out through open stomata into the drier air outside.

Loss of water at the top **pulls** the column of water up the xylem from below, rather like drinking through a straw. The stream also carries mineral salts to the leaves and cools the leaves as water evaporates.

### The role of stomata

Stomata must open in daylight to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, but water vapour escapes whenever they are open. Guard cells close the stomata when the plant is short of water and at night, reducing loss. Most transpiration happens through the stomata; a little passes through the cuticle.

## Factors affecting the rate of transpiration

| Factor | Effect on the rate | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature (higher) | Increases | Water evaporates faster from the mesophyll cells and molecules diffuse faster; warm air holds more water vapour |
| Humidity (higher) | Decreases | Less difference in water vapour concentration between the air spaces and the air outside, so diffusion is slower |
| Wind (stronger) | Increases | Moving air carries away the water vapour near the leaf, keeping the gradient steep |
| Light intensity (brighter) | Increases | Stomata open wider in light; light also warms the leaf |
| Soil water (shortage) | Decreases | Guard cells close the stomata when the plant is short of water |

A **potometer** measures how fast a leafy shoot takes up water; since almost all of this water is lost by transpiration, it is used to compare transpiration rates. An air bubble in a capillary tube moves as the shoot draws water. Another method is to weigh a potted plant, with the pot and soil surface covered in plastic so that only the plant loses water.

:::worked-solution[Interpreting potometer results]
Nyasha uses a potometer on a leafy bean shoot and records how far the air bubble moves in 5 minutes under four conditions.

| Condition | Distance moved in 5 min (mm) | Rate (mm per min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Still air, shade | 15 | 3 |
| Still air, bright light | 30 | 6 |
| Fan (wind), bright light | 55 | 11 |
| Plastic bag over shoot (humid), bright light | 10 | 2 |

1. Rate = distance ÷ time; for still air in shade, $15 \div 5 = 3$ mm per min.
2. Bright light doubled the rate (6 compared with 3), because the stomata opened wider.
3. Wind increased it further, from 6 to 11 mm per min: an increase of $\frac{55 - 30}{30} \times 100 = 83.3\%$, because water vapour was blown away from the leaf.
4. The plastic bag raised the humidity around the leaves, so the gradient for diffusion of water vapour was small and the rate fell to 2 mm per min.
:::

:::worked-solution[Water loss measured by weighing]
Tatenda covers the soil of a potted tomato plant with plastic and weighs it at 08:00 (1,250 g) and at 14:00 (1,214 g).

1. Mass lost = $1250 - 1214 = 36$ g, all of it water lost by the plant.
2. Time = 6 hours, so the mean rate = $36 \div 6 = 6$ g per hour.
3. As a percentage of the starting mass: $\frac{36}{1250} \times 100 = 2.88\%$.
:::

## Wilting

When water is lost by transpiration faster than the roots can absorb it, cells lose water, the vacuoles shrink and the cells become **flaccid**. Leaves and soft stems droop.

- **Temporary wilting** happens on hot, windy afternoons even in moist soil; the plant recovers in the evening when transpiration falls.
- **Permanent wilting** happens when the soil is so dry that the roots cannot take up water at all; the plant does not recover even at night unless the soil is watered, and it may die. The soil water content at this stage is the **permanent wilting point**.

Wilting closes the stomata, so photosynthesis slows and yield falls. Farmers reduce wilting by irrigating on time, mulching, planting windbreaks, transplanting in the evening and shading new transplants.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying water enters roots by diffusion of solutes"}
Water enters root hairs by **osmosis** through a partially permeable membrane. Mineral ions enter mainly by **active uptake**, which needs energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting humidity the wrong way round"}
Higher humidity **lowers** the rate of transpiration, because the air is already full of water vapour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every wilt a disease"}
Wilting on a hot afternoon that recovers by evening is temporary wilting caused by water loss, not a disease such as bacterial wilt.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining a factor"}
For *explain* questions give the effect **and** the reason in terms of evaporation, diffusion gradient or stomatal opening. "Wind increases transpiration because it removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping a steep diffusion gradient" earns both marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Potometer data"}
Rate = distance moved ÷ time. Compare conditions with numbers from the table, and remember that a potometer measures **water uptake**, which is close to, but not exactly, water loss.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water enters root hairs by osmosis, crosses the cortex and rises in the xylem; seeds take up water first by imbibition.
- Mineral ions are taken in by active uptake, which uses energy from respiration and needs oxygen in the soil.
- Transpiration is the evaporation of water from mesophyll cells and its diffusion as vapour through the stomata; it pulls the transpiration stream up the plant.
- Higher temperature, wind and light increase transpiration; higher humidity decreases it.
- Wilting occurs when loss exceeds uptake: temporary wilting recovers, permanent wilting does not without water.
:::
