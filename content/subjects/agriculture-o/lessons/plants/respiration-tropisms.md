## Introduction

A bag of damp maize left in a storeroom in Gweru feels warm when Tatenda pushes his hand into it a week later, and the grain smells sour. In a field nearby, maize seedlings in a waterlogged hollow are yellow and stunted, while seedlings on the ridge are green. Both problems are about **respiration**: the damp grain is respiring and producing heat, and the waterlogged roots cannot get the oxygen they need.

In the same field the young shoots all grow straight up towards the light and the roots grow down into the soil, even where the seed was planted upside down. These growth responses are **tropisms**. This lesson covers both topics, which ZIMSEC examines in Form 4: you must give the equation for aerobic respiration, compare aerobic and anaerobic respiration, compare respiration with photosynthesis and describe experiments on tropisms and their importance.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Respiration"}
The chemical breakdown of food (mainly glucose) in living cells to release energy. It happens all the time, day and night, in every living cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Aerobic respiration"}
Respiration that uses oxygen, breaking glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water and releasing a large amount of energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Anaerobic respiration"}
Respiration without oxygen. In plant cells and yeast glucose is broken down incompletely to ethanol (alcohol) and carbon dioxide, releasing much less energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Tropism"}
A growth movement of part of a plant in response to a stimulus that comes from one direction. Growth towards the stimulus is **positive**; growth away from it is **negative**.
:::

## Aerobic respiration

The word equation is:

$$
\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water} + \text{energy}
$$

The chemical equation is:

$$
\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O} + \text{energy}
$$

Aerobic respiration takes place in the **mitochondria** of every living cell: root cells, leaf cells, cells of germinating seeds, fruits and stored tubers. It is controlled by enzymes, so its rate rises with temperature up to an optimum.

## Anaerobic respiration

When oxygen is short, plant cells can respire anaerobically for a time:

$$
\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{a little energy}
$$

This happens in **waterlogged roots**, in seeds sown too deep in wet, compacted soil and in grain stored damp in airtight heaps. Ethanol is poisonous to plant cells, and so little energy is released that growth and active uptake of minerals almost stop. Brewers use the same process in yeast to make beer from sorghum malt.

| Feature | Aerobic | Anaerobic (plants and yeast) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen | Needed | Not needed |
| Products | Carbon dioxide and water | Ethanol and carbon dioxide |
| Energy released | Large amount | Small amount |
| Breakdown of glucose | Complete | Incomplete |
| Where in the cell | Mitochondria (after the first stage in the cytoplasm) | Cytoplasm |

## Importance of respiration in crop production

- Energy from respiration is used for **cell division and growth**, **active uptake** of mineral ions, making proteins and oils, and moving food in the phloem.
- **Germinating seeds** respire fast, so they need oxygen: seedbeds must be well aerated, not waterlogged or capped.
- **Roots need oxygen**: drainage, ridging and good soil structure keep roots respiring aerobically.
- **Stored produce keeps respiring.** Respiration uses up stored food, causing loss of mass and quality, and produces heat and moisture that encourage moulds and insects. Grain is therefore dried to a low moisture content and stored cool and dry; fruit and vegetables are kept cool to slow respiration.

:::worked-solution[Heat from respiring seeds]
Rudo soaks bean seeds, boils half of them to kill them, rinses both lots in a weak disinfectant to stop bacteria and fungi growing, and puts each lot in a vacuum flask with a thermometer.

| Day | Flask A: live seeds (°C) | Flask B: boiled seeds (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 22.0 | 22.0 |
| 1 | 24.5 | 22.0 |
| 2 | 27.0 | 22.5 |
| 3 | 29.5 | 22.0 |

1. Rise in flask A = $29.5 - 22.0 = 7.5$ °C over 3 days.
2. Mean rise per day = $7.5 \div 3 = 2.5$ °C.
3. Flask B hardly changed, so the heat in A came from the living seeds, not from the surroundings.
4. Conclusion: germinating seeds release energy as heat when they respire. Flask B is the **control**; the disinfectant makes sure the heat is not from microbes.
:::

## Respiration compared with photosynthesis

| Photosynthesis | Respiration |
| --- | --- |
| Builds up glucose (stores energy) | Breaks down glucose (releases energy) |
| Uses carbon dioxide and water; gives out oxygen | Uses oxygen; gives out carbon dioxide and water |
| Needs light and chlorophyll | Does not need light or chlorophyll |
| Only in green cells with chloroplasts | In all living cells, in the mitochondria |
| Only in the light | Day and night |
| Increases dry mass | Decreases dry mass |

The two processes are opposite in their raw materials and products. In bright light photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the plant gains food; at night only respiration happens. For a crop to grow, total photosynthesis must exceed total respiration.

## Tropisms

| Tropism | Stimulus | Shoot response | Root response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phototropism | Light from one side | Positive (grows towards light) | Negative or none |
| Geotropism (gravitropism) | Gravity | Negative (grows up) | Positive (grows down) |
| Hydrotropism | Water | — | Positive (grows towards water) |
| Thigmotropism (haptotropism) | Touch or contact | Tendrils and twining stems coil around supports | — |

Tropisms are controlled by plant growth substances (auxins). In a shoot lit from one side, auxin moves to the shaded side, and cells there grow longer, so the shoot bends towards the light.

### Experiments on tropisms

**Phototropism.** Grow seedlings in a box with a slit on one side so that light enters from one direction. Grow a control set in a box lit evenly from above, or on a **klinostat** (a slowly turning disc) so all sides get equal light. After a few days the shoots in the slit box bend towards the light; the control shoots grow straight.

**Geotropism.** Pin germinating bean seeds with straight radicles horizontally to cork on a klinostat. On a stationary klinostat (or with the dish turned on its side) the radicles curve downwards and the shoots upwards. On the rotating klinostat, gravity acts on all sides equally, so the radicles keep growing horizontally. This control shows that the bending is caused by gravity.

**Hydrotropism.** Plant seeds in a sieve of moist sawdust hung at an angle. The radicles grow down through the mesh because of gravity, then curve back towards the moist sawdust, showing that the roots respond to water.

:::example{title="Why tropisms matter in the field"}
Farai plants maize seed by hand and some seeds land on their sides or upside down. All the seedlings still emerge normally because their roots respond positively to gravity and grow down, and their shoots grow up against gravity towards the light. Later, the roots of crops on his sandy soil spread deeply towards moist layers (hydrotropism), and his climbing beans and cucumbers attach to stakes with tendrils (thigmotropism). Shoots of crops shaded by tall weeds bend and stretch towards light and become weak, another reason for early weeding.
:::

### Importance of tropisms in crop growth

- Roots grow down to **anchor** the plant and reach **water and mineral salts**, whatever way the seed is planted.
- Shoots grow up and towards light, so leaves get the light they need for **photosynthesis**.
- Roots grow towards moisture, helping crops survive dry spells.
- Climbing crops such as climbing beans, cucurbits and passion fruit support themselves by touch responses; farmers provide stakes or trellises.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying plants respire only at night"}
Plants respire **all the time**. At night it is the only process, so they give out carbon dioxide; by day photosynthesis usually hides it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing lactic acid for plants"}
Anaerobic respiration in plants and yeast produces **ethanol and carbon dioxide**, not lactic acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the control"}
A tropism experiment needs a control (even light, or a rotating klinostat) to show that the stimulus caused the bending.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Equations and comparisons"}
Give the balanced equation for aerobic respiration with energy on the right. When *comparing* respiration with photosynthesis, write matching pairs of points, not two separate lists.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describe and evaluate"}
*Describe* a tropism experiment as numbered steps with the control and the result. *Evaluate* the importance of tropisms by linking each response to a benefit for the crop, then say which matters most, for example root gravitropism for establishment.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Aerobic respiration: $\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}$ + energy, in the mitochondria of all living cells.
- Anaerobic respiration in plants: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + a little energy; happens in waterlogged roots and damp stores.
- Respiration supplies energy for growth and active uptake; it also causes losses and heating in stored produce.
- Respiration is the reverse of photosynthesis in raw materials and products and happens day and night.
- Tropisms: phototropism, geotropism, hydrotropism and thigmotropism, shown with slit boxes, klinostats and controls; they anchor roots, find water and bring leaves to the light.
:::
