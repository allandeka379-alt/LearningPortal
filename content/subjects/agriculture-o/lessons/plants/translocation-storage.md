## Introduction

Tendai's grandmother near Chipinge keeps a bed of madumbe (taro), a row of sugar cane and a patch of sweet potatoes beside her house. She also grows groundnuts, beans and maize in the field. None of the food she harvests from these crops is made where it is eaten: the sugar in the cane stem, the starch in the sweet potato roots and the oil in the groundnut kernels were all made in the leaves and carried there. The movement of food through the plant is **translocation**, and the swollen parts that hold the food are **storage organs**.

Farmers use storage organs as food, as planting material (seed potatoes, sweet potato vines, cassava cuttings, onion sets) and as products to sell. Examination questions ask you to define translocation, describe the ring-barking experiment, name storage organs from descriptions and state the food stored in named crops.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Translocation"}
The movement of dissolved food substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids, made in the leaves, through the phloem to other parts of the plant where they are used or stored.
:::

:::definition{term="Source and sink"}
A **source** is a part of the plant that supplies food to the phloem, such as a photosynthesising leaf or a storage organ that is being emptied. A **sink** is a part that receives food, such as a growing tip, a developing fruit, seed or tuber, or the roots.
:::

:::definition{term="Storage organ"}
A root, stem or leaf that is swollen with stored food, which the plant uses to survive an unfavourable season or to grow new shoots, and which farmers often harvest.
:::

:::definition{term="Ring-barking"}
Removing a complete ring of bark (which contains the phloem) from around a woody stem or trunk, leaving the xylem in place.
:::

## Translocation in the phloem

Glucose made in photosynthesis is changed to **sucrose** in the leaf cells, because sucrose is soluble but does not take part in reactions on the way. Amino acids made in the leaves also enter the phloem. The phloem then carries this solution to the sinks. Unlike water in the xylem, which only moves **up**, food in the phloem can move **up or down**:

- **down** from the leaves to the roots and to root or stem tubers;
- **up** from the leaves to the growing tip, flowers and developing fruits and seeds;
- **up** from a storage organ to new shoots when, for example, a seed potato sprouts.

At the sink, sucrose is used in respiration, made into cellulose for new cell walls, or turned into **starch**, **oils** or **proteins** for storage.

## The ring-barking experiment

In a woody stem, the phloem lies in the bark, just outside the xylem. If a ring of bark is cut off, the phloem is removed but the xylem is not.

1. A complete ring of bark about 2 cm wide is removed from the stem of a young tree or woody shoot.
2. After some weeks, the bark **above** the ring swells, because food coming down from the leaves collects there and causes extra growth.
3. The part **below** the ring does not swell and may shrink.
4. The leaves do **not** wilt at first, because the xylem is intact and water still rises.
5. In time the **roots starve**, as no food reaches them, and the whole tree dies.

This shows that food travels down the stem in the bark (the phloem), not in the wood (the xylem). Animals that strip bark, such as goats, porcupines and elephants, can kill trees in the same way.

:::example{title="Interpreting a ring-barked fruit tree"}
Farai ring-barks one branch of a guava tree in his orchard as an experiment. A few weeks later the fruits on that branch above the ring are larger than fruits on other branches, and the bark just above the cut is swollen. The leaves of the branch stay green. The food made by the leaves of that branch cannot move down past the ring, so it is used by the fruits and the tissue above the ring instead. The leaves stay green because water still rises in the uncut xylem. If he ring-barked the main trunk, however, the roots would get no food and the whole tree would eventually die, so the practice is risky.
:::

## Food storage organs

Plants store food so that they can survive the dry season or winter and grow quickly when conditions improve. Different parts are modified for storage.

| Storage organ | What it is | Crop examples | Main food stored |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tap root | Swollen main root | Carrot, beetroot | Sugars and starch |
| Root tuber | Swollen side (adventitious) root, no buds | Sweet potato, cassava | Starch |
| Stem tuber | Swollen end of an underground stem, with buds (eyes) | Irish potato, yam | Starch |
| Bulb | Short stem with fleshy leaf bases wrapped around a bud | Onion, garlic | Sugars |
| Corm | Short, swollen, upright underground stem covered by scale leaves | Taro (madumbe) | Starch |
| Rhizome | Swollen underground stem growing horizontally | Ginger | Starch and other substances |
| Stem (cane) | Thick upright stem | Sugar cane | Sucrose |
| Seed (cotyledons or endosperm) | Food store for the embryo | Maize, groundnut, soya bean, bean, sunflower | Starch (maize), oil and protein (groundnut, soya, sunflower), protein and starch (beans) |
| Fruit | Ripened ovary | Banana, avocado | Sugars and starch (banana), oil (avocado) |

### Telling stems from roots

A storage **stem** has **buds** and often scale leaves: the eyes of an Irish potato, the bud inside an onion bulb, the buds on a corm. A storage **root** has no buds of its own on its surface (although sweet potatoes can sprout shoots from them, they are not arranged at nodes). This is why Irish potatoes are planted as tubers with eyes, while sweet potatoes are usually planted from vine cuttings.

### Foods stored

- **Starch**: the most common store; insoluble, compact and does not affect osmosis (potato, cassava, sweet potato, maize grain).
- **Sugars**: soluble stores in sugar cane, onion, carrot and beetroot; sugar cane stores sucrose, which is extracted at the mill.
- **Oils (lipids)**: in seeds such as groundnut, soya bean, sunflower and cottonseed, and in avocado fruit; oil stores much energy in little space.
- **Proteins**: in legume seeds such as beans, soya beans and groundnuts.

:::example{title="Identifying an unknown storage organ"}
A pupil is given an underground organ that is swollen, has several small buds arranged in shallow pits, and turns blue-black when cut and tested with iodine. Buds at nodes show that it is a **stem**, so it is a **stem tuber**, such as an Irish potato. The blue-black colour shows that it stores **starch**. If the organ had no buds and a pointed root tip at one end, it would be a root tuber such as a sweet potato.
:::

### Importance of storage organs in farming

- They are the harvested product of many crops: potatoes, cassava, sweet potatoes, onions, carrots, sugar cane.
- They are used as **planting material** for vegetative propagation: seed potatoes, onion sets, taro corms.
- Stored food lets a crop survive dry seasons and regrow quickly, for example sugar cane ratoons after cutting.
- Stored food determines quality: high sugar in cane, high starch in cassava, high oil in groundnuts.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying food moves in the xylem"}
Food (sucrose and amino acids) moves in the **phloem**. Water and mineral salts move in the xylem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying leaves above a ring-bark wilt"}
The xylem is not cut, so water still reaches the leaves. Wilting is **not** the result; swelling above the ring and starvation of the roots are.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the Irish potato a root tuber"}
It is a **stem** tuber: its eyes are buds. Sweet potato and cassava are **root** tubers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Ring-barking"}
State what is removed (bark containing **phloem**), what is left (xylem), what is seen (swelling above the ring, roots starve, plant eventually dies, no early wilting) and the conclusion (food moves down in the phloem).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Storage organs"}
In tables of crops, give the **organ** (tap root, root tuber, stem tuber, bulb, corm) and the **food** (starch, sugar, oil, protein) separately. For *explain* questions, link the storage organ to its use in propagation or survival.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Translocation is the movement of dissolved food (sucrose, amino acids) in the phloem from sources to sinks, up or down.
- Ring-barking removes the phloem: the bark above swells, the roots starve and the plant dies, but the leaves do not wilt at first.
- Storage organs: tap roots (carrot), root tubers (sweet potato, cassava), stem tubers (Irish potato), bulbs (onion), corms (taro), rhizomes (ginger), stems (sugar cane), seeds and fruits.
- Foods stored: starch, sugars, oils and proteins, depending on the crop.
:::
