## Introduction

Nyasha's school garden near Marondera is divided into beds of rape, covo, tomatoes, onions, carrots and butternut, with a plot of maize and sugar beans beside it and a row of orange trees along the fence. Her teacher asks the class to sort the crops into groups. Some pupils group them by the part that is eaten, others by how long the plant lives, and others by plant family. Each way of grouping is useful for a different purpose.

Classification helps a farmer plan. Crops in the same family often share the same pests and diseases, so they should not follow each other in a rotation. Crops with the same life cycle need similar land preparation and planning. Knowing whether a crop is a monocotyledon or a dicotyledon tells you about its roots and how it responds to some herbicides. Examination questions ask you to classify named crops, to give the features of monocotyledons and dicotyledons, and to explain why classification matters in crop production.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Annual crop"}
A crop that completes its whole life cycle, from seed to seed, in one growing season or less, and then dies. Maize, beans and groundnuts are annuals.
:::

:::definition{term="Biennial crop"}
A crop that needs two growing seasons to complete its life cycle: it grows leaves and stores food in the first season and flowers and sets seed in the second. Onions, carrots and cabbages are biennials, although they are harvested in the first season.
:::

:::definition{term="Perennial crop"}
A crop that lives for more than two years and usually produces a harvest every year. Citrus, mangoes, bananas, coffee, tea and sugar cane are perennials.
:::

:::definition{term="Monocotyledon"}
A flowering plant whose seed has one seed leaf (cotyledon). Cereals such as maize, sorghum and wheat are monocotyledons.
:::

:::definition{term="Dicotyledon"}
A flowering plant whose seed has two seed leaves (cotyledons). Beans, tomatoes, cabbages and pumpkins are dicotyledons.
:::

## Classification by the part eaten

Farmers and consumers often group crops by the part of the plant that is used as food.

| Part eaten | Examples of crops |
| --- | --- |
| Leaf | Cabbage, rape, covo, lettuce, spinach |
| Root | Carrot, beetroot, sweet potato, cassava |
| Stem tuber | Irish potato, yam |
| Bulb | Onion, garlic |
| Stem | Sugar cane |
| Fruit | Tomato, pumpkin, butternut, orange, mango, banana |
| Seed (grain) | Maize, wheat, sorghum, beans, groundnuts, soya beans |
| Flower head | Cauliflower |

The Irish potato is a swollen underground **stem**, not a root: it has eyes, which are buds in the axils of scale leaves. The sweet potato and cassava are swollen **roots**. This difference matters when the crops are propagated and when questions ask for the part eaten.

## Classification by life cycle

- **Annuals** grow, flower, set seed and die within one season. Most field crops in Zimbabwe are annuals planted with the first effective rains: maize, sorghum, millets, groundnuts, sugar beans, soya beans, cotton and tobacco. Winter wheat is an annual grown under irrigation.
- **Biennials** store food in the first season and flower in the second. The farmer harvests them at the end of the first season, when the stored food is at its best. Onions, carrots, beetroot and cabbages are biennials. Seed producers must keep the plants into a second season to get seed.
- **Perennials** live for many years. They need a large investment at planting but give yields year after year: citrus, mangoes, avocados, bananas, coffee, tea, sugar cane and pasture grasses.

:::example{title="Why the life cycle affects planning"}
Tatenda wants to plant mango trees on part of his land. Mangoes are perennials: the land will be occupied for many years, the trees will not bear fruit for several seasons, and the field cannot be ploughed or rotated like a maize field. He must choose a permanent site with deep soil, protect the young trees and plan for cash flow until the first harvest. A field of maize, an annual, can be changed to another crop next season.
:::

## Monocotyledons and dicotyledons

All flowering crop plants are either monocotyledons or dicotyledons. The difference begins in the seed but shows in the whole plant.

| Feature | Monocotyledon (e.g. maize) | Dicotyledon (e.g. bean) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of cotyledons in the seed | One | Two |
| Leaf veins | Parallel | Net-like (branched) |
| Leaf shape | Long and narrow, often with a sheath around the stem | Broad, often with a stalk (petiole) |
| Root system | Fibrous roots | Tap root with lateral roots |
| Vascular bundles in the stem | Scattered | Arranged in a ring |
| Flower parts | Usually in threes | Usually in fours or fives |

The difference is used in weed control: some **selective herbicides** kill broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) weeds but do not harm a cereal crop, because the two groups take up and respond to the chemical differently.

## Classification by botanical family

Botanists group plants into families by their flowers, fruits and seeds. Crops in one family usually look alike and share pests and diseases.

| Family (common name) | Examples | Shared features and points for the farmer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cereals (grass family) | Maize, sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet, wheat, rice | Monocotyledons; grain is the main product; fibrous roots; heavy users of nitrogen |
| Legumes (pea family) | Sugar beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, soya beans, bambara nuts | Seeds in pods; root nodules with *Rhizobium* bacteria fix nitrogen; high-protein seeds |
| Brassicas (cabbage family) | Cabbage, rape, covo, kale, cauliflower | Leafy vegetables; attacked by aphids and diamondback moth |
| Solanaceous crops (potato family) | Tomato, Irish potato, tobacco, pepper, eggplant | Share diseases such as bacterial wilt and blights |
| Cucurbits (pumpkin family) | Pumpkin, butternut, watermelon, cucumber, squash | Trailing or climbing plants with tendrils; large fleshy fruits |

:::example{title="Using families to plan a vegetable rotation"}
Rudo grew tomatoes in a bed last season and the plants wilted from bacterial wilt. She wants to plant Irish potatoes there next. Both are solanaceous crops and the disease survives in the soil, so the potatoes would probably be attacked too. She should plant a crop from a different family, for example a legume such as beans or a brassica such as rape, and keep solanaceous crops off that bed for several seasons.
:::

## Main types of crops grown locally and their products

| Type of crop | Examples | Main products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cereals | Maize, sorghum, millets, wheat, rice | Grain for mealie-meal and flour, stockfeed, malt for brewing, stover for cattle |
| Legumes (grain and oilseed) | Sugar beans, cowpeas, groundnuts, soya beans | Protein-rich food, cooking oil, peanut butter, oilcake for stockfeed |
| Roots and tubers | Irish potato, sweet potato, cassava, yam | Starchy food, chips, starch |
| Fruits | Oranges, bananas, mangoes, avocados, tomatoes | Fresh fruit, juice, jam |
| Industrial crops | Cotton, tobacco, sugar cane | Lint and cottonseed oil, cured leaf, sugar and molasses |
| Vegetables | Cabbage, rape, covo, onions | Fresh food for the household and markets |

Some crops give more than one product: a soya bean is crushed into oil and the cake left is a protein feed for livestock; maize gives grain for people and stover for cattle.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the Irish potato a root"}
The Irish potato is a **stem tuber** (it has buds called eyes). Sweet potato and cassava are swollen roots.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling onions or cabbages annuals"}
They are harvested in the first season, but their natural life cycle takes two seasons, so they are **biennials**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two ways of naming groups"}
"Legume" names a botanical family; "seed crop" names the part eaten. A groundnut is both. Answer with the kind of classification the question asks for.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* or *name* questions need a single correct term or crop. *Classify* means put each named crop in the right group. *Distinguish* or *differentiate* (monocotyledon from dicotyledon) needs matching pairs of features, for example "parallel veins in a monocotyledon but net veins in a dicotyledon". *Explain why classification is important* needs a use linked to a reason, such as rotation to break pest and disease cycles.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is examined"}
ZIMSEC tests these facts in Paper 1 objective items and short parts of Paper 2. Cambridge asks you to name the main types of crop grown locally and their products in short-answer questions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Crops can be classified by part eaten, life cycle, number of cotyledons and botanical family.
- Annuals live one season, biennials two, perennials many years.
- Monocotyledons: one cotyledon, parallel veins, fibrous roots, scattered bundles. Dicotyledons: two cotyledons, net veins, tap root, bundles in a ring.
- Main families: cereals, legumes, brassicas, solanaceous crops and cucurbits; crops in one family share pests and diseases, so they are separated in a rotation.
- Local crop types are cereals, legumes, roots and tubers, fruits, vegetables and industrial crops, each with its own products.
:::
