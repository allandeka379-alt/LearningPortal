## Introduction

Between 1945 and 1948 the map of Europe was redrawn without a war. Country after country in the east came under communist governments loyal to Moscow, and the United States answered with a new policy and billions of dollars of aid. By 1948 Europe was divided into two camps.

This pattern of superpower influence spread far beyond Europe. African leaders after independence, including the Frontline States that supported Zimbabwe's liberation, often tried to avoid being drawn into either camp, which is one reason the Non-Aligned Movement was founded in 1961. Understanding how the division of Europe happened helps to explain why.

You should already know about the Yalta and Potsdam conferences of 1945 and why the wartime alliance broke down.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Satellite state"}
A country that is formally independent but whose government is controlled by a more powerful state. The communist states of Eastern Europe were Soviet satellites.
:::

:::definition{term="Salami tactics"}
A phrase used for the way communists gained power step by step, slicing away their opponents one group at a time, like slices off a salami. It is associated with the Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi.
:::

:::definition{term="Containment"}
The American policy, from 1947, of stopping communism from spreading to any new countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Marshall Aid"}
American economic help to rebuild Europe after the war (the European Recovery Programme), named after the US Secretary of State George Marshall.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **12 March 1947:** the Truman Doctrine is announced.
- **June 1947:** Marshall proposes aid for Europe.
- **September 1947:** the Cominform is set up.
- **February 1948:** communists take full power in Czechoslovakia.
- **January 1949:** Comecon is set up.
:::

## How the USSR gained control of Eastern Europe

### The Red Army

The most important factor was simple: in 1944-45 the Red Army drove the Germans out of Eastern Europe and stayed. Where Soviet troops were present, local communists had protection, and opponents knew that resistance was dangerous.

### Coalition governments and salami tactics

In most countries the communists did not seize power at once. They first joined **coalition governments** with other parties, as the Yalta promise of democracy seemed to require. Then they made sure that they controlled the key ministries, especially the **Ministry of the Interior**, which ran the police. Step by step they arrested, discredited or frightened their rivals, split other parties, and forced the left-wing socialists to merge with them. Elections were held, but they were often rigged or held in an atmosphere of fear.

- **Poland:** the Soviet-backed government in Lublin formed the core of the new government in 1945; a few politicians from the London-based government joined it. The elections of January 1947 were rigged, and the leading non-communist, Stanisław Mikołajczyk, fled abroad later that year.
- **Hungary:** in the elections of November 1945 the Smallholders' Party won a clear majority. The communists used the police to break it up, and by 1949 Hungary was a one-party state under Rákosi.
- **Bulgaria and Romania:** communist-led governments took over in 1944-45. Opposition leaders were arrested, and in 1947 King Michael of Romania was forced to abdicate.
- **East Germany:** the Soviet zone of Germany was run by the Soviet military administration and German communists.

### Czechoslovakia, 1948

Czechoslovakia was the last democracy in Eastern Europe. The communists won about 38 per cent of the vote in free elections in 1946, the largest share of any party, and their leader Klement Gottwald became Prime Minister in a coalition. In 1947 the Czechoslovak government wanted to accept Marshall Aid, but Moscow made it refuse.

In **February 1948** non-communist ministers resigned in protest at communist control of the police, hoping to force new elections. Instead, the communists organised demonstrations and armed workers' militias, and President Beneš gave way and accepted a government dominated by the communists. In March the foreign minister Jan Masaryk, a non-communist, was found dead below a window of his ministry. Whether he was killed or took his own life has never been settled. The events shocked the West, because Czechoslovakia had been seen as a bridge between east and west.

### Exceptions and controls

**Yugoslavia** was communist, but its leader **Tito** had freed his country largely with his own partisan army and refused to take orders from Stalin. In 1948 Yugoslavia was expelled from the Cominform. To tighten control elsewhere, Stalin set up the **Cominform** (1947) to coordinate communist parties and **Comecon** (1949) to link the satellites' economies to the USSR.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how salami tactics worked"}
1. **Join a coalition:** communists take part in a government with other parties, looking democratic.
2. **Take the key ministry:** a communist becomes Minister of the Interior and controls the police.
3. **Slice away opponents:** rival politicians are accused of plotting, arrested or forced abroad; other parties are split.
4. **Merge and win:** the socialists are forced to merge with the communists, and the next election, held under police pressure, produces a communist-controlled government.

Explaining the process step by step shows *how* control was gained, not just *that* it was gained.
:::

## The American response

### The Truman Doctrine, March 1947

In February 1947 Britain told the USA that it could no longer afford to support the Greek government against communist guerrillas in the Greek civil war, or to help Turkey. On **12 March 1947** President Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars of aid for Greece and Turkey. He argued that the USA should support free peoples who were resisting attempts at subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressure. This became known as the **Truman Doctrine**: the USA would actively **contain** communism, not just disapprove of it.

### Marshall Aid

In June 1947 the Secretary of State, **George Marshall**, offered American help to rebuild the economies of Europe. Truman's government believed that poverty and hunger made people turn to communism, so prosperity would be a defence. The plan was also good for American business, since Europeans would buy American goods.

Congress hesitated at the cost until the Czech coup in February 1948 convinced it of the danger; it approved the programme in April 1948. Between 1948 and 1952 about 13 billion dollars went to sixteen Western European countries.

Aid was offered to the whole of Europe, but Stalin saw it as a way of spreading American influence. The Soviet foreign minister Molotov walked out of the planning talks in Paris, and the satellite states, including Czechoslovakia, were made to refuse.

### Soviet responses

Soviet propaganda described Marshall Aid as "dollar imperialism". Stalin set up the **Cominform** in September 1947 and **Comecon** in January 1949, and tightened control of the satellites. The division of Europe into two economic and political blocs was now clear.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: defensive or aggressive?"}
**The Soviet takeover was defensive:** Stalin wanted a buffer zone after two invasions from the west, and Marshall Aid looked to him like an American attempt to buy influence on his border.
**It was aggressive:** the USSR rigged elections, broke the Yalta promise of free elections and imposed one-party rule on millions of people who had not chosen it.
**Weighing it:** historians still disagree. A strong answer explains both views and judges which fits the evidence better, for example by asking whether Czechoslovakia in 1948 was a threat to the USSR.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the USSR invaded Eastern Europe after the war"}
The Red Army was already there in 1944-45, having driven out the Germans. Control came from occupation followed by political methods, not a new invasion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Truman Doctrine with Marshall Aid"}
The Truman Doctrine (March 1947) was the policy of containment, starting with aid to Greece and Turkey. Marshall Aid (proposed June 1947) was the economic programme to rebuild Europe.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Yugoslavia a Soviet satellite"}
Yugoslavia was communist but independent of Moscow; Tito broke with Stalin in 1948.
:::

Give methods, each with an example: the Red Army's presence; coalitions and control of the police; rigged elections (Poland 1947); the Czech coup (1948). Explain how each method removed opposition.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Red Army's occupation of Eastern Europe in 1944-45 was the foundation of Soviet control.
- Communists used coalitions, control of the police, rigged elections and "salami tactics"; Czechoslovakia fell in February 1948.
- The Truman Doctrine (March 1947) committed the USA to containing communism, starting with Greece and Turkey.
- Marshall Aid (about 13 billion dollars, 1948-52) rebuilt Western Europe; Stalin made the satellites refuse it.
- Stalin answered with the Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949); only Yugoslavia escaped Soviet control.
:::
