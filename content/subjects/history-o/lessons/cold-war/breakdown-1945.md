## Introduction

In 1945 American and Soviet soldiers met on the River Elbe and shook hands as allies. Within two years their governments regarded each other as enemies. The **Cold War** that followed lasted until 1991 and shaped the whole world, including Africa: the superpowers armed and funded rival movements in Angola, Mozambique and the Horn of Africa, and the liberation movements of Zimbabwe drew support from the Soviet Union, China and their allies.

This lesson explains why the "Grand Alliance" of the USA, Britain and the USSR broke down so quickly. The question is examined by both boards: ZIMSEC Paper 2 asks about the origins of the Cold War, and Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 ask "Who was to blame for the Cold War?".

You should already know how the Second World War ended in Europe (8 May 1945) and in Asia (August-September 1945).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cold War"}
A long period of tension and rivalry between the USA and the USSR and their allies (about 1945-1991) in which the two superpowers never fought each other directly, but competed through propaganda, an arms race, alliances and wars fought by others.
:::

:::definition{term="Capitalism"}
An economic system in which land, factories and businesses are mostly privately owned and run for profit. In the USA it went with democracy: free elections, several parties and a free press.
:::

:::definition{term="Communism"}
The ideology of the Soviet Union: the state owns land and industry and plans the economy, in the name of the workers. In practice the USSR was a one-party state where the Communist Party controlled government, the press and the police.
:::

:::definition{term="Superpower"}
A state with so much military, economic and political strength that it can act all over the world. After 1945 only the USA and the USSR fitted this description.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- **Yalta Conference**, February 1945: Roosevelt (USA), Churchill (Britain), Stalin (USSR).
- **Potsdam Conference**, July-August 1945: Truman, Stalin, Churchill and then Attlee.
- **Hiroshima** 6 August and **Nagasaki** 9 August 1945: atomic bombs.
- **Long Telegram**, February 1946: George Kennan, an American diplomat in Moscow.
- **"Iron Curtain" speech**, 5 March 1946: Winston Churchill at Fulton, Missouri.
:::

## Why the allies distrusted each other

The alliance of 1941-45 was a marriage of convenience: the three powers had a common enemy, Hitler, and little else in common.

- **Ideology.** The USA believed in free elections and free trade; the USSR believed that capitalism would eventually be overthrown. Each side saw the other's system as a threat to its own.
- **History.** Britain, the USA and others had sent troops to fight the communists in the Russian Civil War after 1918. The West remembered the Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939. Stalin suspected that Britain and the USA had delayed opening a second front in France until June 1944 to let the USSR bear the heaviest fighting.
- **Security.** The USSR had been invaded through Poland in 1914 and in 1941. Historians estimate that about 20-27 million Soviet citizens died in the Second World War. Stalin wanted friendly governments on his western border as a **buffer zone**. To the West, this looked like expansion.

## The Yalta Conference, February 1945

With victory in sight, the "Big Three" met at Yalta in the Crimea. They agreed:

- Germany (and its capital, Berlin) would be divided into zones of occupation. France was later given a zone, making four.
- Nazi war criminals would be tried, and Germany would pay reparations.
- The USSR would join the war against Japan after Germany was defeated.
- The countries freed from Nazi rule would hold **free elections** (the Declaration on Liberated Europe).
- The USSR would keep the eastern part of pre-war Poland, and Poland would gain German land in the west.
- The new **United Nations** would be set up.

Yalta looked like a success, but the agreement on free elections hid a real disagreement. Stalin had already backed a communist-led Polish government (based in Lublin), while Britain supported the Polish government-in-exile in London. Stalin would accept elections only if they produced governments friendly to the USSR.

## The Potsdam Conference, July-August 1945

By the time the leaders met at Potsdam, near Berlin, much had changed:

- **New leaders.** Roosevelt died in April 1945. His successor, **Harry Truman**, was far more suspicious of Stalin. In the middle of the conference Churchill lost the British general election and was replaced by **Clement Attlee**.
- **The Red Army** now occupied most of Eastern Europe, and Stalin had installed a pro-communist government in Poland.
- **The atomic bomb.** On 16 July 1945 the USA tested the first atomic bomb. Truman told Stalin about a powerful new weapon during the conference; Stalin already knew from Soviet spies.

The leaders confirmed the division of Germany and agreed that each power would take reparations mainly from its own zone, with the USSR also receiving some industrial equipment from the western zones. They accepted, for the time being, a new Polish border along the Oder and Neisse rivers. But they argued over reparations, over Poland's government and over Soviet influence in Eastern Europe. Potsdam ended without the warmth of Yalta.

## The atomic bomb

The bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki helped to end the war with Japan, which surrendered in August 1945.

The bomb changed relations between the allies. The USA had a weapon that the USSR did not. Some historians argue that one reason for using it was to show American power to Stalin as well as to defeat Japan; others argue that ending the war quickly and saving American lives was the main reason. Whatever the motive, Stalin saw the American monopoly as a threat and pushed Soviet scientists to build a bomb. The USSR tested its first atomic bomb in August 1949, beginning the **arms race**.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the atomic bomb increased distrust"}
1. **The USA kept the secret.** The bomb was developed with Britain and Canada, but the USSR was not told until Potsdam, and then only vaguely.
2. **Stalin saw a threat.** He believed the USA might use its monopoly to force the USSR to give way in Eastern Europe.
3. **The USSR responded.** Soviet scientists, helped by spies, raced to build their own bomb.
4. **Result:** each side felt less secure, which made compromise harder. This links the bomb directly to the breakdown of trust in 1945-46.
:::

## The alliance breaks down, 1946

In February 1946 **George Kennan**, an American diplomat in Moscow, sent a long report to Washington, later known as the **Long Telegram**. He argued that Soviet leaders saw the capitalist world as hostile, would try to expand Soviet influence wherever they could, but would back down when faced with firm resistance. His analysis helped shape the American policy later called **containment**.

On **5 March 1946**, at Fulton, Missouri, with Truman present, Churchill (now no longer Prime Minister) said that an "iron curtain" had descended across Europe from the Baltic to the Adriatic, and that the countries behind it were increasingly under Moscow's control. He called for a close alliance between Britain and the USA. Stalin replied that Churchill was a warmonger and compared him to Hitler.

In September 1946 the Soviet ambassador in Washington, Nikolai Novikov, sent his own report to Moscow, describing the USA as seeking world domination. By late 1946 each side saw the other as an aggressor.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: was the breakdown inevitable?"}
**Yes:** the two systems had opposed each other since 1917, and only Hitler had held the alliance together. Once he was defeated, rivalry was bound to return.
**No:** at Yalta the leaders reached real agreements. The change of leaders (Truman, Attlee), the bomb and misunderstandings over "free elections" turned disagreements into hostility.
**Judgement:** many historians see deep causes (ideology, history, security) that made tension likely, and short-term events in 1945-46 that turned tension into a Cold War. Your judgement should weigh both.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting Truman at Yalta"}
Roosevelt went to Yalta (February 1945). Truman, who became President in April 1945, went to Potsdam.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Churchill made the Iron Curtain speech as Prime Minister"}
Churchill had lost the July 1945 election. In March 1946 he spoke as leader of the opposition, in the USA, with Truman in the audience.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the conferences instead of explaining the breakdown"}
A list of what was agreed at Yalta and Potsdam does not explain why the alliance broke down. Link each point to growing distrust: for example, the vague promise of free elections led to arguments over Poland.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1: explain why (b) and how far (c)"}
For "Explain why the alliance broke down in 1945-46", give two or three causes (ideology, Poland and Eastern Europe, new leaders, the bomb) and explain how each increased distrust. For "How far was the atomic bomb responsible…?", weigh the bomb against other causes and reach a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2: origins of the Cold War"}
Questions often ask you to describe the Yalta and Potsdam conferences and explain why the Cold War began. Keep dates and the names of the leaders accurate, and connect each conference to the disagreements it caused.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Sources (Cambridge Paper 2)"}
A source about the Iron Curtain speech needs its provenance: Churchill was out of office, speaking in the USA, trying to persuade Americans to stay involved in Europe. That purpose affects how you judge it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The USA (capitalist, democratic) and the USSR (communist, one-party) had been rivals since 1917; Hitler was their only common enemy.
- At Yalta (February 1945) the Big Three agreed on dividing Germany and on free elections, but disagreed in practice over Poland.
- At Potsdam (July-August 1945) new leaders, the Red Army's control of Eastern Europe and the atomic bomb made relations colder.
- The American atomic monopoly alarmed Stalin and started an arms race; the USSR tested its own bomb in 1949.
- Kennan's Long Telegram and Churchill's Iron Curtain speech (both early 1946) showed that each side now saw the other as an enemy.
:::
