## Introduction

For eleven months in 1948-49 the western half of a great European city was kept alive by aeroplanes. The **Berlin Blockade** was the first major crisis of the Cold War, and it ended with Germany split into two states and Europe divided between two military alliances.

The question of who was to blame for the Cold War is one of the great debates of modern history. Historians in the USA, Russia and elsewhere have changed their answers as new documents and new experiences appeared. Learning to weigh these views is excellent practice for the "how far" questions of both ZIMSEC and Cambridge.

You should already know about Yalta, Potsdam, the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Aid.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blockade"}
Cutting off a place so that people and goods cannot get in or out. In June 1948 the USSR closed all road, rail and canal routes into West Berlin.
:::

:::definition{term="Airlift"}
Supplying a place by air. The British and American airlift flew food, fuel and other goods into West Berlin for nearly a year.
:::

:::definition{term="NATO"}
The North Atlantic Treaty Organisation (April 1949): a military alliance in which members agreed that an attack on one would be treated as an attack on all.
:::

:::definition{term="Historiography"}
The study of how historians' interpretations of an event have developed and changed.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **24 June 1948:** the Berlin Blockade begins.
- **12 May 1949:** the blockade is lifted.
- **4 April 1949:** NATO is founded.
- **1949:** the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany, May) and the German Democratic Republic (East Germany, October) are set up.
- **May 1955:** the Warsaw Pact is formed.
:::

## The occupation of Germany

At Yalta and Potsdam the allies divided Germany into four zones: American, British, French and Soviet. Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, was also divided into four sectors. The four powers were meant to run Germany as one economic unit through an Allied Control Council.

They soon disagreed about Germany's future:

- **The USSR** had suffered terribly from German invasion. Stalin wanted reparations and a weak Germany that could never threaten the USSR again. Factories in the Soviet zone were dismantled and taken east.
- **The Western powers** came to believe that German recovery was needed for the recovery of Europe as a whole, and that a poor Germany would be open to communism.

In January 1947 the British and American zones were merged into **Bizonia**; the French zone was later linked to it. The western zones received Marshall Aid. In June 1948 the Western powers introduced a new currency, the **Deutschmark**, in their zones, and soon in West Berlin. Stalin saw these steps as the creation of a separate West German state, against what had been agreed.

## The Berlin Blockade and airlift, 1948-1949

On **24 June 1948** the Soviets cut all road, rail and canal links between the western zones and West Berlin. About two million West Berliners depended on supplies from outside. Stalin's aim was probably to force the West either to abandon its plans for West Germany or to leave Berlin.

Truman and the British decided not to force a way through by land, which could have started a war, and not to abandon Berlin, which would have looked like surrender. Instead, they supplied the city by air along the three air corridors agreed in 1945. Shooting down the aircraft would have meant Stalin firing the first shot.

The airlift grew into an enormous operation. At its height an aircraft landed in West Berlin every few minutes. In total, historians estimate that about 2.3 million tonnes of supplies were flown in over some 275,000 flights. Coal made up the largest share. Winter was hard and food was rationed, but the city survived. On **12 May 1949** Stalin lifted the blockade.

## Consequences

- **Victory for the West.** Stalin had failed to force the West out of Berlin, and the USA had shown it would stand by its allies without going to war.
- **Two German states.** In May 1949 the western zones became the **Federal Republic of Germany**, with its capital at Bonn. In October the Soviet zone became the **German Democratic Republic**. Germany stayed divided until 1990.
- **NATO.** In April 1949 the USA, Canada, Britain, France and eight other Western European states formed NATO. For the first time in peacetime, the USA committed itself to a military alliance in Europe.
- **The Warsaw Pact.** When West Germany joined NATO in 1955, the USSR and its satellites formed the **Warsaw Pact**. Europe was now divided between two armed alliances.
- **Berlin as a flashpoint.** The city remained a source of tension, leading to the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961.

:::example{title="A consequence explained step by step: from blockade to NATO"}
1. **The blockade frightened Western Europe:** it showed that the USSR was willing to use pressure to get its way.
2. **Western Europeans wanted protection:** they had already formed a defensive pact (the Brussels Treaty, 1948) but could not match Soviet forces alone.
3. **The USA agreed to commit itself:** the North Atlantic Treaty of April 1949 bound the USA to defend its allies.
4. **Result:** the Cold War became a military standoff between alliances, which the Warsaw Pact completed in 1955.
:::

## Who was to blame for the Cold War?

Historians have given three broad answers.

**The traditional (orthodox) view**, common in the West in the 1940s and 1950s, blames the **USSR**. Stalin broke the Yalta promise of free elections, imposed communist governments on Eastern Europe and tried to expand Soviet power. The USA only reacted, through containment.

**The revisionist view**, which became influential in the 1960s, when many Americans were questioning their country's role in Vietnam, blames the **USA**. It argues that the USSR was weak and devastated after the war and wanted security, not world revolution. The USA, with the atomic bomb and the world's strongest economy, used Marshall Aid and its power to spread American trade and influence, and treated Soviet defensive moves as aggression.

**The post-revisionist view**, developed from the 1970s and 1980s, shares the blame. Both sides misunderstood each other: each saw its own actions as defensive and the other's as aggressive. After 1991, the opening of some Soviet archives led many historians to stress Stalin's role again, while still accepting that American actions added to the tension.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: was the blockade aggressive or defensive?"}
**Aggressive:** Stalin tried to starve two million civilians to force the West out of a city it had every right to occupy.
**Defensive:** the West had broken the agreement to treat Germany as one unit, created a new currency without Soviet agreement and was building a West German state that the USSR feared.
**Judgement:** a good answer explains both, then decides which side's actions did more to turn tension into crisis, using evidence such as the Deutschmark and the decision to blockade.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the whole of Berlin was blockaded"}
Only West Berlin (the American, British and French sectors) was cut off. East Berlin was in the Soviet sector.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the order of NATO and the Warsaw Pact"}
NATO came first (1949). The Warsaw Pact followed in 1955, after West Germany joined NATO.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the blame debate as settled"}
There is no single correct answer. Examiners reward answers that weigh the evidence for each side and reach a supported judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'Was the USA or the USSR more to blame?'"}
Give the case against each side with specific evidence (Poland, the Czech coup and the blockade against the USSR; the bomb, Marshall Aid and the Deutschmark against the USA). Then decide, explaining why one side's case is stronger.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2: describe and explain"}
For "Describe the Berlin Blockade", give dates, the airlift and the outcome. For "Explain the results", link it to two German states, NATO and the Warsaw Pact.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Germany and Berlin were divided into four zones; the allies disagreed over reparations and Germany's recovery.
- Bizonia, Marshall Aid and the new Deutschmark convinced Stalin that the West was building a separate German state.
- The blockade (24 June 1948-12 May 1949) was defeated by the airlift, a victory for the West without war.
- Results: two German states (1949), NATO (1949) and later the Warsaw Pact (1955).
- Traditional historians blame the USSR, revisionists the USA, post-revisionists both; the debate continues.
:::
