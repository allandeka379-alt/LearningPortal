## Introduction

For more than forty years after the Second World War the world lived under the shadow of a conflict between the **United States** and the **Soviet Union**. The two superpowers never fought each other directly in a full-scale war, which is why it is called the **Cold War**. But they competed everywhere: in weapons, in space, in propaganda, and in "hot" wars fought by others.

Southern Africa felt this directly. In the liberation struggle, ZAPU and its army ZIPRA received weapons and training mainly from the Soviet Union, while ZANU and ZANLA were helped mainly by China; Ian Smith's government claimed to be defending the West against communism. In Angola, Cuban troops supported by the USSR fought for the government against UNITA, which was backed by South Africa and, at times, the USA. Understanding the Cold War helps to explain why wars in Africa lasted so long.

You should already know why the wartime alliance broke down after 1945 and how Europe was divided by 1949.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cold War"}
A state of hostility between the USA and its allies and the USSR and its allies, from the late 1940s to 1991, waged by every means short of direct full-scale war between the superpowers.
:::

:::definition{term="Proxy war"}
A war in which the superpowers support opposing sides with money, weapons or advisers instead of fighting each other directly.
:::

:::definition{term="Arms race"}
Competition to build more and better weapons, especially nuclear weapons and the missiles to deliver them.
:::

:::definition{term="Deterrence"}
Preventing an attack by threatening massive retaliation. With nuclear weapons this became "mutually assured destruction" (MAD): any nuclear war would destroy both sides.
:::

:::definition{term="Détente"}
An easing of tension between the superpowers, especially in the 1970s.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1949: NATO formed (April); first Soviet atomic test (August).
- 1955: Warsaw Pact formed.
- 4 October 1957: Sputnik, the first artificial satellite.
- August 1961: Berlin Wall built. October 1962: Cuban Missile Crisis.
- 9 November 1989: Berlin Wall opened. December 1991: USSR dissolved.
:::

## Manifestations of the Cold War

### Alliances

| Alliance | Year | Members (main) |
|---|---|---|
| NATO (North Atlantic Treaty Organisation) | 1949 | USA, Canada, Britain, France and other western European states |
| Comecon (economic) | 1949 | USSR and the communist states of eastern Europe |
| SEATO | 1954 | USA, Britain, France, Australia and others in South-East Asia |
| Warsaw Pact | 1955 | USSR and the communist states of eastern Europe |

![Chart of the two Cold War blocs divided by the Iron Curtain: NATO (1949), SEATO (1954), Marshall Aid and West Germany against the Warsaw Pact (1955), Comecon (1949), the Cominform and East Germany.](media:diagrams/cold-war-blocs.png "Figure: the western and eastern blocs and the Iron Curtain")

### The arms race

The USA used atomic bombs in 1945; the USSR tested its own in **1949**. Both then developed hydrogen bombs in the early 1950s and intercontinental ballistic missiles later in the decade. By the 1960s each side could destroy the other many times over. Fear of nuclear war led to arms-control agreements: the **Partial Test Ban Treaty** (1963), the **Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty** (1968) and **SALT I** (1972).

### The space race

The USSR launched **Sputnik**, the first artificial satellite, on 4 October 1957, and **Yuri Gagarin** became the first person in space on 12 April 1961. The USA answered with the Apollo programme, and on 20 July 1969 American astronauts landed on the Moon. Space success was used as proof that each system was superior, and rockets that could reach space could also carry nuclear warheads.

### Propaganda and espionage

Both sides used radio stations, films, posters and newspapers to praise their own system and attack the other. Spies on both sides stole military secrets. In the USA, fear of communism led to the anti-communist campaigns of the early 1950s associated with Senator Joseph McCarthy. Even sport became a battleground: the USA and many allies boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympics, and the USSR and most of its allies boycotted the 1984 Los Angeles Games.

### Crises

- **Berlin:** the Soviet blockade of West Berlin (1948-1949) and the building of the **Berlin Wall** (August 1961).
- **Cuba:** the **Cuban Missile Crisis** (October 1962) brought the world closest to nuclear war.
- **Eastern Europe:** Soviet forces crushed the Hungarian rising (1956) and the Prague Spring in Czechoslovakia (1968).

### Proxy wars

| War | Dates | Superpower involvement |
|---|---|---|
| Korea | 1950-1953 | US-led UN forces for the South; China and Soviet aid for the North |
| Vietnam | US combat troops 1965-1973; war ended 1975 | USA backed the South; USSR and China aided the North |
| Angola | Civil war from 1975 | USSR and Cuba backed the MPLA government; the USA and South Africa backed UNITA |
| Afghanistan | 1979-1989 | Soviet invasion; the USA armed the Afghan resistance |

:::example{title="A case explained step by step: how the Cold War shaped Angola"}
1. **Independence (1975):** when Portuguese rule collapsed, three movements fought for power: the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA.
2. **Outside support:** the MPLA, which held the capital, received Soviet weapons and tens of thousands of Cuban troops; UNITA received help from South Africa and, at times, the USA.
3. **Prolonged war:** with outside supplies, neither side could win, and the war killed and displaced very large numbers of Angolans.
4. **Settlement:** as Cold War tensions eased, agreements in December 1988 linked the withdrawal of Cuban troops from Angola to independence for Namibia, which followed in 1990.

This shows how the Cold War turned local struggles into international conflicts, and how its end helped to settle them.
:::

## Impact of the Cold War on the world

- **Fear:** generations grew up under the threat of nuclear war.
- **Division:** Germany and Europe were split for more than forty years; Korea remains divided.
- **Cost:** huge sums were spent on weapons instead of development.
- **The United Nations:** the veto in the Security Council often blocked action when the superpowers disagreed.
- **The Non-Aligned Movement** (1961) was formed by states, many of them newly independent, that refused to join either bloc.

### Impact on Africa

- Rival liberation movements and governments were armed by different sides, for example in Zimbabwe, Angola and Mozambique.
- The superpowers supported friendly rulers regardless of how they governed; the USA backed Mobutu in Zaire, for example, because he was anti-communist.
- The white minority governments of Rhodesia and South Africa used anti-communism to seek Western sympathy.
- The Congo crisis (1960-1965) and the Ogaden War between Ethiopia and Somalia (1977-1978) drew in the superpowers.
- The end of the Cold War helped to bring independence to Namibia (1990) and encouraged negotiations to end apartheid.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: did the Cold War help or harm Africa?"}
**Help:** liberation movements gained weapons, training and scholarships from the East, and Western powers sometimes pushed for majority rule to keep newly independent states friendly.
**Harm:** wars were prolonged, weapons spread, dictators were propped up, and debt grew.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that the harm outweighed the help, but African movements were not simply puppets: they chose their allies for their own aims.
:::

## The end of the Cold War

After a period of détente in the 1970s, tension rose again with the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979). From 1985 the new Soviet leader, **Mikhail Gorbachev**, introduced reforms (*glasnost*, openness, and *perestroika*, restructuring) and signed arms agreements with the USA, including the INF Treaty (1987). In 1989 communist governments collapsed across eastern Europe, and the **Berlin Wall** was opened on 9 November 1989. Germany was reunited in October 1990, and the **USSR dissolved** in December 1991.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the superpowers fought each other directly"}
They never fought a full-scale war against each other; that is what "cold" means. They fought through proxies, crises and competition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing manifestations without examples"}
Each manifestation needs a named example with a date: NATO (1949), Sputnik (1957), the Berlin Wall (1961), Korea (1950-1953).
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring Africa"}
ZIMSEC asks about the impact on the world. Always include African examples such as Angola, the Congo or the liberation struggle in Zimbabwe.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2: describe the manifestations"}
Use headings: alliances, arms race, space race, propaganda, crises and proxy wars. Give one or two examples for each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate the impact"}
For a "how far" part, weigh harmful effects (wars, cost, fear, division) against effects some see as positive (no world war between the superpowers, space science, support for liberation movements), and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Cold War was a conflict between the USA and the USSR, from the late 1940s to 1991, without direct war between them.
- It showed itself in rival alliances (NATO, Warsaw Pact), the arms and space races, propaganda, crises (Berlin, Cuba) and proxy wars (Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan).
- It spread fear, divided Europe, cost vast sums and often paralysed the UN.
- In Africa it armed rival movements, prolonged wars and propped up dictators, but also supplied liberation movements.
- It ended with Gorbachev's reforms, the opening of the Berlin Wall (1989) and the dissolution of the USSR (1991).
:::
