## Introduction

When the guns fell silent on 11 November 1918, the victorious Allies had to decide what to do with defeated Germany and its partners. In January 1919 delegates from more than thirty countries met in Paris for the **Paris Peace Conference**. The decisions about Germany were taken mainly by three men: President **Woodrow Wilson** of the USA, Prime Minister **Georges Clemenceau** of France and Prime Minister **David Lloyd George** of Britain. Together they are called the **Big Three**.

The settlement mattered far beyond Europe. Germany's colonies, including German East Africa (later Tanganyika, part of today's Tanzania) and South West Africa (today's Namibia), were handed to the victors to run as mandates. Africans who had fought and carried supplies in the war were not consulted at all.

You should already know how the First World War ended. This lesson is about the **aims** of the peacemakers. The terms of the Treaty of Versailles and its effects on Germany are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Paris Peace Conference"}
The conference of the victorious Allies that opened in Paris on 18 January 1919 and drew up the peace treaties after the First World War. ZIMSEC calls the whole settlement the Paris Peace Conference; Cambridge focuses on the Treaty of Versailles with Germany.
:::

:::definition{term="Fourteen Points"}
President Wilson's programme for a just peace, announced to the US Congress on 8 January 1918. It included open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament, self-determination and a League of Nations.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-determination"}
The idea that each nationality should be able to decide how it is governed, usually by forming its own state.
:::

:::definition{term="Reparations"}
Payments demanded from a defeated country to compensate the victors for war damage.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The Big Three"}
- **Woodrow Wilson**: President of the United States (Democrat).
- **Georges Clemenceau**: Prime Minister of France, nicknamed "the Tiger".
- **David Lloyd George**: Prime Minister of Britain.
- Vittorio Orlando, Prime Minister of Italy, made the "Big Four" but left the conference for a time in April 1919 when Italy was refused the port of Fiume.
:::

## The aims of the Big Three

### Wilson: a just and lasting peace

The USA had entered the war late (April 1917) and its territory had not been attacked by German armies. Wilson was an idealist who believed that wars were caused by secret alliances, arms races and nations ruled against their will. His **Fourteen Points** proposed open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament, self-determination for the peoples of Europe and, above all, a **League of Nations** to settle disputes peacefully. He wanted Germany punished, but not so harshly that it would seek revenge.

### Clemenceau: security for France

France had suffered more than any other western Ally. Historians estimate that about 1.3-1.4 million French soldiers were killed, and the war on the Western Front had wrecked towns, mines and farmland in the north-east. Germany had invaded France twice in Clemenceau's lifetime, in 1870 and 1914. He therefore wanted:

- a Germany too weak ever to attack France again (a small army, no forces on the Rhine);
- the return of **Alsace-Lorraine**, taken by Germany in 1871;
- large **reparations** to rebuild France;
- ideally, the Rhineland separated from Germany as a buffer state.

### Lloyd George: a middle way

Lloyd George faced pressure at home. In the general election of December 1918 many voters and newspapers demanded that Germany should be made to pay, and he promised a tough peace. Privately he was more cautious. Britain wanted to keep its naval supremacy and its empire, so it welcomed the reduction of the German navy and the loss of Germany's colonies. But Germany had been one of Britain's most important trading partners before the war, and a ruined Germany could not buy British goods. He also feared that a harsh peace would cause bitterness and might push Germany towards communism, as had happened in Russia. In March 1919 he set out these worries in a document known as the **Fontainebleau Memorandum**.

| Leader | Main aims | Attitude to Germany |
|---|---|---|
| Wilson (USA) | League of Nations, self-determination, disarmament, free trade | Firm but fair; avoid revenge |
| Clemenceau (France) | Security, Alsace-Lorraine, reparations, weak German army | Harsh: cripple Germany |
| Lloyd George (Britain) | Naval supremacy, empire, trade, stability | Punish, but let Germany recover |

## Disagreements and compromises

The three leaders clashed often. Clemenceau thought Wilson's ideas unrealistic; Wilson found French demands vengeful; Lloyd George tried to stand between them while protecting British interests. The final settlement was a series of compromises.

:::example{title="A compromise explained step by step: the Rhineland"}
1. **French demand:** Clemenceau wanted the Rhineland, the German land west of the River Rhine, turned into a separate state so that German armies could never again assemble on France's border.
2. **Objection:** Wilson and Lloyd George argued that this would break the principle of self-determination, because the people there were German, and would create a grievance for the future.
3. **Compromise:** the Rhineland stayed German but was **demilitarised**, and Allied troops would occupy it for fifteen years. Britain and the USA also offered France a treaty of guarantee to defend it if attacked.
4. **Outcome:** the US Senate never approved the guarantee, so Britain's offer lapsed as well. France was left feeling insecure, which explains its harsh attitude to Germany in the early 1920s.
:::

Other compromises:

- **Reparations:** the leaders could not agree a figure, so a Reparations Commission was set up; it fixed the sum in 1921.
- **Colonies:** Germany's colonies were not annexed outright but became **mandates** of the League, run mainly by Britain and France. This kept Wilson's principle on paper, while Britain and France gained territory in practice.
- **The Saar:** France took the coalfield's output for fifteen years, but the region was run by the League, with a plebiscite promised at the end.

## Why the victors did not get everything they wanted

- **Their aims conflicted.** A peace harsh enough for France was too harsh for Wilson; a peace mild enough for Wilson left France insecure.
- **Public opinion.** French and British voters wanted revenge and compensation; leaders who ignored them risked losing office.
- **Wilson's weakness at home.** His Republican opponents won control of Congress in November 1918. The Senate later rejected the treaty, so the USA never joined the League Wilson had designed.
- **Self-determination was hard to apply.** Peoples were mixed together, so new borders always left minorities on the wrong side, and the principle was not applied to Germans who wished to join Austria or to colonial peoples in Africa and Asia.
- **Time and pressure.** The leaders had many treaties to make, revolutions breaking out across central Europe and a fear that delay would spread communism.

## Contemporary opinions: could the settlement be justified?

At the time opinions divided sharply. Many Germans called the treaty a **Diktat**, a dictated peace, because Germany was not allowed to negotiate. In Britain the economist **John Maynard Keynes**, who had been an adviser at the conference, attacked the reparations in his book *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), arguing that they would damage the whole European economy. In France many thought the treaty too **lenient**: Marshal Foch is reported to have said that it was not a peace but an armistice for twenty years.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: was the settlement too harsh?"}
**For "too harsh":** Germany lost territory, its colonies and much of its army, had to accept blame for the war and was not allowed to negotiate. Keynes argued that reparations would ruin Germany and harm all of Europe.

**Against:** the leaders had to satisfy angry voters after a war that killed millions. Germany itself had imposed the very harsh Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on Russia in March 1918. Germany kept most of its land and industry and was soon a great power again.

**Judgement:** historians still disagree. A useful approach is to judge the settlement by what was possible in 1919 rather than by what happened in the 1930s.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the leaders' aims"}
It was Clemenceau, not Lloyd George, who wanted to cripple Germany. Lloyd George publicly promised to make Germany pay but privately wanted a peace that let Germany recover and trade.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the USA joined the League"}
Wilson proposed the League, but the US Senate rejected the treaty, so the USA never joined.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the terms instead of the aims"}
A question on aims asks what each leader wanted and why. List the terms only to show how far each leader succeeded.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain why the victors did not get all they wanted"}
In a Cambridge Paper 1 (b) or a ZIMSEC Paper 2 (b) answer, take each leader in turn: state an aim, show the compromise, and explain the reason (another leader's opposition, public opinion, the US Senate).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How far could the settlement be justified?"}
For a "how far" question, give arguments for and against using contemporary views (German, French, British and American), then reach a judgement. Avoid using hindsight about Hitler as your only argument.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Big Three were Wilson (USA), Clemenceau (France) and Lloyd George (Britain); the conference opened on 18 January 1919.
- Wilson wanted a fair peace based on the Fourteen Points and a League of Nations.
- Clemenceau wanted security, Alsace-Lorraine and reparations; Lloyd George wanted to punish Germany but let it recover and trade.
- Conflicting aims, public opinion and the US Senate meant compromises such as the demilitarised Rhineland, mandates and a Reparations Commission.
- Contemporaries disagreed: Germans saw a Diktat, Keynes thought reparations unwise, many French thought the treaty too lenient.
:::
