## Introduction

On 28 June 1919, exactly five years after the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, German representatives signed the **Treaty of Versailles** in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles near Paris. It was the most important of the treaties that ended the First World War, and for twenty years Germans argued that it was unjust.

The treaty also redrew the map of Africa. Germany lost every colony it held on the continent. Tanganyika went to Britain, Ruanda-Urundi to Belgium, South West Africa (today's Namibia) to South Africa, and Togoland and Kamerun were divided between Britain and France. The new rulers governed them as **mandates** under the League of Nations.

You should already know the aims of Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George. This lesson covers the terms, how Germans reacted, and the impact on Germany to the end of 1923. The Weimar Republic's crises are studied in depth in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diktat"}
A dictated peace. Germans used the word because Germany was not allowed to negotiate the terms, only to sign them.
:::

:::definition{term="War guilt clause (Article 231)"}
The article in which Germany accepted responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war. It was the legal basis for reparations.
:::

:::definition{term="Demilitarised zone"}
An area where no troops, weapons or fortifications are allowed. The Rhineland became one.
:::

:::definition{term="Mandate"}
A former colony of a defeated power, governed by one of the victors on behalf of the League of Nations, in theory until it was ready for self-government.
:::

:::definition{term="Hyperinflation"}
Extremely rapid price rises that destroy the value of money, as in Germany in 1923.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 7 May 1919: terms handed to Germany.
- 21 June 1919: German fleet scuttled at Scapa Flow.
- 28 June 1919: treaty signed.
- 10 January 1920: treaty comes into force.
- 1921: reparations fixed at 132 billion gold marks (£6,600 million).
- January 1923: French and Belgian troops occupy the Ruhr.
:::

## The terms of the Treaty of Versailles

A useful way to remember the terms is under four headings: **land, army, money, blame** (with the League as a fifth).

### Land

- **Alsace-Lorraine** returned to France.
- **Eupen and Malmedy** went to Belgium; **North Schleswig** to Denmark after a plebiscite (1920).
- **West Prussia and Posen** went to the new Poland, creating the **Polish Corridor**, which cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany.
- **Danzig** became a free city run by the League. **Memel** was placed under Allied control.
- The **Saar** was run by the League for fifteen years, with France taking its coal, and then a plebiscite.
- **Upper Silesia** held a plebiscite in 1921, after which part of its industrial land went to Poland.
- Union (**Anschluss**) with Austria was forbidden.
- All German **colonies** became League mandates.

Germany lost about 13 per cent of its European territory and roughly 10 per cent of its population (estimates vary slightly), together with important coal and iron resources.

### Army

- The army was limited to **100,000** men, and conscription was banned.
- No tanks, submarines or military aircraft were allowed.
- The navy was limited to six battleships.
- The **Rhineland** was demilitarised and occupied by Allied troops for fifteen years.

### Money and blame

- **Article 231**: Germany accepted guilt for the war.
- **Reparations**: the total was fixed by a Reparations Commission in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks (£6,600 million).

### The League

The League's Covenant formed the first part of the treaty, but Germany was not allowed to join at first (it joined in 1926).

## The other treaties in outline

| Treaty | Country | Main terms |
|---|---|---|
| Saint-Germain (September 1919) | Austria | Lost land to Italy, Czechoslovakia, Poland and Yugoslavia; army of 30,000; union with Germany forbidden |
| Neuilly (November 1919) | Bulgaria | Lost land to Greece (and its Aegean coast), Yugoslavia and Romania; army of 20,000; reparations |
| Trianon (June 1920) | Hungary | Lost about two-thirds of its territory; army of 35,000 |
| Sèvres (August 1920) | Ottoman Turkey | Lost its Arab lands as mandates; straits put under international control; later replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) |

The treaties broke up the Austro-Hungarian Empire and created or enlarged states such as Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and Poland, but each new state contained minorities, such as the three million Germans of the Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia.

## German reactions

Germans were shocked. Many had not seen Allied troops on German soil and believed that the army had not really been beaten. The government had expected a peace based on the Fourteen Points. Instead:

- Chancellor Philipp Scheidemann resigned rather than sign.
- German sailors scuttled their interned fleet at Scapa Flow rather than hand it over.
- Newspapers and politicians called the treaty a **Diktat** and attacked the war guilt clause as a national humiliation.
- Nationalists blamed the politicians who had accepted the armistice and treaty, calling them the "November criminals", and spread the myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back".

The government signed only because the Allies threatened to resume the war.

## Impact on Germany to the end of 1923

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 28 June 1919 | Treaty signed |
| March 1920 | Kapp Putsch in Berlin |
| August 1921 | Matthias Erzberger, who signed the armistice, assassinated |
| June 1922 | Foreign Minister Walther Rathenau assassinated |
| January 1923 | France and Belgium occupy the Ruhr |
| November 1923 | Hyperinflation peaks; Munich Putsch fails |

### Political impact

The treaty made the new **Weimar Republic** unpopular from birth because its leaders had signed it. In March 1920 Freikorps units, angry that the army cuts would disband them, joined Wolfgang Kapp's attempt to seize Berlin; it collapsed when workers went on a general strike. Right-wing extremists murdered politicians linked to the armistice and the policy of fulfilment, such as Erzberger and Rathenau.

### Economic and social impact

Germany lost coal from the Saar and Upper Silesia and the iron ore of Lorraine, which weakened industry. When Germany failed to deliver reparations in kind at the end of 1922, French and Belgian troops occupied the **Ruhr**, Germany's main industrial region, in January 1923. The government ordered passive resistance and printed money to pay the striking workers. Prices soared; by November 1923 a loaf of bread cost billions of marks. People with savings, pensions or fixed incomes, many of them middle class, were ruined, while some debtors and businessmen gained.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: from reparations to hyperinflation"}
1. **Reparations** placed a heavy burden on a German economy already weakened by war debts.
2. Germany fell behind with deliveries at the end of 1922.
3. France and Belgium **occupied the Ruhr** in January 1923 to take goods directly.
4. German workers went on **passive resistance**, so production fell, and the government printed money to support them.
5. More money chasing fewer goods caused **hyperinflation**.

Note that the treaty was not the only cause: the government had financed the war by borrowing and printing money, and inflation had begun before 1919.
:::

## Was the treaty fair?

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation"}
**Unfair to Germany:** it was dictated, not negotiated; the war guilt clause blamed Germany alone; self-determination was denied to Germans in Austria, the Sudetenland and the Polish Corridor; and reparations helped to cause economic crisis.

**Fair or even lenient:** Germany had imposed a harsher treaty on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in 1918; Germany kept most of its industry and population; France had suffered enormous damage; and reparations were later reduced (Dawes Plan 1924, Young Plan 1929).

**Judgement:** historians disagree. Many now argue that the treaty was neither as crushing as Germans claimed nor strong enough to keep Germany weak.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming the treaty alone for hyperinflation"}
Wartime borrowing and money printing had already weakened the mark. The Ruhr occupation and passive resistance turned high inflation into hyperinflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the treaties"}
Versailles dealt only with Germany. Austria (Saint-Germain), Hungary (Trianon), Bulgaria (Neuilly) and Turkey (Sèvres) had their own treaties.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong numbers"}
The army limit was 100,000 men and the navy six battleships. The reparations figure was not in the 1919 treaty; it was set in 1921.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the terms (Paper 1 (a), ZIMSEC (a) and (b))"}
Use the four headings: land, army, money, blame. Give precise details, such as "army limited to 100,000 men", rather than "the army was reduced".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the impact to 1923"}
Link each term to a result: army cuts led to the Kapp Putsch; signing it led to political murders; reparations led to the Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation. Stop at the end of 1923; Stresemann's recovery belongs to another topic.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 and dictated to Germany without negotiation.
- Germany lost land (Alsace-Lorraine, the Polish Corridor), all its colonies, most of its armed forces and accepted war guilt and reparations.
- Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly and Sèvres dealt with Germany's allies.
- Germans called the treaty a Diktat; it weakened the Weimar Republic and fed extremism.
- Reparations and the Ruhr occupation helped to cause the hyperinflation of 1923; historians still debate whether the treaty was fair.
:::
