## Introduction

Look at a map of Africa and you will see borders drawn with rulers: straight lines across deserts and savanna, and rivers turned into frontiers that split peoples apart. Zimbabwe's own borders with Mozambique, Zambia, Botswana and South Africa were fixed by European agreements in the late nineteenth century, without asking a single African ruler. In about twenty-five years, from around 1880 to the early 1900s, European powers divided almost the whole continent among themselves. This is the **Scramble for Africa**.

This lesson looks at the scramble in southern Africa: who took part, why it happened, what the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 decided and what partition meant for Africans. You should already know about missionaries and other agents of colonisation; the next lesson shows how the scramble reached Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Scramble for Africa"}
The rapid competition between European powers to claim and occupy African territory, mainly between about 1880 and 1914.
:::

:::definition{term="Partition"}
The division of Africa into colonies and spheres of influence by agreements between European powers.
:::

:::definition{term="Sphere of influence"}
An area in which one power claimed the sole right to make treaties and trade, which other powers agreed to respect, even before it was occupied.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective occupation"}
The Berlin Conference principle that a claim to territory (on the coast) was valid only if the power actually controlled it, with officials or forces able to keep order and protect trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Protectorate"}
A territory whose rulers kept some authority but were placed under the "protection" and control of a European power.
:::

## Who took part in southern Africa

- **Britain**: held the Cape Colony and Natal; under Cecil Rhodes, British interests pushed north.
- **Portugal**: held Mozambique and Angola on the coasts and hoped to join them across the interior.
- **Germany**: claimed South West Africa (now Namibia) in 1884 and German East Africa (now mainland Tanzania).
- **The Boer republics**: the South African Republic (Transvaal) and the Orange Free State, which wanted land and access to the sea and looked north to the land beyond the Limpopo.
- **Belgium's King Leopold II** in the Congo, and **France** further north, were also part of the wider scramble.

## Causes of the scramble

### Economic causes
- **Raw materials** for European industry: minerals, rubber, cotton, palm oil.
- **Markets** for manufactured goods and places to **invest** surplus capital.
- **Mineral discoveries** in southern Africa: diamonds at Kimberley (1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) raised hopes of a "second Rand" further north.

### Political causes
- **National prestige**: colonies were a sign of power, especially for newly united Germany and Italy.
- **Rivalry and the balance of power**: each power feared that others would gain an advantage.
- **Nationalism** and public enthusiasm for empire.

### Strategic causes
- Protecting **sea routes**: the Cape route to India, and the Suez Canal (opened 1869); Britain occupied Egypt in 1882.
- Blocking rivals: Britain wanted to stop the Boers, Germans and Portuguese from linking up across the interior.

### Social causes
- Settling **surplus population** and the unemployed in colonies.
- Belief in European racial superiority and a so-called **civilising mission**.

### Religious and humanitarian causes
- **Missionaries** called for European protection and the spread of Christianity.
- Ending the **slave trade**, which was still active in parts of east and central Africa.

### Means that made it possible
- **Quinine** to protect Europeans against malaria; **steamships**, railways and the telegraph; and weapons such as the **Maxim gun**, which gave European forces overwhelming firepower.

:::example{title="Explaining one cause step by step: the 'second Rand'"}
1. Gold was found on the Witwatersrand in 1886, making the Transvaal suddenly rich.
2. Many people in Britain and the Cape believed that equally rich goldfields lay north of the Limpopo, an idea encouraged by earlier gold reports such as Karl Mauch's.
3. Companies and individuals, above all Cecil Rhodes, raced to secure mining rights there before the Boers, Portuguese or Germans.
4. This economic hope became a political race to control the land between the Limpopo and the Zambezi.
:::

## The Berlin Conference, 1884–85

The conference met in **Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885**. It was hosted by the German chancellor **Otto von Bismarck**.

### Why it was called
- **Rivalry over the Congo**: Leopold II, France and Portugal all made claims around the Congo River; Britain had backed Portugal's claim to the mouth of the Congo.
- To **avoid war** between European powers over African territory.
- To agree **rules** for future claims and to secure **free trade** in the Congo and Niger basins.
- Germany's new colonial ambitions gave Bismarck a reason to host and shape the settlement.

### Who attended
Fourteen states: **Germany, Britain, France, Portugal, Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Italy, Austria-Hungary, Russia, Denmark, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire and the United States**. **No African ruler or representative was invited.**

### Main resolutions
1. **Effective occupation**: claims to territory on the coast had to be backed by real control.
2. **Notification**: a power taking new territory had to inform the other powers.
3. **Spheres of influence** behind claimed coasts came to be accepted in practice.
4. **Free navigation and free trade** on the Congo and Niger rivers and in the Congo basin.
5. **Suppression of the slave trade**, and promises to protect missionaries and to improve the "moral and material well-being" of Africans.
6. Leopold II's claim to the Congo was recognised by the powers, and it became his personal **Congo Free State** in 1885.

The conference did not draw the map of Africa itself; it set the rules by which the powers then divided the continent in many separate treaties.

## Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1867 | Diamonds found near Kimberley |
| 1884 | Germany claims South West Africa |
| November 1884 – February 1885 | Berlin Conference |
| 1885 | Bechuanaland becomes a British protectorate |
| 1886 | Gold found on the Witwatersrand |
| 1889 | Royal Charter granted to Rhodes's British South Africa Company |
| 1890 | British Ultimatum forces Portugal to give up claims to the land between Angola and Mozambique |
| 1891 | Anglo-Portuguese Treaty fixes the border between Mozambique and British territory |

Portugal's plan to link Angola and Mozambique, shown on the so-called "rose-coloured map", collided with Rhodes's ambition of British territory from the Cape to Cairo. In January 1890 Britain issued an **ultimatum** demanding that Portugal withdraw, and Portugal gave way; the 1891 treaty fixed the eastern border of what became Southern Rhodesia.

## Effects of partition

**On Africans**
- **Loss of independence and sovereignty**: African states became colonies or protectorates.
- **Arbitrary borders** split peoples between colonies (for example the Shona-speaking Ndau, divided between Southern Rhodesia and Mozambique) and joined rival groups together.
- **Loss of land** to settlers and companies, and forced labour and taxation.
- **Resistance and wars** of conquest, such as the Anglo-Ndebele War and the risings of 1896–97 in Zimbabwe.
- **Economic change**: economies reorganised to produce raw materials for Europe, with mines, plantations and railways.
- Spread of Western education, Christianity and medicine, much of it through missions.

**On Europe**
- Rivalries over Africa added to the tensions between the European powers before 1914, although war over Africa itself was avoided.

:::example{title="Weighing the significance of the Berlin Conference"}
**Claim:** "The Berlin Conference caused the partition of Africa."

1. **For:** it set the rules (effective occupation, notification) that encouraged powers to rush to occupy land.
2. **Against:** the scramble had already begun (Egypt 1882, South West Africa 1884), and the borders were drawn later in separate treaties, such as the Anglo-Portuguese Treaty of 1891.
3. **Judgement:** the conference speeded up and regulated a scramble that was already under way; it did not start it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="'Africa was divided at Berlin'"}
The conference agreed rules; most borders were drawn afterwards in bilateral treaties.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the Boer republics"}
In southern Africa, the Transvaal and Orange Free State were also players in the scramble.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that Africans were excluded"}
No African state was represented at Berlin. Say this clearly in answers on the conference.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Britain, Portugal, Germany and the Boer republics competed for southern Africa.
- Causes were economic (raw materials, markets, minerals), political (prestige, rivalry), strategic (sea routes), social (surplus population, racial ideas) and religious (missions, ending the slave trade).
- The Berlin Conference (November 1884 – February 1885), hosted by Bismarck, involved fourteen states and no Africans.
- Its resolutions included effective occupation, notification, free navigation and suppression of the slave trade.
- Partition brought loss of independence, arbitrary borders, land loss and resistance.
:::
