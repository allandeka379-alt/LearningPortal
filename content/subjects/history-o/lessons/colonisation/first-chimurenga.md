## Introduction

In March 1896 the Ndebele rose against the British South Africa Company (BSAC), and in June many Shona communities joined the struggle. For more than a year settler rule was in real danger: historians estimate that roughly a tenth of the settler population was killed. Zimbabweans call these risings the **First Chimurenga** (Shona) or **First Umvukela** (Ndebele), and the nationalists of the 1960s and 1970s saw their own struggle as the Second Chimurenga.

You should already know how the Company occupied Mashonaland in 1890 and conquered Matabeleland in 1893-94. Examiners ask you to **outline the causes**, **describe the course**, **examine the role of chiefs and spirit mediums** and **analyse the results**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chimurenga / Umvukela"}
Shona and Ndebele words for a rising or struggle. The First Chimurenga/Umvukela is the armed resistance of 1896-97 against Company rule.
:::

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro)"}
A person through whom, Shona people believe, the spirit of an ancestor or guardian spirit speaks. Mediums such as Nehanda and Kaguvi had great religious and political influence.
:::

:::definition{term="Rinderpest"}
A deadly cattle disease that swept through southern Africa in 1896, killing very large numbers of cattle.
:::

:::definition{term="Hut tax"}
A tax charged on every hut, introduced by the Company in 1894, which forced Africans to earn money, usually by working for settlers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="People and dates to learn"}
- March 1896: the rising begins in Matabeleland.
- June 1896: the rising spreads to Mashonaland.
- Religious leaders: **Mkwati** (linked to the Mwari shrines of the Matopo hills), **Kaguvi** (Gumboreshumba) and **Nehanda** (Charwe Nyakasikana).
- August-October 1896: Rhodes's indabas with Ndebele leaders in the Matopo hills.
- April 1898: Nehanda and Kaguvi are executed in Salisbury.
:::

## Causes of the risings

### Long-term grievances

- **Land:** settlers took the best land. After 1893 many Ndebele were expected to move to the poor Gwaai and Shangani reserves, and in Mashonaland settler farms spread along the main roads.
- **Cattle:** the Company and settlers seized most Ndebele cattle after the war of 1893-94; cattle were wealth, food and the basis of marriage payments.
- **Forced labour:** Native Commissioners and chiefs were ordered to supply workers for farms, mines and roads, often with violence.
- **Hut tax:** introduced in 1894 and often collected brutally.
- **The Native Police:** many were young Ndebele men who abused their power, beat people and seized women and property.
- **Loss of authority:** chiefs and Ndebele indunas lost power to Company officials; the Ndebele had lost their king.

### Short-term triggers

In 1896 **rinderpest** killed most of the remaining cattle, and the Company shot herds to try to stop its spread. **Drought** and **locusts** destroyed crops. Many people saw these disasters as signs that the settlers had angered the ancestors and must be driven out.

### The opportunity

In December 1895 most of the Company's police were taken away by Jameson for the **Jameson Raid** on the Transvaal. Its failure early in January 1896 showed that the settlers were not invincible and left the country lightly defended.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: the Jameson Raid"}
1. **Fact:** Jameson took most of the Company's armed police to invade the Transvaal (December 1895).
2. **Effect in Zimbabwe:** few armed men were left to defend the settlers.
3. **Effect on African thinking:** the raiders were defeated and captured, showing that the Company could be beaten.
4. **Link to the rising:** when the Ndebele rose in March 1896 the settlers could not respond quickly, so the rising spread rapidly. The raid did not cause the grievances; it provided the opportunity.
:::

## The course of the risings

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| December 1895-January 1896 | Jameson Raid; most Company police leave the country |
| March 1896 | Rising begins in Matabeleland; settlers flee to laagers in Bulawayo and other centres |
| June 1896 | Rising spreads to Mashonaland, including the Mazowe area and the lands of Mashayamombe and Makoni |
| August-October 1896 | Rhodes holds indabas with Ndebele leaders in the Matopo hills |
| 1897 | Shona communities fight on from fortified hills and caves until they are overcome one by one |
| April 1898 | Execution of Nehanda and Kaguvi |

**Matabeleland.** Fighting began in late March 1896. Settlers in outlying farms and mines were killed and the survivors gathered in laagers in Bulawayo, Gwelo and other centres. Ndebele forces besieged Bulawayo but did not take it. Reinforcements from South Africa and Britain relieved the town and drove the Ndebele into the **Matopo hills**, where they could not easily be defeated. Many regiments fought under leaders loyal to the old royal house, and Lobengula's son Nyamanda was proclaimed king by some.

**Mashonaland.** From June 1896 the rising spread through central Mashonaland, surprising settlers who had believed the Shona would not fight. Leaders included chiefs such as **Mashayamombe** and **Makoni**, encouraged by **Kaguvi** and **Nehanda**. The Shona fought from fortified hills and caves; Company forces attacked them one by one, sometimes using dynamite on the caves.

**The end.** Because the war in the Matopos was costly, Rhodes went unarmed into the hills in August 1896 to negotiate with Ndebele leaders. In a series of indabas he promised to address grievances, and most Ndebele fighters laid down their arms by late 1896. In Mashonaland there was no single negotiated end; resistance was crushed area by area during 1897. Makoni was executed in 1897 and Mashayamombe was killed in the same year. Nehanda and Kaguvi were captured, tried and executed in **April 1898**.

## The role of chiefs and spirit mediums

Chiefs and indunas led their people into battle, decided whether to join the rising and supplied fighters and food. Some chiefs stayed neutral or helped the settlers, which shaped where the fighting happened.

Religious leaders gave the risings spiritual authority. **Mkwati**, linked to the Mwari shrines of the Matopos, encouraged the Ndebele and is said to have helped carry the call to rise into Mashonaland. **Kaguvi** and **Nehanda** urged Shona communities to fight, presenting the rising as the will of the ancestors and the way to drive out the settlers and the disasters linked to them. Their messages helped many separate communities act at about the same time.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: were the risings coordinated?"}
Historians disagree about how far the risings were planned together. In the 1960s Terence Ranger argued that spirit mediums and the Mwari cult linked Ndebele and Shona leaders in a coordinated movement. Later historians such as Julian Cobbing and David Beach questioned this, pointing out that many communities rose for local reasons, that leaders often acted separately and that the evidence for a central plan is thin. A good answer explains both views: religious leaders clearly spread the call and gave it authority, but the risings were also shaped by local grievances and decisions. Let the evidence lead your judgement.
:::

## Reasons for defeat

- **Weapons:** machine guns, modern rifles and later dynamite against older firearms and spears.
- **Reinforcements** of British and South African troops arrived, and the railway from the south was approaching Bulawayo.
- **Lack of unity:** Ndebele and Shona fought largely separately; some communities stayed neutral or supported the settlers.
- **Defensive tactics:** fortified hills and caves could be besieged and starved out.
- **Hunger:** drought, locusts, rinderpest and scorched-earth tactics caused famine.
- **Negotiation:** the Matopos indabas ended most Ndebele fighting, allowing the Company to concentrate on Mashonaland.

## Results

- Many thousands of Africans died in fighting, executions and the famine that followed; the settlers also suffered heavy losses.
- Leaders such as Nehanda, Kaguvi and Makoni were executed, and the Company's control was restored by force.
- The British government imposed closer supervision: the **Southern Rhodesia Order in Council of 1898** created a **Resident Commissioner** and a **Legislative Council** that included elected settlers.
- A **Native Affairs Department** with Native Commissioners was set up; Ndebele indunas were paid as salaried officials, giving them some recognition in return for control.
- Africans learned that open armed resistance could not succeed against such force; many turned to other forms of resistance.
- The memory of 1896-97, especially Nehanda, inspired later nationalists in the Second Chimurenga.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Shona did not resist"}
Settlers in 1896 believed the Shona were too divided to fight, which is why the June rising surprised them. The Shona rising lasted longer than the Ndebele one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting coordination as a settled fact"}
Whether the risings were centrally planned is a historians' debate. Say what each side argues rather than stating one view as fact.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Jameson Raid with the 1893 war"}
The Jameson Raid (1895-96) was an attack on the Transvaal. It was an opportunity for the 1896 risings, not part of the Anglo-Ndebele War.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Ndebele rose in March 1896 and the Shona in June 1896 against Company rule.
- Causes: loss of land and cattle, forced labour, the hut tax and the Native Police; rinderpest, drought and locusts; and the opportunity created by the Jameson Raid.
- Chiefs led the fighting; Mkwati, Kaguvi and Nehanda gave it religious authority. How far the risings were coordinated is debated.
- Superior weapons, reinforcements, disunity, famine and Rhodes's Matopos indabas led to defeat; Nehanda and Kaguvi were executed in April 1898.
- Results included the 1898 Order in Council, the Native Affairs Department and a lasting inspiration for later nationalists.
:::
