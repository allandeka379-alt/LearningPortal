## Introduction

For thirteen days in October 1962 the world stood closer to nuclear war than at any other time. The cause was a small island ninety miles from Florida, where the Soviet Union had secretly placed nuclear missiles. How President **John F. Kennedy** and the Soviet leader **Nikita Khrushchev** found a way out is one of the most studied crises in history.

The crisis grew out of three years of hostility between the USA and Fidel Castro's Cuba. For the United States, Cuba was a test of **containment**, the policy of stopping the spread of communism. For people in Africa and the rest of the world, the crisis showed how decisions made in Washington and Moscow could threaten everyone. Cuba's later role in Angola and southern Africa also grew from its alliance with the USSR, which this crisis sealed.

You should already know how Castro came to power in 1959 and what containment meant after 1947.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Containment"}
The US policy, from 1947, of preventing communism from spreading to new countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Embargo"}
An official ban on trade with a country. The USA placed a partial embargo on Cuba in 1960 and a near-total one in 1962.
:::

:::definition{term="Quarantine"}
The word Kennedy used for the US naval blockade of Cuba in October 1962. A "blockade" could be seen as an act of war, so "quarantine" sounded less aggressive.
:::

:::definition{term="ExComm"}
The Executive Committee of the National Security Council, the group of advisers who met with Kennedy throughout the Missile Crisis.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1 January 1959: Batista flees; Castro takes power.
- 17 April 1961: Bay of Pigs landing begins.
- 14 October 1962: U-2 photographs reveal missile sites.
- 22 October 1962: Kennedy announces the quarantine.
- 27 October 1962: "Black Saturday".
- 28 October 1962: Khrushchev agrees to remove the missiles.
:::

## US reactions to the revolution, 1959-1961

At first the USA recognised Castro's government. But relations soon broke down:

- Castro's **land reform** (1959) and **nationalisation** of American-owned businesses (1960) hit US companies hard.
- In 1960 Cuba agreed to sell sugar to the USSR in return for oil. When US-owned refineries refused to process Soviet oil, Castro nationalised them.
- The USA cut its purchases of Cuban sugar (July 1960) and banned most exports to Cuba (October 1960).
- In January 1961 the USA broke off diplomatic relations.
- The CIA began to train Cuban exiles to overthrow Castro.

## The Bay of Pigs, April 1961

The plan was prepared under President Eisenhower and approved by Kennedy, who took office in January 1961. About 1,400 CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the **Bay of Pigs** on the south coast of Cuba on **17 April 1961**. It was a disaster:

- The air strikes before the landing failed to destroy Castro's air force, and Kennedy refused further open US air support, to hide the US role.
- The landing site was swampy and far from the mountains where survivors could have fled.
- The expected rising of Cubans against Castro did not happen.
- Castro's forces defeated the exiles within three days; more than 1,100 were captured.

:::example{title="A consequence explained step by step: how the Bay of Pigs led to the Missile Crisis"}
1. **Kennedy was humiliated:** the world knew the USA had organised the invasion, and it had failed.
2. **Castro was strengthened:** he declared the revolution socialist and moved closer to the USSR for protection.
3. **The USA kept trying:** covert plans to sabotage Cuba and remove Castro (Operation Mongoose) convinced Cuba and the USSR that another invasion was coming.
4. **Khrushchev saw weakness:** after the Bay of Pigs and their meeting in Vienna in June 1961, he judged Kennedy inexperienced and likely to back down.

These steps help to explain why Khrushchev believed he could place missiles in Cuba in 1962.
:::

## The Cuban Missile Crisis, October 1962

### Why did Khrushchev place missiles in Cuba?

- **To protect Cuba** from another American invasion.
- **To close the missile gap:** the USA had far more long-range missiles; missiles in Cuba could reach most of the USA.
- **In response to US missiles in Turkey:** American Jupiter missiles near the Soviet border had recently been deployed.
- **As a bargaining chip,** perhaps over Berlin.
- **To show strength** at home and to China, which accused him of being soft on the West.

### Events

| Date (1962) | Event |
|---|---|
| 14 October | A US U-2 spy plane photographs missile sites under construction |
| 16 October | Kennedy is shown the photographs; ExComm begins to meet |
| 22 October | Kennedy announces the quarantine in a television broadcast |
| 24 October | The quarantine begins; Soviet ships heading for Cuba stop or turn back |
| 26 October | Khrushchev's first letter: missiles removed if the USA promises not to invade Cuba |
| 27 October | Second letter demands removal of US missiles from Turkey; a U-2 is shot down over Cuba |
| 28 October | Khrushchev announces that the missiles will be removed |

### Kennedy's options

ExComm considered doing nothing, diplomatic pressure, an air strike on the missile sites, a full invasion, and a naval blockade. Kennedy chose the **blockade (quarantine)** because it showed strength without immediately killing Soviet personnel, and it gave Khrushchev time to back down. Military chiefs had pressed for air strikes and an invasion.

### The resolution

On **27 October**, "Black Saturday", the crisis was at its most dangerous: a U-2 was shot down over Cuba and its pilot killed. Kennedy chose to reply publicly to Khrushchev's first letter, promising not to invade Cuba if the missiles were removed. In secret, his brother **Robert Kennedy** told the Soviet ambassador that the Jupiter missiles in Turkey would be removed within months, on condition this was not made public. On **28 October** Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be dismantled.

## Aftermath

- A direct **hotline** between Washington and Moscow was set up in 1963 so the leaders could communicate quickly.
- The **Partial Test Ban Treaty** (August 1963) banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
- The US Jupiter missiles were removed from Turkey in 1963.
- Castro, who had not been consulted, was angry with the USSR, but Cuba remained communist and the US embargo continued.
- Khrushchev was removed from power in 1964; his handling of Cuba was one of the criticisms made against him.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: who won the Missile Crisis?"}
**Kennedy:** the missiles were removed, he appeared to stand firm, and the Turkish deal was kept secret for many years.
**Khrushchev:** he gained a promise that the USA would not invade Cuba and the removal of the Jupiter missiles from Turkey.
**Castro:** his government survived, though he was ignored in the settlement.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that both leaders gained something and that the real winner was peace, because both stepped back and then took steps to reduce the risk of war.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the USA invaded Cuba"}
US forces did not invade. The Bay of Pigs was carried out by Cuban exiles trained by the CIA, and the Missile Crisis ended without an invasion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the secret deal"}
The removal of US missiles from Turkey was part of the settlement, but it was kept secret, so at the time Kennedy appeared to have won outright.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two letters"}
The first letter (26 October) asked only for a no-invasion pledge; the second (27 October) added the Turkish missiles.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Castro's nationalisations led the USA to impose an embargo (1960) and break off relations (January 1961).
- The CIA-backed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961) failed, humiliating Kennedy and pushing Castro towards the USSR.
- Khrushchev placed missiles in Cuba to protect it, close the missile gap and answer US missiles in Turkey; a U-2 found them on 14 October 1962.
- Kennedy chose a naval quarantine; on 28 October Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for a no-invasion pledge and a secret promise on Turkey.
- The aftermath included the hotline and the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963); historians debate who won.
:::
