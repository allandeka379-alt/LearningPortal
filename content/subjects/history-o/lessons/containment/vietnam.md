## Introduction

The Vietnam War was the longest war the United States had fought up to that time, and the first it clearly failed to win. The most powerful country in the world, with huge advantages in money, weapons and technology, could not defeat a communist movement in a small, mainly rural country in South-East Asia.

The war matters for Africa too. The success of the Vietnamese communists, who used guerrilla warfare against a far stronger enemy, was studied by liberation movements across the world, including in southern Africa, where ZANLA and ZIPRA fought their own guerrilla campaigns in the 1970s.

You should already know what containment meant and how the Korean War (1950-53) was fought.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Domino theory"}
The belief that if one country fell to communism, its neighbours would follow, like a row of falling dominoes. President Eisenhower described it in 1954.
:::

:::definition{term="Viet Cong"}
The name used by the Americans and South Vietnamese for the communist guerrillas in South Vietnam, organised in the National Liberation Front (NLF) from 1960.
:::

:::definition{term="Guerrilla warfare"}
Fighting by small, mobile groups who avoid open battle, use surprise attacks and ambushes, and rely on support from the local population.
:::

:::definition{term="Vietnamisation"}
President Nixon's policy, from 1969, of building up the South Vietnamese army so that it could take over the fighting while American troops withdrew.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1954:** French defeat at Dien Bien Phu; Geneva Agreements divide Vietnam at the 17th parallel.
- **August 1964:** Gulf of Tonkin incident and Resolution.
- **March 1965:** bombing campaign (Operation Rolling Thunder) and first US combat troops.
- **January 1968:** the Tet Offensive.
- **27 January 1973:** Paris Peace Accords.
- **30 April 1975:** fall of Saigon.
:::

## Background: from French colony to divided country

Vietnam had been part of French Indochina. The communist-led **Viet Minh**, under **Ho Chi Minh**, fought the French from 1946 and defeated them at **Dien Bien Phu** in 1954. The **Geneva Agreements** of 1954 divided Vietnam temporarily at the **17th parallel**: a communist North under Ho Chi Minh and a non-communist South. Elections to reunite the country were to be held in 1956.

The elections never took place. The South, led from 1955 by **Ngo Dinh Diem**, refused to hold them, with American support, because the USA expected Ho Chi Minh to win.

## Why did the USA become involved?

- **Containment and the domino theory.** American leaders feared that if South Vietnam fell, communism would spread to Laos, Cambodia, Thailand and beyond.
- **Supporting an ally.** The USA had backed the French and then built up Diem's government with money, weapons and advisers.
- **Credibility.** Presidents feared that abandoning South Vietnam would make the USA look weak to the USSR, China and its own allies.

Diem's government was corrupt and unpopular, especially with peasants and with Buddhists, since Diem and many leading officials were Catholics. In 1960 opponents in the South formed the **National Liberation Front**, supported by the North. Under Kennedy the number of American "advisers" rose to about 16,000 by 1963. In November 1963 Diem was overthrown and killed in a military coup, and South Vietnam became even less stable.

## Escalation

In **August 1964** the USA reported that North Vietnamese patrol boats had attacked American destroyers in the **Gulf of Tonkin**. Doubts were later raised about the second reported attack. Congress passed the **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution**, giving President Lyndon **Johnson** power to take all necessary measures to defend American forces, without a declaration of war.

In **March 1965** Johnson began a long bombing campaign against the North, **Operation Rolling Thunder**, and the first American combat troops (Marines) landed at Da Nang. In 1968 the number of American troops in Vietnam passed half a million.

## Tactics and strategy

**The Viet Cong and North Vietnam** used guerrilla warfare. Small units ambushed patrols, laid booby traps and then disappeared into the jungle or among villagers. They built networks of tunnels, such as those at Cu Chi near Saigon. Supplies and soldiers came from the North along the **Ho Chi Minh Trail**, a network of paths through Laos and Cambodia. They often won the support of peasants, sometimes through persuasion and sometimes through intimidation.

**The Americans** relied on their technology and firepower:

- **Bombing** of the North and of the Ho Chi Minh Trail, which failed to cut the supply lines.
- **Search and destroy** missions, in which troops entered villages to find Viet Cong fighters. These often harmed civilians and turned villagers against the Americans. The killing of several hundred unarmed villagers by American soldiers at **My Lai** in March 1968, revealed in 1969, shocked the world.
- **Chemical weapons:** **Agent Orange**, a defoliant, was sprayed to destroy the jungle cover, and **napalm**, a burning jelly, was dropped in bombs. Both caused lasting harm to people and land.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why American tactics often failed"}
1. **The enemy was hard to find:** guerrillas looked like ordinary peasants and hid in tunnels and jungle.
2. **Firepower hit civilians:** bombing, napalm and search-and-destroy raids killed and displaced villagers.
3. **Villagers turned against the Americans:** each raid could create new recruits for the Viet Cong.
4. **Result:** the USA won most battles but could not win the support of the people, which was what the war depended on.
:::

## The Tet Offensive and opposition at home

On 30-31 January 1968, during the Vietnamese New Year (Tet), the Viet Cong and North Vietnamese attacked more than a hundred towns and cities in the South, and even entered the US embassy compound in Saigon. Militarily the offensive failed: the attackers suffered very heavy losses and the Viet Cong were badly weakened. Politically, however, it was a turning point. Americans had been told the war was being won; now they saw fighting on television in the heart of Saigon. In March 1968 Johnson announced that he would not seek re-election.

Opposition in America grew because of rising casualties, the draft (conscription), the cost of the war, and reports of the suffering of Vietnamese civilians. There were large protests, especially among students. In May 1970, during protests against Nixon's extension of the war into Cambodia, the National Guard shot dead four students at **Kent State University** in Ohio.

## Withdrawal and defeat

President **Richard Nixon** (from 1969) promised "peace with honour". He introduced **Vietnamisation** and gradually withdrew American troops, while increasing bombing and attacking supply routes in Cambodia and Laos. After long negotiations, the **Paris Peace Accords** were signed on **27 January 1973**, and the last American combat troops left in March 1973. Without American support, South Vietnam could not hold out. North Vietnamese forces captured **Saigon** on **30 April 1975**, and Vietnam was reunited under communist rule in 1976.

About 58,000 Americans died. Estimates of Vietnamese deaths, soldiers and civilians, North and South, vary widely, but run into millions.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: why did the USA lose?"}
**Military reasons:** guerrilla tactics, the Ho Chi Minh Trail, and support from the USSR and China made the war unwinnable at an acceptable cost.
**Political reasons:** the South Vietnamese government was weak and unpopular, while the communists could appeal to nationalism and the desire to unite the country.
**Reasons at home:** television coverage, casualties, the draft and the protest movement wore down American support.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that the political weakness of South Vietnam was fundamental, because no amount of firepower could create a government the people supported. Others put the decline of support at home first. Weigh them using evidence.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Tet Offensive was a military victory for the communists"}
It failed militarily, with very heavy communist losses. Its importance was political: it destroyed American confidence that the war was being won.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the 1973 accords and the fall of Saigon"}
American troops left after the Paris Peace Accords (1973). Saigon fell two years later, in April 1975.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Congress declared war"}
Congress never declared war on North Vietnam. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) gave the President power to act.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1: 'Explain why the USA withdrew from Vietnam'"}
Use several linked reasons: the failure of military tactics, the political impact of Tet, opposition at home, and the cost. Explain how each made continuing the war harder.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'How far was the USA defeated by guerrilla tactics?'"}
Weigh guerrilla warfare against other factors (South Vietnam's weakness, opinion at home, aid from the USSR and China) and reach a clear judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Vietnam was divided in 1954; the USA backed Diem's South Vietnam to contain communism (domino theory).
- After the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964), Johnson began bombing and sent combat troops (1965).
- The Viet Cong's guerrilla tactics and the Ho Chi Minh Trail defeated American firepower, search and destroy and chemical weapons.
- The Tet Offensive (1968) failed militarily but turned American opinion against the war.
- Nixon's Vietnamisation led to the Paris Peace Accords (1973); Saigon fell on 30 April 1975.
:::
