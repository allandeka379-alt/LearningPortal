## Introduction

In the 1920s the United States became the richest country in the world. Millions of Americans bought their first car, radio or vacuum cleaner, cities filled with skyscrapers, and share prices on the New York Stock Exchange climbed year after year. Yet in the same decade many farmers lost their land, coal miners and textile workers struggled, and a large share of the population stayed poor. In 1929 the boom ended in the Wall Street Crash.

This lesson opens Cambridge Depth study D, "The United States, 1919-41". Its central question, "How far did the US economy boom in the 1920s?", asks you to weigh real prosperity against the people and industries it left behind. The same skill helps when you study economic change anywhere, including the debates about growth and inequality in Zimbabwe after independence.

You should already know that the USA was one of the victors of the First World War (1914-18) and that it did not join the League of Nations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Laissez-faire"}
The idea that the government should interfere as little as possible in business and the economy. The Republican presidents of the 1920s followed it.
:::

:::definition{term="Tariff"}
A tax on imported goods. High tariffs protected American industry from foreign competition.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass production"}
Making large numbers of identical goods quickly and cheaply, often on a moving assembly line where each worker does one small task.
:::

:::definition{term="Hire purchase"}
Buying goods on credit: the buyer pays a deposit, takes the goods home and pays the rest in instalments, with interest.
:::

:::definition{term="Overproduction"}
Producing more goods than people want or can afford to buy, so that prices fall and goods go unsold.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- Republican presidents: **Warren Harding** (1921-23), **Calvin Coolidge** (1923-29), **Herbert Hoover** (1929-33).
- **Andrew Mellon**, Secretary of the Treasury (1921-32), cut taxes.
- **Fordney-McCumber Tariff**, 1922.
- **Henry Ford** introduced the moving assembly line for the Model T in 1913.
:::

## Why did the economy boom?

### Advantages from the war

The First World War helped the American economy. The USA had sold food, raw materials and weapons to the Allies and lent them money, while its own territory was untouched by fighting. European industries were damaged, and American firms took over many of their markets. The USA also had huge natural resources: coal, oil, iron, timber and land.

### Government policies

The Republican governments of the 1920s believed that business should be left to run itself.

- **Laissez-faire:** there were few regulations on business, and the government did little to stop large companies from dominating their industries.
- **Low taxes:** Andrew Mellon cut income tax, especially for the rich, on the argument that they would invest the money and create jobs.
- **Tariffs:** the **Fordney-McCumber Tariff** (1922) put high taxes on imported goods, so American goods were cheaper than foreign ones in American shops.

### Mass production and the motor car

The most important new industry was the **motor car**. Henry Ford's moving **assembly line** cut the time needed to build a Model T dramatically, and Ford lowered the price again and again, so that ordinary families could afford one. By the end of the 1920s millions of cars were on American roads.

The car industry boosted many others. It needed steel, rubber, glass and leather; it created demand for petrol and for roads; and it made new businesses possible, such as garages, roadside restaurants and motels. People could live further from work, so suburbs grew, which in turn helped the building industry.

### New consumer goods and electricity

More and more homes were connected to electricity. This created demand for new **consumer goods**: radios, refrigerators, washing machines, vacuum cleaners and telephones. These were mass-produced too, and their prices fell.

### Credit, hire purchase and advertising

Few people could pay for a car or a refrigerator at once, so they bought on **hire purchase**. Banks also lent money freely. **Advertising** grew into a major industry, using newspapers, magazines, billboards and the new radio stations to persuade people that they needed the latest products. Buying on credit and spending on non-essential goods became part of American life.

### Confidence and the stock market

As businesses made profits, share prices rose. More people began to buy shares, many with borrowed money, expecting prices to keep rising. This confidence encouraged more spending and more investment.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the motor car spread prosperity"}
1. **Mass production cut prices:** the assembly line let Ford sell cars that ordinary workers could afford.
2. **Demand grew:** millions of families bought cars, often on hire purchase.
3. **Linked industries grew:** steel, rubber, glass, oil and road-building all needed more workers.
4. **New businesses appeared:** garages, petrol stations, motels and suburbs.
5. **Result:** one industry created jobs and spending across the whole economy, which is why historians see the car as central to the boom.
:::

## Who did not share in the boom?

### Older industries

Some industries declined. **Coal** faced competition from oil, gas and electricity. **Cotton textiles** faced competition from new artificial fibres such as rayon, and older mills in New England lost business to cheaper mills in the South, where wages were lower. **Railways** lost passengers and goods to cars and lorries. Workers in these industries often faced wage cuts and unemployment.

### Farmers

Agriculture was in difficulty throughout the 1920s, and roughly a quarter of Americans lived on farms. During the war European demand had been high, and farmers had borrowed to buy more land and machinery. After the war, European farming recovered, other countries put tariffs on American goods in return for American tariffs, and new machines such as tractors allowed farmers to produce even more. The result was **overproduction**: prices fell, farm incomes dropped, and many farmers could not repay their debts. Thousands lost their farms.

### Others left behind

- **Black Americans**, most of whom lived in the South as poorly paid farm workers and sharecroppers, gained little. Those who moved to northern cities usually got the lowest-paid jobs.
- **Native Americans** lived mostly on reservations, among the poorest people in the country.
- **New immigrants** often worked in low-paid jobs in the cities.
- **Unskilled workers** saw small wage rises, and trade unions were weak.

Wealth was very unequally shared: the richest Americans gained most from rising profits and tax cuts, while a large share of families had little money left after paying for necessities.

## Weaknesses by the late 1920s

Under the surface, the boom had serious weaknesses:

- **Overproduction:** by the late 1920s factories were producing more consumer goods than people could buy. Those who could afford a car or radio had already bought one.
- **Inequality:** because so many people were poor, demand could not keep rising.
- **Tariffs:** other countries retaliated, making it hard to sell American surpluses abroad.
- **Credit:** many people were in debt, and buying shares with borrowed money pushed share prices far above the real value of companies.
- **Weak banks:** there were thousands of small banks, many poorly regulated, which could collapse if borrowers could not repay.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how far did the economy boom?"}
**A real boom:** industrial production and incomes rose; new industries created millions of jobs; many Americans enjoyed cars, electricity and consumer goods for the first time.
**A limited boom:** farmers, workers in old industries, Black Americans, Native Americans and many immigrants gained little; wealth was concentrated among the rich.
**Judgement:** the boom was real but uneven. Whether it was a boom "for all Americans" depends on whose experience you count. A good answer uses specific groups and industries on both sides.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying everyone was rich in the 1920s"}
Many Americans stayed poor. Farmers, Black Americans, Native Americans and workers in old industries did not share fully in the prosperity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up tariffs and taxes on income"}
Mellon cut income taxes. The Fordney-McCumber Tariff was a tax on imports, which raised the price of foreign goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining them"}
"Hire purchase" is not an explanation. Say how it caused growth: it let people buy goods they could not afford at once, which increased demand and production.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain why the US economy boomed'"}
Choose two or three causes (mass production and the car, government policy, credit and advertising) and explain how each increased production or demand.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c) and Paper 4: 'How far did all Americans benefit?'"}
Balance the groups who gained against those who did not, with specific evidence for each. In Paper 4 you may be asked how important a factor (such as the motor car) was: judge it by comparing it with other causes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The boom rested on wartime gains, natural resources, Republican laissez-faire policies, low taxes and high tariffs.
- Mass production, especially of the motor car, and new electrical consumer goods drove growth and created linked industries.
- Hire purchase, easy credit and advertising increased demand; rising share prices boosted confidence.
- Older industries (coal, textiles, railways) and farmers, suffering from overproduction, did not share in the boom; many groups stayed poor.
- Overproduction, inequality, tariffs, debt and weak banks made the boom fragile by 1929.
:::
