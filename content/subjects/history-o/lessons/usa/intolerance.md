## Introduction

The United States has often described itself as a nation of immigrants and a land of freedom. Yet in the 1920s it shut its doors to most newcomers, arrested people for their political beliefs, put a teacher on trial for teaching evolution, and saw millions join a secret organisation that terrorised Black Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants.

Studying intolerance helps to explain the other side of the "Roaring Twenties". It also connects with themes you meet elsewhere in history, including the segregation laws of colonial Rhodesia and apartheid South Africa: laws and attitudes that treated people differently because of their race, religion or origin. In this lesson, take care to describe these attitudes accurately without repeating the insulting language that was used at the time.

You should already know about the economic boom and the changing society of the 1920s.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nativism"}
The belief that people born in a country (in the USA, usually meaning white Protestants of northern European descent) should be favoured over immigrants.
:::

:::definition{term="Quota"}
A fixed limit on numbers. The immigration laws of 1921 and 1924 set quotas for each nationality.
:::

:::definition{term="Red Scare"}
A wave of fear of communists, anarchists and other radicals, at its height in 1919-20.
:::

:::definition{term="Fundamentalism"}
A strict form of religious belief; in 1920s America, the belief among some Protestants that the Bible should be taken as literally true, including the account of creation.
:::

:::definition{term="Segregation"}
Keeping people of different races apart by law or custom, as under the "Jim Crow" laws of the southern states.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- **1919-1920:** the Red Scare; the Palmer Raids (November 1919-January 1920).
- **1921:** the Emergency Quota Act.
- **1924:** the Immigration Act (also called the Johnson-Reed Act).
- **1925:** the Scopes trial in Dayton, Tennessee.
- **August 1927:** Sacco and Vanzetti executed.
:::

## Immigration restrictions

Between about 1880 and 1914 millions of immigrants had arrived, increasingly from southern and eastern Europe: Italians, Poles, Russians and many Jews. Many were Catholic or Jewish, poor and spoke little English. Some older-established Americans feared them:

- **Jobs and wages:** immigrants were blamed for accepting low wages and taking jobs.
- **Culture and religion:** nativists believed that newcomers would weaken Protestant, English-speaking American culture.
- **Politics:** some immigrants were linked, often unfairly, with communism and anarchism.
- **Crime:** immigrants were often blamed, without good evidence, for crime in the cities.

The **Emergency Quota Act** of 1921 limited immigrants from each country to 3 per cent of the number of people from that country living in the USA in 1910. The **Immigration Act** of 1924 cut this to 2 per cent of the number in 1890, before most southern and eastern Europeans had arrived, so it fell most heavily on them. It also banned immigration from Asia almost completely. People from countries in the Americas, such as Mexico and Canada, were not covered by the quotas.

## The Red Scare

The Russian Revolution of 1917 and a wave of strikes in 1919 made many Americans fear a communist revolution at home. In 1919 anarchists posted and planted bombs; one damaged the home of the Attorney General, **A. Mitchell Palmer**. In the **Palmer Raids** (November 1919-January 1920), thousands of suspected radicals, many of them immigrants, were arrested, often without proper evidence, and hundreds of foreign-born radicals were deported. The panic died down after 1920, when a predicted uprising did not happen.

### Sacco and Vanzetti

In 1920 two Italian immigrants and anarchists, **Nicola Sacco** and **Bartolomeo Vanzetti**, were arrested for a robbery in Massachusetts in which two men were killed. They were convicted in 1921 and, after years of appeals, executed in **August 1927**. Many people at the time, and since, believed that the trial was unfair: the evidence was disputed, and the judge was openly hostile to them as foreigners and radicals. Historians still debate whether either man was guilty, but most agree that prejudice affected the trial. There were protests across the USA and in other countries.

## Religious intolerance: the Scopes trial

Many Protestant Christians, especially in the rural South, were **fundamentalists**. Several southern states passed laws banning the teaching of evolution in state schools. In 1925 **John Scopes**, a teacher in **Dayton, Tennessee**, was put on trial for teaching evolution. The trial, nicknamed the "Monkey Trial", attracted huge publicity. Scopes was defended by the famous lawyer **Clarence Darrow**, and the prosecution was supported by the politician **William Jennings Bryan**. Scopes was found guilty and fined, though the verdict was later overturned on a technicality. The trial showed the gap between modern, urban America and traditional, rural America; many newspapers mocked the fundamentalists.

## Black Americans and the Ku Klux Klan

Most Black Americans lived in the South, where **Jim Crow** laws enforced segregation in schools, transport and public places, and where most Black people were prevented from voting by tests and taxes. Many worked as poorly paid sharecroppers. **Lynching**, the murder of people by mobs without trial, still took place, and the government did not pass a federal law to stop it.

In the **Great Migration** hundreds of thousands moved to northern cities such as Chicago and New York, seeking work and escape from segregation. There they usually found the worst-paid jobs and poor housing, and there were race riots in several cities in 1919. Black Americans organised to resist: the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP, founded in 1909) campaigned through the courts and against lynching, and **Marcus Garvey**'s movement encouraged Black pride and self-reliance.

The **Ku Klux Klan** was refounded in 1915. In the 1920s it spread beyond the South to the Midwest and elsewhere. It was hostile not only to Black Americans but also to Catholics, Jews and immigrants, and it used intimidation, beatings and murder. Historians estimate that its membership reached several million in the mid-1920s. It declined quickly after 1925, when one of its leaders in Indiana was convicted of a brutal murder, which exposed its hypocrisy.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why immigration was restricted"}
1. **Numbers had been rising:** millions arrived before 1914, many from southern and eastern Europe.
2. **Fear grew after the war:** the Red Scare linked immigrants with revolution, and returning soldiers needed jobs.
3. **Nativists organised:** groups including the Klan demanded that America stay white and Protestant.
4. **Congress acted:** the quota acts of 1921 and 1924 cut numbers and favoured northern and western Europeans.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how widespread was intolerance?"}
**Widespread:** Congress passed immigration quotas by large majorities; the Klan had millions of members; segregation was law in the South; the Red Scare affected the whole country.
**Limited:** the Red Scare faded quickly; the Klan collapsed after 1925; many Americans protested against the treatment of Sacco and Vanzetti and mocked the Scopes prosecution; Black Americans built thriving communities and culture, as in the Harlem Renaissance.
**Judgement:** intolerance was common, often official and sometimes violent, but it was challenged, and it varied by region and over the decade.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the 1924 Act stopped all immigration"}
It set low quotas, mainly for southern and eastern Europe, and almost ended Asian immigration, but immigrants from the Americas were not under quotas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Scopes was found not guilty"}
He was found guilty and fined; the verdict was later overturned on a technicality.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Klan only attacked Black Americans"}
In the 1920s it also targeted Catholics, Jews and immigrants.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'How widespread was intolerance?'"}
Cover several kinds (immigration, political, religious, racial) and judge extent: who was affected, where, and for how long. Include evidence that intolerance was opposed.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4: 'How important was the Red Scare…?'"}
Judge importance by its effects: on immigration laws, on the Sacco and Vanzetti case, on trade unions. Compare it with other causes of intolerance before deciding.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nativist fears about jobs, culture and politics led to the immigration quotas of 1921 and 1924, aimed mainly at southern and eastern Europeans and Asians.
- The Red Scare (1919-20) brought the Palmer Raids and deportations; Sacco and Vanzetti were executed in 1927 after a disputed trial.
- The Scopes trial (1925) showed the conflict between fundamentalist and modern America.
- Black Americans faced segregation, lynching and the revived Ku Klux Klan, which also targeted Catholics, Jews and immigrants.
- Intolerance was widespread and often official, but it was challenged and declined in some forms after the mid-1920s.
:::
