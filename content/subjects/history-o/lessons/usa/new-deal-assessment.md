## Introduction

Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected President four times, more than any other American. Yet the New Deal was fiercely attacked from the moment it began. Some called it socialism that would destroy American freedom; others said it did far too little for the poor. And when the USA entered the Second World War in December 1941, millions of Americans were still unemployed.

Was the New Deal a success? Historians have argued about this for decades, and the answer depends on what you think the New Deal was trying to do. This is one of the most common evaluation questions in Cambridge Depth study D. The skill of judging a government's economic programme by its aims and its results is just as useful for debating any government's policies, including those of Zimbabwe after 1980.

You should already know the main measures of the First and Second New Deals.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Unconstitutional"}
Against the Constitution. The Supreme Court can strike down laws it judges to be unconstitutional.
:::

:::definition{term="Federal government"}
The national government in Washington, as opposed to the governments of the individual states.
:::

:::definition{term="Recession"}
A fall in economic activity, smaller or shorter than a depression.
:::

:::definition{term="Court-packing plan"}
Roosevelt's proposal in 1937 to appoint extra Supreme Court judges, which critics said was an attempt to control the Court.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- **1934:** the American Liberty League is founded by wealthy opponents of the New Deal.
- **May 1935:** the Supreme Court strikes down the NIRA (and in January 1936 the AAA).
- **September 1935:** Senator **Huey Long** of Louisiana is assassinated.
- **1937:** the court-packing plan fails in Congress.
- **1937-1938:** recession; unemployment rises again.
:::

## Who opposed the New Deal, and why?

### Republicans, the rich and business

- **Republicans** believed that the New Deal gave the federal government too much power over the economy and the states, and that it went against the American traditions of self-reliance and free enterprise.
- **The rich** objected to higher taxes, such as the Revenue Act of 1935, and some called Roosevelt a traitor to his class.
- **Business leaders** disliked the NRA codes, government regulation, support for trade unions under the Wagner Act, and the TVA, which competed with private electricity companies. In 1934 wealthy opponents set up the **American Liberty League** to campaign against the New Deal.
- Some critics compared the New Deal to communism, or to the growing power of governments in Europe.

### The Supreme Court

The Supreme Court was dominated by conservative judges appointed by earlier presidents. In **May 1935**, in a case brought by a poultry business, it ruled that the **NIRA** was unconstitutional because it gave too much power to the federal government over trade within states. In January 1936 it struck down the **AAA**.

After his landslide re-election in 1936, Roosevelt proposed to add up to six new judges to the Court, one for each judge over 70 who did not retire. This **court-packing plan** was widely criticised, even by many Democrats, as an attack on the independence of the courts, and Congress rejected it in 1937. However, from 1937 the Court began to accept New Deal laws, including the Wagner Act and the Social Security Act, and retirements soon allowed Roosevelt to appoint new judges.

### Critics who wanted more

- **Huey Long**, a senator and former Governor of Louisiana, launched the **"Share Our Wealth"** campaign. He proposed heavy taxes on the very rich and guaranteed incomes for every family. Share Our Wealth clubs claimed millions of members, and he planned to challenge Roosevelt for the presidency, but he was assassinated in 1935.
- **Father Charles Coughlin**, a Catholic priest with a huge radio audience, at first supported Roosevelt but then attacked him for not going far enough, especially against the banks. His broadcasts later became openly antisemitic, and he lost support.
- **Dr Francis Townsend** campaigned for a generous pension for everyone over 60, on condition that they spent it each month. His movement helped to push Roosevelt towards the Social Security Act.
- **Socialists, communists and some trade unionists** argued that the New Deal saved capitalism instead of replacing it.

## Why did unemployment persist?

Unemployment fell from about a quarter of the workforce in 1933 to about one in seven by 1937. In 1937, believing that recovery was well under way, Roosevelt cut government spending to balance the budget, and the Federal Reserve tightened credit. The economy fell into a sharp **recession** in 1937-38, and unemployment rose again, to roughly one in five by 1938. It only fell to low levels when the USA began to produce weapons for the Second World War from 1940-41.

Historians give several reasons why the New Deal did not end unemployment:

- **Spending was too small** for the size of the depression, and it was cut too early in 1937.
- **Business confidence** stayed low: some businesses were reluctant to invest while they feared regulation and higher taxes.
- Many New Deal jobs were **temporary**.
- The world economy remained depressed, limiting exports.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: the recession of 1937-38"}
1. **Recovery seemed under way:** output had risen and unemployment had fallen since 1933.
2. **Roosevelt cut spending:** he wanted to balance the budget, so work programmes such as the WPA were reduced.
3. **Credit was tightened:** borrowing became harder for businesses.
4. **Demand fell again:** the economy slumped, and unemployment rose sharply.
5. **Significance:** many historians argue this shows that the recovery depended on government spending, and that the New Deal had not done enough to restore private investment.
:::

## Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- **Confidence and the banks:** the banking system was saved, and bank failures became rare after deposit insurance was introduced.
- **Relief:** millions received work or help, and many families were saved from hunger and homelessness.
- **Lasting achievements:** dams, roads, schools, airports and electricity from the TVA and the work agencies; Social Security; the right to join unions; a minimum wage.
- **Democracy preserved:** unlike Germany and other countries hit by the Depression, the USA kept its democratic system, and extremists won little support.
- **A new role for government:** Americans came to expect the federal government to act in a crisis.

**Weaknesses**

- **Unemployment** remained high throughout the 1930s; full recovery came only with wartime production.
- **Black Americans** were often treated unequally: some agencies were segregated or paid them less, the AAA hurt many sharecroppers, and Roosevelt avoided supporting a federal anti-lynching law, partly to keep the support of southern Democrats in Congress.
- **Women** were often paid less than men in New Deal programmes, and some agencies, such as the CCC, were for men only.
- **Farm workers and domestic workers** were left out of Social Security at first.
- Opponents argued that it **wasted money**, increased the national debt and gave the federal government too much power.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: was the New Deal a success?"}
**Yes:** it restored confidence, saved the banks, gave relief to millions, created lasting reforms and preserved democracy.
**No:** it did not end unemployment or the Depression; it treated many groups unequally; critics on the right said it went too far, and critics on the left that it did too little.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that it succeeded in relief and reform but only partly in recovery. Your answer should define "success" (by which aim, for whom) and support each side with specific evidence.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the New Deal ended the Depression"}
Unemployment stayed high until wartime production began. The New Deal reduced suffering and reformed the system but did not bring full recovery.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all opponents as the same"}
Some opposed the New Deal for doing too much (Republicans, business, the Liberty League); others for doing too little (Huey Long, Townsend, socialists). Say which kind of critic you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the court-packing plan succeeded"}
Congress rejected it in 1937, though the Court then began to accept New Deal laws.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'How successful was the New Deal?'"}
Judge against each aim (relief, recovery, reform) and for different groups. Use specific evidence such as unemployment figures, named agencies and laws, and the 1937-38 recession. Reach a balanced judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4: 'How important was opposition to the New Deal?'"}
Discuss different ways of judging importance: did opposition stop policies (the Supreme Court), change them (Long and Townsend pushing towards Social Security), or fail (the Liberty League against the 1936 landslide)?
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Republicans, the rich and business (the Liberty League) opposed the New Deal as too costly, too regulatory and a threat to free enterprise.
- The Supreme Court struck down the NIRA (1935) and the AAA (1936); Roosevelt's court-packing plan failed in 1937.
- Radical critics such as Huey Long, Father Coughlin and Dr Townsend demanded more, and helped push Roosevelt towards reform.
- Unemployment fell but stayed high; the 1937-38 recession followed spending cuts, and only war production ended mass unemployment.
- The New Deal succeeded in relief and reform and preserved democracy, but recovery was incomplete and some groups were treated unequally.
:::
