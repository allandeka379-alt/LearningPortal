## Introduction

The 1920s in the United States are remembered as the "Roaring Twenties": a decade of jazz, dance crazes, film stars, sporting heroes and young women in short skirts. They are also remembered for **Prohibition**, the attempt to ban alcohol, which made gangsters such as Al Capone rich and famous.

But how much did American society really change, and for whom? Many of the new freedoms belonged mainly to young, white, middle-class people in the cities. Cambridge Depth study D asks you to describe the Roaring Twenties and to judge how far women's roles changed and why Prohibition failed. The question of how far new freedoms reached ordinary people is one you can also ask about Zimbabwean towns and countryside in the twentieth century.

You should already know about the economic boom of the 1920s, which gave many Americans more money and leisure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Jazz"}
A form of music that grew from Black American communities, especially in New Orleans, mixing blues, ragtime and improvisation. It spread through records, radio and clubs in the 1920s.
:::

:::definition{term="Flapper"}
A young woman of the 1920s who challenged traditional rules: short hair and skirts, make-up, smoking and drinking in public, dancing and going out without a chaperone.
:::

:::definition{term="Prohibition"}
The ban on making, selling and transporting alcoholic drinks in the USA, from 1920 to 1933.
:::

:::definition{term="Speakeasy"}
An illegal bar selling alcohol during Prohibition.
:::

:::definition{term="Bootlegger"}
A person who made, smuggled or sold alcohol illegally.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **January 1920:** Prohibition comes into force (Eighteenth Amendment; Volstead Act).
- **August 1920:** the Nineteenth Amendment gives women the vote in national elections.
- **1927:** *The Jazz Singer*, the first full-length film with synchronised talking scenes.
- **14 February 1929:** the St Valentine's Day Massacre in Chicago.
- **December 1933:** Prohibition repealed (Twenty-first Amendment).
:::

## The Roaring Twenties

### Music and dance

**Jazz** was created by Black American musicians and spread from New Orleans to Chicago and New York. Musicians such as **Louis Armstrong** and **Duke Ellington** became famous, and records and the radio carried the music across the country. New dances such as the Charleston shocked older Americans, who thought jazz encouraged immoral behaviour. In New York, the district of Harlem became the centre of the **Harlem Renaissance**, a flowering of Black American music, writing and art.

### Cinema

Hollywood, in California, became the centre of the world's film industry. By the end of the decade tens of millions of Americans went to the cinema every week. Stars such as **Charlie Chaplin** and **Rudolph Valentino** became household names. In **1927** *The Jazz Singer* introduced synchronised talking scenes, and silent films soon disappeared. Films spread new fashions and attitudes, and critics complained that they encouraged loose morals.

### Radio, newspapers and sport

The first regular commercial radio station began broadcasting in 1920, and within a few years millions of homes had a radio. Radio carried news, music, sport and advertising. Mass-circulation newspapers and magazines grew too. Sport became big business: crowds and radio audiences followed the baseball star **Babe Ruth** and the boxer **Jack Dempsey**.

### Cars and leisure

Cars gave people, especially the young, new freedom to travel and to escape the supervision of parents. Shorter working hours and higher wages for many meant more time and money for leisure.

## Women in the 1920s

### What changed

- **The vote.** The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) gave women the right to vote in all elections.
- **Work.** More women worked, especially in new jobs as typists, secretaries, telephonists and shop assistants. Some single women gained financial independence.
- **Home life.** Electrical goods such as vacuum cleaners and washing machines reduced some household work for those who could afford them.
- **Behaviour and fashion.** **Flappers** cut their hair short, wore short skirts and make-up, smoked, drank and danced in public, and went out with men without a chaperone. Divorce became easier in some states.

### What did not change

- Most women still married young, and most married women did not work outside the home.
- Women were usually paid less than men and were kept out of most professions; very few became politicians.
- Flappers were mainly young, middle-class women in the cities. In rural areas and small towns, and among poorer families, Black women and immigrant women, life changed much less.
- Many older people and religious groups disapproved of flappers, and traditional ideas of a woman's place remained strong.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how far did women's lives change?"}
**Change:** the vote, new jobs, consumer goods and the new freedoms of the flapper.
**Continuity:** unequal pay, few women in professions or politics, and little change for rural, poor, Black and immigrant women.
**Judgement:** change was real but limited, and it depended on age, class, race and place. A good answer names specific groups of women rather than talking about "women" as one group.
:::

## Prohibition and gangsterism

### Why was Prohibition introduced?

- **Temperance movements**, especially the Anti-Saloon League and the Women's Christian Temperance Union, had campaigned for years. They blamed alcohol for crime, poverty, violence in the home and absence from work.
- **Religion:** many Protestant churches, especially in rural areas, saw drinking as sinful.
- **The First World War:** grain was needed for food, not alcohol, and many big brewers were of German origin, which made drinking seem unpatriotic.

The **Eighteenth Amendment** to the Constitution, enforced by the **Volstead Act**, banned the making, sale and transport of alcohol from **January 1920**.

### Why did Prohibition fail?

- **Demand continued.** Millions of Americans wanted to drink. Thousands of **speakeasies** opened; people made their own "moonshine", which was sometimes poisonous.
- **Smuggling.** Bootleggers brought alcohol over the long borders with Canada and Mexico and by sea.
- **Too few agents.** There were only a few thousand Prohibition agents for the whole country.
- **Corruption.** Police officers, judges and politicians were bribed to ignore illegal drinking.
- **Organised crime.** Gangsters made huge profits from bootlegging. In Chicago **Al Capone** controlled speakeasies, gambling and other rackets, and gang wars killed hundreds. In the **St Valentine's Day Massacre** (14 February 1929), seven men linked to a rival gang were shot dead in a Chicago garage. Capone was finally jailed in 1931, not for violence but for not paying income tax.

### Why was Prohibition repealed?

By the late 1920s many Americans believed that Prohibition had made things worse: it had not stopped drinking, but it had increased crime and corruption and made people lose respect for the law. The **Depression** after 1929 added economic arguments: legal brewing would create jobs and the government could tax alcohol again. Franklin Roosevelt promised repeal in the 1932 election, and the **Twenty-first Amendment** ended Prohibition in **December 1933**.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how Prohibition led to gangsterism"}
1. **Demand did not disappear:** millions of people still wanted to drink.
2. **Supply became illegal:** only criminals could provide alcohol, so prices and profits were high.
3. **Gangs competed for markets:** they fought violently to control areas and bribed police and politicians.
4. **Result:** organised crime grew richer and more powerful than before, which was the opposite of what Prohibition was meant to achieve.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all American women were flappers"}
Flappers were a minority, mostly young, urban and middle class. Most women's lives changed far less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Prohibition made drinking legal again in 1929"}
Prohibition lasted from January 1920 until December 1933. The Crash of 1929 helped bring about repeal, but repeal came in 1933.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Al Capone was jailed for murder"}
He was convicted in 1931 of income-tax evasion, because evidence of his other crimes could not be proved in court.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Jazz, cinema (*The Jazz Singer*, 1927), radio and sport created a new mass culture in the 1920s.
- Women gained the vote (1920), new jobs and new freedoms (flappers), but pay, work and social attitudes changed slowly, especially outside the cities.
- Prohibition (1920) was introduced because of temperance campaigns, religion and the First World War.
- It failed because demand continued; speakeasies, bootlegging, corruption and gangsters such as Al Capone flourished.
- Crime, corruption and the Depression led to repeal in December 1933.
:::
