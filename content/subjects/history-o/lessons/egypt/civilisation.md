## Introduction

More than four and a half thousand years ago, on the banks of an African river, people built the Great Pyramid at Giza, which remained the tallest human-made structure in the world for thousands of years. Ancient Egypt was an **African civilisation**, built by people who learned to use the Nile's yearly flood to grow surplus food. That surplus fed scribes, priests, builders, doctors and astronomers.

This lesson explains how the Nile made Egyptian civilisation possible and describes its achievements and legacy. It links back to the origins of humankind (people had lived in the Nile valley long before the pharaohs) and forward to the states of Zimbabwe, where surplus, trade and strong leadership also produced great stone buildings. All dates are approximate and given as BCE (before the Common Era).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Civilisation"}
An advanced way of life in which people live in organised communities with a government, towns or cities, specialised workers, a system of writing or record-keeping, organised religion, and achievements in science, art and building.
:::

:::definition{term="Pharaoh"}
The title of the king of ancient Egypt, who was believed to be a god-king and ruled with complete authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Basin irrigation"}
A farming method in which floodwater from the Nile was trapped in fields surrounded by earth banks (basins) and left to soak into the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Shaduf"}
A device for lifting water: a long pole balanced on a frame, with a bucket on one end and a counterweight on the other.
:::

:::definition{term="Hieroglyphics"}
The ancient Egyptian writing system that used picture signs for sounds, words and ideas.
:::

:::definition{term="Mummification"}
The process of preserving a dead body by drying and wrapping it, because Egyptians believed the body was needed in the afterlife.
:::

## The Nile: the gift that made Egypt

Egypt lies in north-east Africa. Most of it is desert; people lived along a narrow strip of fertile land beside the Nile and in the wide, flat delta where the river meets the Mediterranean Sea. The Nile flows **from south to north**, so the southern valley was called Upper Egypt and the northern delta Lower Egypt.

Every year, rains far to the south made the Nile rise and flood. When the water went down it left a layer of rich black silt on the fields. The Nile gave Egypt:

- **fertile soil and water** for crops in a desert land;
- **a transport route** for boats carrying grain, stone and trade goods;
- **fish, papyrus reeds and clay** for food, writing material and bricks;
- **a reason to organise**: managing water, storing grain and measuring land needed government and record-keeping.

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn (all approximate)"}
Upper and Lower Egypt were united under one king around 3100 BCE. The Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza was built around 2560 BCE.
:::

## Government and society

The pharaoh stood at the top, believed to be a link between the gods and the people. Below him came the **vizier** (chief minister), then nobles who governed the provinces, priests who ran the temples, and scribes who kept records and collected taxes. Craftsmen, traders and soldiers came next. Most people were **farmers**, who also worked on royal building projects during the flood season. At the bottom were servants and enslaved people, many of them prisoners of war.

## Farming with the flood

The Egyptians divided their year into three seasons of four months each, based on the river:

| Season | Months | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Akhet (inundation) | about July to October | The Nile flooded the fields; farmers repaired tools and worked on royal projects |
| Peret (growing) | about November to February | Farmers ploughed and sowed seed in the wet silt |
| Shemu (harvest) | about March to June | Crops were harvested and stored; the river was at its lowest |

They grew wheat and barley (for bread and beer), flax (for linen), vegetables and fruit, and kept cattle, sheep, goats and donkeys. **Basin irrigation** trapped floodwater in earth-banked fields; canals carried water further; and the **shaduf** lifted water from the river or canals to gardens in the dry season. Grain was stored in royal granaries, and scribes recorded the harvest to work out taxes.

:::example{title="Explaining how the Nile led to civilisation, step by step"}
1. The yearly flood made farming very productive, so farmers grew more food than they needed: a **surplus**.
2. The surplus could feed people who did not farm, such as scribes, priests, craftsmen and soldiers: **specialisation**.
3. Storing grain, planning irrigation and collecting taxes needed **government** and **record-keeping**, so writing developed.
4. Measuring the fields again after each flood encouraged **mathematics**, and predicting the flood encouraged **astronomy** and a **calendar**.
5. A wealthy, organised state could build **pyramids and temples**. The river was the foundation of everything else.
:::

## Achievements

### Science, mathematics and the calendar

Egyptians used a decimal system of numbers and practical geometry to measure land and plan buildings. By watching the stars they predicted the flood and created a calendar of **365 days**: twelve months of thirty days plus five extra days. This calendar later influenced the Roman calendar and so, indirectly, the one we use today.

### Medicine

Egyptian doctors treated wounds, set broken bones and used many herbal remedies. Medical papyri such as the Edwin Smith papyrus (on surgery and injuries) and the Ebers papyrus (on remedies) record their knowledge. Their study of the body was helped by mummification.

### Writing

**Hieroglyphics** were carved on temples and tombs; quicker scripts were used for everyday records. Scribes wrote on **papyrus**, a paper-like material made from reeds. After Egyptian writing went out of use, it was deciphered again in the 1820s by the French scholar Jean-François Champollion, using the Rosetta Stone, which carries the same text in hieroglyphs, a later Egyptian script and Greek.

### Religion and mummification

Egyptians worshipped many gods, including Ra (the sun god), Osiris (god of the dead and the afterlife), Isis and Horus. They believed in life after death, so the body was preserved: the internal organs were removed and stored in jars, the body was dried with natron salt, then wrapped in linen. The whole process took about seventy days. Tombs were filled with goods for the afterlife.

### Architecture

The **pyramids** were royal tombs. The Step Pyramid of King Djoser at Saqqara, designed by the official Imhotep, was the first large stone pyramid; the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza is the largest. Egyptians also built great **temples**, such as those at Karnak and Luxor, and carved royal tombs into the rocks of the Valley of the Kings.

## The spread and influence of Egyptian civilisation

- **Nubia and Kush** (in present-day Sudan), Egypt's southern neighbours, traded and fought with Egypt and adopted many Egyptian ideas; the kings of Kush built their own pyramids and for a time ruled Egypt itself.
- **Greece and Rome** learned from Egyptian mathematics, medicine, astronomy and architecture; Greek scholars studied in Egypt, and the Egyptian 365-day calendar shaped the Roman one.
- **Today** the pyramids and temples draw visitors from all over the world, papyrus gave its name to "paper", and Egyptian ideas in geometry and medicine are part of the history of science.

:::example{title="Weighing a claim about legacy"}
**Claim:** "Egypt's greatest legacy is the pyramids."

1. **For:** they show remarkable organisation, engineering and mathematics, and they remain world-famous.
2. **Against:** the calendar, writing on papyrus, medical knowledge and geometry influenced other peoples more deeply and for longer.
3. **Judgement:** the pyramids are the most visible legacy, but the knowledge behind them, in mathematics, astronomy and record-keeping, may be the more important one.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="The Nile flows north"}
It flows from south to north, into the Mediterranean. Upper Egypt is in the south; Lower Egypt (the delta) is in the north.
:::

:::mistake{title="Pyramids as temples"}
Pyramids were royal tombs. Temples such as Karnak were places of worship.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Egypt as separate from Africa"}
Ancient Egypt was an African civilisation, with close links to Nubia and Kush to the south.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ancient Egypt was an African civilisation that grew up along the Nile; Upper and Lower Egypt were united around 3100 BCE.
- The Nile's yearly flood left fertile silt; farming used three seasons, basin irrigation, canals and the shaduf.
- Society was headed by the pharaoh, with viziers, nobles, priests, scribes, craftsmen and a large farming majority.
- Achievements: a 365-day calendar, mathematics, medicine, hieroglyphic writing on papyrus, mummification, pyramids and temples.
- Egyptian ideas spread to Kush, Greece and Rome and still influence the world.
:::
