## Introduction

In 1938 Hitler made two great gains without war. In March he united Austria with Germany, the **Anschluss**, which the Treaty of Versailles had forbidden. In September, at **Munich**, Britain and France agreed that Germany could take the **Sudetenland**, the German-speaking border regions of Czechoslovakia, although Czechoslovakia itself was not invited to the meeting.

The British policy behind these decisions is called **appeasement**. It is one of the most debated policies of the twentieth century. Was Neville Chamberlain a naive man who encouraged a dictator, or a realist who bought time for a country that was not ready to fight? The question of how far a stronger power should negotiate with an aggressive one is still asked today, from Europe to Africa.

You should already know Hitler's aims and the steps he took between 1933 and 1936.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anschluss"}
"Union" or "connection": the union of Austria with Germany, completed in March 1938.
:::

:::definition{term="Appeasement"}
The policy of avoiding war by giving way to an aggressor's demands, provided they seemed reasonable. It is linked above all with Neville Chamberlain, British Prime Minister from May 1937 to May 1940.
:::

:::definition{term="Sudetenland"}
The border regions of Czechoslovakia where about three million German speakers lived, and where Czechoslovakia's main frontier fortifications stood.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- **Kurt Schuschnigg**: Austrian Chancellor in 1938.
- **Konrad Henlein**: leader of the Sudeten German Party.
- **Edouard Daladier**: French Prime Minister at Munich.
- 12 March 1938: German troops enter Austria.
- 15 September 1938: Chamberlain meets Hitler at Berchtesgaden.
- 22-23 September 1938: second meeting at Bad Godesberg.
- 29-30 September 1938: the Munich Conference.
:::

## The Anschluss, March 1938

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| February 1938 | Hitler pressures Schuschnigg to give Austrian Nazis government posts |
| 9 March 1938 | Schuschnigg announces a plebiscite on Austrian independence |
| 11 March 1938 | Under German threats, Schuschnigg cancels the vote and resigns |
| 12 March 1938 | German troops enter Austria without resistance |
| 13 March 1938 | The Anschluss is proclaimed |
| 10 April 1938 | A Nazi-run plebiscite records over 99 per cent approval |

In February 1938 Hitler forced Schuschnigg to appoint the Austrian Nazi **Arthur Seyss-Inquart** as Minister of the Interior, in charge of the police. When Schuschnigg tried to rally support by calling a plebiscite on independence, Hitler demanded that it be cancelled. Schuschnigg resigned, and on 12 March German troops crossed the border. Many Austrians welcomed them, while opponents of Nazism, and above all Austria's Jews, faced immediate persecution.

**Why did no one stop it?** Mussolini, who had protected Austria in 1934, was now Hitler's ally. Britain and France protested but did nothing; many in Britain thought union between two German-speaking countries was natural.

**Consequences:** Germany gained Austria's army, gold reserves and iron ore, and now surrounded western Czechoslovakia on three sides.

## The Sudeten crisis and Munich

Czechoslovakia was a democracy with a well-equipped army, strong fortifications in the Sudeten mountains, the large Skoda arms works and alliances with France and the USSR. But its German minority, organised by Konrad Henlein and encouraged by Hitler, demanded more and more rights, and Hitler threatened to "protect" them.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 15 September 1938 | Berchtesgaden: Hitler demands the areas with a German majority; Britain and France press Czechoslovakia to agree |
| 22-23 September 1938 | Bad Godesberg: Hitler raises his demands, insisting on immediate occupation |
| Late September 1938 | Europe prepares for war; Mussolini suggests a conference |
| 29-30 September 1938 | Munich: Hitler, Chamberlain, Daladier and Mussolini agree the Sudetenland will go to Germany |
| 1-10 October 1938 | German troops occupy the Sudetenland |

At Munich, Germany was allowed to occupy the Sudetenland in stages from 1 October. Czechoslovakia and the USSR were not invited. The Czech government had to accept or fight alone. Chamberlain and Hitler also signed a short declaration that Britain and Germany would never go to war again, and on his return Chamberlain told crowds in London, "I believe it is peace for our time" (30 September 1938).

**Consequences for Czechoslovakia:** it lost its fortifications, much of its industry and about three million people. Poland seized the Teschen area, and Hungary took land in southern Slovakia. In March 1939, as the next lesson shows, Hitler occupied the rest of the Czech lands.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did Britain give way at Munich?"}
1. **Opinion:** many British people believed the Sudeten Germans had a fair claim under self-determination.
2. **Military weakness:** British chiefs of staff warned that Britain was not ready for war, especially against air attack.
3. **The Dominions:** countries such as Canada and South Africa made clear that they would not fight over Czechoslovakia.
4. **France:** France had a treaty with Czechoslovakia but would not act without Britain.
5. **Result:** Chamberlain chose a negotiated settlement that cost Britain nothing in the short term and the Czechs a great deal.
:::

## Why did Chamberlain follow appeasement?

- **Memories of 1914-1918:** almost every family had lost someone; public opinion strongly opposed another war.
- **Versailles seemed unfair:** many accepted that Germany had genuine grievances.
- **Unreadiness:** British rearmament had only recently speeded up, and air defences were not yet ready.
- **Cost:** after the Depression, Britain could not easily afford a major war.
- **Empire and allies:** the Dominions were reluctant, the USA was isolationist, and France was divided.
- **Fear of communism:** some saw a strong Germany as a barrier against the Soviet Union.
- **Other threats:** Japan in Asia and Italy in the Mediterranean also threatened the British Empire.

## Was appeasement justified?

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: the case for and against"}
**Against appeasement (the view of critics such as Winston Churchill, and of the 1940 book *Guilty Men*):** it encouraged Hitler to make more demands; it handed over a democratic ally with a good army and strong defences; it convinced Stalin that Britain and France could not be trusted, helping to bring about the Nazi-Soviet Pact; and Germany used the extra year to grow stronger too.

**For appeasement (the view of many later "revisionist" historians):** Britain was not ready for war in 1938; the year gained allowed more fighter aircraft and radar stations; the Dominions and British public were united in 1939 in a way they were not in 1938; and Hitler's broken promise in March 1939 showed the world that he could not be trusted.

**Judgement:** historians still disagree. A balanced view is that appeasement was understandable given Britain's position, but rested on a mistaken belief that Hitler's aims were limited.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Czechs agreed at Munich"}
Czechoslovakia was not represented at the Munich Conference. Its government was told to accept the decision.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Berchtesgaden, Godesberg and Munich"}
Remember the order: Berchtesgaden (15 September), Godesberg (22-23 September), Munich (29-30 September).
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating appeasement as simple cowardice"}
Examiners reward answers that explain the reasons for appeasement and judge it in the context of 1938, not only with hindsight.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Assess whether appeasement was justified"}
In a Cambridge Paper 1 (c) or ZIMSEC (c) answer, give the case for and the case against, each with specific evidence (dates, events, military facts), then a judgement that says what "justified" means to you.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Source questions on Munich (Paper 2)"}
Expect cartoons and newspaper extracts from 1938. Ask whether the source was written before or after March 1939, because Hitler's occupation of Prague changed many people's view of Munich.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- German troops entered Austria on 12 March 1938; the Anschluss broke Versailles but was not resisted.
- Hitler then demanded the Sudetenland, home to about three million German speakers.
- After meetings at Berchtesgaden and Bad Godesberg, the Munich Agreement (29-30 September 1938) gave Germany the Sudetenland; the Czechs and the USSR were excluded.
- Chamberlain appeased Hitler because of public opinion, British unreadiness, sympathy for German grievances and a lack of allies.
- Historians debate whether appeasement was a disastrous mistake or a realistic policy that bought time.
:::
