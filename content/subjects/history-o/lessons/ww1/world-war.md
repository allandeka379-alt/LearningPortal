## Introduction

When Britain declared war on 4 August 1914, it declared war for its whole empire. Canadians, Indians, Australians, New Zealanders and South Africans fought, and so did Africans from every colony. In Southern Rhodesia white volunteers left for Europe and Africa, and in 1916 the **Rhodesia Native Regiment** was raised to fight in German East Africa. Japan, Britain's ally since 1902, joined in August 1914, and in 1916 Arab forces rose against the Ottoman Empire.

This lesson asks the question set by Cambridge Depth study A, key question 2: **to what extent was it a world war?** It covers British Empire troops on the Western Front (Indian and Canadian case studies), the war in Africa, Japan's role and the Arab Revolt.

You should already know about the Western Front and the stalemate of 1914-16.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Dominion"}
A self-governing country of the British Empire, such as Canada, Australia, New Zealand or South Africa. The dominions ran their own affairs but were at war when Britain was.
:::

:::definition{term="Askari"}
African soldiers serving in the colonial armies of East Africa, especially in the German forces of Lettow-Vorbeck.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrier"}
An African porter who carried food, ammunition and equipment for the armies, often over hundreds of kilometres on foot.
:::

:::definition{term="Arab Revolt"}
The rising of Arab forces led by Sharif Hussein of Mecca and his sons against Ottoman rule, which began in June 1916 with British support.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and facts to learn"}
- **October 1914:** Indian troops in action at Ypres.
- **July 1915:** German South West Africa surrenders to South African forces.
- **June 1916:** the Arab Revolt begins.
- **9-12 April 1917:** Canadian Corps captures Vimy Ridge.
- **25 November 1918:** Lettow-Vorbeck surrenders in Northern Rhodesia.
:::

## British Empire troops on the Western Front

### India

India sent the largest army of any part of the empire: over a million Indians served during the war, in France, East Africa, Mesopotamia, Egypt and Palestine. In late 1914, when Britain's small regular army was being destroyed around **Ypres**, two Indian infantry divisions arrived and helped hold the line. **Khudadad Khan** became the first Indian soldier to win the Victoria Cross, at Ypres in October 1914. The Indian Corps fought at **Neuve Chapelle** (March 1915) and Loos. They faced a cold climate they were not equipped for, heavy losses and difficulties in replacing officers who spoke their languages. At the end of 1915 the Indian infantry left France for other fronts, though Indian cavalry stayed.

### Canada

Canada, a dominion of about eight million people, raised an army of over 600,000 men. Canadians faced the first German gas attack at **Second Ypres** (April 1915) and held their ground. Their most famous victory came at **Vimy Ridge** (9-12 April 1917), when all four Canadian divisions attacked together and took a ridge that earlier French and British attacks had failed to capture. Careful planning, rehearsals and a creeping barrage were key. Under their own commander, Arthur Currie, the Canadians captured Passchendaele (1917) and led several attacks in the Hundred Days (1918).

:::example{title="A significance judged: how important were empire troops on the Western Front?"}
**Very important:** Indian troops filled a crucial gap in late 1914 when the BEF was nearly destroyed; Canadian and Australian divisions became some of the most effective attacking forces of 1917-18.

**Less decisive than the main armies:** the French army and the British army from the United Kingdom provided most of the soldiers on the Western Front, and American entry and German exhaustion explain the victory of 1918.

**Judgement:** empire troops were not the main reason for victory, but at key moments (1914 and 1918) they made a real difference, and their sacrifice changed how Canadians and Indians saw their place in the empire.
:::

## The war in Africa

### Togoland, Kamerun and South West Africa

British and French forces took **Togoland** in August 1914 and **Kamerun** by early 1916. South Africa, led by Louis Botha and Jan Smuts, first had to defeat a rebellion by some Afrikaners who refused to fight for Britain. Then South African forces conquered **German South West Africa** (now Namibia), which surrendered in July 1915.

### German East Africa

The longest African campaign was in **German East Africa** (now mainland Tanzania, Rwanda and Burundi). The German commander, **Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck**, with a small force made up mostly of African askari, aimed to tie down as many British Empire troops as possible.

- In November 1914 an Indian expeditionary force was badly defeated when it tried to land at **Tanga**.
- From 1916 Jan Smuts led a large army of **South African**, **Indian**, British and African troops, including the King's African Rifles, which drove the Germans out of most of the colony.
- Lettow-Vorbeck never surrendered in battle. His force moved into Portuguese East Africa (Mozambique) and then Northern Rhodesia, where he surrendered at Abercorn on **25 November 1918**, two weeks after the armistice.

The campaign depended on **carriers**. There were few roads or railways, and the tsetse fly killed horses and oxen. Estimates vary, but historians commonly suggest that around a million Africans were used as carriers by the armies in East Africa, and that more than 100,000 died, mostly from disease, hunger and exhaustion. Villages lost their young men, food was taken by both sides, and famine followed in parts of the region.

:::example{title="A source of evidence analysed: what can we know about the carriers?"}
Very few carriers left written records. Historians rely on army statistics, which are incomplete; on missionaries' and officials' reports; and on oral histories recorded later. Each has problems: army figures often did not count carriers who deserted or died away from camps, and memories recorded decades later may have changed. Comparing all three types of evidence gives the most reliable picture.
:::

## Japan

Japan declared war on Germany on 23 August 1914. With a small British force, it besieged and captured the German base at **Tsingtao** (Qingdao) in China in November 1914, and it occupied Germany's islands in the North Pacific. The Japanese navy helped hunt German cruisers and escorted troop convoys carrying Australian and New Zealand soldiers across the Indian Ocean. From 1917 Japanese destroyers based at Malta escorted Allied ships in the Mediterranean against U-boats.

Japan's contribution was limited in scale, and it also used the war to increase its own power, for example with the Twenty-One Demands on China (1915). At Versailles it kept Germany's rights in Shandong.

## The Arab Revolt

In 1915-16 British officials in Egypt exchanged letters with **Sharif Hussein** of Mecca, promising support for Arab independence (in unclear terms) if he rose against the Ottomans. The revolt began in **June 1916**. Led by Hussein's sons, especially **Faisal**, and advised by British officers including T. E. Lawrence, Arab forces attacked the Hejaz railway, captured the port of **Aqaba** (July 1917) and entered **Damascus** in October 1918 alongside the British advance under General Allenby.

Historians debate its importance. It tied down thousands of Ottoman troops and protected Allenby's flank, but the main defeats of the Ottoman army were inflicted by Allenby's regular forces, including many Indian troops. Britain and France had also secretly agreed in 1916 (the Sykes-Picot Agreement) to divide the Arab lands between them, which caused lasting resentment.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the war as only the Western Front"}
Cambridge asks how far it was a world war: you need Africa, Asia and the Middle East, with examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Lettow-Vorbeck was defeated in battle"}
He surrendered after the armistice in Europe, undefeated in the field.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the carriers"}
Carriers are central to the East African campaign and to its human cost.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Over a million Indians served; Indian troops helped hold Ypres in 1914 and fought at Neuve Chapelle.
- Canada raised over 600,000 men; its corps took Vimy Ridge in April 1917.
- In Africa, South Africa conquered South West Africa (1915); in East Africa Lettow-Vorbeck held out until 25 November 1918, and very many carriers died.
- Japan took Tsingtao (1914), seized German Pacific islands and escorted Allied shipping.
- The Arab Revolt (from June 1916) helped Allenby defeat the Ottomans; its importance is debated.
:::
