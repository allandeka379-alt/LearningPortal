## Introduction

The trenches of France dominate how the First World War is remembered, but the war was also fought on the oceans, on the cliffs of Gallipoli and across the vast plains of eastern Europe. It reached into ordinary homes too: food was rationed, women took on new work, and civilians were bombed from the air for the first time. Ships carrying food and soldiers passed the coasts of Africa, and U-boats hunted merchant ships across the Atlantic and the Mediterranean.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study A, key question 3: **how important were other fronts?** It examines who won the war at sea, why the Gallipoli campaign failed, how important the Eastern Front was, and how the war affected civilians.

You should already know why the Western Front became a stalemate.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blockade"}
Using naval power to stop goods reaching an enemy country. The British blockade of Germany aimed to cut off food and raw materials.
:::

:::definition{term="Unrestricted submarine warfare"}
Sinking any ship in a declared war zone, including neutral merchant ships, without warning.
:::

:::definition{term="Convoy"}
A group of merchant ships sailing together under the protection of warships.
:::

:::definition{term="Total war"}
A war in which a country's whole population and economy are mobilised, so civilians become part of the war effort and sometimes its targets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}

:::

## The war at sea

**The blockade.** From 1914 the Royal Navy stopped ships carrying goods to Germany. Over time this caused serious shortages of food, fertiliser and raw materials. The winter of 1916-17 became known in Germany as the "turnip winter".

**Jutland.** The only major battle between the British Grand Fleet (Admiral Jellicoe) and the German High Seas Fleet (Admiral Scheer) was fought off Denmark on 31 May-1 June 1916. Britain lost more ships and about twice as many men, so Germany claimed victory. But the German fleet returned to port and never again seriously challenged British control of the North Sea. The blockade continued.

**The U-boats.** Germany's answer to the blockade was the submarine. In 1915 U-boats sank the liner *Lusitania*, killing about 1,200 people, including over 120 Americans. American anger led Germany to limit its attacks. In February 1917 Germany gambled on **unrestricted submarine warfare**, hoping to starve Britain out before the USA could make a difference. Losses of shipping were huge in the spring of 1917, but the USA declared war in April.

**Convoys.** From 1917 Britain began sailing merchant ships in **convoys** protected by destroyers. Losses fell sharply. Depth charges, mines and better detection also helped.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: who won the war at sea?"}
**Germany could claim success:** it inflicted heavier losses at Jutland, and its U-boats came close to cutting Britain's supply lines in 1917.

**But Britain won the war at sea:** it kept control of the oceans, kept supplies and troops moving from the empire and the USA, defeated the U-boat threat with convoys, and its blockade weakened Germany's economy, army and morale. Unrestricted submarine warfare also brought the USA into the war against Germany.

**Judgement:** most historians conclude that Britain won the war at sea, because sea power allowed it to keep fighting and slowly strangled Germany.
:::

## Gallipoli, 1915

By early 1915 the Ottoman Empire had joined Germany. Some British leaders, especially **Winston Churchill** at the Admiralty, argued for an attack through the **Dardanelles** to capture Constantinople, knock the Ottomans out of the war and open a sea route to Russia.

- On 18 March 1915 an Allied fleet tried to force the straits, but mines sank or damaged several battleships, and the fleet withdrew.
- On 25 April British, French, Australian and New Zealand (ANZAC) troops landed on the **Gallipoli peninsula**. The Ottoman defenders, among them the commander **Mustafa Kemal**, held the high ground.
- A new landing at Suvla Bay in August also failed to break out.
- The Allies withdrew in December 1915-January 1916. Historians estimate that each side suffered roughly a quarter of a million casualties.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did the Gallipoli campaign fail?"}
1. **Surprise was lost:** the naval attack in March warned the Ottomans, who strengthened their defences before the landings.
2. **Poor planning and intelligence:** maps were inaccurate, and some troops were landed in the wrong places, facing steep cliffs.
3. **Strong defence:** Ottoman troops, well led and fighting for their homeland, held the heights.
4. **Weak leadership and slow decisions:** commanders failed to exploit early chances, notably at Suvla Bay.
5. **Conditions:** heat, flies, shortages of water and disease such as dysentery weakened the attackers.
:::

## The Eastern Front

The war in the east was more mobile than in the west. After **Tannenberg** (August 1914) and the Masurian Lakes, the Russians were driven out of East Prussia, but they defeated the Austro-Hungarians in Galicia. In 1915 a German and Austro-Hungarian offensive at Gorlice-Tarnów forced the Russians into a long retreat, and Tsar Nicholas II took personal command of the army. The **Brusilov Offensive** of 1916 inflicted huge losses on Austria-Hungary but also on Russia.

Shortages, defeats and enormous casualties helped bring about the revolution of March 1917 (February in the old Russian calendar). After the Bolsheviks seized power in November 1917 (October, old style), Russia made peace at **Brest-Litovsk** on 3 March 1918, giving up Poland, the Baltic lands and Ukraine.

The Eastern Front mattered because it forced Germany to fight on two fronts until 1917 and drew German troops away from France in 1914. Russia's collapse, however, freed German divisions for the Spring Offensive of 1918.

## The impact on civilians

- **Britain:** the Defence of the Realm Act (1914) gave the government wide powers; conscription began in 1916; rationing of sugar, meat and butter was introduced in 1918. Hundreds of thousands of women worked in munitions factories and other jobs. Zeppelin and later aeroplane raids killed civilians in London and other towns.
- **Germany:** the blockade caused hunger, substitute ("ersatz") foods and rising discontent. Estimates of extra civilian deaths linked to the blockade vary widely, but historians agree they were in the hundreds of thousands.
- **Occupied Belgium and northern France:** civilians lived under military rule, and many were deported to work for Germany.
- **The Ottoman Empire:** from 1915 the Armenian population was deported and massacred; historians estimate that between several hundred thousand and over a million Armenians died.
- **Everywhere:** almost every family lost someone. The influenza pandemic of 1918-19 killed tens of millions more around the world.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Britain lost Jutland and therefore the war at sea"}
Britain lost more ships at Jutland but kept control of the North Sea, so the blockade continued.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the March naval attack with the April landings"}
The naval attack on the Dardanelles came on 18 March 1915; the landings at Gallipoli began on 25 April.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the Russian calendar"}
Write "the February (March) Revolution" and "the October (November) Revolution" to show both old- and new-style dates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 part (c): who won the war at sea?"}
Judge by different measures: battle losses, control of the seas, the blockade, and the U-boat campaign. A one-sided answer cannot reach the top level.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the impact on civilians"}
Name the country and give specific examples (DORA, rationing, munitions work, air raids, the turnip winter), then explain the effect on daily life or morale.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Jutland (1916) was indecisive in losses but left Britain in control of the North Sea; the blockade continued.
- Unrestricted U-boat warfare (1917) nearly starved Britain, but convoys defeated it and it brought the USA into the war.
- Gallipoli (April 1915-January 1916) failed through lost surprise, poor planning, strong Ottoman defence and harsh conditions.
- The Eastern Front tied down German forces until Russia's collapse and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918).
- Civilians faced rationing, conscription, bombing, occupation and, in Germany, hunger caused by the blockade.
:::
