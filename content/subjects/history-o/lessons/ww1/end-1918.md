## Introduction

In March 1918 Germany seemed closer to victory than at any time since 1914. Russia had left the war, German troops were moving west, and a huge offensive drove the British and French back towards Paris. Eight months later Germany asked for an armistice, its Kaiser had fled, and a republic had been declared in Berlin. The speed of that collapse shocked many Germans, and the myth that the army had been "stabbed in the back" by traitors at home would later help Hitler.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study A, key question 4: **why did Germany ask for an armistice in 1918?** It also links to the end of the war in Africa, where Lettow-Vorbeck surrendered only after he heard the news from Europe.

You should already know about the stalemate on the Western Front and the defeat of Russia.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Armistice"}
An agreement to stop fighting while peace terms are negotiated. It is not a peace treaty.
:::

:::definition{term="Stormtroopers"}
Specially trained German assault units that used infiltration tactics in 1918: they bypassed strong points and pushed deep into enemy lines.
:::

:::definition{term="Abdication"}
When a monarch gives up the throne.
:::

:::definition{term="Stab-in-the-back myth"}
The false claim, spread in Germany after 1918, that the army had not been defeated in battle but betrayed by socialists, politicians and Jews at home.
:::

## American entry into the war

The USA declared war on 6 April 1917 because Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare sank American ships, and because the **Zimmermann Telegram**, intercepted by British intelligence, showed Germany offering Mexico an alliance against the USA. American loans and trade had also tied the USA to the Allies.

At first the US army was small, and few American troops reached France in 1917. By the autumn of 1918, however, well over a million American soldiers were in France, arriving at a rate of hundreds of thousands a month, under General **Pershing**. They fought at Château-Thierry and Belleau Wood (June 1918), Saint-Mihiel and the Meuse-Argonne (September-November 1918). Just as important were American food, raw materials, ships and loans, and the effect on morale: the Allies knew they would grow stronger every month, while Germany knew it would not.

## The Spring Offensive, 1918

General **Ludendorff** decided to gamble on a knockout blow in the west before the Americans arrived in strength. Using divisions freed from the Eastern Front after Brest-Litovsk, Germany attacked on **21 March 1918**. Short, intense bombardments and **stormtroopers** broke through the British lines, and in places the Germans advanced around 60 kilometres, further than any army on the Western Front since 1914. Further attacks followed in April, May and July.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did the Spring Offensive fail?"}
1. **No clear objective:** Ludendorff kept switching the point of attack instead of concentrating on one decisive aim.
2. **Supply problems:** the stormtroopers advanced faster than food, ammunition and guns could follow across the old battlefields.
3. **Exhaustion and losses:** Germany lost hundreds of thousands of men, including its best assault troops, who could not be replaced.
4. **Morale:** hungry German soldiers stopped to loot Allied supply dumps, discovering that the enemy was well fed.
5. **Allied response:** in April 1918 General **Foch** was made supreme commander of the Allied armies, so reserves could be moved where needed, and American troops began to arrive in large numbers.
:::

## The Allied advance: the Hundred Days

In July the French, with American help, counter-attacked at the **Second Battle of the Marne**. On **8 August 1918**, at **Amiens**, British, Australian, Canadian and French forces attacked with hundreds of tanks, aircraft and accurate artillery, and German units surrendered in large numbers. Ludendorff later called it the black day of the German army. In the **Hundred Days** that followed, the Allies pushed the Germans back, breaking the **Hindenburg Line** in late September.

At the same time Germany's allies collapsed: Bulgaria asked for an armistice at the end of September, the Ottoman Empire at the end of October and Austria-Hungary in early November. On 29 September Ludendorff told the Kaiser that the war was lost and that a new government should ask for peace. In early October the new Chancellor, Prince Max of Baden, asked President Wilson for an armistice based on his **Fourteen Points**.

## Conditions in Germany

- **The blockade** had caused severe food shortages; many people were hungry and weakened, and the influenza pandemic struck in 1918.
- **Strikes** and protests against the war had grown since 1917.
- **The Kiel mutiny:** at the end of October the naval command ordered the fleet to sea for a final battle. Sailors refused, and in early November the mutiny at **Kiel** spread; workers' and soldiers' councils took control in many cities, including Munich.
- **Abdication:** Wilson made clear that he would not negotiate with the Kaiser's government. On **9 November** Prince Max announced the Kaiser's abdication, the Social Democrat **Friedrich Ebert** became head of government, and a republic was proclaimed. Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was Germany defeated in the field, or did it collapse at home?"}
**Military defeat:** after August 1918 the German army was retreating, its allies had surrendered, and its own commanders said the war was lost and asked for an armistice before the revolution began.

**Collapse at home:** the mutiny and revolution in November made continuing the war impossible and forced the Kaiser out.

**Judgement:** historians agree that Germany was defeated militarily; the revolution followed the request for an armistice. The stab-in-the-back myth turned this order upside down for political reasons.
:::

## The Armistice

The German delegation, led by the politician **Matthias Erzberger**, signed the Armistice in Marshal Foch's railway carriage in the forest of **Compiègne** early on 11 November. Fighting stopped at **11 a.m.** Germany had to withdraw from all occupied land and from the left bank of the Rhine, which the Allies would occupy; hand over large quantities of guns, aircraft and its submarines; intern its surface fleet; and cancel the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk. The blockade continued until peace was signed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Armistice with the Treaty of Versailles"}
The Armistice (11 November 1918) stopped the fighting. The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Americans won the war on their own"}
American entry was vital, but German losses in the Spring Offensive, the blockade, Allied tactics in the Hundred Days and the collapse of Germany's allies all mattered.
:::

:::mistake{title="Repeating the stab-in-the-back myth"}
Germany's generals asked for an armistice before the revolution. The myth was propaganda, not history.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 part (b): explain why the Spring Offensive failed"}
Give three or four developed reasons: supply problems, lost elite troops, no single objective, unified Allied command and American reinforcements.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 4: how important was American entry?"}
Judge importance in different ways: troops, supplies and money, morale, and its effect on German decisions such as launching the Spring Offensive. Compare it with the blockade and the Hundred Days.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The USA entered the war in April 1917 after unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram; by late 1918 over a million US soldiers were in France.
- The Spring Offensive (from 21 March 1918) gained ground but failed through supply problems, heavy losses and lack of a clear aim.
- The Allied Hundred Days, from Amiens (8 August), broke the German army; Germany's allies collapsed.
- Hunger, strikes, the Kiel mutiny and revolution led to the Kaiser's abdication on 9 November 1918.
- The Armistice took effect at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918.
:::
