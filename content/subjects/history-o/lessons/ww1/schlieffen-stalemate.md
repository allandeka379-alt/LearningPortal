## Introduction

In August 1914 soldiers on every side expected the war to be short. Many German soldiers expected to be home by the autumn, and crowds in Paris, Berlin and London cheered them on their way. Yet by Christmas 1914 the armies of the Western Front were crouching in trenches that would barely move for more than three years.

This lesson explains why. It follows Germany's **Schlieffen Plan** from its design to its failure, and then explains why both sides turned to a **defensive war of attrition**. It is the first key question of Cambridge Depth study A (The First World War, 1914-18).

You should already know why the war broke out, and that Germany faced France in the west and Russia in the east.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Schlieffen Plan"}
Germany's war plan, drawn up by Count Alfred von Schlieffen (chief of the German General Staff until 1906) and adapted by his successor Helmuth von Moltke. It aimed to defeat France in about six weeks by a great sweep through Belgium, before turning east to face Russia.
:::

:::definition{term="British Expeditionary Force (BEF)"}
The small, highly trained professional army that Britain sent to France and Belgium in August 1914.
:::

:::definition{term="Stalemate"}
A situation in which neither side can defeat the other or make a decisive breakthrough.
:::

:::definition{term="Attrition"}
Wearing down the enemy by killing and wounding more of his men, and using up more of his resources, than he can replace.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **4 August 1914:** German troops enter Belgium.
- **23 August:** Battle of Mons.
- **26-30 August:** Battle of Tannenberg (east).
- **6-12 September:** First Battle of the Marne.
- **October-November:** First Battle of Ypres.
:::

## The plan

| Date (1914) | Event |
|---|---|
| 4 August | Germany invades Belgium; Britain declares war |
| 4-16 August | Belgian forts at Liège hold up the German advance |
| 23 August | Battle of Mons: the BEF delays the Germans, then retreats |
| Late August | Russian armies invade East Prussia; Germany moves troops east |
| 26-30 August | German victory over Russia at Tannenberg |
| 6-12 September | First Battle of the Marne: the German advance is stopped |
| September-October | The "race to the sea" |
| October-November | First Battle of Ypres |
| December | Trench lines stretch from the North Sea to Switzerland |

The plan rested on several assumptions:

1. **Russia would be slow.** Its huge army, poor railways and long distances meant it would need about six weeks to mobilise.
2. **France could be beaten quickly.** Instead of attacking France's strong frontier forts, a powerful right wing would sweep through neutral Belgium, swing round west of Paris and trap the French armies against their own frontier.
3. **Belgium would not resist** seriously, and **Britain would not fight**, or would arrive too late to matter.

Moltke changed the plan before 1914. He kept more troops in the south to protect Alsace-Lorraine and decided not to march through the Netherlands, which squeezed the right wing through a narrower path in Belgium.

## The plan in practice

### Belgium

The Belgian army was small, but the ring of forts around **Liège** held out for about twelve days and blew up railways and bridges. The delay was short, but the plan had no time to spare. German troops also shot civilians and burned towns such as Louvain in the belief that Belgian civilians were firing on them, which turned world opinion against Germany.

### The BEF and Mons

Britain declared war and sent the **BEF** to France. On 23 August at **Mons** it met the German First Army. The British soldiers' rapid, accurate rifle fire held the Germans up for a day before the BEF joined the general Allied retreat. It was a small battle, but it slowed the right wing at a crucial moment.

### Russia mobilises faster than expected

Russian armies invaded East Prussia in mid-August, well before Germany expected. Moltke sent troops from the west to the east, weakening the right wing. These troops arrived too late for **Tannenberg** (26-30 August), where Hindenburg and Ludendorff crushed one Russian army, but they were missing in France.

### The Marne

Tired after weeks of marching, short of supplies and with gaps between its armies, the German right wing turned to pass east of Paris instead of sweeping round it. This exposed its flank. The French commander, **Joffre**, counter-attacked at the **Marne** (6-12 September), helped by the BEF and by reserves rushed from Paris, some famously carried in Paris taxis. The Germans retreated to the River Aisne and dug in on high ground. Moltke was replaced.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did the Schlieffen Plan fail?"}
1. **Unrealistic timetable:** the plan needed everything to go right; Belgian resistance at Liège and the BEF at Mons caused delays.
2. **Russia was quicker than expected:** troops were moved east, weakening the right wing.
3. **Exhaustion and supply problems:** German soldiers marched on foot far beyond their railheads, while the French could move reserves by rail.
4. **Changed direction:** the turn east of Paris exposed the German flank.
5. **The Marne:** the French and British counter-attack forced a retreat. The quick victory was lost and Germany faced a long war on two fronts.
:::

## Why a defensive war had emerged by the end of 1914

After the Marne, each side tried to get round the other's northern flank. This "**race to the sea**" ended when the lines reached the Channel coast in Belgium. At the **First Battle of Ypres** (October-November 1914) the Germans tried to break through to the Channel ports; the British, French and Belgians held on, but most of Britain's pre-war regular army was killed or wounded.

By December 1914 two lines of trenches ran from the North Sea to the Swiss border, a distance of roughly 700 kilometres. There were no open flanks left to attack.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: what caused the stalemate?"}
**Defensive firepower:** machine guns, magazine rifles and quick-firing artillery could cut down attacking infantry crossing open ground. Digging in was the obvious way to survive.

**Railways and reserves:** defenders could bring reserves by train faster than attackers could advance on foot, so any gap could be plugged.

**Exhaustion and equality:** both sides had lost huge numbers of men by the end of 1914 and neither was strong enough to break through.

**Judgement:** historians usually stress that the technology of 1914 favoured defence. Generals had no reliable way to break through a trench line until new weapons and tactics developed in 1917-18.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Schlieffen Plan failed at Mons"}
Mons only delayed the Germans. The plan failed at the Marne, after other delays and the transfer of troops to the east.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Tannenberg with the Marne"}
Tannenberg (August 1914) was a German victory over Russia in East Prussia. The Marne (September 1914) was an Allied victory in France.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing events without explaining"}
A Cambridge part (b) on why the plan failed needs reasons linked to results, not a narrative of the battles.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1, Section B"}
Typical questions: (a) describe the Schlieffen Plan; (b) explain why it failed; (c) how far was the Marne the most important reason for the stalemate? For (c), weigh the Marne against the race to the sea, Ypres and defensive firepower.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 4"}
"Write an account of the war on the Western Front in 1914" needs events in order with dates and a sense of how each led to the next. Then expect a part asking how significant one battle was: judge it by its short-term and long-term effects.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Schlieffen Plan aimed to defeat France in about six weeks through Belgium before Russia was ready.
- Belgian resistance, the BEF at Mons and faster Russian mobilisation all weakened or delayed the plan.
- The French and British counter-attack at the Marne (6-12 September 1914) ended hopes of a quick German victory.
- The race to the sea and the First Battle of Ypres left trench lines from the North Sea to Switzerland.
- Defensive firepower, railways and exhaustion produced a war of attrition by the end of 1914.
:::
