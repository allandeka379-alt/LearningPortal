## Introduction

For more than three years millions of men lived in holes in the ground along the Western Front. They came from France, Britain, Germany and Belgium, and also from India, Canada, Australia, South Africa, Southern Rhodesia and many other parts of the world. Their experience shaped how the whole world remembers the First World War: mud, barbed wire, shell-fire and attacks that cost thousands of lives for a few hundred metres of ground.

This lesson describes trench life, explains how new weapons changed warfare, and examines the two great battles of 1916, **Verdun** and the **Somme**, together with the debate about the British commander, **Sir Douglas Haig**. It belongs to key question 1 of Cambridge Depth study A.

You should already know why stalemate had set in by the end of 1914.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="No man's land"}
The ground between the opposing front-line trenches, covered with barbed wire and shell craters.
:::

:::definition{term="Over the top"}
Climbing out of the trench to attack across no man's land.
:::

:::definition{term="Creeping barrage"}
Artillery fire that moves forward in stages just ahead of advancing infantry, to keep defenders in their dugouts until the attackers arrive.
:::

:::definition{term="Shell shock"}
The term used at the time for the mental breakdown caused by prolonged exposure to combat, shell-fire and fear.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **22 April 1915:** first large-scale German use of poison gas (chlorine) at Ypres.
- **21 February-December 1916:** Battle of Verdun.
- **1 July-18 November 1916:** Battle of the Somme.
- **15 September 1916:** tanks used for the first time, on the Somme.
- **November 1917:** massed tanks at Cambrai.
:::

## Living and fighting in the trenches

The trench system was not a single ditch. Behind the **front line** lay **support** and **reserve** trenches, linked by **communication trenches**. Trenches were dug in a zig-zag pattern so that an enemy who got in could not fire along the whole line, and a raised **fire-step** let soldiers shoot over the parapet. Men slept in dugouts cut into the trench walls.

Soldiers usually spent only a few days at a time in the front line before moving back. Daily life combined boredom and danger:

- **Dirt and disease:** rats and lice were everywhere. Standing in cold water caused **trench foot**, which could lead to amputation.
- **Weather:** in Flanders and the Somme the ground often turned into deep mud; winters were bitterly cold.
- **Constant danger:** snipers and shell-fire killed men even on "quiet" days. Most work, such as repairing wire and carrying supplies, was done at night.
- **Attacks and raids:** going over the top meant crossing no man's land under machine-gun and artillery fire.

## New methods of warfare

**Artillery** caused more casualties than any other weapon. Huge bombardments were meant to destroy wire and trenches before an attack, but they also churned the ground into craters and warned the enemy that an attack was coming.

**Machine guns** could fire hundreds of rounds a minute. A few well-placed guns could stop an attack by thousands of men, which made them the key defensive weapon.

**Poison gas** was first used on a large scale by Germany at Ypres in April 1915; later both sides used it, including chlorine, phosgene and mustard gas. It caused terror and painful injuries, but gas masks soon reduced its effect, and it caused only a small share of deaths. It never broke the stalemate.

**Tanks** were a British invention, first used on the Somme on 15 September 1916. The early machines were slow and often broke down or got stuck. Used in large numbers at **Cambrai** (1917) and **Amiens** (1918), alongside infantry, artillery and aircraft, they became much more effective.

**Aircraft** were at first used to observe enemy trenches and direct artillery. Fighter planes developed to shoot down enemy observers, and bombing began, including German Zeppelin raids on Britain.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: how important were the new weapons?"}
**Important:** machine guns and artillery explain why the stalemate lasted so long; tanks and aircraft, combined with better artillery tactics, helped the Allies break through in 1918.

**Less important than they seem:** gas never broke the stalemate, and early tanks were unreliable. What mattered in 1918 was using all the weapons together ("combined arms"), together with German exhaustion and American manpower.

**Judgement:** defensive weapons were decisive in creating the stalemate; offensive weapons mattered only once commanders learned to combine them.
:::

## Verdun, 1916

On 21 February 1916 the German army attacked **Verdun**, a fortress city of great symbolic importance to France. The German commander, Falkenhayn, later claimed that his aim was to wear down the French army by forcing it to defend Verdun at any cost; historians debate whether this was really his plan from the start or a justification afterwards. General **Pétain** organised the defence and kept supplies moving along a single road, later called the "Sacred Way". The fighting lasted until December. Historians estimate that the two sides together suffered roughly 700,000 casualties (killed, wounded and missing) for almost no change in the front line.

## The Somme, 1916

The British-led offensive on the **Somme** was planned with the French. When Verdun began, its aim also became to relieve pressure on the French. A week-long bombardment of more than a million shells was meant to destroy the German wire and defences. But many shells were faulty or the wrong type, and the Germans sheltered in deep dugouts.

On **1 July 1916** British troops advanced and were met by machine-gun fire. The British army suffered about 57,000 casualties, nearly 20,000 of them killed, on that one day: the worst day in its history. The battle went on until November 1916. The Allies advanced at most about ten kilometres. Total casualties on all sides are estimated at over a million.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did the first day of the Somme go so badly?"}
1. **The bombardment failed:** many shells did not explode, and shrapnel did not cut the thick wire.
2. **German defences were strong:** deep dugouts protected soldiers, who came up and manned their machine guns as soon as the shelling stopped.
3. **Tactics were rigid:** many battalions advanced in lines at walking pace, carrying heavy loads.
4. **Result:** heavy casualties for small gains, except in the south, where British and French troops did take their objectives.
:::

## Haig: a debated reputation

**Douglas Haig** commanded the British forces in France from December 1915 to the end of the war.

- **Critics** argue that Haig was stubborn and out of touch, kept attacking long after hope of a breakthrough had gone (at the Somme and at Passchendaele in 1917), and wasted lives. In the 1960s this view of generals as "donkeys" leading brave soldiers became very popular.
- **Defenders** argue that Haig had to fight in France to support his allies; that no commander had a way to avoid huge losses with the weapons of 1916; that the Somme relieved Verdun and badly weakened the German army; and that the British army learned and improved, winning a series of victories under Haig in 1918.

Historians still disagree. A balanced answer explains both views and judges them against evidence.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying tanks broke the stalemate in 1916"}
Early tanks were few and unreliable. They became effective only in large numbers, combined with other arms, in 1917-18.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Verdun and the Somme"}
Verdun was a German attack on the French (from February 1916); the Somme was a British and French attack on the Germans (from July 1916).
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving casualty figures as exact facts"}
Use the well-known first-day figure for the Somme, and describe other totals as historians' estimates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trench systems had front, support and reserve lines; soldiers faced rats, lice, trench foot, mud and constant danger.
- Artillery and machine guns made defence stronger than attack; gas never broke the stalemate.
- Tanks (first used September 1916) and aircraft became effective only when combined with other arms in 1917-18.
- Verdun (February-December 1916) and the Somme (July-November 1916) caused enormous losses for little ground.
- Haig's reputation is debated: a wasteful commander to critics, a general who learned and won in 1918 to defenders.
:::
