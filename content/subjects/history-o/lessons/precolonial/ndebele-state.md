## Introduction

Bulawayo, Zimbabwe's second city, takes its name from the capital of King Lobengula. The Ndebele state that he ruled had been founded by his father, **Mzilikazi**, who led his followers on a journey of nearly twenty years from present-day KwaZulu-Natal across the Highveld to south-western Zimbabwe. Along the way the Ndebele absorbed Sotho, Tswana and, later, Shona people, so the nation that settled near the Matobo Hills was a mixture of many peoples bound together by the king and the regiments.

This lesson explains how the Ndebele state was founded and organised, how it changed under Lobengula, and why it fell. You need to know about the Mfecane and Shaka's reforms, which Mzilikazi adapted. The war of 1893–94 is covered in its own lesson; here it is given in outline only.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Incorporation"}
The absorption of conquered or captured people into the Ndebele nation, especially by placing young men in the regiments and young women in Ndebele families.
:::

:::definition{term="Ibutho (plural amabutho)"}
A military regiment; in the Ndebele state each regiment also lived in its own regimental town and farmed.
:::

:::definition{term="Zansi, Enhla and Hole"}
The three social groups (often called castes) of the Ndebele state: Zansi (Nguni from the south), Enhla (people incorporated on the Highveld, mainly Sotho and Tswana) and Hole (people incorporated in Zimbabwe, mainly Shona).
:::

:::definition{term="Inxwala"}
The great annual ceremony of the Ndebele nation, linked with the first fruits, at which the king's authority was renewed and the regiments assembled.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
Mzilikazi broke with Shaka in about 1822. The Ndebele settled in south-western Zimbabwe in about 1838–1840. Mzilikazi died in 1868; Lobengula was installed as king in 1870. The state fell in the Anglo-Ndebele War of 1893–94.
:::

## Mzilikazi's break with Shaka and the migration

**Mzilikazi**, of the Khumalo clan, was a commander in Shaka's army. In about **1822**, after a raid, he refused to hand over the captured cattle to Shaka, and fled north with a small following to escape Shaka's anger.

1. He crossed the Drakensberg onto the **Highveld** of the present-day Transvaal (Gauteng area), raiding and absorbing Sotho and Tswana communities.
2. His people moved several times, settling for some years north of the Vaal and then further west, near present-day Zeerust (Mosega).
3. In **1836–1837** they clashed with the Voortrekkers (Boer emigrants from the Cape). After defeats by Boer commandos, with Griqua and Tswana allies, in 1837, Mzilikazi decided to move north, beyond their reach.
4. The Ndebele split into two groups. One, under the induna **Gundwane Ndiweni**, with the king's son **Nkulumane**, went to the area of the Matobo Hills; Mzilikazi led the other on a longer route to the west and north.
5. The main group, believing Mzilikazi dead, installed Nkulumane as king. When Mzilikazi rejoined them in about **1840**, he punished the izinduna responsible; tradition says they were executed at the hill now called **Ntabazinduna** ("hill of the izinduna"). What happened to Nkulumane is uncertain.

The Ndebele settled on the high, healthy grasslands of the south-west, good for cattle and free of tsetse fly, where the weakened Rozvi state had collapsed.

:::example{title="Explaining why Mzilikazi settled in south-western Zimbabwe"}
1. **Safety:** it was far from the Zulu and the Boers who had defeated him.
2. **Land:** the plateau's grasslands were good for cattle and crops and free of tsetse fly.
3. **Weak opposition:** the Rozvi state had been broken by Nguni attacks in the 1830s, so there was no strong power to resist.
4. **Resources and people:** there were Shona communities who could be incorporated or made to pay tribute, and the Mwari shrines of the Matobo Hills were nearby.
:::

## Political organisation

- **The king (inkosi)** was the head of state, commander of the army, chief judge and religious leader. He owned the nation's cattle in trust and could appoint and remove officials.
- **Izinduna** were senior officials and commanders who governed regiments and areas; the most senior advised the king in council.
- **Regiments (amabutho)** were the backbone of the state. Each regiment lived in its own **regimental town** under an induna, farmed and herded there, and fought when called. New regiments were formed from young men of the same age, including incorporated captives.
- **The capital**: Mzilikazi's capitals included Mhlahlandlela; Lobengula built **koBulawayo** in 1870 and later moved it a short distance.
- **Tributary areas**: many Shona communities outside the core of the state kept their own chiefs but paid tribute in cattle, grain or other goods; others were raided.

## Economic organisation

- **Cattle** were the main form of wealth. The king lent cattle to loyal subjects to herd and use (the *ukusisa* system), which built loyalty.
- **Raiding** neighbouring communities for cattle, grain and captives was an important source of wealth.
- **Tribute** in cattle, grain, iron goods, hoes and other products was paid by subject Shona communities.
- **Agriculture**: sorghum, millet, maize, pumpkins and other crops, grown largely by women.
- **Hunting** for meat, hides and especially ivory.
- **Trade** with European hunters and traders: ivory and cattle for guns, ammunition, cloth, beads and other goods, especially under Lobengula.
- **Crafts**: iron working, pottery and basket-making, much of it by incorporated Shona and Sotho people.

## Social and religious organisation

Ndebele society was divided into three groups:

| Group | Origin | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Zansi | Nguni who came with Mzilikazi from the south | Highest; many senior izinduna |
| Enhla | Sotho, Tswana and others incorporated on the Highveld | Middle |
| Hole | Shona and others incorporated in Zimbabwe | Lowest |

Marriage between the groups was discouraged, especially for Zansi, but the regiments mixed young men from all three, and individuals could rise through bravery and loyalty. Over time the divisions became less sharp. The **Inxwala** ceremony each year united the nation around the king. The Ndebele respected the **Mwari shrines** in the Matobo Hills and consulted them, and honoured their own ancestors.

## Lobengula's reign

When Mzilikazi died in 1868, there was a two-year **succession dispute**. Some regiments, especially the Zwangendaba regiment under Mbiko Masuku, supported the claim of Nkulumane (whose fate was unknown). **Lobengula** was installed in 1870 and defeated the rebel regiment. His reign saw growing pressure from outsiders: hunters, traders, missionaries and, after gold was found on the Rand in 1886, concession seekers. He tried to protect his kingdom by diplomacy, playing rivals against one another, but the Rudd Concession of 1888 and the Royal Charter of 1889 opened the way for the occupation of Mashonaland in 1890.

## Decline and fall

- **Internal divisions**: the succession dispute of 1868–1870 and tensions between the social groups.
- **European pressure**: missionaries, hunters and concession seekers undermined the king's authority; the Rudd Concession (1888) was used by Rhodes to obtain the Royal Charter.
- **Loss of tributaries**: the occupation of Mashonaland in 1890 cut Lobengula off from Shona tributary areas; disputes over raiding near Fort Victoria in 1893 gave the British South Africa Company its excuse for war.
- **Military defeat**: in the Anglo-Ndebele War (1893–94) Company forces with machine guns defeated the regiments; Bulawayo was burned and Lobengula fled north, where he died in early 1894. The state fell.

:::example{title="Weighing a judgement on the fall"}
**Claim:** "The Ndebele state fell because of its own weaknesses."

1. **For:** succession disputes and the resentment of raided and tributary Shona communities weakened it; raiding gave the Company an excuse to attack.
2. **Against:** the decisive causes were external: Rhodes's ambitions, the concessions obtained by deception, and the Company's machine guns.
3. **Judgement:** internal weaknesses existed, but the state fell mainly because of colonial aggression it could not match militarily.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing the Ndebele as only Nguni"}
Most Ndebele were incorporated Sotho, Tswana and Shona people. The Zansi were a minority.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Ndebele 'destroyed' the Rozvi"}
The Rozvi state had already collapsed under Nguni attacks in the 1830s before the Ndebele settled.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Mzilikazi's and Lobengula's reigns"}
Mzilikazi founded the state and ruled until 1868. Lobengula ruled from 1870 and faced the concession seekers and the Company.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mzilikazi broke with Shaka in about 1822, migrated across the Highveld and, after clashes with the Boers, settled in south-western Zimbabwe in about 1838–1840.
- The state grew by incorporating conquered peoples into regiments and families.
- The king ruled through izinduna and regiments living in regimental towns; tributary Shona communities paid tribute.
- The economy rested on cattle (lent through ukusisa), raiding, tribute, farming, hunting and trade.
- Society was divided into Zansi, Enhla and Hole; the Inxwala ceremony united the nation.
- Lobengula (from 1870) faced concession seekers; the state fell in the war of 1893–94.
:::
