## Introduction

In the early nineteenth century, wars and migrations spread out from the land between the Drakensberg and the Indian Ocean, in what is now KwaZulu-Natal, and changed the map of southern and central Africa. New states were born, including the Zulu, Swazi, Sotho, Gaza and Ndebele kingdoms, and Nguni-speaking groups travelled as far as present-day Zambia, Malawi and Tanzania. Some of them crossed Zimbabwe and helped bring down the Rozvi state; one of them, the Ndebele, settled there.

This period is called the **Mfecane**. This lesson explains what it was, why it happened (a question historians still debate), how Shaka built the Zulu state, and what its effects were. You should know about the Rozvi state; this lesson leads directly to the Ndebele state.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mfecane"}
A Nguni word usually translated as "the crushing" or "the scattering": the period of wars, state-building and migrations in southern Africa in about the 1810s to the 1830s. The Sotho-Tswana name is **Difaqane** (Lifaqane).
:::

:::definition{term="Amabutho (age regiments)"}
Military regiments formed from young men of about the same age, who served the king together; singular *ibutho*.
:::

:::definition{term="Iklwa"}
The short, broad-bladed stabbing spear associated with Shaka's reforms, used at close quarters instead of throwing.
:::

:::definition{term="Cow-horn formation"}
Shaka's battle tactic: the "chest" attacked the enemy head-on, the two "horns" encircled the enemy from the sides, and the "loins" waited in reserve.
:::

:::definition{term="Induna (plural izinduna)"}
A senior official or commander appointed by the king.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
Dingiswayo led the Mthethwa until his death in about 1817–1818. Shaka became chief of the Zulu in about 1816 and was assassinated in 1828. Zwangendaba's followers crossed the Zambezi in 1835. The Zulu kingdom was defeated by Britain in the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879.
:::

## Causes of the Mfecane: a debate

Historians agree that the region north of the Thukela (Tugela) River saw great upheaval, but they disagree about why. The main explanations are:

1. **Population growth and land pressure:** the population of the region seems to have grown in the eighteenth century, so good grazing and farmland became scarce.
2. **Drought and famine:** a severe drought and famine around the beginning of the nineteenth century, remembered as the **Madlathule** famine, increased competition for food and land.
3. **Trade competition:** the ivory and cattle trade with the Portuguese port at Delagoa Bay (Maputo) made controlling trade routes valuable, encouraging rulers to build stronger states.
4. **The rise of strong, rival states:** the Mthethwa under Dingiswayo, the Ndwandwe under Zwide and the Ngwane under Sobhuza competed for land and power; their wars drove weaker groups away.
5. **Shaka's military power and expansion:** the older view gave Shaka the central role.

Some historians, notably Julian Cobbing in the late 1980s, challenged the idea that the Mfecane was caused mainly by Zulu expansion. They argued that **slave-raiding** from Delagoa Bay and labour raiding from the Cape Colony frontier caused much of the disruption, and that settler writers had exaggerated Shaka's role to justify taking land that they claimed was "empty". Other historians reply that the evidence for large-scale slave-trading from Delagoa Bay before the 1820s is weak and that African state-building was the main driver. The debate continues; a good answer presents more than one view.

:::example{title="Explaining a cause step by step: land pressure"}
1. The population of the region grew, and herds of cattle grew with it.
2. The best well-watered grazing and farmland became scarce, especially when drought struck.
3. Chiefdoms competed for this land; stronger leaders organised larger forces to win it.
4. Defeated groups were absorbed or forced to flee, carrying the conflict to new areas.

This shows how a long-term condition (land pressure) led to short-term events (wars and migration).
:::

## Dingiswayo and the Mthethwa

**Dingiswayo**, ruler of the Mthethwa, built a large confederacy of chiefdoms in the early nineteenth century. He organised young men into **age regiments** rather than by clan, controlled trade with Delagoa Bay, and usually allowed defeated chiefs to keep their positions if they accepted his authority. He was killed in about 1817–1818 by **Zwide** of the Ndwandwe.

## Shaka's rise

**Shaka**, son of the Zulu chief Senzangakhona and Nandi, grew up partly in exile and served as a soldier in Dingiswayo's army, where he became a successful commander. With Dingiswayo's support he became chief of the small Zulu chiefdom in about 1816. After Dingiswayo's death he took over the Mthethwa confederacy and defeated the Ndwandwe under Zwide in battles in 1818–1819, after which the Zulu became the strongest power in the region.

### Shaka's military reforms

- **Age regiments (amabutho)** housed in royal military homesteads (*amakhanda*); loyalty was to the king, not to clan chiefs.
- **The iklwa** stabbing spear and a large cowhide shield, for close combat.
- **The cow-horn formation** to surround the enemy.
- **Strict discipline and training**; warriors are said to have been made to go barefoot for speed and mobility.
- **Delayed marriage**: men could marry only when the king gave a regiment permission, keeping them in service longer.
- **A standing army** ready at all times, supplied by the king's herds and by captured cattle.
- **Incorporation** of defeated peoples into the regiments, so the army and the state grew together.

## Organisation of the Zulu state

### Political

The **king** was supreme commander, judge and lawmaker. He appointed **izinduna** to command regiments and govern areas, replacing many hereditary chiefs with loyal officials. Royal homesteads across the kingdom were centres of royal power. Defeated chiefdoms were absorbed and became "Zulu".

### Economic

Wealth was in **cattle**; the king controlled huge herds from raids and tribute and redistributed them to reward loyal subjects. Women grew sorghum, millet and other crops. The king controlled trade in ivory and cattle with Delagoa Bay and, from the 1820s, with British traders at Port Natal (Durban).

### Social and religious

The age-regiment system mixed young men from different clans and built a common Zulu identity. The king led the important **first-fruits ceremony** (*umkhosi*), which strengthened the nation and his role as its religious head; ancestors were honoured and diviners consulted.

## Effects of the Mfecane

**Destructive effects**
- Widespread loss of life (numbers are not known with certainty), destruction of homes, crops and herds, famine and refugees.
- Some areas, such as parts of Natal and the Highveld, were left thinly populated for a time, which white settlers later used to claim that the land was empty.

**Constructive effects (state-building)**
- New, larger kingdoms: the Zulu (Shaka), Swazi (Sobhuza), Sotho (Moshoeshoe, who gathered refugees in the mountains), Gaza (Soshangane, in southern Mozambique), Ndebele (Mzilikazi).
- Spread of Nguni military methods and age regiments.

**Effects on central Africa and Zimbabwe**
- **Zwangendaba**'s Ngoni crossed Zimbabwe, raided the Rozvi and crossed the Zambezi in 1835, settling eventually in present-day Malawi, Zambia and Tanzania.
- **Nxaba** and **Nyamazana** also raided the Rozvi lands; tradition links the collapse of the Rozvi state and the death of the last Changamire with these invasions in the 1830s.
- **Soshangane**'s Gaza kingdom dominated the south-east, including parts of present-day south-eastern Zimbabwe.
- The **Kololo** under Sebetwane moved to the upper Zambezi (Barotseland).
- **Mzilikazi**'s Ndebele settled in south-western Zimbabwe in about 1838–1840.

:::example{title="Weighing the effects: destruction or state-building?"}
1. **Destruction:** loss of life, refugees and famine were real and severe, and they weakened older states such as the Rozvi.
2. **Construction:** at the same time, leaders such as Moshoeshoe and Mzilikazi built new, larger states that protected their people.
3. **Long-term:** depopulated areas were later claimed by Boer and British settlers, so the Mfecane indirectly helped colonisation.
4. **Judgement:** the Mfecane was both destructive and creative; which effect seems greater depends on the region and the time scale you choose.
:::

## Decline of the Zulu state

Shaka was **assassinated in 1828** by his half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana. Reasons suggested include harsh rule and constant wars, the extreme demands he made after his mother Nandi's death, and ambition within the royal family. Under **Dingane** the kingdom faced the Voortrekkers and was defeated at **Ncome (Blood River)** in December 1838. **Mpande** overthrew Dingane with Boer help in 1840 and ruled more peacefully. Under **Cetshwayo**, Britain invaded in 1879; the Zulu won a famous victory at **Isandlwana** but were defeated at **Ulundi**, and the kingdom was broken up.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shaka 'caused' the whole Mfecane"}
This is one view, now strongly challenged. Mention land pressure, drought, trade and rival states, and present the debate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Dingiswayo and Dingane"}
Dingiswayo was the Mthethwa ruler who helped Shaka rise. Dingane was Shaka's half-brother, who killed him and became king in 1828.
:::

:::mistake{title="Exaggerated casualty figures"}
Do not give precise death tolls. Historians do not know them; say "heavy loss of life".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Mfecane (Difaqane) was a period of wars, state-building and migration in about the 1810s–1830s.
- Its causes are debated: land pressure, drought, trade at Delagoa Bay, rival states, Shaka's expansion, and possibly slave and labour raiding.
- Dingiswayo created age regiments; Shaka built on them with the iklwa, the cow-horn formation, strict discipline and a standing army.
- The Zulu king ruled through izinduna, controlled cattle and trade, and led the first-fruits ceremony.
- Effects included destruction and refugees, new kingdoms, and Nguni migrations that crossed Zimbabwe and helped end the Rozvi state.
- The Zulu kingdom weakened after Shaka's assassination (1828) and was defeated by Britain in 1879.
:::
