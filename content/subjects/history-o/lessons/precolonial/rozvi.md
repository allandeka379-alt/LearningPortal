## Introduction

West of Bulawayo, on the banks of the Khami River, stand stone platforms decorated with chequer and herringbone patterns. Further east, at Naletale near Gweru, a single stone wall carries some of the finest decorative stonework in southern Africa. These sites belong to the states that ruled south-western Zimbabwe after Great Zimbabwe: first the **Torwa** state, then the **Rozvi** state of the Changamire kings, whose armies drove the Portuguese off the plateau at the end of the seventeenth century.

This lesson describes both states, how the Rozvi rose and were organised, and why they declined. You should know about Great Zimbabwe and the Mutapa state first; the Rozvi story continues into the Mfecane and the arrival of the Ndebele.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Torwa (Butua)"}
The state that ruled south-western Zimbabwe from about the mid-fifteenth century, with its capital at Khami; the Portuguese called its land Butua.
:::

:::definition{term="Changamire"}
The title of the rulers of the Rozvi state, taken from the line founded by Changamire Dombo.
:::

:::definition{term="Rozvi"}
The name of the people and state of the Changamire kings; it is often explained as meaning "destroyers", from their military successes.
:::

:::definition{term="Mwari cult"}
The religious system centred on shrines to Mwari (God) in the Matobo Hills (Matonjeni), whose priests and oracles were consulted on rain and important matters.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
The Torwa state at Khami flourished from about 1450 to the 1680s. Changamire Dombo defeated the Portuguese at Maungwe in 1684 and destroyed their settlement at Dambarare in 1693. Dombo died in the mid-1690s. Nguni invaders crossed the Rozvi lands in the 1830s.
:::

## The Torwa state and Khami

After the decline of Great Zimbabwe in the fifteenth century, power in the south-west passed to the **Torwa** rulers. Their capital, **Khami**, near present-day Bulawayo, was built on a series of terraced stone platforms, with decorated retaining walls (chequer, herringbone and cord patterns) and houses on top for the elite. Imported goods found at Khami, such as Chinese porcelain and glass beads, show that it continued the long-distance trade in gold and ivory. Khami became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986.

Khami appears to have been destroyed by fire in the seventeenth century, probably during a civil war in the Torwa state. In the 1680s the south-west was taken over by Changamire Dombo.

## The rise of the Rozvi under Changamire Dombo

**Changamire Dombo** first appears in Portuguese records as a herder and rising leader in the north-east of the plateau, within the Mutapa sphere. He built up a powerful following and army and, in the 1680s, took over the Torwa lands in the south-west. He then turned on the Portuguese, whose traders and armed followers had spread across the plateau after the Mutapa became their vassal:

1. In **1684**, at the battle of **Maungwe**, Dombo's army defeated a Portuguese force.
2. In **1693**, he attacked and destroyed the Portuguese trading settlement at **Dambarare**, near present-day Mazowe, and other Portuguese posts.
3. The Portuguese were **driven off the plateau**; afterwards they were allowed to trade only at fairs on the edges of the plateau, under Rozvi supervision.

The Rozvi rose because of:

- **strong military leadership** and a disciplined army;
- **the weakness of the Torwa and Mutapa** states, divided by civil wars;
- **African resentment of the Portuguese**, whose traders and armed followers had seized land and interfered in politics;
- **control of gold and cattle** and of the trade routes;
- **religious support**, especially links with the Mwari shrines.

:::example{title="Explaining why Dombo could expel the Portuguese"}
1. The Portuguese on the plateau were **spread thinly** in scattered trading posts and fairs, with few soldiers.
2. Their power depended on **African allies** and on divided African rulers; Dombo united a large following against them.
3. Dombo's army was **mobile and well led**, able to strike quickly at posts such as Dambarare.
4. Disease and distance from the coast meant the Portuguese could not send **reinforcements** quickly.
5. Many Africans **resented** Portuguese land seizures and interference, so Dombo gained support.
:::

## How the Rozvi state was organised

### Political organisation

The **Changamire** was the supreme ruler, with military, political and religious authority. He ruled from capitals such as **Danangombe** (Dhlo Dhlo) and later **Manyanga** (Ntaba zikaMambo). A council of senior officials and relatives advised him. **Provincial chiefs** governed subject areas, paid tribute and supplied soldiers; the Changamire could appoint or remove them. A strong army protected the state and collected tribute.

### Economic organisation

- **Cattle** were the main form of wealth; the king owned large herds.
- **Agriculture**: sorghum, millet and other crops.
- **Gold mining** and trade: gold and ivory were traded with the Portuguese at fairs on the edges of the plateau, such as Masekesa in Manica, under Rozvi control.
- **Tribute** in cattle, grain, ivory and other goods.
- **Crafts**: iron working, pottery, and building with stone at sites such as Danangombe and Naletale.

### Social organisation

The Rozvi ruling group formed the elite, with subject peoples below them. Kinship, totems and bridewealth in cattle organised family life. Stone-walled centres such as Naletale, with their decorated walls, show the wealth and status of the ruling group.

### Religious organisation

The Rozvi rulers were closely linked with the **Mwari cult** at the shrines in the Matobo Hills. Oracles there advised on rain, war and succession, and messengers carried offerings from across the state. Ancestral spirits were also honoured. This religious support helped hold the state together.

## Why the Rozvi state declined

- **Succession disputes** among the Changamire family after Dombo's death weakened central power.
- **Provinces broke away**, and the state shrank.
- **Drought** and famine in the early nineteenth century weakened the people.
- **Decline in trade**, as gold output fell and Portuguese trade declined.
- **Nguni invasions in the 1830s**: groups fleeing the Mfecane, including those led by Zwangendaba, Nxaba and Nyamazana, crossed and raided the Rozvi lands. Tradition says the last Changamire died at Ntaba zikaMambo during this time.
- **The arrival of the Ndebele** under Mzilikazi in about 1838–1840, who incorporated or made tributary many Rozvi and other Shona communities.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes of decline"}
**Claim:** "The Nguni invasions destroyed the Rozvi state."

1. **For:** the Nguni attacks of the 1830s were the immediate cause of collapse; the capital fell and the last Changamire died.
2. **Against:** the state was already weakened by succession disputes, breakaway provinces, drought and falling trade; a strong, united state might have resisted.
3. **Judgement:** the Nguni invasions were the final blow to a state already in decline, so both long-term and short-term causes are needed in a good answer.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the Torwa and the Rozvi"}
Khami was the Torwa capital. The Rozvi under Changamire Dombo took over the Torwa lands in the 1680s; their centres included Danangombe and Naletale.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Rozvi drove the Portuguese out of Africa"}
They drove the Portuguese off the Zimbabwean plateau. The Portuguese remained in the Zambezi valley and on the coast, and still traded at fairs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming only the Ndebele"}
The Rozvi state was already collapsing under Nguni attacks before the Ndebele settled. Give the internal causes as well.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Torwa state ruled the south-west from Khami (about 1450 to the 1680s), continuing the gold and ivory trade.
- Changamire Dombo took over the Torwa lands in the 1680s, defeated the Portuguese at Maungwe (1684) and destroyed Dambarare (1693), driving them off the plateau.
- The Changamire ruled with officials, provincial chiefs and a strong army; centres included Danangombe and Naletale.
- The economy rested on cattle, farming, gold and controlled trade at fairs; the Mwari cult gave religious support.
- Decline came from succession disputes, breakaways, drought, falling trade and the Nguni invasions of the 1830s; the Ndebele then settled the south-west.
:::
