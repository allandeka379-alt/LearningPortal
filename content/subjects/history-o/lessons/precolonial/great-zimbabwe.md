## Introduction

When Zimbabwe became independent in 1980, the new nation took its name from **Great Zimbabwe**, the stone-built capital near Masvingo, and the soapstone **Zimbabwe Bird** found there became a national symbol. The name comes from the Shona *dzimba dza mabwe*, "houses of stone". Great Zimbabwe was the centre of a powerful state that traded gold and ivory with the Indian Ocean coast, and its walls are the largest ancient stone structures in Africa south of the Sahara.

This lesson explains why the state rose, how it was organised, why it declined and how the truth about its African builders was established. It builds on the Early Iron Age (farming, cattle and trade) and leads to the Mutapa and Torwa-Rozvi states that followed. Dates are approximate and based on archaeology.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="State"}
A large political community under a central ruler, with a capital, officials, a system of collecting tribute or taxes, and control over a defined area and its peoples.
:::

:::definition{term="Tribute"}
Payments in goods (cattle, grain, ivory, gold, labour) given by subjects or conquered chiefs to a ruler as a sign of loyalty.
:::

:::definition{term="Social differentiation"}
The division of a society into groups with different wealth, status and power, for example a ruling elite and commoners.
:::

:::definition{term="Dry-stone walling"}
Building with shaped stone blocks fitted together without mortar to hold them.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
Mapungubwe flourished in about 1220–1290 CE. Great Zimbabwe was occupied from about the eleventh century and reached its height in about the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries; it declined in the fifteenth century. Great Zimbabwe became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986.
:::

## Mapungubwe: the forerunner

**Mapungubwe** lies on the south bank of the Limpopo River near where it meets the Shashe, close to where South Africa, Zimbabwe and Botswana meet today. In the thirteenth century it was the capital of the first large state in the region.

- **Trade:** Mapungubwe's rulers controlled the trade in ivory and gold down the Limpopo valley to the coast, receiving glass beads and cloth in return.
- **Social differentiation:** for the first time the ruler and elite lived apart from commoners, on top of a hill, while ordinary people lived below. Graves on the hilltop contained gold objects, including the famous small **golden rhinoceros**.
- **Decline:** by about 1290 Mapungubwe had been abandoned. Many historians link this to changes in climate and to the rise of Great Zimbabwe, which took over the trade.

## The rise of Great Zimbabwe

Historians explain the rise of the state by several factors working together:

1. **Cattle:** good grazing on the plateau allowed large herds; cattle were wealth that rulers could lend or give to win followers.
2. **Agriculture:** fertile soils and reliable rainfall supported a large population.
3. **Gold and ivory trade:** the rulers controlled trade routes to the coast. Gold from the plateau and ivory went to Sofala and then to the Swahili city of Kilwa; glass beads, cloth, Chinese porcelain and Persian pottery came back. A copper coin of a Kilwa ruler was found at Great Zimbabwe.
4. **Location:** the site lay on the edge of the plateau, with granite for building, wood, water and access to both highland and lowland grazing, and it was free of tsetse fly.
5. **Religion:** the ruler's link with the ancestors and with rain-making gave him spiritual authority.
6. **Leadership:** strong rulers who controlled trade and tribute could reward followers and organise labour.

:::example{title="Explaining the rise: how the factors connect"}
1. Good grazing and farming produced **wealth in cattle and food**.
2. That wealth let rulers attract followers and feed the workers who built walls and mined gold.
3. Control of **trade routes** brought luxury imports (beads, cloth, porcelain) that rulers gave to loyal chiefs.
4. Religious authority made the ruler's position seem sacred, so people accepted his rule.

A strong answer shows that no single factor was enough: trade made rulers rich, but cattle, farming and religion kept them in power.
:::

## How the state was organised

### Political organisation

The **king** lived in the Hill Complex and was the political, military and religious head. He ruled with the help of a council of advisers and royal relatives. Provincial chiefs governed outlying areas and sent **tribute** to the capital. Archaeologists have found more than a hundred smaller stone-walled sites of the same style across the plateau, suggesting provincial centres linked to the capital.

### Economic organisation

- **Cattle-keeping** was the base of wealth; cattle bones at the site show that the elite ate a lot of beef.
- **Agriculture:** sorghum, millet and other crops.
- **Mining:** gold was mined and panned on the plateau; copper and iron were also worked.
- **Trade:** long-distance trade with the coast; local trade in salt, iron, copper and cattle.
- **Tribute** in cattle, ivory, gold and grain flowed to the king.
- **Crafts:** iron working, spinning cotton (spindle whorls have been found), pottery and soapstone carving.

### Social organisation

Society was **differentiated**: the king and royal family lived inside the stone walls, while most of the population, possibly over ten thousand people at its height (estimates vary widely), lived in pole-and-daga huts outside them. Wealth was shown by cattle, imported goods and gold ornaments. Families were organised by kinship and totems.

### Religious organisation

People believed in **Mwari** (God) and honoured the ancestral spirits. The king was believed to communicate with the royal ancestors and played a part in rain-making ceremonies. Eight carved soapstone birds were found at the site, most in the Hill Complex; many historians think they represent royal ancestors.

## The stone buildings

| Part | Description |
|---|---|
| Hill Complex | The oldest part, built on a granite hill using natural boulders; probably the ruler's residence and religious centre, where most of the soapstone birds were found |
| Great Enclosure | A huge oval wall in the valley, about 250 m around and up to about 11 m high, with a solid **conical tower** inside; probably a royal residence or ceremonial centre |
| Valley Ruins | Smaller enclosures between the hill and the Great Enclosure, built later, probably for members of the elite |

The walls were built of granite blocks without mortar (**dry-stone walling**), with decorated patterns such as chevrons along the top of parts of the Great Enclosure.

## Why Great Zimbabwe declined

The causes of the decline in the fifteenth century are debated. The main **theories** are:

- **Overpopulation and environmental exhaustion:** a large population used up firewood, grazing and fertile soil around the capital.
- **Shift of trade routes:** the gold and ivory trade moved north to the Zambezi valley, so the rulers lost their main source of wealth.
- **Succession disputes:** quarrels within the royal family may have divided the state.
- **Shortage of salt** and other resources, which some traditions mention.
- **Drought** and pressure on food supplies.

:::example{title="Weighing the theories of decline"}
1. Archaeology shows the population was large and the site was occupied for centuries, which supports the **environmental exhaustion** theory.
2. Power moved north (Mutapa) and south-west (Torwa at Khami) at about the same time as trade shifted towards the Zambezi, which supports the **trade route** theory.
3. Oral traditions of Mutota leaving to find salt support the **resource shortage** theory but are hard to date.
4. **Judgement:** most historians think several causes combined. A good answer weighs at least two and says which seems strongest and why.
:::

## The myths and the evidence

When European explorers and settlers saw the ruins in the late nineteenth century, many refused to believe that Africans had built them. Karl Mauch (1871) linked them to the Queen of Sheba; others credited Phoenicians or Arabs. These myths were used to justify colonial rule by suggesting that Africans had no history of achievement. Treasure-hunting and careless digging, including by Richard Hall in the early 1900s, destroyed valuable evidence.

Archaeology refuted the myths. David Randall-MacIver (1905) and Gertrude Caton-Thompson (1929) found that the objects at the site, including the pottery and the style of building, were African and dated to medieval times; later radiocarbon dating confirmed this. The builders were the **ancestors of the Shona**. Even so, the Rhodesian government of the 1960s and 1970s discouraged official publications from stating this clearly.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Repeating the myths"}
Never suggest that Great Zimbabwe was built by outsiders. Archaeology shows it was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
:::

:::mistake{title="One cause of decline stated as fact"}
The decline is explained by theories. Write "historians suggest" and weigh more than one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Hill Complex and the Great Enclosure"}
The Hill Complex is the older hilltop site; the Great Enclosure is the large oval wall with the conical tower in the valley.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mapungubwe (c. 1220–1290) on the Limpopo was the first large state, with trade in gold and ivory and clear social differentiation.
- Great Zimbabwe rose through cattle, farming, control of the gold and ivory trade to the coast, a good location, religion and strong leadership.
- The king ruled with advisers and provincial chiefs who paid tribute; the elite lived inside the stone walls and commoners outside.
- The Hill Complex, Great Enclosure and Valley Ruins were built by dry-stone walling.
- Theories of decline include environmental exhaustion, shifting trade routes and succession disputes.
- Archaeology proved that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
:::
