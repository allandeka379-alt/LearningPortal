## Introduction

When Portuguese ships reached the coast of what is now Mozambique at the start of the sixteenth century, they heard of a great king in the interior whose lands produced gold. His title was the **Mwene Mutapa** (often written Munhumutapa or Monomotapa), and his state stretched across northern Zimbabwe towards the Zambezi. For more than a century the Mutapa kings dealt with Swahili and Portuguese traders as equals, until civil wars and Portuguese interference weakened them.

This lesson describes how the Mutapa state began, how it was organised, how it dealt with the Portuguese and why it declined. You should already know about Great Zimbabwe, because the Mutapa state is often seen as its northern successor. Detailed Portuguese activities and the prazo system are covered in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mwene Mutapa"}
The title of the ruler of the Mutapa state. Traditions explain it as meaning "lord of the conquered lands" or "master pillager", from the conquests of the founder.
:::

:::definition{term="Mhondoro"}
A royal ancestral spirit (literally "lion"), believed to protect the land and bring rain, which speaks through a spirit medium (svikiro).
:::

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro)"}
A person through whom an ancestral spirit is believed to speak; mhondoro mediums advised kings and approved successions.
:::

:::definition{term="Royal fire"}
A sacred fire kept burning at the king's court; tradition says that chiefs relit their own fires from it each year as a sign of loyalty.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
Nyatsimba Mutota is said to have founded the state in about the 1430s–1450s; his son **Matope** expanded it. Gonçalo da Silveira was killed at the Mutapa court in 1561. In 1607 Mutapa Gatsi Rusere promised the Portuguese access to the mines; in 1629 Mutapa Mavhura signed a treaty making him a Portuguese vassal.
:::

## Origins and rise

Oral traditions say that **Nyatsimba Mutota**, a prince from the Great Zimbabwe area, led his followers north in the first half of the fifteenth century. Traditions give different reasons: a shortage of **salt** in the south, overcrowding, or the wish to control trade. He conquered the Tavara and Tonga peoples of the Dande area, south of the Zambezi, who called him "Mwene Mutapa". His son **Matope** extended the state eastwards towards the coast and the Indian Ocean trade.

These are **traditions**, passed down orally and recorded later; historians accept their broad outline (a move of power northwards in the fifteenth century) because archaeology and Portuguese writings point the same way, but they treat the personal details with care.

The state rose because of:

- **control of trade:** the Zambezi valley gave access to Swahili traders at Sena, Tete and the coast;
- **gold and ivory** from the plateau and the valley;
- **a strong army** and conquest of neighbouring peoples;
- **fertile land and good grazing** in the Dande and on the plateau;
- **religion:** the king's link with the royal mhondoro gave him spiritual authority.

## How the state was organised

### Political organisation

The **Mutapa** was the supreme ruler, believed to be close to the ancestors. He lived in a capital surrounded by a wooden palisade, with a large court of officials: royal relatives, advisers, the queen mother and senior wives, and officers in charge of the army, the treasury and the king's household. **Provincial chiefs**, often relatives or loyal followers, ruled the outlying areas, sent tribute and provided soldiers. The royal fire symbolised the king's authority over all chiefs.

### Economic organisation

- **Agriculture:** sorghum, millet and other crops; women did much of the farming.
- **Cattle:** the measure of wealth, given as tribute and used in bridewealth.
- **Mining:** gold was mined and panned, especially in the dry season; tradition says that some gold finds belonged to the king.
- **Trade:** gold, ivory and copper were exchanged with Swahili and later Portuguese traders for cloth, beads and other goods, at markets and later at trading fairs.
- **Tribute:** chiefs and subjects paid in cattle, grain, ivory, gold and labour.
- **Hunting:** elephants were hunted for ivory, and traditions and Portuguese accounts say the king claimed a share of the ivory.

### Social organisation

Society was divided between the royal family and nobles, commoners (farmers, herders, miners, craftsmen) and conquered peoples. Families were organised by kinship and totems, with bridewealth (roora, paid in cattle) at marriage.

### Religious organisation

The people believed in **Mwari** and in ancestral spirits. The **mhondoro** mediums were powerful: they advised the king, took part in choosing a successor, and conducted rain ceremonies. A king who lost their support could lose the people's loyalty.

## Relations with the Portuguese

The Portuguese built a fort at Sofala in 1505 and set up posts at Sena and Tete on the Zambezi in the 1530s. At first the Mutapa kings controlled the trade and treated the Portuguese like other foreign traders, allowing them to trade at agreed markets.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| c. 1430s–1450s | Nyatsimba Mutota moves north and founds the Mutapa state; Matope expands it |
| 1505 | The Portuguese build a fort at Sofala |
| 1561 | The Jesuit Gonçalo da Silveira baptises the Mutapa but is killed at his court |
| 1569–1573 | The Barreto expedition fails to conquer the gold lands |
| 1607 | Mutapa Gatsi Rusere promises the Portuguese the mines in return for military help |
| 1629 | Mutapa Mavhura signs a treaty making him a Portuguese vassal |
| 1690s | The Rozvi under Changamire Dombo drive the Portuguese from the plateau |

Gatsi Rusere faced rebellions and turned to the Portuguese for military help, promising them the mines in 1607. In 1629 the Portuguese helped **Mavhura** (Mamvura) to replace Kaparidze as Mutapa; in return he accepted Portuguese overlordship, allowed missionaries and traders to move freely, and gave them land and mining rights. From then on the Mutapa kings were weakened, while Portuguese traders and prazo holders gained power in the valley.

:::example{title="Explaining how civil war opened the door to the Portuguese"}
1. **Disputes** over succession split the royal family; rival claimants each looked for allies.
2. A Mutapa facing rebellion, such as Gatsi Rusere, asked the Portuguese for **armed help**, because their guns and soldiers could tip the balance.
3. The **price** of that help was concessions: access to the mines (1607), and later vassal status (1629).
4. Each concession made the king **more dependent** and less respected by his subjects, which encouraged further rebellion. Civil war and Portuguese interference fed each other.
:::

## Why the Mutapa state declined

- **Succession disputes and civil wars**, which divided the ruling family and weakened central control.
- **Portuguese interference**: treaties, the installation of puppet kings such as Mavhura, the spread of prazos, and the loss of control of trade and mines.
- **Rebellions by provinces**, which broke away; the Changamire line in the south-west became independent, leading to the Rozvi state.
- **The rise of the Rozvi** under Changamire Dombo in the later seventeenth century, which took over much of the plateau.
- **Loss of trade income**, as gold production fell and trade moved into Portuguese hands.
- **Nineteenth-century invasions** by Nguni groups further weakened the remnant state in the Zambezi valley.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes of decline"}
**Claim:** "The Portuguese were the main cause of the decline of the Mutapa state."

1. **For:** the treaties of 1607 and 1629 reduced the Mutapa to a vassal, and Portuguese traders and prazeros took land, trade and mines.
2. **Against:** the Portuguese gained influence only because Mutapa kings were already fighting rebels and rivals; the rise of the Rozvi took away the plateau.
3. **Judgement:** internal divisions made Portuguese interference possible, so a strong answer links the two rather than treating them separately.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating traditions as exact history"}
Write "according to tradition, Mutota moved north…". Dates for the founding are approximate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Mutapa and Rozvi"}
The Mutapa state was in the north and Zambezi valley; the Rozvi (Changamire) state grew in the south-west and drove the Portuguese off the plateau in the 1690s.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Portuguese as conquerors from the start"}
For most of the sixteenth century the Mutapa controlled the trade. Portuguese power grew in the seventeenth century, through African divisions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- According to tradition, Nyatsimba Mutota moved north from the Great Zimbabwe area and founded the Mutapa state in the fifteenth century; Matope expanded it.
- The state rose through trade, gold and ivory, conquest, good land and religious authority.
- The Mutapa ruled with a court and provincial chiefs who paid tribute; mhondoro mediums gave spiritual backing.
- Relations with the Portuguese moved from trade on African terms to dependence: 1561 (Silveira), 1607 (Gatsi Rusere), 1629 (Mavhura as vassal).
- Decline came from civil wars, Portuguese interference, provincial breakaways, the rise of the Rozvi and loss of trade.
:::
