## Introduction

Climb into a granite rock shelter in the Matobo Hills or at Domboshava and you may see paintings of people hunting, animals such as kudu and giraffe, and figures in dance. They were painted by **San** hunter-gatherers, whose ancestors lived in Zimbabwe for many thousands of years. Later, farming peoples who made iron tools and pottery settled the land, and Zimbabwe's history of villages, chiefdoms and, eventually, great states began.

This lesson describes the Stone Age and the Early Iron Age in Zimbabwe and explains what changed between them. It builds on the origins of humankind (stone tool-making began in Africa) and leads to Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe. Most evidence comes from **archaeology**: tools, pottery, food remains and rock art. All dates are approximate, and archaeologists give slightly different ranges.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stone Age"}
The long period when people made their tools mainly from stone, as well as wood and bone, and lived by hunting animals and gathering wild food.
:::

:::definition{term="Hunter-gatherers"}
People who live by hunting wild animals and collecting wild plants, fruits, roots and honey, moving from place to place with the seasons.
:::

:::definition{term="Iron Age"}
The period when people smelted iron to make tools and weapons; in Zimbabwe it is linked with settled farming, herding and pottery.
:::

:::definition{term="Microliths"}
Very small, sharp stone blades and points made in the Late Stone Age and fixed to arrows, spears and knives.
:::

:::definition{term="Smelting"}
Heating iron ore with charcoal in a furnace so that the iron separates from the rock and can be worked into tools.
:::

## The Stone Age

Archaeologists divide the Stone Age into three stages according to the tools people made.

| Stage | Approximate time | Tools | Way of life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Stone Age | from more than a million years ago to about 250,000 years ago | Large, heavy hand axes and cleavers, shaped on both sides | Early humans hunted and scavenged animals and gathered plants; small wandering groups |
| Middle Stone Age | about 250,000 to 40,000 years ago | Smaller flakes, scrapers and pointed blades, some fixed to wooden handles as spears | Better hunters; regular use of fire; people sheltered in caves |
| Late Stone Age | from about 40,000 years ago until the arrival of farmers (and alongside them) | Microliths fixed to arrows and spears; bows and arrows; bone tools; ostrich-eggshell beads | Skilled hunter-gatherers, the ancestors of the San; rock painting |

### Life in the Late Stone Age

Late Stone Age people lived in small bands of related families, perhaps twenty to fifty people. They moved with the seasons, following game and ripening wild foods. Men usually hunted, using bows and poisoned arrows; women gathered roots, fruits, nuts, insects and honey. Decisions were made by discussion, with respect for elders and skilled hunters: there were no chiefs or kings. They shared food and owned few possessions, because everything had to be carried.

**Rock paintings** are found across Zimbabwe, especially in the Matobo Hills, and at sites such as Domboshava and Silozwane. They show animals, hunting, gathering and ceremonies, and many experts think some paintings show spiritual beliefs and the visions of healers in trance. Paints were made from natural materials such as ochre (coloured earth), charcoal and animal fat.

## The Early Iron Age

From roughly 2,000 years ago, Bantu-speaking farming communities moved into southern Africa from the north. In Zimbabwe, Early Iron Age farming villages appear from about the third to the fifth centuries CE. Archaeologists name their pottery styles after sites, such as Gokomere near Masvingo and Ziwa in the Nyanga area.

### Economic organisation

- **Crop farming:** sorghum, millet, beans and pumpkins, grown in fields cleared with iron hoes and axes.
- **Herding:** cattle, sheep and goats.
- **Iron working:** iron ore was smelted in clay furnaces using charcoal and bellows; smiths made hoes, axes, knives and spearheads.
- **Pottery:** decorated clay pots for cooking and storing grain and beer.
- **Hunting and gathering** continued to add to the diet.
- **Trade:** iron goods, salt, copper and pottery were exchanged between villages; later, some goods such as glass beads from the coast reached the interior.

### Social organisation

Farmers lived in **permanent villages** of pole-and-daga (mud) huts, with grain bins and cattle enclosures. Families were larger, and kinship (descent from a common ancestor) bound the village together. Ancestors were respected and consulted through rituals. There was a division of labour: women did much of the farming and pottery; men herded cattle, cleared land, hunted and worked iron. Smiths had special skills and status.

### Political organisation

Villages were led by a **headman** or chief, usually the senior man of the founding family, advised by elders. Disputes were settled at the chief's court. Political units were small, but wealth in cattle and control of trade later allowed some leaders to become much more powerful, as at Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe.

## Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| more than 1,000,000 years ago | Early Stone Age: hand axes made in southern Africa |
| about 250,000 years ago | Middle Stone Age tools appear: smaller flakes and hafted points |
| about 40,000 years ago | Late Stone Age: microliths, bows and arrows; ancestors of the San |
| about 200–500 CE | Early Iron Age farmers settle in Zimbabwe (Gokomere, Ziwa pottery traditions) |
| about 900–1000 CE | Later Iron Age communities with larger cattle herds and wider trade develop, leading to Mapungubwe |

:::example{title="Explaining the change from the Stone Age to the Iron Age"}
1. **Food production:** Iron Age people *produced* food by farming and herding, instead of only finding it. This gave a more reliable supply and a surplus.
2. **Settlement:** farming tied people to their fields, so they built **permanent villages** rather than moving in bands.
3. **Population:** more food supported larger families and larger communities.
4. **Technology:** iron hoes and axes cleared and worked land faster than stone tools; pottery stored grain.
5. **Society and politics:** cattle and stored grain were wealth that could be owned and inherited, so differences in wealth and the power of chiefs grew.

The key idea is a chain: farming → surplus → settlement → more people → wealth and leaders.
:::

:::example{title="What the San and the farmers did when they met"}
Archaeology and later accounts suggest a mixed picture. In some areas hunter-gatherers and farmers traded (meat, skins and honey for grain, pottery and iron); some San joined farming communities or married into them; elsewhere they moved to drier or hillier land that farmers did not want, or lost land to farming and herding. Over centuries the hunter-gatherer way of life became rarer in Zimbabwe. A good answer avoids saying that one group simply "replaced" the other overnight.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Exact dates for the Stone Age"}
Give approximate ranges ("about 40,000 years ago"). Archaeologists' dates differ and are revised.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Stone Age people had chiefs"}
Hunter-gatherer bands had no chiefs or kings; leadership came from elders and skilled hunters. Chiefs developed among farming communities.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting pottery and herding"}
The Early Iron Age is not only about iron. Pottery, settled crop farming and cattle are just as important in describing it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Stone Age is divided into Early (hand axes), Middle (flakes, hafted points) and Late (microliths, bows and arrows) stages.
- Late Stone Age hunter-gatherers, the ancestors of the San, lived in small mobile bands without chiefs and painted on rocks.
- Early Iron Age farmers settled in Zimbabwe from roughly the third to fifth centuries CE.
- They grew crops, herded cattle, smelted iron, made pottery and lived in permanent villages under headmen.
- Farming produced surplus, settlement, larger populations, wealth and stronger leaders, preparing the way for states.
:::
