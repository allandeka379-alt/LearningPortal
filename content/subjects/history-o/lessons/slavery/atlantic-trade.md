## Introduction

Between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries millions of Africans were forced across the Atlantic Ocean to work on plantations in the Americas. This was the largest forced movement of people by sea in history. Its effects can still be seen today: in the African communities of Brazil, the Caribbean and the United States, and in the long debate about how the trade affected Africa's development.

The trade is called **triangular** because ships made a three-sided voyage between Europe, Africa and the Americas. In this lesson you will learn the areas involved, the routes, the goods exchanged on each leg, the **Middle Passage** and life on plantations in outline. You should already know the forms and causes of slavery in Africa (the previous lesson).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Triangular trade"}
The three-legged trade route of the Atlantic slave trade: Europe to Africa with manufactured goods, Africa to the Americas with enslaved Africans, and the Americas back to Europe with plantation produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Middle Passage"}
The second leg of the triangle: the voyage across the Atlantic from Africa to the Americas, on which enslaved Africans were carried in terrible conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Plantation"}
A large estate growing a single cash crop for export, such as sugar, tobacco, cotton or coffee, worked by large numbers of labourers; in the Americas these were mostly enslaved Africans.
:::

:::definition{term="Factory (trading fort)"}
A fortified European trading post on the African coast where goods were stored and captives were held before being shipped, for example Elmina in present-day Ghana.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Numbers to know (historians' estimates)"}
- Historians estimate that about **12 million** Africans were embarked on ships for the Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries.
- Roughly 10 to 11 million survived the crossing; well over a million died on the Middle Passage.
- Brazil received more enslaved Africans than any other country.
:::

## Areas involved

The trade linked four areas:

- **Europe:** the main slave-trading nations were Portugal, Britain, France, the Netherlands and Spain. Major ports included Liverpool, Bristol and London in Britain, Nantes in France and Lisbon in Portugal.
- **West Africa:** from Senegambia along the Gold Coast to the Bight of Benin and the Bight of Biafra (present-day Senegal to Nigeria and Cameroon).
- **West-Central Africa:** the Kongo and Angola region, which supplied the largest number of captives of any region.
- **The Americas and the Caribbean:** Brazil, the Caribbean islands (such as Jamaica, Barbados and Saint-Domingue), the Spanish colonies and North America.

## The three legs of the triangle

| Leg | Route | Cargo |
|---|---|---|
| First (outward) | Europe to the African coast | Manufactured goods: cloth, guns and gunpowder, iron and copper goods, alcohol, beads |
| Second (Middle Passage) | Africa to the Americas | Enslaved Africans |
| Third (homeward) | The Americas to Europe | Plantation produce: sugar, tobacco, cotton, rum, coffee |

On the first leg European goods were exchanged on the African coast for captives. On the third leg the profits from selling the captives were used to buy plantation produce, which was sold in Europe. Not every ship followed a neat triangle: many Portuguese and Brazilian ships sailed directly between Brazil and Angola, carrying goods such as tobacco and rum to Africa.

:::example{title="Following one voyage around the triangle"}
1. **Leg one:** a ship leaves Liverpool loaded with cloth, guns and metal goods and sails to the Bight of Benin.
2. **On the coast:** the captain trades these goods with African merchants for captives held at a coastal fort. Buying a full cargo can take months.
3. **Leg two:** the ship crosses the Atlantic to Jamaica. Many captives die of disease on the way.
4. **In the Caribbean:** the survivors are sold at auction to plantation owners.
5. **Leg three:** the captain buys sugar and rum and sails home, where these goods are sold for a profit.
6. **Why it mattered:** each leg carried a cargo that could be sold, so merchants profited three times on one voyage.
:::

## The Middle Passage

The crossing usually took several weeks, often around six to eight, and longer if winds failed.

- **Crowding:** captives were chained in pairs and packed into the lower decks, with little room to sit up or move.
- **Disease:** dysentery, smallpox and fevers spread quickly in the heat and filth; food and water were often short.
- **Violence:** crews used whips and chains to keep control; women were often abused.
- **Death:** historians estimate that on average roughly one captive in seven or eight died on the crossing, and on some voyages many more.
- **Resistance:** captives resisted by refusing food, by rebellion on board and sometimes by jumping overboard rather than remain enslaved.

:::example{title="Weighing evidence: the plan of a slave ship"}
In the late 1780s British abolitionists published a plan of the Liverpool ship *Brookes*, showing captives packed side by side in rows on the lower decks.
1. **What it shows:** the extreme crowding of the Middle Passage.
2. **Purpose:** it was published to shock the public and win support for abolition, so its creators chose a powerful image.
3. **Reliability:** it was based on official measurements of a real ship, and other evidence such as sailors' accounts and the memoirs of formerly enslaved Africans confirms severe crowding.
4. **Judgement:** produced for a campaign, but strongly supported by other evidence, so it is reliable on the conditions it shows.
:::

## Plantation slavery in the Americas

On arrival, captives were sold at auctions or "scrambles" to planters. Most were sent to **sugar plantations** in Brazil and the Caribbean, where work was hardest and death rates highest. Others grew tobacco, rice and later cotton in North America, or worked in mines, homes and towns.

- Work lasted from dawn to dusk, and longer during the sugar harvest, under drivers with whips.
- Enslaved people were property: they could be sold, separating families, and their children were born enslaved.
- Laws (slave codes) banned them from learning to read in some colonies, owning weapons or moving without permission.
- Enslaved people resisted in many ways: working slowly, running away (some formed free "maroon" communities), keeping African languages, music and religion alive, and open revolt. The revolution in Saint-Domingue (1791-1804) created the independent state of Haiti.

## Timeline

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1440s | Portuguese sailors begin taking captives from West Africa to Portugal |
| 1482 | The Portuguese build Elmina castle on the Gold Coast |
| Early 1500s | Enslaved Africans are first shipped across the Atlantic to the Americas |
| 1600s | Sugar plantations spread in Brazil and the Caribbean |
| 1700s | The trade reaches its peak; Britain becomes the leading carrier |
| 1791-1804 | Revolution in Saint-Domingue creates Haiti |
| 1807 | Britain abolishes its slave trade |
| 1888 | Brazil abolishes slavery, the last country in the Americas to do so |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Muddling the order of the legs"}
Goods went from Europe to Africa first; captives went from Africa to the Americas (the Middle Passage); produce went from the Americas to Europe. The Middle Passage is always the second leg.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving exact death tolls as facts"}
Numbers are historians' estimates. Write "historians estimate about 12 million were embarked", not an exact figure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting West-Central Africa"}
The trade did not come only from West Africa. The Kongo and Angola region supplied the largest number of captives.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: list, state and describe"}
"List the areas involved" needs Europe, West Africa (and West-Central Africa) and the Americas or Caribbean. "State the goods exchanged" needs goods for **each** leg. "Describe the routes" needs the three legs in order, with direction and cargo.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing the Middle Passage"}
Give several features (crowding, disease, violence, deaths, resistance), each with a short explanation, rather than a general statement that conditions were bad.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The triangular trade linked Europe, West and West-Central Africa, and the Americas and the Caribbean.
- Leg one carried manufactured goods to Africa; leg two (the Middle Passage) carried captives; leg three carried sugar, tobacco, cotton and rum to Europe.
- Historians estimate about 12 million Africans were embarked and well over a million died at sea.
- The Middle Passage meant crowding, disease, violence and death, and captives resisted.
- Most survivors were sold to plantations, above all sugar plantations in Brazil and the Caribbean, where they also resisted.
:::
