## Introduction

For more than a thousand years people were captured in Africa and sold, first across the Sahara Desert and the Indian Ocean and later across the Atlantic. Some of the caravan routes that carried ivory and captives in the nineteenth century reached the lands just north of the Zambezi, close to modern Zimbabwe. Understanding how and why people were enslaved helps to explain the wars, insecurity and loss of population that shaped much of Africa before colonisation.

This lesson covers the **forms** of slavery, the **causes** of the slave trade and the **methods** used to obtain slaves. The Atlantic (triangular) trade and the effects and abolition of the trade are separate lessons. You do not need any earlier knowledge, but it helps to know where West Africa, East Africa and the Sahara are on a map.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Slavery"}
A system in which one person is owned by another, can be bought and sold, and is forced to work without pay or freedom to leave.
:::

:::definition{term="Domestic (household) slavery"}
A form of slavery found in many African societies in which enslaved people lived and worked within the owner's household or community. They usually had some rights, could marry, and their children or grandchildren were often absorbed into the family as free members.
:::

:::definition{term="Chattel slavery"}
A form of slavery in which the enslaved person is treated as property (a "chattel"), can be sold at any time, has no rights and passes the status of slave to his or her children. It was the form used on plantations in the Americas.
:::

:::definition{term="Slave trade"}
The buying, transporting and selling of enslaved people as goods, usually over long distances and for profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Pawnship"}
A practice in which a person (often a relative of a debtor) was handed to a creditor as security for a debt. If the debt was not repaid, the pawn could be enslaved or sold.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The three great slave trades from Africa"}
- **Trans-Saharan trade:** across the Sahara to North Africa and the Middle East, carried by camel caravans, for many centuries before Europeans arrived.
- **East African (Indian Ocean) trade:** from the Swahili coast to Arabia, the Persian Gulf and India, and in the nineteenth century to the clove plantations of Zanzibar and Pemba and the sugar islands of the Indian Ocean.
- **Atlantic (triangular) trade:** from West and West-Central Africa to the Americas, from the fifteenth to the nineteenth century.
:::

## Forms of slavery in Africa

Slavery in Africa took several forms, and it is a mistake to treat them all as the same.

1. **Domestic or household slavery.** Enslaved people worked in fields, homes and courts. In many societies they could marry, own some property and rise in status; some served as soldiers or officials. Over generations their descendants were often absorbed into the lineage.
2. **Pawnship.** People were pledged as security for debts. A pawn was not always a slave, but unpaid debts could turn pawns into slaves.
3. **Court and military slavery.** Some rulers kept large numbers of enslaved soldiers, servants and officials who depended on the ruler alone.
4. **Chattel slavery and the slave trade.** As long-distance trades grew, captives were increasingly treated as goods to be sold. In the Americas chattel slavery became hereditary and based on race.

:::example{title="Comparing two forms of slavery"}
1. **Domestic slavery:** a captive taken in a local war might work in the household of a chief. Over time he could marry, and his children might be treated as junior members of the family.
2. **Chattel slavery:** a captive sold to a plantation in the Caribbean had no rights at all, could be sold away from family members at any time, and his children were born enslaved.
3. **The key difference:** in domestic slavery the enslaved person could often be absorbed into society; in chattel slavery he or she remained property for life, and so did the children.
4. **But do not romanticise:** domestic slaves still lost their freedom, could be punished harshly and were sometimes sold on.
:::

## The trans-Saharan and East African trades in outline

The **trans-Saharan trade** linked West Africa with North Africa. Camel caravans carried gold, ivory, kola nuts and captives north, and brought salt, horses, cloth and metal goods south. Captives were taken to serve as domestic servants, soldiers and labourers in North Africa and the Middle East.

The **East African trade** ran along the Indian Ocean coast through Swahili towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa and Zanzibar. Arab, Swahili and Indian merchants shipped captives to Arabia, the Persian Gulf and India. In the nineteenth century the trade grew sharply: Omani rulers moved their capital to Zanzibar, clove plantations on Zanzibar and Pemba needed labour, and French sugar islands such as Réunion wanted workers. Caravans led by Swahili-Arab traders and by African traders such as the Yao travelled inland to the region of Lake Malawi and beyond, buying ivory and captives.

| Trade | Main routes | Main destinations | Goods exchanged with captives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trans-Saharan | West Africa across the Sahara | North Africa, the Middle East | Gold, salt, horses, cloth |
| East African | Interior to the Swahili coast | Arabia, the Gulf, India, Zanzibar, Indian Ocean islands | Ivory, cloth, beads, guns |
| Atlantic | West and West-Central African coast | The Americas and the Caribbean | Cloth, guns, metal goods, alcohol |

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| From about the ninth century CE | Trans-Saharan caravans regularly carry captives north alongside gold |
| 1440s | Portuguese sailors begin taking captives from the West African coast to Europe |
| 1500s | Enslaved Africans are first shipped across the Atlantic to the Americas |
| 1700s | The Atlantic trade reaches its peak |
| 1840 | The Sultan of Oman moves his capital to Zanzibar; the East African trade expands |
| 1873 | The Zanzibar slave market is closed under a treaty with Britain |

## Causes of the slave trade

Examiners expect causes grouped as **economic, social and political**.

### Economic causes

- **Demand for labour** abroad: plantations in the Americas (sugar, tobacco, cotton) and, later, the clove plantations of Zanzibar and the sugar islands of the Indian Ocean.
- **Profit:** merchants, ship owners and African traders all made money from the trade.
- **Desire for imported goods:** African rulers and traders wanted cloth, guns, metal goods and luxury items, and captives became one of the main things they could exchange for them.
- **Ivory and slaves together:** in East Africa captives were sometimes used to carry ivory to the coast and then sold.

### Social causes

- **Status and wealth:** in many societies wealth was measured in people (dependants and followers) rather than land, so owning slaves raised a person's status.
- **Punishment:** people convicted of crimes such as theft, adultery or witchcraft could be sold.
- **Debt:** people who could not pay debts, or pawns whose debts were not cleared, could be enslaved.
- **Famine and hardship:** in times of drought, families sometimes pawned or sold members to survive.

### Political causes

- **Wars between states** produced prisoners, who were sold.
- **The gun-slave cycle:** rulers sold captives to buy guns, and guns made it easier to capture more people, so neighbouring states were forced to join in to defend themselves.
- **Expansion of strong states:** some states, such as Dahomey and Asante in West Africa, grew powerful partly through control of the trade.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: the gun-slave cycle"}
1. A ruler on the coast sells war captives to European traders and receives firearms.
2. His army, now better armed, raids weaker neighbours and takes more captives.
3. The neighbours, threatened, also need guns and can only buy them by selling captives of their own.
4. **Result:** warfare and raiding spread inland, and more people are enslaved, even by states that did not want to take part.
:::

## Methods of obtaining slaves

| Method | How it worked |
|---|---|
| Raids | Armed groups attacked villages at night or at dawn and seized people, especially the young. |
| Wars | Prisoners of war between states were sold rather than ransomed or released. |
| Kidnapping | Individuals, including children, were seized while travelling, farming or fetching water. |
| Debt and pawnship | Debtors or their pawns were enslaved when debts were not repaid. |
| Punishment | Courts sold people convicted of crimes. |
| Trade | Captives were bought at inland markets and passed from trader to trader towards the coast. |
| Tribute | Some rulers demanded people as tribute from subject communities. |

Most European traders on the Atlantic coast did **not** capture people themselves. They bought captives from African rulers and merchants at coastal forts and markets. In East Africa, by contrast, armed Swahili-Arab caravans and their African allies often raided directly in the interior.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all slavery in Africa as the same as plantation slavery"}
Domestic slavery in many African societies allowed some rights and absorption into the family. Chattel slavery in the Americas was hereditary and gave no rights. Show the difference.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up causes and methods"}
A cause explains **why** the trade happened (demand for labour, profit, guns). A method explains **how** people were obtained (raids, kidnapping, debt). Keep them in separate paragraphs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Europeans captured most slaves themselves"}
On the West African coast Europeans mostly bought captives from African rulers and traders. This does not reduce European responsibility, since European demand drove the trade.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: describe the forms"}
"Identify and describe the forms of slavery" needs each form named and then described with one or two features (rights, work, inheritance of status).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: outline the causes"}
Group causes under economic, social and political headings. For each cause, add a short explanation ("because…, so…") rather than a single word.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the methods"}
For "explain the methods used to obtain slaves", say how each method worked and give a context, for example that raids increased when guns became available.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Forms of slavery included domestic (household) slavery, pawnship, court and military slavery and chattel slavery.
- The trans-Saharan and East African trades long pre-dated the Atlantic trade; the East African trade grew sharply in the nineteenth century.
- Economic causes: demand for labour, profit and the desire for imported goods; social causes: status, debt and punishment; political causes: wars, the gun-slave cycle and state expansion.
- Methods included raids, wars, kidnapping, debt, punishment, trade and tribute.
- Most Atlantic captives were bought by Europeans from African rulers and merchants on the coast.
:::
