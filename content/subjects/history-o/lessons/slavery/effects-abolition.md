## Introduction

The slave trade took millions of people from Africa over several centuries. Historians still debate how far it damaged African societies and how far it explains Africa's later poverty. Its ending was also a long struggle: Britain abolished its own slave trade in 1807, but captives were still being shipped from East Africa, including from regions close to the Zambezi, decades later. Missionaries such as David Livingstone, who later influenced events in Zimbabwe, used the horrors of the East African trade to argue for European involvement in Africa.

You should already know the causes and methods of the slave trade and how the triangular trade worked. This lesson covers the **effects** on Africa, the **campaign for abolition**, the **reasons** for abolition and the **challenges** of ending the trade.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Abolition"}
The ending of something by law. Historians distinguish abolition of the **slave trade** (buying and shipping people) from abolition of **slavery** itself (freeing those already enslaved).
:::

:::definition{term="Abolitionist"}
A person who campaigned to end the slave trade or slavery.
:::

:::definition{term="Emancipation"}
The freeing of enslaved people.
:::

:::definition{term="Legitimate trade"}
The name Europeans gave in the nineteenth century to trade in goods such as palm oil, groundnuts and ivory, which they hoped would replace the trade in people.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1787: the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade is founded in London.
- 1807: Britain abolishes its slave trade.
- 1808: the United States bans the import of enslaved people; Britain's naval patrol off West Africa begins.
- 1833: Britain's Slavery Abolition Act; it comes into force on 1 August 1834.
- 1873: the Zanzibar slave market is closed.
- 1888: Brazil abolishes slavery.
:::

## Effects on Africa

### Negative effects

- **Depopulation:** millions of people, mostly young men and women, were taken. Many more died in raids, wars and on the march to the coast. Some regions lost population or grew much more slowly than they would have done.
- **Insecurity and wars:** raids and the gun-slave cycle spread fear; people moved to defensive sites, and farming and trade were disrupted.
- **Decline of crafts:** imported cloth and metal goods competed with local weavers and smiths in some areas.
- **Decline of agriculture:** the loss of young workers and constant insecurity reduced food production.
- **Brutalisation and division:** violence became part of politics, and trust between communities broke down.
- **Long-term underdevelopment:** some historians, notably Walter Rodney in *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* (1972), argue the trade drained Africa of labour and held back its development while helping Europe to grow rich.

### Positive effects (for some)

- **New crops:** maize, cassava and groundnuts came from the Americas and became important food crops in Africa.
- **Rise of some states:** states such as Dahomey and Asante grew powerful by controlling the trade.
- **Wealth for some rulers and merchants:** coastal rulers and traders grew rich, and some coastal towns grew.
- **Contact with the wider world:** new goods and ideas arrived.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: did the slave trade underdevelop Africa?"}
1. **The view:** Rodney argued that the trade removed Africa's most productive people and turned its economy towards exporting captives rather than producing goods.
2. **Evidence for:** large population losses in West and West-Central Africa; wars and insecurity; local crafts undercut by imports.
3. **Evidence against or qualifying:** the effects varied by region; some states grew stronger; other factors, such as colonial rule after 1885, also shaped African economies.
4. **Judgement:** most historians agree the trade did great harm, but they disagree about how far it alone explains later poverty. The "positive" effects benefited a small group of rulers and merchants, not most people.
:::

## The campaign for abolition

| Group | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Quakers | Religious society that opposed slavery early; Quakers formed most of the founders of the 1787 Society |
| Evangelical Christians | Argued slavery was a sin; William Wilberforce led the campaign in Parliament |
| Humanitarians | Thomas Clarkson collected evidence; Granville Sharp fought legal cases |
| Formerly enslaved Africans | Olaudah Equiano published his life story in 1789; Ottobah Cugoano wrote against the trade |
| Enslaved people | Revolts, especially the revolution in Saint-Domingue (1791-1804) and the Jamaica rising of 1831-32, showed slavery was dangerous to keep |
| Ordinary people | Petitions to Parliament and a boycott of slave-grown sugar |

## Reasons for abolition

### Humanitarian and religious reasons

Campaigners argued that slavery was cruel and against Christian teaching. Evidence of the Middle Passage, the plan of the slave ship *Brookes* and the writings of formerly enslaved Africans changed public opinion in Britain.

### Economic reasons

- **The Industrial Revolution:** British industry increasingly needed raw materials (palm oil, cotton) and markets in Africa rather than captives. Africans working at home could produce raw materials and buy British goods.
- **Free trade:** new economic ideas held that free labour was more productive than slave labour.
- **Sugar:** competition from other sugar producers reduced the importance of the British Caribbean.

### The debate

In *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) the Trinidadian historian Eric Williams argued that abolition came mainly because slavery was becoming less profitable to an industrialising Britain. Other historians reply that the trade was still profitable in 1807 and that the religious and humanitarian campaign, together with the resistance of enslaved people, was decisive. Most textbooks now present abolition as the result of several factors working together.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: the Industrial Revolution and abolition"}
1. **Change:** by the early nineteenth century Britain's factories produced goods on a huge scale.
2. **Need:** factories needed raw materials such as palm oil (for soap and machine grease) and new markets.
3. **Link:** Africans enslaved and shipped abroad could neither produce these materials in Africa nor buy British goods there.
4. **Result:** some merchants and politicians came to favour "legitimate trade", which strengthened the case for abolition. This argument is debated, so present it as one view.
:::

## Challenges of ending the trade

- **Continuing demand:** plantations in Brazil, Cuba and the southern United States still wanted labour, so the trade continued illegally.
- **Policing the ocean:** the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron captured slave ships, and freed Africans were often landed at Freetown in Sierra Leone, but the coast was long and many ships escaped.
- **Other nations:** Britain had to persuade or pressure other countries to sign treaties allowing searches of their ships.
- **African interests:** some rulers and merchants depended on the income and resisted the change.
- **The East African trade:** it grew in the nineteenth century, run from Zanzibar, and lasted until the 1870s and beyond; slavery itself continued on Zanzibar until 1897.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing 1807 and 1833"}
1807 abolished Britain's slave **trade**. The 1833 Act abolished **slavery** itself in most of the British Empire, from 1 August 1834.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying abolition was only the work of white campaigners"}
Formerly enslaved writers such as Equiano and the revolts of enslaved people were part of the story. Include them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing positive effects without balance"}
If you mention new crops or the rise of states, also say who benefited and that most Africans suffered.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: effects"}
"Describe the effects" needs both negative and positive effects, each explained. Most marks come from explaining how each effect happened (for example, why insecurity reduced farming).
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The trade caused depopulation, insecurity, wars and the decline of some crafts and farming; some states, rulers and merchants gained, and new crops spread.
- Historians debate how far the trade caused Africa's later underdevelopment.
- Quakers, evangelicals, humanitarians, formerly enslaved writers and enslaved people's own resistance all pushed for abolition.
- Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833-34; the role of economic change is debated.
- Ending the trade was hard because of continuing demand, a long coastline, other nations and the East African trade.
:::
