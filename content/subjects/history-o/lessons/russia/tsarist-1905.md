## Introduction

In 1900 the Russian Empire stretched from Poland to the Pacific Ocean, covering about a sixth of the world's land. It was ruled by one man, Tsar **Nicholas II**, who believed God had chosen him to govern with unlimited power. Twelve years after 1905, the dynasty that had ruled Russia for three hundred years was overthrown, and Russia became the world's first communist state.

The Russian Revolution shaped the whole twentieth century, including Africa: the Soviet Union later supported many liberation movements, and both ZANU and ZAPU received support from communist countries during Zimbabwe's armed struggle.

ZIMSEC studies the causes and stages of the Russian Revolution from 1905 (Paper 2, topic 14), and Cambridge studies this as Depth study C, key question 1: *Why did the Tsarist regime collapse in 1917?* No earlier knowledge is needed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tsar"}
The emperor of Russia. Nicholas II ruled from 1894 until 1917.
:::

:::definition{term="Autocracy"}
Government by one ruler with unlimited power. The Tsar was not bound by a constitution or an elected parliament before 1905.
:::

:::definition{term="Duma"}
The elected Russian parliament created after the 1905 Revolution. There were four Dumas between 1906 and 1917.
:::

:::definition{term="Soviet"}
A council elected by workers (and later soldiers). The first important soviet was formed in St Petersburg in 1905.
:::

:::definition{term="Russification"}
The policy of forcing the empire's non-Russian peoples, such as Poles, Finns and Ukrainians, to use the Russian language and follow Russian customs and the Orthodox Church.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn (new style, with old style in brackets)"}
- **1894:** Nicholas II becomes Tsar.
- **1904-05:** Russo-Japanese War.
- **22 January 1905 (9 January):** Bloody Sunday.
- **30 October 1905 (17 October):** the October Manifesto.
- **1906:** the first Duma meets; Stolypin becomes Prime Minister.
- **1911:** Stolypin assassinated.
:::

:::note{title="Russian dates"}
Until February 1918 Russia used the old Julian calendar, which in the twentieth century was 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar used in Western Europe. This lesson gives new-style dates with the old-style date in brackets.
:::

## Russia before 1914

### Ruling Russia

The Tsar ruled through ministers he appointed and dismissed, a large and often inefficient civil service, the army and the **Okhrana**, the secret police, which watched, arrested and exiled opponents. The **Orthodox Church** taught that the Tsar was God's representative and that obedience was a religious duty.

### Russian society

- **Peasants** made up about four-fifths of the population. Serfdom had been abolished in 1861, but most peasants were poor, farmed small strips of land using old methods, and had to make redemption payments for their land. Many resented the landlords who owned the best land.
- **Industrial workers** were a small but fast-growing group. From the 1890s, under the finance minister **Sergei Witte**, Russia industrialised rapidly with foreign loans, building railways, including the Trans-Siberian Railway, mines and factories. Workers in St Petersburg and Moscow lived in overcrowded housing and worked long hours for low pay.
- **The middle classes** of businessmen, lawyers and teachers were growing and many wanted a parliament.
- **Nobles** owned much of the land and held the top posts in government and the army.
- **National minorities**: fewer than half of the Tsar's subjects were Russian. **Russification** caused anger among Poles, Finns, Ukrainians and others, and Jews suffered discrimination and violent attacks called pogroms.

### Opposition

- **Liberals** wanted a constitution and an elected parliament.
- **Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs)** wanted land for the peasants; some used terrorism.
- **Social Democrats** were Marxists who believed the industrial workers would lead a revolution. In 1903 they split into the **Bolsheviks**, led by **Lenin**, who wanted a small, disciplined party of professional revolutionaries, and the **Mensheviks**, who wanted a broader party.

## The 1905 Revolution

### Causes

- **Defeat by Japan:** in 1904 Russia went to war with Japan over influence in Manchuria and Korea. It suffered humiliating defeats: Port Arthur fell in January 1905 and the Russian fleet was destroyed at Tsushima in May 1905.
- **Economic hardship:** the war raised prices and caused shortages; an industrial slump had already cost many workers their jobs.
- **Long-term grievances** of peasants, workers, the middle classes and national minorities.

### Bloody Sunday

On **22 January 1905** (9 January), a priest, **Father Gapon**, led a large, peaceful procession of workers and their families to the Winter Palace in St Petersburg to present a petition to the Tsar asking for better conditions and an elected assembly. They carried icons and pictures of the Tsar. Troops opened fire. Estimates of the number killed range from about a hundred to several hundred. The Tsar was not in the palace, but many workers' trust in him as the "little father" of his people was destroyed.

### Revolution spreads

During 1905 strikes spread across the cities, peasants attacked landlords' estates, there were risings in the national borderlands, and the sailors of the battleship *Potemkin* mutinied in June. In October a **general strike** paralysed the country, and workers in St Petersburg set up a **soviet**, in which the young Marxist **Leon Trotsky** played a leading role.

### The October Manifesto

On the advice of Witte, the Tsar issued the **October Manifesto** on **30 October 1905** (17 October). It promised:

- civil rights, including freedom of speech, assembly and religion;
- an elected parliament, the **Duma**, without whose consent no law could be passed.

## Why did the Tsar survive?

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how Nicholas II survived 1905"}
1. **The opposition was divided:** the October Manifesto satisfied most liberals, while socialists rejected it, so they no longer worked together.
2. **Peace with Japan:** the Treaty of Portsmouth (September 1905) ended the war, freeing troops to restore order.
3. **The army stayed loyal:** despite some mutinies, most soldiers obeyed orders. In December the leaders of the St Petersburg Soviet were arrested and a rising in Moscow was crushed with artillery.
4. **Concessions to peasants:** redemption payments were reduced and then cancelled, calming the countryside.
5. **Result:** by 1906 the Tsar was back in control, and he began to take back what he had promised.
:::

## Attempts at reform, 1906-14

### The Dumas

Just before the first Duma met in 1906, the Tsar issued the **Fundamental Laws**, which declared that he was still the supreme autocrat; he could dissolve the Duma, rule by decree when it was not sitting, and choose his own ministers. The first two Dumas were critical of the government and were dissolved within months. In 1907 the voting system was changed to favour landowners, and the third and fourth Dumas were more conservative and lasted longer, though they still criticised the government.

### Stolypin

**Pyotr Stolypin**, Prime Minister from 1906, used a policy of repression and reform:

- **Repression:** special courts tried thousands of suspected revolutionaries, and many were hanged.
- **Land reform:** peasants were allowed to leave the village commune, combine their strips into separate farms and buy land through a Peasant Land Bank; others were encouraged to resettle in Siberia. He hoped to create a class of prosperous peasants loyal to the Tsar.

By 1914 only a minority of peasants had set up separate farms, and Stolypin had enemies at court and among revolutionaries. He was assassinated in **1911**.

### Russia on the eve of war

Strikes increased again from 1912, after troops shot striking workers at the Lena goldfields. The royal family's reliance on **Rasputin**, a Siberian holy man who seemed able to help the Tsar's son Alexei, who suffered from haemophilia, damaged the monarchy's reputation.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was Tsarism doomed by 1914?"}
**Doomed:** the Tsar had broken his 1905 promises, the Dumas had little power, peasants still wanted land, strikes were rising and Rasputin damaged respect for the monarchy.
**Not doomed:** the economy was growing fast, the army was loyal, Stolypin's reforms had some success, and the outbreak of war in 1914 brought a wave of patriotic support.
**Judgement:** historians disagree. Many argue that Tsarism had deep weaknesses but might have survived without the strains of the First World War.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Tsar ordered the troops to fire on Bloody Sunday"}
Nicholas II was not in St Petersburg that day. But people blamed him as the ruler, and the event destroyed his image as the people's protector.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the October Manifesto created a democracy"}
The Fundamental Laws of 1906 kept the Tsar's autocratic powers. The Duma could be dissolved, and the government was not answerable to it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the Bolsheviks and the Socialist Revolutionaries"}
The Bolsheviks were Marxists who looked to the industrial workers; the SRs looked to the peasants.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nicholas II ruled as an autocrat, supported by the army, the Okhrana and the Church, over a poor, divided and changing society.
- Defeat by Japan and economic hardship led to the 1905 Revolution, sparked by Bloody Sunday (22 January 1905).
- The October Manifesto promised civil rights and an elected Duma.
- The Tsar survived because the opposition divided, peace was made with Japan and the army stayed loyal.
- The Dumas were weakened by the Fundamental Laws; Stolypin combined repression with land reform until his murder in 1911.
:::
