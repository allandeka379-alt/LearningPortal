## Introduction

In 1928 the Soviet Union was still mainly a country of small peasant farms using horses and wooden ploughs. By 1941 it had become one of the world's leading industrial powers, able to produce the tanks and aircraft that later helped to defeat Nazi Germany. The cost was enormous: millions of peasants were forced off their land or died in famine, and workers laboured under harsh discipline.

The question of how a poor, farming country can industrialise quickly, and who pays the price, is one that many countries, including newly independent African states, have faced. Some African governments after independence were influenced by the Soviet model of state planning, so it is worth understanding both its achievements and its human costs.

ZIMSEC studies Stalin's domestic policies (Paper 2, topic 14), and Cambridge Depth study C, key question 4. You should know about the NEP and how Stalin came to power. Numbers of deaths in this lesson are historians' estimates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Five-Year Plan"}
A set of production targets for the whole economy over five years, drawn up by the state planning agency, **Gosplan**. The first began in 1928.
:::

:::definition{term="Collectivisation"}
The policy of joining individual peasant farms into large collective farms (*kolkhozy*), controlled by the state, from 1929.
:::

:::definition{term="Kulak"}
A term for a better-off peasant. Stalin's government used it for any peasant who resisted collectivisation, and set out to "liquidate the kulaks as a class".
:::

:::definition{term="Stakhanovite"}
A worker who greatly exceeded production targets, named after the miner Alexei Stakhanov, who in 1935 was reported to have cut many times his quota of coal in one shift.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1928-32:** First Five-Year Plan (declared complete in four years).
- **From late 1929:** forced collectivisation; dekulakisation.
- **1932-33:** famine in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus and the Volga region.
- **1933-37:** Second Five-Year Plan. **1935:** Stakhanov's record.
- **1938-41:** Third Five-Year Plan, interrupted by the German invasion.
:::

## Why did Stalin change course?

By 1928 Stalin had decided to abandon the NEP. Reasons included:

- **Backwardness and fear of attack:** Stalin argued that the USSR was far behind the capitalist countries and must catch up within about ten years or be crushed by its enemies.
- **Communist ideas:** industry would create a large working class, the base of socialism, and end the NEP's private traders and better-off peasants.
- **A grain crisis:** in 1927-28 peasants sold less grain to the state because prices were low, threatening food supplies to the cities and the army.
- **Paying for industry:** grain could be exported to pay for foreign machinery and expertise.
- **Power:** a planned economy and collective farms would give the party control over the economy and the countryside, and the change helped Stalin defeat Bukharin and the Right.

## The Five-Year Plans

Gosplan set targets for every mine, factory and region. The plans concentrated on **heavy industry**: coal, iron and steel, oil, electricity, machinery and, increasingly, armaments.

| Product (approximate Soviet official figures) | 1928 | 1932 | 1937 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal (million tonnes) | 35 | 64 | 128 |
| Steel (million tonnes) | 4 | 6 | 18 |
| Oil (million tonnes) | 12 | 21 | 29 |

Historians believe some official figures were exaggerated, but most accept that growth was very rapid.

### Achievements

- New industrial cities such as **Magnitogorsk** were built from nothing in the Ural Mountains, and huge projects such as the **Dnieper dam** (completed in 1932) supplied electricity. The Moscow Metro opened in 1935.
- Industry was moved east, beyond the reach of invaders, which proved vital after 1941.
- Unemployment disappeared, education and technical training spread, and many workers and peasants' children rose to become engineers and managers.

### Problems

- **Quality and waste:** managers chased quantity to meet targets, so much was badly made; equipment was misused and wasted.
- **False figures:** managers who failed risked arrest as "wreckers", so many exaggerated their results.
- **Consumer goods neglected:** housing, clothing and food were in short supply; queues were normal.
- **Forced labour:** prisoners of the **Gulag** built canals, railways and mines; thousands died building the White Sea Canal (opened in 1933).

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the plans drove workers"}
1. **Targets were set from above**, often unrealistically high.
2. **Managers passed the pressure down** to workers through piece rates (pay by output) and bonuses.
3. **Model workers** such as Stakhanov were publicised; Stakhanovites earned higher pay and privileges, which other workers often resented.
4. **Punishments** enforced discipline: internal passports (1932) restricted movement, and lateness or absence could lead to dismissal or prosecution.
5. **Result:** production rose fast, but at the cost of exhaustion, poor safety and resentment.
:::

## Collectivisation

### How it was carried out

From late 1929 Stalin ordered rapid, **forced collectivisation**. Peasants were made to hand over their land, animals and tools to collective farms, which had to deliver fixed quotas of grain to the state at low prices. Tractors and other machinery were supplied through state **Machine Tractor Stations**, which also kept watch on the farms.

Peasants who resisted were labelled **kulaks**. Party activists and the secret police seized their property; many were shot, and historians estimate that about 1.8 million people were deported to Siberia, Central Asia and the far north in 1930-31, where many died.

Many peasants resisted by **slaughtering their animals** rather than hand them over; the number of livestock fell by about half. In March 1930 Stalin blamed local officials for going too fast and allowed peasants to leave the collective farms; many did, but pressure soon resumed. By the mid-1930s about nine-tenths of peasant households were in collective farms.

### The famine of 1932-33

In 1932 the state kept taking grain from collective farms even when harvests were poor, and continued to export grain. Peasants were forbidden to leave their villages to look for food, and a law of 1932 punished even taking small amounts of grain from collective fields. The result was a terrible **famine** in Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus and the Volga region.

Historians estimate that between about 5 and 7 million people died across the USSR, with some estimates higher. In Ukraine, where the famine is remembered as the **Holodomor**, estimates of deaths are in the millions; in Kazakhstan, where nomadic herders had been forced to settle, a very large share of the Kazakh people died. Historians and governments disagree about whether the famine was deliberately aimed at Ukrainians as a nation, or was the result of brutal policies applied to all peasants; they agree that Stalin's government knew of the famine and continued policies that made it worse.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was collectivisation a success?"}
**From the regime's point of view:** the state gained control of the countryside and of grain supplies, fed the growing cities and paid for industrialisation; peasants who had resisted the party were broken.
**By other measures:** grain production fell in the early 1930s and livestock numbers took decades to recover; millions died in deportations and famine; collective farms remained inefficient, and peasants produced much of their food on small private plots.
**Judgement:** collectivisation achieved Stalin's political aims, control and grain for industry, but as agriculture and in human terms it was a disaster.
:::

## Life in the Soviet Union

- **Industrial workers:** jobs were plentiful and education spread, but real wages fell in the early 1930s, housing was overcrowded, and discipline was harsh. Skilled workers and Stakhanovites did better.
- **Peasants:** collective farm members were paid little, often in kind; they could not leave without permission; private plots provided much of their food.
- **Women:** millions entered paid work in factories, building and offices, and more trained as doctors and engineers; free crèches helped some. But most women still did all the housework, a "double burden". From 1936 the regime stressed family life, banning abortion and making divorce harder.
- **Ethnic minorities:** in the 1920s national languages and cultures had been encouraged; in the 1930s the Russian language was made compulsory in schools, national leaders were purged, and some whole peoples, such as the Koreans of the Soviet Far East in 1937, were deported. Kazakh nomads suffered terribly from forced settlement.
- **The new elite:** party officials, managers and engineers enjoyed better housing, shops and holidays.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing collectivisation with the NEP"}
The NEP (1921) allowed private farming and trade. Collectivisation (from 1929) abolished private farms and replaced them with collective farms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Five-Year Plans improved living standards"}
They built heavy industry. Consumer goods, housing and food were neglected, and living standards fell in the early 1930s.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating famine figures as exact"}
Say "historians estimate between about 5 and 7 million" and note the debate, rather than giving a single figure as fact.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stalin abandoned the NEP in 1928 to industrialise fast, strengthen defence, control the peasants and build socialism.
- The Five-Year Plans, run by Gosplan, greatly increased heavy industry but neglected consumer goods and relied on harsh discipline and forced labour.
- Forced collectivisation from 1929 destroyed private farming; "kulaks" were deported and livestock numbers halved.
- The famine of 1932-33 killed millions (estimates about 5-7 million), especially in Ukraine and Kazakhstan.
- Workers gained jobs and education but faced hardship; women joined the workforce but carried a double burden; minorities faced Russification and deportation.
:::
