## Introduction

The town of Tete, on the Zambezi in Mozambique, is only a few hours' drive from Zimbabwe's north-eastern border. In the sixteenth century it was one of the first places where Portuguese traders settled inland, hoping to reach the gold of the Mutapa state. Over the next three centuries the Portuguese traded, preached, fought and took land in the Zambezi valley, and the **prazo system** they created changed the lives of many African communities.

This lesson explains why the Portuguese came, what they did, how they related to Africans and how the prazo system worked. You should know about the Mutapa and Rozvi states first; this lesson adds the Portuguese side of that story.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prazo"}
A large estate of land in the Zambezi valley granted by the Portuguese Crown to a settler, usually for three generations, in return for a rent and loyalty.
:::

:::definition{term="Prazero"}
The holder of a prazo; prazeros (often Afro-Portuguese) ruled their estates like chiefs.
:::

:::definition{term="Achikunda"}
The armed followers of the prazeros, many of them originally enslaved or captured people, who acted as soldiers, hunters and collectors of tribute.
:::

:::definition{term="Feira (trading fair)"}
A market post inland where Portuguese traders, with the permission of African rulers, exchanged cloth, beads and other goods for gold and ivory.
:::

:::definition{term="Mussoco"}
The tribute in grain, goods or labour that people living on a prazo had to pay to the prazero.
:::

## Why the Portuguese came

After Vasco da Gama sailed round Africa to India in 1497–1498, the Portuguese set out to control the Indian Ocean trade. On the East African coast they had several aims:

- **Gold**: they believed the interior held rich gold mines; some even linked them with the legendary land of Ophir.
- **Ivory** and other goods for the Indian Ocean trade.
- **Trade rivalry**: they wanted to take over the gold trade from the **Swahili-Arab** (Muslim) traders who controlled it through Sofala.
- **Christianity**: to spread the Catholic faith and oppose Islam.
- **Prestige and empire**: to build a Portuguese trading empire.

They built a fort at **Sofala** in 1505 and occupied Mozambique Island in 1507. In the 1530s they set up posts at **Sena** and **Tete** on the Zambezi, giving access to the Mutapa state.

## Portuguese activities

### Trade and the fairs

Portuguese traders, and African agents working for them, travelled inland. With the Mutapa's permission they traded at **fairs** such as Masapa, Luanze and Bukutu, and later at Dambarare. At Masapa a Portuguese official recognised by the Mutapa, the "captain of the gates", supervised the traders. Cloth, beads and other goods were exchanged for gold and ivory.

### Gonçalo da Silveira's mission (1561)

The Jesuit **Gonçalo da Silveira** reached the Mutapa court in 1561. He baptised the Mutapa and members of his family, but within weeks he was killed on the king's orders. Portuguese accounts say Muslim traders at the court had persuaded the Mutapa that Silveira was a spy and a sorcerer.

### The Barreto expedition (1569–1573)

Portugal sent **Francisco Barreto** with an army, officially to punish the Mutapa for Silveira's death, but mainly to seize the gold mines. The expedition went up the Zambezi in 1571–1572. It failed:

1. **Disease**, especially malaria, killed many soldiers.
2. **Tsetse fly** killed horses and cattle.
3. **African resistance**: fighting with local peoples cost lives and supplies.
4. **Heat, distance and poor supplies** wore the army down.

Barreto himself died at Sena in 1573. A second attempt under Vasco Fernandes Homem through Manica also failed.

### Treaties with the Mutapa

Later the Portuguese gained influence through **treaties** with weakened Mutapa kings: Gatsi Rusere promised them the mines in 1607 in return for military help, and in 1629 Mavhura accepted Portuguese overlordship. Portuguese traders and settlers spread across the plateau until Changamire Dombo drove them out in the 1690s.

## Relations with Africans

Relations changed over time:

- **Cooperation in trade**: for most of the sixteenth century, African rulers controlled the trade and set its terms; the Portuguese gave gifts to the Mutapa in return for permission to trade.
- **Religious contact**: missionaries baptised some rulers and people, but few converted deeply.
- **Conflict**: the killing of Silveira, the Barreto expedition and fighting with local peoples.
- **Interference and domination**: in the seventeenth century the Portuguese exploited civil wars to install puppet kings and take land.
- **Intermarriage**: Portuguese men married African women, creating an **Afro-Portuguese** population, which became important in the prazos.

## The prazo system

From the early seventeenth century, Portuguese settlers, traders and soldiers gained land in the Zambezi valley, by grants from African rulers, purchase or conquest. The Portuguese Crown turned these into **prazos da coroa** (Crown estates), granted to settlers in return for rent and loyalty.

### Characteristics

- Prazos were usually granted **for three lifetimes** (generations).
- In theory they were passed down through **women**: a prazo was inherited by the eldest daughter, who had to marry a Portuguese-born man. This was meant to keep the land in Portuguese hands, but in practice many prazo-holders were Afro-Portuguese women and men, the famous *donas* among them.
- The **prazero** ruled like an African chief: settling disputes, collecting **mussoco** (tribute), and controlling trade.
- Prazeros kept private armies of **achikunda**, who hunted elephants, collected tribute and fought.
- Prazeros traded ivory and gold and, especially in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, **enslaved people**.
- Prazeros often became **independent** of the Portuguese government, fighting each other and ignoring orders from Lisbon.

:::example{title="Explaining why the prazeros became independent warlords"}
1. **Distance:** Lisbon and even the coast were far away; Portuguese officials were few and weak inland.
2. **Armies:** each prazero had his own achikunda, so he did not need Portuguese soldiers.
3. **Wealth:** ivory, gold and later slave trading made prazeros rich.
4. **Local roots:** most prazeros were Afro-Portuguese, married into African families and ruled like chiefs, so their power rested on local alliances, not on Portugal.
5. **Result:** by the nineteenth century powerful prazero families acted as independent rulers. Portugal tried to abolish the prazo system by law in the nineteenth century, but in practice the strongest families kept their power until late in the century.
:::

### Impact on Africans

**Negative**
- **Loss of land and independence**: African communities came under the rule of prazeros.
- **Exploitation**: heavy tribute (mussoco) and forced labour.
- **Slave trading and raiding**, especially in the nineteenth century, which caused insecurity, depopulation and famine.
- **Wars** between prazeros and with African states.

**Other effects**
- **Cultural mixing**: an Afro-Portuguese society with its own customs, language and religion developed.
- **New crops and goods** spread, such as maize and cassava from the Americas.
- Some Africans gained power as achikunda soldiers and later as independent leaders.

:::example{title="Weighing the impact of the prazo system"}
**Claim:** "The prazo system brought nothing but suffering to Africans."

1. **For:** tribute, forced labour, slave raiding and wars caused great hardship and loss of land.
2. **Against:** some Africans, especially achikunda, gained power and wealth; new crops spread; prazeros often ruled through African customs.
3. **Judgement:** for most ordinary people the system was exploitative, but its effects were not the same for every group.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Portuguese conquered the Mutapa in the sixteenth century"}
The Barreto expedition failed. Portuguese influence came later, through treaties with weakened kings in the seventeenth century.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking prazeros were obedient Portuguese officials"}
Many became independent warlords who ignored Lisbon.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Silveira and Barreto"}
Silveira was a missionary killed in 1561. Barreto led the military expedition of 1569–1573.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Portuguese came for gold, ivory, control of trade from the Swahili-Arabs, Christianity and empire.
- They built Sofala (1505), set up Sena and Tete (1530s) and traded at fairs with the Mutapa's permission.
- Silveira was killed in 1561; the Barreto expedition (1569–1573) failed because of disease, tsetse and resistance.
- Treaties of 1607 and 1629 gave the Portuguese power over weakened Mutapa kings until the Rozvi drove them out in the 1690s.
- The prazo system granted estates to prazeros, who ruled with achikunda armies, collected mussoco and later traded in slaves.
- Africans suffered loss of land, exploitation and slave raiding, while an Afro-Portuguese society developed.
:::
