## Introduction

Land was at the heart of Zimbabwe's history. It was taken from African communities after 1890, divided by race under the Land Apportionment Act (1930) and the Land Tenure Act (1969), and its return was a central aim of the liberation struggle. At independence in 1980, however, the land remained divided much as it had been, and the Lancaster House constitution protected existing owners for ten years.

How Zimbabwe tried to solve the land question, and the results, are among the most debated topics in its recent history. Supporters see land reform as the completion of the liberation struggle; critics point to falling production and the way it was carried out. Your task is to understand the aims, the phases, the outcomes and the different views.

Examiners ask you to **describe the reasons for land reform**, **assess the role of the Constitution**, **describe indigenisation policies** and **explain the reaction of Western countries**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
Changing the ownership and use of land, especially by redistributing it from large landowners to landless or land-short people.
:::

:::definition{term="Willing-seller, willing-buyer"}
The principle, in the Lancaster House constitution, that the government could acquire land for resettlement only by buying it from an owner who chose to sell, at a fair price.
:::

:::definition{term="Fast Track Land Reform Programme"}
The programme from 2000 under which the government compulsorily acquired most large commercial farms and redistributed the land, mainly under the A1 (smallholder) and A2 (commercial) models.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenisation"}
Policies to increase ownership and control of the economy by indigenous Zimbabweans, in particular the requirement that larger businesses be at least 51% owned by indigenous Zimbabweans.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1980-1990: willing-seller, willing-buyer resettlement.
- 1992: Land Acquisition Act allows compulsory acquisition with compensation.
- February 2000: draft constitution rejected in a referendum; farm occupations follow.
- July 2000: Fast Track Land Reform Programme launched.
- 2007: Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act.
- 2013: new Constitution, including provisions on land.
:::

## The land question at independence

In 1980 a few thousand white commercial farmers owned a large share of the country's best farmland, mostly in the wetter natural regions, while most African families farmed crowded and often drier communal areas (the former reserves and Tribal Trust Lands). Many people who had supported the liberation struggle expected land to be returned quickly. But the Lancaster House constitution allowed land to be bought only from willing sellers, with adequate compensation, for ten years.

## Phases of land reform

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1980 | Resettlement on willing-seller, willing-buyer basis begins |
| 1990 | Ten-year Lancaster House restriction ends |
| 1992 | Land Acquisition Act |
| 1997 | Government lists many farms for acquisition |
| 1998 | International donors' conference on land in Harare |
| 2000 | Referendum rejects draft constitution (February); farm occupations; Fast Track programme launched (July) |
| 2001-2002 | United States and European Union measures against Zimbabwe |
| 2005 | Constitutional amendment vests acquired agricultural land in the state |
| 2013 | New Constitution entrenches land reform and compensation for improvements |

### Phase 1: willing-seller, willing-buyer (1980s)

The government bought farms that owners offered for sale and resettled families on them, with some funding from Britain. Tens of thousands of families were resettled, but far fewer than the government's target. Problems included the high price and often poor quality of the land offered, limited government funds, and slow planning.

### Phase 2: the 1990s

After the Lancaster House restriction ended, the **Land Acquisition Act (1992)** allowed compulsory acquisition with fair compensation. In 1997 the government listed many farms for acquisition. Britain's new government said it did not accept a special responsibility to pay for land purchase, while a donors' conference in 1998 agreed principles but little money. Progress remained slow and frustration grew.

### Phase 3: the Fast Track Land Reform Programme (from 2000)

In February 2000 voters rejected a draft constitution that included a land clause. Soon afterwards, war veterans and others began occupying commercial farms. In July 2000 the government launched the **Fast Track Land Reform Programme**, acquiring most large commercial farms. Land was allocated under two main models:

- **A1:** small plots for families, usually with shared grazing;
- **A2:** medium and large commercial farms for people expected to farm commercially.

According to government figures and independent studies, well over a hundred thousand families received land.

## The role of the Constitution

The Constitution was changed several times to make land reform possible:

- **Lancaster House (1980):** protected property and required willing-seller, willing-buyer purchase for ten years.
- **Amendments from 1990:** allowed compulsory acquisition; a 2000 amendment stated that compensation for the land itself was the responsibility of the former colonial power, with Zimbabwe paying only for improvements such as buildings and dams; a 2005 amendment vested acquired agricultural land in the state and limited challenges in the courts.
- **The 2013 Constitution:** confirms that land acquired for resettlement belongs to the state, that compensation is paid for improvements rather than for the land, and sets up a **Zimbabwe Land Commission**. It also commits the state to equal access to land for women and men.

:::example{title="Weighing the role of the Constitution"}
1. **As an obstacle:** the Lancaster House constitution slowed redistribution in the 1980s by protecting existing owners.
2. **As a tool:** later amendments and the 2013 Constitution gave legal force to compulsory acquisition and made land reform hard to reverse.
3. **As a source of debate:** critics argue that amendments limiting court challenges weakened property rights and the rule of law; supporters argue they corrected a colonial injustice that the courts had protected.
4. **Judgement:** the Constitution first restricted and later enabled land reform, which shows how political decisions shaped the law.
:::

## The reaction of Western countries

- **Britain** criticised the fast-track programme and the violence on some farms, and relations deteriorated sharply.
- **The European Union** from 2002 imposed measures it described as targeted: an arms embargo and travel bans and asset freezes on named individuals and companies.
- **The United States** passed the Zimbabwe Democracy and Economic Recovery Act (2001), which restricted support for Zimbabwe from international financial institutions.
- Aid and investment fell; Zimbabwe was suspended from the Commonwealth in 2002 and left it in 2003.

The Zimbabwean government described these measures as illegal sanctions that damaged the whole economy; Western governments argued that they were aimed at individuals and at concerns about human rights and governance. Students should be able to explain both views.

## Benefits and problems

:::example{title="Assessing outcomes: different views"}
**Recognised benefits:** large numbers of previously land-short families gained land; studies of some resettlement areas found many new farmers producing and investing; smallholder tobacco production grew strongly in the 2010s; ownership of farmland changed from a small minority to a much wider group of Zimbabweans.

**Recognised problems:** agricultural output, especially of commercial crops and exports, fell sharply in the early 2000s, contributing to food shortages; many farm workers lost jobs and homes; there was violence on some farms; new farmers often lacked title, credit, inputs and equipment; some prominent people received more than one farm; legal disputes and loss of investor confidence followed. In 2020 the government agreed to compensate former farm owners for improvements.

**Judgement:** historians and economists still debate how far the problems resulted from the programme itself, from the speed and manner of implementation, from drought and from the wider economic crisis and sanctions.
:::

## Indigenisation and economic empowerment

- The **Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act (2007)** required that businesses above a set size be at least **51% owned by indigenous Zimbabweans**.
- **Community Share Ownership Trusts** gave communities in mining areas a share in mining companies.
- Funds and agencies were set up to lend to indigenous entrepreneurs.
- Supporters said these measures gave Zimbabweans a fair share of their economy; critics said they discouraged foreign investment and benefited a few. In 2018 the law was changed so that the 51% rule applied only to certain minerals, and it was relaxed further afterwards.

### Gender equity

The 2013 Constitution guarantees equality between women and men, requires gender balance in public institutions, reserves 60 seats in the National Assembly for women elected by proportional representation (for a fixed number of parliaments), and created a **Zimbabwe Gender Commission**. Land policy aims to give women equal access to land, although in practice fewer women than men received land under the fast-track programme.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Starting the story in 2000"}
Land reform began in 1980. Explain the willing-seller, willing-buyer phase and the 1990s before the fast-track programme.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
Examiners expect aims, benefits and problems, and the views of both the government and its critics. Avoid slogans.
:::

:::mistake{title="Invented figures"}
Use dates you are sure of and describe outcomes in general terms or as estimates; do not invent production or settlement statistics.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1: describe and assess"}
"Describe the reasons for land reform" needs the colonial background, land inequality in 1980, the slow pace of willing-seller, willing-buyer, and political pressure. "Assess the role of the Constitution" needs the Lancaster House limits, later amendments and the 2013 Constitution, with a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: Western reactions"}
For "Explain the reaction of Western countries", name the actions (criticism, EU measures from 2002, US act of 2001, Commonwealth suspension) and give both the Western explanation and the Zimbabwean government's view.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- At independence land remained very unequally divided, and the Lancaster House constitution protected existing owners for ten years.
- Phases: willing-seller, willing-buyer (1980s); compulsory acquisition with compensation (1992 Act); the Fast Track Land Reform Programme from 2000 (A1 and A2 models).
- Constitutional amendments and the 2013 Constitution made land reform legal and hard to reverse, with compensation for improvements only.
- Western countries criticised the programme and imposed measures; the government called them illegal sanctions.
- Many families gained land, but output fell, workers were displaced and disputes followed; indigenisation (2007) and gender measures aimed to widen economic ownership.
:::
