## Introduction

Independence in 1980 raised great hopes. The new government promised to bring schools, clinics, jobs and equal rights to the majority who had been excluded under colonial rule. In the 1980s Zimbabwe became known across Africa for its rapid expansion of education and health. But the decade also saw a painful conflict in Matabeleland and the Midlands, and in the 1990s economic reforms brought hardship. Since 2000 the country has faced serious economic and political challenges.

You should already know about independence and the policy of reconciliation. Examiners ask you to **describe and explain** the policies of the 1980s and to **assess** social, economic and political developments since independence. Land reform and foreign policy are separate topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Growth with equity"}
The government's economic policy statement of 1981: the economy should grow, and the benefits of growth should be shared fairly, especially with the majority who had been excluded.
:::

:::definition{term="ESAP (Economic Structural Adjustment Programme)"}
The economic reform programme adopted from 1991, supported by the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, which reduced government controls and spending and opened the economy to the market.
:::

:::definition{term="Retrenchment"}
Dismissing workers to reduce costs, often when a company or the state cuts its workforce.
:::

:::definition{term="Executive presidency"}
A system in which the President is both head of state and head of government with executive power. Zimbabwe adopted it at the end of 1987, replacing the post of Prime Minister.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1981: growth with equity policy.
- 1982: Legal Age of Majority Act.
- 1983-1987: disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands (Gukurahundi).
- 22 December 1987: Unity Accord between ZANU-PF and PF-ZAPU.
- 31 December 1987: executive presidency begins.
- From 1991: Economic Structural Adjustment Programme (ESAP).
- 2013: new Constitution.
:::

## Social policies

### Education

The government treated education as a right. Primary education became free from 1980, and thousands of new primary and secondary schools were opened, many built by communities. Enrolment grew very fast: primary enrolment roughly doubled within a few years, and secondary enrolment grew many times over. To find enough teachers, the government trained them on the job through programmes such as **ZINTEC**, and adult literacy campaigns were launched. By the 1990s Zimbabwe had one of the highest literacy rates in Africa.

**Problems:** rapid growth meant crowded classes, untrained teachers and shortages of books; many school leavers could not find jobs.

### Health

Free health care was introduced for people on low incomes, and many rural health centres were built. Village health workers, immunisation programmes and clean-water projects reduced child deaths and improved life expectancy in the 1980s. From the late 1980s, however, the HIV/AIDS epidemic caused great suffering and reversed many gains.

### Women's rights

The **Legal Age of Majority Act (1982)** made all Zimbabweans, women included, legal adults at 18. Women could now sign contracts, own property and marry without a guardian's consent. A ministry responsible for women's affairs was created, and women were encouraged into education, employment and politics. Customary practices and unequal access to land remained obstacles.

### Recreation and sport

Sport and recreation were opened to all races and promoted through schools and a government ministry. Zimbabwe's women's hockey team won Olympic gold in Moscow in 1980, and the country gained Test cricket status in 1992. New stadiums and community facilities were built.

### Growth with equity (1980s)

The government kept the mixed economy it inherited but sought to share its benefits. It introduced **minimum wages**, controlled prices of basic goods, subsidised food, expanded state companies (parastatals), and directed spending to schools, clinics, roads and water in the former Tribal Trust Lands (now Communal Areas). Peasant farmers received credit and extension services, and their maize and cotton sales to the marketing boards rose sharply. However, unemployment remained high, government debt grew and foreign investment was limited.

### ESAP (from 1991)

By the end of the 1980s the economy was growing slowly and the government was spending more than it earned. In 1991 it adopted the **Economic Structural Adjustment Programme**. Its measures included:

- removing price controls and subsidies;
- devaluing the Zimbabwe dollar and liberalising trade;
- cutting the civil service and government spending;
- introducing fees ("cost recovery") in health and education;
- commercialising or privatising parastatals.

:::example{title="A policy analysed step by step: the effects of ESAP"}
1. **Aim:** make the economy more efficient and competitive, reduce government debt and attract investment.
2. **Short-term effects on people:** prices of food and transport rose as subsidies were removed; school and clinic fees made services harder to afford.
3. **Effects on jobs:** retrenchments in the civil service and in companies that could not compete with imports, such as some textile and clothing firms.
4. **Made worse by drought:** the severe drought of 1991-92 hit just as ESAP began, increasing hardship.
5. **Positive effects claimed:** more goods in the shops, fewer shortages and some growth in exports.
6. **Judgement:** most historians and economists conclude that ESAP brought hardship to ordinary people without delivering the growth it promised, although its supporters argue that the reforms were needed and were not fully carried out.
:::

### Later economic challenges

From the late 1990s Zimbabwe faced growing economic difficulties: unbudgeted spending, falling agricultural and industrial output after 2000, the loss of foreign support, and from the mid-2000s very high inflation. In 2009 the country moved to using foreign currencies such as the US dollar, which brought prices under control. Since then governments have introduced and changed national currencies several times. Historians and economists continue to debate the relative weight of the causes, including land reform, sanctions and government economic management.

## Political developments

### The disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands (1983-1987)

After independence, relations between ZANU-PF and PF-ZAPU broke down. In 1982 arms caches were found on properties linked to ZAPU, and Joshua Nkomo and other ZAPU leaders were dismissed from the government; some former ZIPRA commanders were arrested. Armed "dissidents" became active in parts of Matabeleland and killed civilians. The government sent the army's **Fifth Brigade** into Matabeleland North from January 1983 and later into Matabeleland South and parts of the Midlands. The period is known as **Gukurahundi**.

Many thousands of civilians, most of them Ndebele-speaking people with no part in the fighting, were killed, beaten or detained, and food supplies to some areas were cut off. Estimates of the number killed range from several thousand to about 20,000. A report by the Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace and the Legal Resources Foundation, *Breaking the Silence* (1997), documented these events. The disturbances remain a deeply painful memory, and processes to acknowledge them and to support survivors have been discussed and begun since.

### The Unity Accord (22 December 1987)

Talks between the two parties led to the **Unity Accord** of 22 December 1987: PF-ZAPU merged into ZANU-PF, and Joshua Nkomo later became a Vice-President. An amnesty in 1988 led the remaining dissidents to surrender, and the violence ended.

### Constitutional changes

- **1987:** the 20 reserved white seats were abolished, and from 31 December 1987 Robert Mugabe became **executive President**, combining the roles of head of state and head of government.
- **1990:** the Senate was abolished (it was later restored in 2005).

### Later political developments (in outline)

From the late 1990s new opposition parties and civic groups grew, notably the Movement for Democratic Change (1999). In February 2000 voters rejected a draft constitution in a referendum. Elections in the 2000s were closely contested and disputed. After the 2008 elections a power-sharing government ran from 2009 to 2013, during which a new **Constitution** was approved by referendum and adopted in 2013. In November 2017 Robert Mugabe resigned after military intervention, and Emmerson Mnangagwa became President.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the 1980s as one success story"}
The 1980s brought real achievements in education and health, but also the disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands. A balanced answer includes both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing ESAP with growth with equity"}
Growth with equity (1980s) relied on state controls, subsidies and social spending; ESAP (from 1991) removed many of these controls and subsidies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving invented statistics"}
Use well-established facts (dates of acts, the Unity Accord, ESAP from 1991). Where numbers are disputed, such as deaths in the 1980s or inflation figures, say they are estimates or describe the trend.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Assess developments"}
For "Assess the economic, political and social developments in Zimbabwe", give achievements and problems in each area, then reach a judgement supported by evidence.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Writing about sensitive events"}
Write about the disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands factually: dates, what happened, who was affected, estimates labelled as estimates, and how they ended. Avoid slogans and blame of individuals.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Education and health expanded rapidly after 1980; the Legal Age of Majority Act (1982) gave women adult legal status at 18.
- Growth with equity (1981) aimed to share the benefits of growth through wages, subsidies and social spending.
- ESAP (from 1991) removed controls and subsidies and introduced fees; with drought it caused job losses and hardship.
- The disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands (1983-1987) killed many thousands of civilians; the Unity Accord of 22 December 1987 ended the conflict.
- The executive presidency began at the end of 1987; later decades brought economic crisis, contested politics and the 2013 Constitution.
:::
