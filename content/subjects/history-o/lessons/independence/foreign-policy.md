## Introduction

When Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980 it entered a world divided by the Cold War and a region still dominated by apartheid South Africa. The new state had to decide which countries to work with, how to protect its independence and how to help neighbours still under minority rule. Since then its relations with the rest of the world have changed greatly: from wide Western goodwill in the 1980s, to sharp disputes with Britain, the European Union and the United States after 2000, to closer ties with China.

You should already know how Zimbabwe won independence and the main policies of the government after 1980. This lesson covers the **principles** of foreign policy, relations with the **West**, the **Look East Policy** and **peacekeeping and peacemaking**. The international organisations themselves (SADC, the OAU/AU, the Commonwealth, the Non-Aligned Movement and the UN) have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign policy"}
The aims and methods a country uses in its relations with other countries and international organisations.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-alignment"}
Refusing to join either side in the Cold War (the Western bloc led by the United States or the Eastern bloc led by the Soviet Union).
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions (restrictive measures)"}
Penalties imposed by one state or group of states on another state, or on named people and companies, to force a change of policy: for example travel bans, asset freezes, arms embargoes or limits on aid and loans.
:::

:::definition{term="Peacekeeping"}
Sending troops, police or observers, usually under the United Nations or a regional body, to keep the peace after a ceasefire.
:::

:::definition{term="Peacemaking"}
Diplomatic or military action to end a conflict, for example mediation or intervention to support a government.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1980: independence (18 April); Zimbabwe joins the Commonwealth, the OAU and the UN, and adopts non-alignment.
- From 1982: Zimbabwean troops protect the Beira corridor in Mozambique.
- 1986: Zimbabwe hosts the Non-Aligned Movement summit in Harare.
- 1998: Zimbabwe sends troops to the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC).
- 2002: the EU adopts restrictive measures against Zimbabwe; the Commonwealth suspends Zimbabwe.
- December 2003: Zimbabwe leaves the Commonwealth.
- From about 2003: the Look East Policy.
:::

## Principles of Zimbabwe's foreign policy

1. **Sovereignty and non-interference:** respect for the independence of states and opposition to outside interference in domestic affairs.
2. **Non-alignment:** staying out of the Cold War blocs while trading with both.
3. **Pan-Africanism and African solidarity:** working with African states through the OAU/AU and SADC.
4. **Support for liberation:** helping the struggles against apartheid in South Africa and for Namibia's independence.
5. **Peaceful co-existence and the peaceful settlement of disputes.**
6. **Economic development:** using foreign relations to attract trade, investment and aid.
7. **Multilateralism:** working through the UN and other international bodies.

## Relations with Britain, the EU and the West

In the 1980s relations were mostly good. Britain, the United States and other Western countries gave aid for reconstruction, and Western donors supported the expansion of schools and clinics. Disagreements arose over South Africa, because Zimbabwe called for sanctions against apartheid while Britain under Margaret Thatcher opposed them.

Relations broke down after 2000. The fast-track land reform programme, violence around elections and disputes over the rule of law led to strong criticism from Britain, the EU and the United States.

- In 2001 the United States passed the Zimbabwe Democracy and Economic Recovery Act (ZDERA), which among other things directed US representatives at international lenders to oppose new loans to Zimbabwe.
- In 2002 the European Union adopted **restrictive measures**: a travel ban and asset freeze on named individuals, an arms embargo and the suspension of most development co-operation with the government.
- The Commonwealth suspended Zimbabwe in 2002; Zimbabwe left it in December 2003.

:::example{title="Weighing different views on sanctions"}
1. **The Zimbabwe government's view:** the measures were illegal sanctions imposed as punishment for land reform; they cut off credit and investment, deepened the economic crisis and hurt ordinary people.
2. **The EU and US view:** the measures were targeted at named individuals and companies because of concerns about human rights, elections and the rule of law, and humanitarian aid continued.
3. **Other observers:** many economists point out that the economy's decline also had domestic causes, such as falling farm output, budget deficits and inflation, while also noting that the measures and the loss of foreign credit made recovery harder.
4. **Your judgement:** decide which evidence is strongest, and show that you understand each view.
:::

From the 2010s relations began to improve slowly. The EU removed most of its measures over the following years, although some remained, and after 2017 the government adopted a policy of "engagement and re-engagement" with Western countries.

## The Look East Policy and relations with China

From about 2003 the government announced a **Look East Policy**, seeking closer economic and political ties with China and other Asian countries such as Malaysia, Indonesia and India.

**Reasons for the Look East Policy:**
- Western sanctions and the loss of Western credit and investment.
- The rapid growth of China's economy and its demand for raw materials.
- Old friendship: China had supported ZANU and ZANLA during the liberation struggle.
- China's policy of not attaching political conditions about domestic affairs to its loans and trade.
- Support at the UN: in 2008 China and Russia vetoed a proposed Security Council resolution to impose sanctions on Zimbabwe.

**Relations with China** grew in trade, investment and loans. China became an important buyer of Zimbabwean tobacco and minerals. Chinese-funded or Chinese-built projects have included the National Sports Stadium in Harare (opened in the 1980s), expansions of power stations such as Kariba South and Hwange, and a new Parliament building. Critics point to the debts created by loans, the quality of some goods, poor working conditions in some firms and the environmental impact of some mining. Supporters stress the new infrastructure and the investment that came when Western lenders stayed away.

## Peacekeeping and peacemaking

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1980 | Independence; Zimbabwe joins the UN, OAU and Commonwealth |
| 1982 | Zimbabwean troops begin protecting the Beira corridor in Mozambique |
| 1986 | Harare hosts the Non-Aligned Movement summit |
| 1992 | The Rome peace accords end Mozambique's civil war; Zimbabwean troops later withdraw |
| 1990s | Zimbabwe contributes to UN missions, including in Angola and Somalia |
| 1998 | Zimbabwe, Angola and Namibia send troops to the DRC |
| 2002 | Zimbabwean troops withdraw from the DRC |

### Mozambique (1980s)

From 1982 thousands of Zimbabwean troops were deployed in Mozambique to protect the **Beira corridor** (the railway, road and oil pipeline linking Zimbabwe to the port of Beira) against attacks by RENAMO rebels, who were backed first by Rhodesia and then by South Africa. The corridor gave Zimbabwe a route to the sea that did not pass through South Africa. The troops withdrew after the Rome General Peace Accords of 1992.

### UN missions

Zimbabwe has contributed soldiers, police officers and military observers to United Nations missions, including in Angola and Somalia in the 1990s and to later missions elsewhere in Africa.

### The DRC (1998-2002)

In August 1998 Zimbabwe, Angola and Namibia sent troops to support the government of Laurent Kabila in the Democratic Republic of the Congo against rebels backed by Rwanda and Uganda. Zimbabwe described the intervention as a SADC operation to defend a member state's sovereignty. The Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement was signed in 1999, and Zimbabwe withdrew its troops in 2002.

:::example{title="Assessing the DRC intervention"}
1. **Arguments for:** it helped prevent the overthrow of a recognised government of a SADC member; it upheld the principle that borders and sovereignty should be respected; it contributed to the ceasefire and later peace talks.
2. **Arguments against:** it was very expensive at a time of economic difficulty; SADC members were divided over it; critics alleged that business interests in Congolese minerals influenced the decision.
3. **Judgement:** a balanced answer weighs the aims and outcome against the costs and the debate.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all Western measures \"sanctions on the whole country\" without explanation"}
Explain what the measures were (travel bans, asset freezes, arms embargo, limits on aid and loans) and give both the government's view and the Western view.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Mozambique and the DRC"}
Mozambique (1982-1992) was about protecting the Beira corridor from RENAMO. The DRC (1998-2002) was about supporting Laurent Kabila's government against rebels backed by Rwanda and Uganda.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: describe relations"}
"Describe Zimbabwe's relations with China and the European Union" needs specific examples for each: trade, loans and projects for China; aid, the 2002 measures and later re-engagement for the EU.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the Look East Policy"}
Give several reasons and link them: sanctions closed Western doors, so the government turned to fast-growing China, an old ally from the liberation struggle.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe's foreign policy rests on sovereignty, non-alignment, Pan-Africanism, support for liberation, peaceful co-existence and development.
- Relations with the West were good in the 1980s but broke down after 2000; the EU's 2002 restrictive measures are viewed very differently by the government and by Western states.
- The Look East Policy (from about 2003) built closer ties with China in trade, loans and infrastructure, with benefits and criticisms.
- Zimbabwe protected the Beira corridor in Mozambique (1982-1992), served in UN missions and intervened in the DRC (1998-2002).
- Zimbabwe left the Commonwealth in December 2003 and later sought re-engagement with the West.
:::
