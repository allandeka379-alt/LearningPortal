## Introduction

From 1885 to 1908 a huge area of central Africa, about seventy-five times the size of Belgium, was the personal property of one man: King **Leopold II** of Belgium. He never visited it. He called it the **Congo Free State**, but for millions of Congolese it was a place of forced labour, hostage-taking and violence, organised to extract wild rubber and ivory for profit.

The Congo Free State is the clearest example of what Cambridge calls **private imperialism**: a colony run for the profit of an individual and companies rather than a government. It also produced one of the first international human rights campaigns. Zimbabwe's own history of forced labour (*chibharo*) under company rule shows that the Congo was an extreme case of practices found across colonial Africa.

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 5, focus point on the varied impacts of imperialism on Africans. You should know the motives for imperialism and the Berlin Conference.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Private imperialism"}
Colonial rule by an individual or a company for private profit, rather than by a national government. Leopold II's Congo Free State is the main example.
:::

:::definition{term="Concession company"}
A private company given the right by a colonial ruler to exploit the resources of a large area, often with powers to tax and police the people living there.
:::

:::definition{term="Force Publique"}
The army of the Congo Free State: European officers commanding African soldiers, many of them conscripted. It enforced the rubber system.
:::

:::definition{term="Congo Reform Association"}
The campaign organisation founded in Britain in 1904 by E. D. Morel and Roger Casement to expose the abuses in the Congo and end Leopold's rule.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1865:** Leopold II becomes King of the Belgians.
- **1876:** Leopold sets up the International African Association.
- **1879-1884:** Henry Morton Stanley makes treaties with Congolese rulers for Leopold.
- **1885:** the Congo Free State is recognised as Leopold's personal possession.
- **1904:** the Casement Report is published; the Congo Reform Association is founded.
- **1908:** Belgium takes over the Congo as the Belgian Congo.
:::

## How Leopold gained the Congo

Leopold believed that a colony would make Belgium, a small and recently independent country, rich and powerful, but the Belgian government and parliament were not interested. So he acted privately. In 1876 he organised a conference in Brussels and set up the **International African Association**, presented as a scientific and humanitarian body to explore Africa and fight the East African slave trade.

He then hired the explorer **Henry Morton Stanley**, who in 1879-1884 travelled up the Congo River, built stations and persuaded or pressured hundreds of local rulers to sign treaties, many of which they could not read, handing over their land and labour.

Leopold used skilful diplomacy. He promised free trade to other powers and presented himself as a humanitarian. During the **Berlin Conference** of 1884-85 the powers recognised his claim, and in 1885 the **Congo Free State** was established as his personal possession, not a Belgian colony.

## The rubber system

At first the colony lost money. Then, in the 1890s, world demand for rubber soared because of the bicycle tyre, the motor car and electrical cables. Wild rubber vines grew across the Congo's rainforest.

- Leopold declared most of the land "vacant" and therefore the property of the state. Congolese people lost the right to sell what grew on it.
- Large areas were handed to **concession companies**, in which Leopold held shares, and the rest was exploited directly by the state.
- Villages were given **quotas** of rubber to collect. Men had to go deep into the forest for weeks to tap the vines.
- The **Force Publique** and company sentries enforced the quotas. Women and children were taken as **hostages** until the men returned with rubber; villages that failed were burned; people were flogged with the *chicotte*, a whip of hippopotamus hide, and many were killed. Soldiers had to account for their bullets, and the severing of hands as proof became one of the most notorious abuses.

Rubber exports rose dramatically, and Leopold made a vast personal fortune, which he spent on buildings and monuments in Belgium.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the rubber system led to atrocities"}
1. **Demand:** world demand for rubber rose sharply in the 1890s.
2. **Profit motive:** Leopold and the companies wanted as much rubber as possible at the lowest cost, so they paid little or nothing for it.
3. **Forced labour:** without payment, rubber could only be collected by force, so quotas were enforced by soldiers and sentries.
4. **Rewards for violence:** agents earned bonuses based on how much rubber they collected, so they had reasons to be brutal.
5. **Result:** hostage-taking, floggings, mutilation and killing became part of the system itself, not occasional crimes.
:::

## The impact on the Congolese

The population of the Congo fell severely during Leopold's rule because of killings, the flight of people from their villages, famine when men were forced to collect rubber instead of farming, and epidemics such as sleeping sickness. There was no census, so the scale is uncertain: historians' estimates vary widely, and some estimate that the population fell by as much as half, a loss of several million people. Whatever the exact figure, it was a catastrophe for the peoples of the Congo. Societies were broken up, and the fear and distrust it created lasted long after 1908.

Congolese people resisted: some fled into the forest, some refused to collect rubber, and there were armed risings and mutinies within the Force Publique itself.

## The international campaign

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1885 | Congo Free State established |
| 1890 | George Washington Williams publishes an open letter to Leopold describing abuses |
| 1890s | Rubber boom; missionaries begin to report atrocities |
| 1903 | British Parliament calls for an inquiry; Casement is sent to investigate |
| 1904 | Casement Report published; Congo Reform Association founded |
| 1905 | Leopold's own commission of inquiry confirms many abuses |
| 1908 | Belgium annexes the Congo |

Reports came first from visitors and missionaries. The African American writer George Washington Williams described abuses in an open letter to Leopold in 1890. Missionaries, including the African American Presbyterian William Sheppard and the British missionary Alice Seeley Harris, whose photographs of mutilated people shocked the public, sent evidence home.

The shipping clerk **E. D. Morel** noticed that ships arriving in Belgium from the Congo carried rubber and ivory, but ships leaving for the Congo carried mainly guns and ammunition, not trade goods. He concluded that the rubber was being taken by force. The British consul **Roger Casement** investigated in 1903, and his report, published in 1904, confirmed the abuses. Morel and Casement founded the **Congo Reform Association**, which held mass meetings in Britain and the United States. Writers such as Mark Twain and Arthur Conan Doyle joined the campaign.

Leopold's own commission of inquiry reported in 1905 and confirmed many abuses. Under growing pressure, the Belgian parliament voted to take over the territory, and in **1908** the Congo Free State became the **Belgian Congo**. Leopold died in 1909.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: did the Belgian takeover change things for the Congolese?"}
**Some change:** the worst abuses of the rubber system were gradually brought to an end, and the wild rubber trade declined as plantation rubber from Asia became cheaper.
**Little change:** many officials stayed in post, forced labour continued in other forms (on plantations, mines and railways), and Congolese had no political rights until independence in 1960.
**Judgement:** 1908 ended Leopold's private rule and the most extreme abuses, but the Belgian Congo remained an exploitative colony.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Congo was a Belgian colony from 1885"}
From 1885 to 1908 the Congo Free State belonged personally to Leopold II. It became a Belgian colony only in 1908.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving an exact death toll as a fact"}
There was no census. Say that historians' estimates vary widely and that the population fell by millions, according to many estimates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing atrocities without explaining the system"}
Examiners want you to explain why abuses happened: quotas, profit, payment of agents by results and the absence of any outside control.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain why there were atrocities in the Congo Free State'"}
Link each reason to the rubber system: rising demand, Leopold's need for profit, forced labour quotas, bonuses for agents and the lack of any government supervision.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (overseas empires prescribed for June 2027)"}
For Congo sources, compare who wrote them: Leopold's officials defended the regime, missionaries and campaigners attacked it. A photograph described in a source is strong evidence of an event, but ask who took it and why.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Leopold II gained the Congo through Stanley's treaties and diplomacy at the Berlin Conference; from 1885 it was his personal possession.
- The rubber boom of the 1890s led to a system of forced labour, quotas, hostages and violence enforced by the Force Publique and concession companies.
- The population fell sharply; estimates vary, but many historians judge that millions died from violence, famine and disease.
- Missionaries, Morel, Casement and the Congo Reform Association exposed the abuses.
- Belgium took over the Congo in 1908; the worst abuses ended, but exploitation continued until independence in 1960.
:::
