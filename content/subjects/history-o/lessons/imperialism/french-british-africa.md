## Introduction

Once European powers had claimed African territory, they had to decide how to govern it with very few officials. France and Britain, the two largest colonial powers in Africa, are often contrasted: France is said to have ruled directly and tried to turn some Africans into French citizens, while Britain ruled indirectly through African rulers. In practice both systems were more mixed than the labels suggest, and their impacts on Africans varied greatly.

In Southern Rhodesia, the British South Africa Company and later the settler government ruled through white Native Commissioners and through chiefs whose powers they controlled. As you read, compare that with the systems in Senegal and Nigeria.

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 5, the focus point on the varied impacts of European imperialism on Africans. You should know the motives for imperialism and the outline of the Scramble for Africa.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Assimilation"}
The French policy of making colonised people culturally and legally French, through the French language, education and law, with French citizenship for those who qualified.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct rule"}
Government by officials of the colonial power, who replaced or controlled African rulers and governed through a chain of command from the colonial capital.
:::

:::definition{term="Indirect rule"}
The British system of governing through existing African rulers and institutions, supervised by British officials.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigénat"}
The French code of rules for colonial "subjects" (non-citizens) that allowed officials to punish Africans without trial and to demand forced labour.
:::

:::keyfact{title="People and dates to learn"}
- **Louis Faidherbe:** French governor of Senegal, 1854-1861 and 1863-1865.
- **The Four Communes:** Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque and Dakar, whose African inhabitants had French citizenship rights.
- **Frederick Lugard:** High Commissioner of Northern Nigeria (1900-1906), Governor-General of Nigeria (1914-1919).
- **1903:** British forces capture Kano and Sokoto.
- **1 January 1914:** Northern and Southern Nigeria amalgamated.
:::

## France in Senegal

The French had trading posts on the Senegal coast, at Saint-Louis and Gorée, for centuries. Under **Louis Faidherbe**, governor from 1854, France expanded inland. Faidherbe:

- Built forts along the Senegal River and fought African states that resisted, including the forces of the Muslim leader **Umar Tal**, which besieged the French fort at Médine in 1857.
- Created the **Tirailleurs Sénégalais** (1857), African soldiers under French officers who later fought across French Africa.
- Set up a school for the sons of chiefs to train African intermediaries loyal to France.
- Encouraged the growing of **groundnuts** for export to France.

The port of Dakar was occupied during his governorship and later became the capital of French West Africa.

### Assimilation in practice

French policy promised that Africans could become French. In the **Four Communes**, African inhabitants (the *originaires*) had citizenship rights, voted in elections and sent a deputy to the parliament in Paris; in 1914 Blaise Diagne became the first Black African elected to it. A small elite of Africans was educated in French and worked as clerks, teachers and traders.

But outside the communes, the great majority of Africans were **subjects, not citizens**. From the late 1880s they were governed under the **indigénat**, which allowed French officials to fine or imprison them without trial and to demand forced labour. Very few ever qualified for citizenship. By the early twentieth century French officials talked less of assimilation and more of "association", which accepted that most Africans would stay subjects.

### Direct rule

French West Africa was run through a chain of French officials: a governor-general, governors of each colony and *commandants de cercle* in charge of districts. Chiefs were kept but turned into French agents who collected taxes and recruited labour; the French appointed and dismissed them, and many had no traditional claim to authority.

## Britain in Nigeria

Britain annexed Lagos in 1861 and the Royal Niger Company traded along the Niger. In 1900 the British government took over, and **Frederick Lugard** became High Commissioner of the Protectorate of Northern Nigeria. In 1903 his forces captured **Kano** and **Sokoto**, the centres of the **Sokoto Caliphate**, a large Muslim state of emirates with its own system of taxation and courts.

Lugard had few officials and little money. He decided to rule through the emirs:

- **Emirs stayed in office** if they accepted British authority, supervised by British **Residents**.
- **Native Authorities** collected taxes, part of which they kept in native treasuries to pay for local services.
- **Native courts** continued to judge cases using Islamic law, except for punishments the British banned.
- Christian missionaries were restricted in the Muslim north to avoid unrest.

Lugard became Governor-General when Northern and Southern Nigeria were joined on **1 January 1914**, and he described his ideas in a book in 1922.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why indirect rule worked better in the north than in the south-east of Nigeria"}
1. **Existing structures:** the northern emirates had rulers, tax systems and courts that the British could use.
2. **Different societies:** many Igbo communities in the south-east were governed by councils of elders and village assemblies, with no single ruler.
3. **Invented chiefs:** the British appointed "warrant chiefs" there who had no traditional authority and were often seen as corrupt.
4. **Result:** indirect rule was accepted in much of the north but caused anger in the south-east, which later broke out in protests such as the Women's War of 1929.
:::

## Comparing the impacts on Africans

| | French rule in Senegal | British rule in Nigeria |
|---|---|---|
| African rulers | Turned into appointed agents of French officials | Emirs kept power under British supervision |
| Rights | Citizenship for the Four Communes; most others subjects under the indigénat | Very few political rights; rule through Native Authorities |
| Education | French-language schools for a small elite | Missions and schools in the south; little Western education in the north |
| Economy | Groundnuts for export; taxes and forced labour | Groundnuts, cocoa and palm oil for export; taxes; railways |

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: were the two systems really different?"}
**Different:** France aimed, at least in theory, to make Africans French and governed through its own officials; Britain preserved African rulers and institutions, especially in the north.
**Similar:** both depended on African chiefs and soldiers; both collected taxes and demanded labour; neither gave most Africans any say in government; and both organised the economy around exports to Europe.
**Judgement:** the systems differed in theory and in their effects on African elites, but for most Africans the experience of taxation, labour demands and rule by outsiders was similar.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all Senegalese became French citizens"}
Only the inhabitants of the Four Communes had citizenship rights. Most Africans in French territories were subjects under the indigénat.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing indirect rule as freedom"}
Under indirect rule the British had the final say: emirs who resisted were removed, and taxes and laws served colonial aims.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Lugard ruled the whole of Nigeria from 1900"}
In 1900 Lugard governed Northern Nigeria. Nigeria as a single colony dates from 1914.
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Faidherbe expanded French Senegal inland from 1854, created the Tirailleurs Sénégalais and encouraged groundnut exports.
- French assimilation gave citizenship to the Four Communes, but most Africans were subjects under the indigénat and direct rule.
- Lugard conquered the Sokoto Caliphate (1903) and ruled Northern Nigeria indirectly through emirs, Native Authorities and native courts.
- Indirect rule suited the north but failed where there were no kings, as with the warrant chiefs in the south-east.
- Impacts varied by place and class, but both systems imposed taxes, labour demands and rule without consent.
:::
