## Introduction

In 1870 European states controlled only small coastal areas of Africa. By 1914 almost the whole continent had been divided among seven European powers; only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. In the same period Britain, France, the Netherlands, Russia, Germany, Japan and the United States extended their rule across Asia and the Pacific. This was the age of the "new imperialism".

For Zimbabwe this is not distant history. Cecil Rhodes and the British South Africa Company occupied the country from 1890 because they hoped to find gold, wanted land for settlers, and aimed to extend British power northwards from the Cape. Understanding why empires expanded helps explain why Zimbabwe was colonised and what colonial rule was designed to do.

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 5: *Why, and with what effects, did nations gain and expand their overseas empires in the nineteenth century?* You should know where the main European powers and the United States are on a map.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Imperialism"}
The policy of a state extending its power over other territories and peoples, by conquest, settlement, economic control or political influence.
:::

:::definition{term="Colony and protectorate"}
A colony is a territory ruled directly by another country. A protectorate is a territory whose local rulers keep some power but whose foreign affairs and defence are controlled by an outside power.
:::

:::definition{term="Social Darwinism"}
A misuse of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution which claimed that some races and nations were "fitter" than others and had a right to dominate them. Modern science rejects it completely.
:::

:::definition{term="Civilising mission"}
The claim made by imperial powers that they were bringing "civilisation" to peoples they conquered. In French it was called the *mission civilisatrice*. It was used to justify conquest and ignored the achievements of the societies being conquered.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Examples to learn"}
- **1869:** Suez Canal opens; Britain occupies Egypt in 1882 to protect the route to India.
- **1884-85:** Berlin Conference sets rules for European claims in Africa.
- **1885:** the Congo Free State is recognised as the personal possession of Leopold II of Belgium.
- **1898:** the United States annexes Hawaii and wins Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines from Spain.
:::

## Economic motives

The industrial revolution in Europe and the United States created new needs:

- **Raw materials:** factories needed cotton, rubber, palm oil, copper, tin and other materials that grew or were mined in the tropics. Palm oil from West Africa was used to make soap and to lubricate machines; rubber was needed for tyres and electrical cables from the 1890s.
- **Markets:** manufacturers wanted new customers, especially when other industrial countries raised tariffs against their goods during the economic depression that began in the 1870s.
- **Investment:** banks and investors looked for profitable places to lend money, such as railways and mines.

At the time, some critics argued that imperialism served the interests of a small group of investors. The British writer J. A. Hobson made this argument in 1902, and Lenin, the Russian revolutionary, later argued that imperialism was a stage of capitalism. Other historians point out that many colonies cost more to rule than they produced in profit, which suggests economics was not the only motive.

## Strategic and political motives

- **Strategic bases and routes:** naval powers needed ports and coaling stations for their steamships. Britain's empire was organised around the sea route to **India**; after the **Suez Canal** opened in 1869, Britain bought shares in it (1875) and occupied Egypt (1882).
- **Rivalry and prestige:** after its defeat by Prussia in 1871, France looked to empire to restore national pride. Newly united Germany and Italy wanted colonies to show they were great powers. Once one power claimed a territory, its rivals hurried to claim others before they were taken: this is one explanation of the speed of the "Scramble for Africa".
- **Nationalism and public opinion:** newspapers and popular books celebrated explorers and soldiers, and governments found that empire was popular with voters.

## Religious and cultural motives

**Missionaries** from Europe and the United States wanted to spread Christianity, and many also campaigned against the slave trade in Africa. David Livingstone, the Scottish missionary and explorer, argued that Christianity and legitimate trade together would end the East African slave trade. Missionaries built schools and hospitals, but they often also called for their governments to take control, and many were contemptuous of African and Asian beliefs and customs.

Ideas of **racial superiority** were widespread among Europeans and white Americans. Writers and politicians claimed that white people had a duty to rule others. The British poet Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (1899), addressed to the United States as it took the Philippines, expressed this view. These ideas were false: they ignored the history and achievements of societies such as Great Zimbabwe, the Asante kingdom or Mughal India, and they were used to justify land seizure, forced labour and violence.

## What made expansion possible

| Development | Effect |
|---|---|
| Quinine (widely used from the mid-nineteenth century) | Reduced deaths from malaria among Europeans in tropical Africa |
| Steamships and railways | Allowed troops and goods to move quickly inland and across oceans |
| The telegraph | Linked colonies to European capitals |
| Breech-loading rifles and the Maxim machine gun (1880s) | Gave small European forces a huge advantage in firepower |

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1869 | Suez Canal opens |
| 1882 | Britain occupies Egypt |
| 1884-1885 | Berlin Conference on Africa |
| 1890 | Pioneer Column occupies Mashonaland for the British South Africa Company |
| 1898 | USA annexes Hawaii and defeats Spain |
| 1902 | Hobson publishes his economic criticism of imperialism |

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why Britain occupied Egypt in 1882"}
1. **A vital route:** the Suez Canal (1869) became the quickest route from Britain to India.
2. **A financial stake:** Britain bought the Egyptian ruler's canal shares in 1875, and British and French investors were owed large sums by the Egyptian government.
3. **A crisis:** a nationalist movement led by Colonel Urabi challenged foreign control, and riots broke out in Alexandria in 1882.
4. **Result:** Britain bombarded Alexandria and defeated Urabi's army, beginning an occupation that lasted for decades. Strategic and economic motives worked together.
:::

## The United States

The United States also expanded overseas at the end of the century. Its motives included **markets** for its booming industry, especially in Asia; **naval bases**, as argued by the naval officer Alfred Thayer Mahan in a widely read book of 1890 on sea power; a belief that it was destined to spread its institutions (sometimes called **manifest destiny**); and the same ideas of racial superiority found in Europe. It annexed Hawaii in 1898 and took colonies from Spain in the same year (taught in a separate lesson).

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: which motive mattered most?"}
**Economic:** raw materials, markets and investment explain why businessmen such as Rhodes pushed for expansion, and why the Congo's rubber was exploited so ruthlessly.
**Political and strategic:** many colonies were taken to protect routes (Egypt) or to block rivals, not for profit; some were never profitable.
**Cultural:** missionary pressure and racist ideas made conquest seem acceptable to European voters.
**Judgement:** the motives were mixed and varied from place to place. A strong answer explains which motive mattered most in a particular case, rather than giving one answer for every empire.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating 'civilising mission' as a fact"}
Present the civilising mission and racial ideas as claims made by imperialists to justify conquest, not as true descriptions of colonised peoples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing motives with methods"}
The Maxim gun and quinine explain how imperialism was possible, not why powers wanted colonies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one motive"}
Questions on motives reward a range: economic, strategic, political, religious and cultural, with examples for each.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain the motives behind European imperialism'"}
Explain two or three motives, each with a specific example: rubber in the Congo, the Suez route and Egypt, French prestige after 1871, missionaries in Africa.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (overseas empires prescribed for June 2027)"}
Sources from this period often contain the racist language of their time. Treat that language as evidence of the writer's attitude and purpose, not as fact, and use your knowledge of the colonised societies to challenge it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Between 1870 and 1914 European powers, and later the United States, rapidly expanded their empires in Africa, Asia and the Pacific.
- Economic motives included raw materials, markets and investment.
- Strategic and political motives included sea routes (Suez and Egypt), rivalry between the powers and national prestige.
- Missionary activity and ideas of racial superiority, now rejected, were used to justify conquest.
- Quinine, steamships, railways, the telegraph and the Maxim gun made rapid conquest possible.
:::
