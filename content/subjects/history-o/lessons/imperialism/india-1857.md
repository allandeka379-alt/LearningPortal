## Introduction

In May 1857 Indian soldiers at Meerut, near Delhi, rose against their British officers, marched to Delhi and proclaimed the elderly Mughal emperor as their leader. Within weeks much of northern and central India was in revolt. The rising was crushed by 1858, but it ended the rule of the East India Company and changed the way Britain governed India for the next ninety years.

The name of these events is itself a matter of debate. British writers of the time called them the **Indian Mutiny**, stressing the soldiers' revolt. Many Indian historians call them the **First War of Independence**, stressing popular resistance to foreign rule. Others use neutral terms such as the **Rebellion** or **Rising of 1857**. In Zimbabwe, the risings of 1896-97 have similarly been described in different ways by settlers and by nationalists; how events are named reflects how they are interpreted.

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 5: why Indians resisted British rule and with what effects. You should know the motives for imperialism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="East India Company"}
A British trading company, chartered in 1600, which by the mid-nineteenth century ruled much of India with its own armies on behalf of Britain.
:::

:::definition{term="Sepoy"}
An Indian soldier serving in the armies of the East India Company. In 1857 sepoys greatly outnumbered British soldiers in India.
:::

:::definition{term="Doctrine of Lapse"}
The policy under which the Company annexed an Indian princely state if its ruler died without a natural male heir, refusing to recognise adopted sons.
:::

:::definition{term="The Raj"}
British Crown rule over India from 1858 to 1947, headed by a Viceroy.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and people to learn"}
- **1757:** the Company's victory at Plassey begins its rise to power in Bengal.
- **1848-1856:** Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General, annexes several states.
- **10 May 1857:** sepoys rise at Meerut and march to Delhi.
- **Bahadur Shah II:** the last Mughal emperor, proclaimed leader of the rising.
- **Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi:** a rebel leader, killed in battle in June 1858.
- **1858:** the Government of India Act transfers India from the Company to the Crown.
:::

## The East India Company in India

The Company came to India to trade in textiles, spices and tea. As the Mughal Empire weakened in the eighteenth century, it used its armies of sepoys to take territory, beginning with victory at **Plassey** in Bengal in 1757. By the 1850s it ruled much of India directly and controlled the remaining **princely states** through treaties. It collected land revenue, ran courts and raised armies, while the British government supervised it from London.

## Causes of the rising

The causes were political, economic, religious and military, and they affected different groups in different ways.

**Political grievances**
- Under the **Doctrine of Lapse**, Governor-General Dalhousie annexed states such as Satara, **Jhansi** and Nagpur when rulers died without natural heirs.
- In 1856 the Company annexed **Awadh** (Oudh), claiming misgovernment. Many sepoys of the Bengal army came from Awadh, and the annexation offended them and their families.
- The Mughal emperor in Delhi had been told that the title would end with his death.

**Economic grievances**
- Heavy land revenue demands and new land settlements dispossessed some landlords and burdened peasants.
- Imports of British factory-made cloth harmed Indian hand-weavers.

**Religious and social fears**
- Christian missionaries were active, and many Indians feared that the British intended to convert them.
- Laws such as the ban on *sati* (1829) and the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856), seen by the British as reforms, were seen by some Indians as interference in religion and custom.

**Military grievances**
- Sepoys resented low pay and limited promotion compared with British soldiers.
- A law of 1856 required new recruits to serve overseas if ordered, which for many high-caste Hindus meant a loss of caste.
- The trigger was the new **Enfield rifle**. Its cartridges had to be bitten open, and a rumour spread that they were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindus and Muslims alike.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the cartridges triggered the rising"}
1. **Underlying anger:** sepoys were already resentful over pay, overseas service and the annexation of Awadh.
2. **The rumour:** the cartridges for the new rifle were said to be greased with cow and pig fat, which would break the religious rules of both Hindus and Muslims.
3. **Punishment:** at Meerut in May 1857, sepoys who refused the cartridges were publicly humiliated and sentenced to long prison terms.
4. **Result:** their comrades rose on 10 May, freed them and marched to Delhi. The cartridges were the trigger, not the only cause.
:::

## Course and suppression

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 29 March 1857 | Sepoy Mangal Pandey attacks officers at Barrackpore; he is later hanged |
| 10 May 1857 | Rising at Meerut; rebels march to Delhi |
| May-June 1857 | Bahadur Shah II proclaimed leader; risings spread across northern and central India |
| June-July 1857 | Siege and massacres at Kanpur (Cawnpore); siege of the Lucknow Residency begins |
| September 1857 | British recapture Delhi |
| March 1858 | British recapture Lucknow |
| June 1858 | Rani of Jhansi killed in battle near Gwalior |
| November 1858 | Queen Victoria's proclamation announces Crown rule |

The rising was strongest in the north and centre: Delhi, Awadh with its capital Lucknow, Kanpur under Nana Sahib, and Jhansi under **Rani Lakshmibai**. Peasants, landlords and dispossessed princes joined the sepoys in many areas. Both sides killed civilians: rebels killed British men, women and children at Kanpur, and British forces carried out mass executions of rebels and of many Indians who were not involved, burning villages as they advanced.

The rising failed to spread across all of India. The armies of Bombay and Madras stayed largely loyal, and the Punjab, recently conquered, provided Sikh soldiers for the British, as did Gurkhas from Nepal. Many princes stayed loyal. The rebels had no single leader or plan, and the British could bring in troops by sea. Delhi was retaken in September 1857, Bahadur Shah was exiled to Burma, and resistance was crushed by the end of 1858, although fighting continued in places into 1859.

## Results: the British Raj

- The **Government of India Act 1858** abolished the Company's rule. India was governed by the **Crown** through a Secretary of State for India in London and a **Viceroy** in India.
- **Queen Victoria's proclamation** of November 1858 promised religious toleration, an end to annexations and respect for the rights of the princes. The Doctrine of Lapse was abandoned.
- The **army** was reorganised: the proportion of British to Indian soldiers increased, artillery was kept in British hands, and recruitment shifted towards groups that had stayed loyal, such as Sikhs and Gurkhas.
- The **Mughal Empire** ended. In 1877 Victoria took the title Empress of India.
- Relations between British and Indians became more distant and mistrustful, and racial attitudes hardened.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: mutiny or war of independence?"}
**Mutiny:** it began among soldiers over military grievances, was concentrated in the Bengal army's recruiting areas, and many regions and princes did not join.
**War of independence:** civilians, peasants and princes took part; rebels proclaimed the Mughal emperor, a symbol of Indian rule; and later nationalists saw it as the first great challenge to British power.
**Judgement:** it was more than a mutiny, because of its wide civilian support in the north, but it was not a united national movement. Many historians therefore prefer neutral terms such as "rebellion" or "rising".
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the cartridges as the only cause"}
The greased cartridges were the trigger. Explain the political, economic, religious and military grievances behind them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all of India rose"}
The rising was concentrated in the north and centre. The Bombay and Madras armies, the Punjab and many princes stayed loyal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Company with the British government"}
Until 1858 India was ruled by the East India Company, supervised from London. After 1858 the Crown ruled directly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- By the 1850s the East India Company ruled much of India through armies of sepoys.
- The Doctrine of Lapse, the annexation of Awadh, land revenue, religious fears and military grievances caused widespread resentment.
- The rumour about greased cartridges triggered the rising at Meerut on 10 May 1857; it spread across northern and central India.
- The British, helped by loyal troops and the rebels' divisions, recaptured Delhi (September 1857) and crushed the rising by 1858.
- The Government of India Act 1858 replaced Company rule with the Raj; the army was reorganised and Victoria's proclamation promised toleration.
:::
