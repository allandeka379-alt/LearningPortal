## Introduction

The Great Powers that went to war in 1914 had spent the previous thirty years dividing Africa between them. The scramble for colonies brought them into crisis after crisis, and some of those crises, such as the one over Morocco, pushed Britain and France closer together against Germany. Southern Africa was part of this story: when the Jameson Raid against the Transvaal failed in January 1896, Kaiser Wilhelm II sent President Kruger a telegram of congratulation, which caused fury in Britain.

Yet the spark for the war came not from Africa but from the **Balkans**, the mountainous peninsula of south-eastern Europe where the Ottoman Empire was retreating and new nations were fighting for land. This lesson explains how far colonial problems created tension between the powers, and why Balkan problems were so hard to solve.

You should already know about the two alliance groups (the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Colonial rivalry"}
Competition between European powers for colonies, trade and influence overseas, especially in Africa and Asia.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
The belief that people who share a language, culture and history should have their own independent state. In the Balkans it drove Serbs, Bulgarians and Greeks to seek more land.
:::

:::definition{term="Pan-Slavism"}
The idea that all Slav peoples (such as Russians, Serbs and Bulgarians) belonged together. Russia used it to present itself as the protector of the Balkan Slavs.
:::

:::definition{term="Annexation"}
Taking over another territory and making it formally part of your own state.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1898:** Fashoda crisis (Britain and France on the Nile).
- **1905-06:** First Moroccan crisis; Algeciras Conference (1906).
- **1908-09:** Bosnian crisis: Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia-Herzegovina.
- **1911:** Second Moroccan (Agadir) crisis.
- **1912-13:** the two Balkan Wars.
:::

## Colonial rivalries

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1884-85 | Berlin Conference sets rules for claiming African territory |
| 1896 | Kaiser's telegram to Kruger after the Jameson Raid angers Britain |
| 1898 | Fashoda crisis: France backs down before Britain on the upper Nile |
| 1904 | Entente Cordiale: Britain and France settle Egypt and Morocco |
| 1905-06 | First Moroccan crisis; Algeciras Conference |
| 1911 | Second Moroccan (Agadir) crisis |

### Fashoda, 1898

In July 1898 a small French force reached Fashoda on the upper Nile, in Sudan. In September a much larger British army under Kitchener arrived, fresh from defeating the Mahdist state at Omdurman. For a few weeks war between Britain and France seemed possible, but France backed down. The crisis showed that colonial quarrels could bring the powers close to war, but also that they could be settled: six years later the two countries signed the **Entente Cordiale**.

### The Moroccan crises

Germany came late to the scramble and wanted, in a phrase often linked with the German statesman Bülow, a "place in the sun". It also wanted to test the new friendship between Britain and France.

- **First Moroccan crisis (1905-06):** Wilhelm II visited Tangier in March 1905 and declared support for Moroccan independence, challenging French influence. At the **Algeciras Conference** (1906) only Austria-Hungary backed Germany. France kept its influence, and Britain and France began secret military talks. Germany had pushed its rivals closer together.
- **Second Moroccan crisis (1911):** when French troops entered the Moroccan city of Fez, Germany sent the gunboat *Panther* to the port of **Agadir**. Britain warned publicly that it would stand by France. Germany accepted part of the French Congo in exchange for recognising French control of Morocco. In 1912 Britain and France agreed that the French navy would concentrate in the Mediterranean and the British navy in the North Sea.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: how far did colonial problems create tension?"}
**They did create tension:** Fashoda and the two Moroccan crises brought the powers close to war. Germany's resentment at being blocked from a "place in the sun" grew, and each Moroccan crisis tightened the links between Britain and France.

**But they were usually settled:** every colonial crisis before 1914 ended in negotiation, not war. Britain and Germany even agreed over the Baghdad railway and Portugal's colonies in 1913-14, and relations between them were improving just before the war.

**Judgement:** colonial rivalry poisoned relations and shaped the alliances, but it did not start the war. The crisis of 1914 was about the Balkans, not colonies.
:::

## The Balkans: "the powder keg of Europe"

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1878 | Congress of Berlin: Serbia independent; Austria-Hungary occupies Bosnia-Herzegovina |
| 1908 | Young Turk revolution in the Ottoman Empire; Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia (October) |
| 1909 | Russia, under German pressure, accepts the annexation (March) |
| 1912 | First Balkan War: the Balkan League defeats the Ottomans |
| 1913 | Second Balkan War: Bulgaria defeated by its former allies |
| 28 June 1914 | Archduke Franz Ferdinand assassinated in Sarajevo |

### Why the region was unstable

1. **The decline of the Ottoman Empire.** The "sick man of Europe" had been losing Balkan land for a century. As it withdrew, the new states and the Great Powers competed to fill the gap.
2. **Serbian and Slav nationalism.** Serbia wanted to unite the South Slavs, including the Serbs living inside Austria-Hungary and in Bosnia.
3. **Austria-Hungary's fears.** Austria-Hungary ruled many nationalities. Its leaders believed that a strong Serbia would encourage the empire's Slavs to break away, so they saw Serbia as a threat to the empire's survival.
4. **Russia's interests.** Russia presented itself as protector of the Slavs and wanted influence over the Straits (the Bosphorus and Dardanelles), through which its grain exports passed.
5. **Germany's support for Austria-Hungary,** its only reliable ally.

### The Bosnian crisis, 1908-09

In October 1908 Austria-Hungary **annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina**, which it had occupied since 1878. Serbia was furious, because many Bosnians were Serbs. Russia protested, but in March 1909, faced with firm German support for Austria-Hungary, it backed down. Russia had been rebuilding its army after defeat by Japan (1904-05) and was not ready to fight. Russia's leaders felt humiliated and determined not to retreat again, a feeling that mattered in July 1914.

### The Balkan Wars, 1912-13

In the **First Balkan War** (1912-13), Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro (the Balkan League) drove the Ottomans out of almost all their European land. The allies then quarrelled over the spoils. In the **Second Balkan War** (1913) Bulgaria attacked Serbia and Greece and was defeated by them, together with Romania and the Ottomans.

Serbia almost doubled in size and became more confident. Austria-Hungary insisted on the creation of a new state, **Albania**, partly to block Serbia's access to the sea. A conference of the Great Powers in London kept the peace, but Austria-Hungary's leaders concluded that Serbia had to be dealt with, and Bulgaria and Turkey were left resentful.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why were Balkan problems so hard to solve?"}
1. **Too many claims on the same land:** Serbs, Bulgarians, Greeks and Albanians all claimed Macedonia and the former Ottoman lands.
2. **Great Powers behind the small states:** Russia backed Serbia, Germany backed Austria-Hungary, so a small local war could involve the great alliances.
3. **Survival was at stake:** Austria-Hungary believed Serbian nationalism threatened its existence, so compromise looked like weakness.
4. **Pride after humiliation:** Russia had backed down in 1909 and would not easily do so again.
5. **Result:** each crisis left the next one more dangerous, until the assassination of 1914 brought all these tensions together.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two Moroccan crises"}
Tangier and the Algeciras Conference belong to 1905-06; the gunboat at Agadir belongs to 1911.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking Bosnia was a new conquest in 1908"}
Austria-Hungary had occupied and run Bosnia since 1878. In 1908 it formally annexed it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying colonies caused the war directly"}
Colonial crises raised tension and shaped the alliances, but all of them were settled peacefully. The war began with a Balkan crisis.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Colonial crises (Fashoda 1898, Morocco 1905-06 and 1911) brought the powers close to war but were all settled peacefully.
- Germany's challenges in Morocco backfired by drawing Britain and France closer.
- The decline of the Ottoman Empire left a power vacuum in the Balkans, where Serbian nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary.
- In the Bosnian crisis (1908-09) Russia was humiliated; in the Balkan Wars (1912-13) Serbia doubled in size.
- Balkan problems were hard to solve because local quarrels were linked to the interests and pride of the Great Powers.
:::
