## Introduction

On 28 June 1914 two shots fired in Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, killed the heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary and his wife. Five weeks later Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia, France and Britain were at war, and within months the fighting had spread to Africa, Asia and the oceans. In southern Africa, South African forces were soon invading German South West Africa, and men from Southern Rhodesia volunteered to fight.

How did a political murder in the Balkans become a world war? This lesson follows the **July Crisis** day by day and then looks at the long debate about which country, if any, was most to blame.

You should already know about the two alliance groups, the arms race and the Balkan crises of 1908-13.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ultimatum"}
A final set of demands that must be accepted within a fixed time, with the threat of serious action (usually war) if they are refused.
:::

:::definition{term="The blank cheque"}
Germany's promise, given on 5-6 July 1914, to support Austria-Hungary in whatever action it took against Serbia, even if that meant war with Russia.
:::

:::definition{term="Neutrality"}
Not taking sides in a war. Belgium's neutrality had been guaranteed by the Great Powers, including Britain and Prussia, in the Treaty of London of 1839.
:::

:::definition{term="War guilt"}
Responsibility for causing a war. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) placed responsibility on Germany and its allies.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **28 June 1914:** assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo.
- **23 July:** Austro-Hungarian ultimatum to Serbia.
- **28 July:** Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia.
- **1 August:** Germany declares war on Russia; **3 August:** on France.
- **4 August:** Germany invades Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany.
:::

## From Sarajevo to world war

| Date (1914) | Event |
|---|---|
| 28 June | Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie are shot in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip |
| 5-6 July | Germany gives Austria-Hungary the "blank cheque" |
| 23 July | Austria-Hungary sends Serbia an ultimatum with a 48-hour deadline |
| 25 July | Serbia accepts most demands but not all; Serbia mobilises |
| 28 July | Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia |
| 30 July | Russia orders general mobilisation |
| 1 August | Germany declares war on Russia |
| 3 August | Germany declares war on France |
| 4 August | German troops enter Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany |

### The assassination

Archduke **Franz Ferdinand** visited Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. A group of young Bosnian Serbs, armed with the help of a secret Serbian nationalist society known as the **Black Hand**, planned to kill him. After a bomb attack failed that morning, the Archduke's car later took a wrong turn, and **Gavrilo Princip** shot the Archduke and his wife.

Austria-Hungary's leaders saw the murder as a chance to crush Serbia, which they blamed for encouraging Slav nationalism. There is no evidence that the Serbian government ordered the killing, but members of Serbian military intelligence were linked to the Black Hand.

### The blank cheque and the ultimatum

Austria-Hungary would not act without knowing Germany's position, because Russia might defend Serbia. On 5-6 July Wilhelm II and his Chancellor, **Bethmann Hollweg**, promised Austria-Hungary full support: the **blank cheque**. German leaders hoped for a quick local war, but they accepted the risk that Russia would intervene.

Austria-Hungary took more than two weeks to prepare its demands. On 23 July it sent Serbia an **ultimatum** that it expected to be rejected. It demanded, among other things, the suppression of anti-Austrian propaganda and groups, and that Austro-Hungarian officials take part in the investigation inside Serbia. Serbia's reply on 25 July accepted most points but refused to allow Austro-Hungarian officials to take part in the inquiry on Serbian soil. Austria-Hungary broke off relations and declared war on 28 July.

### Mobilisation and the Schlieffen Plan

Russia would not see Serbia crushed, and after its humiliation in 1909 it was determined not to back down. On 30 July it ordered **general mobilisation** against both Austria-Hungary and Germany. France, Russia's ally, began its own preparations.

Germany's only war plan, the **Schlieffen Plan**, assumed that war with Russia meant war with France, and that France must be defeated quickly before Russia's huge army was ready. So when Russia refused to stop mobilising, Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August) and demanded that Belgium let German troops pass through. Belgium refused.

### Why Britain went to war

Britain had no binding alliance with France or Russia, and its Cabinet was divided. The German invasion of **Belgium** on 4 August settled the matter. Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in 1839, and it could not accept German control of the Channel coast opposite Britain. Britain also feared that a German victory over France and Russia would leave Germany dominant in Europe. Its ultimatum to Germany expired at 11 p.m. London time on 4 August, and Britain, with its whole empire, including Southern Rhodesia, was at war.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how did the assassination lead to a general war?"}
1. **Austria-Hungary chose war with Serbia,** using the murder to crush a dangerous neighbour.
2. **Germany backed it** with the blank cheque, accepting the risk of Russian involvement.
3. **Russia mobilised** to protect Serbia and its own prestige.
4. **Germany's war plan** turned war with Russia into war with France as well, through Belgium.
5. **Britain entered** to defend Belgium and the balance of power.
Each step was a decision by political leaders, made under the pressure of alliances, plans and pride.
:::

## Who was responsible? A historians' debate

The question has been argued about for more than a century, and historians still disagree.

- **The Versailles view (1919):** Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles placed responsibility on Germany and its allies. Germans resented this deeply, and in the 1920s and 1930s many writers argued that all the powers shared the blame.
- **The Fischer view (1960s):** the German historian **Fritz Fischer** argued from German government records that Germany's leaders had aims of expansion and deliberately risked, or even sought, a major war in 1914. His work caused a fierce debate in Germany.
- **Shared responsibility:** other historians stress the decisions of Austria-Hungary (determined to crush Serbia), Russia (early general mobilisation), Serbia (tolerating nationalist groups) and France (support for Russia), and Britain's unclear position. Christopher Clark's book *The Sleepwalkers* (2012) argued that the leaders of all the powers, unable to see the full danger, stumbled into war together.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was Germany mainly responsible?"}
**For:** Germany's blank cheque made Austria-Hungary's war possible; its war plan turned a Balkan war into a European one; it declared war on Russia and France and invaded neutral Belgium.

**Against:** Austria-Hungary chose to attack Serbia; Russia mobilised fully before Germany did; Serbia had allowed the Black Hand to operate; the alliances and the arms race involved every power.

**How to judge:** decide which decisions made a general war most likely, and explain why. A balanced answer can still reach a firm conclusion.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the date of the murder with the start of the war"}
The assassination was on 28 June 1914; Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia exactly one month later, on 28 July.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Serbia rejected the whole ultimatum"}
Serbia accepted most of the demands. It refused the point that would have let Austro-Hungarian officials investigate on Serbian soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the debate as settled"}
Do not write that one country "caused the war" as a fact. Present the views and support your own judgement with evidence.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain how the assassination led to war"}
For Cambridge Paper 1 part (b) or ZIMSEC Paper 2 part (b), show the chain of decisions in order, linking each step to the next: blank cheque, ultimatum, Russian mobilisation, Schlieffen Plan, Belgium.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How far was Germany to blame?"}
In a part (c) answer, weigh Germany against Austria-Hungary, Russia and Serbia, and against long-term causes. Mention a historians' view accurately if you can, but your argument matters more than names.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2"}
The causes of the First World War are a prescribed Paper 2 topic in November 2028. Expect cartoons and statements made in July 1914; check the date carefully, because what a government said on 25 July may differ from what it said on 5 August.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 by Gavrilo Princip, helped by the Black Hand.
- Germany's blank cheque (5-6 July) encouraged Austria-Hungary to send Serbia an ultimatum (23 July) and declare war (28 July).
- Russian general mobilisation (30 July) led Germany, following the Schlieffen Plan, to declare war on Russia and France.
- The German invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the war on 4 August 1914.
- Responsibility is debated: from Germany's guilt (Versailles, Fischer) to shared responsibility (Clark).
:::
