## Introduction

In the summer of 1914 a quarrel between Austria-Hungary and Serbia, two countries that most people in Britain or southern Africa could not have found on a map, turned within a week into a war between all the Great Powers of Europe. Soldiers from Southern Rhodesia, South Africa, India and Canada were soon fighting in it. To understand how a local crisis grew so fast, you need to know how Europe had divided into two armed groups in the twenty-five years before 1914.

This lesson covers the **alliance system**, European diplomacy (including the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902), the **arms race** on land and at sea, and military planning. It answers a question both boards ask: did the alliances make war more likely or less likely?

You should already know that Germany was united in 1871 after defeating France, and that France lost Alsace-Lorraine to the new German Empire.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alliance"}
A formal agreement between states to support each other, usually with military help if one of them is attacked.
:::

:::definition{term="Entente"}
A friendly understanding between states that settles disputes between them. Unlike an alliance, an entente did not bind the partners to fight for each other.
:::

:::definition{term="Arms race"}
Competition between states to build bigger armies, navies and stocks of weapons than their rivals.
:::

:::definition{term="Weltpolitik"}
"World policy": the policy of Kaiser Wilhelm II's Germany from the late 1890s of seeking colonies, influence and a large navy so that Germany would be a world power, not just a European one.
:::

:::definition{term="Mobilisation"}
Calling up reserve soldiers and moving armies, usually by railway, into position for war.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1882:** Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy.
- **1894:** Franco-Russian Alliance.
- **1902:** Anglo-Japanese Alliance.
- **1904:** Entente Cordiale between Britain and France.
- **1906:** HMS *Dreadnought* launched.
- **1907:** Anglo-Russian Entente completes the Triple Entente.
:::

### Bismarck's system and its collapse

After 1871 the German Chancellor, **Otto von Bismarck**, wanted to keep France isolated so that it could never take revenge for its defeat. He made the **Dual Alliance** with Austria-Hungary (1879), which Italy joined in 1882 to form the **Triple Alliance**, and he kept Russia friendly with a secret Reinsurance Treaty (1887).

In 1890 the young Kaiser **Wilhelm II** dismissed Bismarck and let the treaty with Russia lapse. Russia, now isolated, turned to France. French loans helped build Russian railways and industry, and the **Franco-Russian Alliance** came into force in 1894. Germany now faced possible enemies on two sides.

### Britain ends its isolation

For most of the nineteenth century Britain avoided permanent alliances and relied on its navy. By 1900 this looked risky: Britain had quarrelled with France over Africa, with Russia over Asia, and its army had struggled in the South African War (1899-1902), while German public opinion openly sided with the Boers.

- **The Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)** was Britain's first peacetime alliance in decades. Each partner would stay neutral if the other fought one enemy in East Asia, and would help if the other faced two. It protected British interests in China and the Pacific against Russia and let Britain keep more of its fleet in home waters. In 1914 Japan used the alliance to enter the war against Germany.
- **The Entente Cordiale (1904)** settled old colonial disputes with France: France accepted British control of Egypt, Britain accepted French influence in Morocco. It was not a military alliance.
- **The Anglo-Russian Entente (1907)** divided Persia into zones of influence and settled disputes over Afghanistan and Tibet.

These three agreements left Europe with two groups: the **Triple Alliance** and the **Triple Entente** of Britain, France and Russia.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did Britain move closer to France?"}
1. **Fear of Germany's navy:** from 1898 Germany's Navy Laws built a battle fleet that could only be aimed at Britain, the greatest naval power.
2. **Isolation felt dangerous:** the South African War showed how unpopular Britain was in Europe and how stretched its forces were.
3. **Old quarrels could be settled:** France and Britain agreed over Egypt and Morocco in 1904.
4. **German pressure backfired:** when Germany challenged France in Morocco in 1905-06, Britain stood by France and the two countries' generals began secret talks.
5. **Result:** an entente that was not an alliance, but which made it likely, though never certain, that Britain would side with France in a war against Germany.
:::

## The arms race

### At sea: the Dreadnought race

Wilhelm II and his naval minister, Admiral **Alfred von Tirpitz**, wanted a fleet that would make Britain respect Germany. Britain, an island that depended on imported food and on its empire, saw any rival fleet in the North Sea as a direct threat.

In 1906 Britain launched **HMS *Dreadnought***, a battleship with big guns and turbine engines that made every older battleship out of date. This was a gamble: it also meant Germany could start the race almost level. Both countries built "dreadnoughts" as fast as they could. In 1909 a press and public campaign demanded more ships, and the government eventually agreed to build eight in one programme. A British attempt in 1912 to agree limits with Germany failed. By 1914 Britain still had more dreadnoughts than Germany, but the race had left deep mistrust.

### On land: armies and war plans

On the continent the army mattered more than the navy. All the Great Powers except Britain used **conscription** and kept millions of trained reservists. In 1913 Germany enlarged its army, France extended military service to three years and Russia began a large rearmament programme due to be complete around 1917.

Every general staff had detailed plans that depended on **railway timetables**. Germany's **Schlieffen Plan** assumed a war on two fronts: Germany would defeat France quickly by marching through Belgium, then turn east against Russia, which was expected to mobilise slowly. This meant that if Russia mobilised, Germany believed it had to attack France at once. Plans like this made it very hard for politicians to wait or negotiate once a crisis began.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: did the alliances make war more or less likely?"}
**More likely:** the alliances divided Europe into two armed camps that mistrusted each other. Germany felt "encircled" by France and Russia. Treaty obligations and war plans meant that a quarrel between two powers could drag in all of them, which is what happened in 1914.

**Less likely:** the alliances were defensive and could deter attack, and for over twenty years they helped keep peace between the Great Powers. They did not force anyone to fight: Italy stayed neutral in 1914 and then joined the other side in 1915, and Britain's ententes did not oblige it to go to war. Germany's promise of support to Austria-Hungary in July 1914 was a choice, not a treaty duty.

**Judgement:** many historians conclude that the alliances did not cause the war by themselves, but they made a local war much more likely to become a general one, especially when combined with mobilisation plans.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the Triple Entente an alliance"}
Britain's agreements with France (1904) and Russia (1907) were ententes, not military alliances. Britain's decision to fight in 1914 was triggered by the German invasion of Belgium.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting Italy on the wrong side"}
Italy was a member of the Triple Alliance, but it stayed neutral in 1914 and joined the Allies in 1915.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the 1902 and 1904 agreements"}
1902 was the alliance with Japan; 1904 was the Entente Cordiale with France.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing instead of explaining"}
Naming the alliances earns little. Explain how each one changed what a country was likely to do in a crisis.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe questions"}
For "Describe the alliance system" (ZIMSEC Paper 2 or Cambridge Paper 1 part (a)), give each group, its members and a date. Four precise points are better than a long story.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain and how far questions"}
For "Explain why the naval race increased tension", link the cause to a result: Germany's fleet threatened Britain's security, so Britain moved closer to France. For "How far did the alliances cause the war?" (Cambridge Paper 1 part (c), or a ZIMSEC (c) part), weigh the alliances against other causes such as the Balkans, colonial rivalry, militarism and the July Crisis, and finish with a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Source questions"}
Cambridge Paper 2 sets the causes of the First World War as a prescribed topic in November 2028. Expect cartoons and newspaper extracts about the naval race: ask who made the source, for whom, and what they wanted readers to think.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bismarck's alliances isolated France; after 1890 Russia allied with France (1894), so Germany faced two possible enemies.
- Britain ended its isolation with Japan (1902), France (1904) and Russia (1907), creating the Triple Entente.
- Germany's Weltpolitik and Navy Laws, and the *Dreadnought* race from 1906, turned Britain against Germany.
- Conscript armies and rigid war plans such as the Schlieffen Plan made crises hard to control.
- The alliances were defensive and did not force anyone to fight, but they helped turn a local war into a general one.
:::
