## Introduction

In November 1918 Germany lost the First World War, its emperor fled, and a democratic republic was declared. Within five years that republic had faced armed uprisings from the left and the right, the murder of leading politicians, a foreign occupation of its main industrial region and a collapse of its currency so complete that people needed wheelbarrows of banknotes to buy bread.

New democracies across the world, including many in Africa after independence, have faced similar tests: building trust in new institutions while the economy is weak and armed groups challenge the government. The Weimar Republic is a powerful case study of what can go wrong, and of how a democracy can survive a crisis.

This lesson is the start of Cambridge Depth study B, key question 1: *Was the Weimar Republic doomed from the start?* You should know how the First World War ended and the main terms of the Treaty of Versailles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weimar Republic"}
The name given to the German democratic republic of 1918-1933. Its constitution was written by a National Assembly that met in the town of Weimar in 1919, because Berlin was unsafe.
:::

:::definition{term="Proportional representation"}
A voting system in which each party receives seats in parliament in proportion to its share of the votes.
:::

:::definition{term="Article 48"}
The article of the Weimar Constitution that allowed the President, in an emergency, to rule by decree without the Reichstag's approval.
:::

:::definition{term="Putsch"}
An attempt by a small armed group to seize power by force.
:::

:::definition{term="Hyperinflation"}
Extremely rapid price rises, in which money loses almost all its value. In Germany it reached its peak in November 1923.
:::

:::definition{term="Freikorps"}
Volunteer armed units, mostly of ex-soldiers, formed in 1918-19. They were strongly nationalist and anti-communist.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- **9 November 1918:** the Kaiser abdicates; a republic is proclaimed. **Friedrich Ebert** becomes head of government and, in 1919, President.
- **January 1919:** the Spartacist rising in Berlin; elections for the National Assembly.
- **28 June 1919:** Treaty of Versailles signed. **August 1919:** Weimar Constitution in force.
- **March 1920:** the Kapp Putsch.
- **January 1923:** French and Belgian troops occupy the Ruhr. **November 1923:** hyperinflation peaks.
:::

## From empire to republic

By the autumn of 1918 Germany's army was retreating, its people were short of food, and its allies were collapsing. In late October and early November sailors at Kiel mutinied, and workers' and soldiers' councils took control of many cities. On **9 November 1918** the Kaiser was forced to abdicate and went into exile in the Netherlands, and a republic was proclaimed in Berlin. Friedrich **Ebert**, leader of the Social Democrats (SPD), became head of a temporary government, which agreed the armistice of 11 November.

Ebert wanted order and elections, not a workers' revolution like the one in Russia. On 10 November he agreed with the army leadership that the army would support the government against revolution, and in return the government would leave the army's officers in place. This kept order, but it left the army, the judges and the civil service in the hands of men who often disliked the republic.

## The Weimar Constitution

Elections in January 1919, in which women voted for the first time, produced a National Assembly in which the parties supporting the republic won over three-quarters of the vote. It met in Weimar and agreed a new constitution in 1919.

| Part of the constitution | What it said |
|---|---|
| President | Elected by the people every seven years; appointed the Chancellor; commanded the armed forces; could dissolve the Reichstag; emergency powers under **Article 48** |
| Reichstag | The parliament, elected at least every four years by **proportional representation**; all men and women over 20 could vote; made laws and controlled the budget |
| Chancellor | Head of government; needed the support of a majority in the Reichstag |
| Reichsrat | Represented the German states (*Länder*); could delay laws |
| Rights | Freedom of speech, the press, religion and assembly guaranteed |

**Strengths:** it was one of the most democratic constitutions in the world, with votes for women, guaranteed rights and a parliament elected fairly.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Proportional representation** let many small parties win seats, so no party ever had a majority and governments were short-lived coalitions.
- **Article 48** let a President govern without parliament, which was dangerous in the wrong hands.
- The **army, judges and civil servants** were left over from the empire and often hostile to democracy.

## German reactions to Versailles

The Treaty of Versailles, signed on **28 June 1919**, was hated across Germany. Germans called it a **Diktat**, a dictated peace, because their government had not been allowed to negotiate. The war guilt clause, the loss of land and colonies, reparations and the reduction of the army to 100,000 men all caused anger (the terms are studied in detail with the Versailles settlement).

The new republic was blamed for signing it. Nationalists called the politicians who had agreed to the armistice and treaty the **"November criminals"**, and spread the **"stab in the back"** myth: the false claim that the army had not been defeated in battle but betrayed by socialists, Jews and politicians at home. This myth linked democracy with defeat and humiliation from the very beginning.

## Threats from the left and the right

### The Spartacist rising, January 1919

The **Spartacists**, led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, wanted a communist revolution. In **January 1919** they joined an uprising in Berlin. Ebert's government used the army and **Freikorps** units to crush it, and Luxemburg and Liebknecht were captured and murdered. Further left-wing risings, including a short-lived soviet republic in Bavaria in the spring of 1919, were also put down by force.

### The Kapp Putsch, March 1920

In **March 1920** Freikorps units, angry at being disbanded under the treaty, marched into Berlin and declared Wolfgang **Kapp**, a nationalist politician, the head of a new government. The army refused to fire on them, and the government had to leave the city. But the trade unions called a **general strike**: transport, power and services stopped, and after four days Kapp fled.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the Kapp Putsch failed"}
1. **The workers refused to co-operate:** the trade unions called a general strike across Berlin.
2. **The city stopped working:** without electricity, transport or civil servants, Kapp could not govern.
3. **Kapp had no wider support:** the civil service would not obey him, and most of the army leadership stayed neutral rather than joining him.
4. **Result:** the putsch collapsed after four days. But the army had refused to defend the government, which showed how weak the republic was.
:::

### Political murders

Right-wing extremists murdered hundreds of opponents in the early years, including **Matthias Erzberger** (1921), who had signed the armistice, and the foreign minister **Walther Rathenau** (1922). Courts often treated right-wing killers lightly, while left-wing rebels received harsh sentences.

## The crisis of 1923

### Reparations and the Ruhr

In 1921 reparations were fixed at 132 billion gold marks. When Germany failed to make payments at the end of 1922, French and Belgian troops occupied the **Ruhr**, Germany's main industrial region, in **January 1923**, to take coal and goods instead. The German government ordered **passive resistance**: workers went on strike and refused to co-operate. The French responded by expelling and arresting workers, and some Germans were killed in clashes.

### Hyperinflation

Germany's money was already losing value because of debts from the war. To pay the striking Ruhr workers, the government printed more and more money, and in 1923 prices went out of control.

| Date | Approximate value of 1 US dollar in German marks |
|---|---|
| 1914 | about 4 |
| January 1922 | about 200 |
| January 1923 | about 17,000 |
| November 1923 | about 4.2 trillion (4,200,000,000,000) |

Workers were paid twice a day and rushed to spend their wages before prices rose again.

:::example{title="Weighing the effects: who lost and who gained from hyperinflation?"}
**Losers:** people with savings, pensions or fixed incomes, often from the middle classes, saw their money become worthless. Many never forgave the republic.
**Gainers:** people with debts, including the government and many businesses and farmers, could repay them with worthless money. Some industrialists bought up rivals cheaply.
**Judgement:** the long-term damage was to trust. The middle classes, who might have been the republic's strongest supporters, were left bitter and afraid of another crisis.
:::

Later in 1923 the republic faced a communist rising in Hamburg and, in November, Hitler's Munich Putsch (studied with the rise of the Nazis). Yet it survived: in late 1923 Gustav Stresemann ended passive resistance and introduced a new currency, which began the recovery covered in the next lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Spartacists were Nazis"}
The Spartacists were communists on the far left. The Kapp Putsch and, later, the Munich Putsch came from the nationalist far right.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hyperinflation was caused only by reparations"}
Germany's war debts had already weakened the mark. The immediate cause of hyperinflation in 1923 was the government printing money to pay workers during the Ruhr occupation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the republic was doomed"}
"Doomed" is a judgement to be argued. The republic survived every crisis of 1919-23, which is evidence against it, even though the crises left lasting weaknesses.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 Section B (c): 'Was the Weimar Republic doomed from the start?'"}
Weigh the weaknesses (Versailles, the stab-in-the-back myth, proportional representation, Article 48, a hostile army) against its survival in 1919-23 and its recovery after 1924. Give a clear judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4: 'Write an account of the crisis of 1923'"}
Tell the story in order: missed payments, the Ruhr occupation, passive resistance, printing money, hyperinflation, and how Stresemann ended it. Use dates and keep every paragraph linked to the question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Kaiser abdicated on 9 November 1918 and Ebert's government agreed the armistice; the army and old officials stayed in place.
- The Weimar Constitution was very democratic but had weaknesses: proportional representation and Article 48.
- The republic was blamed for Versailles through the "November criminals" and "stab in the back" myths.
- It survived the Spartacist rising (1919), the Kapp Putsch (1920) and political murders.
- The Ruhr occupation and hyperinflation of 1923 ruined savers and damaged trust in the republic.
:::
