## Introduction

When Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933 there were only three Nazis in a cabinet of twelve, and the conservatives around President Hindenburg expected to control him. Within eighteen months every other party had been abolished, the trade unions destroyed, rivals inside the Nazi movement murdered, and Hitler had become *Führer*, the sole leader of Germany, with the army sworn to obey him personally.

Studying how a dictatorship is built, step by step and often under the cover of law, helps us to recognise why constitutions, courts, a free press and independent organisations matter to every democracy, including our own.

ZIMSEC studies Hitler's domestic policies as part of the rise of European dictators (Paper 2), and Cambridge as Depth study B, key questions 2 and 3(a). You should know how Hitler became Chancellor.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Enabling Act"}
The law of 23 March 1933 that allowed Hitler's government to make laws without the approval of the Reichstag.
:::

:::definition{term="Gleichschaltung (co-ordination)"}
The Nazi process of bringing every part of German life, from state governments and trade unions to clubs and professions, under Nazi control.
:::

:::definition{term="SS"}
The *Schutzstaffel*, led by Heinrich Himmler: at first Hitler's bodyguard, it became the main instrument of terror, running the concentration camps and, with the Gestapo, the police system.
:::

:::definition{term="Gestapo"}
The secret state police, which investigated and arrested opponents of the regime.
:::

:::definition{term="Totalitarian state"}
A state in which one party and leader try to control every aspect of people's lives, public and private, using terror and propaganda.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **27 February 1933:** the Reichstag Fire.
- **5 March 1933:** Reichstag election; the Nazis win 43.9%.
- **23 March 1933:** the Enabling Act.
- **May-July 1933:** trade unions abolished; other parties banned or dissolved.
- **30 June 1934:** the Night of the Long Knives.
- **2 August 1934:** Hindenburg dies; Hitler becomes Führer.
:::

## From Chancellor to dictator, 1933-34

### The Reichstag Fire and the March election

On **27 February 1933** the Reichstag building in Berlin was set on fire. A young Dutch communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested inside. Historians still debate whether he acted alone; what is certain is that the Nazis used the fire. Hitler claimed it was the start of a communist revolution, and the next day Hindenburg signed an **emergency decree** that suspended freedom of speech, of the press and of assembly and allowed arrest without trial. Thousands of communists and other opponents were arrested.

In the election of **5 March 1933**, held amid SA violence, the Nazis won **43.9%** of the vote and 288 seats. With the Nationalist party they had a small majority, but not the two-thirds needed to change the constitution.

### The Enabling Act

On **23 March 1933** the Reichstag met in the Kroll Opera House, surrounded by SA and SS men. The Communist deputies had been arrested or had fled. The Catholic Centre Party, promised that the rights of the Church would be respected, voted for the **Enabling Act**. Only the Social Democrats voted against. The Act passed by 444 votes to 94 and gave Hitler the power to make laws without the Reichstag. The Reichstag no longer mattered.

### Removing opposition

Using the Enabling Act, the Nazis carried out **Gleichschaltung**:

- **State governments** were taken over by Nazi governors; the state parliaments were later abolished.
- **Trade unions** were abolished on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front.
- **Political parties**: the SPD was banned in June 1933, the others dissolved themselves, and in July 1933 the Nazi Party became the only legal party.
- Civil servants who were Jewish or politically unreliable were dismissed.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the Reichstag Fire led to dictatorship"}
1. **The fire** gave Hitler an excuse to claim a communist threat.
2. **The emergency decree** suspended civil rights, allowing mass arrests of communists and others.
3. **The March election** was held under these conditions, increasing the Nazi vote.
4. **Without the Communist deputies**, and with Centre Party votes, the two-thirds majority for the Enabling Act was achieved.
5. **Result:** a law that ended parliamentary government was passed by legal means within four weeks of the fire.
:::

### The Night of the Long Knives, 30 June 1934

The greatest threat to Hitler now came from inside his own movement. The **SA**, with millions of members, was led by **Ernst Röhm**, who wanted a "second revolution" against big business and wanted the SA to take over the army. The army's generals, whose support Hitler needed, were alarmed, and so were business leaders.

On **30 June 1934** SS units arrested and shot Röhm and other SA leaders. Hitler also used the purge to kill old enemies, including the former Chancellor **Schleicher** and Gregor Strasser, a former Nazi leader. Historians estimate that at least 85 people were killed, and possibly many more. Hitler told the Reichstag that he had acted to save Germany from a plot, and the killings were declared legal after the event.

### Hitler becomes Führer

When President **Hindenburg** died on **2 August 1934**, Hitler combined the posts of Chancellor and President and took the title **Führer**. Every soldier swore an oath of personal loyalty to Hitler, not to the constitution. A plebiscite in August 1934 approved the change, with about 90% voting yes.

## Methods of control

### Terror and repression

- **The SS**, led by Himmler, became a huge organisation controlling the police and the camps.
- **The Gestapo** arrested people without trial. Research on its records shows that it had relatively few officers and relied heavily on information from ordinary Germans who denounced neighbours, colleagues or relatives.
- **Concentration camps**, starting with Dachau in March 1933, held communists, socialists, trade unionists and others; later they held many more groups.
- **Courts** were controlled: judges had to support the regime, and a new People's Court tried cases of treason.
- **Local party officials**, such as block wardens, watched each street.

### Propaganda, culture and the media

**Joseph Goebbels** became Minister of Propaganda in March 1933.

- **Radio:** cheap radio sets were sold so that most homes could hear Hitler's speeches; loudspeakers were placed in streets and workplaces.
- **The press** was controlled; editors had to follow government instructions and opposition newspapers were closed.
- **Rallies and events:** the Nuremberg rallies and the 1936 Berlin Olympics displayed Nazi power and order.
- **Culture:** writers, artists, musicians and film-makers had to belong to the Reich Chamber of Culture. Books by Jewish and anti-Nazi authors were burned in May 1933, and modern art was condemned as "degenerate".

### The churches

In July 1933 Hitler signed a **Concordat** with the Pope: the Catholic Church would stay out of politics in return for its freedom of worship. The Nazis soon broke it, closing Catholic schools and youth groups. The Nazis set up a "Reich Church" for Protestants; some pastors, led by **Martin Niemöller**, formed the **Confessing Church** in opposition, and many, including Niemöller, were sent to camps.

## Opposition to Nazi rule

Open opposition was very dangerous, but it existed:

- **Communists and socialists** formed secret groups and distributed leaflets; many were caught.
- **Church leaders** criticised some policies, such as the killing of disabled people.
- **Young people:** groups such as the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth rejected Nazi youth culture, and the **White Rose** students in Munich, including Hans and Sophie Scholl, distributed anti-Nazi leaflets until they were executed in 1943.
- **The army:** on **20 July 1944** officers led by Colonel Claus von **Stauffenberg** tried to kill Hitler with a bomb. It failed, and thousands of people were arrested and many executed.

## Was Nazi Germany a totalitarian state?

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: total control or limited control?"}
**Totalitarian:** there was one party and one leader; all other parties, trade unions and independent organisations were abolished; terror, the SS and the Gestapo crushed opponents; propaganda reached every home.
**Not fully totalitarian:** the regime was often chaotic, with Nazi leaders competing for power; the Gestapo depended on denunciations by ordinary people; the churches kept some independence; and many Germans conformed out of support or self-interest rather than fear.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that Nazi Germany aimed at total control and achieved a great deal of it, but that its power rested on a mixture of terror, propaganda and genuine popular support.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Hitler became dictator in January 1933"}
In January 1933 he was a Chancellor leading a coalition. The Enabling Act (March 1933) and Hindenburg's death (August 1934) made him dictator.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the SA and the SS"}
The SA (Brownshirts, led by Röhm) was the mass street army. The SS (led by Himmler) carried out the Night of the Long Knives and later ran the camps.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying there was no opposition"}
There was opposition, from communists, socialists, churches, young people and army officers, but it was small, divided and brutally repressed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Reichstag Fire (February 1933) led to an emergency decree and mass arrests of opponents.
- The Enabling Act (March 1933) let Hitler make laws without the Reichstag; unions and other parties were destroyed.
- The Night of the Long Knives (June 1934) removed Röhm and the SA threat and won the army's support.
- After Hindenburg's death (August 1934) Hitler became Führer, with the army's oath of loyalty.
- Control rested on terror (SS, Gestapo, camps), propaganda (Goebbels) and some popular support; opposition was limited.
:::
