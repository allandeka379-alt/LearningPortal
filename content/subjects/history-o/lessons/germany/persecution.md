## Introduction

Between 1933 and 1945 the Nazi state persecuted millions of people because of who they were: their supposed race, their disabilities, their sexuality or their beliefs. The persecution began with insults, boycotts and laws, and ended in the murder of about six million Jews and hundreds of thousands of other people. This genocide is called the **Holocaust** (in Hebrew, the **Shoah**).

We study it to remember the victims as people, with families, work and lives, and to understand how prejudice, when it is turned into law and backed by state power, can lead to mass murder. Africa has its own experience of genocide, in German South West Africa (now Namibia) in 1904-08 and in Rwanda in 1994, and survivors everywhere have asked the world never to forget.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study B, key question 3(a). You should know how the Nazis took and kept power. The lesson describes events accurately but without graphic detail; numbers of victims are historians' estimates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anti-Semitism"}
Hostility to, prejudice against or hatred of Jewish people.
:::

:::definition{term="Aryan"}
In Nazi ideology, a supposed "master race" of northern Europeans. The idea has no scientific basis.
:::

:::definition{term="Genocide"}
The deliberate killing of a large group of people, especially those of a particular nation or ethnic group, with the aim of destroying that group.
:::

:::definition{term="Final Solution"}
The Nazi term for the plan, carried out from 1941-42, to murder all the Jews of Europe.
:::

:::definition{term="Ghetto"}
A closed area of a town in which Jews were forced to live, cut off from the rest of the population. The Nazis created ghettos in occupied Poland and elsewhere from 1939-40.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1 April 1933:** boycott of Jewish shops and businesses.
- **July 1933:** law for the compulsory sterilisation of people with certain disabilities.
- **15 September 1935:** the Nuremberg Laws.
- **9-10 November 1938:** Kristallnacht.
- **1939:** the murder of disabled people begins.
- **20 January 1942:** the Wannsee Conference.
:::

## Nazi racial ideas

The Nazis divided humanity into a racial hierarchy, with "Aryans" at the top. They wanted a **people's community** (*Volksgemeinschaft*) of racially "pure", healthy Germans, and they regarded anyone outside it as a threat. **Jews** were at the centre of Nazi hatred: Hitler blamed them for Germany's defeat in 1918, for communism and for the Depression, contradictory claims that show how anti-Semitism worked as a conspiracy theory. Hatred of Jews had a long history in Europe, which helps explain why many Germans accepted or ignored the persecution.

In 1933 there were about half a million Jews in Germany, less than one per cent of the population. Most were fully part of German life as doctors, lawyers, teachers, shopkeepers, scientists and soldiers; many had fought for Germany in the First World War.

## The persecution of German Jews, 1933-39

Persecution increased step by step, so that each new measure seemed only a little worse than the last.

**1933:**
- On **1 April** the SA organised a **boycott** of Jewish shops, standing outside them to turn customers away.
- Jews were dismissed from the civil service, including teaching, and later excluded from many professions and from the arts.

**1935:**
- The **Nuremberg Laws** (15 September) took away German citizenship from Jews and banned marriage and relationships between Jews and non-Jewish Germans. A decree later in 1935 defined who counted as a Jew by the religion of a person's grandparents, not by the person's own beliefs.

**1936:** persecution eased slightly during the Berlin Olympics, when the regime wanted to impress foreign visitors.

**1938:**
- Jews had to register their property, and Jewish businesses were increasingly taken over by non-Jews.
- On **9-10 November**, after a young Jewish refugee shot a German diplomat in Paris, the Nazis organised **Kristallnacht** ("the night of broken glass"). Across Germany and Austria, Nazi mobs destroyed Jewish homes and shops and burned hundreds of synagogues. Historians estimate that about 90 Jews were killed that night, and about 30,000 Jewish men were sent to concentration camps.
- The Jewish community was then fined one billion Reichsmarks, and Jewish children were expelled from German schools.

Many Jews tried to emigrate, and about half of Germany's Jews had left by 1939. But emigration was difficult: Jews had to leave most of their property behind, and many countries limited the number of refugees they would accept. About 10,000 Jewish children were taken to safety in Britain in 1938-39, most without their parents.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how Kristallnacht marked a turning point"}
1. **From laws to open violence:** for the first time, the regime organised nationwide attacks on Jewish people and property.
2. **Mass arrests:** tens of thousands of Jewish men were sent to camps, released only if they promised to emigrate.
3. **Economic exclusion:** the fine and the seizure of businesses removed Jews from economic life.
4. **The world's response:** foreign governments condemned it, but few opened their borders more widely.
5. **Result:** life in Germany became impossible for Jews, and the regime learned that most Germans would not resist.
:::

## The persecution of other groups

- **Roma and Sinti** were also classed as racial enemies. They were registered, banned from marrying Germans, placed in camps and, during the war, deported and murdered. Historians estimate that between about 200,000 and 500,000 Roma and Sinti were killed across Europe.
- **Disabled people:** a law of July 1933 ordered the sterilisation of people with certain physical and mental disabilities; historians estimate that about 400,000 people were sterilised. From 1939 a secret programme killed disabled children and adults in hospitals. After protests, including a sermon by the Catholic bishop of Münster, Clemens von Galen, in 1941, the main programme was officially stopped, but killings continued in secret.
- **Homosexual men** were persecuted under a strengthened law of 1935; thousands were sent to concentration camps, where they were forced to wear a pink triangle.
- **Jehovah's Witnesses** refused to give the Hitler salute or serve in the army. Thousands were imprisoned in camps, where many died.
- Political opponents, people labelled "asocial" and others also filled the camps.

## The Final Solution

The war brought millions more Jews under Nazi rule, and persecution turned into mass murder. (The experience of occupied Poland is studied in more detail in the Second World War depth study.)

- **Ghettos:** after the conquest of Poland in 1939, Jews were forced into ghettos such as those of Warsaw and Łódź, where hunger and disease killed many.
- **Mass shootings:** after the invasion of the USSR in June 1941, SS units called *Einsatzgruppen* followed the army and shot Jewish men, women and children. Historians estimate that they murdered well over a million people.
- **The Wannsee Conference:** on **20 January 1942** senior officials met near Berlin to co-ordinate the deportation and murder of all the Jews of Europe.
- **Death camps:** at camps in occupied Poland, including **Treblinka, Sobibór, Bełżec, Chełmno** and **Auschwitz-Birkenau**, people were murdered in gas chambers. At Auschwitz alone, historians estimate that about 1.1 million people were murdered, about a million of them Jews.

By 1945 about **six million Jews** had been murdered, around two-thirds of the Jews of Europe before the war.

Jews and others resisted where they could: through armed revolts, such as the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943, through escape, smuggling and hiding, and by keeping up education, religion and records in the ghettos. Some non-Jews risked their lives to hide or rescue Jews.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was the Holocaust planned from the start?"}
**Planned from the start:** some historians stress that Hitler's hatred of Jews was clear from the 1920s, so the Holocaust was the result of his long-held intentions.
**Developed step by step:** others argue that there was no plan for mass murder in 1933; policy became more radical as Nazi leaders competed to carry out Hitler's wishes and as the war created new opportunities and pressures.
**What both agree on:** Hitler's anti-Semitism was central, and the murder was carried out by a modern state with the participation or acceptance of very many people.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the death camps were in Germany"}
The death camps built for mass murder were in occupied Poland. Camps in Germany, such as Dachau, were concentration camps where many people also died.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the other victims"}
Roma and Sinti, disabled people, homosexual men, Jehovah's Witnesses and political opponents were also persecuted, and many were murdered.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing victims only as numbers"}
Good answers remember that each victim was a person, and that Jews and others resisted in many ways.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain why the Nazis persecuted many groups in German society'"}
Explain the racial ideology of the people's community, the use of Jews as scapegoats, the economic gains from seizing property, and how war radicalised policy. Give a specific example for each reason.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4 (a): 'Write an account of the persecution of Jews in Germany, 1933-39'"}
Write in chronological order with dates (1933 boycott, 1935 Nuremberg Laws, 1938 Kristallnacht) and show how persecution increased over time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nazi racial ideas placed "Aryans" at the top and treated Jews as the main enemy of the people's community.
- Persecution of German Jews increased step by step: the 1933 boycott, the 1935 Nuremberg Laws and Kristallnacht in 1938.
- Roma and Sinti, disabled people, homosexual men and Jehovah's Witnesses were also persecuted.
- During the war persecution became genocide: ghettos, mass shootings, the Wannsee Conference (1942) and the death camps.
- Historians estimate that about six million Jews were murdered; Jews and others resisted in many ways.
:::
