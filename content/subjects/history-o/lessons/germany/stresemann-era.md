## Introduction

At the end of 1923 the Weimar Republic seemed close to collapse: its currency was worthless, foreign troops occupied the Ruhr, and armed groups on the left and right had tried to seize power. Five years later Germany had a stable currency, rising production, a seat on the Council of the League of Nations and a lively cultural life admired across the world. These years are often called the **"Golden Twenties"**.

Recovery that depends on foreign loans can disappear quickly when the lenders are in trouble, a lesson that many developing economies, including Zimbabwe's, have also learned. As you read, ask whether Weimar's recovery was real or fragile.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study B, key question 1, from 1924 to 1929. You should know about the crisis of 1923.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rentenmark"}
The temporary currency introduced in November 1923 to end hyperinflation. It was issued in strictly limited amounts and backed by a mortgage on German land and industrial property.
:::

:::definition{term="Dawes Plan"}
The 1924 agreement, worked out by a committee chaired by the American banker Charles Dawes, that set reparation payments Germany could afford in the early years and arranged a large loan, mainly from the USA.
:::

:::definition{term="Young Plan"}
The 1929 agreement, named after the American Owen Young, that reduced the total of reparations and spread payments over 59 years.
:::

:::definition{term="Locarno Treaties"}
Agreements of 1925 in which Germany, France and Belgium accepted their shared western borders, with Britain and Italy as guarantors, and Germany accepted that the Rhineland would remain demilitarised.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **August-November 1923:** Stresemann is Chancellor; he remains foreign minister until his death in **October 1929**.
- **November 1923:** the Rentenmark.
- **1924:** the Dawes Plan.
- **1925:** the Locarno Treaties; Hindenburg elected President.
- **1926:** Germany joins the League of Nations.
- **1929:** the Young Plan.
:::

## Gustav Stresemann

Gustav **Stresemann** was leader of the German People's Party (DVP), a party of business and the middle classes. He had been a nationalist during the war, but he came to believe that Germany could only recover by co-operating with the victors and fulfilling the treaty while negotiating to change it. He was Chancellor for about a hundred days in 1923 and then foreign minister in every government until he died.

## Economic recovery

### Ending hyperinflation

In September 1923 Stresemann called off **passive resistance** in the Ruhr, which angered nationalists but allowed the government to stop printing money to pay strikers. In November the **Rentenmark** was introduced. Because only a limited amount was printed, people began to trust it. In 1924 it was replaced by a new permanent currency, the **Reichsmark**.

### The Dawes Plan, 1924

The **Dawes Plan** did not reduce the total reparations, but it made them manageable:

- Payments began at a lower level and would rise gradually as the economy recovered.
- Germany received a large **loan**, mostly from American banks, to help rebuild industry.
- The German central bank was reorganised under international supervision.
- French and Belgian troops left the Ruhr by 1925.

Over the following years American money poured into Germany. It paid for new factories, roads, housing, schools and swimming pools. By 1928 industrial production was back above its pre-war level, and wages rose.

### The Young Plan, 1929

The **Young Plan** went further: it **reduced the total** of reparations and allowed Germany to pay over **59 years**, until 1988. It also led to Allied troops leaving the Rhineland early, by 1930. Nationalists, including the newspaper owner Alfred Hugenberg and Hitler, campaigned against it in a referendum in 1929, arguing that it still accepted war guilt. They lost the vote heavily, but the campaign gave the Nazis national publicity.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how the Dawes Plan helped and harmed the recovery"}
1. **Reparations were rescheduled:** Germany paid less in the early years, so the budget could balance.
2. **American loans arrived:** investment rebuilt industry, created jobs and paid for public projects.
3. **Confidence returned:** the currency was stable and the Ruhr was evacuated.
4. **But a weakness was built in:** many of the loans were short-term and could be called back at any time.
5. **Result:** when the American economy crashed in 1929, the loans were recalled and the German recovery collapsed.
:::

## Foreign policy

Stresemann's foreign policy was to win back Germany's position step by step, by peaceful means:

- **Locarno, 1925:** Germany accepted its western borders with France and Belgium and the demilitarisation of the Rhineland. It did not accept its eastern borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia as final, but agreed to settle disputes there peacefully. The treaties were celebrated as the start of a new era of trust.
- **League of Nations, 1926:** Germany joined the League and was given a permanent seat on its Council, showing that it was again treated as a great power.
- **Kellogg-Briand Pact, 1928:** Germany was one of the first to sign this agreement to reject war as a way of settling disputes.

Stresemann and the French foreign minister Aristide Briand shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926. At home, nationalists attacked him for accepting Versailles' borders in the west, while he argued that co-operation would bring more gains, such as the end of the Rhineland occupation.

## Cultural achievements

The Weimar years were a time of great creativity, especially in Berlin, as censorship ended and artists experimented with new forms.

- **Architecture and design:** the **Bauhaus** school, founded by Walter Gropius in Weimar in 1919 and moved to Dessau in 1925, combined art, craft and industry in simple, functional designs.
- **Art:** painters such as Otto Dix and George Grosz showed the horrors of war and the inequality of German society.
- **Cinema:** German films, such as Fritz Lang's *Metropolis* (1927), were among the most advanced in the world.
- **Theatre, music and literature:** Bertolt Brecht's plays, cabaret and jazz flourished. Erich Maria Remarque's novel *All Quiet on the Western Front* (1929) described the experience of ordinary soldiers.

Many conservative Germans, especially in rural areas and small towns, saw this culture as immoral and un-German, which added to their dislike of the republic.

## How far did the Republic recover?

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: a golden age or a fragile recovery?"}
**Real recovery:** the currency was stable, production passed pre-war levels, Germany was accepted back as a great power, and extremist parties declined: the Nazis won only 2.6% of the vote in 1928, and the parties that supported the republic did better.
**Fragile recovery:** the economy depended on short-term American loans; farmers suffered from low prices and debts; unemployment remained significant; coalitions changed frequently; and in 1925 voters elected Hindenburg, a monarchist field marshal, as President. Middle-class savers ruined in 1923 had not recovered their losses.
**Judgement:** the recovery was real but shallow. The republic was more stable in 1928 than at any other time, but it rested on foundations that the Depression could, and did, destroy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Dawes Plan cancelled reparations"}
The Dawes Plan rescheduled payments and arranged loans; it did not reduce the total. The Young Plan of 1929 reduced the total.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Locarno settled all of Germany's borders"}
At Locarno Germany accepted its western borders only. It did not guarantee its eastern borders with Poland and Czechoslovakia.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing achievements without assessing them"}
A question on how far the republic recovered needs both the achievements and their limits, then a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 Section B (c): 'How far did the Weimar Republic recover under Stresemann?'"}
Organise your answer by area: economy, foreign policy, politics and culture. For each, give the achievement and its limitation, then make an overall judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4 (b): 'How important was Stresemann to the Weimar Republic?'"}
Judge importance in different ways: in the short term (ending the 1923 crisis), the long term (whether his recovery lasted), and compared with other factors such as American money.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stresemann ended passive resistance and introduced the Rentenmark (1923), ending hyperinflation.
- The Dawes Plan (1924) rescheduled reparations and brought American loans; the Young Plan (1929) reduced the total.
- Locarno (1925), League membership (1926) and the Kellogg-Briand Pact (1928) restored Germany's international position.
- Weimar culture flourished (the Bauhaus, cinema, theatre, art) but offended many conservatives.
- The recovery was real but fragile, because it depended on short-term American loans.
:::
