## Introduction

For many Germans who were not persecuted by the regime, the years after 1933 felt at first like a return to normal life: there were jobs again, the streets were orderly, and the country seemed respected abroad. Yet every part of daily life, from what children learned at school to how women were expected to live and how workers spent their holidays, was shaped by Nazi ideas, and all of it was directed towards preparing Germany for war.

Governments everywhere try to shape family life, schooling and work, and Zimbabwe's own history includes youth movements and national campaigns. The Nazi case shows how far a dictatorship can go when there are no limits on its power.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study B, key question 3(b): *What was it like to live in Nazi Germany?* You should know how the Nazis came to power and how they controlled Germany. (The persecution of Jews and other minorities is covered in a separate lesson.)

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Autarky"}
Economic self-sufficiency: producing everything the country needs, especially for war, without relying on imports.
:::

:::definition{term="German Labour Front (DAF)"}
The Nazi organisation, led by Robert Ley, that replaced the trade unions in May 1933. It set wages and working conditions and ran leisure schemes.
:::

:::definition{term="Strength Through Joy (KdF)"}
The part of the Labour Front that offered workers cheap holidays, cruises, concerts and sports, to keep them loyal and productive.
:::

:::definition{term="Hitler Youth"}
The Nazi youth movement for boys (the Hitler Youth itself for 14-18-year-olds, the German Young People for 10-14), with the League of German Maidens for girls. It became the only legal youth organisation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- **Hjalmar Schacht:** Economics Minister 1934-37. **Hermann Göring:** in charge of the Four-Year Plan from 1936.
- **May 1933:** German Labour Front set up. **June 1933:** marriage loans introduced.
- **1935:** six months' Reich Labour Service made compulsory for young men; conscription reintroduced.
- **1936:** Four-Year Plan; Hitler Youth Law. **1939:** Hitler Youth membership compulsory.
- **1942:** Albert Speer becomes Minister of Armaments. **1943:** "total war" declared after Stalingrad.
:::

## The economy

### Work and recovery, 1933-36

The Nazis' first promise was work. Under the economist **Hjalmar Schacht**, the government:

- spent heavily on **public works**, such as the new motorways (*Autobahnen*), housing and public buildings;
- set up the **Reich Labour Service**, which became compulsory for young men for six months in 1935;
- reintroduced **conscription** in 1935, taking young men into the armed forces;
- controlled imports and made trade deals with countries in south-eastern Europe, exchanging German goods for food and raw materials.

Registered unemployment fell from about six million in early 1933 to a few hundred thousand by 1939. But the figures hid some things: women and Jews pushed out of their jobs were not counted as unemployed, and many men were in the Labour Service or the army.

### Rearmament and the Four-Year Plan

From 1936 the economy was turned towards war. **Göring's Four-Year Plan** aimed at **autarky** and rearmament: Germany was to produce substitutes (*ersatz*) such as synthetic rubber and oil from coal, and to be ready for war within four years. Spending on weapons rose enormously. Self-sufficiency was never achieved, and Germany still depended on imports of many raw materials when war came.

### Workers and the Labour Front

The trade unions were abolished in May 1933. The **German Labour Front** set wages and hours; strikes were illegal and workers could not easily change jobs. In return the regime offered:

- **Strength Through Joy**: cheap holidays, cruises, theatre trips and sports.
- **Beauty of Labour**: campaigns to improve workplaces with canteens, washrooms and better lighting.
- The **Volkswagen** ("people's car") scheme, in which workers paid weekly instalments towards a car. No car was ever delivered to a saver: the factory switched to military production when war began.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: did most Germans benefit from Nazi rule?"}
**Benefited:** millions of unemployed people found work; big businesses such as arms and chemical firms made large profits; workers gained leisure schemes; farmers were protected from losing their farms.
**Did not benefit:** wages were frozen while working hours rose; workers lost the right to strike and their unions; food was dearer and less plentiful as resources went into weapons; small shopkeepers gained little.
**The excluded:** Jews, Roma and Sinti, disabled people and political opponents did not belong to the "people's community" and suffered persecution.
**Judgement:** many "Aryan" Germans felt better off than in the Depression, mainly because they had work, but the benefits were limited and were paid for by losing freedom and by the exclusion of others.
:::

## Women and the family

The Nazis believed that a woman's role was to be a wife and mother, summed up in the slogan about "children, kitchen, church". Their policies aimed to raise the birth rate:

- **Marriage loans** (from June 1933): newly married couples could borrow money, and part of the loan was cancelled for each child born.
- **The Mother's Cross** (from 1938): bronze, silver and gold medals for mothers of four, six and eight or more children.
- Women were pushed out of many professions, such as senior posts in the civil service, medicine and the law.
- Women were encouraged to avoid make-up and smoking and to dress simply.

The birth rate did rise in the 1930s, though historians debate how far this was due to Nazi policy rather than economic recovery. And the policy contradicted itself: from 1937 rearmament caused a **shortage of workers**, so women were encouraged back into factories. During the war women's labour became essential, but the regime was slow to conscript women; only in 1943 were women aged 17 to 45 required to register for work.

## Young people

The Nazis knew that the future of their regime depended on the young.

### The Hitler Youth

Boys joined the German Young People at 10 and the **Hitler Youth** at 14; girls joined the Young Girls and then the **League of German Maidens**. Activities included camping, sport, marching and military-style training for boys, and preparation for motherhood and domestic work for girls. Members learned Nazi ideas and loyalty to Hitler. Membership rose from about two million in 1933 to several million by 1936; the Hitler Youth Law of 1936 made it the official state youth organisation, and in 1939 membership became compulsory.

### Schools

- Teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League and could be dismissed if they were unreliable.
- The curriculum was rewritten: history glorified Germany's past, "race science" taught Nazi racial ideas, and much more time was given to physical education.
- Girls studied domestic science; elite schools trained future leaders.

### Non-conformity

Not all young people were won over. Some found the Hitler Youth boring and over-regimented, and attendance fell off in the late 1930s. Groups such as the **Swing Youth**, middle-class teenagers who loved American jazz, and the **Edelweiss Pirates**, working-class young people who went hiking and sometimes clashed with Hitler Youth patrols, showed their rejection of Nazi culture. During the war some Edelweiss Pirates helped deserters and escaped prisoners, and some were executed.

:::example{title="A source analysed: a Hitler Youth camp photograph"}
**What it shows:** a typical published photograph shows smiling, healthy boys in uniform around a campfire.
**Purpose:** such images were propaganda, showing a happy, united youth loyal to Hitler.
**What it leaves out:** compulsion after 1939, boredom with drill, and non-conformist groups.
**Use:** it is good evidence of what the regime wanted Germans to believe, but weak evidence of what young people really felt.
:::

## The impact of war

War changed daily life sharply after September 1939:

- **Rationing** of food and clothing began at the start of the war, and shortages grew worse as it went on.
- After defeat at **Stalingrad** (early 1943), Goebbels declared **"total war"**: shops and businesses not needed for the war effort were closed and all resources put into fighting. **Albert Speer**, Minister of Armaments from 1942, raised weapons production until 1944.
- **Millions of foreign workers**, many of them forced labourers and prisoners of war from occupied countries, were brought to work in German factories and farms.
- **Allied bombing** destroyed homes and killed many civilians in cities such as Hamburg (1943) and Dresden (1945), and millions became refugees.
- By late 1944 boys and old men were called up into a people's militia.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Nazis ended unemployment by public works alone"}
Rearmament, conscription, the Labour Service and the removal of women and Jews from the figures were all part of the fall in unemployment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Nazi policy kept all women at home"}
The policy changed: from 1937 labour shortages brought women back into work, and in the war their work was essential.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming all young people supported the regime"}
Many enjoyed the Hitler Youth, but there was boredom, non-conformity and some resistance.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'How successful were Nazi policies towards women?'"}
Judge success against the Nazis' own aims (more births, women at home) and show where the aims were met and where they were abandoned (labour shortages after 1937, the war).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 4 (b): 'How important was the Hitler Youth to the Nazi regime?'"}
Discuss importance in different ways: training soldiers, spreading ideas, controlling families; and its limits, such as boredom and non-conformist groups.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Public works, the Labour Service, conscription and rearmament cut unemployment from about six million to a few hundred thousand by 1939.
- Göring's Four-Year Plan (1936) aimed at autarky and rearmament but did not achieve self-sufficiency.
- Workers gained jobs and leisure schemes but lost unions and the right to strike; wages were frozen.
- Women were encouraged to be mothers (marriage loans, the Mother's Cross), but labour shortages brought many back to work.
- The Hitler Youth and schools spread Nazi ideas, though some young people rejected them; war brought rationing, total war and bombing.
:::
