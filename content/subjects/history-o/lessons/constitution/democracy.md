## Introduction

When Zimbabweans queued to vote in February 1980, many were voting for the first time. Universal adult suffrage, one person one vote, had been a central demand of the liberation struggle. Since then Zimbabwe has held regular elections, adopted a new Constitution and created independent commissions, but its elections and democratic institutions have also been the subject of strong debate at home and abroad.

You should know the main events since independence and the outline of the 2013 Constitution. This lesson covers the **meaning** and **principles** of democracy, its **types**, and **how government has promoted democracy** in Zimbabwe, presented in a balanced way.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Democracy"}
A system of government in which power belongs to the people, who govern either directly or through representatives they choose in free and fair elections. The word comes from the Greek *demos* (people) and *kratos* (rule).
:::

:::definition{term="Universal adult suffrage"}
The right of every adult citizen to vote, regardless of race, sex, wealth or education. In Zimbabwe the voting age is 18.
:::

:::definition{term="Accountability"}
The duty of those in power to explain their actions and accept responsibility for them, for example to Parliament, the courts and voters.
:::

:::definition{term="Multi-party system"}
A political system in which several parties may form, campaign and compete for power.
:::

:::keyfact{title="A famous definition"}
In his Gettysburg Address (1863) the American President Abraham Lincoln described democracy as "government of the people, by the people, for the people".
:::

## Principles of democracy

| Principle | What it means |
|---|---|
| Popular participation | Citizens take part in decisions: voting, joining parties, attending meetings |
| Free and fair elections | Regular elections by secret ballot, with a free campaign and an honest count |
| Rule of law | Everyone, including leaders, is subject to the law |
| Separation of powers | Executive, legislature and judiciary are separate and check each other |
| Accountability and transparency | Leaders explain their actions; information is open |
| Freedom of expression and the media | People may criticise the government without fear |
| Majority rule with minority rights | The majority decides, but minorities keep their rights |
| Human rights | Basic rights and freedoms are protected |

## Types of democracy

### Direct democracy

Citizens make decisions themselves. In ancient Athens, adult male citizens voted on laws in an assembly (women, slaves and foreigners were excluded). Today **referendums**, such as Zimbabwe's constitutional referendums of 2000 and 2013, are a form of direct democracy.

### Representative (indirect) democracy

Citizens elect representatives (MPs, senators, councillors and a president) to make decisions for them. Most modern democracies are representative.

### Parliamentary democracy

The government is formed from the party or coalition with a majority in parliament, and the head of government (the **Prime Minister**) is answerable to parliament. The head of state may be a monarch or a largely ceremonial president. Britain is an example. Zimbabwe used this system from 1980 to 1987, with Robert Mugabe as Prime Minister and Canaan Banana as ceremonial President.

### Presidential democracy

An executive **President** is elected separately from the legislature and is both head of state and head of government. The United States is an example. Zimbabwe adopted an executive presidency from the end of 1987.

:::example{title="Comparing parliamentary and presidential systems"}
1. **Who leads the government?** Parliamentary: a Prime Minister from parliament. Presidential: an elected President.
2. **How is the leader chosen?** Parliamentary: by the majority in parliament. Presidential: by a separate election.
3. **How is the leader removed?** Parliamentary: by a vote of no confidence. Presidential: usually only at an election or by a special process such as impeachment.
4. **Zimbabwe:** parliamentary system 1980-1987; executive presidency since then.
:::

## How government has promoted democracy in Zimbabwe

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| February 1980 | First elections under universal adult suffrage |
| 1987 | Seats reserved for whites abolished; executive presidency created |
| 1990s | Regular elections continue; opposition parties contest |
| February 2000 | A draft constitution is rejected in a referendum |
| 2004 | The Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC) is established |
| 2009-2013 | Inclusive government under the Global Political Agreement |
| March 2013 | The new Constitution is approved in a referendum |

Measures that governments have pointed to include:

- **Universal adult suffrage** from 1980 and **regular elections** at national and local level.
- A **multi-party system**: opposition parties have contested elections and won seats in Parliament and control of many urban councils.
- **Referendums**, including the 2000 vote, which the government lost and accepted, and the 2013 vote.
- **Independent commissions** under the 2013 Constitution, including the Zimbabwe Electoral Commission, the Human Rights Commission, the Gender Commission and the Media Commission.
- **Decentralisation and devolution**: elected rural and urban councils and provincial councils.
- **Representation of women and youth**, for example seats in the National Assembly reserved for women and elected by proportional representation.
- **A Declaration of Rights** protecting freedoms of expression, assembly and association, and term limits for the President.

:::example{title="Weighing different views: how democratic has Zimbabwe been?"}
1. **One view:** Zimbabwe has held regular multi-party elections since 1980, accepted the referendum defeat of 2000, created independent commissions and adopted a Constitution with strong rights and term limits.
2. **Another view:** observers and opposition parties have disputed the results of several elections; violence surrounded some campaigns, especially in 2008; laws such as the Public Order and Security Act (2002) restricted meetings and the media; and the Gukurahundi disturbances of the 1980s are part of the record.
3. **Further evidence:** in 2008 SADC mediated between the parties, which led to the Global Political Agreement and an inclusive government (2009-2013); some restrictive laws were later replaced.
4. **Judgement:** a balanced answer recognises the democratic institutions that exist and the debate over how fully they have worked in practice.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing principles without explaining them"}
"Rule of law" alone earns little. Say what it means and why it matters for democracy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing types of democracy"}
Direct democracy means citizens decide themselves (a referendum); representative democracy means citizens elect people to decide. Parliamentary and presidential are two kinds of representative democracy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing one-sided answers"}
For "how has the government promoted democracy?" describe the measures accurately; for "how far" questions, also give the criticisms and reach a balanced judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1: describe the principles and types"}
Name each principle or type and give a short explanation with an example, ideally from Zimbabwe.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain government measures"}
For "explain how the government has promoted democracy", link each measure to a principle: regular elections (participation), ZEC (free and fair elections), courts (rule of law), commissions (accountability).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Democracy means rule by the people, directly or through elected representatives.
- Its principles include participation, free and fair elections, rule of law, separation of powers, accountability, free expression and human rights.
- Types include direct, representative, parliamentary and presidential democracy; Zimbabwe moved from a parliamentary to a presidential system in 1987.
- Government measures include universal suffrage, regular multi-party elections, referendums, independent commissions, devolution and the 2013 Declaration of Rights.
- How fully democracy has worked in practice is debated, especially over disputed elections and restrictive laws.
:::
