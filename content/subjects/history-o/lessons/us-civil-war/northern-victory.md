## Introduction

In April 1861 many people on both sides expected a short war. It lasted four years. Historians long accepted an estimate of about 620,000 soldiers' deaths; more recent research suggests the figure may have been about 750,000, most of them from disease rather than battle. When it ended, the Union had been saved and about four million people had been freed from slavery.

Why did the North win? It had many more people and factories, but large resources do not always win wars, as the success of guerrilla armies against colonial powers in twentieth-century Africa shows. The South only needed to survive until the North gave up. This lesson explains the resources, strategies, turning points and leadership that decided the war.

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 4. You should already know why eleven Southern states seceded in 1860-61.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Union and Confederacy"}
The Union was the United States government and the states loyal to it (the North). The Confederacy was the Confederate States of America formed by the eleven seceding states (the South).
:::

:::definition{term="Anaconda Plan"}
The Union strategy proposed in 1861 to squeeze the South like a snake: blockade its ports and take control of the Mississippi River to split it in two.
:::

:::definition{term="Emancipation Proclamation"}
Lincoln's order, which took effect on 1 January 1863, declaring enslaved people in the areas in rebellion to be free.
:::

:::definition{term="Total war"}
Warfare aimed not only at the enemy's army but at the economic resources and morale that keep it fighting, as in Sherman's march through Georgia.
:::

:::keyfact{title="People to know"}
- **Abraham Lincoln:** Union President, 1861-1865.
- **Jefferson Davis:** Confederate President.
- **Robert E. Lee:** commander of the Confederacy's main army in Virginia.
- **Ulysses S. Grant:** Union general who took Vicksburg and became commander of all Union armies in 1864.
- **William T. Sherman:** Union general who took Atlanta and marched through Georgia in 1864.
:::

## Resources and strategy

| | Union (North) | Confederacy (South) |
|---|---|---|
| States | 23, including four border slave states | 11 |
| Population (1860, approximate) | About 22 million | About 9 million, of whom about 3.5 million were enslaved |
| Industry | About nine-tenths of the country's manufacturing | Little industry; had to import weapons |
| Railways | Most of the country's railway network | A smaller network with different track widths |
| Navy | Most of the navy and merchant ships | Almost no navy at the start |

The South had advantages too. It only needed to defend its territory, not conquer the North. It had many experienced officers, including **Robert E. Lee**, and its soldiers were fighting on home ground. Its leaders hoped that Britain and France, which needed Southern cotton for their textile mills, would recognise and support the Confederacy ("King Cotton" diplomacy).

The Union adopted the **Anaconda Plan**: a naval **blockade** of Southern ports and control of the **Mississippi**. It also needed to capture the Confederate capital, Richmond, and destroy Lee's army, which proved much harder.

## Key turning points

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| July 1861 | Confederate victory at the First Battle of Bull Run |
| 17 September 1862 | Battle of Antietam stops Lee's invasion of Maryland |
| 1 January 1863 | Emancipation Proclamation takes effect |
| 1-3 July 1863 | Battle of Gettysburg: Lee's invasion of Pennsylvania defeated |
| 4 July 1863 | Vicksburg surrenders to Grant |
| March 1864 | Grant becomes commander of all Union armies |
| September-December 1864 | Sherman takes Atlanta and marches to the sea |
| November 1864 | Lincoln re-elected President |
| 9 April 1865 | Lee surrenders at Appomattox Court House |

- **Antietam (September 1862):** the bloodiest single day of the war. Lee's invasion of Maryland was stopped, which gave Lincoln the chance to announce emancipation.
- **Gettysburg (July 1863):** Lee's second invasion of the North was defeated in Pennsylvania. He never again invaded Northern territory.
- **Vicksburg (July 1863):** Grant captured the last major Confederate fortress on the Mississippi, splitting the Confederacy in two.
- **Sherman's march (1864):** Sherman captured Atlanta in September, helping Lincoln win re-election, then marched to Savannah, destroying railways, crops and supplies.
- **Grant in Virginia (1864-65):** Grant fought Lee continuously, accepting heavy losses because the North could replace men and the South could not. Richmond fell in April 1865, and Lee surrendered at **Appomattox** on 9 April.

## Emancipation and Black soldiers

At first Lincoln's aim was to save the Union, not to end slavery, partly because he needed to keep the border slave states loyal. But thousands of enslaved people escaped to Union lines, and the war made emancipation both a moral and a military question. After Antietam, Lincoln issued the preliminary proclamation, and the **Emancipation Proclamation** took effect on **1 January 1863**.

It freed enslaved people only in areas in rebellion, not in the loyal border states, so on the day it was issued it freed few people in practice. But it changed the war: every advance of the Union army now brought freedom, it made British or French support for the Confederacy much less likely, and it allowed Black men to join the Union army. About 180,000 Black soldiers served, around one in ten of the Union army by the end of the war, and Black sailors served in the navy. Regiments such as the 54th Massachusetts proved their courage despite lower pay at first and the threat of enslavement or death if captured.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the Emancipation Proclamation helped the North win"}
1. **A new war aim:** the war became a war against slavery as well as for the Union.
2. **Foreign policy:** British public opinion opposed slavery, so Britain was now less likely to recognise the Confederacy.
3. **Manpower:** about 180,000 Black soldiers joined the Union army.
4. **Weakening the South:** escapes to Union lines took labour away from Southern farms.
5. **Result:** the North gained men and support while the South lost hope of foreign help.
:::

## The role of Lincoln

Lincoln kept the border states, especially Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri, in the Union. He held together a divided Republican Party and won re-election in 1864. He searched for a winning general until he found Grant, and he explained the purpose of the war powerfully, for example in the Gettysburg Address of November 1863. Jefferson Davis, by contrast, struggled with state governors who resisted his demands for men and supplies in the name of states' rights.

## Why the North won

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: resources or leadership?"}
**Resources:** the North had more than twice the population, most of the industry, railways and ships, and could replace its losses. The blockade starved the South of imports, and inflation and shortages weakened Southern morale.
**Leadership and decisions:** Lincoln's political skill, Grant's and Sherman's strategy, the Emancipation Proclamation and Black soldiers, the capture of the Mississippi and Southern problems such as states' rights and desertion all mattered. With different leadership, the North might have given up, as many Northerners wanted in 1864.
**Judgement:** the North's resources made victory possible, but they had to be used by effective leaders; without the victories of 1863-64 and Lincoln's re-election, war-weariness might have produced a negotiated peace.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Emancipation Proclamation freed all enslaved people"}
It applied only to areas in rebellion. Slavery was abolished everywhere in the United States by the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Gettysburg as the end of the war"}
Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 were turning points, but the war lasted almost two more years.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying resources alone explain the result"}
The South did not need to win battles in the North; it needed the North to give up. Explain why the North did not give up.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The North had much larger resources (people, industry, railways, navy); the South hoped to survive and gain foreign support.
- The Anaconda Plan's blockade and control of the Mississippi (Vicksburg, July 1863) split and starved the South.
- Antietam and Gettysburg ended Lee's invasions; Sherman's march and Grant's campaign in Virginia led to Lee's surrender at Appomattox on 9 April 1865.
- The Emancipation Proclamation (1 January 1863) made the war a war against slavery and brought about 180,000 Black soldiers into the Union army.
- Lincoln's leadership and the North's resources together explain the Union victory.
:::
