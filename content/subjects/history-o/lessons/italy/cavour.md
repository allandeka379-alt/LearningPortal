## Introduction

In 1849 Piedmont had been beaten twice by Austria. Ten years later, with a French army at its side, it defeated Austria and doubled in size. The man most responsible was Count Camillo di **Cavour**, Prime Minister of Piedmont from 1852. He was not a romantic revolutionary like Mazzini but a practical politician who believed that Italy's problems would be solved by modern economics and clever diplomacy.

The lesson of 1848-49 had been that Italians could not defeat Austria alone. Cavour's answer was to find a powerful ally. Small states in modern Africa have faced the same dilemma: how do you gain what you want when a more powerful neighbour stands in the way, and what price do you pay an ally?

This is Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 2. You should already know about Austrian influence in Italy and why the revolutions of 1848-49 failed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Realpolitik"}
Politics based on practical calculation of power and interests rather than on ideals. Cavour, like Bismarck in Germany, is often described as a practitioner of it.
:::

:::definition{term="Plombières agreement"}
The secret agreement of July 1858 between Cavour and Napoleon III to fight a war against Austria in return for French gains of Nice and Savoy.
:::

:::definition{term="Plebiscite"}
A vote by the whole people on a single question, such as whether to join another state.
:::

:::definition{term="National Society"}
An organisation founded in 1857 by former republicans and moderates that supported Italian unity under the leadership of Piedmont's king.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}

## Piedmont under Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour

**Victor Emmanuel II** kept the Statuto after 1849, so Piedmont remained a constitutional monarchy with an elected parliament. Exiles from all over Italy settled in Turin, and Piedmont became the hope of moderate nationalists.

Cavour became Prime Minister in **1852**. He had travelled in Britain and France and admired their economies. He believed that a strong, modern Piedmont would earn the respect of Europe and attract the support of other Italians. His government:

- Signed **free trade treaties** with Britain, France and other countries, so trade grew.
- Encouraged the building of **railways**, banks and the port of Genoa.
- Reduced the power of the Church, for example by closing many monasteries in 1855.
- Reformed the finances and strengthened the **army**.

Many former followers of Mazzini now accepted that Italy would be united, if at all, under the House of Savoy. The **National Society** (1857) campaigned for this.

## Diplomacy: the Crimean War and Plombières

In 1855 Piedmont joined Britain and France in the **Crimean War** against Russia and sent a force of about 18,000 men. It gained no territory, but Cavour was invited to the peace congress at Paris in 1856, where he was able to raise the "Italian question" and complain about Austria's role in Italy. Piedmont gained goodwill, especially from Britain and France.

**Napoleon III** of France was sympathetic to Italian nationalism, wanted to weaken Austria and hoped to extend French influence in Italy. In January 1858 an Italian nationalist, Felice Orsini, tried to kill him with bombs in Paris. Rather than turn against the Italian cause, Napoleon decided to act on it.

At **Plombières** in July 1858 Napoleon and Cavour secretly agreed:

- France would help Piedmont fight Austria, provided Austria could be made to appear the aggressor.
- Piedmont would gain Lombardy, Venetia and parts of central Italy, forming a Kingdom of Upper Italy.
- France would receive **Nice and Savoy**.
- Italy would become a confederation, with the pope as its president, and a central Italian kingdom would be created.
- Victor Emmanuel's daughter Clotilde would marry Napoleon's cousin.

The agreement was not about a united Italy. It would have left the south and Rome outside Piedmont's control.

## The war of 1859

Cavour now needed Austria to start the war. Piedmont mobilised its army and enrolled volunteers, among them a unit commanded by Garibaldi. Austria sent an ultimatum demanding that Piedmont disarm. Cavour refused, and Austria invaded at the end of April 1859, so France joined the war.

The French and Piedmontese won at **Magenta** (4 June) and entered Milan, then won the very bloody battle of **Solferino** (24 June). Then, on **11 July 1859**, Napoleon III suddenly agreed an armistice with Emperor Franz Joseph at **Villafranca**, without consulting Cavour:

- Lombardy would go to Piedmont (passed through France).
- Venetia would stay Austrian.
- The rulers of the central duchies would be restored.

Napoleon stopped because of heavy casualties, the strength of Austria's Quadrilateral fortresses, the risk that Prussia would attack France on the Rhine, and Catholic anger in France at revolts in the pope's lands. Cavour, furious, resigned.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how Piedmont gained central Italy"}
1. **The rulers fled:** during the war of 1859 the rulers of Tuscany, Parma and Modena left, and the Romagna rose against papal rule.
2. **Assemblies voted for union:** nationalists, helped by the National Society, set up governments that asked to join Piedmont.
3. **A deal with France:** Cavour, back in office from January 1860, offered Nice and Savoy so that Napoleon would accept the annexations.
4. **Plebiscites:** in March 1860 large majorities voted to join Piedmont, and Nice and Savoy went to France.
:::

## Did Cavour help or hinder unification?

Historians disagree, and you should be able to argue both sides.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: Cavour and Italian unification"}
**He helped:** he made Piedmont modern and respected, won a powerful ally in France, provoked the war that drove Austria out of Lombardy, and secured central Italy in 1860. Without French armies, Italians had already shown in 1848-49 that they could not beat Austria.
**He hindered, or aimed lower:** some historians argue that his aim was an enlarged northern kingdom, not a united Italy; the Plombières plan left the south and Rome outside Piedmont's control. He gave away Nice and Savoy, and he distrusted Garibaldi and the democrats.
**Judgement:** Cavour was an opportunist who did not plan the whole of unification, but his diplomacy created the situation in which it became possible.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Cavour planned a united Italy at Plombières"}
The Plombières plan was for a Kingdom of Upper Italy and an Italian confederation, not a single state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Piedmont gained Venetia in 1859"}
Villafranca left Venetia under Austria. Italy gained it only in 1866.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the price of French help"}
France received Nice and Savoy in 1860. Nice was Garibaldi's birthplace, which helps explain his anger with Cavour.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Victor Emmanuel II kept the Statuto, and Cavour, Prime Minister from 1852, modernised Piedmont's economy, army and finances.
- Piedmont's part in the Crimean War gave Cavour a voice at the Congress of Paris in 1856.
- At Plombières (July 1858) Napoleon III agreed to help Piedmont against Austria in return for Nice and Savoy.
- The war of 1859 (Magenta, Solferino) won Lombardy, but Villafranca left Venetia to Austria.
- Central Italy joined Piedmont by plebiscite in March 1860, at the cost of Nice and Savoy; historians debate whether Cavour planned unification.
:::
