## Introduction

Every year on 17 August southern African countries mark SADC Day. The Southern African Development Community links Zimbabwe with its neighbours in trade, transport, energy, food security and peace-making. It began in 1980 as the Southern African Development Co-ordination Conference (SADCC), created by newly independent states that wanted to escape economic dependence on apartheid South Africa. Zimbabwe, independent only weeks earlier, was a founding member.

You should already know about the Frontline States and Zimbabwe's independence in 1980. This lesson covers the **origins**, **members**, **aims** and **structure** of SADCC and SADC, SADC's **role in development**, and its **successes and failures**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regional integration"}
Closer co-operation between neighbouring countries in trade, transport, energy and politics, sometimes leading to shared institutions and free trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic dependence"}
Relying on another country for trade routes, ports, jobs, goods or power. In 1980 most southern African states depended heavily on South Africa.
:::

:::definition{term="Protocol"}
A legal agreement signed by SADC members on a particular area of co-operation, such as trade, energy or gender.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove most tariffs (import taxes) on goods traded between them.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and places to learn"}
- 1 April 1980: SADCC founded at Lusaka by nine states.
- 17 August 1992: SADC Treaty signed at Windhoek, Namibia.
- Headquarters: Gaborone, Botswana.
- South Africa joined SADC in 1994, after the end of apartheid.
- SADC Free Trade Area launched in 2008.
:::

## Origins: SADCC (1980)

The Frontline States had worked together politically against minority rule. In 1979, at a meeting in Arusha, Tanzania, their leaders discussed economic co-operation as well. On **1 April 1980**, in Lusaka, nine states signed the Lusaka Declaration, *Southern Africa: Toward Economic Liberation*, creating the **Southern African Development Co-ordination Conference (SADCC)**.

The nine founding members were **Angola, Botswana, Lesotho, Malawi, Mozambique, Swaziland (now Eswatini), Tanzania, Zambia and Zimbabwe**. Namibia joined on independence in 1990.

### Aims of SADCC

1. To reduce economic dependence, especially on apartheid South Africa.
2. To build regional links (transport, communications, energy) for real and fair integration.
3. To mobilise resources for national and regional development projects.
4. To win international support (aid and investment) for these aims.

SADCC was a loose conference, not a strong organisation. Each member co-ordinated a **sector**: for example Mozambique co-ordinated transport and communications, and Zimbabwe co-ordinated food security.

## From SADCC to SADC (1992)

By the early 1990s the situation had changed: Namibia was independent, apartheid was ending, and the Cold War was over. Members wanted a stronger, legally binding body that could promote trade and integration, not only co-ordinate projects. On **17 August 1992**, at Windhoek, the **SADC Treaty** created the **Southern African Development Community**.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1979 | Frontline leaders meet at Arusha to plan economic co-operation |
| 1 April 1980 | SADCC founded in Lusaka by nine states |
| 1990 | Namibia joins on independence |
| 17 August 1992 | SADC Treaty signed at Windhoek |
| 1994 | South Africa joins SADC |
| 1996 | SADC Organ on Politics, Defence and Security Co-operation set up |
| 2008 | SADC Free Trade Area launched; SADC mediation in Zimbabwe |

Membership has grown to sixteen states, including South Africa, Mauritius, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Madagascar, Seychelles and Comoros.

### Aims of SADC

- To promote economic growth and development and reduce poverty.
- To achieve regional integration, including free trade.
- To promote peace, security and democracy.
- To make the best use of shared resources, such as rivers, and protect the environment.
- To strengthen the links between the peoples and cultures of the region.
- To fight diseases such as HIV and AIDS.

## Structure in outline

| Organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Summit of Heads of State and Government | Supreme policy-making body; meets every year |
| Organ on Politics, Defence and Security Co-operation | Handles peace, security and conflict prevention |
| Council of Ministers | Oversees how policies are carried out |
| Secretariat (Gaborone) | Main administrative body, led by the Executive Secretary |
| SADC Tribunal | Court set up to settle disputes under the Treaty; suspended in 2010 and its role later reduced |
| National Committees | Link SADC with each member country |

## SADC's role in socio-economic development

- **Transport:** development corridors such as the Beira, Maputo and Nacala corridors gave landlocked states routes to the sea.
- **Energy:** the Southern African Power Pool (1995) lets countries share and trade electricity across national grids.
- **Trade:** the Free Trade Area (2008) removed most tariffs on goods traded between participating members.
- **Food security:** early-warning systems for drought and shared seed and grain policies.
- **Health:** joint programmes against HIV and AIDS, malaria and tuberculosis.
- **Peace and politics:** election observer missions, mediation and peace-keeping.

:::example{title="A development role explained: the Beira corridor"}
1. **The problem:** landlocked Zimbabwe, Zambia and Malawi depended on South African ports in the 1980s.
2. **The SADCC response:** the Beira corridor (railway, road and pipeline from Zimbabwe to the Mozambican port of Beira) was rebuilt as a priority project, with international funding.
3. **Security:** Zimbabwean troops protected the corridor from RENAMO attacks.
4. **Result:** members gained a shorter route to the sea that did not pass through South Africa, which reduced their dependence.
:::

## Successes and failures

### Successes

- Helped landlocked states reduce dependence on apartheid South Africa through corridors.
- Created lasting institutions, protocols and regular summits; a sense of regional identity.
- Energy and transport links (Power Pool, corridors) and a free trade area.
- Peace efforts: mediation in Lesotho, Madagascar and Zimbabwe (2008, leading to the Global Political Agreement), and the end of war in the DRC with others.
- Election observer missions and common principles for democratic elections.

### Failures and weaknesses

- Heavy dependence on foreign donors for funding projects in the early years.
- Slow implementation of protocols; members' poverty and debt.
- Unequal benefits: South Africa's large economy dominates trade.
- Divisions, for example over the DRC intervention of 1998.
- Overlapping membership of other bodies (such as COMESA and SACU) complicates trade rules.
- Critics argue that SADC has been reluctant to criticise member governments; the suspension of the Tribunal in 2010 was widely debated.

:::example{title="Weighing a judgement: has SADC been a success?"}
1. **For success:** SADCC achieved its main early aim, reducing dependence on apartheid South Africa, and SADC built lasting institutions, infrastructure and peace-making experience.
2. **Against:** integration is slow, poverty remains high, members depend on donors and on South Africa, and some decisions have been criticised.
3. **Judgement:** a fair answer might say SADC is a partial success: strongest in transport, energy and diplomacy, weakest in trade integration and holding members to agreed standards.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up SADCC and SADC"}
SADCC (Co-ordination **Conference**) was founded in Lusaka in 1980 to reduce dependence on apartheid South Africa. SADC (Development **Community**) replaced it at Windhoek in 1992, with a binding treaty and wider aims.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing South Africa as a founder"}
South Africa joined only in 1994, after the end of apartheid. SADCC was formed partly to reduce dependence on it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SADCC was founded at Lusaka on 1 April 1980 by nine states, including Zimbabwe, mainly to reduce dependence on apartheid South Africa.
- SADC replaced it at Windhoek on 17 August 1992 with a binding treaty; South Africa joined in 1994; headquarters are in Gaborone.
- The Summit, the Organ on Politics, Defence and Security, the Council of Ministers and the Secretariat are its main organs.
- SADC promotes transport corridors, the Power Pool, free trade, food security, health and peace-making.
- Successes include reduced dependence, infrastructure and mediation; weaknesses include donor dependence, slow integration and inequality between members.
:::
