## Introduction

On 25 May 1963 the leaders of 32 independent African states met in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and signed the Charter of the Organisation of African Unity (OAU). The day is still celebrated as Africa Day. The OAU played a major part in the liberation of southern Africa: its Liberation Committee helped fund and supply the movements that fought for Zimbabwe's independence. In 2002 it was replaced by the African Union (AU), which has wider powers.

You should already know the ideas of Pan-Africanism and the main events of decolonisation in the 1950s and 1960s. This lesson covers the **origins** and **aims** of the OAU, its **organs**, its **work**, **successes and failures**, and the **African Union**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pan-Africanism"}
The idea that Africans and people of African descent share a common history and interests and should unite politically and economically.
:::

:::definition{term="Sovereignty"}
The right of a state to govern itself without outside interference.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-interference"}
The principle that states should not intervene in the internal affairs of other states. It was central to the OAU Charter.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-indifference"}
The AU principle that Africa cannot ignore grave crimes inside a member state; the AU may intervene in cases of war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 25 May 1963: OAU founded at Addis Ababa by 32 states.
- 1963: Liberation Committee set up, based in Dar es Salaam.
- 1964: the Cairo summit accepts the borders inherited at independence.
- 1981: African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights adopted (in force 1986).
- 1999: Sirte Declaration calls for an African Union.
- 2002: the African Union is launched in Durban, South Africa.
:::

## Origins of the OAU

By the early 1960s most African states were independent, but they disagreed about how far to unite.

- The **Casablanca group** (including Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah, Guinea, Mali, Egypt and Morocco) wanted rapid political union, even a continental government.
- The **Monrovia group** (most other states, including Nigeria, Liberia and many former French colonies) wanted looser co-operation that respected each state's sovereignty.

Emperor Haile Selassie of Ethiopia hosted a conference at Addis Ababa in May 1963. The compromise was the OAU: a co-operative organisation of sovereign states, not a single government.

## Aims and principles

**Aims (from the Charter):**
1. To promote the unity and solidarity of African states.
2. To co-ordinate co-operation to achieve a better life for the peoples of Africa.
3. To defend their sovereignty, territorial integrity and independence.
4. To eradicate all forms of colonialism from Africa.
5. To promote international co-operation, having regard to the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

**Principles** included the sovereign equality of members, **non-interference** in internal affairs, respect for territorial integrity, peaceful settlement of disputes, condemnation of political assassination, dedication to the liberation of dependent territories, and non-alignment.

## Organs of the OAU

| Organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Assembly of Heads of State and Government | Supreme organ; met once a year to decide policy |
| Council of Ministers | Foreign ministers; prepared the Assembly's work and carried out its decisions |
| General Secretariat (Addis Ababa) | Administration, led by the Secretary-General |
| Commission of Mediation, Conciliation and Arbitration | Intended to settle disputes between members peacefully |
| Specialised commissions | For example economic and social, educational and cultural, health, defence |

## The work of the OAU

- **Political:** the **Liberation Committee** (Co-ordinating Committee for the Liberation of Africa, based in Dar es Salaam) gave money, weapons, training and diplomatic recognition to liberation movements in Rhodesia, Mozambique, Angola, Guinea-Bissau, Namibia and South Africa. The OAU led African pressure for UN sanctions on Rhodesia and South Africa.
- **Diplomatic:** it gave Africa a common voice at the UN and mediated some disputes, such as the border war between Algeria and Morocco (1963).
- **Economic:** it encouraged co-operation; the Lagos Plan of Action (1980) and the Abuja Treaty (1991) planned an African Economic Community.
- **Social:** refugee conventions, health and education commissions, and the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights.

:::example{title="The OAU and Zimbabwe's liberation, step by step"}
1. **Recognition:** the OAU recognised ZANU and ZAPU as liberation movements and refused to recognise UDI.
2. **Resources:** the Liberation Committee in Dar es Salaam channelled funds and supplies to them.
3. **Diplomacy:** OAU members pressed Britain and the UN for sanctions on Rhodesia.
4. **Result:** the struggle had continental backing, and the new Zimbabwe joined the OAU in 1980.
:::

## Successes and failures of the OAU

**Successes:**
- Helped bring an end to colonial rule, including in Zimbabwe (1980) and Namibia (1990), and to apartheid (1994).
- Gave Africa a united voice in world affairs.
- Kept the colonial borders, which prevented many border wars.
- Created the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights.

**Failures:**
- **Non-interference** meant it did little about civil wars and abuses inside states: the Nigerian civil war (1967-1970), Idi Amin's rule in Uganda (Amin even chaired the OAU in 1975-1976), and the Rwandan genocide (1994).
- It had **no army** and little power to enforce decisions.
- It was short of money because many members did not pay their contributions.
- Many coups and one-party states; critics called it a "club of heads of state".
- Divisions, such as over Western Sahara: Morocco left the OAU in 1984.

## The African Union

By the late 1990s many African leaders believed the OAU was too weak to deal with wars, poverty and globalisation. The **Sirte Declaration** (1999) called for a new union; the **Constitutive Act** was adopted at Lomé in 2000; and the **African Union** was launched at **Durban in July 2002**.

### Aims of the AU

- Greater unity and solidarity among African countries and peoples.
- Peace, security and stability on the continent.
- Democracy, good governance, human rights and the rule of law.
- Economic integration and sustainable development.
- A stronger voice for Africa in world affairs.

### Main organs of the AU

| Organ | Function |
|---|---|
| Assembly | Heads of State and Government; supreme organ |
| Executive Council | Ministers; co-ordinate policy |
| AU Commission (Addis Ababa) | The secretariat, led by a Chairperson |
| Pan-African Parliament (Midrand, South Africa) | Consultative body representing African peoples |
| Peace and Security Council | Decides on conflicts and peace-support operations |
| African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights | Rules on human rights cases |
| Economic, Social and Cultural Council | Gives a voice to civil society |

The biggest change was the AU's right to intervene in a member state in grave circumstances (war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity), moving from non-interference to **non-indifference**.

### Assessment of the AU

- **Achievements:** peace-support missions, for example in Darfur (Sudan) and Somalia; suspension of governments that seize power by coups; Agenda 2063; the African Continental Free Trade Area, under which trading began in 2021.
- **Problems:** dependence on outside funding for its budget and missions; slow implementation; reluctance to act against powerful members; continuing conflicts and unconstitutional changes of government.

:::example{title="Comparing the OAU and the AU"}
1. **Same:** both are unions of sovereign African states based in Addis Ababa, with an Assembly of Heads of State as the supreme organ.
2. **Different:** the OAU stressed non-interference; the AU allows intervention in grave circumstances.
3. **Different:** the AU has more organs, including a parliament, a court and a Peace and Security Council.
4. **Judgement:** the AU has stronger powers on paper, but like the OAU it is limited by money and by members' political will.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the AU was founded in 1963"}
The OAU was founded in 1963. The AU replaced it in 2002.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling non-interference a success without explanation"}
Non-interference protected new states' sovereignty, but it also stopped the OAU acting against abuses inside states. Show both sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The OAU was founded at Addis Ababa on 25 May 1963 by 32 states as a compromise between the Casablanca and Monrovia groups.
- Its aims were unity, sovereignty, ending colonialism, a better life and international co-operation; non-interference was a key principle.
- Its Liberation Committee in Dar es Salaam supported liberation movements, including Zimbabwe's.
- It failed to act on civil wars and abuses, lacked an army and money, and suffered divisions.
- The African Union (2002) has more organs and the right to intervene in grave circumstances, but faces funding and political limits.
:::
