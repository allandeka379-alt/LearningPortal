## Introduction

Zimbabwe's independence was won by its own people, but the liberation movements could not have fought without help from neighbouring countries. Guerrillas trained in camps in Tanzania, Zambia and Mozambique; refugees crossed into Botswana; and the leaders of these countries pushed for negotiations that led to Lancaster House. These neighbours became known as the **Frontline States** because they stood on the "front line" between independent Africa and the white minority governments of the south.

You should already know the main nationalist parties (ZANU and ZAPU) and the course of the armed struggle. This lesson covers the **members**, **aims** and **role** of the Frontline States in Zimbabwe's liberation, the **costs** they bore, and **other external support** in outline.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Frontline States (FLS)"}
An informal grouping of independent southern African states, formed in the mid-1970s, that co-ordinated support for liberation movements fighting minority rule in Rhodesia, Namibia and South Africa.
:::

:::definition{term="Liberation movement"}
A political and military organisation fighting to end colonial or minority rule, such as ZANU (with its army ZANLA) and ZAPU (with its army ZIPRA).
:::

:::definition{term="Détente"}
A relaxation of tension. In 1974-1975 the leaders of Zambia and South Africa tried to bring about talks between Smith and the nationalists.
:::

:::definition{term="Patriotic Front"}
The alliance of ZANU and ZAPU formed in 1976 for negotiations, encouraged by the Frontline States.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Members and leaders"}
- **Tanzania:** Julius Nyerere (chairman of the Frontline States).
- **Zambia:** Kenneth Kaunda.
- **Botswana:** Seretse Khama.
- **Mozambique:** Samora Machel (independent 25 June 1975).
- **Angola:** Agostinho Neto (independent 11 November 1975).
- **Zimbabwe** joined after independence in 1980.
:::

## Aims of the Frontline States

1. To end colonial and minority rule in Rhodesia, Namibia and South Africa.
2. To co-ordinate their support for the liberation movements, including bases, training and supplies.
3. To press for negotiations leading to majority rule, and to put pressure on Britain and the West.
4. To encourage unity among the liberation movements.
5. To reduce their own economic dependence on Rhodesia and South Africa.

## The role of the Frontline States in Zimbabwe's liberation

### Bases, training and transit

- **Tanzania** hosted training camps for guerrillas and the headquarters of the OAU Liberation Committee in Dar es Salaam.
- **Zambia** hosted ZAPU's headquarters in Lusaka and ZIPRA camps; ZANU was also based there until 1975.
- **Mozambique**, after independence in 1975, allowed ZANLA to use its territory for rear bases and refugee camps, which opened the long eastern border to infiltration and greatly expanded the war.
- **Botswana** took in refugees and recruits, many of whom passed through to Zambia.
- **Angola** hosted some ZIPRA training.

### Diplomacy and pressure in negotiations

- The Frontline leaders took part in the détente exercise of 1974-1975 and the Victoria Falls conference of 1975.
- They pressed ZANU and ZAPU to unite, leading to the **Patriotic Front** in 1976, and backed it at the Geneva Conference (1976).
- They lobbied the OAU, the UN, the Commonwealth and Western governments to refuse recognition of Rhodesia and the "internal settlement" of 1978.
- At the Commonwealth meeting in Lusaka in August 1979 Kaunda and Nyerere, with other leaders, helped persuade Britain to call an all-party conference.
- During the Lancaster House Conference, Machel and Kaunda urged the Patriotic Front leaders to accept the agreement rather than continue a war that was costing their countries dearly.

:::example{title="A role explained step by step: Mozambique's independence and the war"}
1. **Change:** Mozambique became independent under FRELIMO in June 1975.
2. **Opportunity:** FRELIMO allowed ZANLA to set up rear bases along Rhodesia's eastern border, which was over 1,000 km long.
3. **Effect on the war:** guerrillas could cross at many points, so the war spread across much of the country from 1976.
4. **Pressure:** in March 1976 Mozambique closed its border and applied sanctions, cutting Rhodesia's rail routes to Beira and Maputo.
5. **Result:** Rhodesia's economy and forces were stretched, which helped to force Smith towards negotiations.
:::

## The costs borne by the Frontline States

- **Military raids:** Rhodesian forces attacked camps in Mozambique and Zambia. At **Nyadzonia** (Mozambique, 9 August 1976), **Chimoio** (Mozambique, November 1977) and **Freedom Camp** (Zambia, October 1978) large numbers of refugees and recruits were killed. Bridges, roads and railways were also bombed.
- **Economic losses:** Zambia lost its cheapest trade routes through Rhodesia and had to use costly alternatives such as the TAZARA railway to Dar es Salaam (completed in 1975 with Chinese help). Mozambique lost transit revenue and income from migrant workers.
- **Refugees:** tens of thousands of Zimbabwean refugees had to be fed and housed.
- **Internal security:** Rhodesia and South Africa supported rebel groups, such as RENAMO in Mozambique, which caused great destruction.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how important were the Frontline States?"}
1. **View 1:** without bases, transit routes and diplomatic support, the guerrilla war could not have reached the scale it did by 1979.
2. **View 2:** the decisive factor was the commitment of the guerrillas and the support of rural people inside Zimbabwe; external help made this possible but did not replace it.
3. **A complication:** Frontline pressure sometimes pushed the liberation movements to accept terms, as at Lancaster House, that they would not have chosen.
4. **Judgement:** the Frontline States were essential partners, but independence came from the combination of the armed struggle inside the country, external support and international pressure.
:::

## Other external support in outline

- **The OAU Liberation Committee** (1963, Dar es Salaam) channelled money and supplies to recognised liberation movements.
- **The Soviet Union** and its allies supported ZAPU and ZIPRA with weapons and training.
- **China** supported ZANU and ZANLA with weapons and training.
- **Cuba** gave some training and was closely involved in Angola.
- **The United Nations** imposed mandatory sanctions on Rhodesia in 1966 and widened them in 1968, and refused to recognise the regime.
- Churches, the Commonwealth and anti-apartheid groups in many countries also gave aid and support.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing South Africa or Malawi as Frontline States"}
South Africa was the main minority-ruled state the Frontline States opposed. Malawi under Banda kept close ties with South Africa and was not a member.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up which country supported which army"}
China mainly supported ZANLA; the Soviet Union mainly supported ZIPRA. Zambia hosted ZAPU's headquarters; Mozambique hosted ZANLA's rear bases after 1975.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the raids in graphic detail"}
Give the place, date and the fact that many refugees and recruits were killed. Accurate, respectful statements earn the marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Frontline States (Tanzania, Zambia, Botswana, Mozambique, Angola; Zimbabwe from 1980) co-ordinated support for liberation in southern Africa.
- They provided bases, training, transit and refugee camps, and Mozambique's independence in 1975 transformed the war.
- They pushed for unity (the Patriotic Front), lobbied internationally and pressed both sides towards Lancaster House.
- They paid a heavy price: raids such as Nyadzonia, Chimoio and Freedom Camp, economic losses and destabilisation.
- The OAU Liberation Committee, the Soviet Union, China, Cuba and the UN also supported the struggle.
:::
