## Introduction

Imagine two accounts of the same football match: one written by a supporter of the winning team, one by the losing coach. Both were "there", yet they will not tell the same story. Historians face this problem with every source, whether it is a settler's letter from Bulawayo, a cartoon from a European newspaper or an elder's memory of the colonial period.

This lesson teaches the skills that turn a source into **evidence**. They are tested in the source-based question of ZIMSEC Paper 1 and throughout Cambridge IGCSE Paper 2, and they help in every essay. You should already know the kinds of source (archaeology, documents, oral tradition, pictures, buildings) and the difference between primary and secondary sources. Every source in the examples below has been **invented for practice**; real exam sources work in exactly the same way.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Provenance"}
The origin of a source: who produced it, when, where, for whom (the audience) and why (the purpose). It is usually given in the caption or introduction to the source.
:::

:::definition{term="Message"}
What the author of a source wants the audience to think or feel. A cartoon's message is the point the cartoonist is making.
:::

:::definition{term="Inference"}
Something you can work out from a source that it does not state directly.
:::

:::definition{term="Bias"}
A one-sided view that favours one person, group or opinion, often by leaving things out or exaggerating.
:::

:::definition{term="Reliability"}
How far a source can be trusted to give an accurate account of what happened.
:::

:::definition{term="Utility (usefulness)"}
How much a source can tell a historian about a particular question. Even an unreliable source can be useful, for example as evidence of what its author believed.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-referencing"}
Checking a source against other sources and against your own knowledge of the period.
:::

## The questions to ask of every source

Work through the source in two layers: its **content** (what it says or shows) and its **provenance** (who made it, when and why).

| Step | Question to ask | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Comprehension | What does the source say or show? | The surface information |
| 2. Inference and message | What can I work out from it? What does the author want me to think? | Deeper meaning |
| 3. Provenance and purpose | Who made it, when, for whom and why? | The reason it says what it says |
| 4. Cross-reference | Does it agree with my own knowledge and the other sources? | A test of accuracy |
| 5. Judgement | How reliable or useful is it for this question? How far do the sources support the claim? | Your conclusion |

### Comprehension and inference

A comprehension answer picks out information. An inference goes further. If a fictional settler's letter of 1891 complains that "no one can be found to work on the farms for less than ten shillings", you can infer that Africans were refusing low-paid labour, even though the letter never says so directly. Good inferences are **supported by a detail from the source**.

### Message and purpose

To find the message of a cartoon, describe what it shows and then say what point it is making. Purpose asks *why* the source was produced: to persuade voters, to justify an action, to win support from a government, to frighten, to inform. Link purpose to the **context**: why would someone want to say this at that time?

### Reliability and bias

Never say a source is reliable or unreliable "because it is primary" or "because it is biased" without explanation. Instead:

1. **Use the provenance.** Was the author in a position to know? Did they have a reason to exaggerate or hide something? Who was the audience?
2. **Check the content.** Is what it says supported by your own knowledge and by the other sources?

A biased source can still be **useful**: a speech full of exaggeration is excellent evidence of what the speaker wanted people to believe.

### Comparing two sources

Look for specific points of agreement **and** disagreement, quoting or describing details from both. Then explain *why* they differ, often because of different authors, dates or purposes.

### Reaching a judgement

"How far do these sources show…?" questions need both sides: the sources (or parts of them) that support the claim, the ones that challenge it, how reliable each is, and a conclusion.

:::keyfact{title="Remember NOP"}
For provenance, think **N**ature (what kind of source), **O**rigin (who, when, where) and **P**urpose (why, for whom). Then test the content against your own knowledge.
:::

## Using own knowledge: a checking timeline

Own knowledge lets you catch errors and exaggerations. Suppose an invented source dated 1888 talks about the town of Fort Salisbury. The timeline shows why it cannot be what it claims to be.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1887 | Grobler Treaty between Lobengula and the South African Republic (Transvaal) |
| February 1888 | Moffat Treaty |
| 30 October 1888 | Rudd Concession |
| 29 October 1889 | Royal Charter granted to the British South Africa Company |
| 12 September 1890 | The Pioneer Column raises the flag at Fort Salisbury |

Fort Salisbury was founded in 1890, so a source dated 1888 that mentions it is either misdated or not genuine. Checking dates, names and events against what you know is one of the simplest tests of reliability.

:::example{title="Analysing a written source"}
**Source X** (invented for this lesson): a letter from a fictional mining company official in Bulawayo to his directors in London, 1895.

> Everything here is peaceful and the native people are content. The country is rich in gold and we need only more money from shareholders to begin full production.

1. **Message:** the colony is calm and profitable, so investors should put in more money.
2. **Provenance:** a company official writing to his directors; he needs their money and his job depends on good news.
3. **Own knowledge:** in March 1896 the Ndebele rose against Company rule, and the Shona followed in June 1896. Africans had lost land and cattle and suffered forced labour and the hut tax, so "content" is very doubtful.
4. **Judgement:** unreliable as evidence of African feeling in 1895, but **useful** as evidence of how the Company presented the colony to investors.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing two sources (Cambridge Paper 2 style)"}
**Source Y** (invented): an editorial in a fictional British newspaper, 1919, calls the peace treaty with Germany "a just punishment that will keep Europe safe". **Source Z** (invented): a cartoon described in words, of the kind published in German newspapers in 1919, shows a German worker chained to a huge block marked "reparations".

1. **Agree:** both show that the treaty placed heavy burdens on Germany.
2. **Disagree:** Y thinks the burden is fair and makes Europe safer; Z presents it as unbearable and enslaving.
3. **Why they differ:** different nations and audiences. A British paper reflected public anger after the war; a German cartoon reflected German anger at a peace they had not negotiated.
4. **Judgement:** neither is neutral, but together they show how differently the two countries saw the same treaty.
:::

## How the two boards ask source questions

| Board and paper | Typical source questions |
|---|---|
| ZIMSEC Paper 1, source-based question | (a) comprehension from the sources; (b) inference, message or purpose; (c) reliability and usefulness, or "how far do the sources show…", using own knowledge |
| Cambridge IGCSE Paper 2 | Several sources on one prescribed topic; questions on message, comparison of two sources, purpose ("Why was this published at this time?"), reliability ("Are you surprised by…?", "How far does Source X prove Source Y wrong?") and a final "How far do these sources provide convincing evidence that…?" |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Copying out the source"}
Long quotations without comment earn little. Pick a short detail and explain what it shows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'biased, so useless'"}
Bias is a reason to be careful, not to throw a source away. Explain what the source *is* useful for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring provenance"}
The caption is part of the source. Who wrote it, when and why is often the key to the answer.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided comparisons"}
A comparison needs points of agreement and disagreement, each supported from both sources.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1 source question"}
For part (a), use the sources' own information briefly. For (b), state the message or inference and quote a supporting detail. For (c), judge reliability from provenance AND content checked against own knowledge, then conclude.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2"}
Answer the question asked: "How far does Source X prove Source Y wrong?" needs both agreement and disagreement and a judgement. "Why was this source published at this time?" needs context, message and purpose together.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the marks as a guide"}
A 2-mark inference needs one supported point; an 8- to 12-mark evaluation needs developed points on both sides and a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read a source for content (what it says) and provenance (who, when, why, for whom).
- An inference is worked out from the source and supported by a detail from it.
- Judge reliability from the author's position and purpose and by checking the content against own knowledge and other sources.
- A biased source can still be useful, especially as evidence of attitudes.
- Comparisons need specific agreement and disagreement, explained by differences in origin or purpose.
- "How far" questions need both sides and a supported judgement.
:::
