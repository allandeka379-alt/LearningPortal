## Introduction

After a war that killed millions, many people believed that the old system of alliances and secret diplomacy had failed. The answer, they hoped, was an international organisation in which countries would settle disputes by discussion and act together against any aggressor. That organisation was the **League of Nations**, which began work on 10 January 1920 with its headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland.

The League is worth studying for Zimbabwe and Africa too. Its **Mandates Commission** oversaw former German colonies such as Tanganyika and South West Africa, and its failure over Abyssinia (Ethiopia) in 1935-1936 showed Africans how little the great powers would do to protect an independent African state. The United Nations, which Zimbabwe joined at independence in 1980, was designed in 1945 to avoid the League's mistakes.

You should already know the aims of the peacemakers of 1919, especially Wilson's Fourteen Points.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Covenant"}
The League's founding agreement, written into all the peace treaties of 1919-1920. It set out the League's aims, organs and rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective security"}
The idea that all members act together against an aggressor, so that an attack on one is treated as an attack on all.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions"}
Penalties used to put pressure on an aggressor. The League's sanctions, in order, were moral condemnation, economic sanctions (stopping trade) and, as a last resort, military action by members.
:::

:::definition{term="Unanimity"}
The rule that decisions of the Assembly and the Council had to be agreed by all members present.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The League began work on 10 January 1920; headquarters in Geneva.
- 42 members at the start; the most at any time was 58, in the mid-1930s.
- The USA never joined.
- Germany joined in 1926 and left in 1933; the USSR joined in 1934 and was expelled in 1939.
- Japan announced its withdrawal in 1933 and Italy in 1937.
:::

## Aims of the League

The Covenant gave the League four broad aims:

1. **To discourage aggression** and keep the peace through collective security.
2. **To encourage disarmament.** Members promised to reduce their armaments.
3. **To encourage co-operation**, especially in business and trade.
4. **To improve living and working conditions** and to tackle social problems such as disease, slavery, the drugs trade and refugees.

## The structure of the League

| Organ | Who / how | Main functions |
|---|---|---|
| Assembly | Every member, one vote; met once a year | Discussed general policy, admitted members, voted the budget, elected non-permanent Council members |
| Council | Permanent members (at first Britain, France, Italy, Japan) plus elected members; met several times a year and in emergencies | Dealt with crises; could apply sanctions; each permanent member could block a decision |
| Secretariat | International civil servants in Geneva | Kept records, prepared reports, ran the administration |
| Permanent Court of International Justice | Judges in The Hague, Netherlands (from 1922) | Settled legal disputes between states and advised the League; could not enforce its rulings |
| International Labour Organisation (ILO) | Government, employer and worker representatives | Improved working conditions (hours, wages, child labour) |
| Commissions and committees | Specialists | Mandates, refugees, slavery, health, disarmament, drugs |

:::example{title="How the League was meant to stop a war: a step-by-step case"}
Imagine Country X invades Country Y, both League members.
1. Y appeals to the **Council**, which meets in emergency session.
2. The Council investigates, perhaps sending a commission, and may ask the **Permanent Court** for an opinion.
3. The Council orders X to withdraw. If X refuses, members condemn it (**moral sanction**).
4. Members stop trading with X (**economic sanctions**).
5. As a last resort, members provide troops (**military sanctions**).

The weak points are clear: every decision needed agreement, trade bans only worked if big trading nations took part, and the League had no army of its own.
:::

## Membership

Forty-two states joined at the start, most of them European or Latin American. Africa's only members in the early years were Liberia, Ethiopia (joined 1923) and the Union of South Africa, a British dominion. The Indian delegation was appointed by Britain.

Three great powers were missing at the start:

- **The USA**: the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, and many Americans favoured isolationism, so the country that proposed the League never joined.
- **Germany**: as a defeated power it was excluded until 1926.
- **The USSR**: the communist state was distrusted and excluded until 1934.

This left **Britain and France** as the leading powers. Both were weakened by the war, and each had its own priorities: Britain cared most about its empire and trade, while France was mainly concerned with security against Germany.

## Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths:**

- It was the first permanent world organisation for peace, with a clear set of rules.
- Its agencies brought experts together to tackle disease, refugees and working conditions.
- Small states had a voice in the Assembly.

**Weaknesses:**

- **Membership:** without the USA, economic sanctions were weak, because members could still trade with America.
- **No army:** military sanctions depended on members, who were reluctant to fight.
- **Unanimity:** decisions were slow and easily blocked.
- **Self-interest:** Britain and France often put national interests first.
- **Link to the treaties:** many Germans saw the League as part of the hated Versailles settlement.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: was failure inevitable?"}
**Yes:** some historians argue that a League without the USA, without an army and needing unanimous votes could never stand up to a determined great power.

**No:** others point out that the League had real successes in the 1920s, and that it failed in the 1930s mainly because of the Depression and the choices of Britain and France, not because of its structure alone.

**A balanced judgement:** the weaknesses made failure likely if a great power chose aggression, but the timing and scale of failure depended on events in the 1930s.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Assembly and the Council"}
The Assembly included every member and met once a year; the smaller Council dealt with crises and met more often.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the League had its own army"}
It had none. Military sanctions depended on members sending forces, which never happened in a major crisis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the League and the United Nations"}
The League (1920-1946) was replaced by the United Nations, founded on 24 October 1945. Keep the organs separate: the UN has a Security Council and a General Assembly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The League began on 10 January 1920 in Geneva to keep peace through collective security, disarmament, co-operation and social improvement.
- Its main organs were the Assembly, the Council, the Secretariat, the Permanent Court and agencies such as the ILO.
- Sanctions went from moral condemnation to economic sanctions to military action, but the League had no army.
- The USA never joined; Germany and the USSR joined late; Britain and France dominated.
- Historians debate whether these weaknesses made failure inevitable or whether the 1930s crises were decisive.
:::
