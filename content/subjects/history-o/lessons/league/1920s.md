## Introduction

In the 1920s the League of Nations faced its first tests. Some disputes between small states it settled quickly and fairly; others showed that a determined power could ignore it. Away from the headlines, its agencies quietly helped hundreds of thousands of refugees and prisoners of war, fought epidemics and pressed governments to improve working conditions and end slavery.

This record matters for Africa as well as Europe. The League's 1926 **Slavery Convention** committed members to end slavery, and in 1930 a League-backed commission of inquiry reported on forced labour in Liberia, one of the League's few African members.

You should already know how the League was organised and why the USA was not a member.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conference of Ambassadors"}
A body of ambassadors of the main Allied powers (Britain, France, Italy and Japan) set up to sort out problems arising from the peace treaties. It sometimes took decisions that overlapped with, or overrode, the League.
:::

:::definition{term="Plebiscite"}
A vote by the people of an area to decide a single question, such as which country they wish to belong to.
:::

:::definition{term="Repatriation"}
Returning people, such as prisoners of war or refugees, to their own country.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Disputes to learn"}
- **Vilna (1920):** Poland seized it from Lithuania; the League failed to make Poland leave.
- **Aaland Islands (1920-21):** Sweden and Finland accepted the League's ruling for Finland.
- **Corfu (1923):** Mussolini forced Greece to pay compensation; seen as a failure.
- **Bulgaria (1925):** Greece obeyed the League and withdrew; a success.
:::

## Peacekeeping in the 1920s

| Date | Dispute | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1920 | Vilna: Poland v. Lithuania | Failure: Poland kept Vilna |
| 1920-1921 | Aaland Islands: Sweden v. Finland | Success: islands to Finland, accepted by Sweden |
| 1923 | Corfu: Italy v. Greece | Failure: Greece paid compensation to Italy |
| 1925 | Bulgaria: Greece v. Bulgaria | Success: Greece withdrew and paid compensation |

### Vilna, 1920

Vilna (today's Vilnius) was made the capital of the new state of Lithuania, but most of its inhabitants were Poles or Jews, and few were Lithuanians. In October 1920 a Polish army under General Żeligowski seized the city. Lithuania appealed to the League, which asked Poland to withdraw. Poland refused. Britain and France, the powers that might have enforced a decision, were unwilling to act: France saw Poland as an ally against Germany, and Britain would not send troops alone. The city stayed Polish.

### The Aaland Islands, 1920-1921

The Aaland Islands lie between Sweden and Finland. Most islanders spoke Swedish and wanted to join Sweden, but the islands had been part of Finland. Both countries asked the League to decide. After an inquiry, the League ruled in 1921 that the islands should stay with Finland but be demilitarised and that the islanders' Swedish language and culture should be protected. Sweden accepted the decision.

### Corfu, 1923

In August 1923 an Italian general, Enrico Tellini, and members of his staff were murdered on Greek territory while marking the border between Greece and Albania. Mussolini blamed the Greek government, demanded compensation and then bombarded and occupied the Greek island of Corfu. Greece appealed to the League. The Council discussed the crisis, but Mussolini refused to accept its authority and insisted that the **Conference of Ambassadors** should settle the matter. It ordered Greece to apologise and pay compensation to Italy. Mussolini then withdrew from Corfu.

:::example{title="A case analysed: why was Corfu seen as a failure?"}
1. **Greece was the victim** of an armed attack on its territory, yet it ended up paying.
2. **The League was bypassed**: the decision was taken by the Conference of Ambassadors, a body of great-power ambassadors, under pressure from Mussolini.
3. **A great power got its way** by force. Small states learned that the League might not protect them against a great power.
4. **On the other hand**, Italy did leave Corfu within a few weeks, so the crisis was ended without a wider war.

Overall Corfu suggested that the League worked when the parties were small states, but not when a great power was determined to have its way.
:::

### Bulgaria, 1925

In October 1925 a Greek soldier was killed in an incident on the border with Bulgaria, and Greek troops invaded Bulgaria. Bulgaria appealed to the League, which condemned the invasion and ordered Greece to withdraw and pay compensation. Greece complied, though Greeks complained that one rule seemed to apply to small countries and another to great powers such as Italy.

## Humanitarian work

| Area | Body | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Prisoners of war and refugees | Refugee work led by Fridtjof Nansen | Helped return over 400,000 prisoners of war (an often quoted estimate); from 1922 issued "Nansen passports" to stateless refugees; helped refugees after the Greek-Turkish war of 1922 |
| Health | Health Organisation | Worked against epidemics such as typhus and malaria, collected health statistics; it was the forerunner of the World Health Organization |
| Working conditions | International Labour Organisation | Recommended an eight-hour day and 48-hour week (1919) and a ban on white lead in paint (1921); many members did not adopt the measures |
| Slavery | Slavery Commission | Slavery Convention of 1926; investigations of forced labour, including in Liberia (1930) |
| Economic help | Financial Committee | Arranged international loans to rescue Austria (1922) and Hungary (1923) |

:::example{title="Weighing importance: the humanitarian work"}
**Important:** this work changed real lives: prisoners of war got home, stateless people could travel and find work, and epidemic diseases were tracked across borders. Much of it continued after 1945 through the UN's agencies.

**Limited:** the League could only recommend. Members ignored ILO conventions they disliked, and colonial powers resisted outside inspection of forced labour in their own territories.

**Judgement:** the humanitarian work was arguably the League's most lasting success, even though it could not keep the peace.
:::

## How successful was the League in the 1920s?

**Successes:** the Aaland Islands and Bulgaria were settled peacefully; Austria and Hungary were rescued; refugees, prisoners and the sick were helped.

**Failures:** Vilna and Corfu showed that a determined state, especially a great power, could defy the League; disarmament made almost no progress.

**Who deserves the credit?** Some of the most important agreements of the decade were made outside the League: the **Locarno treaties** (1925), in which Germany accepted its western borders, and the **Kellogg-Briand Pact** (1928), in which states renounced war. This suggests that the great powers preferred to act on their own.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Muddling Corfu and Bulgaria"}
Both involved Greece. In Corfu (1923) Greece was attacked by Italy and made to pay; in Bulgaria (1925) Greece was the invader and was made to withdraw and pay.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every dispute a success"}
Vilna and Corfu were failures. A balanced answer gives both successes and failures and explains the pattern.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the humanitarian work"}
Questions on "how successful" the League was in the 1920s expect you to consider refugees, health, the ILO and slavery as well as disputes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): how successful was peacekeeping?"}
Take two successes and two failures, explain why each turned out as it did (who was involved, whether great powers were willing to act), and reach a judgement. The pattern of small states versus great powers is a strong line of argument.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2: evaluate the work of the League up to 1939"}
Split the answer into the 1920s and the 1930s. For the 1920s, give named disputes with dates and outcomes, then the agencies' work.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Vilna (1920) and Corfu (1923) were failures: Poland and Mussolini's Italy got their way.
- The Aaland Islands (1920-21) and Bulgaria (1925) were successes because the parties accepted the League's decisions.
- The League's agencies helped prisoners of war and refugees, fought disease, promoted better working conditions and opposed slavery.
- Important agreements such as Locarno (1925) and Kellogg-Briand (1928) were made outside the League.
- Overall the League worked best with small states and on humanitarian problems.
:::
