## Introduction

Before Zimbabwe's nationalist parties of the 1950s and 1960s, there was a wider movement that linked people of African descent across the world: **Pan-Africanism**. It began among Africans in the Americas, the Caribbean and Europe who had experienced slavery's legacy and racism, and it grew into a demand for the freedom of Africa itself. After the Second World War this idea was taken up by millions of ordinary Africans, producing **mass nationalism** and, within twenty years, the independence of most of the continent.

This topic is the background to Zimbabwe's own nationalist movements (studied separately). Examiners ask you to **define Pan-Africanism**, **describe the reasons for its rise**, **explain the roles of Du Bois and Garvey**, and **define mass nationalism and outline the factors that led to its rise**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pan-Africanism"}
The belief that people of African descent, in Africa and around the world, share a common history and destiny and should unite to end racism and colonial rule and to restore Africa's dignity and independence.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
The desire of a people to govern themselves in their own nation-state. In colonial Africa it meant the demand for independence from colonial rule.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass nationalism"}
Nationalism supported by large numbers of ordinary people (workers, peasants, women, youth and students), organised in mass parties that demanded immediate independence, as opposed to the earlier protests of small educated elites.
:::

:::definition{term="Diaspora"}
People of African descent living outside Africa, mainly as a result of the Atlantic slave trade.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- 1900: Pan-African Conference in London, organised by Henry Sylvester Williams.
- 1914: Marcus Garvey founds the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA).
- 1919: first Pan-African Congress, Paris, led by W. E. B. Du Bois.
- 1945: fifth Pan-African Congress, Manchester.
- 6 March 1957: Ghana becomes independent under Kwame Nkrumah.
- 25 May 1963: the Organisation of African Unity (OAU) is founded.
:::

## Why Pan-Africanism arose

- **Slavery and its legacy:** millions of Africans had been taken to the Americas; their descendants faced racism and segregation even after slavery ended.
- **Racism:** theories claiming that Africans were inferior were used to justify slavery, segregation and colonial rule. Pan-Africanists set out to disprove them.
- **Colonialism:** the Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference (1884-85) divided the continent among European powers, who took land, imposed taxes and forced labour, and excluded Africans from government.
- **Education:** Africans and people of African descent who studied in Europe and America met each other, read about liberty and rights, and saw the gap between these ideals and colonial practice.
- **Events that united people:** the Italian invasion of Ethiopia in 1935, one of Africa's two independent states, angered people of African descent everywhere and strengthened the movement.

## Leaders and congresses

### W. E. B. Du Bois

Du Bois was an African-American scholar and writer, often called the "father of Pan-Africanism". He:

- organised a series of **Pan-African Congresses** (1919, 1921, 1923, 1927 and 1945) that brought together people of African descent to discuss the condition of Africa;
- argued, through his writing and research, that people of African descent should have equal rights and that Africa's history deserved respect;
- helped found the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in the United States (1909), which fought segregation;
- chaired sessions of the Manchester Congress in 1945, linking the older movement to the new generation of African leaders. He later settled in Ghana, where he died in 1963.

### Marcus Garvey

Garvey, from Jamaica, built the largest mass movement of people of African descent in the early twentieth century. He:

- founded the **Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA)** in 1914; it grew into a mass organisation with branches in many countries;
- preached pride in being Black and in Africa, under the slogan **"Africa for the Africans"**;
- encouraged people of African descent to return to Africa (the "Back to Africa" movement) and set up the **Black Star Line** shipping company;
- spread his ideas through the newspaper *Negro World*, which was read (and often banned) in colonial Africa.

Garvey's businesses failed and he was deported from the United States, but his message of pride and self-reliance inspired later African nationalists.

:::example{title="Comparing two leaders: Du Bois and Garvey"}
Both men worked for the dignity and freedom of people of African descent, but in different ways. **Du Bois** was an intellectual who worked through conferences, research and petitions, seeking equal rights and gradual change. **Garvey** was a mass organiser who appealed to ordinary people with pride, economic self-help and the dream of returning to Africa. The two disagreed sharply with each other at the time. A good answer shows that the movement needed both: Du Bois gave it organisation and arguments, Garvey gave it mass appeal.
:::

### The Manchester Congress, 1945

The fifth Congress, held in Manchester in October 1945, was a turning point. It was organised by George Padmore and Kwame Nkrumah, and delegates included future leaders such as Jomo Kenyatta of Kenya, as well as trade unionists and students. Unlike earlier congresses, it:

- demanded **independence** for Africa, not just reform;
- was dominated by Africans from the continent rather than the diaspora;
- called for mass action by workers and peasants to win freedom.

### Kwame Nkrumah

Nkrumah returned to the Gold Coast in 1947 and founded the Convention People's Party (1949), a mass party that used strikes, boycotts and "positive action". When the Gold Coast became independent as **Ghana on 6 March 1957**, it was widely seen as the first colony in sub-Saharan Africa to win independence from colonial rule. Nkrumah declared that Ghana's independence was meaningless unless it was linked to the total liberation of Africa; he hosted Pan-African meetings in Accra in 1958, supported other nationalist movements and pushed for the African unity that led to the OAU in 1963.

## Why mass nationalism rose after 1945

1. **The Second World War:** many thousands of Africans served in the Allied armies in Africa, Asia and the Middle East. They saw Europeans killed and defeated, learned new skills and returned expecting rewards that did not come.
2. **The Atlantic Charter (1941) and the United Nations (1945):** both spoke of the right of peoples to choose their own government, which nationalists used against colonial rule.
3. **The weakness of Britain and France:** the war left them in debt, while the two new superpowers, the USA and USSR, both criticised colonial empires.
4. **Independence in Asia:** India and Pakistan became independent in 1947, showing that powerful empires could be forced to leave.
5. **Education and leadership:** a growing educated class, many trained in mission schools or abroad, led new parties.
6. **Urbanisation and trade unions:** growing towns brought people together, spread ideas and created workers' organisations that could strike.
7. **Colonial grievances:** land loss, low wages, taxes, racial discrimination and lack of political rights affected ordinary people, giving parties mass support.
8. **Ghana's example (1957):** the first success in sub-Saharan Africa inspired nationalists elsewhere, including in Zimbabwe.

:::example{title="A factor explained step by step: the Second World War"}
1. **Experience:** African soldiers fought alongside Europeans and saw them as ordinary people who could be beaten.
2. **Ideas:** the Allies said they were fighting for freedom and against racism.
3. **Disappointment:** after the war, veterans returned to the same colour bars, low wages and lack of rights.
4. **Result:** many joined or supported nationalist movements, which turned the language of wartime freedom against colonial rule.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Pan-Africanism with nationalism in one country"}
Pan-Africanism is about the unity and freedom of all people of African descent; nationalism is the demand for independence of a particular country. They supported each other but are not the same.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Du Bois and Garvey"}
Du Bois organised the Pan-African Congresses; Garvey founded the UNIA and the Black Star Line. Do not credit Garvey with the 1945 Manchester Congress.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without explaining them"}
For "outline the factors", short points may be enough, but for "explain" each factor must show how it encouraged nationalism.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Define precisely"}
A definition of Pan-Africanism should include unity of people of African descent, and the aims of ending racism and colonial rule. A definition of mass nationalism should include ordinary people and mass parties demanding independence.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the roles of leaders"}
When asked to explain the role of Du Bois or Garvey, say what they did (congresses, UNIA, newspapers) and why it mattered (spreading ideas, uniting people, inspiring later leaders).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pan-Africanism is the belief in the unity of people of African descent and the freedom of Africa; it grew from slavery's legacy, racism and colonialism.
- Du Bois organised Pan-African Congresses from 1919; Garvey's UNIA spread pride and the slogan "Africa for the Africans".
- The Manchester Congress (1945), organised by Padmore and Nkrumah, demanded independence and mass action.
- Mass nationalism rose after 1945 because of war experience, the Atlantic Charter and UN, weakened colonial powers, Indian independence, education, urbanisation and colonial grievances.
- Ghana's independence on 6 March 1957 under Nkrumah inspired nationalists across Africa, including Zimbabwe.
:::
