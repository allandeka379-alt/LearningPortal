## Introduction

On 11 November 1965 the Rhodesian Front government of Ian Smith declared Southern Rhodesia independent of Britain without British consent. This **Unilateral Declaration of Independence (UDI)** was meant to keep power in the hands of the white minority. No country formally recognised the new state, the United Nations imposed sanctions, and within a few years Rhodesia was fighting a full-scale liberation war.

You should already know why the Federation collapsed in 1963 and how African nationalist parties had been banned. Examiners ask you to **outline the reasons for UDI** and to **describe and analyse the policies and developments during UDI** and their impact on the indigenous people. The liberation war itself is a separate topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="UDI (Unilateral Declaration of Independence)"}
A declaration of independence made by one side alone. On 11 November 1965 the Rhodesian Front government declared independence without the agreement of Britain, the colonial power.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions"}
Penalties, usually economic (such as bans on trade, oil or investment), imposed to force a government to change its policy.
:::

:::definition{term="NIBMAR"}
"No Independence Before Majority African Rule": the British policy, adopted in December 1966, that Rhodesia would not be granted legal independence until there was majority rule.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions-busting"}
Getting round sanctions, for example by trading secretly through neighbouring countries or making goods locally instead of importing them.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1962: the Rhodesian Front wins the Southern Rhodesian election (December).
- April 1964: Ian Smith becomes Prime Minister.
- 11 November 1965: UDI.
- December 1966: talks on HMS *Tiger*; NIBMAR adopted.
- October 1968: talks on HMS *Fearless*.
- 1969: new constitution and Land Tenure Act.
- 2 March 1970: Rhodesia declares itself a republic.
- 1972: the Pearce Commission finds that Africans reject the Smith-Home proposals.
:::

## Reasons for UDI

1. **Fear of majority rule:** after Ghana (1957) and the rapid independence of much of Africa in 1960-1964, settlers feared that Britain would hand power to the African majority.
2. **British conditions:** Britain would grant independence only if there was clear progress towards majority rule (the "five principles"). The Rhodesian Front would not accept this.
3. **The end of the Federation:** Malawi and Zambia became independent under African governments in 1964, while Southern Rhodesia, the most developed territory, was refused independence. Settlers felt cheated.
4. **Protecting privilege:** settlers wanted to keep their land, jobs, farms and political control.
5. **Confidence:** Smith's government believed Britain would not use force, that South Africa and Portuguese-ruled Mozambique would help, and that sanctions would not work. It had the federal armed forces, and in May 1965 it won every European roll seat in a general election.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the end of the Federation pushed settlers towards UDI"}
1. **What happened:** the Federation was dissolved on 31 December 1963.
2. **The comparison:** Nyasaland and Northern Rhodesia became independent in 1964 under African majority governments.
3. **Settler reaction:** Southern Rhodesia's settlers, who had governed themselves since 1923, asked for independence too, but Britain insisted on progress to majority rule.
4. **Result:** settlers concluded that negotiation would never give them independence on their terms, which strengthened support for a unilateral declaration.
:::

## Reactions to UDI

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 11 November 1965 | UDI declared by Ian Smith's government |
| 1965-1966 | Britain declares UDI illegal and imposes sanctions, including an oil embargo |
| December 1966 | HMS *Tiger* talks fail; NIBMAR adopted; UN mandatory sanctions on key goods |
| 1968 | HMS *Fearless* talks fail; UN extends sanctions to almost all trade |
| 1969 | New constitution and Land Tenure Act approved |
| 1970 | Rhodesia declares itself a republic (2 March) |
| 1971 | Smith-Home proposals agreed by the two governments |
| 1972 | Pearce Commission reports that Africans reject the proposals |

### Britain

The Labour government of Harold Wilson declared UDI illegal but **ruled out the use of force**. Instead it relied on economic sanctions, including an oil embargo; the Royal Navy patrolled off the Mozambican port of Beira to stop oil tankers. Wilson met Smith twice, on HMS *Tiger* (1966) and HMS *Fearless* (1968), but both talks failed because Smith would not accept guarantees of progress to majority rule. In 1971 a later British government agreed new proposals with Smith (the Smith-Home proposals), on condition that they were acceptable to the people as a whole.

### The Pearce Commission (1972)

A British commission led by Lord Pearce toured the country in early 1972 to test opinion. The newly formed **African National Council**, led by Bishop Abel Muzorewa, organised a campaign against the proposals. The Commission reported that most Africans rejected them, so the settlement collapsed.

### The international community

- The **United Nations** called on states not to recognise Rhodesia and in 1966 imposed its first ever mandatory economic sanctions, widened in 1968.
- The **OAU** condemned UDI and supported the nationalists.
- **Zambia** suffered heavily because its trade routes ran through Rhodesia.
- **South Africa** and **Portugal** (which ruled Mozambique until 1975) did not apply sanctions, and much trade passed through them.

## Policies and developments during UDI

- **The 1969 constitution:** approved by a mainly white electorate, it gave Europeans 50 seats in the House of Assembly and Africans 16 (half directly elected, half chosen through chiefs). African representation could rise only as African income-tax payments grew, and could never exceed parity, so majority rule was ruled out.
- **The Land Tenure Act (1969):** replaced the Land Apportionment Act and divided the land into roughly equal European and African areas, even though Africans outnumbered Europeans by more than twenty to one. The African reserves were now called Tribal Trust Lands.
- **The republic (1970):** Rhodesia cut its last link with the British Crown; no state formally recognised it.
- **Sanctions-busting:** goods and oil passed through South Africa and Mozambique; exports such as tobacco were sold secretly; local factories made goods that had been imported (import substitution), so manufacturing grew. The United States bought Rhodesian chrome for some years after 1971.
- **Security laws:** nationalist leaders remained in detention or restriction, parties were banned, the press was censored and states of emergency were renewed.
- **Protected villages:** from about 1973 the government moved rural people in war zones into fenced, guarded villages to cut them off from guerrillas.

:::example{title="Analysing an interpretation: did sanctions fail?"}
Some writers argue that sanctions failed, because the Rhodesian economy grew in the late 1960s and early 1970s and UDI lasted fourteen years. Others point out that sanctions raised costs, limited investment and foreign currency, and isolated Rhodesia, so that when the war intensified and Mozambique became independent (1975) the economy could not cope. A balanced judgement: sanctions did not force Smith to give in quickly, but combined with the armed struggle they helped to weaken the regime by the late 1970s.
:::

## Impact on the indigenous people

- **Political exclusion:** the 1969 constitution blocked majority rule; African leaders were detained or in exile.
- **Land:** the Land Tenure Act kept Africans on about half the land, much of it poor; Tribal Trust Lands became more crowded.
- **Economy:** most new jobs and the benefits of import substitution went to Europeans; African wages stayed low and rural poverty grew.
- **Repression:** curfews, collective fines, detentions and, in war zones, protected villages disrupted family life and farming.
- **Resistance:** these conditions drove many young people to join the liberation movements.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking Britain used force against UDI"}
Britain ruled out military force from the start and relied on sanctions and negotiations.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the African National Council with the old ANC"}
The African National Council (1971), led by Bishop Muzorewa, campaigned against the Smith-Home proposals. The Southern Rhodesia African National Congress was a different, earlier party (1957-1959).
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about the war in a UDI question"}
UDI questions are about the declaration, reactions and policies. Mention the armed struggle only as a consequence or as context.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1: outline, describe, assess"}
"Outline the reasons for UDI" needs several short, accurate reasons. "Assess the impact of UDI policies on the indigenous people" needs each policy linked to its effect, with a judgement about which effects mattered most.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Name the talks correctly"}
Learn the sequence: *Tiger* (1966), *Fearless* (1968), Smith-Home proposals (1971), Pearce Commission (1972). Each failed because the African majority or Britain would not accept terms that delayed majority rule.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ian Smith's Rhodesian Front declared UDI on 11 November 1965 to prevent majority rule.
- Britain declared UDI illegal, refused to use force and relied on sanctions; the UN imposed mandatory sanctions from 1966.
- Talks on HMS *Tiger* (1966) and HMS *Fearless* (1968) failed; NIBMAR was adopted; the Pearce Commission (1972) found Africans rejected the Smith-Home proposals.
- The 1969 constitution, Land Tenure Act and 1970 republic entrenched minority rule; sanctions-busting kept the economy going for years.
- Africans faced exclusion, land shortage, repression and protected villages, which pushed many towards the armed struggle.
:::
