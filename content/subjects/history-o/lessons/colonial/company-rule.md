## Introduction

For its first 33 years the colony was governed not by a government but by a business: the British South Africa Company (BSAC). The Company had to make a profit for shareholders while running a country, and its policies on land, tax and labour shaped Zimbabwe for the rest of the colonial period. Many features you will meet later, such as reserves, migrant labour and a settler parliament, began under Company rule.

You should already know how the Company gained its Royal Charter (1889), occupied Mashonaland (1890), conquered Matabeleland (1893-94) and defeated the risings of 1896-97. Examiners ask you to **describe Company rule**, **outline its policies** and **analyse why it ended**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chartered company"}
A private company given a charter by the British Crown to govern territory on Britain's behalf. The BSAC's charter of 29 October 1889 allowed it to make laws, keep a police force and grant land.
:::

:::definition{term="Administrator"}
The Company's chief official in the colony, in charge of government. Leander Starr Jameson held the post from 1891 to 1896.
:::

:::definition{term="Land alienation"}
The taking of land from African communities for settlers, companies or the state.
:::

:::definition{term="Chibaro"}
Forced or contract labour, especially recruited labour on mines and farms at low wages under harsh conditions.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key dates"}
- 1894: hut tax introduced; Land Commission creates the Gwaai and Shangani reserves.
- 1898: Southern Rhodesia Order in Council creates a Legislative Council and a Resident Commissioner.
- 1903: Rhodesia Native Labour Bureau set up to recruit workers.
- 1918: the Privy Council rules that unalienated land belongs to the Crown, not the Company.
- 27 October 1922: referendum; settlers choose Responsible Government rather than union with South Africa.
- 1 October 1923: Responsible Government begins; Company rule ends.
:::

## How the Company governed

### Political structure

At first the Administrator ruled with a small council of officials. After the risings of 1896-97 the British government insisted on closer control. The **Southern Rhodesia Order in Council of 1898** created:

- a **Legislative Council** with members nominated by the Company and members elected by settlers (Africans had almost no votes because of income and property qualifications);
- a **Resident Commissioner**, a British official who watched over the Company, especially its treatment of Africans;
- a **Native Affairs Department**, with Native Commissioners in each district who collected tax, recruited labour, heard cases and controlled chiefs.

Over time the elected settler members gained a majority in the Legislative Council, so settlers increasingly shaped policy.

### Economic policies

**Mining.** The Company first hoped for great gold mines. When these failed to appear, it allowed many small workers to mine gold, charged royalties, and developed coal at Wankie (Hwange).

**Settler farming.** From about 1908 the Company turned to promoting white farming: it sold land cheaply, advertised for settlers, and supported tobacco and maize. Railways (to Bulawayo in 1897 and to Salisbury from Beira by 1899) linked mines and farms to the coast.

**Land.** Land alienation continued. Africans were pushed into **native reserves**, often on poorer, drier land far from railways and markets. A commission in 1914-15 fixed reserve boundaries and cut some land from them. Africans living on settler-owned land had to pay rent or provide labour.

**Tax and labour.** The **hut tax** (1894), later replaced by a tax on adult men, forced Africans to earn cash. Because local Africans preferred to grow and sell their own crops or to seek better pay in South Africa, the Company and employers recruited workers from Nyasaland, Northern Rhodesia and Mozambique through the **Rhodesia Native Labour Bureau** (1903). Wages were low and conditions in mine compounds were often dangerous. Laws such as the Masters and Servants Ordinance made it a crime for a worker to break a contract.

### Social policies

- **Segregation:** Africans and Europeans lived in separate areas; towns had separate "locations" for African workers, and pass laws controlled movement.
- **Education:** left mainly to missionaries; the Company spent very little on African schooling.
- **Health:** hospitals served mainly settlers; African workers suffered from disease in compounds.

:::example{title="A policy analysed: why the Company used tax to obtain labour"}
1. **The problem:** settlers and mines needed many cheap workers, but most Africans could feed themselves and sell surplus grain.
2. **The policy:** a cash tax had to be paid each year, in money.
3. **The effect:** to find the money, men had to sell crops or, increasingly, go to work for settlers.
4. **The link to land:** as land was alienated and reserves became crowded, selling crops became harder and wage labour became necessary. Tax and land policy worked together to create a labour supply.
:::

## African responses

- **Armed resistance:** the risings of 1896-97 and later local resistance such as that of **Mapondera** in the north-east in the early 1900s.
- **Peasant production:** many Africans sold maize and other crops to mines and towns, which allowed them to pay tax without wage labour, until competition from settler farmers and rents made this harder.
- **Choosing employers:** workers avoided the worst mines and went where pay was better, including the South African mines.
- **Religion and education:** many joined mission churches and schools; others formed independent churches.
- **Petitions:** some Ndebele leaders, including Lobengula's son Nyamanda, campaigned around 1919 for a national homeland, appealing to the British Crown.

## Why Company rule ended

1. **Financial problems:** the Company never found a second Rand, and governing the colony cost money. It paid no dividend to shareholders while it ruled, so it wanted to be rid of the costs of government.
2. **Settler pressure:** settlers resented being ruled by a profit-making company that also owned land, railways and mineral rights. The elected members of the Legislative Council campaigned for self-government, led by **Charles Coghlan**.
3. **The land case (1918):** the Privy Council ruled that unalienated land belonged to the Crown, not the Company, weakening the Company's position.
4. **British policy:** Britain did not want to pay to govern the colony; the charter was extended in 1914 for ten years with the expectation of change.
5. **The 1922 referendum:** settlers voted between joining the **Union of South Africa** and **Responsible Government**. A clear majority (about three-fifths) chose Responsible Government, partly from fear of Afrikaner influence and of losing their own control.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes: economic or political?"}
Some explanations stress the Company's wish to escape the costs of government; others stress settler demands for self-rule. They are linked: settlers could gain self-government only because the Company and Britain were willing to let go. A good answer explains each and then judges which made the change happen in 1923. Notice that Africans had no say in the referendum, which was decided by an almost entirely white electorate.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the 1922 referendum gave independence"}
Responsible Government was self-government for settlers inside the British Empire, with Britain keeping some reserved powers. It was not independence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing policies without their effects"}
Questions on Company rule often ask for the impact on Africans. Always link each policy (tax, reserves, labour recruitment) to what it meant for African communities.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the 1898 Order in Council and the 1923 constitution"}
1898 created the Legislative Council and Resident Commissioner under the Company; 1923 replaced Company rule with a settler government and parliament.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1: describe and analyse"}
"Describe Company rule" needs the institutions (Administrator, Legislative Council, Resident Commissioner, Native Commissioners) and policies. "Analyse the factors that led to the end of Company rule" needs each factor explained and linked: finance, settler pressure, the land case, British policy and the referendum.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How far questions"}
For "How far were financial problems the reason Company rule ended?", explain the Company's finances first, then the settler, legal and British factors, and finish with a judgement that weighs them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The BSAC governed Southern Rhodesia from 1890 to 1923 through an Administrator, a Legislative Council (from 1898) and a Native Affairs Department.
- Policies of land alienation, reserves, hut tax and labour recruitment served mines and settler farms and harmed Africans.
- Africans responded through resistance, peasant production, choosing employers, churches, schools and petitions.
- Company rule ended because of financial losses, settler pressure, the 1918 land judgment and British unwillingness to govern directly.
- In the 1922 referendum settlers chose Responsible Government, which began on 1 October 1923.
:::
