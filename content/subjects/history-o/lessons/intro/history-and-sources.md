## Introduction

Ask an elder in your family where your great-grandparents lived, what crops they grew or what happened to them during the colonial period, and you are doing history. Ask how we know that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona, and you are asking the historian's most important question: **what is the evidence?**

This lesson is the foundation for the whole course. Every later topic, from the Stone Age to independence and from the First World War to the Cold War, depends on sources. You need no earlier knowledge: just curiosity and a habit of asking "How do we know?". In ZIMSEC Paper 1 this topic appears under "Conceptualisation of History", and it prepares you for the source-based question.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="History"}
The study of past human activities and events, based on evidence, which explains what happened, why it happened and what difference it made.
:::

:::definition{term="Source"}
Anything that gives us information about the past: an object, a document, a story passed down by word of mouth, a picture or a building.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary source"}
A source produced at the time of the events or by someone who took part in them or saw them, such as a diary, a letter, a treaty, a photograph or a tool found on a site.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary source"}
A source produced later by someone who was not there, usually by studying primary sources, such as a textbook or a historian's book.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral tradition"}
Knowledge of the past passed down by word of mouth from one generation to the next: stories, praise poetry, songs, proverbs and family genealogies.
:::

:::definition{term="Archaeology"}
The study of the human past through the material remains people left behind: tools, pottery, bones, buildings and other objects found on sites, often by excavation.
:::

## Why we study history

History is not just a list of dates. Studying it helps us to:

- **understand the present**, because today's land question, borders and institutions all have roots in the past;
- **know who we are**, by learning about our ancestors, our culture and our national heritage;
- **learn from the past**, so that mistakes such as discrimination or war are understood and, we hope, not repeated;
- **develop skills** such as weighing evidence, detecting bias, arguing a case and writing clearly, which are useful in law, journalism, teaching and many other careers;
- **become responsible citizens** who understand rights, government and the struggles that shaped the country.

## Types of history

Historians often divide the past into types according to the part of life they study.

| Type of history | What it studies | A Zimbabwean example |
|---|---|---|
| Political | Government, rulers, laws, wars and power | The Mutapa kings and their councils |
| Economic | How people produced, traded and earned a living | The gold and ivory trade of Great Zimbabwe |
| Social | Everyday life, families, education, health, religion | Village life in Early Iron Age farming communities |
| Local | The past of one place: a town, district or school | The history of Masvingo or of your own school |
| Family | The past of one family across generations | A family tree traced through the totem and elders |

The types overlap. The rise of Great Zimbabwe, for example, is political (leadership), economic (trade) and social (who lived on the hill and who lived below).

## The main sources of history

### Archaeology and artefacts

Archaeologists excavate sites and study objects such as stone tools, pottery, iron hoes, glass beads and bones. Methods such as radiocarbon dating give approximate dates for organic material such as charcoal.

- **Advantages:** archaeology gives evidence for periods and peoples who left no writing, such as Stone Age hunters and Iron Age farmers; objects do not tell lies on purpose.
- **Disadvantages:** excavation is expensive and slow; objects must be interpreted and do not tell us people's beliefs or names directly; much has decayed or been destroyed, including by careless digging in the past.

### Written documents

These include letters, diaries, treaties, government records, newspapers, travellers' accounts and books. The National Archives of Zimbabwe in Harare keeps many of them.

- **Advantages:** they can give exact dates, names and the views of people at the time; many can be checked against one another.
- **Disadvantages:** most of Zimbabwe's past before the sixteenth century has no written record; early documents were mostly written by outsiders (Portuguese traders, missionaries, colonial officials), who often misunderstood African societies or wrote to serve their own interests; documents can be lost, forged or one-sided.

### Oral tradition

Oral tradition is carried by elders, spirit mediums, praise singers and families. It includes the traditions about Nyatsimba Mutota and the founding of the Mutapa state.

- **Advantages:** it preserves the African point of view and the history of societies without writing; it is cheap to collect and keeps culture alive.
- **Disadvantages:** details can change as stories are retold; dates are hard to fix; stories may exaggerate the deeds of a ruling family; knowledge is lost when elders die before it is recorded.

### Pictures, photographs and films

Rock paintings by San hunter-gatherers, drawings by travellers, and photographs from the late nineteenth century onwards all show the past visually.

- **Advantages:** they show what people, places and events looked like.
- **Disadvantages:** pictures can be staged, selected, edited or painted from imagination; a photograph shows one moment only and the photographer chose what to include.

### Buildings and sites

Great Zimbabwe, Khami, Naletale and the colonial forts are evidence in themselves.

- **Advantages:** they show skills, wealth, organisation and the size of a society.
- **Disadvantages:** they are often damaged or rebuilt, and they need other sources to explain who used them and how.

:::keyfact{title="Learn these"}
The five groups of sources to learn: archaeology and artefacts; written documents; oral tradition; pictures and photographs; buildings and sites. Every source has advantages and disadvantages: historians use several together.
:::

## Building knowledge from several sources

No single source is enough. Historians **cross-check** one type of source against another. The table shows how evidence about Great Zimbabwe built up over time.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| c. 1100s–1400s CE | Great Zimbabwe is built and occupied; the stones, pottery and trade goods left behind are archaeological evidence |
| 1500s | Portuguese writers record second-hand accounts, from traders, of a great stone building inland: a written source by outsiders |
| 1871 | The German geologist Karl Mauch visits the site and wrongly links it to the Queen of Sheba |
| 1905 | The archaeologist David Randall-MacIver excavates and concludes that the builders were African |
| 1929 | Gertrude Caton-Thompson's excavations confirm the African origin of the site |
| From the 1950s | Radiocarbon dating of material from the site supports the dates found by excavation |

:::example{title="Why oral tradition and archaeology work best together"}
Shona oral traditions say that Nyatsimba Mutota moved north from the Great Zimbabwe area to found the Mutapa state. On its own, this is a story whose details may have changed over centuries.

1. **Archaeology** shows that Great Zimbabwe declined in the fifteenth century and that stone-walled sites appeared further north.
2. **Written sources** by Portuguese visitors in the sixteenth century describe a powerful kingdom in the north, ruled by the Mutapa.
3. Because three different kinds of source point the same way, historians accept the broad outline (a shift of power northwards in the fifteenth century), while treating the personal details of the tradition with care.
:::

:::example{title="Primary or secondary? It depends on the question"}
A history textbook written in 1975 about the Pioneer Column is a **secondary** source about 1890. But if you are studying how schools in Rhodesia taught history in the 1970s, the same textbook is a **primary** source, because it was produced at the time you are studying.

The lesson: a source is primary or secondary in relation to the topic being investigated, not by its type alone.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking primary sources are always reliable"}
A primary source can be biased, mistaken or written to persuade. A settler's letter from 1896 is primary, but it gives one side. Being close to the event is useful; it is not the same as being accurate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Africa had no history before writing"}
Societies without writing still have history. Archaeology, oral tradition, rock art and buildings are all evidence. Great Zimbabwe was studied mainly from archaeology and oral tradition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages without disadvantages"}
Questions usually ask for both. A list of five advantages of oral tradition with no weaknesses gains only part of the marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- History is the study of past human activities and events based on evidence.
- We study it to understand the present, our identity and heritage, to learn from the past and to build skills.
- Types of history include political, economic, social, local and family history.
- The main sources are archaeology and artefacts, written documents, oral tradition, pictures and photographs, and buildings and sites; each has advantages and disadvantages.
- Primary sources come from the time of the events; secondary sources are produced later, often from primary sources.
- Historians cross-check different kinds of source to reach reliable conclusions.
:::
