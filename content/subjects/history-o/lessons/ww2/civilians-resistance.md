## Introduction

In the Second World War civilians were in the front line. Cities were bombed from the air, whole countries lived under foreign occupation, and ordinary people had to decide whether to obey, collaborate or resist. Far more civilians died in this war than in the First World War.

For southern Africa the war meant rationing, shortages of imported goods, African and European men recruited into the forces, and new demands on African labour; it also brought news of a world in which empires were under strain.

This lesson covers part of Cambridge Depth study E, key question 3: **the impact of bombing**, **Japanese control in Malaya and Singapore** and **resistance in Malaya and France**. Nazi rule in occupied Poland and the Holocaust are studied in a separate lesson.

You should already know the course of the war in Europe to 1943 and Japan's conquest of Malaya in 1941-42.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="The Blitz"}
The German bombing campaign against British cities from September 1940 to May 1941.
:::

:::definition{term="Area bombing"}
Bombing a whole urban area, rather than a precise military target, to destroy industry, housing and the morale of the workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Collaboration"}
Working with an occupying enemy, for example by serving in its administration or police, or by informing on neighbours.
:::

:::definition{term="Resistance"}
Opposition to an occupying power, from refusing to co-operate and printing secret newspapers to intelligence work, sabotage and armed attacks.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}

:::

## Bombing: the Blitz, 1940-41

From **7 September 1940** the Luftwaffe bombed London on almost every night for two months, and then attacked other cities and ports, including **Coventry** (14 November 1940), Birmingham, Liverpool, Glasgow, Plymouth, Hull and Belfast. The Blitz ended in May 1941, when Germany turned east. Historians estimate that about 40,000 or more British civilians were killed, and over a million homes in London alone were damaged or destroyed.

**Effects on daily life:**

- **Shelters:** families used Anderson shelters in gardens, later Morrison shelters indoors, and public shelters; thousands slept in London Underground stations.
- **The blackout:** no lights could show at night, which led to many road accidents.
- **Evacuation:** about 1.5 million people, mainly children, had been evacuated from cities in September 1939; many more left when the bombing began.
- **Volunteers:** air-raid wardens, fire-watchers and rescue workers, many of them women.

**Morale:** the government's propaganda presented a "Blitz spirit" of calm determination. Morale did not collapse, and production continued. But there was also fear, exhaustion, looting and anger at poor shelters in working-class areas. Censorship meant that newspapers and newsreels showed the most positive picture.

## Bombing Germany, 1943-45

From 1942 RAF Bomber Command, led by **Arthur Harris**, adopted **area bombing** of German cities at night; from 1943 the US air force bombed by day, aiming at factories and transport. In July-August 1943 raids on **Hamburg** created a firestorm that killed tens of thousands of people; in February 1945 **Dresden** was devastated. Estimates of German civilian deaths from bombing range from about 350,000 to over half a million.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: how effective was the bombing of Germany?"}
**Effective:** it forced Germany to use huge numbers of guns, fighters and men to defend its cities instead of fighting at the front; attacks on oil plants and railways in 1944-45 caused severe fuel and transport shortages.

**Less effective than hoped:** German war production actually rose until late 1944, and morale did not break, just as British morale had not broken in the Blitz. Bomber Command also lost about 55,000 aircrew.

**A moral debate:** some, then and since, have argued that area bombing of civilians, especially Dresden late in the war, could not be justified; others argue that it shortened a war of national survival.
:::

## Japanese control: Malaya and Singapore

After February 1942 Singapore was renamed **Syonan-to** ("Light of the South").

- **The Chinese** were treated most harshly because many had supported China against Japan. In the **Sook Ching** operation of February-March 1942, Chinese men were screened and many were taken away and killed; estimates range from several thousand (the figure later admitted by Japan) to tens of thousands.
- **Malays and Indians** were treated less harshly at first. Japan encouraged Indian nationalism: the **Indian National Army**, led from 1943 by **Subhas Chandra Bose**, was recruited partly from Indian prisoners of war.
- **The Kempeitai** (military police) used arrests, torture and informers to control the population.
- **Economic hardship:** trade collapsed, food was scarce and people grew tapioca and sweet potatoes; the occupation currency, nicknamed "banana money", lost its value through inflation.
- **Forced labour:** tens of thousands of Asian labourers and Allied prisoners were sent to build the Burma-Thailand railway, where very many died of disease, hunger and ill-treatment.
- **Propaganda:** Japanese language and customs were taught in schools, and Japan presented itself as liberating Asia from Western rule.

## Resistance: Malaya and France

### Malaya

The **Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army** (MPAJA), formed in 1942, was mainly Chinese and led by the Malayan Communist Party. From jungle camps it gathered intelligence, attacked collaborators and small Japanese posts, and carried out sabotage. From 1943 British agents of **Force 136** made contact and later supplied weapons and training. Its military effect was limited, and Japan surrendered before a planned Allied invasion of Malaya. But it kept resistance alive and became politically important: after the war many of its members became the core of the communist insurgency that began in 1948.

### France

French resistance grew from small beginnings:

- **De Gaulle's Free French** in London (from June 1940) kept France in the war and broadcast to occupied France.
- **Internal resistance groups** printed underground newspapers, gathered intelligence for Britain, and ran escape lines for Allied airmen. The communists joined in large numbers after Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941.
- **Jean Moulin**, sent by de Gaulle, united the main groups in the National Council of the Resistance (May 1943); he was arrested in June 1943 and died after torture.
- The **Maquis** grew in the countryside when young men fled forced labour in Germany (from 1943).
- In June 1944 the resistance sabotaged railways and telephone lines to delay German reinforcements heading to Normandy, and in August 1944 an uprising in Paris helped liberate the city.

The Germans responded with brutal reprisals: on 10 June 1944 an SS unit killed about 640 inhabitants of the village of **Oradour-sur-Glane**.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: how effective was the French Resistance?"}
**Effective:** its intelligence and sabotage helped D-Day succeed; it rescued airmen; it gave the French self-respect and a role in their own liberation.

**Limited:** until 1943 it was small and divided; most French people neither resisted nor actively collaborated; Vichy and many French officials co-operated with Germany; reprisals caused great suffering; liberation depended on Allied armies.

**Judgement:** the resistance mattered most in 1944, as a support to the Allied invasion, and in shaping post-war France.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Repeating the Blitz-spirit myth without question"}
Morale held, but there was fear, looting and anger too; official sources were censored. Weigh both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all groups in Malaya as having the same experience"}
The Chinese suffered the harshest treatment; Malays and Indians were treated differently, and some co-operated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overstating the resistance's military role"}
Resistance movements supported the Allied armies; they did not defeat the occupiers by themselves.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the impact of bombing (Paper 1 part (b))"}
Explain physical effects (deaths, homes, shelters) and effects on morale and the war economy, using both Britain and Germany.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Blitz (September 1940-May 1941) killed about 40,000 British civilians; morale held, but not as perfectly as propaganda claimed.
- Allied bombing of Germany (Hamburg 1943, Dresden 1945) killed hundreds of thousands; its effectiveness and morality are debated.
- Japanese rule in Malaya and Singapore brought terror (Sook Ching), shortages, forced labour and propaganda; the Chinese suffered most.
- The MPAJA, helped by Force 136, resisted in the jungle; its political effect outlasted its military one.
- The French Resistance grew from 1943 and was most effective supporting D-Day and the liberation of Paris.
:::
