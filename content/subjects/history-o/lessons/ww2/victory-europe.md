## Introduction

At the start of 1943 Germany still controlled most of Europe. By May 1945 Hitler was dead, Berlin was in ruins and Germany had surrendered unconditionally. Victory came from three directions: from the south through Italy, from the west through France, and, above all in scale, from the east, where the Red Army fought most of the German army.

Soldiers from many countries took part. South African, Indian, New Zealand, Polish, Free French and Brazilian troops all fought in Italy alongside British and American forces, and many white Southern Rhodesians served in the Allied forces in the Mediterranean and in Europe.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study E, key question 4 (the European part): **how did the Allies achieve victory over the Axis powers in Europe?**

You should already know about Stalingrad and the turn of the war in the east in 1942-43.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Second front"}
An Allied invasion of western Europe that would force Germany to fight on two major fronts. Stalin demanded it from 1942; it came with D-Day in 1944.
:::

:::definition{term="Unconditional surrender"}
Surrender without any agreed terms. The Allies announced at Casablanca in January 1943 that they would accept nothing less from the Axis.
:::

:::definition{term="Operation Overlord"}
The code name for the Allied invasion of Normandy, which began on D-Day, 6 June 1944.
:::

:::definition{term="Deception"}
Misleading the enemy about your plans. Before D-Day, Operation Fortitude used fake armies and false radio messages to suggest the invasion would come at the Pas-de-Calais.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **10 July 1943:** Allied landings in Sicily.
- **25 July 1943:** Mussolini overthrown.
- **4 June 1944:** Rome liberated.
- **6 June 1944:** D-Day.
- **June 1944:** Soviet Operation Bagration.
- **30 April 1945:** Hitler's suicide; **8 May 1945:** Victory in Europe (VE) Day.
:::

## Sicily and Italy

After the Axis surrender in North Africa in May 1943, the Allies invaded **Sicily** on 10 July 1943. On 25 July Mussolini was voted down by the Fascist Grand Council and arrested on the King's orders. Sicily was taken by mid-August. In September the Allies landed in southern Italy, and the new Italian government surrendered. But German forces quickly occupied most of the country, rescued Mussolini and set up a puppet state for him in the north.

The campaign then became slow and costly. Italy's mountains and rivers gave the Germans strong defensive lines. The Allies were held for months at the **Gustav Line** around **Monte Cassino**, which finally fell in May 1944 after Polish troops captured the ruined monastery; a landing at Anzio in January 1944 failed to break the deadlock quickly. **Rome** was liberated on 4 June 1944, two days before D-Day. Fighting continued in the north until German forces in Italy surrendered at the beginning of May 1945.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: how important was the Allied advance through Italy?"}
**Important:** it knocked Italy out of the war, tied down many German divisions that could have fought in France or Russia, gave the Allies airfields from which to bomb southern Germany and its allies, and gained battle experience for D-Day.

**Limited:** progress was very slow and casualties were heavy; the mountains favoured the defenders; resources used in Italy might have been used for an earlier invasion of France. Italy was not the "soft" target some leaders had hoped.

**Judgement:** the Italian campaign contributed to victory by stretching Germany, but the decisive blows came in France and on the Eastern Front.
:::

## D-Day and the advance to Germany

On **6 June 1944** Allied forces under the supreme commander **General Eisenhower** landed in **Normandy**. Overnight, airborne troops landed behind the beaches; at dawn about 156,000 troops came ashore on five beaches, code-named Utah and Omaha (American), Gold and Sword (British) and Juno (Canadian). The landings at Omaha were the most costly.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did D-Day succeed?"}
1. **Planning and resources:** two years of preparation, thousands of ships and landing craft, artificial "Mulberry" harbours and huge supplies, many from the USA.
2. **Deception:** Operation Fortitude convinced Hitler that the main attack would come at the Pas-de-Calais, so strong German forces were held back there for weeks.
3. **Air superiority:** Allied air forces controlled the skies and bombed bridges and railways, slowing German reinforcements.
4. **German weaknesses:** Rommel wanted the tanks near the beaches, other commanders wanted them held back, and Hitler kept control of key reserves, so the response was slow.
5. **The French Resistance** sabotaged railways and communications.
:::

The fighting in Normandy lasted for weeks. After the breakout in late July and the destruction of German forces in the **Falaise pocket** in August, the Allies advanced fast. A second landing took place in southern France on 15 August. **Paris** was liberated on 25 August and Brussels in early September. The attempt to cross the Rhine at **Arnhem** in September failed, and in December 1944 Hitler's surprise attack in the Ardennes, the **Battle of the Bulge**, pushed the Americans back before it was defeated. In March 1945 the Allies crossed the Rhine and moved into the heart of Germany.

## The Soviet advance from the east

The Red Army fought by far the largest part of the German army; most German military deaths occurred on the Eastern Front. After the German defeat at **Kursk** (July 1943), the Soviets advanced steadily. In June 1944, timed to follow D-Day, **Operation Bagration** destroyed German Army Group Centre and carried the Red Army into Poland. Romania and Bulgaria changed sides in August-September 1944.

In January 1945 the Soviets attacked from the Vistula and reached the River Oder, about 70 kilometres from Berlin. On 16 April they launched the final assault on **Berlin**. On 25 April American and Soviet troops met at Torgau on the River Elbe, cutting Germany in two.

## Why Germany was on the point of collapse by April 1945

- **Fighting on several fronts:** east, west and south at once, against enemies with far greater resources.
- **Soviet and American production:** the USSR built huge numbers of tanks and guns; American factories supplied the Western Allies and, through Lend-Lease, the USSR as well.
- **Bombing:** attacks on oil plants and railways from 1944 left German tanks and aircraft short of fuel and wrecked transport.
- **Losses:** Germany had lost millions of soldiers and could not replace them; boys and old men were called up into the Volkssturm militia.
- **Hitler's leadership:** his orders to hold ground at all costs led to the loss of whole armies, and he wasted reserves in the Ardennes offensive.

## The surrender

As Soviet troops fought their way into Berlin, Hitler killed himself in his bunker on **30 April 1945**. Berlin surrendered on 2 May. Germany's unconditional surrender was signed at Reims on 7 May and took effect on 8 May, celebrated in the West as **VE Day**; a second ceremony was held in Berlin, and the USSR marks victory on 9 May.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying D-Day alone won the war"}
D-Day opened a vital second front, but the Red Army destroyed most of the German army, and bombing and Allied production also mattered.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Italian surrender with the end of the war in Italy"}
Italy surrendered in September 1943, but German forces fought on in Italy until May 1945.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the beaches"}
Utah and Omaha were American, Gold and Sword British, and Juno Canadian.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Allies took Sicily (July-August 1943); Mussolini fell; Italy surrendered, but the Germans fought on in Italy until May 1945.
- D-Day (6 June 1944) succeeded through planning, deception, air superiority and German mistakes; Paris was liberated on 25 August.
- The Red Army fought most of the German army; Bagration (1944) and the 1945 offensives took it to Berlin.
- By April 1945 Germany was collapsing under attack from all sides, short of fuel, men and equipment.
- Hitler killed himself on 30 April 1945; Germany surrendered unconditionally, effective 8 May (VE Day).
:::
