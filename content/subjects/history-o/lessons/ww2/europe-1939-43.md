## Introduction

Between September 1939 and June 1940 Germany defeated Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium and France. Its speed astonished the world. A year later German armies were deep inside the Soviet Union, and by the summer of 1942 they had reached the River Volga at Stalingrad. Then, in the ruins of that city, a whole German army was destroyed.

The war was felt in southern Africa too: men from Southern Rhodesia joined the Royal Air Force, and Rhodesian pilots flew in the Battle of Britain. Southern Rhodesia also hosted RAF flying schools under the Rhodesian Air Training Group, where thousands of aircrew were trained.

This lesson covers Cambridge Depth study E, key question 1: **how did the Second World War in Europe develop?**

You should already know how the war began with the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blitzkrieg"}
"Lightning war": fast attacks by tanks and motorised troops supported by dive-bombers, aiming to break through and encircle the enemy before it could react.
:::

:::definition{term="Vichy France"}
The French government led by Marshal Pétain after the armistice of June 1940, based in the town of Vichy, which ruled the unoccupied south and co-operated with Germany.
:::

:::definition{term="Operation Barbarossa"}
The code name for the German invasion of the Soviet Union, launched on 22 June 1941.
:::

:::definition{term="Encirclement"}
Surrounding an enemy army so that it is cut off from supplies and reinforcements.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **10 May 1940:** Germany attacks the Netherlands, Belgium and France.
- **26 May-4 June 1940:** Dunkirk evacuation.
- **22 June 1940:** French armistice.
- **July-October 1940:** Battle of Britain.
- **22 June 1941:** Operation Barbarossa.
- **August 1942-February 1943:** Battle of Stalingrad.
:::

## German conquest by 1940

After the quick defeat of Poland there were months of little fighting in the west, the "Phoney War". In April 1940 Germany seized **Denmark** and **Norway**. On **10 May** it attacked the Netherlands, Belgium and France. The main German tank force advanced through the hilly, wooded **Ardennes**, which the French thought unsuitable for tanks, crossed the River Meuse at Sedan and raced to the Channel coast, cutting the Allied armies in two.

**Dunkirk:** trapped British and French troops were evacuated from the beaches and harbour of Dunkirk between 26 May and 4 June 1940 by naval ships and hundreds of small civilian boats. About 338,000 soldiers were rescued, but almost all their heavy equipment was left behind. Hitler's order to halt his tanks for a few days, and the defence of the perimeter by British and French rearguards, helped make the escape possible.

**The fall of France:** Italy entered the war on 10 June, Paris fell on 14 June, and France signed an armistice on 22 June. Germany occupied the north and the Atlantic coast; Marshal **Pétain**'s government at **Vichy** ruled the south and collaborated with Germany. From London, **General de Gaulle** called on the French to keep fighting.

By mid-1940 Germany controlled or dominated most of western and central Europe, with Italy as an ally and the USSR bound by the Nazi-Soviet Pact. But Britain, with its empire and navy, was still fighting.

## Why Germany could not conquer Britain

Hitler hoped Britain would make peace. When it refused, he ordered preparations for an invasion, **Operation Sea Lion**. An invasion across the Channel needed control of the air, so the German air force, the **Luftwaffe**, had to destroy the Royal Air Force first.

The **Battle of Britain** was fought from July to October 1940. The Luftwaffe attacked shipping, then airfields and radar stations in August. In early September, with the RAF under heavy strain, Germany switched to bombing London. This gave the RAF time to recover. After heavy German losses on 15 September, Sea Lion was postponed, and it was never carried out.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why did Germany fail to conquer Britain?"}
1. **Radar and organisation:** the chain of radar stations and the RAF's control system gave warning of raids, so fighters could meet them without wasting fuel on patrols.
2. **Aircraft and pilots:** Spitfires and Hurricanes were a match for German fighters. British factories built fighters faster than Germany in 1940. Pilots from Poland, Czechoslovakia, the dominions, Southern Rhodesia and elsewhere fought alongside the British.
3. **Home advantage:** RAF pilots who were shot down over Britain could fly again; German aircrew were captured. German fighters had only a short time over England before their fuel ran low.
4. **German mistakes:** switching from airfields to London relieved pressure on the RAF.
5. **The Royal Navy:** even with air superiority, an invasion fleet would have faced the strongest navy in the world.
:::

The consequences were large: Britain survived as a base from which the liberation of Europe would later be launched; Germany faced a continuing war in the west; and Britain received growing American support, including Lend-Lease from March 1941.

## Operation Barbarossa, 1941

On **22 June 1941** about three million German soldiers, with their allies, invaded the Soviet Union along a front of more than 1,500 kilometres, in three army groups aimed at Leningrad, Moscow and Ukraine.

**Why Hitler invaded:**

- **Living space (Lebensraum):** Hitler had long planned to take land in the east for German settlement.
- **Ideology:** he saw communism as Germany's greatest enemy, and Nazi racial ideas treated Slavs as inferior.
- **Resources:** the grain of Ukraine and the oil of the Caucasus.
- **Strategy:** defeating the USSR would remove Britain's last hope of an ally in Europe.
- **Confidence:** the Red Army had been weakened by Stalin's purges of its officers and had performed badly against Finland in 1939-40.

**Early successes:** surprise was almost complete, because Stalin had ignored warnings. In huge encirclements, such as around Kiev in September 1941, the Germans captured hundreds of thousands of prisoners. Leningrad was besieged from September 1941, and by late November German troops were close to Moscow.

**But the attack stalled:** distances were vast, roads turned to mud in the autumn, and the German army was not equipped for winter. The USSR moved hundreds of factories east of the Urals, and on 5 December 1941 fresh Soviet troops, including divisions brought from Siberia, counter-attacked before Moscow.

## Stalingrad, 1942-43

In 1942 Hitler's summer offensive aimed at the oilfields of the Caucasus and at **Stalingrad**, an industrial city on the Volga that bore Stalin's name. From August the German Sixth Army under **General Paulus** fought its way into the city, street by street. The Soviet defenders held a narrow strip along the river.

On **19 November 1942** the Red Army launched a counter-offensive against the weaker Romanian forces on the German flanks and, within days, surrounded the Sixth Army. Hitler refused to let Paulus break out. An airlift promised by the Luftwaffe chief, **Göring**, could not deliver enough supplies, and a relief attack in December failed. Paulus surrendered on 31 January 1943; the last German troops in the city gave up on 2 February. About 91,000 German soldiers were taken prisoner, and only a few thousand of them ever returned home.

:::example{title="A significance judged: why was Stalingrad a turning point?"}
**Military:** a whole German army was destroyed for the first time, and the Red Army took the initiative on the Eastern Front.

**Morale:** German propaganda could not hide the defeat; Soviet confidence and international prestige rose.

**Strategic:** after the failure of the German offensive at Kursk in July 1943, Germany was on the defensive in the east for the rest of the war.

**Qualification:** some historians argue that the defeat before Moscow in December 1941 was the real turning point, because Germany then lost its chance of a quick victory. Stalingrad confirmed that the war in the east could not be won.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling Dunkirk a victory"}
Dunkirk was a successful evacuation after a heavy defeat. It saved the army's soldiers but not their equipment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Battle of Britain was fought over France"}
It was fought in the skies over southern England and the Channel in 1940.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Barbarossa (1941) with Stalingrad (1942-43)"}
Barbarossa was the first invasion of the USSR; Stalingrad came during the second German summer offensive.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 part (c)"}
"To what extent had Germany gained control of Europe by 1940?" Weigh German conquests against what it did not control (Britain, the seas, the USSR, neutral states) and how secure its control was.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 4"}
For "How significant was Stalingrad?", judge significance in different ways: military, morale, long-term effect on the war. Compare it with Moscow (1941) and Kursk (1943).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- By June 1940 Germany had conquered Poland, Denmark, Norway, the Low Countries and France; Vichy France collaborated.
- About 338,000 Allied soldiers were evacuated from Dunkirk (26 May-4 June 1940).
- Radar, fighter production, pilots from many countries and German mistakes won the Battle of Britain; Sea Lion was postponed.
- Barbarossa (22 June 1941) was driven by Lebensraum, anti-communism and resources; it came close to Moscow but stalled.
- The surrender at Stalingrad (January-February 1943) destroyed a German army and gave the initiative to the Red Army.
:::
