## Introduction

The Second World War was not only a war between armies. In the lands Nazi Germany conquered, especially in eastern Europe, it became a war against whole peoples. Poland suffered more than almost any other country: historians estimate that around three million Polish Jews were murdered, and that close to two million non-Jewish Polish civilians were killed under German occupation.

The **Holocaust** (in Hebrew, the **Shoah**) was the deliberate, state-organised murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children by Nazi Germany and its collaborators. Studying it means remembering the victims as people, with names, families and communities, not only as numbers. This lesson describes what happened accurately and without graphic detail.

It covers part of Cambridge Depth study E, key question 3: **Nazi control in occupied Europe (case study: Poland), including the circumstances leading to the Holocaust and its impact.**

You should already know that the Nazis persecuted Jews in Germany from 1933 and that Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Holocaust (Shoah)"}
The systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators between 1933 and 1945, most of them between 1941 and 1945.
:::

:::definition{term="Ghetto"}
A closed area of a city in which the Nazis forced Jews to live, cut off from the rest of the population, in overcrowded conditions with little food.
:::

:::definition{term="Einsatzgruppen"}
Mobile SS and police killing units that followed the German army into the Soviet Union from June 1941 and murdered Jews and others, mainly by mass shootings.
:::

:::definition{term="Genocide"}
The deliberate destruction of a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. The word was created in 1944 by Raphael Lemkin, a Polish-Jewish lawyer, to describe Nazi crimes.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **September 1939:** Poland invaded and divided by Germany and the USSR.
- **1940:** large ghettos sealed in Łódź (spring) and Warsaw (November).
- **June 1941 onwards:** mass shootings by the Einsatzgruppen in the occupied USSR.
- **20 January 1942:** Wannsee Conference.
- **19 April 1943:** Warsaw Ghetto Uprising begins.
- **27 January 1945:** Soviet troops liberate Auschwitz.
:::

## The division of Poland

The secret protocol of the Nazi-Soviet Pact (August 1939) divided Poland. Germany invaded on 1 September, the USSR on 17 September, and Warsaw surrendered at the end of the month.

- **The USSR** took eastern Poland, arresting and deporting hundreds of thousands of Poles. In 1940 the Soviet secret police shot about 22,000 Polish officers and other prisoners in the Katyn massacres.
- **Germany** annexed western Poland into the Reich and expelled Poles to make room for German settlers.
- The rest of German-held Poland became the **General Government**, ruled from Kraków by the Nazi official **Hans Frank** as a source of labour and resources.

## Terror against Poles

Nazi ideology treated Poles, as Slavs, as racially inferior people to be used as labourers. The occupiers set out to destroy Polish leadership and culture:

- Teachers, priests, lawyers, officials and other leaders were arrested and many were shot or sent to camps; in November 1939 professors of Kraków's university were arrested.
- Secondary schools and universities were closed; Poles were to receive only basic education.
- Hundreds of thousands of Poles were sent to Germany as forced labourers.
- Public executions and collective punishments were used to terrorise the population.

Despite this, Poland built one of the largest resistance movements in Europe, the **Home Army**, which organised secret schools and newspapers and, in August 1944, launched the **Warsaw Uprising**, crushed by German forces after two months.

## The ghettos

Poland had the largest Jewish population in Europe, over three million people. From late 1939 Jews in occupied Poland had to wear the Star of David, lost their property and jobs, and were forced into **ghettos**. The **Warsaw ghetto**, sealed in November 1940, held over 400,000 people at its peak in a small part of the city. Hunger and diseases such as typhus killed tens of thousands.

Life in the ghettos was not only suffering. People ran secret schools, prayer meetings, soup kitchens, concerts and libraries. In Warsaw the historian **Emanuel Ringelblum** organised a secret archive, buried in milk cans and boxes, to record ghetto life for the future; much of it was recovered after the war.

:::example{title="A source analysed: why is the Ringelblum archive so valuable?"}
1. **Who and when:** it was created inside the Warsaw ghetto, at the time, by Jews recording their own experience.
2. **Purpose:** to leave evidence for the future, so its makers aimed to record the truth as fully as they could.
3. **Content:** diaries, essays, posters, letters and reports cover daily life, hunger, deportations and resistance.
4. **Limits:** parts were lost; it records what its contributors could see in one ghetto.
5. **Conclusion:** it is one of the most important sources on the Holocaust because it gives the victims' own voices.
:::

## The circumstances leading to the Holocaust

Historians agree that the Holocaust grew out of Nazi antisemitism, but they debate how it developed. Some stress Hitler's long-held intentions; others stress how policies became more radical step by step, as officials competed and the war created new circumstances. Most see both factors at work.

1. **Ideology:** Nazi racial antisemitism blamed Jews for Germany's problems and treated them as an enemy race.
2. **Persecution in Germany, 1933-39:** boycotts, the Nuremberg Laws (1935) and Kristallnacht (1938) removed Jews' rights step by step.
3. **War and conquest:** the conquest of Poland in 1939 brought millions of Jews under Nazi rule, and the ghettos were a "temporary" measure.
4. **The invasion of the USSR, June 1941:** the Einsatzgruppen began mass shootings of Jewish men, then of women and children. At Babi Yar near Kiev, more than 33,000 Jews were murdered in two days in September 1941.
5. **Death camps:** from December 1941 gas was used at **Chełmno**. At the **Wannsee Conference** (20 January 1942) senior officials coordinated the "Final Solution": the murder of all Europe's Jews.

## The death camps

The Nazis built death camps in occupied Poland: **Chełmno**, **Bełżec**, **Sobibór**, **Treblinka**, **Majdanek** and **Auschwitz-Birkenau**. Jews were deported there by train from ghettos across Poland and from all over Europe. Most were murdered in gas chambers soon after arrival. At Auschwitz, the largest camp, about 1.1 million people were murdered, about a million of them Jews; Poles, Roma and Sinti, and Soviet prisoners of war were also killed there.

Jews resisted where they could: in the **Warsaw Ghetto Uprising** (April-May 1943) young fighters held out against German troops for weeks, and there were uprisings at Treblinka and Sobibór in 1943. Some Poles risked their lives to hide Jews, though helping Jews in occupied Poland was punishable by death; the underground council **Żegota** organised aid. Others were indifferent, and some betrayed Jews to the Germans.

## The impact of the Holocaust

- **Human loss:** historians estimate that about six million Jews were murdered, around two-thirds of Europe's Jews; about 90 per cent of Poland's Jews were killed. The Nazis also murdered Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war and others.
- **Survivors** had lost families and communities; many could not return home and lived for years in displaced persons' camps.
- **Justice:** leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg (1945-46) for crimes including crimes against humanity.
- **International law:** the UN Genocide Convention (9 December 1948) and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (10 December 1948) were shaped by the Holocaust.
- **Memory:** 27 January, the day Auschwitz was liberated, is International Holocaust Remembrance Day.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Holocaust began in 1933"}
Persecution began in 1933; systematic mass murder began in 1941, after the invasion of the USSR.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting non-Jewish Polish victims"}
Nazi rule in Poland also aimed to destroy Polish leadership and culture; around two million non-Jewish Polish civilians are estimated to have been killed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting the victims as passive"}
Jews resisted in many ways: armed uprisings, escape, smuggling, keeping schools and records.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In 1939 Poland was divided by Germany and the USSR; western Poland was annexed and the rest became the General Government.
- Nazi terror targeted Polish leaders, education and culture; Poles were used as forced labour; the Home Army resisted.
- Jews were forced into ghettos (Warsaw sealed in November 1940), where hunger and disease killed many.
- Mass shootings from June 1941, the Wannsee Conference (January 1942) and the death camps in occupied Poland made up the "Final Solution".
- About six million Jews were murdered; the Holocaust shaped the Nuremberg trials, the Genocide Convention and human rights law.
:::
