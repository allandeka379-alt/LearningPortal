## Introduction

In the spring of 1848 something happened that Europe had never seen before. Within about two months, crowds rose against their governments in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Milan, Venice, Budapest, Prague and dozens of smaller cities. Kings promised constitutions, ministers fled, and for a few weeks it looked as if the old order of 1815 would collapse. People at the time called it the "Springtime of Peoples".

Why did so many revolutions happen at once? The answer combines long-term ideas (liberalism and nationalism), a political system that blocked change (the Metternich system) and a short-term economic crisis that brought hunger and unemployment. The same pattern of new ideas, a government that refuses reform and hard times appears again and again in history, including in the rise of African nationalism in the twentieth century.

This topic is part of Cambridge 0470 Option A, key question 1: *Were the revolutions of 1848 important?* The events of 1848 in Italy and Germany are taught with Italian and German unification; this lesson explains the causes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Liberalism"}
The belief that government should be limited by a written constitution, with an elected parliament, the rule of law, and freedoms such as freedom of speech and of the press. Most liberals of the 1840s wanted the vote only for men with property, not for everyone.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
The belief that people who share a language, culture and history form a nation and should govern themselves in their own state.
:::

:::definition{term="Metternich system"}
The policies of the Austrian chancellor Prince Metternich after 1815 to keep the order set up at the Congress of Vienna: censorship, police spies, control of universities and the readiness of the great powers to intervene against revolutions.
:::

:::definition{term="Absolute monarchy"}
A system in which the ruler holds full power, without a constitution or an elected parliament to limit it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1815:** Congress of Vienna redraws the map of Europe.
- **1819:** Carlsbad Decrees in the German Confederation.
- **1830:** revolution in Paris; Belgium breaks away from the Netherlands.
- **1845-47:** potato blight, poor harvests and a financial crisis.
- **12 January 1848:** rising in Palermo, Sicily.
- **24 February 1848:** King Louis Philippe of France abdicates.
- **13 March 1848:** Metternich resigns after a rising in Vienna.
:::

## The settlement of 1815 and the Metternich system

After the defeat of Napoleon, the great powers met at the **Congress of Vienna** (1814-15). They restored many of the old ruling families and redrew borders to keep a balance of power. They paid little attention to the wishes of peoples: Italians remained divided into several states with Austria controlling Lombardy and Venetia; Germans were split among 39 states in a loose German Confederation; and the Austrian Empire ruled Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Croats, Italians and others.

The Austrian chancellor **Metternich** believed that liberalism and nationalism threatened this order, especially the multinational Austrian Empire. He used:

- **Censorship** of newspapers and books, and police spies.
- **Control of universities**, as in the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819, which banned student societies in the German states.
- **Intervention** by the great powers: Austrian troops crushed revolts in Naples and Piedmont in 1821 and in central Italy in 1831.

The system kept order for a generation, but it also meant that reformers had no legal way to change things. Pressure built up instead of being released.

## Why liberalism and nationalism had grown by 1848

Several long-term changes spread these ideas:

- **The legacy of the French Revolution and Napoleon.** People across Europe remembered ideas of citizens' rights and equality before the law. Napoleon had also reduced the number of German and Italian states and introduced modern laws, showing that the old borders could change.
- **The growing middle class.** Lawyers, doctors, teachers, merchants and factory owners grew in number. They were educated and prosperous but shut out of power, and they wanted constitutions, parliaments and free trade.
- **Education, newspapers and Romanticism.** Writers and scholars celebrated national languages, folk songs and histories. Student societies and secret groups, such as the Carbonari in Italy, kept ideas alive.
- **The example of 1830.** In 1830 a revolution in Paris replaced Charles X with the more liberal Louis Philippe, and Belgium won independence. A Polish rising against Russia was crushed in 1831, but these events showed that revolution could succeed.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: from ideas to frustration"}
1. **Ideas spread:** the middle class read newspapers and pamphlets about constitutions and national unity.
2. **The system refused change:** censorship and police control blocked peaceful reform, and rulers such as the Austrian emperor would not grant constitutions.
3. **Frustration grew:** educated people felt excluded from decisions about taxes, trade and laws.
4. **Result:** by the 1840s many liberals and nationalists believed that only pressure from the streets would force rulers to change.
:::

## The economic crisis of 1845-47

Ideas alone did not bring crowds onto the streets. The immediate cause was economic:

- **Harvest failures.** Potato blight struck northern Europe from 1845, causing terrible famine in Ireland, and the grain harvest of 1846 was poor across much of the continent. Food prices rose sharply in 1846-47, and there were food riots.
- **Financial crisis and unemployment.** In 1847 a commercial and banking crisis led to business failures. Factories cut production and many workers lost their jobs just as bread cost the most.
- **Distress of artisans.** Skilled craftsmen such as handloom weavers were already losing work to factory machines, and many of them took part in the risings.
- **Peasant grievances.** In central and eastern Europe many peasants still owed labour services and dues to landlords, and they wanted these abolished.

By early 1848 the harvest of 1847 was better, but unemployment and debt were still high, and townspeople blamed their governments.

## How revolution spread in 1848

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 12 January 1848 | Rising in Palermo against the King of the Two Sicilies |
| 22-24 February 1848 | Revolution in Paris; Louis Philippe abdicates and a republic is proclaimed |
| 13 March 1848 | Rising in Vienna; Metternich resigns and flees |
| 18 March 1848 | Street fighting in Berlin; Milan's Five Days begin |
| 22 March 1848 | Venice declares the Republic of St Mark |
| April 1848 | The Hungarian April Laws give Hungary its own government |

The trigger came in **Paris**. When the government of Louis Philippe banned a political banquet held by reformers in February 1848, demonstrations turned into street fighting. The National Guard would not defend the king, and he abdicated on **24 February**. A republic was proclaimed.

News travelled quickly by railway, telegraph and newspaper. The fall of the French king encouraged opponents of other governments and shook the confidence of rulers, who remembered how revolution had spread from France in 1789. In March risings broke out in **Vienna**, where Metternich resigned on 13 March, and in **Berlin**, Milan, Venice, Budapest and Prague. Most rulers gave way quickly and appointed liberal ministers or promised constitutions.

Britain and Russia did not have revolutions. In Britain the Chartists held a mass meeting in London in April 1848, but Parliament and the reforms of earlier years meant there was no rising. In Russia the tsar's police state was too strong.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was France the main cause of the revolutions?"}
**Yes:** Paris was the trigger. Within three weeks of Louis Philippe's fall, Vienna and Berlin had risen, and news from France gave reformers confidence.
**No:** the first rising, in Palermo, came before Paris. The causes (economic distress, liberal and national demands, the rigid Metternich system) already existed in each country.
**Judgement:** France explains the timing and the speed of the spread, but the revolutions happened because the conditions for revolt were already present across Europe.
:::

## What the revolutions had in common

- **Leadership:** mostly middle-class liberals, supported in the cities by workers and artisans.
- **Demands:** constitutions, elected parliaments, freedom of the press and an end to censorship.
- **National aims:** in Italy and Germany, unity; in Hungary and among the Czechs, self-government within or outside the Austrian Empire.
- **Social demands:** peasants wanted an end to labour services; workers wanted jobs and better wages.
- **Speed:** most governments collapsed or gave way within days, which suggests that they had lost confidence rather than been defeated.

These common features also contained weaknesses. Liberals and workers wanted different things, and different nationalities had rival aims. The next lesson explains how these divisions helped the old rulers to recover.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking all revolutionaries wanted democracy"}
Most liberals wanted a constitution and a parliament elected by men of property. Universal male suffrage was a radical demand, achieved in France in 1848 but feared by many moderates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the revolutions began in Paris"}
Paris was the main trigger, but the rising in Palermo began on 12 January 1848, about six weeks before the French king fell.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without linking them"}
A strong answer shows how the long-term ideas, the blocking of reform and the short-term crisis worked together, rather than listing each cause separately.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain why there were so many revolutions in 1848'"}
Develop two or three reasons. For each, say what the cause was, give a specific example (the harvest failures of 1846, the Carlsbad Decrees, the fall of Louis Philippe) and show how it led people to revolt.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c) and Paper 2"}
For "How far was economic distress the main cause?", weigh the crisis of 1845-47 against liberalism, nationalism and the example of Paris, then reach a judgement. In Paper 2, when 1848 is the prescribed topic, ask who produced each source: a liberal, a radical, a worker or a supporter of the old order.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The 1815 settlement ignored national feelings and restored old rulers, and Metternich's system of censorship and intervention blocked reform.
- Liberalism and nationalism grew through the legacy of the French Revolution, the rise of the middle class, education and the example of 1830.
- Harvest failures (1845-47), a financial crisis and unemployment turned discontent into revolt.
- The fall of Louis Philippe on 24 February 1848 triggered risings across Europe, and Metternich fell on 13 March.
- The revolutions shared middle-class leadership and liberal and national demands, but their supporters wanted different things.
:::
