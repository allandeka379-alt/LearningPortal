## Introduction

After the failure of 1848-50, Austria was again the leading power in Germany. Sixteen years later its army was beaten in a single great battle, Austria was pushed out of German affairs, and Prussia controlled all of northern Germany. The man at the centre of this change was **Otto von Bismarck**, a conservative Prussian landowner who became Minister-President in 1862.

Leaders who build states often use both diplomacy and force, and historians argue about how much they plan and how much they seize chances. Bismarck is a classic case for that debate.

This lesson covers Cambridge Option A, key question 3, from 1862 to 1866. You should know about the German Confederation, the Zollverein and why the Frankfurt Parliament failed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Minister-President"}
The head of the Prussian government, appointed by and answerable to the king, not to parliament.
:::

:::definition{term="Constitutional crisis"}
The deadlock of 1862 in which the Prussian parliament (Landtag), led by liberals, refused to approve the money for the king's army reforms.
:::

:::definition{term="Realpolitik"}
Politics based on practical interests and power rather than on principles or ideals. Bismarck's diplomacy is often described this way.
:::

:::definition{term="North German Confederation"}
The union of the German states north of the River Main, led by Prussia, created after Austria's defeat in 1866. Its constitution came into force in 1867.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- **Wilhelm I:** King of Prussia from 1861. **Albrecht von Roon:** War Minister. **Helmuth von Moltke:** Chief of the General Staff.
- **September 1862:** Bismarck appointed Minister-President.
- **1864:** war with Denmark over Schleswig and Holstein.
- **August 1865:** Convention of Gastein.
- **3 July 1866:** battle of Königgrätz (also called Sadowa).
- **23 August 1866:** Treaty of Prague.
:::

## The army and the constitutional crisis

Wilhelm I, who became regent in 1858 and king in 1861, was a soldier who believed Prussia's army was too small and badly organised. With his War Minister, **Roon**, he planned to enlarge the regular army, extend military service to three years and reduce the role of the part-time reserve.

![Timeline of Prussia's rise: Bismarck becomes Minister-President in 1862, Austria and Prussia defeat Denmark in 1864, Prussia wins at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866, the Treaty of Prague follows and the North German Confederation is formed in 1867.](media:diagrams/unification-austria-timeline.png "Figure: timeline of Prussia and Austria, 1861-1867")

The liberals who controlled the Prussian parliament supported a strong Prussia but did not trust an army controlled by the king. In 1862 they refused to approve the budget. Wilhelm even thought of abdicating. Instead, on Roon's advice, he appointed Bismarck as Minister-President in **September 1862**.

Bismarck told a committee of the parliament that the great questions of the day would not be decided by speeches and majority votes, which he said had been the mistake of 1848-49, but by "iron and blood". He then **collected taxes and carried out the army reforms without parliament's approval** for four years, arguing that the constitution did not say what should happen when king and parliament disagreed.

## Diplomacy before war

Bismarck's aim was to make Prussia at least equal to Austria in Germany. To do this he needed the other great powers to stay out.

- **Russia:** in 1863, when the Poles rose against Russian rule, Prussia offered Russia help in stopping rebels crossing the border. Russia remembered this goodwill.
- **Austria's reform plan:** in 1863 Austria called a congress of German princes to reform the Confederation. Bismarck persuaded Wilhelm not to attend, and without Prussia the plan failed.

## Schleswig-Holstein, 1863-65

The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were ruled by the King of Denmark but were not part of Denmark itself; Holstein was a member of the German Confederation and largely German-speaking. In 1863 a new Danish king, Christian IX, accepted a constitution that tied Schleswig more closely to Denmark. German nationalists were furious and supported a German prince's claim to the duchies.

Bismarck acted not with the nationalists but **with Austria**. In 1864 Prussia and Austria together defeated Denmark, and Denmark gave up the duchies to the two powers jointly. Joint rule soon caused friction. By the **Convention of Gastein** (August 1865), Prussia was to administer Schleswig and Austria Holstein, which lay between Schleswig and Prussia. Holstein, cut off from Austria, was a likely source of future quarrels.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how Schleswig-Holstein helped Bismarck against Austria"}
1. **Joint action:** by fighting Denmark alongside Austria, Prussia stopped Austria from leading German opinion on the issue.
2. **Joint rule:** Austria now shared responsibility for two duchies far from its own lands.
3. **Gastein:** Austria was given Holstein, surrounded by Prussian-controlled territory.
4. **A ready quarrel:** any dispute over Holstein could be turned into a reason for war when Prussia was ready.
:::

## Isolating Austria

Before fighting, Bismarck made sure Austria would stand alone:

- **France:** at **Biarritz** in October 1865, Bismarck met Napoleon III, who seems to have expected a long war between the German powers from which France could gain. He promised to stay neutral; Bismarck made only vague hints about compensation.
- **Italy:** in April 1866 Prussia made an alliance with **Italy**, which would attack Austria and receive **Venetia** if Prussia went to war within three months.
- **Russia** was friendly after 1863, and **Britain** had no wish to become involved.
- **German nationalists:** Bismarck proposed that a reformed Confederation should have a parliament elected by all adult men, to win nationalist opinion away from Austria.

In June 1866 Prussian troops entered Holstein. Austria persuaded the Confederation to mobilise against Prussia, and war followed.

## The Austro-Prussian War, 1866

The war lasted about seven weeks. Most of the medium-sized German states, such as Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover, sided with Austria. Austria beat the Italians at Custoza, but in the decisive campaign **Moltke** used Prussia's railways to move three armies quickly into Bohemia, where they met at **Königgrätz** on **3 July 1866** and defeated the main Austrian army.

Reasons for the Prussian victory included:

- **Railways and the telegraph**, which allowed fast mobilisation and co-ordination.
- **The needle gun**, a breech-loading rifle that could be reloaded quickly while lying down, while most Austrian infantry still used muzzle-loaders.
- **Moltke's planning** and the army enlarged by the reforms of 1860-62.
- **Austria fighting on two fronts**, against Prussia and Italy.

## The Treaty of Prague and its consequences

Bismarck persuaded Wilhelm and the generals **not to march on Vienna** or take Austrian land. He wanted a quick peace before France could intervene, and he did not want Austria as a permanent enemy. By the **Treaty of Prague** (23 August 1866):

- Austria lost **no German land**, but Venetia went to Italy.
- The **German Confederation was dissolved**, and Austria accepted that it would play no part in Germany's future.
- Prussia **annexed** Schleswig-Holstein, Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau and Frankfurt.
- The states north of the River Main formed the **North German Confederation** under Prussia. The southern states (Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and part of Hesse-Darmstadt) stayed independent but signed secret military alliances with Prussia.

At home, the victory ended the constitutional crisis. Parliament passed an **Indemnity Bill** in September 1866, approving Bismarck's spending since 1862 after the event. Many liberals now supported him, forming the National Liberal Party in 1867.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: did Bismarck plan the war of 1866?"}
**Planned:** he had long seen Austria as Prussia's rival, isolated it carefully (Russia, Biarritz, Italy) and left Holstein as a source of conflict.
**Opportunist:** at Gastein he agreed a compromise that could have lasted, and he seems to have kept several options open, deciding on war only when conditions were favourable.
**Judgement:** many historians combine the two: Bismarck had a clear aim, Prussian equality or leadership in Germany, but was flexible about methods and timing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Austria lost German territory in 1866"}
Austria lost Venetia to Italy, but no German land. Bismarck's leniency was deliberate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Germany was unified in 1866"}
1866 created the North German Confederation. The southern states joined only in 1870-71, after the war with France.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Bismarck as a nationalist idealist"}
Bismarck was a Prussian conservative. He used German nationalism when it helped Prussia, but his aim was Prussian power.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain how Bismarck brought about Austria's defeat'"}
Link diplomacy and military factors: isolation of Austria (Russia, France, Italy), the Schleswig-Holstein quarrel, and Prussia's military advantages at Königgrätz. Develop each reason with a specific example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (Germany prescribed for March 2027)"}
When a source is from Bismarck's side, such as an official Prussian statement, ask what he wanted people to believe at that moment, for example that Austria was the aggressor in 1866.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wilhelm I appointed Bismarck in 1862 to force through the army reforms against the liberal parliament.
- Bismarck used the Schleswig-Holstein question (1864, Gastein 1865) to create a quarrel with Austria.
- He isolated Austria: Russian goodwill, French neutrality at Biarritz, and an alliance with Italy.
- Railways, the needle gun and Moltke's planning brought victory at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866.
- The Treaty of Prague excluded Austria from Germany and led to the Prussian-led North German Confederation (1867).
:::
