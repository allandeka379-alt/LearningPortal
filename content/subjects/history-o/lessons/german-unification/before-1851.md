## Introduction

In 1815 there was no country called Germany. German-speaking people lived in dozens of separate states, large and small, each with its own ruler, laws and army. By 1871 most of them had been joined into a single German Empire led by Prussia. This lesson covers the first stage: the ideas and economic links that pushed Germans towards unity, and the failed attempt of 1848-49 to unite Germany through an elected parliament.

Africans living under colonial rule later faced a similar question to the German liberals of 1848: can a nation be built by speeches and votes, or does it need power? Keep that question in mind as you read.

Cambridge examines this as part of Option A, key question 3: *How was Germany unified?* You should already know what liberalism and nationalism mean and that revolutions broke out across Europe in 1848.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="German Confederation"}
The loose union of 39 German states set up at the Congress of Vienna in 1815, with a Diet (assembly of ambassadors) at Frankfurt chaired by Austria. It was not a nation: each state kept its own ruler and army.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
The belief that people who share a language, culture and history form a nation and should live in one independent state.
:::

:::definition{term="Liberalism"}
The belief in constitutional government, an elected parliament, the rule of law and freedoms such as freedom of speech and of the press.
:::

:::definition{term="Zollverein"}
The German customs union led by Prussia, which came into force on 1 January 1834. Its members removed customs duties on trade between them and charged a common tariff on imports. Austria was not a member.
:::

:::definition{term="Grossdeutschland and Kleindeutschland"}
"Greater Germany" (a united Germany that included the German lands of the Austrian Empire) and "Little Germany" (a united Germany without Austria, which would be led by Prussia).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **1815:** German Confederation created.
- **1834:** the Zollverein comes into force.
- **March 1848:** revolution in Berlin.
- **18 May 1848:** the Frankfurt Parliament opens.
- **April 1849:** Frederick William IV of Prussia refuses the imperial crown.
- **November 1850:** the agreement of Olmütz; the Confederation is restored in 1851.
:::

## Germany before 1848

### The German Confederation

The Congress of Vienna did not restore the hundreds of small territories of the old Holy Roman Empire. Instead it created the **German Confederation** of 39 states. The two most powerful were **Austria**, a multinational empire whose emperor ruled Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Italians and others, and **Prussia**, a German kingdom stretching from the Rhineland in the west to the Baltic in the east. Austria held the presidency of the Diet.

The Austrian chancellor, **Metternich**, saw nationalism and liberalism as threats to the multinational Austrian Empire. After a student nationalist murdered a conservative writer in 1819, the Confederation issued the **Carlsbad Decrees**, which censored newspapers, placed universities under supervision and banned student societies.

### The growth of nationalism

Nationalist ideas spread nonetheless, mainly among students, professors, lawyers and businessmen. Writers celebrated a shared German language, folk tales and history. Student societies held festivals, such as the Wartburg Festival of 1817, and in 1832 a large crowd at the Hambach Festival called for a free and united Germany. Most ordinary Germans, however, especially peasants, still felt loyal first to their own state, their ruler and their church.

### The Zollverein

Economic change did more than speeches to link the German states. Prussia had created a single customs system for all its own lands in 1818. Because its territory was split in two, it encouraged neighbouring states to join, and in **1834** the **Zollverein** came into force. By the 1840s it covered most German states outside Austria.

The Zollverein helped German industry and trade to grow, and the railways built from the 1830s carried goods and people across state borders. It also mattered politically:

- Smaller states became used to co-operating with Prussia and depended on it for trade.
- **Austria was left outside**, because its industries feared competition and its leaders wanted to protect the empire's own markets.

:::example{title="An interpretation weighed: was the Zollverein a step towards unification?"}
**Yes:** it tied the economies of most German states to Prussia, excluded Austria, and gave Prussia economic leadership that later helped it win wars and attract support.
**No:** the member states joined for their own economic benefit, and several of them sided with Austria against Prussia in 1866. Membership of the customs union did not make them want to be ruled from Berlin.
**Judgement:** the Zollverein did not cause unification on its own, but it gave Prussia an economic lead over Austria and made a "Little Germany" seem natural to many businessmen.
:::

## The revolutions of 1848-49

### Revolution in Prussia

News of the February revolution in Paris in 1848 set off protests across the German states. Rulers quickly appointed liberal ministers and promised constitutions. In Berlin, street fighting broke out on **18 March 1848**. King **Frederick William IV** withdrew his troops from the city, promised a constitution and appeared in public wearing the black, red and gold colours of the nationalists, declaring that Prussia would merge into Germany.

The concessions did not last. In November 1848 the army returned to Berlin, the Prussian assembly was dissolved in December, and the king **imposed his own constitution**. It was revised in 1850 to give much more voting power to the wealthy through a three-class voting system.

### The Frankfurt Parliament

Elections were held across the Confederation, and the **Frankfurt Parliament** opened on **18 May 1848**. Its members were mostly well-educated middle-class men: lawyers, professors, officials and businessmen. It set out to write a constitution for a united Germany and a list of basic rights.

It faced serious problems:

- **No power of its own.** It had no army, no taxes and no civil service. When Prussia made an armistice with Denmark over Schleswig-Holstein in August 1848, the parliament protested but in the end had to accept it.
- **Divisions.** Members argued for months over a Greater Germany or a Little Germany, and moderate liberals feared radical democrats as much as they feared the princes.
- **The return of the old powers.** By late 1848 Austria and Prussia had recovered control of their own capitals and armies.

In March 1849 the parliament finally chose the Little German solution and elected Frederick William IV as Emperor of the Germans. In **April 1849** he refused. He would not accept a crown from an elected assembly, he believed kings ruled by the grace of God, and he feared war with Austria. Austria withdrew its members, and most others left. A small remnant moved to Stuttgart and was dispersed by troops in June 1849, and risings in Baden and Saxony were crushed, largely by Prussian soldiers.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the Frankfurt Parliament failed"}
1. **It depended on the princes:** with no army, it could only act if Prussia or Austria agreed.
2. **It was slow and divided:** debates over borders and rights gave the rulers time to recover.
3. **The rulers recovered:** by the end of 1848 the Austrian and Prussian armies had restored order.
4. **The crown was refused:** without Prussia's king, a Little Germany had no leader and no army.
5. **Result:** the parliament collapsed in 1849, showing that German unity would need power as well as ideas.
:::

## Austria restores its influence, 1849-50

Frederick William IV still wanted to lead Germany, but on terms agreed between princes. In 1849-50 Prussia tried to form the **Erfurt Union**, a union of German states under Prussian leadership. Austria, now recovered under its chief minister Schwarzenberg and supported by Russia, demanded that the old Confederation be restored. The two sides came close to war in 1850. Prussia backed down and, by the agreement of **Olmütz** in November 1850, abandoned its plan. The German Confederation was restored in 1851, with Austria again in the chair. Many Prussians called Olmütz a humiliation.

## Why Germany was not unified in 1848-50

Historians usually combine several reasons:

- **Weakness of the liberals:** no army, little support among peasants and workers, and divisions between moderates and radicals.
- **The Greater or Little Germany question:** Austria would not give up its non-German lands, and Prussia would not accept Austrian leadership.
- **The strength of the old order:** the armies of Austria and Prussia stayed loyal to their rulers.
- **Frederick William IV's refusal** of the crown in 1849.
- **Austria's recovery and Russian support**, which forced Prussia to back down at Olmütz.

The lesson many Germans drew was that unification would come from Prussian power, not from parliaments.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the German Confederation with a united Germany"}
The Confederation of 1815 was a loose league of sovereign states. Each kept its own ruler and army; the Diet was a meeting of ambassadors, not an elected parliament.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Austria led the Zollverein"}
Prussia led the Zollverein. Austria stayed outside it, which is one reason Prussia gained economic leadership in Germany.
:::

:::mistake{title="Narrating 1848 instead of explaining failure"}
A question on why Germany was not unified in 1848-50 needs reasons (weakness, divisions, the princes' recovery, the refusal of the crown, Austria), not a diary of events.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The German Confederation of 1815 was a loose league of 39 states chaired by Austria.
- Nationalism grew among the educated middle class, while the Prussian-led Zollverein (1834) linked the economies of most German states and left Austria outside.
- In 1848 the Frankfurt Parliament tried to unite Germany by constitutional means but had no army and was divided.
- Frederick William IV refused the imperial crown in April 1849, and the parliament collapsed.
- At Olmütz (1850) Austria forced Prussia to abandon the Erfurt Union, and the Confederation was restored in 1851.
:::
