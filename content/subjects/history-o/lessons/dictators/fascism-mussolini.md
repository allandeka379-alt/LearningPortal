## Introduction

Between the two world wars democracy collapsed in much of Europe. In Italy in 1922 and in Germany in 1933, leaders came to power who promised order, national greatness and the destruction of their enemies. Their ideas, **fascism** and **Nazism**, led to dictatorship at home and aggression abroad, including Italy's brutal conquest of Ethiopia in 1935-1936.

ZIMSEC Paper 2 asks you to explain the concepts of fascism and Nazism, to outline the problems that led to dictatorship in Italy and Germany, and to describe the domestic and foreign policies of Mussolini and Hitler. This lesson covers the ideas, the problems and Mussolini; Hitler's rise and rule are covered in the lessons on Nazi Germany.

You should already know the outcome of the First World War and the peace settlement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fascism"}
A political movement that puts the nation and the state above the individual. It rejects democracy and communism and relies on a single party, a supreme leader, propaganda and force. The name comes from the *fasces*, a bundle of rods with an axe, the ancient Roman symbol of authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Nazism (National Socialism)"}
The German form of fascism led by Adolf Hitler. Besides the general features of fascism it placed racism and antisemitism at its centre, claiming that "Aryan" Germans were a master race that needed living space in the east.
:::

:::definition{term="Dictatorship"}
Rule by one person or party with unlimited power, not checked by elections, courts, a free press or opposition parties.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate state"}
Mussolini's system in which employers and workers in each industry were organised in state-controlled corporations, in theory to end class conflict. Independent trade unions and strikes were banned.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- March 1919: Mussolini founds the *Fasci di Combattimento* in Milan.
- October 1922: March on Rome; Mussolini becomes Prime Minister.
- June 1924: murder of the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti.
- 11 February 1929: Lateran Treaty with the Pope.
- October 1935: invasion of Abyssinia.
- 1936: Rome-Berlin Axis; May 1939: Pact of Steel.
:::

## Features of fascism and Nazism

| Feature | Fascism (Italy) | Nazism (Germany) |
|---|---|---|
| Nation and state | The state is everything; extreme nationalism | The racial nation (Volk) is everything |
| Leader | Il Duce, whose word is law | Der Führer (the "leader principle") |
| Democracy | Rejected; one-party state | Rejected; one-party state |
| Enemies | Communists, socialists, liberals | Communists, Jews and others labelled "enemies" of the Volk |
| Race | Not central at first; anti-Jewish laws in 1938 | Central: antisemitism and "racial purity" |
| War | Glorified; empire in the Mediterranean and Africa | Glorified; Lebensraum in eastern Europe |
| Methods | Propaganda, censorship, secret police, violence | Propaganda, censorship, secret police, terror |

## Problems that favoured dictatorship

### Italy

- **A "mutilated victory":** Italy had fought on the winning side and lost over half a million soldiers (estimates vary), yet at Paris it did not receive Fiume or most of Dalmatia. Many Italians felt cheated.
- **Economic crisis:** war debts, inflation, rising unemployment and returning soldiers without jobs.
- **Fear of communism:** in 1919-1920 there were waves of strikes, workers occupied factories and peasants seized land. Industrialists, landowners and the middle classes feared revolution.
- **Weak governments:** a series of short-lived coalition governments failed to restore order.

### Germany

- **Resentment of Versailles:** the "Diktat", war guilt and reparations.
- **Economic disasters:** hyperinflation in 1923 and the Great Depression after 1929, when unemployment rose to about six million by 1932.
- **Weak governments:** coalitions collapsed, and from 1930 Chancellors ruled by emergency decree.
- **Fear of communism:** the growth of the Communist Party frightened the middle classes and big business.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: how fear of communism helped Mussolini"}
1. In 1919-1920 strikes and factory occupations spread across northern Italy.
2. The government seemed unable or unwilling to stop them.
3. Mussolini's Blackshirt squads attacked socialists, broke strikes and burned union offices.
4. Industrialists and landowners gave money, and the police and army often looked the other way.
5. Many respectable Italians came to see fascism as the only defence against revolution.
:::

## Mussolini's rise to power

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| March 1919 | *Fasci di Combattimento* founded in Milan |
| 1920-1922 | Blackshirt violence against socialists and trade unions |
| May 1921 | Fascists win 35 seats in parliament |
| November 1921 | National Fascist Party formed |
| October 1922 | March on Rome; the King appoints Mussolini Prime Minister |
| 1923 | Acerbo Law gives two-thirds of seats to the largest party |
| June 1924 | Matteotti murdered |
| 1925-1926 | Dictatorship established; opposition parties banned |

In October 1922 thousands of Blackshirts converged on Rome. Prime Minister Facta asked King **Victor Emmanuel III** to declare martial law, but the King refused and instead invited Mussolini to become Prime Minister. Mussolini then built a dictatorship step by step. The **Acerbo Law** (1923) ensured a large majority in the 1924 election. When the socialist deputy **Giacomo Matteotti**, who had denounced fascist violence, was murdered by fascists in 1924, opposition deputies walked out of parliament, but the King did not dismiss Mussolini. In January 1925 Mussolini took responsibility for what had happened and moved towards one-party rule; by 1926 other parties were banned.

## Domestic policies

- **Repression:** censorship of the press, a secret police (the OVRA), special courts, and internal exile (*confino*) for opponents.
- **Propaganda:** the cult of *Il Duce*, slogans on walls, control of radio, film and schools; youth organisations such as the Balilla.
- **The corporate state:** corporations for each sector replaced free trade unions; strikes were banned.
- **Economic "battles":** the **Battle for Grain** (from 1925) raised wheat output but at the expense of other crops; the **Battle for the Lira** revalued the currency; the **Battle for Births** (from 1927) failed to raise the birth rate much; marshes such as the Pontine Marshes were drained and roads built.
- **The Church:** the **Lateran Treaty** (1929) recognised the Vatican City as an independent state and made Catholicism the state religion, ending the quarrel between the Pope and the Italian state since 1870 and winning Mussolini Catholic support.
- **Race:** in 1938 anti-Jewish laws were introduced, following the Nazi example.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how successful were Mussolini's domestic policies?"}
**Successes claimed:** order restored, agreement with the Church, public works and land drainage, more wheat.
**Failures:** living standards for many workers fell, the south stayed poor, the Battle for Births failed, and the costs of war in Ethiopia and Spain strained the economy.
**Judgement:** historians generally argue that fascist propaganda exaggerated the regime's achievements; its control of society was less complete than Nazi control of Germany.
:::

## Foreign policy

- **Corfu (1923):** forced Greece to pay compensation.
- **Fiume (1924):** gained by agreement with Yugoslavia.
- **Albania:** turned into an Italian dependency in the 1920s and invaded in April 1939.
- **Austria (1934):** troops to the Brenner Pass to deter a Nazi takeover; **Stresa Front** with Britain and France (1935).
- **Abyssinia (1935-1936):** conquered with aircraft and poison gas; Italy left the League in 1937.
- **Spain (1936-1939):** troops and aircraft sent to help Franco.
- **Germany:** Rome-Berlin Axis (1936), Anti-Comintern Pact (1937), acceptance of the Anschluss (1938), Munich mediator (1938), **Pact of Steel** (May 1939). Italy entered the Second World War in June 1940.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Mussolini seized power by force"}
The March on Rome was a threat of force; Mussolini was legally appointed Prime Minister by the King. His dictatorship was built afterwards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating fascism and Nazism as identical"}
They share many features, but racism and antisemitism were central to Nazism from the start, while Italian fascism adopted anti-Jewish laws only in 1938.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Hitler's policies in a Mussolini question"}
Read the question carefully. Use German examples only when the question asks about both dictators or about Nazism.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2: explain the concept"}
"Explain the concept of fascism" needs a definition and several features, each with an Italian example (one-party state: opposition parties banned in 1926).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How far were inter-war problems responsible for Mussolini's rise?"}
Weigh the problems (mutilated victory, economic crisis, fear of communism, weak governments) against Mussolini's own methods (violence, propaganda) and the King's decision in 1922.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fascism puts the nation and state above the individual, rejects democracy and communism, and relies on one party, a leader, propaganda and force; Nazism added racism at its core.
- In Italy the "mutilated victory", economic crisis, fear of communism and weak governments favoured Mussolini.
- After the March on Rome (October 1922) the King appointed Mussolini, who built a dictatorship by 1926.
- His domestic policies included repression, propaganda, the corporate state, the economic "battles" and the Lateran Treaty (1929).
- His foreign policy moved from Corfu and Stresa to Abyssinia, Spain and alliance with Hitler.
:::
