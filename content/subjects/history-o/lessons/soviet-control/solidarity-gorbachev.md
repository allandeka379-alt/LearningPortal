## Introduction

In 1980 the Soviet Union controlled half of Europe and seemed permanent. By the end of 1989 communist rule had collapsed in Poland, Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria and Romania, and in December 1991 the Soviet Union itself ceased to exist. Few people had predicted it.

The story begins with workers in a Polish shipyard and ends with a Soviet leader who refused to send in the tanks. The collapse changed the world: the Cold War ended, and in southern Africa the end of superpower rivalry helped open the way to Namibia's independence in 1990 and to the negotiations that ended apartheid in South Africa.

You should already know how the USSR controlled Eastern Europe, and how it crushed Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Solidarity"}
The independent trade union founded in Poland in 1980, led by Lech Wałęsa. It became a mass movement for reform.
:::

:::definition{term="Martial law"}
Rule by the army, with normal rights suspended. General Jaruzelski declared martial law in Poland in December 1981.
:::

:::definition{term="Perestroika"}
"Restructuring": Gorbachev's policy of reforming the Soviet economy, including some private enterprise and less central control.
:::

:::definition{term="Glasnost"}
"Openness": Gorbachev's policy of allowing more freedom of speech and more honest discussion of problems and of the past.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- **August 1980:** strikes at the Lenin Shipyard, Gdańsk; the Gdańsk Agreement recognises Solidarity.
- **13 December 1981:** martial law in Poland.
- **March 1985:** **Mikhail Gorbachev** becomes Soviet leader.
- **June 1989:** partly free elections in Poland; Solidarity wins almost every seat it could contest.
- **9 November 1989:** the Berlin Wall is opened.
- **December 1991:** the USSR is dissolved.
:::

## Solidarity in Poland

### Why Solidarity emerged

Poland had a history of protest against communist rule: there had been strikes and riots in 1956, 1970 and 1976, often over food prices. By the late 1970s the economy was in crisis and the government was deeply in debt to Western banks. Poles also had a powerful source of national identity outside the party: the **Catholic Church**. In 1978 a Pole, Karol Wojtyła, became **Pope John Paul II**, and his visit to Poland in 1979 drew enormous crowds and gave people confidence.

### 1980-1981

In **August 1980**, after food price rises, workers at the **Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk** went on strike, led by an electrician, **Lech Wałęsa**. Strikes spread across the country. The government gave way: in the **Gdańsk Agreement** (31 August 1980) it accepted the right to form free trade unions and to strike, and promised more freedom of expression. **Solidarity** was formed and grew to about ten million members, roughly a third of Poland's working-age population.

The USSR was alarmed: an independent organisation of this size challenged the Communist Party's claim to represent the workers. Soviet troops held exercises near the border. On **13 December 1981** the Polish leader, **General Wojciech Jaruzelski**, declared **martial law**. Solidarity was banned, and Wałęsa and thousands of activists were detained. Jaruzelski later claimed he had acted to prevent a Soviet invasion; historians still debate whether the USSR would have invaded.

### Survival underground

Solidarity continued underground, with secret newspapers and support from the Church and the West. Wałęsa was awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize** in 1983. Poland's economy did not recover, and by 1988 a new wave of strikes forced the government to negotiate.

## Gorbachev and the USSR

**Mikhail Gorbachev** became General Secretary of the Soviet Communist Party in **March 1985**. He inherited serious problems:

- A **stagnating economy**, falling behind the West in technology and living standards.
- The **arms race**: military spending took a large share of national wealth, and US President Ronald Reagan's plans for space-based missile defence (the Strategic Defence Initiative) threatened a costly new race.
- The **war in Afghanistan** (from 1979), which was costly in lives and money and could not be won.
- The cost of supporting the economies of Eastern Europe.

His answer was reform at home through **perestroika** and **glasnost**, and better relations abroad. He signed arms-control agreements with the USA (the INF Treaty, 1987) and withdrew Soviet troops from Afghanistan (completed in February 1989).

Most importantly for Eastern Europe, Gorbachev made clear that the USSR would **no longer use force** to keep communist governments in power. In a speech to the United Nations in December 1988 he announced large cuts in Soviet forces in Europe and spoke of every nation's freedom to choose its own path. In 1989 a Soviet spokesman joked that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the "Sinatra Doctrine", after the song *My Way*: each country could now go its own way.

## The revolutions of 1989

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| August 1980 | Gdańsk strikes; Solidarity formed |
| December 1981 | Martial law in Poland |
| March 1985 | Gorbachev becomes Soviet leader |
| February-April 1989 | Round Table talks in Poland |
| June 1989 | Polish elections: Solidarity triumphs |
| September 1989 | Hungary opens its border with Austria to East Germans |
| 9 November 1989 | The Berlin Wall is opened |
| November-December 1989 | "Velvet Revolution" in Czechoslovakia |
| December 1989 | Revolution in Romania |
| 3 October 1990 | Germany reunited |
| December 1991 | The USSR is dissolved |

- **Poland.** Round Table talks between the government and Solidarity (February-April 1989) led to partly free elections in **June 1989**. Solidarity won almost every seat it was allowed to contest. In August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki of Solidarity became Prime Minister, the first non-communist head of government in the Soviet bloc since the 1940s. Gorbachev did not intervene.
- **Hungary.** Reform communists had already allowed other parties. In 1989 Hungary dismantled the fence on its border with Austria, and in September it let East Germans cross freely.
- **East Germany.** Tens of thousands of East Germans fled through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, while mass demonstrations, especially in Leipzig, demanded change. Gorbachev, visiting in October, urged reform, and the Soviet troops stationed in East Germany stayed in their barracks. On **9 November 1989** the government opened the border crossings in Berlin.
- **Czechoslovakia.** Peaceful mass protests in November 1989, the "Velvet Revolution", forced the communists out; the playwright and dissident **Václav Havel** became President in December.
- **Bulgaria and Romania.** Bulgaria's long-serving leader was removed in November 1989. In Romania the revolution was violent: the dictator Nicolae Ceaușescu was overthrown and executed in December 1989.

The Warsaw Pact was dissolved in 1991, and in December 1991 the Soviet Union broke up into fifteen independent states.

:::example{title="A significance explained step by step: why Solidarity mattered"}
1. **It was the first independent mass organisation** in the Soviet bloc, showing that workers, in whose name the party ruled, rejected the system.
2. **Repression did not destroy it:** martial law drove it underground, but it survived with the support of the Church and the West.
3. **It forced negotiation:** by 1989 the government needed Solidarity's cooperation to deal with the economic crisis.
4. **It led the way:** Poland's elections of June 1989, accepted by Moscow, showed others that change was now possible.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: how far was Gorbachev personally responsible?"}
**Gorbachev was decisive:** by refusing to use force and abandoning the Brezhnev Doctrine, he removed the threat that had crushed Hungary and Czechoslovakia. Glasnost also encouraged criticism of the system.
**Other factors:** long-term economic failure; the cost of the arms race and of Afghanistan; pressure from the USA under Reagan; the courage of people in Eastern Europe, such as Solidarity and the crowds in Leipzig and Prague; the influence of the Church; Western television showing a richer life.
**Judgement:** many historians argue that the underlying causes made collapse likely in the long run, but that Gorbachev's decisions explain why it happened in 1989, so quickly and, except in Romania, so peacefully.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Gorbachev wanted to end communism"}
Gorbachev wanted to reform and save communism, not end it. The collapse went far beyond what he intended.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Solidarity won power in 1981"}
In 1981 Solidarity was banned under martial law. It came to share power only after the elections of June 1989.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the fall of the Wall with the end of the USSR"}
The Wall opened in November 1989; Germany was reunited in October 1990; the USSR was dissolved in December 1991.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (c): 'How far was Gorbachev responsible…?'"}
Explain Gorbachev's role (reforms, refusal to use force) and then at least two other factors (economic failure, Solidarity and popular protest, the arms race and Reagan). Your judgement should explain how the factors were connected.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain the significance of Solidarity'"}
Significance means effects: show what changed because of Solidarity, in Poland and in the rest of the bloc, rather than retelling its story.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic crisis, the Catholic Church and the Pope's 1979 visit helped Solidarity to emerge from the Gdańsk strikes of August 1980.
- Jaruzelski's martial law (December 1981) drove Solidarity underground, but it survived and won the partly free elections of June 1989.
- Gorbachev (from 1985) faced economic stagnation, the arms race and Afghanistan; he introduced perestroika and glasnost and abandoned the Brezhnev Doctrine.
- In 1989 communist rule collapsed across Eastern Europe; the Berlin Wall opened on 9 November.
- Gorbachev's refusal to use force explains the timing and peaceful nature of the collapse, but long-term weaknesses and popular protest were also essential.
:::
