## Introduction

On the morning of 13 August 1961, Berliners woke to find their city cut in two by barbed wire and armed guards. Within days the wire was being replaced by concrete. For the next twenty-eight years the **Berlin Wall** divided families, streets and even cemeteries, and it became the best-known symbol of the Cold War.

The Wall raises a question that runs through the study of Soviet control in Eastern Europe: if communist rule was secure, why did a government need to wall in its own people? Many states in Africa and elsewhere have controlled their borders, but few have built a barrier mainly to stop their own citizens from leaving.

You should already know how Germany was divided after the Berlin Blockade (1948-49) and how the USSR controlled Eastern Europe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="German Democratic Republic (GDR)"}
Communist East Germany, set up in the Soviet zone in October 1949, with East Berlin as its capital. Its leader in 1961 was Walter Ulbricht.
:::

:::definition{term="Federal Republic of Germany (FRG)"}
Democratic West Germany, set up in May 1949, a member of NATO from 1955.
:::

:::definition{term="Brain drain"}
The loss of educated and skilled people, such as doctors, engineers and teachers, who emigrate to another country.
:::

:::definition{term="Checkpoint Charlie"}
The best-known crossing point between East and West Berlin, used by Allied personnel and foreigners.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **November 1958:** Khrushchev demands that the Western powers leave Berlin.
- **June 1961:** Kennedy and Khrushchev meet in Vienna.
- **13 August 1961:** the border through Berlin is closed; building of the Wall begins.
- **October 1961:** Soviet and American tanks face each other at Checkpoint Charlie.
- **26 June 1963:** Kennedy speaks in West Berlin.
- **9 November 1989:** the Wall is opened.
:::

## The problem of Berlin

Berlin lay more than 150 kilometres inside East Germany, but West Berlin belonged in practice to the West. Its American, British and French sectors were protected by Western troops and linked to West Germany.

For the GDR and the USSR, West Berlin caused three problems:

- **An escape route.** Although the border between East and West Germany had been closed and fortified since 1952, the border inside Berlin remained open. East Germans could take a train to West Berlin and then fly to West Germany. Historians estimate that roughly 2.5 to 3 million people left East Germany between 1949 and 1961.
- **A brain drain.** Many of those who left were young and skilled: doctors, engineers, teachers and skilled workers. Their loss damaged the East German economy and was deeply embarrassing for a state that claimed communism was better.
- **A shop window for capitalism.** After Marshall Aid, West Berlin was prosperous, with shops full of goods, while East Berlin was drab. West Berlin was also a centre for Western spies and radio propaganda.

### Khrushchev's pressure

In **November 1958** Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers leave West Berlin within six months and make it a "free city". The West refused and the deadline passed. At the **Vienna summit** in June 1961 he renewed his demands to the new and inexperienced President **John F. Kennedy**. Kennedy responded by increasing American defence spending and military readiness, but he made clear that the USA would defend its rights in West Berlin, not East Berlin.

Meanwhile the flow of refugees increased sharply in 1961 as East Germans feared that the door would soon close.

## Building the Wall

In the early hours of **13 August 1961**, East German troops and police closed the border between East and West Berlin with barbed wire and roadblocks. Over the following days and months, the wire was replaced by a concrete wall. Later the Wall was strengthened into a deep border zone with a second wall, watchtowers, searchlights, dogs and a cleared "death strip".

The GDR called it the "Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart", claiming it protected East Germany from Western aggression. In reality its purpose was to stop East Germans from leaving.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the Wall was built in August 1961"}
1. **The open border:** Berlin was the one place where East Germans could still reach the West easily.
2. **Rising numbers:** emigration increased sharply in 1961, especially among young and skilled people.
3. **Economic damage and humiliation:** the GDR was losing its workforce, and every refugee was propaganda for the West.
4. **No other way out:** Khrushchev's demands for the West to leave Berlin (1958 and 1961) had failed.
5. **Result:** closing the border was a way to solve the problem without fighting the West, since Western rights in West Berlin were not touched.
:::

## Reactions

**The West** protested but did not try to remove the Wall. Kennedy is reported to have said privately that a wall was better than a war: the Wall was built on East German territory, and it left West Berlin and Western access to it untouched. Many West Berliners, however, felt abandoned.

Tension rose in **October 1961**, when a dispute over the rights of American officials to enter East Berlin led to Soviet and American tanks facing each other at **Checkpoint Charlie** for about sixteen hours before both sides withdrew.

On **26 June 1963** Kennedy visited West Berlin and spoke to an enormous crowd. He declared "Ich bin ein Berliner" ("I am a Berliner") (John F. Kennedy, speech in West Berlin, 26 June 1963), expressing American solidarity with the city. The speech was a propaganda triumph for the West.

## Significance until 1989

- **A stabiliser.** The Wall stopped the flow of refugees and allowed the GDR's economy to stabilise. Some historians argue that, by ending the dispute over Berlin, it reduced the danger of war in Europe.
- **A symbol of failure.** For the West, a communist state that had to imprison its own people had shown that its system could not win support freely. The Wall became the most powerful symbol of the division of Europe.
- **Human cost.** Families were separated for decades. Later research has found that at least 140 people were killed at the Wall while trying to cross or in connection with it.
- **Its end.** On **9 November 1989**, under pressure from mass protests and a flood of East Germans leaving through Hungary, the GDR opened the border crossings. Crowds climbed on the Wall and began to break it down. Germany was reunited on 3 October 1990.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: did the Wall show Soviet strength or weakness?"}
**Strength:** the USSR and GDR solved the refugee problem in a single night, and the West accepted it without a fight.
**Weakness:** the regime could keep people only by force; the Wall admitted that many East Germans preferred the West.
**Judgement:** in the short term the Wall strengthened Soviet control, but in the long term it was a lasting propaganda defeat and, when it opened in 1989, a sign of collapse.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Wall surrounded all of Berlin from 1949"}
The Wall was built in August 1961. It enclosed West Berlin, separating it from East Berlin and the surrounding GDR.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Blockade and the Wall"}
The Blockade (1948-49) tried to force the West out of Berlin. The Wall (1961) was built to stop East Germans from leaving.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Wall was knocked down in 1991"}
The crossings were opened on 9 November 1989. Germany was reunited in October 1990; the USSR collapsed in December 1991.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1 (b): 'Explain why the Berlin Wall was built'"}
Explain the refugees, the brain drain, the embarrassment to the GDR and the failure of Khrushchev's demands. Link each to the decision of August 1961.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2: Kennedy's speech or Wall cartoons"}
Ask what the source was for. Kennedy's 1963 speech aimed to reassure West Berliners and win a propaganda victory; it does not show what he had said privately in 1961.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- West Berlin, inside East Germany, was an escape route, a shop window for capitalism and a centre of Western propaganda.
- Roughly 2.5 to 3 million people left East Germany between 1949 and 1961, many of them young and skilled.
- Khrushchev's demands (1958, Vienna 1961) failed; the border was closed on 13 August 1961 and the Wall was built.
- The West protested but accepted the Wall; Kennedy's 1963 speech showed support for West Berlin.
- The Wall stabilised the GDR but became a symbol of communist failure until it opened on 9 November 1989.
:::
