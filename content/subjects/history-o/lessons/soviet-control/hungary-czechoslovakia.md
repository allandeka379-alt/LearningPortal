## Introduction

Twice in twelve years, Soviet tanks rolled into the capital of an allied communist country to stop it from going its own way. In Budapest in 1956 there was fierce fighting; in Prague in 1968 there was mostly peaceful protest. Both times the USSR restored control, and both times the West protested but did not intervene.

These crises are central to the Cambridge key question "How secure was the USSR's control over Eastern Europe, 1948-c.1989?". They also show a problem familiar to students of African history: an outside power can keep a government in place by force, but force alone does not win the people's loyalty.

You should already know how the USSR gained control of Eastern Europe by 1948 and what the Warsaw Pact was.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="De-Stalinisation"}
Khrushchev's policy, from 1956, of criticising Stalin's crimes and easing some of the harshest features of his rule.
:::

:::definition{term="Prague Spring"}
The period of reform in Czechoslovakia in 1968 under Alexander Dubček, ended by the Warsaw Pact invasion in August.
:::

:::definition{term="Socialism with a human face"}
Dubček's description of his aim: to keep communism, but with more freedom of speech, less censorship and less control by the secret police.
:::

:::definition{term="Brezhnev Doctrine"}
The Soviet claim, set out in 1968, that the communist states had the right to intervene in any member state where communism was threatened.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- **February 1956:** Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" attacking Stalin.
- **23 October 1956:** demonstrations in Budapest begin the Hungarian Rising; **Imre Nagy** becomes Prime Minister.
- **4 November 1956:** Soviet forces attack Budapest; **János Kádár** takes power.
- **January 1968:** **Alexander Dubček** becomes leader of the Czechoslovak Communist Party.
- **20-21 August 1968:** Warsaw Pact forces invade Czechoslovakia.
:::

## Soviet control after 1948

After 1948 the satellites were run on the Soviet model. Each had a single ruling communist party, a secret police, censorship and a planned economy tied to the USSR through **Comecon**. Soviet troops were stationed in several of them, and from 1955 the **Warsaw Pact** bound them in a military alliance. Living standards were low, consumer goods were scarce, and religion was restricted. In 1953, soon after Stalin's death, a workers' rising in East Germany was put down by Soviet troops.

## Hungary, 1956

### Causes of opposition

- **Hatred of Rákosi's rule.** Mátyás Rákosi, a hard-line Stalinist, ruled through the secret police (the ÁVH). Thousands were imprisoned.
- **Poverty and Soviet exploitation.** Living standards were low, and Hungarians resented that the USSR took Hungarian goods and resources on unfair terms.
- **Religion and national feeling.** Most Hungarians were Catholic, and the communists persecuted the Church. Many resented Soviet troops on Hungarian soil.
- **Hope from de-Stalinisation.** Khrushchev's Secret Speech of February 1956 and his reconciliation with Tito's Yugoslavia suggested that some independence might now be allowed. In June 1956 workers' protests in Poznań in Poland led to reforms under Gomułka there. In July 1956 the USSR replaced Rákosi.

### Events

On **23 October 1956** students and workers demonstrated in Budapest. They pulled down a huge statue of Stalin, and fighting broke out when the secret police fired on the crowd. Soviet troops in the city were attacked. **Imre Nagy**, a reform communist, became Prime Minister, and Soviet forces withdrew from Budapest at the end of October.

Nagy announced reforms: free elections, freedom of speech and the release of political prisoners. Then, on 1 November, he announced that Hungary would **leave the Warsaw Pact** and become neutral.

For Khrushchev this went too far. On **4 November** large Soviet forces returned and attacked Budapest. Hungarians fought back, but they were crushed within days. Historians estimate that about 2,500 Hungarians were killed, and that about 200,000 fled abroad. **János Kádár** was installed as leader. Nagy was arrested, and in 1958 he was secretly tried and executed.

### Why did the West not help?

Radio Free Europe, funded by the USA, had encouraged Hungarians to resist, and many expected Western help. But the USA was not prepared to risk war with the USSR over a country in the Soviet sphere. In addition, Britain and France were involved in the **Suez Crisis** at the same moment, which divided the West and distracted the United Nations.

## Czechoslovakia, 1968

### Causes of opposition

By the 1960s Czechoslovakia's economy, once one of the most advanced in Europe, was stagnating. Writers and students demanded more freedom, and many resented the dull, repressive rule of Antonín Novotný. In **January 1968** the party replaced Novotný with **Alexander Dubček**, a reformer.

### The Prague Spring

Dubček's **Action Programme** (April 1968) promised "socialism with a human face": less censorship, freedom to travel, more power for parliament and some economic reform. Unlike Nagy, Dubček insisted that Czechoslovakia would stay communist and remain in the **Warsaw Pact**. Even so, the Soviet leader **Leonid Brezhnev**, and the leaders of East Germany and Poland, feared that the reforms would spread to their own countries.

### The invasion

On the night of **20-21 August 1968** Soviet troops, with forces from other Warsaw Pact states, entered Czechoslovakia; historians estimate that up to half a million troops eventually took part. Romania refused to join in. Dubček told the people not to resist with force. Instead there was widespread **non-violent resistance**: people argued with soldiers, removed road signs and painted slogans. Dubček was taken to Moscow and forced to reverse most reforms; in April 1969 he was replaced by Gustáv Husák. In January 1969 a student, **Jan Palach**, set himself on fire in protest and died.

The USSR justified the invasion with what became known as the **Brezhnev Doctrine**: the socialist states had a duty to act when socialism was threatened in any one of them.

:::example{title="A comparison explained: Hungary 1956 and Czechoslovakia 1968"}
**Similarities:** both were caused by resentment of Soviet control and hopes of reform; both reformers were communists; both were crushed by Soviet-led forces; in both the West did nothing; both leaders were replaced by men loyal to Moscow.
**Differences:**
1. **Aims:** Nagy took Hungary out of the Warsaw Pact; Dubček promised to stay in it.
2. **Resistance:** Hungarians fought, and thousands died; Czechoslovaks mostly used non-violent resistance.
3. **Forces:** Hungary was crushed by Soviet troops; Czechoslovakia was invaded by the Warsaw Pact.
4. **Fate of the leader:** Nagy was executed; Dubček was demoted and survived.
These differences help to explain why the bloodshed was far greater in 1956.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation: did these crises show strength or weakness?"}
**Strength:** the USSR restored control quickly both times, and the West accepted it. The Brezhnev Doctrine warned others not to try.
**Weakness:** the USSR could keep control only with tanks. The crises showed that many people rejected Soviet rule, and that the system lacked consent. In the 1980s, when the USSR was no longer willing to use force, its control collapsed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Dubček wanted to leave the Warsaw Pact"}
That was Nagy in Hungary in 1956. Dubček promised that Czechoslovakia would remain in the Pact.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Czechoslovaks fought a war against the invaders"}
Resistance in 1968 was mainly non-violent. Dubček told people not to resist with arms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the leaders"}
Hungary: Rákosi, then Nagy, then Kádár. Czechoslovakia: Novotný, then Dubček, then Husák. Soviet leaders: Khrushchev in 1956, Brezhnev in 1968.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 1: compare the two crises"}
For "How similar were…?", organise by theme (causes, aims, events, Soviet reaction, results), not by telling one story and then the other. Finish with a judgement on whether they were more alike or more different.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2: sources on 1956 or 1968"}
Many sources are Soviet justifications or Western condemnations. Ask who produced each, for whom and why, and check its claims against what you know (for example, the Soviet claim that it was invited to help).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After 1948 the satellites had one-party rule, secret police and economies tied to the USSR; resentment was widespread.
- Hungary 1956: de-Stalinisation raised hopes; Nagy promised free elections and to leave the Warsaw Pact; Soviet forces crushed the rising on 4 November.
- Czechoslovakia 1968: Dubček's Prague Spring promised "socialism with a human face" but stayed in the Pact; the Warsaw Pact invaded on 20-21 August.
- The Brezhnev Doctrine (1968) claimed the right to intervene wherever communism was threatened.
- The West did not intervene in either crisis; Soviet control survived, but only by force.
:::
