## Introduction

Between 1957 and 1964 African politics in Southern Rhodesia changed completely. Small associations led by a few educated men gave way to mass parties that held huge rallies, organised strikes and boycotts, petitioned the United Nations and demanded **one person, one vote**. Each party was banned in turn, and a new one took its place. By 1964 the main leaders were in detention and the movement had split into ZAPU and ZANU, the two parties that would lead the armed struggle.

You should already know the grievances created by colonial laws such as the Land Apportionment Act and the Native Land Husbandry Act, and why mass nationalism spread across Africa after 1945. Examiners ask you to **identify the movements**, **describe their aims and activities** and **explain the challenges they faced**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nationalist movement"}
An organisation that aims to end colonial rule and win independence and majority rule for the people of a country.
:::

:::definition{term="Majority rule"}
Government chosen by all adult citizens voting equally (one person, one vote), so that the government reflects the majority of the population.
:::

:::definition{term="Restriction and detention"}
Detention is imprisonment without trial; restriction is confining a person to a remote area. Both were used against nationalist leaders.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Parties and dates to learn"}
- 1955: City Youth League formed in Salisbury.
- 12 September 1957: Southern Rhodesia African National Congress (SRANC) formed; banned 26 February 1959.
- 1 January 1960: National Democratic Party (NDP) formed; banned December 1961.
- 17 December 1961: Zimbabwe African People's Union (ZAPU) formed; banned September 1962.
- 8 August 1963: Zimbabwe African National Union (ZANU) formed.
- August 1964: ZANU and the People's Caretaker Council (PCC) banned; leaders detained.
:::

### The City Youth League (1955)

Young activists in Salisbury, including **James Chikerema** and **George Nyandoro**, formed the City Youth League. It was more radical than earlier associations and used direct action, most famously a **bus boycott** in Salisbury in 1956 against higher fares. It showed that ordinary township residents could be mobilised.

### The Southern Rhodesia African National Congress (1957-1959)

The Youth League joined with the older African National Congress of Bulawayo to form the **SRANC** on 12 September 1957, with **Joshua Nkomo** as president.

- **Aims:** one person, one vote; an end to racial discrimination; repeal of the Native Land Husbandry Act and other unjust laws.
- **Activities:** rallies in towns, campaigns in the reserves against destocking and land allocation, and representations to the government.
- **Fate:** on 26 February 1959 the government declared a state of emergency, banned the SRANC and detained hundreds of its members without trial.

### The National Democratic Party (1960-1961)

The **NDP** was formed on 1 January 1960. Its first president was Michael Mawema; Joshua Nkomo became president later that year.

- **Activities:** mass rallies; demonstrations after the arrest of NDP leaders in July 1960, which led to marches in Salisbury and rioting in Salisbury and Bulawayo in which police killed several people; petitions to Britain and the United Nations.
- **The 1961 constitution:** NDP delegates attended the constitutional conference of 1960-61. The new constitution offered Africans 15 of the 65 seats in parliament. After strong criticism from its members, the NDP rejected it and organised its own unofficial referendum, in which Africans voted overwhelmingly against it.
- **Fate:** banned on 9 December 1961.

### ZAPU (1961-1962)

Within days the same leaders formed the **Zimbabwe African People's Union (ZAPU)** on 17 December 1961, again under Nkomo. ZAPU continued rallies and petitions, and during 1962 some supporters turned to sabotage, such as burning property. ZAPU was banned in September 1962. In December 1962 the Rhodesian Front, which opposed any move towards majority rule, won the election.

### The split: ZANU and the PCC (1963-1964)

In 1963 disagreements grew within the banned ZAPU leadership over strategy and leadership, including a plan to set up a government in exile. A group of leaders broke away and formed the **Zimbabwe African National Union (ZANU)** on 8 August 1963, with **Ndabaningi Sithole** as president and **Robert Mugabe** as secretary-general; other founders included Herbert Chitepo, Leopold Takawira and Enos Nkala. Nkomo's supporters formed the **People's Caretaker Council (PCC)** to continue ZAPU's work inside the country.

The split led to bitter rivalry and clashes between supporters in the townships in 1963-64. In August 1964 the government of Ian Smith banned both ZANU and the PCC and sent their leaders to detention or restriction, including camps such as Gonakudzingwa and Sikombela. Most would remain there for about ten years.

:::example{title="A balanced explanation of the 1963 split"}
Historians and participants explain the split in different ways. Those who formed ZANU argued that ZAPU's leadership was too ready to negotiate and to rely on outside pressure, and that the movement needed a more determined strategy inside the country. Nkomo's supporters argued that the split weakened African unity at the worst possible time. Personal rivalries also played a part. A good answer explains both points of view without taking sides, and notes the result: two parties, two armies and a divided struggle until the Patriotic Front of 1976 and the Unity Accord of 1987.
:::

## Challenges the movements faced

1. **Bans:** each party was banned in turn (1959, 1961, 1962, 1964), so leaders had to rebuild organisations again and again.
2. **Detention and restriction:** leaders were held without trial, separating them from their supporters.
3. **Repressive laws:** the **Unlawful Organisations Act (1959)**, the **Preventive Detention Act (1959)** and the **Law and Order (Maintenance) Act (1960)** gave the government sweeping powers to ban, detain and punish.
4. **Divisions:** the 1963 split and rivalry between supporters weakened the movement.
5. **Settler politics:** the Rhodesian Front's victory in 1962 and Ian Smith's rise in 1964 closed the door to reform.
6. **Limited outside help:** Britain did not intervene, and petitions to the United Nations brought words rather than action.
7. **Lack of resources:** parties had little money, no weapons and limited means of communication in rural areas.

:::example{title="A challenge analysed: why were the bans effective in the short term?"}
1. **What happened:** each ban made membership a crime and allowed police to seize property and records.
2. **Immediate effect:** leaders were detained, meetings stopped and supporters were afraid.
3. **How nationalists responded:** they formed a new party under a new name within weeks or months (SRANC, NDP, ZAPU).
4. **Long-term effect:** bans did not destroy nationalism, but by 1964, with leaders detained and peaceful protest blocked, many concluded that only armed struggle could succeed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Getting the order of the parties wrong"}
Learn the sequence: City Youth League (1955), SRANC (1957), NDP (1960), ZAPU (1961), ZANU (1963). ZANU was not formed before ZAPU.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking sides in the split"}
Examiners reward balanced, factual explanations. Explain each side's reasons and the effects of the split rather than blaming one leader.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the laws"}
"Challenges" answers are much stronger with named laws and dates: the Unlawful Organisations Act (1959), the Preventive Detention Act (1959) and the Law and Order (Maintenance) Act (1960).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify, describe, explain"}
On Paper 1, an **identify** part needs the names of parties and leaders. A **describe** part needs activities with examples (bus boycott, rallies, petitions, the 1961 unofficial referendum). An **explain** part on challenges needs each challenge and its effect on the movement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link to the next topic"}
A "how far" question may ask whether the failure of peaceful politics caused the armed struggle. Use the bans, detentions and the Rhodesian Front's rise from this topic as evidence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The City Youth League (1955) brought direct action such as the 1956 bus boycott.
- The SRANC (1957), NDP (1960) and ZAPU (1961), led by Joshua Nkomo, demanded one person, one vote and were each banned.
- In 1963 ZANU was formed under Ndabaningi Sithole; Nkomo's supporters formed the PCC; rivalry followed.
- Bans, detentions, repressive laws (1959-1960), divisions and the rise of the Rhodesian Front were the main challenges.
- By 1964 the leaders of both ZANU and ZAPU were detained, and peaceful politics had been blocked.
:::
