## Introduction

While the war was being fought in the countryside, politicians tried again and again to end it at the conference table. Between 1974 and 1979 there were talks on a railway bridge, in Geneva, in Salisbury and finally at **Lancaster House** in London. Most failed. The last succeeded, producing a ceasefire, a constitution and the elections that brought independence in April 1980.

Understanding why the earlier talks failed and why Lancaster House succeeded is a classic exam task. You should already know about UDI, the failed talks of 1966-1972 and the second phase of the armed struggle. Examiners ask you to **explain and analyse the attempts to end the war by peaceful means**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Détente"}
An easing of tension between opponents. In southern Africa in 1974-75 it meant South Africa's John Vorster and Zambia's Kenneth Kaunda working together to push Smith and the nationalists into talks.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal Settlement"}
The agreement of 3 March 1978 between Ian Smith and internal African leaders (Bishop Abel Muzorewa, Ndabaningi Sithole and Chief Jeremiah Chirau), which excluded the Patriotic Front.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceasefire"}
An agreement by both sides to stop fighting, usually with arrangements for monitoring and for the movement of forces.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- December 1974: nationalist leaders released from detention during détente.
- 25-26 August 1975: Victoria Falls conference.
- October-December 1976: Geneva conference.
- 3 March 1978: Internal Settlement.
- 1 June 1979: Zimbabwe-Rhodesia formed under Muzorewa.
- 10 September-15 December 1979: Lancaster House Conference; agreement signed 21 December 1979.
:::

### Détente and the Victoria Falls conference (1974-1975)

After the Portuguese coup of April 1974, South Africa's Prime Minister **John Vorster** feared that the war in Rhodesia would spread, while Zambia's President **Kenneth Kaunda** wanted peace and an open border. Together they pressed Smith to release the detained leaders in December 1974. ZANU, ZAPU and other groups agreed to unite under Muzorewa's African National Council.

On 25-26 August 1975 Smith and the nationalist leaders met in a railway carriage halfway across the **Victoria Falls bridge**, between Rhodesia and Zambia, with Vorster and Kaunda present.

**Why it failed:** Smith would not accept majority rule; the two sides could not even agree the conditions for further talks inside Rhodesia (such as immunity from arrest for exiled leaders); and the nationalist leaders were divided among themselves.

### The Geneva conference (1976)

In 1976 the US Secretary of State, Henry Kissinger, with South African support, persuaded Smith to announce (September 1976) that he accepted majority rule within two years. A conference chaired by Britain met in **Geneva** from October to December 1976, attended by Smith, the newly formed **Patriotic Front** (Nkomo and Mugabe), Muzorewa and Sithole.

**Why it failed:** the parties disagreed about the length of the transition and, above all, about who would control the army and police during it. The Patriotic Front would not accept a transition in which Smith's forces remained in control, and Smith would not give it up. The conference adjourned and never resumed.

### The Internal Settlement and Zimbabwe-Rhodesia (1978-1979)

Smith then negotiated with leaders inside the country. The **Internal Settlement** of 3 March 1978 created a transitional government. In April 1979 an election under a new constitution gave Muzorewa's United African National Council most of the African seats, and on 1 June 1979 the country was renamed **Zimbabwe-Rhodesia** with Muzorewa as Prime Minister.

**Why it failed:** whites kept 28 of the 100 seats and control of the army, police, judiciary and civil service, so real power changed little. The Patriotic Front was excluded and the war intensified. No country recognised the new government and sanctions stayed.

:::example{title="Analysing a failure: was the Internal Settlement majority rule?"}
1. **The claim:** an African Prime Minister led a government elected by all adults, so supporters called it majority rule.
2. **The test:** who controlled the state? Whites held 28% of the seats, enough to block constitutional change, and the security forces and civil service stayed under white control.
3. **The missing parties:** ZANU and ZAPU, whose armies were fighting the war, took no part.
4. **Conclusion:** it gave the appearance of change without the substance, so it could not end the war or win recognition.
:::

## The Lancaster House Conference (1979)

At the **Commonwealth meeting in Lusaka** (August 1979) African leaders and others persuaded the new British Prime Minister, Margaret Thatcher, not to recognise Zimbabwe-Rhodesia but to call an all-party conference. It met at Lancaster House in London from 10 September to 15 December 1979, chaired by the British Foreign Secretary, **Lord Carrington**. The delegations were the Patriotic Front, led by Joshua Nkomo and Robert Mugabe, and the Zimbabwe-Rhodesia government, led by Bishop Muzorewa and including Ian Smith. The agreement was signed on **21 December 1979**.

### What was agreed

**The constitution:**

- a House of Assembly of 100 seats, of which **20 were reserved for whites for seven years**;
- a Bill of Rights;
- protection of property: for **ten years**, land could be acquired for resettlement only on a **willing-seller, willing-buyer** basis with adequate compensation;
- a Prime Minister leading the government and a ceremonial President.

**The transition:**

- Rhodesia returned briefly to British rule under a Governor, **Lord Soames**, who arrived in December 1979;
- sanctions were lifted;
- elections would be held in February 1980 under Commonwealth observation.

**The ceasefire:**

- guerrillas gathered at **assembly points** inside the country;
- a **Commonwealth Monitoring Force** supervised the ceasefire.

### Why Lancaster House succeeded

1. **The war:** by 1979 the Rhodesian forces could not win; martial law covered most of the country, whites were emigrating and the economy was failing.
2. **Pressure from the Frontline States:** Mozambique and Zambia, suffering raids and economic damage, pressed the Patriotic Front to accept a deal.
3. **British involvement:** Britain took direct responsibility for the transition, which none of the earlier talks had offered.
4. **Commonwealth and international pressure** made recognition of Zimbabwe-Rhodesia impossible.
5. **Compromise on land:** the Patriotic Front accepted the land clauses after informal assurances that Britain and the United States would help to fund land purchase.

:::example{title="Weighing the agreement: a fair settlement?"}
Supporters argue that Lancaster House ended a war that had killed tens of thousands and delivered majority rule through free elections. Critics point out that the land clauses protected existing landowners for ten years, delaying land reform, and that the reserved white seats kept minority influence. A balanced judgement: the agreement achieved its main aim, independence with majority rule, but left the land question unresolved, which shaped Zimbabwe's history after 1980.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the 1975 and 1979 conferences"}
The Victoria Falls conference (1975) met on the bridge and failed. Lancaster House (1979) met in London and succeeded.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Internal Settlement brought independence"}
Zimbabwe-Rhodesia was not recognised by any country. Legal independence came on 18 April 1980, after Lancaster House and the February 1980 elections.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing terms without explaining success"}
Questions often ask why the conference succeeded. Explain the pressures on each side, not just the terms.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Compare the attempts"}
For "Explain why the attempts to end the war failed before 1979", take each attempt in turn (détente and Victoria Falls, Geneva, the Internal Settlement) and give a specific reason for each failure.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="How far questions on Lancaster House"}
"How far was the war the main reason for the success of Lancaster House?" needs the war's role first, then Frontline pressure, British involvement and international pressure, with a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Détente (1974-75) led to the release of leaders and the Victoria Falls conference (August 1975), which failed.
- The Geneva conference (1976) failed over the transition and control of the security forces.
- The Internal Settlement (3 March 1978) and Zimbabwe-Rhodesia (1 June 1979) excluded the Patriotic Front, left whites in control and won no recognition.
- Lancaster House (10 September-15 December 1979; signed 21 December) agreed a constitution, a British-supervised transition and a ceasefire.
- It succeeded because of the war, Frontline and Commonwealth pressure and Britain's direct role; its land clauses delayed land reform.
:::
