## Introduction

By 1964 every African nationalist party in Southern Rhodesia had been banned and its leaders were in detention. In November 1965 the Rhodesian Front declared UDI, and Britain refused to use force. Nationalists concluded that majority rule would not come through petitions and conferences. ZANU and ZAPU sent young men abroad for military training and began an armed struggle, known as the **Second Chimurenga/Umvukela**.

The first years of the war were hard and costly. Small groups of fighters crossed the Zambezi and were soon tracked down. Yet these early battles, above all **Chinhoyi on 28 April 1966**, became symbols of sacrifice, and their failures taught lessons that changed the war in the 1970s.

You should already know about the nationalist movements of 1957-1964 and about UDI. Examiners ask you to **explain the causes of the armed struggle**, **identify the countries where fighters trained**, **describe recruitment**, **describe the early battles** and **explain the failure of the early phase**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Armed struggle"}
The use of military force by a liberation movement to overthrow colonial or minority rule.
:::

:::definition{term="ZANLA and ZIPRA"}
The armed wings of the two nationalist parties: the **Zimbabwe African National Liberation Army (ZANLA)** of ZANU and the **Zimbabwe People's Revolutionary Army (ZIPRA)** of ZAPU.
:::

:::definition{term="Conventional warfare"}
Fighting with organised units in open battle against an enemy's forces, as regular armies do.
:::

:::definition{term="Guerrilla warfare"}
Fighting by small, mobile groups that rely on surprise, hit-and-run attacks and the support of the local population, avoiding open battle with a stronger army.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and places to learn"}
- 28 April 1966: battle of Chinhoyi (Sinoia); seven ZANLA fighters are killed.
- August-September 1967: Hwange (Wankie) campaign by ZIPRA with fighters of the ANC of South Africa.
- 1967-1968: Sipolilo campaign in the north.
- From 1967: South African police units deployed in Rhodesia.
:::

## Why the nationalists turned to armed struggle

1. **Bans and detentions:** the SRANC, NDP, ZAPU, ZANU and PCC were all banned, and the leaders were detained; peaceful organisation had become impossible.
2. **Failed negotiations:** conferences and petitions, such as those around the 1961 constitution, brought no progress towards majority rule.
3. **UDI (11 November 1965):** the settler government declared independence to prevent majority rule.
4. **Britain's refusal to use force:** Britain relied on sanctions, so there was no outside power willing to remove the regime.
5. **Land and other grievances:** land alienation, the Land Apportionment Act and the Native Land Husbandry Act left deep anger, especially in rural areas.
6. **Examples elsewhere:** armed struggles in Algeria and Kenya, and the wars beginning in Portuguese-ruled Mozambique and Angola, showed that colonial powers could be forced to negotiate.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why UDI pushed nationalists to arms"}
1. **Before UDI:** some nationalists hoped Britain would refuse independence until there was majority rule and might act against the settlers.
2. **UDI:** on 11 November 1965 the Rhodesian Front cut the legal link to Britain.
3. **Britain's response:** Wilson ruled out force and relied on sanctions.
4. **Conclusion drawn by nationalists:** no one else would remove the regime; only Africans themselves, with arms, could win majority rule. UDI therefore turned a choice into a necessity.
:::

## Recruitment and training

Both parties set up offices in exile, especially in **Lusaka (Zambia)** and **Dar es Salaam (Tanzania)**, with support from the **OAU Liberation Committee**, set up in 1963.

**Recruitment strategies** included:

- recruiting Zimbabweans living or working in Zambia and elsewhere;
- recruiting young people who had left the country, including students and refugees, sometimes after promising them education abroad;
- party branches secretly encouraging young men to cross the borders;
- in some cases, pressure or force, which participants and historians have acknowledged.

**Training** took place in friendly countries, including **Tanzania, Zambia, Ghana, Algeria and Egypt** in Africa, and further afield in the **Soviet Union, China, Cuba and North Korea**. In general ZIPRA received most of its training and weapons from the Soviet Union and its allies, while ZANLA was increasingly supported by China.

| 1970-1971 | Divisions within ZAPU in exile |

### Chinhoyi (28 April 1966)

A ZANLA group that had crossed into the country was located near Chinhoyi (then Sinoia). Rhodesian police and air force helicopters attacked, and all seven fighters were killed. Rhodesia presented it as a victory, but for nationalists the **Chinhoyi Seven** became heroes, and the battle is widely remembered as the start of the Second Chimurenga.

### Hwange (Wankie) and Sipolilo (1967-1968)

In August 1967 a large joint force of **ZIPRA** fighters and fighters of the **African National Congress of South Africa** (whose own route home lay through Rhodesia) crossed the Zambezi into the Hwange area. There were fierce battles with Rhodesian forces over several weeks; both sides suffered casualties, and the guerrillas were eventually killed, captured or driven back. A further campaign in the **Sipolilo** area of the north in 1967-68 met a similar fate. As a result of these campaigns, South Africa sent police units to support the Rhodesian forces.

## Why the early phase failed

1. **Conventional tactics:** fighters moved in large groups and sought battle, which suited the better-armed Rhodesian forces, with their helicopters, aircraft and trackers.
2. **No political preparation:** the local people had not been mobilised in advance, so fighters lacked food, shelter and information, and some were reported to the police.
3. **Poor intelligence and terrain:** the Zambezi valley was hot, dry and thinly populated, so groups were easy to track and hard to supply.
4. **Strong enemy:** Rhodesian security forces were well trained and equipped, and from 1967 South African police reinforced them; the Portuguese in Mozambique were also hostile.
5. **Divisions:** ZANU and ZAPU fought separately, and around 1970-71 ZAPU's exiled leadership was itself divided.
6. **Few bases near the border:** fighters had to cross the Zambezi from Zambia; there were not yet friendly bases along the long eastern border with Mozambique.

:::example{title="Weighing an explanation: tactics or politics?"}
Some accounts explain the failure mainly by military factors, such as Rhodesian air power and the use of large groups. Others stress politics: without the support of the people, guerrillas could not hide, eat or learn where the enemy was. A balanced answer shows how the two were linked. Because the people had not been prepared, fighters had to move in large, self-supporting groups, which made them easier targets. The movements drew exactly this lesson, and in the 1970s they politicised the people before fighting, using small groups.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the early phase as pointless"}
The early battles failed militarily, but they showed the regime would be resisted, inspired recruits and taught lessons that shaped the successful strategy of the 1970s.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up ZANLA and ZIPRA"}
ZANLA was ZANU's army (China was its main external supporter by the 1970s); ZIPRA was ZAPU's army (the Soviet Union was its main supporter). Chinhoyi was a ZANLA battle; Hwange was a ZIPRA campaign with the ANC of South Africa.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong date for Chinhoyi"}
The battle of Chinhoyi took place on 28 April 1966, after UDI, not in 1965 or 1972.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bans, detentions, failed negotiations, UDI, Britain's refusal to use force and land grievances led nationalists to armed struggle.
- Recruits came from exiles, workers, students and refugees; they trained in Tanzania, Zambia, Ghana, Algeria, Egypt, the USSR, China and elsewhere.
- Chinhoyi (28 April 1966) is remembered as the start of the Second Chimurenga; Hwange (1967) and Sipolilo (1967-68) followed.
- The early phase failed because of conventional tactics, lack of political preparation, poor intelligence, strong Rhodesian and South African forces and divisions.
- Its lessons led to the guerrilla strategy and politicisation of the people in the 1970s.
:::
