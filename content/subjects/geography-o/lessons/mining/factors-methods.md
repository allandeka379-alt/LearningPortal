## Introduction

Finding a mineral is only the first step. Many deposits in Zimbabwe are known but not worked, while others have been mined for over a century. Whether a deposit is mined depends on how rich and how deep it is, whether it can be reached and transported, what the mineral sells for and what the government allows. Once the decision to mine is made, the **method** depends mainly on how deep the mineral lies and what shape the deposit has.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mining"}
The extraction of valuable minerals from the Earth's crust, either at the surface or underground.
:::

:::definition{term="Grade"}
The concentration of the valuable mineral in the ore, for example grams of gold per tonne of rock (g/t) or the percentage of metal in the ore.
:::

:::definition{term="Overburden"}
The soil and rock lying on top of a mineral deposit that must be removed before surface mining can begin.
:::

:::definition{term="Reserves"}
The amount of a mineral in a deposit that can be extracted profitably with present technology and prices.
:::

## Factors influencing mining

### Physical factors

- **Size of the deposit:** a large deposit justifies the cost of roads, power lines and plant because it will last many years.
- **Grade:** rich ore yields more mineral per tonne of rock mined, so costs per kilogram are lower.
- **Depth and shape:** shallow deposits can be mined cheaply from the surface; deep ones need expensive shafts, ventilation and pumping.
- **Water:** needed to process ore, but flooding of underground workings must be pumped out.
- **Relief and access:** steep slopes or remote valleys make roads and railways costly.

### Economic factors

- **Capital:** a large mine can cost hundreds of millions of US dollars before it produces anything; this often comes from foreign investors.
- **Transport:** bulky, low-value minerals such as coal and chrome need rail; gold, platinum and diamonds are light and valuable for their weight.
- **Power:** crushing, hoisting, pumping and smelting all need large, reliable electricity supplies.
- **Markets and prices:** high world prices make more deposits profitable; a price crash can close mines.
- **Technology and skilled labour:** modern machinery, geologists and engineers make deep or low-grade deposits workable.

### Political factors

- **Government policy:** licences, royalties, taxes, ownership rules and rules on processing minerals before export all affect profits.
- **Political stability and security of investment:** investors want confidence that the mine and its profits are secure.

:::example{title="Worked example: is the deposit worth mining? (fictional figures)"}
A deposit holds 600,000 tonnes of ore at a grade of 3 g/t gold.

1. Gold in the ore = 600,000 × 3 = 1,800,000 g = **1,800 kg**.
2. If 90% is recovered in processing: 1,800 × 0.9 = **1,620 kg**.
3. The company compares the value of 1,620 kg at the expected price with the cost of building and running the mine. If prices fall, the same deposit may no longer be worth mining.
:::

## Mining methods

### Surface methods

| Method | How it works | Example or use |
|---|---|---|
| Open-cast (open-pit) | Overburden is blasted and removed; the ore is dug from a large pit with steps (benches) on its sides | Coal at Hwange; many gold and lithium deposits near the surface |
| Strip mining | Long strips of overburden are removed to expose a flat, shallow seam; spoil from each new strip is dumped into the last one | Shallow, horizontal coal seams |
| Alluvial mining | Minerals in river sand and gravel are separated using water (panning, sluicing) | Alluvial gold in rivers; diamonds at Marange |
| Dredging | A floating machine scoops sand and gravel from a river bed or lake floor | Alluvial gold and diamonds, sand |
| Quarrying | Rock is cut or blasted from an open face | Granite, limestone for cement, building stone |

**Advantages of surface mining:** cheaper and quicker to start; large machines extract big tonnages; safer than underground work (no roof falls or underground gases); nearly all the ore can be removed.

**Disadvantages:** huge scars on the landscape; large waste dumps; loss of farmland and vegetation; dust, noise and blasting; only possible where the mineral is shallow.

### Underground methods

:::example{title="A diagram in words: three ways underground"}
Imagine a cross-section of a hillside with a mineral seam inside.

1. **Shaft mine:** a vertical shaft is sunk from the flat ground above the deposit. Horizontal tunnels (levels) run from the shaft to the ore, which is hoisted up the shaft in skips. Used for deep ore, as in many gold mines.
2. **Adit mine:** a horizontal tunnel is driven straight into the side of the hill to reach the seam. Water drains out naturally and ore is carried out by rail or conveyor.
3. **Drift (incline) mine:** a sloping tunnel follows the seam or leads down to it from the surface, so vehicles or conveyors can carry ore out. Common for coal seams that dip gently.
:::

**Advantages of underground mining:** reaches deep, rich ore; less surface disturbance and smaller scars; can continue in all weather.

**Disadvantages:** expensive to sink shafts and to ventilate, light and pump the workings; dangerous (rock falls, flooding, explosive gases such as methane in coal mines, dust); some ore must be left as pillars to hold up the roof.

:::example{title="Case study: Hwange coal"}
Coal has been mined at Hwange since the early 1900s. Early working was underground; in 1972 an underground explosion at Hwange (then Wankie) colliery killed more than 400 miners, one of the worst mining disasters in Africa. Today much of the coal comes from open-cast pits, which are cheaper and safer for workers but leave large pits and spoil heaps. The coal supplies the Hwange power station, industry and coke for smelting.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up adit and shaft"}
A **shaft** is vertical; an **adit** is a horizontal tunnel into a hillside; a **drift** slopes. Remember: a shaft goes down like a well.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying underground mining has no environmental impact"}
It causes less surface damage, but it still produces waste rock and tailings, can cause subsidence and may pollute groundwater.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-word factors"}
"Capital" alone earns little. Explain: "a large open-cast mine needs millions of dollars of machinery before any ore is sold, so investors are needed".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Physical factors: size, grade, depth and shape of the deposit, water and access.
- Economic factors: capital, transport, power, markets and prices, technology and skilled labour.
- Political factors: government policy on licences, taxes, ownership and processing; political stability.
- Surface methods (open-cast, strip, alluvial, dredging, quarrying) suit shallow deposits; they are cheaper and safer but scar the land.
- Underground methods (shaft, adit, drift) reach deep ore; they are costly and dangerous but disturb less of the surface.
:::
