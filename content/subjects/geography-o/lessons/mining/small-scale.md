## Introduction

Walk along many rivers in Zimbabwe's gold belts in the dry season and you may see people shaking gravel and water in metal dishes, or small groups working shallow pits with picks and hand winches. These are **small-scale and artisanal miners**, often called *makorokoza*. Hundreds of thousands of people earn a living this way, and in several recent years small-scale producers delivered more gold to the national refinery than the large mines did. Yet the same activity can scar river banks, poison water with mercury and cost lives when pits collapse.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prospecting"}
Searching for mineral deposits that may be worth mining.
:::

:::definition{term="Alluvial (placer) deposit"}
Heavy, durable minerals such as gold or diamonds that have been weathered out of rock, carried by rivers and concentrated in sand and gravel.
:::

:::definition{term="Gold panning"}
Separating gold from river sand and gravel by swirling it with water in a shallow pan, so the light material is washed out and the heavy gold stays behind.
:::

:::definition{term="Small-scale mining"}
Mining by individuals, families, syndicates or small companies with little capital and simple equipment. Unregistered, mostly manual work is called **artisanal mining**.
:::

## Prospecting

Modern prospectors use **geological maps**, **soil and stream-sediment sampling** (testing for traces of metal), **geophysical surveys** (for example measuring magnetism from the air), **satellite images**, then **trenches, pits and drilling** to measure the size and grade of a deposit.

**Indigenous knowledge** has also guided prospecting for centuries:

- **Ancient workings:** many modern gold mines in Zimbabwe were opened at old pits and trenches dug by earlier miners.
- **Termite mounds:** termites carry soil up from depth, so grains of a mineral in a mound can reveal what lies below.
- **Rock and soil colour:** rusty, stained ground or white quartz outcrops can point to mineral veins.
- **Indicator plants:** some plants grow well, or poorly, on soils rich in particular metals.

## Properties of alluvial minerals

Gold and diamonds collect in rivers because of their properties:

| Property | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **High density** (gold is about 19 times as dense as water) | Heavy grains drop out where the river slows, and sink to the bottom of a pan |
| **Resistant to weathering** (chemically stable; gold does not rust or tarnish) | The grains survive long journeys in water unchanged |
| **Durable / hard** (diamond is the hardest natural mineral) | Grains are not ground away by other pebbles |
| **Insoluble** | They do not dissolve in river water |

Gold can be **identified** by its bright yellow colour that does not tarnish, its golden-yellow streak, and its softness: it can be flattened or dented with a pin (malleable). Iron pyrite ("fool's gold") looks similar but is harder, brittle, and leaves a greenish-black streak.

:::example{title="A diagram in words: where alluvial gold collects"}
Imagine looking down on a winding river.

1. On the **inside bend** of a meander the water slows, so heavy grains are dropped on the point bar.
2. **Behind boulders and rock bars**, eddies let gold settle.
3. In **potholes** and cracks in the bedrock, grains are trapped.
4. Below **rapids and waterfalls** and where a fast tributary meets a slower river, the sudden drop in speed deposits heavy minerals.
:::

## Gold panning

1. Dig gravel from a likely spot, ideally close to the bedrock where gold is concentrated.
2. Put it in a shallow pan, add water and break up lumps of clay; throw out large stones.
3. Swirl and tilt the pan under water so the light sand and mud wash over the rim.
4. Repeat until only a little dark, heavy sand remains; gold specks show at the bottom.
5. Pick out the gold or collect it. Many small-scale miners add **mercury**, which binds to gold to form an amalgam; the amalgam is then heated to drive off the mercury as a poisonous vapour.

A **sluice box** speeds up the process: gravel is washed down a sloping channel with ridges (riffles) that trap the heavy grains.

## Small-scale mining in Zimbabwe

**Characteristics:** low capital; picks, shovels, hand winches and sometimes small crushers (stamp mills) and pumps; shallow shafts and pits; work by individuals, families, syndicates or cooperatives; many workers unregistered; mostly gold, but also chrome and gemstones.

**Contribution:** jobs and incomes in rural areas where farming is unreliable; a large share of national gold deliveries (sold to Fidelity Gold Refinery, the national gold buyer and refiner); foreign currency; spending in local towns; it can be a first step towards larger, formal mining.

**Problems:**

- **Land degradation:** open pits and trenches left unfilled, stream banks dug away, trees cut for fuel and pit props.
- **Siltation** of rivers and dams as dug-out gravel washes downstream.
- **Mercury pollution** of water, fish and soil, and mercury poisoning of miners and their families.
- **Unsafe shafts** with no support or ventilation, which collapse or flood.
- **Illegal mining and smuggling**, theft, violence between rival groups, and children leaving school to work.
- **Low recovery:** simple methods lose much of the gold.

**Solutions:** registering and formalising miners; organising them into cooperatives; training in safe methods and mercury-free processing (for example better gravity concentration); access to loans and to equipment and processing centres; buying centres that pay fair prices; filling pits and rehabilitating land; policing of illegal activity and of dangerous sites.

## The legal framework in outline

- The **Mines and Minerals Act** sets out who may prospect and mine. A person must hold a **prospecting licence**, then **peg** (mark out) and **register a claim** with the Ministry of Mines and Mining Development's provincial office. Registered claims must be worked and inspected.
- Gold must be sold to authorised buyers under the **Gold Trade Act**, so dealing in gold without a licence is illegal.
- The **Environmental Management Act**, enforced by the Environmental Management Agency (EMA), requires environmental assessment and rehabilitation of mined land.

:::example{title="Worked example: gold recovered by panning (fictional figures)"}
A syndicate washes 12 tonnes of gravel with an average grade of 1.5 g/t but recovers only 60% of the gold.

1. Gold in gravel = 12 × 1.5 = 18 g.
2. Gold recovered = 18 × 0.6 = **10.8 g**; 7.2 g is lost, which shows why better methods matter.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying gold collects where rivers flow fastest"}
Heavy grains are **deposited** where the water **slows**: inside bends, behind obstacles and below rapids.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all small-scale mining as illegal"}
Many small-scale miners are registered and legal. "Artisanal" or "illegal" describes only part of the sector.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Prospecting uses maps, sampling, surveys and drilling, and indigenous clues such as ancient workings, termite mounds, rock colour and indicator plants.
- Alluvial minerals are dense, durable, insoluble and resistant to weathering, so they collect where rivers slow.
- Panning swirls gravel and water so light material washes away and gold stays behind; mercury use is dangerous.
- Small-scale mining gives jobs and much of Zimbabwe's gold, but causes land degradation, siltation, mercury pollution and accidents.
- Mining rights come from prospecting licences and registered claims under the Mines and Minerals Act; gold sales are regulated.
:::
