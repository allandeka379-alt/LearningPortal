## Introduction

Mining has given Zimbabwe its railways, its mining towns and most of its export earnings. It has also left open pits, grey dumps of waste rock, silted rivers and, at old asbestos mines, dust that harms people's lungs. Minerals are **non-renewable**: once a deposit is worked out it is gone. This lesson asks how mining can be done with less harm to people and the environment, and how a country can decide whether a new mine is worth it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rehabilitation"}
Restoring mined land to a safe, stable and useful state, for example by filling pits, reshaping dumps, replacing topsoil and replanting vegetation.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid mine drainage"}
Acidic water, often carrying dissolved metals, that forms when sulphide minerals in mine workings and waste are exposed to air and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable use of minerals"}
Using mineral resources in ways that meet present needs while leaving resources, a healthy environment and economic opportunities for future generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)"}
A method of comparing all the costs of a project (economic, social and environmental) with all its benefits, to decide whether it should go ahead.
:::

## Environmental impacts of mining

| Impact | Cause | Example in Zimbabwe |
|---|---|---|
| Land degradation | Open pits, trenches, waste dumps, subsidence above old workings | Open-cast pits and spoil heaps around Hwange; unfilled pits in gold-panning areas |
| Deforestation | Clearing for pits and roads; wood for fuel and pit props | Around small-scale gold workings |
| Water pollution | Acid mine drainage, cyanide spills, mercury, overflowing tailings dams | Mercury in rivers where gold is panned |
| Siltation | Loose soil and gravel washed into rivers and dams | Rivers such as the Mazowe in gold-panning areas |
| Air pollution | Dust from blasting, roads and dumps; sulphur dioxide from smelters | Dust at open-cast mines; fumes near smelters |
| Noise and vibration | Blasting, crushers, heavy trucks | Cracked walls in homes near blasting |
| Loss of habitats and biodiversity | Vegetation cleared, rivers polluted | Wildlife disturbed by mining near protected areas |

## Mitigation and rehabilitation

:::example{title="A diagram in words: rehabilitating an open-cast mine"}
Picture the same site at four stages.

1. **Before mining:** topsoil is stripped and stored separately in low heaps.
2. **During mining:** waste rock is dumped in planned areas; water from the pit is pumped to settling ponds; haul roads are sprayed with water to keep dust down.
3. **After mining:** the pit is partly backfilled with waste rock, and steep slopes are reshaped to gentle gradients so they do not erode.
4. **Restoration:** stored topsoil is spread back, grasses and indigenous trees are planted, and the land is monitored until it can be used for grazing, forestry or a water reservoir.
:::

Other measures:

- **Environmental Impact Assessment** before a mine is approved, with an environmental management plan (required by law and checked by the Environmental Management Agency, EMA).
- **Lined tailings dams** and drains to catch run-off; treating polluted water before release.
- **Scrubbers and filters** on smelter chimneys to reduce sulphur dioxide and dust.
- **Banning or reducing mercury**, and training small-scale miners in safer methods.
- **Fines and rehabilitation bonds**: money set aside by the company so that the land is restored even if the mine closes.

## Health and safety in mining

| Hazard | Effect | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Rock falls and collapses | Injury and death | Roof bolts, timber or steel supports, inspections |
| Explosive or poisonous gases (methane in coal mines) | Explosions, suffocation | Ventilation fans, gas detectors, banning naked flames |
| Dust (silica, coal, asbestos) | Lung diseases such as silicosis and asbestosis | Water sprays, dust masks and respirators, medical checks |
| Noise | Hearing loss | Ear protection, quieter machines |
| Flooding | Drowning, trapped miners | Pumps, mapping of old workings |
| Chemicals (cyanide, mercury) | Poisoning | Safe storage, protective clothing, training |
| Machinery and blasting | Crush injuries | Training, guards on machines, blasting procedures |

**Why health and safety matter:** they save lives and prevent disease; healthy workers are more productive; accidents halt production and cost money in compensation and repairs; laws require them; and a safe record helps a company keep its licence and attract investors.

## Sustainable use of mineral resources

1. **Efficient extraction and processing:** better recovery means less ore is wasted.
2. **Reprocessing old dumps and tailings** with modern methods to recover metal left behind.
3. **Recycling** scrap steel, copper, aluminium and old batteries, and **reusing** materials.
4. **Substitution:** using more plentiful materials where possible.
5. **Using mineral revenue wisely:** investing it in farming, industry, education and infrastructure so the economy does not collapse when the mine closes.
6. **Planning for closure:** rehabilitation, and new jobs for mining towns.

## Cost-benefit analysis of a mining project

A CBA lists and, where possible, values everything the project would gain and lose.

- **Costs:** building and running the mine; loss of farmland, grazing and forest; pollution; relocation of households; health effects; damage to roads.
- **Benefits:** export earnings; royalties and taxes; jobs; new roads, power lines, clinics and schools; business for local suppliers.

:::example{title="Worked example: a benefit-cost ratio (fictional figures)"}
A proposed mine has estimated benefits of US\$540 million and costs of US\$450 million over its life.

1. Benefit-cost ratio = total benefits ÷ total costs = 540 ÷ 450 = **1.2**.
2. A ratio greater than 1 means benefits exceed costs, so on these figures the project is worthwhile.
3. Net benefit = 540 − 450 = **US\$90 million**.

The decision can change if costs that are hard to value, such as the loss of a sacred site or a wetland, are given more weight.
:::

**Why CBA is important in mining:** it helps the government and communities decide whether a mine should go ahead, shows who gains and who loses, makes hidden environmental and social costs visible, and helps set conditions such as rehabilitation bonds and compensation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing impacts without causes"}
"Mining causes pollution" earns little. Say "blasting and haul trucks raise dust that settles on crops near the mine".
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking rehabilitation begins after the mine closes"}
Good rehabilitation is planned before mining starts (stored topsoil, planned dumps) and continues throughout.
:::

:::mistake{title="CBA only counts money"}
A CBA should include social and environmental costs and benefits, even when they are hard to put a price on.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 Section B"}
Questions often give a description of a mine and ask you to "describe the impacts" and "suggest how the land could be rehabilitated". Match each impact with a specific measure. For "evaluate", use the language of CBA: weigh benefits against costs, say who gains and who loses, and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mining causes land degradation, deforestation, water and air pollution, siltation, noise and habitat loss.
- Mitigation includes EIA, lined tailings dams, water treatment, dust control, filters on smelters and less mercury use; rehabilitation backfills, reshapes, re-soils and replants land.
- Safety measures (supports, ventilation, gas detectors, protective equipment, training) protect lives and productivity.
- Sustainable use means efficient extraction, reprocessing, recycling, substitution and investing mineral wealth in other sectors.
- Cost-benefit analysis compares all costs with all benefits; a benefit-cost ratio above 1 suggests a project is worthwhile.
:::
