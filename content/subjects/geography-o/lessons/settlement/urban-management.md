## Introduction

No city can stop people arriving, but every city can choose how it responds. A council can ignore growth and watch shacks spread over wetlands, or it can plan: serviced plots for new homes, buses that move thousands of commuters, recycling that turns waste into income. This lesson looks at the main strategies for managing urban growth, how to judge whether they work, and a detailed example of one city's attempts.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Site-and-service scheme"}
A scheme in which the authorities provide plots with basic services (roads, water pipes, sewers, sometimes electricity), and families build their own houses on them, often in stages.
:::

:::definition{term="Satellite town"}
A town built some distance from a large city to house people and relieve pressure on it, often with its own jobs and services; if most residents commute to the city it is called a *dormitory town*.
:::

:::definition{term="Green belt"}
A zone of protected open land around a city where building is restricted, to stop urban sprawl.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable city"}
A city that meets the needs of its present residents without harming the environment or the ability of future residents to meet their needs: efficient public transport, renewable energy, recycling, green spaces and community involvement.
:::

:::definition{term="Smart city"}
A city that uses digital technology and data (sensors, apps, smart meters) to manage traffic, energy, water and services more efficiently.
:::

## Strategies for managing urban growth

| Problem | Strategy | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing shortage | **Site-and-service schemes**, aided self-help | Cheap for the council; families build at their own pace; basic services from the start | Plots may still be too expensive for the poorest; slow to complete |
| Unplanned settlements | **In-situ upgrading** and regularisation (see the lesson on unplanned settlements) | Keeps communities and jobs in place; gives security of tenure | Needs funding; some homes must be moved for roads and pipes |
| Overcrowding in the main city | **Satellite towns** and **growth points** | Spread population and jobs; reduce pressure on one city | Often become dormitory towns with long commutes if jobs do not follow |
| Sprawl | **Green belts** and land-use planning | Protect farmland, wetlands and recreation | Push development further out; raise land prices; hard to enforce |
| Congestion and pollution | **Public transport**: buses, bus rapid transit (BRT), commuter trains; ring roads; park-and-ride | Moves many people cheaply; less pollution per passenger | Expensive to build; needs regular, reliable service to attract users |
| Water shortages and pollution | Repair leaks, expand treatment works, protect catchments and wetlands, rainwater harvesting | Cleaner water, less disease | High cost; needs maintenance and reliable electricity |
| Waste | Regular collection, **recycling**, composting, sanitary landfill | Cleaner streets, jobs in recycling, less disease | Collection vehicles and landfills cost money |
| Rural-urban migration | **Rural development**: growth points, rural electrification, better rural services | Reduces push factors | Slow; may not match urban pull factors |

## How to evaluate a strategy

Ask these questions:

1. **Who benefits?** The poorest, or mainly middle-income families?
2. **Cost and funding:** can the council or government afford to build *and maintain* it?
3. **Scale:** does it match the size of the problem?
4. **Sustainability:** does it reduce pollution, energy use and waste for the future?
5. **Participation:** were residents involved? Schemes planned with communities are usually better maintained.

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: Harare, Zimbabwe"}
**Causes of growth:** rural-urban migration accelerated after independence in 1980 (jobs, education, services; drought and land shortage in communal areas), plus natural increase among young urban families.

**Challenges:** housing shortages and long waiting lists; backyard shacks and unplanned settlements such as parts of Epworth and Caledonia; unreliable water supply and pollution of Lake Chivero; cholera outbreaks (2008-09 and 2018); uncollected refuse; congestion; unemployment.

**Opportunities:** the largest concentration of jobs, universities, hospitals and markets in the country; a large informal sector supporting many households.

**Management:**
- **Housing:** site-and-service and aided self-help schemes in high-density suburbs; regularisation and upgrading in Epworth.
- **Satellite towns:** Chitungwiza (south of Harare) and growth in Norton and Ruwa house many people who work in Harare.
- **Growth points:** a national policy since the 1980s to develop rural service centres (for example Gokwe and Murambinda) to reduce migration to the main cities.
- **Transport:** regulation of kombis, designated ranks, and public buses (ZUPCO) for commuters.
- **Water and sanitation:** rehabilitation of treatment works and sewers, and drilling of community boreholes.
- **Waste:** refuse collection, community clean-up campaigns and informal recycling of plastics and metals.

**Evaluation:** site-and-service schemes produced many homes but not enough for the waiting list; satellite towns reduced pressure on housing but created long, costly commutes; water and sewerage remain overloaded because infrastructure has not kept pace with growth and funding is limited. Strategies work best where funding is sustained and residents are involved.
:::

:::example{title="Example of a sustainable city: Curitiba, Brazil"}
- **Bus rapid transit (BRT):** dedicated bus lanes and raised "tube" stations allow fast boarding, so many residents use buses rather than cars.
- **Green space:** parks built on flood-prone land act as flood storage and recreation areas.
- **Recycling:** residents separate waste, and a "green exchange" scheme has traded recyclable waste for food or bus tickets in poorer areas.

Curitiba shows that low-cost, well-planned schemes can improve transport, flooding and waste together.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a strategy without evaluating it"}
"Evaluate" needs strengths **and** weaknesses and a judgement. Say who benefits, what it costs and whether it solves the problem at the right scale.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking satellite towns solve congestion"}
If jobs stay in the main city, satellite towns become dormitory towns and commuting traffic increases.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 7)"}
The extended part often asks for a detailed specific example of a named urban area: give causes of growth, challenges, opportunities and management, with real place names, then judge success.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"Suggest solutions to the problems of urbanisation" rewards one solution per problem, clearly linked: site-and-service for housing, buses for congestion, recycling for waste, growth points for migration.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Strategies include site-and-service schemes, upgrading, satellite towns, growth points, green belts, public transport, water and sanitation improvements, waste recycling and smart, sustainable design.
- Evaluate by who benefits, cost and maintenance, scale, sustainability and community involvement.
- Harare has used site-and-service schemes, satellite towns, growth points, transport regulation and water rehabilitation, with partial success limited by funding and the pace of growth.
- Curitiba's bus rapid transit, parks and recycling show how a city can manage growth sustainably.
:::
