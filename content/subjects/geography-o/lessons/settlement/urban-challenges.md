## Introduction

Cities are where most of Zimbabwe's universities, hospitals, factories and offices are found, and where many young people hope to build their futures. Yet the same cities face long queues at boreholes, uncollected refuse, packed kombis in rush-hour traffic and sewage flowing into rivers. Rapid urban growth brings both **opportunities** and **challenges**.

This lesson weighs up the opportunities of urban living, the challenges that appear when towns grow faster than services can be provided, and the effects of urban sprawl on the land at the edge of the city.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urban sprawl"}
The uncontrolled spread of a town's built-up area outwards into the surrounding countryside, usually as low-density housing, shopping centres and roads.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Small-scale economic activities that are not officially registered or taxed, such as street vending, small workshops and unlicensed transport.
:::

:::definition{term="Service provision"}
Supplying the services a population needs: piped water, sewerage, electricity, refuse collection, health care, schools and transport.
:::

:::definition{term="Inequality"}
Large differences in income, housing and access to services between groups of people in the same city.
:::

## Opportunities of urban living

| Opportunity | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Jobs and income** | More formal jobs in manufacturing, commerce and government, and many informal opportunities in vending, transport and small industry |
| **Services** | Hospitals, specialist clinics, secondary schools, colleges and universities |
| **Utilities** | Greater access to piped water, electricity, sanitation and internet than in rural areas |
| **Housing** | A range of housing, including council and private schemes |
| **Culture and leisure** | Sport, music, entertainment, places of worship, a wider social life |
| **Markets** | Larger markets for farmers and businesses; access to banks and credit |

Urban growth also benefits the country: cities concentrate investment, skills and innovation, and money sent home by urban workers supports rural families.

## Challenges of rapid urban growth

When a city's population grows faster than its services and jobs, problems appear.

1. **Housing shortages:** long council waiting lists; overcrowding as lodgers rent single rooms; growth of backyard shacks and **unplanned settlements**.
2. **Water supply:** demand exceeds treatment capacity; burst pipes and power cuts interrupt pumping; households queue at boreholes or buy water.
3. **Sanitation and disease:** overloaded or blocked sewers overflow; untreated sewage enters rivers. Poor water and sanitation spread **cholera** and **typhoid**.
4. **Waste:** refuse is not collected regularly, so illegal dumps grow and are burned, blocking drains and breeding flies and rats.
5. **Traffic congestion:** more vehicles than the roads can carry; long commutes; accidents; air pollution.
6. **Unemployment and underemployment:** too few formal jobs, so many work in the informal sector with low and uncertain incomes.
7. **Pollution:** air pollution from vehicles, industry and burning waste; water pollution of rivers and reservoirs; noise.
8. **Inequality and crime:** rich and poor live side by side with very different services; poverty can contribute to crime.

:::example{title="Case study: challenges in Harare"}
- **Water and sanitation:** much of Harare's water comes from **Lake Chivero** on the Manyame River, downstream of the city. Sewage and industrial effluent flowing into the lake raise treatment costs and cause algal growth and water hyacinth. Supply shortfalls have left many suburbs without reliable piped water, so residents rely on boreholes and wells.
- **Disease:** a major **cholera** outbreak in 2008-09 caused about 100,000 cases and about 4,000 deaths in Zimbabwe, with Harare's high-density suburbs badly affected. Another outbreak in 2018 began in the suburbs of Glen View and Budiriro.
- **Waste:** irregular refuse collection has led to illegal dumping in open spaces and along streams.
- **Transport:** congestion in and around the CBD at peak times, with commuters relying on kombis and buses.
- **Employment:** many residents earn a living in the informal sector, for example vending and small-scale manufacturing.
:::

## Urban sprawl and the rural-urban fringe

As cities spread outwards, the **rural-urban fringe** changes.

| Negative impacts | Positive impacts |
|---|---|
| Loss of farmland and smallholdings to housing | New housing for a growing population |
| Destruction of natural vegetation and **wetlands** (vleis), increasing flooding and reducing groundwater recharge | New jobs in construction, shops and industry |
| More traffic and longer commuting journeys | New roads and services reach former rural areas |
| Higher land prices, so farmers sell up | Farmers near the city can sell vegetables and milk to urban markets |
| Waste sites, sewage works and quarries located on the fringe | Recreation: golf courses, parks, sports grounds |
| Unplanned settlements may grow without services | |

:::example{title="Sprawl around Harare"}
Harare's built-up area has spread into surrounding farmland and towards the satellite towns of **Chitungwiza**, **Ruwa** and **Norton**, and towards **Epworth**. New housing developments have been built on former farms, and some have encroached on wetlands that feed the city's rivers and reservoirs. Commuters from these areas travel daily into the city, adding to congestion on the main roads.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without explaining them"}
"Water shortages" earns little. Explain the link: "the population grew faster than the treatment works were expanded, so supply cannot meet demand".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the opportunities"}
Questions on urban growth often ask for opportunities **and** challenges. Many people move to towns because life there offers more than in rural areas, despite the problems.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 7)"}
"Describe the impact of urban sprawl on the rural-urban fringe" needs both negative and positive impacts with clear consequences (loss of farmland, wetlands; new jobs, services). A named city makes the answer stronger.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
For "describe the effects of urbanisation", organise your answer into social (housing, disease, crime), economic (jobs, informal sector) and environmental (pollution, waste, loss of wetlands) effects, using Harare or Bulawayo as examples.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cities offer jobs, services, utilities, housing, culture and larger markets.
- Rapid growth creates challenges: housing shortages, water and sanitation problems, disease, waste, traffic congestion, unemployment, pollution and inequality.
- In Harare, pollution of Lake Chivero, unreliable water supply, cholera outbreaks (2008-09 and 2018), waste and congestion illustrate these challenges.
- Urban sprawl takes farmland and wetlands, adds traffic and raises land prices on the rural-urban fringe, but also brings housing, jobs and services.
:::
