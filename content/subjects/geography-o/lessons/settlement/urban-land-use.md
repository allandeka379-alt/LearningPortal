## Introduction

Travel by kombi from Mbare into central Harare and on to Borrowdale and the town changes around you: crowded high-density houses, then factories and warehouses near the railway, then tall office blocks, then large houses with gardens and trees. Towns are not random. Each kind of land use competes for space, and the result is a set of **zones** that geographers have tried to describe with simple **models**.

This lesson describes the main urban land-use zones, the three classic urban models, and how far they fit Zimbabwean towns.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urban land use"}
The way land in a town is used: commercial, industrial, residential, recreational, transport and open space.
:::

:::definition{term="Central business district (CBD)"}
The commercial heart of a town, with the main shops, offices, banks and government buildings, the highest land values and the tallest buildings.
:::

:::definition{term="Residential density"}
The number of houses (or people) on a given area of land. High-density areas have small plots and many houses; low-density areas have large plots and few houses.
:::

:::definition{term="Rural-urban fringe"}
The zone at the edge of a town where urban and rural land uses mix: farms, smallholdings, new housing, industry, waste sites and recreation.
:::

:::definition{term="Urban model"}
A simplified diagram that shows the general arrangement of land-use zones in a town.
:::

## The main land-use zones

| Zone | Characteristics | Why it is located there |
|---|---|---|
| **CBD** | Tall buildings, offices, banks, department stores, hotels, government offices; very high land values; few residents; heavy traffic and many pedestrians | Most accessible point: routes meet here, so businesses can reach the most customers and workers and can afford the high rents |
| **Industrial zone** | Factories, warehouses, depots, railway sidings; often noise and pollution | Flat land, access to railway and main roads, cheaper land than the CBD; often separated from better residential areas |
| **High-density residential** | Small plots, small houses, often built by councils for workers; many people per hectare; some overcrowding | Cheaper land near industrial areas so workers can walk or use cheap transport |
| **Medium-density residential** | Medium plots, semi-detached or detached houses, flats | Between high- and low-density areas |
| **Low-density residential** | Large plots, large houses, gardens, swimming pools, trees | Pleasant, quieter areas away from industry, often on higher ground; residents have cars |
| **Recreational land** | Parks, sports grounds, golf courses, gardens | Planned open spaces; land unsuitable for building, such as river banks and vleis |
| **Rural-urban fringe** | Mixed: smallholdings, new suburbs, shopping centres, waste sites, cemeteries, sewage works | Cheap, available land at the edge of town |

## Urban models

### Concentric zone model (Burgess, 1925)

Imagine a dartboard. At the centre is the **CBD**. Around it, in rings, are:

1. a **zone of transition** with old factories, warehouses and deteriorating housing;
2. a ring of **low-income (working-class) housing**;
3. a ring of **medium-income housing**;
4. an outer **commuter zone** of high-income housing.

Land values and building density fall away from the centre, and wealth rises with distance from it. The model was based on Chicago, a city on flat land.

### Sector model (Hoyt, 1939)

Imagine the dartboard cut into slices like a cake. The town grows outwards in **sectors along transport routes**:

1. an **industrial sector** along a railway, river or main road, with **low-income housing** beside it;
2. a **high-income housing sector** extending away from industry, often along an attractive route or high ground;
3. **medium-income** sectors between them.

Once a sector has a certain land use it tends to keep growing outwards in the same direction.

### Multiple-nuclei model (Harris and Ullman, 1945)

The town grows round **several centres (nuclei)**, not just one CBD. Besides the CBD there may be outlying **business districts** and shopping centres, separate **industrial districts**, a university or airport, and **suburban** centres. Similar activities cluster together, while activities that conflict (heavy industry and high-income housing) stay apart. This model fits large, car-using cities better.

## Applying the models to Zimbabwean towns

:::example{title="Case study: Harare"}
1. **CBD:** high-rise offices, banks, shops and government buildings, with the highest land values.
2. **Industrial sectors:** mainly to the **south and south-west** of the CBD along the railway (for example Workington and Southerton) and in the **east** (Msasa).
3. **High-density suburbs:** mostly to the **south and west**, near the industrial areas: for example Mbare (the oldest), Highfield, Glen View, Budiriro and Kuwadzana.
4. **Low-density suburbs:** mostly to the **north and north-east**, away from industry: for example Borrowdale and Mount Pleasant.
5. **Other nuclei:** suburban shopping centres and offices in the northern suburbs, and growing satellite towns such as Chitungwiza and Ruwa.

**Fit with the models:** Harare's wedges of industry with high-density housing, and of low-density housing extending in the opposite direction, fit the **sector model**. Its suburban shopping and office centres fit the **multiple-nuclei model**. It fits the concentric model poorly, because wealthy residents did not move outwards in rings.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: Bulawayo"}
Bulawayo shows a similar division. High-density suburbs such as Makokoba (the oldest), Mzilikazi, Entumbane and Nkulumane lie mainly to the **west**, with industrial areas near the railway lines. Low-density suburbs such as Hillside and Burnside lie mainly to the **east and south-east**. This again fits a **sector** pattern more than a concentric one.
:::

### Why Zimbabwean towns differ from the models

- The models were based on cities in the USA; African towns grew under different conditions.
- **Colonial planning** separated residential areas by race, placing African townships near industry and away from European suburbs. This, not only land value, shaped the sectors.
- **Relief, rivers and wetlands** prevent perfect rings or sectors.
- **Informal settlements** and **satellite towns** have grown at the edges, and the CBD has spread into nearby suburbs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing the model instead of the town"}
If the question asks about Harare, name zones and places in Harare, then say which model they fit. Do not simply draw Burgess's rings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying land values are highest at the edge"}
Land values are highest in the CBD, the most accessible point, and generally fall with distance from it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"Describe the characteristics of the CBD" needs features you could see: tall buildings, offices and shops, traffic, few houses, high land values. "Outline the main features of the sector model" needs the shape (wedges along routes), what lies in each sector and why.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 map work"}
On a town map, the CBD is where roads converge and there are many public buildings; industrial areas lie beside the railway; high-density areas show closely packed small plots.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Towns have zones: CBD, industrial, high-, medium- and low-density residential, recreational and the rural-urban fringe.
- The CBD has the highest land values and accessibility; industry needs flat land and transport; high-density housing is near industry; low-density housing is in pleasant areas away from it.
- Burgess's concentric model shows rings round the CBD; Hoyt's sector model shows wedges along routes; Harris and Ullman's multiple-nuclei model shows several centres.
- Harare and Bulawayo fit the sector and multiple-nuclei models best, shaped by colonial planning, relief and transport routes.
:::
