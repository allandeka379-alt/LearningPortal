## Introduction

On the edge of many African cities, and in gaps between planned suburbs, homes are built without plans, permits or services: walls of brick, timber or scrap metal, paths of bare earth, water carried from a distant tap. These **unplanned settlements** house hundreds of millions of people worldwide. They are a response to a real need for cheap housing, and they are also full of enterprise, from tailors to welders. This lesson explains what they are like, where and why they grow, what effects they have, and how they can be improved.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Unplanned (informal) settlement"}
An area of housing built without official planning permission, usually without legal ownership of the land and without adequate services. Other names include squatter settlement, shanty town and, in Brazil, favela.
:::

:::definition{term="Security of tenure"}
The legal right to stay on a piece of land or in a house without fear of eviction, for example through a title deed, lease or permit.
:::

:::definition{term="Upgrading (in-situ upgrading)"}
Improving an existing settlement where it stands by adding water, sanitation, roads, drainage and electricity, rather than moving the residents.
:::

:::definition{term="Regularisation"}
Giving official recognition to an informal settlement and legal tenure to its residents, so that it can be planned and serviced.
:::

## Characteristics

1. **Building materials:** often makeshift at first (plastic, scrap metal, timber, mud bricks), improved gradually to brick and cement.
2. **High density:** homes packed closely with narrow, unpaved paths; overcrowded rooms.
3. **Lack of services:** little or no piped water, sewerage, refuse collection, electricity or street lighting; residents use shared taps, wells, pit latrines or open spaces.
4. **Insecure tenure:** residents have no title deeds, so they risk eviction.
5. **Poor access:** few roads for ambulances, fire engines or refuse trucks.
6. **Informal economy:** many residents run small businesses from home: vending, tailoring, carpentry, welding, hair salons.
7. **Community organisation:** many settlements develop strong community groups, savings clubs and churches.

## Where they develop

Unplanned settlements grow on land that nobody else is using or wants, usually because it is hazardous or unsuitable for formal development:

- on the **edge of the town** (the rural-urban fringe), where land is available;
- on **river banks, flood plains and wetlands** (vleis);
- on **steep slopes** (as in many Latin American favelas);
- **beside railways, main roads and industrial areas**, close to jobs;
- **near rubbish dumps**, where people scavenge for recyclable materials;
- in **backyards** of formal houses in high-density suburbs (backyard shacks).

## Causes

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Rapid rural-urban migration and natural increase | The urban population grows faster than formal housing can be built |
| Poverty and low incomes | Many people cannot afford formal rents or house prices |
| Housing shortage | Long council waiting lists; too few serviced plots |
| High cost of building standards | Formal building regulations make legal houses too expensive for the poor |
| Weak planning control | Councils lack money and staff to plan and enforce |
| Land issues | Unclear ownership of land, or illegal sale of plots by "land barons" |
| Need to live near work | Informal workers need to be close to markets, industry and the CBD |

## Effects

| Social | Economic | Environmental |
|---|---|---|
| Diseases such as cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea from unsafe water and poor sanitation | Cheap housing allows workers to live in the city and earn income | Pollution of rivers by sewage and waste |
| Overcrowding spreads respiratory diseases such as tuberculosis | Vibrant informal economy and small industries | Destruction of wetlands and vegetation |
| Fire risk from close-packed homes and open fires | Residents provide labour for formal industry | Soil erosion and landslides on cleared slopes |
| Insecurity, crime and fear of eviction | No rates paid, so councils have less money for services | Flooding where drains are absent or blocked |
| Strong community spirit and self-help | Little investment because of insecure tenure | Illegal dumping and burning of waste |

:::example{title="Case study: Epworth, Harare"}
Epworth lies on the south-eastern edge of Harare. It began as a mission settlement and grew rapidly, largely without formal planning, as people arrived from rural areas and from other parts of the city. Many homes lacked piped water and sewerage, and residents relied on wells and pit latrines, which can contaminate groundwater.

Rather than removing the settlement, the authorities and community organisations have worked on **regularisation and upgrading**: surveying and planning the area, laying out roads, providing water points and helping residents secure tenure. Community savings groups (such as those linked to the Zimbabwe Homeless People's Federation) have helped residents map their settlement and contribute to improvements.
:::

:::example{title="Removal versus improvement"}
Some governments have demolished informal settlements. In 2005 Zimbabwe's **Operation Murambatsvina** removed many informal homes and business structures in urban areas; a United Nations report estimated that around 700,000 people lost their homes, livelihoods or both. Elsewhere, for example in parts of Kibera in Nairobi, Kenya, programmes have tried to upgrade in place. Experience in many countries shows that clearance without alternative housing tends to move the problem elsewhere, while upgrading keeps communities and livelihoods intact.
:::

## Measures to improve unplanned settlements

1. **In-situ upgrading:** add water standpipes, toilets, drains, paved paths, street lights and electricity.
2. **Regularisation:** survey plots and give residents leases or title deeds; security encourages people to invest in better homes.
3. **Site-and-service schemes:** provide new serviced plots so families can build legally.
4. **Low-cost housing:** council or government housing at affordable rents or prices; core houses that families extend.
5. **Self-help schemes:** residents provide labour; authorities provide materials, loans or expertise.
6. **Community savings and microcredit:** small loans to improve homes and start businesses.
7. **Relocation** only from hazardous sites (flood plains, steep slopes), to safe serviced land with transport to jobs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing unplanned settlements only negatively"}
They also have strong communities and busy informal economies, and they provide affordable housing that the formal market does not. Balanced answers score higher.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing high-density planned suburbs with unplanned settlements"}
Mbare or Highfield are planned high-density suburbs with services, even if crowded. An unplanned settlement lacks planning permission, tenure and services.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"State the characteristics" needs observable features (materials, density, services, tenure). "Explain the causes" needs reasons linked to migration, poverty and housing supply. "Suggest measures" should be practical and linked to the problems.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 7)"}
When describing the challenges of rapid urban growth, unplanned settlements are a key example: describe conditions and link them to health, safety and the economy.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Unplanned settlements are built without permission, tenure or adequate services, at high density, often of makeshift materials.
- They grow on marginal land: city edges, river banks, wetlands, steep slopes, beside railways, near dumps and in backyards.
- Causes: rapid urban growth, poverty, housing shortage, costly building standards, weak planning and the need to live near work.
- Effects include disease, fire and eviction risk, pollution and loss of wetlands, but also affordable homes, community spirit and informal enterprise.
- Improvement measures include upgrading, regularisation, site-and-service schemes, low-cost and self-help housing, and community savings; Epworth in Harare is an example of regularisation and upgrading.
:::
