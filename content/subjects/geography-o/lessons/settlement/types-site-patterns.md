## Introduction

Look at any village in a communal area. The homesteads may stand close together near a borehole, or be scattered across the hillsides, or line up along a main road beside a business centre. None of this is accidental. People choose where to live because of water, good soil, flat land, safety and access to roads and markets, and those choices shape the pattern of settlement you see on a map.

This lesson explains the types of settlement, the difference between **site** and **situation**, the patterns of rural settlement, the laws that guide land use in Zimbabwe, and how settlements can be sited and built to survive hazards such as floods and landslides.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Settlement"}
A place where people live, from a single homestead to a large city, together with the buildings and services they use.
:::

:::definition{term="Site"}
The actual land a settlement is built on: its relief, soil, drainage, water supply and the physical features of that piece of ground.
:::

:::definition{term="Situation"}
The position of a settlement in relation to its surroundings: other settlements, routes, rivers, resources and the wider region.
:::

:::definition{term="Settlement pattern"}
The way homesteads and buildings are arranged across an area: nucleated, dispersed or linear.
:::

:::definition{term="Settlement hierarchy"}
The ranking of settlements by size and number of services, from isolated homesteads at the bottom to a capital city at the top.
:::

## Types of settlement

| Feature | Rural settlement | Urban settlement |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Small: homesteads, villages, business centres | Large: towns and cities |
| Main activities | Primary: farming, herding, some mining and fishing | Secondary and tertiary: manufacturing, trade, services |
| Services | Few: primary school, clinic, grinding mill, a few shops | Many: hospitals, banks, colleges, supermarkets, offices |
| Density | Low; homesteads spread over farmland | High; buildings close together |
| Administration | Rural district council, traditional leaders | Municipal or town council |

Between the two sit **growth points** and **service centres** in Zimbabwe, which were developed to bring urban services closer to rural people. As settlements rise up the hierarchy they become fewer, larger and offer a wider range of services with a larger **sphere of influence** (the area they serve).

## Site and situation

When people founded a settlement they looked for a good **site**. The main site factors are:

1. **Water supply:** a river, spring or a place where a borehole can reach groundwater (a *wet-point site*). In very wet areas a raised, well-drained spot is chosen instead (a *dry-point site*).
2. **Relief:** gently sloping or flat land is easier to build on and farm; steep slopes are avoided.
3. **Soils:** fertile soils for crops and pasture.
4. **Defence:** in the past, hilltops or river bends were easier to defend; many old sites in southern Africa used hills and kopjes.
5. **Building materials and fuel:** timber, grass for thatch, clay for bricks, stone.
6. **Shelter:** protection from strong winds or cold.
7. **Freedom from hazards:** above flood level, away from unstable slopes, away from swamps where mosquitoes breed.

**Situation** explains why some settlements grow while others stay small. A settlement at a **route centre** (where roads or railways meet), at a **bridging point** of a river, near a **mineral deposit** or in the centre of a rich farming region becomes a market and service centre and attracts more people.

:::example{title="Site and situation in words: a fictional town"}
Imagine a cross-section from west to east. On the west a steep granite kopje rises; at its foot is a gentle, well-drained slope with red soils, and below that a perennial river with a sandy bed.

- **Site:** the town was built on the gentle slope (flat enough to build, above flood level) close to the river (water) with fertile soils nearby.
- **Situation:** the main road crosses the river at a bridge here, and a branch road leads to a gold mine 15 km to the north. The bridge and the junction made the town a route centre, so shops and a bus terminus grew there.
:::

## Rural settlement patterns

| Pattern | Description | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nucleated** (clustered) | Homesteads grouped closely together, often round a water point, a chief's homestead, a school or a business centre | Shared water supply (borehole, spring); defence in the past; social ties and kinship; planned villages; limited flat land; land-use planning in communal areas |
| **Dispersed** (scattered) | Single homesteads spread widely, separated by fields or grazing land | Large farms (commercial farms and ranches), plentiful water everywhere, peace and security, each family living on its own plot |
| **Linear** (ribbon) | Buildings in a line along a road, railway, river, valley floor or ridge | Access to transport and trade; a narrow strip of flat land between steep slopes; a line of water supply such as a river |

On a map extract, a nucleated pattern appears as a tight cluster of building symbols, a dispersed pattern as isolated huts across the map, and a linear pattern as huts lined up along a road or stream.

## Laws governing land use in Zimbabwe

Several laws control how land is used and where people may build. Examples include:

- the **Regional, Town and Country Planning Act**, which provides for master and local plans and planning permission for development;
- the **Urban Councils Act** and the **Rural District Councils Act**, which give councils powers over land use, services and building in their areas;
- the **Communal Land Act** and the **Traditional Leaders Act**, which govern the allocation and use of communal land;
- the **Environmental Management Act**, which requires an environmental impact assessment (EIA) for major projects and protects wetlands and river banks.

These laws aim to prevent haphazard building, protect farmland and the environment, and make sure services can be provided.

## Siting settlements safely and disaster-resilient infrastructure

Disasters such as **Cyclone Idai** (March 2019, Chimanimani and Chipinge districts) showed how dangerous some sites are: homes built on steep slopes and near rivers were swept away by landslides and floods. Factors considered in siting settlements safely:

1. Avoid **flood plains** and river banks; build above the highest known flood level.
2. Avoid **steep and unstable slopes**, especially where soil is deep and saturated.
3. Avoid **wetlands (vleis)**, which flood and are protected.
4. Use hazard maps and an **EIA** before building.

Features of **disaster-resilient infrastructure**:

- Strong foundations and reinforced walls; roofs tied down firmly against strong winds.
- Raised floors or buildings on stilts in flood-prone areas.
- Bridges and culverts large enough for flood flows; good storm drains.
- Retaining walls, terraces and vegetation to stabilise slopes.
- Evacuation routes, safe shelters on high ground and early-warning systems.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up site and situation"}
"The town is near a river" describes the **site** only if the river is on or beside the land itself. "The town is where two main roads meet" is **situation**. Site = the ground; situation = the surroundings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a pattern without a cause"}
Saying "the pattern is nucleated" earns little. Link it to evidence on the map (huts clustered round a borehole) and a reason (shared water supply).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 Section C"}
Map-work MCQs often ask you to identify a settlement pattern or the site factor of a named place. In Paper 2, "describe the site" needs physical features of the land; "explain the situation" needs links to routes, resources and other places. Give one reason per mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Disaster-resilient buildings"}
For "suggest features of disaster-resilient infrastructure", match each feature to a hazard: raised floors for floods, tied-down roofs for strong winds, retaining walls for landslides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rural settlements are small with primary activities and few services; urban settlements are large with secondary and tertiary activities and many services.
- Site is the land a settlement stands on (water, relief, soil, defence, materials, safety); situation is its position relative to routes, resources and other places.
- Rural patterns are nucleated (clustered round water or services), dispersed (scattered on large farms) or linear (along roads, rivers or valleys).
- Zimbabwean laws such as the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act, the Urban Councils and Rural District Councils Acts, the Communal Land Act and the Environmental Management Act control land use.
- Safe siting avoids flood plains, steep slopes and wetlands; resilient infrastructure uses strong foundations, raised floors, secure roofs, drains, slope protection and early warning.
:::
