## Introduction

Every week buses arrive at Mbare Musika in Harare carrying young people from rural areas who hope to find work, finish their education or start a business in the city. Their arrival, together with the children born to families already living in town, makes Zimbabwe's towns grow. The same process is happening, much faster, in cities across Africa and South Asia.

This lesson explains what urbanisation is, why towns grow at different speeds in different parts of the world, why growth is so rapid in low-income countries (LICs), and how urbanisation has unfolded in Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urbanisation"}
An increase in the **proportion** (percentage) of a country's population that lives in urban areas.
:::

:::definition{term="Urban growth"}
An increase in the **number** of people living in towns and cities, or in the area they cover. A city can grow even if the urban percentage of the country stays the same.
:::

:::definition{term="Rural-urban migration"}
The movement of people from the countryside to towns and cities, usually to live and work permanently.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural increase"}
Birth rate minus death rate; in towns it adds people even without migration.
:::

:::definition{term="Counter-urbanisation"}
The movement of people out of large cities to smaller towns and rural areas, common in many high-income countries (HICs).
:::

:::definition{term="Megacity"}
A city with more than 10 million people.
:::

## Calculating the level of urbanisation

The level of urbanisation is the urban population as a percentage of the total population.

:::example{title="Worked example (fictional country)"}
A fictional country has 18.4 million people, of whom 6.9 million live in towns.

1. Level of urbanisation = urban population ÷ total population × 100
2. = 6.9 ÷ 18.4 × 100 = **37.5%**

Ten years later the urban population is 9.2 million out of 21.6 million: 9.2 ÷ 21.6 × 100 = 42.6%. Urbanisation has taken place (37.5% → 42.6%), and urban growth too (6.9 → 9.2 million).
:::

## Global patterns of urban growth

Since about 2007, more than half of the world's people have lived in towns and cities (United Nations estimates). But the speed of growth varies.

| Region | Level of urbanisation | Rate of urban growth | Main reasons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HICs** (e.g. Western Europe, North America, Japan) | High: often about 75-90% | **Slow**, sometimes falling in inner cities | Urbanisation happened during industrialisation in the 1800s and 1900s; few rural people left to move; low natural increase; counter-urbanisation |
| **MICs** (e.g. much of Latin America, China) | Medium to high | Moderate to fast | Industrial growth attracts workers; Latin America is already highly urbanised, so growth has slowed |
| **LICs** (e.g. much of sub-Saharan Africa, parts of South Asia) | Low: often 20-45% | **Fastest** in the world | Rapid rural-urban migration plus high natural increase in young urban populations |

Most of the world's future urban growth is expected in **sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia**, where urban populations are young and still growing quickly. Some of the fastest-growing cities are in countries such as Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo and India.

## Why urban growth is rapid in LICs

There are two main causes.

### 1. Rural-urban migration

People move because of **push factors** in rural areas and **pull factors** in towns.

| Push factors (rural areas) | Pull factors (towns) |
|---|---|
| Drought, crop failure and land degradation | Jobs in factories, offices, construction and the informal sector |
| Shortage of land as families grow | Higher wages and more chance of earning cash |
| Poverty and low farm incomes | Better schools, colleges and hospitals |
| Few jobs outside farming | Piped water, electricity, entertainment |
| Lack of services (clinics, secondary schools, electricity) | Family and friends already living in town |
| Natural disasters and conflict | The "bright lights": perceived better quality of life |

Many migrants find the reality harder than they expected: there may be no formal job and no affordable housing. But they often stay because opportunities, however small, are still greater than in the village.

### 2. Natural increase in towns

Migrants are mostly **young adults** in their reproductive years. Their children are born in the town, so the urban population grows naturally. Better health care in towns also lowers death rates, so natural increase is high.

## Urbanisation in Zimbabwe

1. **Colonial period (before 1980):** towns such as Salisbury (now Harare) and Bulawayo were founded as administrative, mining, railway and trading centres. Africans' movement to and residence in towns was restricted by law, and many men worked in town while their families stayed in rural areas. Urban growth was therefore controlled.
2. **After independence (1980):** restrictions were removed. Rural-urban migration increased sharply as people sought jobs, education and services, and towns grew quickly.
3. **1990s onwards:** economic problems, job losses in formal industry and the high cost of urban living slowed formal employment growth. The informal sector expanded, and many households keep links with both town and rural home (*circular migration*).
4. **Pattern today:** roughly a third of Zimbabweans lived in urban areas at the 2012 census, a lower share than many neighbouring countries. Harare and its surrounding towns (Chitungwiza, Epworth, Ruwa, Norton) form the largest urban area, followed by Bulawayo. Mining towns, growth points and border towns such as Beitbridge have also grown.

:::example{title="Case study: why people move to Harare"}
- **Push:** drought years and small plots in communal areas; few jobs in rural districts; lack of secondary schools and colleges nearby.
- **Pull:** jobs in commerce, manufacturing and government; the informal sector (vending, transport, small industry in areas such as Mbare and Glen View); universities and colleges; hospitals; relatives already in the city.
- **Natural increase:** young migrant families raise children in the city, adding to growth.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using urbanisation and urban growth as the same thing"}
Urbanisation is a change in the **percentage** urban. Urban growth is a change in the **number** of urban people or the area of a city. Use the right term, especially in data questions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting natural increase"}
Rapid urban growth in LICs is not caused by migration alone. Young migrants have children in town, so natural increase is a major cause.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Urbanisation is a rise in the percentage of people living in towns; urban growth is a rise in the number of urban people or urban area.
- Level of urbanisation = urban population ÷ total population × 100.
- HICs are highly urbanised but growing slowly; LICs are less urbanised but growing fastest, especially in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- Rapid urban growth in LICs comes from rural-urban migration (push and pull factors) and high natural increase among young urban populations.
- In Zimbabwe, colonial controls limited African urban growth; growth accelerated after 1980 and later slowed with economic difficulties, leaving roughly a third of people urban at the 2012 census.
:::
