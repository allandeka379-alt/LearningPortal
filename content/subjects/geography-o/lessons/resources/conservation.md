## Introduction

At night on Lake Kariba, rows of lights shine across the water: kapenta rigs are fishing, using lamps to attract the small silver fish into their nets. Kapenta are not native to Kariba; they were brought from Lake Tanganyika in the 1960s and now support an important fishery. But if too many rigs fish too hard, catches fall and the fishery can collapse.

The same is true of water in the dry season, of woodland around growing towns and of soil on farmland. **Conservation** means using resources wisely so that they last. This lesson explains why conservation is needed and how water, forests, fish and soil are conserved, with examples from Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
The careful management and wise use of natural resources so that they are protected from waste and damage and remain available in the future.
:::

:::definition{term="Afforestation"}
Planting trees on land that has not recently been forested. Planting trees on land where forest has been cut is **reforestation**.
:::

:::definition{term="Quota"}
A limit on the amount of fish (or another resource) that may be caught or taken in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Closed season"}
A period when fishing (or hunting) is not allowed, usually during breeding, so that stocks can recover.
:::

:::definition{term="Water harvesting"}
Collecting and storing rainwater, for example from roofs or rock surfaces, for later use.
:::

## Why conserve resources?

- Many resources are **limited**, and even renewable ones can be used up if they are used faster than they are replaced.
- **Population growth** and economic development increase demand.
- **Droughts** are common in Zimbabwe, so water must be stored and used carefully.
- Over-use causes **environmental damage**: deforestation, soil erosion, siltation, loss of wildlife.
- Resources support **livelihoods**: fishing, farming, timber and tourism provide jobs and income.
- Future generations have a right to the same resources (**sustainability**).

## Conserving water

| Method | How it conserves water |
| --- | --- |
| Dams and reservoirs | Store wet-season runoff for use in the dry season (e.g. Lake Chivero for Harare; many small dams on farms and in communal areas) |
| Rainwater harvesting | Roof gutters fill tanks; tied ridges and small pits trap rain in fields |
| Efficient irrigation | Drip irrigation delivers water to the roots, losing far less to evaporation than flood or overhead irrigation; watering at night or early morning also helps |
| Recycling and reuse | Treated waste water can irrigate parks and crops; grey water from washing can water gardens |
| Reducing waste | Repairing leaking pipes and taps; metering and charging for water |
| Protecting catchments and wetlands | Vleis and vegetated catchments store water and reduce silt reaching dams |
| Reducing evaporation | Mulching soil; covering tanks; planting windbreaks |

## Conserving forests and woodland

| Method | How it conserves forests |
| --- | --- |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Plantations supply timber so natural woodland is spared; replanting restores cut areas |
| Controlled (selective) cutting | Only mature trees are cut, by permit, leaving younger trees to grow |
| Alternative fuels | Electricity, solar cookers, biogas and LPG reduce demand for firewood |
| Fuel-efficient stoves | Use much less wood for the same cooking |
| Woodlots | Community or school plots of fast-growing trees for poles and firewood |
| Fire control | Fireguards and early burning prevent destructive late-season fires |
| Laws and protected areas | Permits for cutting and moving timber; forest reserves |
| Education | Awareness campaigns and tree-planting days |

:::example
**Case study: Forestry Commission plantations, Eastern Highlands.** The cool, wet Eastern Highlands around Nyanga, Mutasa and Chimanimani have large plantations of exotic trees such as pine, eucalyptus and wattle. The Forestry Commission manages state forests and regulates forestry in Zimbabwe. Plantations supply timber for sawmills, poles, paper and building, which reduces pressure on natural woodland. Trees are planted in blocks of different ages, so that some blocks can be felled and replanted each year (a rotation). Plantations face problems too: forest fires, and in 2019 Cyclone Idai caused severe damage in Chimanimani. Exotic plantations also support fewer native species than natural forest.
:::

## Conserving fish

| Method | How it conserves fish |
| --- | --- |
| Quotas and licences | Limit the total catch and the number of boats or rigs |
| Closed seasons | No fishing during the breeding season, so fish can reproduce |
| Minimum net mesh sizes | Young fish escape through larger holes and grow to breeding size |
| Restricted areas | Breeding grounds, river mouths and shallow bays closed to fishing |
| Banning destructive methods | No poison, explosives or very fine nets |
| Fish farming (aquaculture) | Fish such as bream (tilapia) raised in ponds or cages supply food without depleting wild stocks |
| Restocking | Young fish released into dams and lakes |
| Pollution control | Clean water protects fish breeding and food supply |

:::example
**Case study: the Lake Kariba kapenta fishery.** Lake Kariba was formed when the Kariba dam was completed on the Zambezi in 1959. Kapenta (a small sardine-like fish) were introduced from Lake Tanganyika in the 1960s. The fishery is shared by Zimbabwe and Zambia. Rigs fish at night using lights to attract the fish into large lift nets; the catch is dried in the sun and sold widely. To conserve stocks, authorities control the number of fishing permits and rigs, and catches have been monitored for many years. When too many rigs fish, the catch per rig falls, a warning sign of over-fishing. Cage fish farming on the lake also supplies bream without taking wild fish.
:::

## Conserving soil (in outline)

Soil is conserved by contour ploughing and contour ridges, terracing steep slopes, crop rotation, mulching and cover crops, reducing stock to the carrying capacity, planting trees as windbreaks, and filling gullies with check dams and vegetation. These methods are studied in more detail with land degradation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing conservation with preservation"}
Conservation allows careful **use** of a resource; preservation means leaving it untouched.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague methods"}
"Plant trees" is weak. Say "establish woodlots of fast-growing eucalyptus so that families cut poles there instead of in natural woodland".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting how a method works"}
For closed seasons, say **why**: fish can breed without being caught, so stocks are replaced.
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conservation means using resources wisely so they last; it is needed because of limited supply, population growth, drought and environmental damage.
- Water: dams, rainwater harvesting, drip irrigation, recycling, fixing leaks, protecting catchments and wetlands.
- Forests: afforestation and reforestation, controlled cutting, alternative fuels, efficient stoves, woodlots, fire control and laws (e.g. Forestry Commission plantations in the Eastern Highlands).
- Fish: quotas, licences, closed seasons, mesh sizes, restricted areas and fish farming (e.g. Lake Kariba kapenta and cage farming).
- Soil: contour ridges, terracing, crop rotation, mulching and controlled grazing.
:::
