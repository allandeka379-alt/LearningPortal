## Introduction

In parts of the Zambezi valley, a family can lose a season's maize in one night when elephants raid the fields. Yet the same elephants attract tourists and safari hunters who bring money into the district. Wildlife is a valuable resource for Zimbabwe, but people who live beside it often bear the costs.

This lesson explains how wildlife is managed in Zimbabwe, why conflict between people and wildlife happens and how it can be reduced, and how the CAMPFIRE programme tries to make wildlife benefit rural communities.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Wildlife management"}
The control and care of wild animals and their habitats so that populations stay healthy and the resource can be used sustainably.
:::

:::definition{term="Human-wildlife conflict"}
Any interaction between people and wild animals that harms people, their crops, livestock or property, or harms the animals.
:::

:::definition{term="Problem animal"}
A wild animal that repeatedly damages crops, livestock or property, such as elephants raiding fields, baboons eating crops or bushpigs digging up groundnuts.
:::

:::definition{term="Dangerous animal"}
A wild animal that can injure or kill people, such as a lion, crocodile, buffalo, hippopotamus or elephant.
:::

:::definition{term="CAMPFIRE"}
The Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources: a Zimbabwean programme, started in 1989, in which rural district councils and communities manage wildlife and other natural resources on communal land and receive income from them.
:::

## Types of protected area

| Type | Main purpose | Examples in Zimbabwe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National park | Conservation of wildlife and scenery; photographic tourism; no hunting | Hwange (the largest), Gonarezhou, Mana Pools, Matobo, Victoria Falls |
| Safari area | Wildlife conservation with controlled sport hunting | Areas next to parks, e.g. in the Zambezi valley |
| Sanctuary | Protection of particular species | Small areas for threatened species |
| Conservancy | Neighbouring private ranches combine land for wildlife, removing internal fences | Lowveld conservancies |
| Game park / ranch | Private land stocked with wildlife for tourism, hunting or meat | Many commercial properties |

National parks and safari areas are managed by the Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority.

:::example
**Four national parks to know.**
- **Hwange National Park**, in the north-west near Victoria Falls, is Zimbabwe's largest. It is famous for large elephant herds; in the dry season, water pumped into pans keeps animals in the park.
- **Gonarezhou National Park**, in the south-east Lowveld, has red sandstone cliffs along the Runde River and is part of the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park shared with South Africa and Mozambique.
- **Mana Pools National Park**, on the Zambezi floodplain below Kariba, is a World Heritage Site known for its pools, floodplain wildlife and canoe safaris.
- **Matobo National Park**, south of Bulawayo, protects granite hills, rock art and rhinos; the Matobo Hills are a World Heritage Site.
:::

## Benefits of wildlife management

- **Tourism**: foreign currency, jobs for guides, lodge staff and craft sellers.
- **Conservation**: protects species and habitats for the future and for research.
- **Controlled hunting**: high fees per animal from safari hunters; meat for local people.
- **Use of marginal land**: dry, hot areas (natural regions IV and V) may earn more from wildlife than from crops.
- **Education and research**, cultural and scenic value.

## Problems of wildlife management

- **Poaching** for ivory, rhino horn, bushmeat and pangolins.
- **Overpopulation of some species**: too many elephants in a park can destroy trees and habitat.
- **Drought**: water shortages in parks; animals die or move out.
- **Costs**: rangers, vehicles, fences and water pumping are expensive.
- **Human-wildlife conflict** on park boundaries.
- **Disease** spread between wildlife and livestock (for example foot-and-mouth disease between buffalo and cattle).
- **International bans** and import restrictions on ivory or trophies can reduce income.

## Human-wildlife conflict

**Causes**
- Population growth and settlement expanding next to or into wildlife areas and corridors.
- Fields on the animals' migration routes or near water.
- Drought, when animals leave parks in search of food and water.
- Unfenced boundaries; broken fences.
- Growing animal numbers (e.g. elephants) inside parks.

**Effects**
- Crops destroyed, livestock killed, people injured or killed.
- Damage to granaries, fences and water points.
- Retaliatory killing or poisoning of animals.
- Children kept from school to guard fields.

**Solutions**
- **Problem animal control**: trained teams scare away or, as a last resort, destroy persistent problem animals.
- **Deterrents**: chilli fences, chilli-dung smoke, beehive fences and noise to keep elephants out of fields.
- **Electric fences** around fields or along park boundaries.
- **Kraaling** livestock in strong enclosures at night.
- **Land-use planning**: keep settlement and fields away from corridors and water points.
- **Compensation** or insurance for losses.
- **Benefit sharing** so communities gain from wildlife, which is the aim of CAMPFIRE.
- **Education** about safety near dangerous animals.

## The CAMPFIRE programme

**Why was it set up?** Wildlife on communal land was owned by the state, so local people suffered the costs of living with wild animals but received little of the benefit. Many saw animals only as pests and poached or killed them. CAMPFIRE gave rural district councils the authority to manage wildlife in their areas, so that income from wildlife would go back to the communities that live with it.

**How does it work?**
1. The district council sells hunting concessions to safari operators and leases land for photographic tourism.
2. Most income has come from trophy hunting, especially of elephant and buffalo.
3. Part of the income goes to wards and villages, which decide how to use it.
4. Funds pay for community projects such as schools, clinics, boreholes and grinding mills, and sometimes cash payments to households, as well as for problem animal control.

:::example
**Case study: Nyaminyami District (Kariba).** Nyaminyami, on the southern shore of Lake Kariba, was one of the first two districts to join CAMPFIRE in 1989 (with Guruve). Many of its people are Tonga families who were moved from the Zambezi valley floor when Lake Kariba filled. The area is hot, dry and poor for crops, but rich in elephant, buffalo and other wildlife. Income from safari hunting and tourism was used for local projects such as schools, clinics and water supplies, and for problem animal control. The district shows both the promise of CAMPFIRE, as communities gained income from wildlife, and its problems, as income varied from year to year with hunting quotas, tourism and international restrictions.
:::

**Benefits**
- Income for poor rural communities in areas unsuited to crops.
- Community projects: schools, clinics, boreholes.
- Local people value wildlife, so poaching may fall.
- Wildlife habitat is conserved outside parks.
- Uses marginal land sustainably.

**Problems and sustainability**
- Income depends heavily on trophy hunting, which can fall when international rules change or tourism declines.
- Money may be kept by councils rather than reaching households.
- Payments shared among many households can be small compared with crop losses.
- Growing human populations and new settlement reduce wildlife habitat.
- Drought and disease affect animal numbers.

CAMPFIRE is sustainable only if wildlife numbers are kept healthy, income reaches the people who bear the costs, and income sources are diversified (for example photographic tourism, crafts, honey and other natural products).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up problem and dangerous animals"}
A problem animal damages property, crops or livestock; a dangerous animal threatens human life. Some animals, such as elephants, can be both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying CAMPFIRE is only about hunting"}
Hunting has provided most of the income, but CAMPFIRE is about communities managing and benefiting from all natural resources.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing solutions without saying how they work"}
"Chilli fences" earns more as "chilli fences irritate elephants' sensitive noses, so they avoid the fields".
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Protected areas: national parks (Hwange, Gonarezhou, Mana Pools, Matobo), safari areas, sanctuaries, conservancies and game parks.
- Benefits: tourism, conservation, hunting income, use of marginal land; problems: poaching, overpopulation of elephants, drought, costs, disease and conflict.
- Problem animals damage crops, livestock or property; dangerous animals threaten life.
- Conflict is caused by settlement near wildlife, drought and animal movement; solutions include problem animal control, chilli and electric fences, kraaling, planning, compensation and benefit sharing.
- CAMPFIRE (from 1989; early districts Nyaminyami and Guruve) gives communities income from wildlife, mainly from hunting, for local projects; its sustainability depends on income reaching households and on diversifying income.
:::
