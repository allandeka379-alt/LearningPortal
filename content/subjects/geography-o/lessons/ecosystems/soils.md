## Introduction

Dig a pit on a granite ridge near Masvingo and you find pale, gritty sand that drains quickly and dries out soon after rain. Dig on the dolerite or basalt land of parts of the highveld and you find heavy, dark-red clay that holds water and nutrients. Dig in a vlei and you find grey, sticky, waterlogged soil. Each soil suits different crops and different farming methods.

Soil is the thin layer between rock and atmosphere on which nearly all farming depends. This lesson explains what soil contains, how its properties are measured, how tropical soils form, and how to describe a soil profile.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soil"}
The loose surface layer of the land, made of weathered rock particles, organic matter (humus), water and air, in which plants grow.
:::

:::definition{term="Humus"}
Dark, decomposed organic matter in the soil, formed from dead plants and animals; it holds water and nutrients.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil texture"}
The proportions of sand, silt and clay particles in a soil, which decide how it feels and how it drains.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil profile"}
A vertical section through the soil from the surface down to the parent rock, showing its layers (horizons).
:::

:::definition{term="Parent material"}
The rock or sediment from which a soil develops by weathering.
:::

## Components of soil

A typical well-structured loam contains roughly (by volume):

| Component | Approximate share | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mineral particles | about 45% | from weathered rock; supply plant nutrients and form the soil's framework |
| Organic matter | about 5% | humus, roots and organisms; holds water and nutrients, binds particles |
| Water | about 25% | dissolves nutrients for roots; varies with the weather |
| Air | about 25% | oxygen for roots and soil organisms; fills pore spaces not taken by water |

Water and air share the pore spaces, so when a soil is waterlogged there is little air.

## Soil properties

- **Texture.** Sand particles are the largest and feel gritty; silt feels smooth like flour; clay particles are the smallest and feel sticky when wet. A **loam** has a balanced mix and is the best farming soil.
- **Structure.** The way particles clump together into aggregates (crumbs, blocks, plates). A crumb structure lets air, water and roots move easily.
- **Colour.** Dark brown or black often means plenty of humus; red means iron oxides in a well-drained soil; grey or blue-grey with rusty mottles means waterlogging (gleying); pale or whitish layers mean leaching.
- **pH.** How acidic or alkaline the soil is (most crops prefer about pH 6-7). Heavy rain and leaching make soils more acidic; farmers add lime to raise the pH.
- **Organic and mineral content** affect fertility and water-holding capacity.

### Sand, clay and loam compared

| Property | Sandy soil | Clay soil | Loam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drainage | fast, dries out | slow, may waterlog | good |
| Water holding | poor | high | good |
| Nutrients | low, easily leached | high | good |
| Working | light, easy to plough | heavy, sticky when wet, hard when dry | easy |
| Warming in spring | quick | slow | moderate |

In Zimbabwe, **granite** weathers to sandy soils (common in many communal areas), while **dolerite** and **basalt** weather to heavier, more fertile red clays. Western Zimbabwe has deep **Kalahari sands**.

## Simple soil tests

:::example
**Four tests you can do at school.**

1. **Texture by feel.** Moisten a small sample and rub it between finger and thumb. Gritty = sandy; smooth = silty; sticky and able to form a ribbon = clay.
2. **Sedimentation (jar) test.** Shake soil with water in a tall jar and leave it to settle. Sand settles first at the bottom, then silt, then clay; humus floats. Measure each layer to estimate the proportions.
3. **Water content.** Weigh a fresh sample, dry it in a warm oven, and weigh again. Water content (%) = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ fresh mass × 100.
4. **Organic content.** Weigh the dry sample, heat it strongly to burn off the organic matter, and weigh again. Organic content (%) = (dry mass − burnt mass) ÷ dry mass × 100.

**Worked example (fictional data).** A fresh sample weighs 80 g; after drying it weighs 68 g; after burning it weighs 64.6 g.
- Water content = (80 − 68) ÷ 80 × 100 = **15%**.
- Organic content = (68 − 64.6) ÷ 68 × 100 = **5%**.

pH is tested by mixing soil with distilled water and adding universal indicator, or by using a pH meter or test kit.
:::

## Soil-forming factors

Soils depend on **parent material**, **climate** (rainfall and temperature control weathering, leaching and decay), **relief** (steep slopes have thin soils; hollows collect water), **organisms** (plants supply humus; termites and earthworms mix the soil) and **time**.

## Soil-forming processes in the tropics

- **Leaching.** Rainwater moving down through the soil dissolves and removes soluble minerals and nutrients, making the upper soil poorer and more acidic. It is strongest where rainfall is heavy.
- **Laterisation.** In hot, wet tropical climates, intense weathering and leaching remove silica and bases, leaving iron and aluminium oxides behind. Soils become deep, red and infertile (latosols or ferralitic soils). Where these oxides harden on exposure, a hard crust called **laterite** forms.
- **Gleying.** Where soil is waterlogged, for example in vleis and on badly drained valley floors, there is little oxygen. Iron compounds are reduced, giving grey or blue-grey colours with rusty orange mottles where some air enters. The result is a **gley soil** (hydromorphic soil).
- **Cheluviation.** Acids released by decaying plant litter combine with iron and aluminium (chelation) and carry them down the profile. A bleached, pale layer is left near the top and the iron and aluminium are deposited lower down. This process forms **podzolic** soils, and is most likely under acid litter on sandy, free-draining soils.

## Soil profiles and horizons

Imagine the wall of a freshly dug pit:

| Horizon | Name | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| O | Organic layer | leaf litter and partly decomposed matter on the surface |
| A | Topsoil | dark, rich in humus, most roots and soil life; nutrients may be leached out (eluviation) |
| E | Eluviated layer | pale, bleached layer where minerals have been washed out (clear in podzolic soils) |
| B | Subsoil | lighter colour; minerals washed in from above (illuviation) may collect here, sometimes as a hard pan |
| C | Weathered parent material | broken, partly weathered rock |
| R | Bedrock | solid, unweathered rock |

Not every soil has every horizon, and boundaries may be sharp or gradual.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing texture and structure"}
Texture is the size mix of particles (sand, silt, clay). Structure is how the particles clump together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking red soils are always fertile"}
Red shows iron oxides and good drainage. Heavily leached red latosols can be very infertile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up eluviation and illuviation"}
Eluviation is washing **out** of a horizon (E, A); illuviation is washing **into** a horizon (B).
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Soil contains mineral particles (about 45%), organic matter (about 5%), water and air (about 25% each in a typical loam).
- Properties: texture (sand, silt, clay; loam is best), structure, colour, pH, organic and mineral content.
- Simple tests: feel, sedimentation jar, water content by drying and organic content by burning.
- Tropical processes: leaching, laterisation (red latosols, laterite), gleying (grey gley soils in waterlogged areas) and cheluviation (podzolic soils).
- Profiles show O, A, E, B, C and R horizons; eluviation removes material, illuviation deposits it.
:::
