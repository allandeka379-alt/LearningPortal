## Introduction

Antarctica is the continent around the South Pole. Almost all of it is buried under ice, nobody lives there permanently, and yet the ocean around it is one of the most productive on Earth, feeding whales, seals, penguins and seabirds. Its ice holds most of the world's fresh water, so what happens there matters for sea levels everywhere, including the coasts of Mozambique and South Africa.

This lesson explains where Antarctica is, why its climate is so extreme, what the ecosystem is like and how plants and animals survive. The next lesson looks at threats to Antarctica and how it is managed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ice sheet"}
A very thick mass of glacier ice covering a large area of land, more than about 50,000 km². The Antarctic ice sheet is on average about 2 km thick.
:::

:::definition{term="Sea ice"}
Frozen sea water floating on the ocean surface. Around Antarctica it spreads out in winter and shrinks in summer.
:::

:::definition{term="Krill"}
Small, shrimp-like crustaceans that feed on phytoplankton and are the main food of many Antarctic whales, seals, penguins, fish and seabirds.
:::

:::definition{term="Albedo"}
The proportion of incoming sunlight that a surface reflects. Fresh snow and ice have a very high albedo.
:::

:::definition{term="Katabatic wind"}
A wind that blows downhill when cold, dense air flows down a slope, such as off the high Antarctic ice sheet towards the coast.
:::

## Location

- Antarctica lies almost entirely south of the **Antarctic Circle (about 66.5° S)** and is centred on the South Pole.
- It is surrounded by the **Southern Ocean**. The nearest other continent is South America, across the Drake Passage from the Antarctic Peninsula.
- The **Antarctic Peninsula** is a long arm of land reaching north towards South America; it is the mildest part of the continent.
- Its area is about 14 million km², larger than Europe.
- International agreements apply to the area south of 60° S.

## Climate

| Feature | Description (approximate values) |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | Very cold all year. The interior averages far below freezing; the lowest temperature measured on the surface, about −89 °C, was at Vostok station in 1983. The coast and the Peninsula are milder, sometimes just above 0 °C in summer. |
| Precipitation | Very low: the interior is a **polar desert** with less than about 50 mm (water equivalent) a year; the coasts receive more. |
| Wind | Strong katabatic winds blow off the ice sheet towards the coast. |
| Light | Long periods of continuous daylight in summer and continuous darkness in winter. |

**Why is it so cold and dry?**

1. **High latitude and low sun angle.** The sun is always low in the sky, so its energy is spread over a large area and passes through more atmosphere.
2. **Long polar night.** For months in winter the sun does not rise at all.
3. **High albedo.** Snow and ice reflect most of the sunlight that does arrive.
4. **High altitude.** Much of the ice sheet is more than 2,000 m above sea level, and temperature falls with height.
5. **High pressure.** Cold, dense air sinks over the continent, forming a polar high. Sinking air is dry, so very little snow falls, and it flows outwards as katabatic winds.
6. **Isolation.** The Southern Ocean and its circumpolar current cut Antarctica off from warmer air and water.

## Features of the ecosystem

- **The ice sheet** covers about 98% of the land. Where ice flows into the sea it forms floating **ice shelves**, which break off as icebergs.
- **Ice-free land** is limited mainly to coasts, mountain tops and the Peninsula. Here **mosses, lichens and algae** grow, and just **two flowering plants** are found: a grass (Antarctic hair grass) and a pearlwort, mainly on the Peninsula.
- **Sea ice** grows to cover a huge area of ocean in winter and melts back in summer. Algae grow on the underside of the ice.
- **The Southern Ocean** is cold but rich in nutrients brought up by upwelling currents. In the long summer days, **phytoplankton** bloom.

### The marine food web

The whole ecosystem depends on the ocean, not the land.

| Level | Organisms |
| --- | --- |
| Producers | phytoplankton and ice algae |
| Primary consumers | krill and other zooplankton |
| Secondary consumers | baleen whales (such as blue and humpback whales), crabeater seals, Adélie penguins, fish, squid |
| Higher consumers | leopard seals, emperor penguins, Weddell seals, killer whales (orcas) |

Krill is the **keystone species**: if krill numbers fall, whales, seals, penguins and seabirds all suffer.

## Links between abiotic and biotic factors

:::example
**A chain of links (a "diagram in words").**

1. Summer brings almost 24 hours of daylight and sea ice melts back (abiotic).
2. Light and upwelled nutrients allow phytoplankton and ice algae to bloom (biotic).
3. Krill feed on the algae and multiply in huge swarms.
4. Whales migrate south to feed on krill; penguins and seals raise their young while food is plentiful.
5. In winter, darkness and spreading sea ice end the bloom; many animals migrate north, and those that stay depend on stored fat.

Without the abiotic conditions of summer light and cold, nutrient-rich water, the food web would collapse.
:::

## Adaptations

**Plants**
- Mosses and lichens grow very slowly and can survive being frozen and dried out for long periods.
- They grow low to the ground or in cracks, out of the wind.
- Dark colours help some absorb heat.

**Animals**
- **Blubber** (thick fat) and dense, waterproof fur or feathers insulate seals, whales and penguins.
- **Emperor penguins** breed during the winter on the sea ice; males balance the egg on their feet under a fold of skin, and the birds **huddle** in large groups, taking turns on the cold outside.
- **Weddell seals** keep breathing holes open in the ice by grinding them with their teeth.
- Some **fish** have special proteins in their blood that act like antifreeze.
- **Krill** can survive winter by feeding on algae under the ice and using stored reserves.
- Many whales and seabirds **migrate** north in winter.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Arctic and the Antarctic"}
The Arctic is a frozen ocean surrounded by land, with polar bears. Antarctica is a continent surrounded by ocean, with penguins and no polar bears.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Antarctica has heavy snowfall"}
The interior is a polar desert. The ice is thick because the little snow that falls has built up over hundreds of thousands of years with very little melting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a land-based food web"}
Almost all Antarctic food chains begin in the ocean with phytoplankton. Land plants are few and support only small invertebrates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Antarctica is a continent around the South Pole, surrounded by the Southern Ocean, with about 98% of its land under an ice sheet.
- Its climate is the coldest, driest and windiest on Earth because of the low sun angle, polar night, high albedo, high altitude and high pressure with katabatic winds.
- The ecosystem is mainly marine: phytoplankton → krill → whales, seals, penguins and seabirds; krill is the keystone species.
- Summer light, sea-ice melt and upwelled nutrients drive a seasonal bloom that the whole food web depends on.
- Adaptations include blubber, dense feathers, huddling, antifreeze proteins, migration, and slow-growing mosses and lichens.
:::
