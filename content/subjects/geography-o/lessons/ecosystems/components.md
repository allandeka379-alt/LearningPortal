## Introduction

Walk across a vlei near your school in January. The grass is green and wet, frogs call from the pools, egrets stalk insects and cattle graze at the edge. Turn over a log and you find beetles, millipedes and white threads of fungi breaking down the wood. Everything you see is connected: the grass needs the sunlight and the water, the frogs need the insects, and the fungi return nutrients to the soil for the grass.

Geographers call such a connected unit an **ecosystem**. Understanding ecosystems helps us explain why forests, grasslands and wetlands look the way they do, and what happens when people burn, clear or drain them. This lesson gives you the vocabulary you will use in every other ecosystems lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="System"}
A set of parts (components) that are linked together and work as a whole. A system has **inputs**, **processes** (also called throughputs) and **outputs**.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecosystem"}
A community of living organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living environment in a particular area. It can be as small as a pond or as large as a forest.
:::

:::definition{term="Biotic component"}
A living part of an ecosystem: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
:::

:::definition{term="Abiotic component"}
A non-living part of an ecosystem: sunlight, temperature, rainfall, water, air, soil, rock and relief.
:::

:::definition{term="Habitat"}
The place where a plant or animal lives, for example a pool in a vlei for a frog.
:::

## Biotic and abiotic components

Every ecosystem has two groups of components.

| Biotic (living) | Abiotic (non-living) |
| --- | --- |
| Producers: grasses, trees, algae | Sunlight (energy) |
| Consumers: grasshoppers, cattle, birds, lions | Temperature and rainfall (climate) |
| Decomposers: bacteria, fungi | Soil, rock and minerals |
| Detritivores: termites, earthworms, millipedes | Water and air (oxygen, carbon dioxide) |

The two groups depend on each other. Rainfall and temperature decide which plants can grow; plants in turn shade the soil, add leaf litter and protect it from erosion.

## Producers, consumers and decomposers

- **Producers** (autotrophs) are green plants and algae. They use sunlight to make food by **photosynthesis**, turning carbon dioxide and water into sugars and releasing oxygen.
- **Consumers** (heterotrophs) eat other organisms. **Primary consumers** (herbivores) eat plants, such as a kudu browsing on leaves. **Secondary consumers** eat herbivores, such as a snake eating a mouse. **Tertiary consumers** eat secondary consumers, such as an eagle eating a snake. Animals that eat both plants and animals, such as baboons and people, are **omnivores**.
- **Decomposers** (bacteria and fungi) break down dead plants, dead animals and dung, releasing nutrients back into the soil. Without them, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter.

## Inputs, processes and outputs of a micro-ecosystem

A **micro-ecosystem** is a small ecosystem that you can study on a school field trip, such as a pond, a vlei, a rotting log or a termite mound.

:::example
**A termite mound (an annotated "diagram in words").** Imagine a mound about two metres high beside a school football pitch.

1. **Inputs:** sunlight warms the mound; rain falls on it; termites carry in grass, dead wood and leaf litter; air enters through tunnels.
2. **Processes:** termites chew and digest the plant material; fungi grown inside the mound break it down further; termites breathe (respiration) and mix clay with saliva to build walls; trees and grasses growing on the mound photosynthesise.
3. **Outputs:** heat and carbon dioxide escape through vents; waste and dead termites become nutrients; the mound soil, richer in nutrients, supports different plants from the surrounding veld; winged termites (*ishwa*) fly out after the first rains and are eaten by birds and people.
:::

The same thinking applies to a whole forest: inputs of sunlight, rain and nutrients from weathered rock; processes of photosynthesis, feeding, decay and nutrient uptake; outputs of oxygen, water vapour (transpiration), timber and runoff.

## Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** shows how energy passes from one organism to the next as each is eaten. Arrows point **in the direction energy flows**, from the food to the feeder.

$$
\text{grass} \rightarrow \text{grasshopper} \rightarrow \text{lizard} \rightarrow \text{hawk}
$$

Each step is a **trophic (feeding) level**: grass is the producer (level 1), the grasshopper the primary consumer (level 2), the lizard the secondary consumer (level 3) and the hawk the tertiary consumer (level 4).

Real animals eat more than one kind of food, so food chains join together into a **food web**. In a savanna web, grass may be eaten by grasshoppers, impala and zebra; impala may be eaten by leopards and wild dogs; zebra by lions. A web shows **interdependence**: if poachers removed most of the lions, zebra numbers might rise, grass would be overgrazed and grasshoppers and impala would then have less food.

## Pyramids of numbers and energy

A **pyramid of numbers** shows how many organisms are at each trophic level. Usually there are many producers, fewer herbivores and very few carnivores.

A **pyramid of energy** shows how much energy is stored at each level. It is always pyramid-shaped because energy is lost at every step: organisms use most of the energy they take in for respiration, movement and keeping warm, and some is lost in waste and uneaten parts. As a rough rule, only **about 10%** of the energy at one level is passed to the next.

![Pyramid of energy for the food chain grass, grasshopper, lizard, hawk: the grass stores 30,000 units a year and each level passes on only about 10 %, giving 3,000, 300 and 30 units, which is why food chains are short.](media:diagrams/energy-pyramid-food-chain.png "Figure: a pyramid of energy (fictional data) for a four-level food chain")

:::example
**Worked example (fictional data).** In a study plot, the grass stores 30,000 units of energy in a year.

1. Primary consumers receive about 10%: $30\,000 \times 0.1 = 3\,000$ units.
2. Secondary consumers receive about 10% of that: $3\,000 \times 0.1 = 300$ units.
3. Tertiary consumers receive about $300 \times 0.1 = 30$ units.

This loss explains why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels and why top predators such as lions are few and need large territories.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Arrows pointing the wrong way"}
Writing "hawk → lizard" because the hawk "goes to" the lizard. Arrows show the flow of energy, so they point from the organism eaten to the eater: lizard → hawk.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling decomposers consumers at the top of the chain"}
Decomposers act at every level, breaking down dead material from producers and all consumers. They are not the "last link" eaten by top predators.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing soil as biotic"}
Soil contains living things, but soil itself (minerals, water, air) is an abiotic component. Earthworms and bacteria in the soil are biotic.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 often asks you to **identify** inputs, processes and outputs of a named micro-ecosystem. Use three clear lists and name real items (rain, leaf litter; photosynthesis, decay; oxygen, runoff), not vague words like "things".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **draw** a food chain from a food web, use at least three organisms, start with a producer and keep every arrow pointing towards the feeder. When asked to **explain** a pyramid, mention energy used in respiration and lost as heat at each level.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and their non-living environment.
- Biotic components are living (producers, consumers, decomposers); abiotic components are non-living (sunlight, climate, soil, water, air).
- Systems have inputs, processes and outputs; a vlei, pond or termite mound is a micro-ecosystem you can study.
- Food chains and webs show energy flow; arrows point from the food to the feeder.
- About 10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next, so pyramids of energy narrow upwards and food chains are short.
:::
