## Introduction

Most of Zimbabwe is savanna. The miombo woodland of the highveld, the mopane of the Zambezi valley and the Lowveld, and the open grassland with scattered trees in Hwange are all parts of the tropical continental (savanna) biome. To the west, across Botswana and Namibia, rainfall falls further until the Kalahari gives way to the true desert of the Namib on the Atlantic coast.

Knowing how these biomes work helps explain why Zimbabwe's veld turns brown in the dry season, why farmers burn grass, and how plants and animals survive heat and drought. This lesson compares the savanna and the hot desert.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Savanna"}
A tropical biome of grassland with scattered trees and shrubs, or open woodland, found where there is a hot wet season and a long dry season.
:::

:::definition{term="Hot desert"}
A biome that receives very little rain (less than about 250 mm a year), with high daytime temperatures, sparse vegetation and a large daily temperature range.
:::

:::definition{term="Xerophyte"}
A plant adapted to survive drought, for example by storing water, reducing water loss or growing very long roots.
:::

:::definition{term="Deciduous"}
Describes trees that shed their leaves in an unfavourable season; in the savanna this is the dry season.
:::

:::definition{term="Ephemeral"}
A plant with a very short life cycle that germinates, flowers and sets seed quickly after rain.
:::

## Where are they found?

| Biome | Location in Africa (approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Savanna | Between the rainforest and the deserts, roughly 5-15° (and in places up to about 20°) north and south of the equator: the Sahel fringe, East Africa (Serengeti), and much of Southern Africa including Zimbabwe, Zambia and Mozambique |
| Hot desert | Roughly 15-30° north and south, especially on the western sides of continents: the Sahara in the north; the Namib along the Atlantic coast and the Kalahari (a semi-desert) in the south |

## Climate

| Feature | Savanna (e.g. Zimbabwe highveld) | Hot desert |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rainfall | about 500-1,500 mm, mainly in a hot wet season (in Zimbabwe about November to March) | less than about 250 mm, very unreliable |
| Seasons | wet season and a long dry season | dry all year |
| Temperature | hot (cooler in the dry winter at altitude) | very hot days; cold nights |
| Daily range | moderate | very large (clear skies) |

**Why?** The savanna has a wet season when the ITCZ and overhead sun move over it, bringing rising air and convectional rain, and a dry season when it lies under the subtropical high pressure belt. Hot deserts lie under the subtropical high pressure belt for most of the year: air sinks and warms, so clouds rarely form. On west coasts, cold ocean currents such as the Benguela Current off Namibia cool the air above them, so it cannot hold much moisture and fog forms instead of rain. Clear skies let the sun heat the ground strongly by day and let heat escape at night.

## Vegetation and adaptations

### Savanna

- **Grasses** grow tall in the wet season and die back in the dry season; their roots survive underground and regrow after fire or rain.
- **Trees** such as acacias (thorn trees) and the baobab:
  - **Deep roots** reach water far below the surface.
  - **Small or feathery leaves** and **thorns** reduce water loss and protect against browsing.
  - **Thick, corky bark** resists fire.
  - The **baobab** stores water in its massive, spongy trunk.
  - Many trees are **deciduous**, shedding leaves in the dry season to save water.
- **Umbrella-shaped crowns** shade the roots and catch the most light.

:::example
**Zimbabwe's savanna woodlands.**

- **Miombo woodland** covers much of the higher, wetter plateau. Its trees (such as *msasa*) are deciduous. Before the rains, many trees put out new leaves in shades of red, copper and bronze that later turn green.
- **Mopane woodland** grows in the hotter, drier low-lying areas such as the Zambezi valley and the Lowveld, often on heavier soils. Mopane leaves are shaped like a butterfly and fold together in the midday heat, which reduces water loss. Mopane worms (caterpillars of an emperor moth) feed on the leaves and are harvested as food.
:::

### Hot desert

- **Succulents** store water in fleshy stems or leaves.
- **Long tap roots** reach deep groundwater; **wide, shallow roots** catch light rain quickly.
- **Small, waxy or spiny leaves** reduce transpiration.
- **Ephemerals** survive as seeds and complete their life cycle in a few weeks after rain.
- In the Namib, some plants and animals collect moisture from coastal fog.

## Animal adaptations

| Savanna | Hot desert |
| --- | --- |
| Large herds migrate to follow rain and fresh grass (e.g. wildebeest in East Africa) | Many animals are nocturnal or burrow to avoid the heat |
| Giraffes browse high branches; zebra, wildebeest and buffalo graze grass, so species share food | Camels store fat in their humps, can go long periods without drinking and have wide feet for sand |
| Many animals breed in the wet season when food is plentiful | Large ears (e.g. the fennec fox) help to lose heat |
| Predators (lions, cheetahs) use camouflage in tall, dry grass | Some beetles in the Namib collect fog droplets on their bodies |

## Soils

- **Savanna soils** are often red, leached in the wet season, with a thin humus layer, as leaf and grass litter is limited and fire burns much of it. In Zimbabwe, granite gives sandy, light soils and dolerite or basalt gives heavier red clays.
- **Desert soils** are thin, sandy or stony, with very little organic matter. Evaporation can draw salts to the surface, forming salty crusts.

## Inputs, processes and outputs

| | Savanna | Hot desert |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inputs | sunlight, seasonal rain, nutrients from weathered rock and dung, seeds | sunlight, very little and irregular rain, wind-blown dust and fog on coasts |
| Processes | photosynthesis mainly in the wet season, grazing and browsing, decomposition (fast in the wet season, slow in the dry), burning, termite activity | slow plant growth, slow decay, strong evaporation |
| Outputs | water vapour by evapotranspiration, carbon dioxide and ash from fires, runoff in storms, animal products | water vapour, heat lost at night, wind-blown sand |

## The role of fire and grazing

Fire is a natural part of the savanna. Lightning at the start of the rains and people burning the veld in the dry season remove dead grass, kill tree seedlings and stop the woodland closing in, so savanna stays open. Fire-resistant trees with thick bark and grasses with underground roots survive. Grazing animals and browsers also keep grass and shrubs in check and recycle nutrients in dung. But **too much** burning and grazing reduces ground cover, exposes the soil to erosion and allows unpalatable shrubs to spread (bush encroachment).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a desert is always hot"}
Hot deserts have very hot days but can be cold at night, because clear skies let heat escape. The key feature is the low rainfall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all savanna 'grassland'"}
In Zimbabwe most savanna is woodland (miombo and mopane). Savanna ranges from open grassland with scattered trees to fairly dense deciduous woodland.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing an adaptation without its function"}
"Baobab: thick trunk" earns little. Write "the baobab stores water in its spongy trunk, so it survives the long dry season".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Savanna lies roughly 5-15° (in places to about 20°) from the equator; it has a hot wet season and a long dry season; Zimbabwe is mainly savanna woodland (miombo and mopane).
- Hot deserts lie about 15-30° from the equator under subtropical high pressure, often on west coasts beside cold currents, with less than about 250 mm of rain.
- Savanna plants have deep roots, thick bark, thorns, small leaves and are often deciduous; the baobab stores water; desert plants are succulents, deep- or wide-rooted, or ephemerals.
- Animals migrate, share food or are nocturnal, burrowing or able to lose heat.
- Fire and grazing keep savannas open, but too much causes erosion and bush encroachment.
:::
