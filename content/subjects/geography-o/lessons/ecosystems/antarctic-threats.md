## Introduction

Antarctica has no permanent population and no government of its own, yet it is not untouched. Scientists live at research stations, tourist ships visit the Peninsula every summer, fishing fleets work the Southern Ocean, and the whole continent is affected by a warming climate caused far away. Because the ecosystem depends on so few key species, especially krill, damage can spread quickly.

This lesson describes the threats to Antarctica, their impacts and how the continent is managed through international agreements. It asks how effective that management is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antarctic Treaty"}
An international agreement signed in 1959 (in force from 1961) by 12 countries, now with many more parties, which reserves Antarctica for peaceful purposes and scientific research and puts territorial claims on hold.
:::

:::definition{term="Protocol on Environmental Protection"}
An addition to the Antarctic Treaty, signed in Madrid in 1991 (in force from 1998), which designates Antarctica as a natural reserve, bans mineral resource activities other than scientific research, and requires environmental impact assessment.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental impact assessment (EIA)"}
A study carried out before an activity, such as building a research station, to predict its effects on the environment and decide whether and how it can go ahead.
:::

:::definition{term="Overfishing"}
Catching fish or other marine animals faster than their populations can replace themselves.
:::

## Threats

### Climate change

- The **Antarctic Peninsula** has warmed markedly in recent decades.
- **Ice shelves** on the Peninsula have broken up; for example, a large part of the Larsen B ice shelf collapsed in 2002.
- **Sea ice** changes affect krill, which feed on algae under the ice in winter. Less krill means less food for penguins, seals and whales.
- **Melting land ice** adds water to the oceans and raises global sea level.
- Some penguin species that depend on sea ice have declined in parts of the Peninsula, while others have spread.

### Fishing

- **Krill** are caught in large quantities for animal feed (including fish farms) and food supplements. Over-harvesting could take food from whales, seals and penguins.
- **Toothfish** are valuable fish, and illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing has threatened stocks. Longlines have also caught and drowned seabirds such as albatrosses.
- In the past, **whaling** and **sealing** reduced many species to very low numbers. Commercial whaling was stopped by an international moratorium in the 1980s, and some whale populations are recovering.

### Resource exploitation

Antarctica may contain minerals, oil and gas. The Environmental Protocol bans mining, but some people worry about pressure for resources in the future if the rules are reviewed.

### Tourism

- Tens of thousands of tourists visit each summer, mostly by ship to the Peninsula, and numbers have grown.
- Possible impacts: disturbance of breeding penguins and seals, trampling of mosses, introduction of non-native seeds and microbes on clothing and boots, fuel spills from ships, and waste.

### Pollution and research stations

Stations need fuel, buildings, roads and waste disposal. Fuel spills, sewage and old rubbish dumps have caused local pollution, and air pollution and plastics reach Antarctica from elsewhere.

## Impacts of damage

| Damage | Impact |
| --- | --- |
| Less sea ice | Less krill → fewer whales, seals, penguins |
| Over-harvesting of krill | Food web collapses from the bottom up |
| Ice-shelf collapse and land-ice melt | Faster flow of glaciers, rising global sea level |
| Disturbance by tourists | Lower breeding success at colonies |
| Invasive species | Native mosses and invertebrates outcompeted |
| Oil and fuel spills | Seabirds and seals harmed; slow breakdown in the cold |

Because it is so cold, damage in Antarctica recovers **very slowly**: a footprint in moss or a fuel spill can last for decades.

## Management

| Strategy | What it does | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Antarctic Treaty (1959) | Peaceful use, science, no new territorial claims, inspections | Cooperation between many countries; no military bases | Not all countries have signed; depends on agreement by consensus |
| Environmental Protocol (1991) | Natural reserve, mining ban, EIA for all activities, waste rules | Strong legal protection; mining banned | Enforcement relies on each country; protects against local threats, not climate change |
| Fishing conservation agreement (the Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources, CCAMLR) | Catch limits for krill and toothfish, protected marine areas, rules to protect seabirds | Takes the whole ecosystem into account; large marine protected areas such as in the Ross Sea | Illegal fishing is hard to police in a huge ocean; decisions need consensus |
| Tourism guidelines (tour operators' association) | Limits on numbers ashore, minimum distances from wildlife, cleaning boots and clothing | Self-regulation by the industry; most operators take part | Voluntary; numbers keep growing |
| Climate agreements | Reduce global greenhouse gas emissions | Address the root cause of warming | Depend on all countries, most of which are far from Antarctica |

:::example
**Applying the rules to a new research station (a worked example).** A country wants to build a new station on an ice-free coast near a penguin colony.

1. Under the Protocol it must carry out an **environmental impact assessment**. A large project needs a comprehensive assessment that other Treaty parties can comment on.
2. The assessment considers the effects of building, fuel storage, waste, noise and people on the penguins, mosses and coast.
3. Changes might include moving the station further from the colony, using wind and solar power to reduce fuel use, and removing all waste from Antarctica.
4. Other countries can inspect the station later to check that the rules are being followed.
:::

## Evaluation

Management of Antarctica is often seen as a success story of international cooperation: there has been no mining, no military conflict, whale numbers are recovering and large marine protected areas have been set up. But local rules cannot stop the biggest threat, **climate change**, which comes from emissions around the world. Illegal fishing and growing tourism also test agreements that rely on consensus and self-regulation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nobody manages Antarctica"}
No single country owns it, but it is managed by the Antarctic Treaty system and its agreements.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Treaty stops climate change"}
The Treaty and Protocol control activities in Antarctica. Climate change needs global action on emissions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing threats without impacts"}
Link each threat to an effect on the ecosystem: "krill fishing reduces the food supply of penguins and whales".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Threats: climate change (warming, ice-shelf collapse, sea-ice change), fishing (krill, toothfish), possible resource exploitation, tourism and pollution.
- Damage spreads through the food web because so much depends on krill, and recovery is slow in the cold.
- Management: the Antarctic Treaty (1959), the Environmental Protocol (1991) with its mining ban and EIA, fishing agreements with catch limits and marine protected areas, tourism guidelines and global climate agreements.
- Management has prevented mining and conflict, but it cannot stop climate change alone, and illegal fishing and tourism growth remain challenges.
:::
