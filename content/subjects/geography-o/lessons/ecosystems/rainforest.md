## Introduction

Fly north-west from Harare for a few hours and the brown, open woodland of the dry season gives way to an unbroken green carpet: the rainforest of the Congo Basin. It covers a much smaller share of the land than savanna, yet tropical rainforests hold more kinds of plants and animals than any other land biome. They also store huge amounts of carbon and help to drive the water cycle over whole continents.

This lesson explains where rainforests are, why their climate is hot and wet every month, how the forest is built in layers, and how soils, plants, animals and people fit together. The next lesson looks at threats to rainforests and how they can be managed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biome"}
A large-scale ecosystem covering a wide area, such as tropical rainforest, savanna or hot desert, defined mainly by its climate and natural vegetation.
:::

:::definition{term="Tropical rainforest"}
A dense, evergreen, broad-leaved forest found in hot, wet equatorial lowlands, with several layers of trees and very high biodiversity.
:::

:::definition{term="Convectional rainfall"}
Rain formed when the ground is strongly heated, warm moist air rises, cools and condenses into tall cumulonimbus clouds, giving heavy afternoon showers and thunderstorms.
:::

:::definition{term="Leaching"}
The washing of soluble minerals and nutrients downwards through the soil, out of reach of plant roots, by heavy rainfall.
:::

:::definition{term="Epiphyte"}
A plant that grows on another plant for support, not for food, such as orchids and bromeliads on rainforest branches.
:::

## Where are tropical rainforests?

Tropical rainforests lie in a belt either side of the equator, mostly within about 10° north and south of it, where the land is low and there is no long dry season.

| Region | Main areas |
| --- | --- |
| South and Central America | The Amazon Basin (the largest rainforest), parts of Central America |
| Africa | The Congo Basin and parts of the West African coast |
| Asia and Oceania | Indonesia, Malaysia (including Borneo), Papua New Guinea and other parts of South-East Asia |

There is no rainforest in Zimbabwe. Zimbabwe lies between about 15° and 22° south, in the savanna belt, with a long dry season.

## The equatorial climate

| Feature | Typical value (approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Mean monthly temperature | about 26-28 °C every month |
| Annual temperature range | small, often only 2-3 °C |
| Annual rainfall | usually more than 2,000 mm |
| Dry season | none or very short |
| Humidity | very high (often above 80%) |

**Why is it like this?**

1. **High sun angle.** The midday sun is high in the sky all year and directly overhead twice a year, so insolation is strong and temperatures stay high in every month.
2. **Low pressure and rising air.** Strong heating makes air rise, forming a belt of low pressure where the trade winds meet (the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone, ITCZ).
3. **Convectional rain.** Hot, moist air rises, cools, condenses and forms cumulonimbus clouds. A typical day starts clear, clouds build by midday, and heavy rain and thunder follow in the afternoon.
4. **Cloud cover at night** traps heat, so the daily range is also fairly small.

Because the climate barely changes through the year, there is a year-round growing season, and different trees flower, fruit and drop leaves at different times. The forest stays evergreen.

## The layered structure of the forest

Imagine a cross-section through the forest, from the top down:

| Layer | Height (approximate) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergents | up to 50 m or more | The tallest trees poke above the canopy; they face strong sun and wind; small leaves |
| Canopy | about 25-40 m | A dense, continuous roof of crowns; most photosynthesis, flowers, fruit and animal life |
| Under-canopy | about 10-20 m | Young trees and shade-tolerant species waiting for a gap |
| Shrub layer | up to about 5-10 m | Shrubs and saplings with large, dark green leaves to catch weak light |
| Forest floor | ground | Very dim (only a few per cent of the sunlight reaches it); leaf litter, fungi, seedlings |

When a large tree falls it leaves a gap; light floods in and saplings race to fill it. This is how the forest renews itself.

## Soils and the nutrient cycle

Rainforest soils are usually deep, red or yellow and **infertile**. Heavy rain leaches nutrients downwards, and the red colour comes from iron oxides left behind (geographers call these soils latosols or ferralitic soils).

Yet the forest is lush. The answer is a **fast nutrient cycle**:

1. Leaves fall all year round.
2. In the hot, wet conditions, decomposers (bacteria, fungi, termites) break down litter within weeks, so the litter layer is thin.
3. Shallow, spreading roots, often helped by fungi, take up the released nutrients almost at once.
4. Most nutrients are therefore stored in the **biomass** (the living plants), not in the soil.

If the forest is cleared, this cycle is broken: there is no new leaf fall, rain leaches the remaining nutrients, and the soil quickly becomes infertile and easily eroded.

## Adaptations of plants

- **Buttress roots**: wide, plank-like roots at the base of tall trees support them in shallow soil.
- **Drip tips**: pointed leaf tips shed heavy rain quickly, stopping fungi and algae growing on the leaves.
- **Thin, smooth bark**: no need for protection from cold or fire; the smooth surface stops other plants climbing up.
- **Lianas**: woody climbers rooted in the ground that climb trees to reach the light.
- **Epiphytes**: orchids and ferns perch on branches high in the canopy to reach light, collecting water and nutrients from rain and debris.
- **Large leaves** in the lower layers catch the little light available.

## Adaptations of animals

- Many animals live in the canopy, where food is plentiful. Monkeys such as spider monkeys have strong limbs and **prehensile** (gripping) tails.
- **Camouflage** helps animals such as chameleons and stick insects hide from predators.
- Many animals are **nocturnal**, such as many frogs and bats, which avoids the heat and some predators.
- Birds such as toucans and hornbills have large beaks for reaching and opening fruit.
- Sloths move very slowly and use little energy on a low-energy diet of leaves.

## Interrelationships: people and the forest

Indigenous peoples have lived in rainforests for thousands of years by hunting, gathering and **shifting cultivation**: a small plot is cleared and burned, crops are grown for a few years until the soil is exhausted, and then the people move on, letting the forest regrow. At low population densities this is sustainable because the forest has time to recover.

:::example
**Linking abiotic and biotic factors.** High temperature and rainfall (abiotic) allow plants to grow all year (biotic). Dense growth creates a closed canopy, which shades the forest floor and keeps it humid. The heat and humidity speed up decomposition, which feeds the plants. Heavy rain leaches the soil, which is why plants keep nutrients in their own biomass. Each part depends on the others.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying rainforest soils are fertile"}
The forest is lush because nutrients cycle quickly through the plants, not because the soil is rich. Once cleared, the soil soon becomes poor.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a wet and dry season"}
The equatorial climate has rain in every month. A clear wet season and dry season describes the savanna climate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up lianas and epiphytes"}
Lianas are rooted in the ground and climb; epiphytes grow on branches without roots in the ground.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tropical rainforests lie mostly within about 10° of the equator: the Amazon, Congo Basin and South-East Asia.
- The equatorial climate is hot (about 26-28 °C) and wet (over about 2,000 mm) all year, with a small temperature range and afternoon convectional rain.
- The forest has layers: emergents, canopy, under-canopy, shrub layer and forest floor.
- Soils are leached and infertile; nutrients cycle quickly and are stored mainly in the biomass.
- Plants (buttress roots, drip tips, lianas, epiphytes) and animals (canopy life, camouflage, nocturnal habits) are adapted to the conditions.
:::
