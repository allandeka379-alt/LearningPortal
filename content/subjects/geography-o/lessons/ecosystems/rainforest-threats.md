## Introduction

Every year, large areas of tropical rainforest are cut down or burned. Some of the timber ends up as furniture, and some of the land becomes cattle pasture, soya fields or oil-palm plantations that supply markets around the world, including the cooking oil and animal feed sold in Southern Africa. What happens in the Amazon or Borneo therefore affects the global climate, world biodiversity and the people who live in the forest.

This lesson explains why rainforests are being destroyed, what the impacts are, and how far different management strategies can protect them. It ends with a detailed specific example of the Brazilian Amazon.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The permanent removal of forest, so that the land is used for something else such as farming, mining or settlement.
:::

:::definition{term="Selective logging"}
Cutting only some mature trees of chosen species and sizes, leaving the rest of the forest standing so that it can regenerate.
:::

:::definition{term="Agroforestry"}
Growing crops among or beneath trees, so that the tree cover protects the soil while people still farm the land.
:::

:::definition{term="Debt-for-nature swap"}
An agreement in which part of a country's foreign debt is cancelled or reduced in return for the country spending money on conservation.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecotourism"}
Small-scale, low-impact tourism to natural areas that conserves the environment and benefits local people.
:::

## Causes of deforestation

| Cause | How it destroys forest |
| --- | --- |
| Commercial logging | Valuable hardwoods (such as mahogany) are felled; heavy machinery and logging roads damage many other trees |
| Cattle ranching | Large areas are cleared and burned to plant grass for beef cattle |
| Commercial farming | Soya, oil palm and other cash crops are planted on cleared land |
| Subsistence farming | Growing numbers of farmers clear plots, often following new roads |
| Mining | Open-pit mines, waste tips and processing plants clear forest; gold mining pollutes rivers with mercury |
| Roads | Roads open up remote forest to loggers, ranchers and settlers |
| HEP schemes | Dams flood large areas of forest behind them |
| Settlement | Governments have moved people from crowded areas into the forest; towns grow along roads |

Behind these direct causes are **underlying causes**: population growth, poverty, the need to earn foreign currency and pay off debt, and global demand for beef, soya, palm oil, timber and minerals.

## Impacts of deforestation

### Local impacts

- **Soil erosion and infertility.** Without the canopy and roots, heavy rain washes away the thin topsoil and leaches the nutrients. Cleared land may become useless within a few years.
- **Silting and flooding.** Eroded soil fills rivers and reservoirs; faster runoff increases flooding downstream.
- **Loss of biodiversity.** Habitats are destroyed and species, many not yet studied, may become extinct.
- **Indigenous peoples** lose land, food sources and their way of life; conflict may break out with loggers, ranchers and miners.
- **Drier local climate.** Less transpiration means less water vapour, fewer clouds and less rain.
- **Economic gains**: jobs, exports, roads and energy, although profits often go to large companies.

### Global impacts

- **Climate change.** Trees store carbon; burning and decay release carbon dioxide, and fewer trees remain to absorb it.
- **Loss of resources** for the world: future medicines, foods and genetic material.
- **Changes to rainfall** far from the forest, because rainforests return huge amounts of moisture to the air.

## Management strategies

| Strategy | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected areas (national parks, indigenous reserves) | Legal protection of large areas; protects indigenous land rights | Hard to patrol huge, remote areas; illegal logging and mining continue |
| Selective logging and replanting | Keeps the forest standing and earns income | Machinery still damages other trees; rules are often broken |
| Certification of timber | Consumers can choose timber from sustainably managed forests | Covers only part of the trade; checking is costly |
| Agroforestry | Protects soil while giving food and income | Lower yields than intensive farming |
| Ecotourism | Gives local people income from keeping the forest | Only a few areas attract tourists; too many visitors cause damage |
| Debt-for-nature swaps | Fund conservation and reduce debt | Small compared with the scale of clearance |
| International agreements and funding | Pay countries to reduce deforestation and emissions | Depend on political will and reliable monitoring |
| Satellite monitoring | Detects new clearing quickly so that it can be stopped | Useless without enforcement on the ground |

Sustainable strategies are those that meet people's needs today without destroying the forest for future generations, for example agroforestry, ecotourism and selective logging that is properly enforced.

## Detailed specific example: the Brazilian Amazon

:::example
**Location.** The Amazon rainforest covers much of northern Brazil, mainly in states such as Amazonas, Pará, Mato Grosso and Rondônia, and extends into neighbouring countries.

**Threats.**
- **Cattle ranching** is the largest single cause of clearance: forest is cut and burned and sown with grass.
- **Soya farming** has expanded on cleared land, especially in Mato Grosso, mainly for animal feed exports.
- **Roads** such as the Trans-Amazonian Highway (begun in the 1970s) opened the forest to settlers, loggers and ranchers; clearing spreads out along roads in a "fishbone" pattern seen from space.
- **Mining**: the Carajás area in Pará has one of the world's largest iron-ore mines; small-scale gold miners clear riverbanks and use mercury.
- **HEP**: large dams such as Belo Monte on the Xingu River flooded forest and affected indigenous communities.
- **Logging**, legal and illegal, often comes first and opens access for farming.

**Impacts.** Loss of biodiversity; release of carbon dioxide from burning; soil erosion; mercury pollution of rivers and fish; conflict over land with indigenous peoples; but also export earnings, jobs and energy for Brazil's cities and industry.

**Management.**
- Large areas are protected as national parks and **indigenous territories**.
- Brazil's **Forest Code** requires landowners in the Amazon to keep a large share of their land under forest (a "legal reserve").
- **Satellite monitoring** of forest loss guides enforcement teams to new clearings.
- The **Soy Moratorium** (an agreement by traders from 2006) not to buy soya grown on newly deforested Amazon land.
- The **Amazon Fund** receives international donations to pay for projects that reduce deforestation.

**Evaluation.** The annual rate of deforestation fell sharply in the decade after the mid-2000s, when monitoring, enforcement and the moratorium worked together. It has since risen and fallen again as government policy and enforcement changed. Management works best when laws are enforced, local people benefit from keeping the forest, and global buyers refuse products from newly cleared land.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining them"}
"Mining" alone earns little. Say how it destroys forest: "open-pit mines and waste tips clear the forest and mercury pollutes rivers".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying rainforests produce the world's oxygen"}
Rainforests are vital carbon stores and affect rainfall, but this popular claim is exaggerated. Focus on carbon storage and biodiversity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaluating with only advantages"}
"Evaluate" needs strengths **and** limitations of a strategy, and a judgement about how effective it is.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 extended parts often ask for a **detailed specific example** of threats, impacts and management in a named rainforest. Use place names (states, rivers, projects) and specific details, not general points.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "evaluate strategies", give each strategy a strength, a limitation and a final judgement: "Satellite monitoring is very effective at finding clearings, but only if inspectors can act on the information."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Causes of deforestation: logging, cattle ranching, commercial and subsistence farming, mining, roads, HEP and settlement, driven by population growth, poverty, debt and global demand.
- Local impacts: erosion, infertile soil, flooding, loss of species and of indigenous ways of life; global impacts: carbon release and loss of resources.
- Management: protected areas, selective logging, certification, agroforestry, ecotourism, debt-for-nature swaps, international funding and satellite monitoring; each has strengths and limitations.
- In the Brazilian Amazon, ranching, soya, roads, mining and HEP drive clearance; protected areas, the Forest Code, monitoring and the Soy Moratorium have reduced it when enforced.
:::
