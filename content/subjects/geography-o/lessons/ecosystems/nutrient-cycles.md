## Introduction

A farmer near Murehwa grows maize on the same field year after year. Each harvest carries nutrients away in the cobs and stalks, and the yields slowly fall unless she adds manure or fertiliser, or rotates the maize with groundnuts. In a natural woodland nobody adds fertiliser, yet the trees keep growing. The difference is that in the woodland the nutrients are **recycled**: leaves fall, rot and feed the soil, and the trees take the nutrients up again.

Energy flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat, but **matter is recycled**. This lesson covers three of these recycling systems, called **biogeochemical cycles**: the nutrient cycle, the nitrogen cycle and the carbon cycle.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nutrients"}
Chemical elements that plants and animals need to grow, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium and magnesium.
:::

:::definition{term="Biogeochemical cycle"}
The continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio), rocks and soil (geo) and the air and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Store"}
A part of a cycle where an element is held, for example nutrients in leaves (biomass), in dead leaves on the ground (litter) or in the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Transfer (flow)"}
The movement of an element from one store to another, for example leaf fall from biomass to litter.
:::

:::definition{term="Leaching"}
The washing of dissolved nutrients downwards through the soil, out of reach of plant roots, by heavy rainfall.
:::

## The nutrient cycle: three stores

Geographers often show the nutrient cycle as three circles (stores) joined by arrows (transfers). Imagine three circles in a triangle: **biomass** at the top, **litter** on the left and **soil** on the right.

1. **Biomass to litter (fallout):** leaves, twigs, fruits and dead animals drop to the ground.
2. **Litter to soil (decomposition):** bacteria, fungi, termites and earthworms break the litter down and release nutrients into the soil.
3. **Soil to biomass (uptake):** plant roots absorb dissolved nutrients.

There are also **inputs** and **outputs**:

- Inputs: nutrients released by the **weathering** of rock, and nutrients dissolved in **rainfall** or carried in dust.
- Outputs: nutrients lost by **leaching**, by **surface runoff** and soil erosion, and by people **harvesting** crops, timber and firewood.

The size of the circles and arrows changes from one ecosystem to another.

![Three-store nutrient cycle (biomass, litter, soil) for three ecosystems: in the tropical rainforest the biomass store is largest, in the savanna the soil store is largest, and in the hot desert all three stores are small.](media:diagrams/nutrient-cycle-three-stores.png "Figure: the nutrient cycle in a rainforest, a savanna and a hot desert (circle size shows store size)")

| Ecosystem | Largest store | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tropical rainforest | Biomass | Hot, wet climate: litter rots in weeks and nutrients are taken up quickly by the dense vegetation; heavy rain leaches the soil |
| Savanna and grassland | Soil | Grasses die back each dry season and decay into the soil; less leaching than in the rainforest |
| Hot desert | All stores small | Little rain, little plant growth, slow decay |

## The nitrogen cycle

Nitrogen makes up about 78% of the air, yet plants cannot use nitrogen gas directly. They need it as **nitrates** in the soil.

1. **Fixation:** nitrogen gas is changed into compounds plants can use. This happens through **nitrogen-fixing bacteria** in the root nodules of legumes (groundnuts, beans, soya, acacia trees), through **lightning**, and in factories that make **fertiliser**.
2. **Uptake:** plants absorb nitrates through their roots and build them into proteins.
3. **Feeding:** animals obtain nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.
4. **Decomposition (ammonification):** when organisms die or excrete waste, decomposers release the nitrogen as ammonium compounds.
5. **Nitrification:** nitrifying bacteria change ammonium into nitrites and then into nitrates.
6. **Denitrification:** in waterlogged soils (such as a vlei), denitrifying bacteria change nitrates back into nitrogen gas, which returns to the air.

![Nitrogen cycle in six steps: fixation turns nitrogen gas into nitrates (legume bacteria, lightning, fertiliser), plants take up nitrates, animals eat plants, decomposers release ammonium from dead matter, nitrification makes nitrates again, and denitrification in waterlogged soil returns nitrogen gas to the air.](media:diagrams/nitrogen-cycle.png "Figure: the nitrogen cycle (steps 1 to 6 as in the lesson)")

:::example
**Why farmers rotate maize with groundnuts.** Groundnuts carry nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their roots. After the harvest, their roots and leaves decay and add nitrogen to the soil, so the next maize crop needs less fertiliser. This is a practical use of the nitrogen cycle on a communal-area plot.
:::

## The carbon cycle

Carbon is held in the air as carbon dioxide (about 0.04% of the air), in living things, in soils, in the oceans and in rocks and fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas).

- **Photosynthesis** removes carbon dioxide from the air and stores the carbon in plants.
- **Respiration** by plants, animals and decomposers returns carbon dioxide to the air.
- **Decomposition** of dead matter releases carbon dioxide; some carbon is stored in soil as humus.
- **Combustion**: burning wood, charcoal, crop waste, veld fires and fossil fuels such as coal releases carbon dioxide.
- **Fossilisation**: over millions of years some dead matter is buried and becomes coal, oil or gas.
- **Oceans** dissolve carbon dioxide from the air and release some back.

Forests are **carbon sinks** when they take in more carbon than they give out.

## How people disrupt the cycles

:::example
**Human impacts (a checklist for exam answers).**

1. **Deforestation** removes the biomass store; in a rainforest most nutrients are lost with it, and the exposed soil is leached and eroded.
2. **Burning** forests, veld and fossil fuels releases stored carbon quickly, adding carbon dioxide to the air and contributing to global warming.
3. **Harvesting** crops and wood removes nutrients from the cycle each year.
4. **Too much fertiliser** washes into rivers and dams, causing **eutrophication**: algae and water weeds grow rapidly, then decay and use up the oxygen in the water, and fish die.
5. **Draining wetlands** changes where denitrification takes place.
:::

## Why the cycles matter

- They keep soils fertile without outside inputs.
- They supply the nutrients for all plant growth and so for every food chain, including our crops.
- The carbon cycle helps control the amount of carbon dioxide in the air and so the climate.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying energy is recycled"}
Energy flows through an ecosystem and is lost as heat. Matter (carbon, nitrogen, nutrients) is recycled.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking rainforest soils are rich"}
Rainforests look lush, but most of their nutrients are in the plants, not the soil. Once the trees are cleared, the soil quickly becomes infertile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nitrification and denitrification"}
Nitrification makes nitrates (useful to plants). Denitrification destroys nitrates and returns nitrogen gas to the air, mainly in waterlogged soils.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 2, **explain** questions on cycles reward named stores, named transfers and a cause. Write "leaf fall transfers nutrients from biomass to litter", not "leaves go to the ground".
:::

:::exam-tip
If asked to **compare** nutrient cycles, say which store is largest in each ecosystem and give the climatic reason (rate of decay, leaching).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Matter is recycled in biogeochemical cycles; energy is not.
- The nutrient cycle has three stores (biomass, litter, soil) linked by fallout, decomposition and uptake, with inputs from weathering and rain and outputs by leaching, runoff and harvesting.
- The nitrogen cycle depends on bacteria: fixation, nitrification and denitrification; legumes add nitrogen to the soil.
- The carbon cycle moves carbon by photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion and fossilisation.
- Deforestation, burning, harvesting and excess fertiliser disrupt the cycles.
:::
