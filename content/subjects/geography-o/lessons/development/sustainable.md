## Introduction

Development that wrecks soils, empties rivers or leaves huge debts for the next generation is not real progress. **Sustainable development** asks for growth that improves lives now *and* keeps the resources, environment and finances that future generations will need. This lesson looks at what that means, how countries try to achieve it, and whether trade, aid and debt relief help poorer countries close the development gap.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sustainable development"}
Development that meets the needs of people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. The idea was made widely known by the United Nations' Brundtland Report (1987).
:::

:::definition{term="Fair trade"}
A trading system in which producers in poorer countries are paid a guaranteed minimum price plus an extra premium for community projects, in return for meeting labour and environmental standards.
:::

:::definition{term="International aid"}
Help in the form of money, goods, services or expertise given by one country or organisation to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Debt relief"}
Cancelling or reducing the debts owed by a poor country, or lowering the interest on them.
:::

## The three parts of sustainability

| Pillar | Aim | Example strategies |
|---|---|---|
| **Social** | Fair access to education, health, housing and rights | Free primary schooling, community health workers, gender equality, clean water and sanitation |
| **Economic** | Lasting jobs and income, not short booms | Diversifying the economy, adding value to raw materials, small-business loans (microfinance), skills training |
| **Environmental** | Protect resources and ecosystems | Conservation farming, renewable energy (solar, HEP), reforestation, protected areas, waste recycling |

The United Nations' **Sustainable Development Goals** (17 goals adopted in 2015, with targets for 2030) set shared aims such as ending poverty and hunger, quality education, clean water and climate action.

**Appropriate (intermediate) technology** is an important strategy in LICs: simple, affordable technology that local people can build, use and repair, such as hand pumps on boreholes, solar lanterns, improved cookstoves and drip irrigation kits.

## Trade

Trade can earn more than aid, but its benefits depend on **what** is traded and **on what terms**.

| | Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| Exporting raw materials | Earns foreign currency; creates jobs | Low and unstable prices; poor terms of trade |
| **Adding value** (processing, manufacturing) | Higher prices; more skilled jobs | Needs investment, energy and access to markets |
| **Trade blocs** (e.g. SADC, the African Continental Free Trade Area) | Bigger markets, fewer tariffs | Competition can hurt weaker local industries |
| **Fair trade** | Guaranteed minimum price and premium; better working conditions | Small share of world trade; certification costs; products cost more |

## International aid

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term (emergency)** | Food, shelter, medicine after a disaster | Food aid during droughts; relief after Cyclone Idai (2019) |
| **Long-term (development)** | Projects to improve lives permanently | Wells, schools, clinics, farmer training |
| **Bilateral** | From one government to another | — |
| **Multilateral** | Through international organisations (World Bank, UN agencies) | Global health funds for HIV, malaria and TB |
| **Voluntary (NGO)** | From charities, funded by donations | Community water and health projects |
| **Tied aid** | Given on condition the recipient buys goods or services from the donor | — |

| Benefits of aid | Problems of aid |
|---|---|
| Saves lives in emergencies | Can create **dependency** |
| Funds health, education, water | **Tied aid** benefits the donor's firms |
| Transfers skills and technology | Large **top-down** projects may not meet local needs |
| | **Corruption** may divert funds |
| | Food aid can undercut local farmers' prices |

Small, **bottom-up** projects run with local communities and using appropriate technology are often the most sustainable form of aid.

## Debt relief

Many LICs borrowed heavily in the 1970s and 1980s; repayments then took money away from schools and clinics. Under the **Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative** (from 1996) and later schemes, many countries, most of them in Africa, had debts cancelled in return for economic reforms and plans to reduce poverty.

| Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|
| Frees money for health, education and infrastructure | Conditions (reforms, spending cuts) can be unpopular and hurt the poor |
| Breaks the cycle of borrowing to repay old debts | May encourage new borrowing |
| | Money saved may be lost to corruption without good governance |

:::example{title="Worked example: debt repayments versus services (fictional data)"}
A fictional LIC's government earns US\$2.4 billion a year and spends US\$540 million on debt repayments, US\$300 million on health and US\$420 million on education.

1. **Share of revenue on debt** = 540 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = **22.5%**.
2. **Health and education together** = 300 + 420 = 720 million = **30%** of revenue.
3. If half the debt were cancelled, US\$270 million a year could be redirected, nearly doubling the health budget (to US\$570 million).
:::

## Case study: development strategies in Zimbabwe (a lower-middle-income country)

1. **Development level:** the World Bank classes Zimbabwe as lower-middle-income. It has good literacy by regional standards, but large numbers work in the informal sector and in small-scale farming, and incomes have been hit by economic crises and droughts.
2. **Government plans:** **Vision 2030** aims for Zimbabwe to become an upper-middle-income society by 2030; it is carried out through national development strategies such as **NDS1 (2021-2025)**.
3. **Economic strategies:** mining (gold, platinum, lithium, diamonds, chrome, coal) with a push to process minerals locally instead of exporting raw ore; agriculture (tobacco, maize, horticulture); tourism (Victoria Falls, Hwange, Great Zimbabwe).
4. **Environmental and farming strategies:** **Pfumvudza/Intwasa** conservation agriculture (small plots, minimum tillage, mulching, planting holes) to raise yields and resist drought; solar energy projects alongside Kariba HEP; protected areas and CAMPFIRE in wildlife areas.
5. **Social strategies:** primary health care, HIV treatment programmes (often supported by multilateral aid), and investment in education.
6. **Trade, aid and debt:** member of SADC and of the African Continental Free Trade Area; receives humanitarian and health aid, especially during droughts and after Cyclone Idai; carries large external debt arrears, which limit access to new international loans.
7. **Evaluation:** the country has strong natural resources and a relatively educated workforce, but drought, debt, power shortages and dependence on primary exports remain obstacles. Value-added processing and climate-smart farming are the most sustainable parts of its strategy.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all aid as good (or bad)"}
Evaluate by type: emergency aid saves lives; long-term bottom-up aid builds capacity; tied aid and large top-down projects are more likely to cause problems.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing fair trade as the whole answer"}
Fair trade helps the producers involved, but it is a small share of world trade. Adding value and wider trade access affect far more people.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sustainable development meets present needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs; it has social, economic and environmental pillars.
- Strategies include education and health care, diversification and adding value, renewable energy, conservation farming and appropriate technology.
- Trade earns most when value is added; fair trade guarantees prices but is small in scale.
- Aid saves lives and funds services but can create dependency; bottom-up projects are usually more sustainable than tied or top-down aid.
- Debt relief frees money for services but comes with conditions; Zimbabwe (lower-middle income) pursues Vision 2030 through mining, farming, tourism and conservation agriculture while facing drought and debt.
:::
