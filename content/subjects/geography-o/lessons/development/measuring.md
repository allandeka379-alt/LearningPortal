## Introduction

Is Zimbabwe more developed than Malawi? Less developed than South Africa? How would you prove it? "Development" means improving people's lives: more income, better health and education, more choices and more freedom. Because no single number can capture all of that, geographers use a set of **indicators**, each measuring one part of development, and combine them to compare countries and track change over time.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Development"}
Progress in economic growth, health, education, technology and people's well-being and freedom.
:::

:::definition{term="Development indicator"}
A measurable value used to compare how developed places are, such as GNI per capita or life expectancy.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross domestic product (GDP)"}
The total value of goods and services produced within a country in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross national income (GNI) per capita"}
The total income earned by a country's people and businesses in a year, including income from abroad, divided by the population. Often adjusted for **purchasing power parity (PPP)**, which allows for differences in the cost of living.
:::

:::definition{term="Human Development Index (HDI)"}
A composite index from 0 to 1 published by the United Nations Development Programme, combining life expectancy, education (years of schooling) and GNI per capita.
:::

## Economic indicators

| Indicator | What it shows | In general, HICs vs LICs |
|---|---|---|
| GDP per capita | Output per person | Much higher in HICs |
| GNI per capita (PPP) | Income per person, adjusted for cost of living | Much higher in HICs |
| Percentage working in primary industry | Dependence on farming, fishing, mining | High in LICs; low in HICs |
| Energy use per person | Level of industry and technology | High in HICs |

The **World Bank** groups countries by GNI per capita into **low-income (LIC)**, **lower-middle-income**, **upper-middle-income** and **high-income (HIC)** countries; the two middle groups together are **MICs**. The thresholds are updated every year: roughly, a country is low-income below about US\$1,150 per person and high-income above about US\$14,000 (approximate values). Zimbabwe is classed as a lower-middle-income country.

## Social indicators

| Indicator | Definition | Why it reflects development |
|---|---|---|
| Life expectancy | Average number of years a newborn can expect to live | Shows health care, diet, safe water |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of babies under 1 year per 1,000 live births | Very sensitive to health care and sanitation |
| Adult literacy rate | Percentage of adults who can read and write | Shows access to education |
| Calorie intake | Average food energy per person per day | Shows food security |
| People per doctor | Population ÷ number of doctors | Shows access to health care |
| Access to safe water | Percentage with safe drinking water | Shows infrastructure and health |

## The Human Development Index

The HDI combines three dimensions:

1. **A long and healthy life:** life expectancy at birth.
2. **Knowledge:** mean years of schooling of adults and expected years of schooling for children.
3. **A decent standard of living:** GNI per capita (PPP).

Values run from 0 to 1. Countries such as Norway and Switzerland score above 0.95; the lowest-ranked countries, several of them in the Sahel, score about 0.4 or less (approximate values). The HDI is more useful than income alone because two countries with similar incomes can have very different health and education.

:::example{title="Worked example: calculating and comparing indicators (fictional data)"}
| | Country P | Country Q |
|---|---|---|
| GNI (US\$ million) | 18,600 | 412,000 |
| Population (million) | 15.5 | 8.0 |
| Doctors | 3,100 | 32,000 |
| Infant mortality (per 1,000) | 38 | 3 |

1. **GNI per capita, P** = 18,600 million ÷ 15.5 million = **US\$1,200**.
2. **GNI per capita, Q** = 412,000 ÷ 8.0 = **US\$51,500**, about 43 times P.
3. **People per doctor, P** = 15,500,000 ÷ 3,100 = **5,000**; Q = 8,000,000 ÷ 32,000 = **250**.
4. **Conclusion:** every indicator shows Q is far more developed; P would be a LIC or lower-MIC and Q a HIC.
:::

## How useful are the indicators?

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Numbers allow countries to be compared and ranked | **Averages hide inequality** between rich and poor, regions, men and women, rural and urban |
| Change can be measured over time | **Informal economy** and subsistence farming are often not counted in GDP/GNI |
| Social indicators show how income is used | Data in some LICs are out of date or unreliable (no recent census) |
| Composite indices (HDI) combine several aspects | Exchange rates distort income unless PPP is used |
| | Indicators miss freedom, happiness, security and the environment |

**Use several indicators together**, both economic and social, for a fair picture. Indicators are **correlated**: richer countries usually have lower infant mortality and higher literacy, but there are exceptions. For example, some oil-rich states have high incomes but less widespread education, while some poorer countries with strong public health systems have long life expectancy.

## Standard of living and quality of life

:::definition{term="Standard of living"}
The material wealth of a person or group: income, possessions, housing and access to goods and services. It can be measured with numbers.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality of life"}
How good people's lives are overall, including health, safety, freedom, the environment, family and community. It is partly subjective and harder to measure.
:::

**Factors affecting quality of life and standard of living:** income and employment; health care and clean water; education; housing; food security; safety and political stability; human rights and freedom; environmental quality (air, noise); access to transport, electricity and the internet; family and community ties. A rural family in Zimbabwe may have a low income but strong community support and clean air, while a richer city household may face traffic, pollution and crime.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using a single indicator"}
GNI per capita alone can mislead: a few very rich people raise the average. Always combine economic and social indicators and mention inequality.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading infant mortality the wrong way round"}
A **high** infant mortality rate means **low** development. The same applies to people per doctor: more people per doctor means worse access.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 8)"}
"Describe" questions on a table of indicators need data quoted with units. "Evaluate the usefulness" needs strengths and limitations and a judgement, such as "HDI is more useful than GNI per capita because…". Practise calculating per-capita values and differences.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Development is measured with economic indicators (GDP, GNI per capita PPP, employment structure) and social indicators (life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, calorie intake, people per doctor, safe water).
- The HDI combines life expectancy, years of schooling and GNI per capita on a scale of 0 to 1.
- The World Bank classifies countries as LICs, MICs (lower and upper) and HICs by GNI per capita; Zimbabwe is lower-middle-income.
- Indicators are averages that hide inequality, miss the informal economy and can be unreliable; use several together.
- Standard of living is material wealth; quality of life is broader well-being, including health, safety, freedom and environment.
:::
