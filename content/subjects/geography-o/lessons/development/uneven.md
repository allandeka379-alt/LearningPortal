## Introduction

A child born in Japan can expect to live about 84 years; a child born in some countries of West and Central Africa can expect to live roughly 25-30 years less (approximate values). Average incomes in the richest countries are dozens of times those in the poorest. This difference in wealth and well-being between the richest and poorest countries is the **development gap**. Understanding why it exists, and why it has narrowed for some countries but not for others, is the first step towards closing it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Uneven development"}
The way development has happened faster and further in some countries and regions than in others.
:::

:::definition{term="Development gap"}
The difference in wealth and quality of life between the richest and poorest countries (or groups of people).
:::

:::definition{term="Terms of trade"}
The value of a country's exports compared with the value of its imports. Countries exporting cheap raw materials and importing expensive manufactured goods have poor terms of trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Landlocked country"}
A country with no coastline, which must send exports through a neighbour's ports (Zimbabwe, Zambia and Malawi are examples).
:::

## The global pattern

| Group | Typical regions (generalised) |
|---|---|
| **High-income countries (HICs)** | North America, most of Europe, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, some Gulf oil states |
| **Upper-middle-income** | Much of Latin America, China, parts of South-East Asia, South Africa, Botswana |
| **Lower-middle-income** | Much of South Asia, parts of Africa including Zimbabwe, Kenya and Nigeria (classification can change year to year) |
| **Low-income countries (LICs)** | Mostly in sub-Saharan Africa, with a few in Asia, e.g. Afghanistan |

Older descriptions divided the world by the **Brandt Line** (1980) into a rich "North" and a poor "South". It is now out of date: countries such as China, Brazil, Malaysia and Botswana have grown rapidly, and Australia and New Zealand were always "North" despite lying south. Today the world is better seen as a **continuum** from LICs through MICs to HICs.

:::example{title="Diagram in words: a world choropleth map of GNI per capita"}
Imagine a world map shaded from dark (high GNI per capita) to light (low). The darkest shades cover North America, Western Europe, Japan and Australia. Middle shades cover most of Latin America, China, Russia and the Middle East. The lightest shades are concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, especially the Sahel, Central Africa and the Horn of Africa, with a few in Asia.
:::

## Reasons for uneven development

| Type | Reasons | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Historical** | Colonialism took land, minerals and labour and built railways to export raw materials rather than to link neighbours; borders drawn by colonial powers; a late start to industrialisation | Many African economies still depend on exporting minerals and crops |
| **Economic** | Dependence on a few primary exports whose prices rise and fall; poor terms of trade; little manufacturing; debt repayments; limited investment | A fall in world mineral prices reduces export earnings sharply |
| **Social** | Low literacy and skills; poor health (HIV, malaria) reduces productivity; rapid population growth spreads resources thinly | Children kept out of school to work or care for the sick |
| **Environmental** | Droughts, floods and cyclones destroy crops and infrastructure; tropical diseases; poor soils; being **landlocked** raises transport costs | Cyclone Idai (2019) damaged roads, homes and farms in eastern Zimbabwe |
| **Political** | War and conflict destroy infrastructure and drive people away; corruption and weak institutions discourage investment; unstable policies | Conflict-affected countries are among the poorest |

Most countries are affected by **several** of these at once, and the factors reinforce each other in a **cycle of poverty**: low incomes → little saving and tax → poor schools, clinics and roads → low productivity → low incomes.

:::example{title="Worked example: terms of trade (fictional prices)"}
A fictional country exports coffee and imports tractors.

| Year | Price of 1 tonne of coffee (US\$) | Price of 1 tractor (US\$) |
|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 3,000 | 30,000 |
| Year 10 | 2,400 | 36,000 |

1. **Tonnes of coffee needed to buy one tractor, Year 1** = 30,000 ÷ 3,000 = **10 tonnes**.
2. **Year 10** = 36,000 ÷ 2,400 = **15 tonnes**.
3. The country must export **50% more** coffee to buy the same tractor: its terms of trade have worsened, widening the development gap.
:::

## Why some countries have narrowed the gap

Countries such as South Korea, Singapore and, more recently, China moved from low to middle or high income through investment in education, manufacturing for export, infrastructure and stable government. Botswana used diamond revenue to build roads, schools and clinics. These examples show that the gap is **not fixed**, but also that success depends on a combination of factors.

## The gap within countries

Uneven development also exists **inside** countries: between cities and rural areas, between regions, and between rich and poor households. In Zimbabwe, for example, Harare and Bulawayo have more jobs, services and electricity than remote communal areas, which is one reason for rural-urban migration.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving one cause only"}
Uneven development has many interacting causes. Answers that blame only climate, only colonialism or only corruption are too simple and score low.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'North' and 'South' literally"}
The Brandt Line is a historical idea. Use LIC, MIC and HIC, and remember that development changes over time.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 8)"}
"Explain the reasons for differences in development" needs a range of reasons (historical, economic, social, environmental, political), each explained with "this means that…". To "describe the global pattern" from a map, name continents and regions and use data if given.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The development gap is the difference in wealth and well-being between the richest and poorest countries.
- HICs are concentrated in North America, Europe, East Asia and Australasia; LICs mostly in sub-Saharan Africa; MICs in between. The Brandt Line is out of date.
- Reasons: historical (colonialism), economic (primary exports, poor terms of trade, debt), social (education, health), environmental (hazards, disease, being landlocked) and political (conflict, corruption).
- Factors interact in a cycle of poverty, but some countries have narrowed the gap through education, industry and stable government.
- Uneven development also exists within countries, between urban and rural areas.
:::
