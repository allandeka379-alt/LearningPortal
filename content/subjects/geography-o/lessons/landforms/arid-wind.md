## Introduction

Drive south from Masvingo towards Beitbridge in October and the land becomes lower, hotter and drier: thorny bush, baobabs, bare red soil and dry, sandy riverbeds. This is **semi-arid** Zimbabwe. Further west, across Botswana and Namibia, lie the semi-arid Kalahari and the true desert of the Namib, where wind shapes the land into dunes.

About a third of the world's land is arid or semi-arid. In these regions there is little vegetation to protect the surface, so **wind** becomes an important agent of erosion, transport and deposition. This lesson explains why dry regions exist, how wind works and which landforms it produces.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Arid region (desert)"}
An area with very low rainfall, often taken as less than about 250 mm a year, where evaporation far exceeds precipitation and vegetation is very sparse.
:::

:::definition{term="Semi-arid region"}
An area with low and unreliable rainfall, often about 250-500 mm a year, with a long dry season and scattered drought-resistant vegetation, such as thorn bush and short grass.
:::

:::definition{term="Deflation"}
The removal of loose, fine material (dust and sand) from the ground by the wind.
:::

:::definition{term="Abrasion (wind)"}
The wearing away of rock surfaces by sand grains carried by the wind, like a sandblaster. It is strongest close to the ground, within about 1-2 m.
:::

Rainfall limits for "arid" and "semi-arid" differ between textbooks; describe the conditions rather than relying on one figure.

## Characteristics of arid and semi-arid regions

- **Rainfall**: low and highly unreliable, often falling in short, heavy storms.
- **Evaporation**: very high, because skies are clear and temperatures high.
- **Temperature**: hot days, and a **large daily temperature range** because clear skies let heat escape at night.
- **Vegetation**: sparse; plants (xerophytes) have deep or wide roots, small or waxy leaves, thorns, or store water (baobab, cacti).
- **Soils**: thin, sandy or stony, low in organic matter; salts may collect at the surface.
- **Drainage**: rivers flow only after rain (ephemeral); wide sandy riverbeds.

## Distribution and causes

Most hot deserts lie between about 15° and 30° north and south of the equator, often on the **western sides of continents**: the Sahara, Namib and Kalahari in Africa, the Arabian Desert, the Australian deserts and the Atacama in South America.

| Cause | How it makes an area dry | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Subtropical high pressure | Air that rose at the equator sinks at about 30°; sinking air warms and is compressed, so clouds cannot form | Sahara, Kalahari |
| Cold ocean currents | Cold water chills the air above it, so it is stable and cannot rise; it gives fog but little rain | Namib (Benguela current), Atacama |
| Rain shadow | Air loses its moisture on the windward side of mountains and descends, warming and drying, on the leeward side | Leeward sides of mountain ranges |
| Continentality | Places far from the sea receive air that has already lost most of its moisture | Interior of Asia (Gobi) |

### Arid and semi-arid areas in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has no true desert, but large parts are **semi-arid**: natural region V and the drier parts of region IV, including the south-east lowveld (Save-Limpopo), the Zambezi valley and much of south-western Matabeleland. They are dry because they are low-lying and hot (high evaporation), lie far from moisture sources, and receive rain only in the hot wet season; parts of the Save valley also lie in the rain shadow of the Eastern Highlands. In the west, around Hwange, the land is covered by deep **Kalahari sands**, laid down by wind when the region was drier in the past.

## Wind erosion and transport

Wind can only move loose material, so it works best where the ground is dry and bare.

**Erosion processes**

1. **Deflation** lifts and blows away dust and sand, lowering the surface and leaving **deflation hollows** and a stony surface (**desert pavement** or reg) of pebbles too heavy to move.
2. **Abrasion** blasts rock with sand grains, smoothing, grooving and undercutting it near the ground.
3. **Attrition**: grains colliding with each other become smaller and rounder.

**Transport** (by grain size):

- **Suspension**: fine dust is carried high in the air, sometimes for thousands of kilometres.
- **Saltation**: sand grains bounce along in short hops, rarely more than about 1-2 m high.
- **Surface creep**: larger grains are rolled and pushed along by the impact of bouncing grains.

**Deposition** happens when the wind slows down, for example behind a rock or bush, or when it loses energy.

## Desert landforms

### Landforms of wind deposition

:::example{title="Diagram in words: a barchan dune"}
Look down on the dune from above: it is a **crescent**, with two **horns pointing downwind**.

1. On the windward side the slope is gentle (about 10-15°); sand is pushed up it by saltation.
2. At the crest the sand avalanches down the steep **slip face** on the leeward side (about 30-35°).
3. Sand moves faster round the lower edges, so the edges advance further and form the horns.
4. The whole dune moves slowly downwind.

Barchans form where the wind blows mainly from one direction and sand is limited.
:::

- **Seif dunes**: long, narrow, sharp-crested ridges of sand lying **parallel** to the main wind direction, sometimes tens of kilometres long; they form where winds blow from two main directions. They are common in the Sahara and Namib.

![Barchan dune: a crescent whose horns point downwind; sand is pushed up the gentle windward slope (about 10 to 15 degrees) and avalanches down the steep slip face (about 30 to 35 degrees), so the whole dune creeps downwind.](media:diagrams/barchan-dune-plan-section.png "Figure: a barchan dune in plan view and in cross-section")

### Landforms of wind erosion

| Landform | Description | How it forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rock pedestal (mushroom rock) | A rock with a narrow "stalk" and a wider top | Abrasion is strongest near the ground, so the base is undercut more than the top |
| Zeuge (plural zeugen) | Flat-topped ridges of hard rock on softer rock, separated by trenches | Horizontal layers: wind and weathering cut down through joints in the hard cap and scour the soft rock below |
| Yardang | Long ridges of hard rock separated by grooves, lying parallel to the wind | Vertical bands of hard and soft rock run parallel to the wind; the soft bands are scoured out |

### Landforms shaped by wind and water

- **Mesa**: a flat-topped table hill with steep sides and a hard cap rock, wider than it is high.
- **Butte**: a smaller remnant of a mesa, higher than it is wide.
- **Inselberg**: an isolated steep hill rising from a plain (in Zimbabwe, granite inselbergs are mainly weathering landforms).
- **Pediment**: a gently sloping rock surface at the foot of a mountain or inselberg, worn back by weathering and sheet floods.

### The role of water in deserts

Rain is rare but often violent. Flash floods carry large loads and cut steep-sided dry valleys (**wadis**), spread **alluvial fans** where they leave the mountains, and form temporary salt lakes (**playas**) in basins. Many desert landforms were also shaped in wetter periods of the past. In most deserts water does more work than wind, but the wind sorts and moves what the water leaves behind.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Barchan horns pointing the wrong way"}
The horns point **downwind**, and the steep slip face is on the leeward side, between the horns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying deserts are always hot"}
Days may be very hot, but clear skies make nights cold, and some deserts (Gobi, Antarctica) are cold. Low precipitation, not heat, defines a desert.
:::

:::mistake{title="Seif dunes across the wind"}
Seif dunes lie **parallel** to the main wind direction; barchans lie across it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Arid regions receive very little rain (often under about 250 mm) and semi-arid regions little and unreliable rain (often about 250-500 mm), with high evaporation, big daily temperature ranges and sparse vegetation.
- Hot deserts lie mainly at 15-30° N and S, caused by subtropical high pressure, cold ocean currents, rain shadows and continentality.
- Zimbabwe's semi-arid areas include the south-east lowveld, the Zambezi valley and south-western Matabeleland; western Zimbabwe has Kalahari sands.
- Wind erodes by deflation and abrasion, transports by suspension, saltation and surface creep, and deposits dunes (barchans across the wind, seifs parallel to it).
- Erosion landforms include rock pedestals, zeugen and yardangs; mesas, buttes, inselbergs and pediments are shaped by weathering, wind and occasional floods.
:::
