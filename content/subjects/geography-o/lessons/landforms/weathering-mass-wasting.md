## Introduction

The balancing rocks at Epworth near Harare look as if a giant stacked them. The bare dome of Domboshava rises smooth and grey above the fields. Inside the Chinhoyi Caves, a deep blue pool lies at the bottom of a collapsed cavern. None of these was built; all were shaped by **weathering**, the slow breakdown of rock where it lies.

Once rock is broken down, gravity can move it downhill. During Cyclone Idai in March 2019, saturated slopes in Chimanimani collapsed in landslides and debris flows that buried homes. This movement is **mass wasting**. This lesson explains both processes, the landforms they produce and how people can reduce the risk.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weathering"}
The breakdown of rock in its original place (in situ) by physical, chemical or biological processes. No transport is involved.
:::

:::definition{term="Erosion"}
The wearing away and removal of material by a moving agent: rivers, waves, wind or ice.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass wasting (mass movement)"}
The downslope movement of soil and rock under the pull of gravity, without a transporting agent such as a river carrying it.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint"}
A crack in a rock along which there has been no movement. Water enters along joints, so they guide weathering.
:::

:::definition{term="Regolith"}
The layer of loose, weathered material lying on top of solid rock.
:::

## Physical (mechanical) weathering

Rock breaks into smaller pieces **without any change in its minerals**.

| Process | How it works | Where it is common |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exfoliation (onion-skin weathering) | Outer layers heat and expand by day and cool and contract at night; stress makes curved sheets peel off. Release of pressure as overlying rock is removed also makes granite crack into curved sheets | Hot deserts and semi-arid areas with a large daily temperature range; granite domes |
| Block disintegration | Repeated expansion and contraction opens joints until the rock splits into blocks | Well-jointed rock in hot dry areas |
| Granular disintegration | Dark and light minerals expand by different amounts, so individual grains loosen and fall off | Coarse rocks such as granite |
| Frost action (freeze-thaw) | Water in cracks freezes and expands by about 9 %, forcing the crack wider; repeated freezing and thawing breaks the rock | Cold climates and high mountains; rare in Zimbabwe |

Plant roots growing in cracks and salt crystals growing in pores also force rock apart.

## Chemical weathering

Minerals are **changed or dissolved** by reactions with water, oxygen or acids. It is fastest where it is **hot and wet**.

- **Hydrolysis**: water reacts with feldspar in granite and turns it into clay, so the rock "rots" and crumbles; the quartz grains are left as sand.
- **Oxidation**: oxygen reacts with iron in minerals, forming iron oxides that colour rock and soil red or brown and weaken the rock.
- **Carbonation**: rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes a weak carbonic acid, which reacts with limestone and dolomite and carries them away in solution.
- **Solution**: some minerals, such as rock salt, simply dissolve in water.

## Factors that control weathering

- **Climate**: hot and wet favours chemical weathering; hot and dry with a big daily range favours exfoliation and disintegration; cold with temperatures crossing 0 °C favours frost action.
- **Rock type**: minerals differ in resistance (quartz resists, feldspar rots); limestone dissolves in weak acid.
- **Rock structure**: closely spaced joints let water in and give more surfaces to attack.
- **Vegetation**: roots break rock, and decaying plants add acids to soil water.
- **Time and relief**: older surfaces and gentle slopes, where weathered material stays in place, are more deeply weathered.

## Granite landforms

Much of Zimbabwe is granite, so these landforms are everywhere.

:::example{title="Diagram in words: how a kopje forms (two stages)"}
1. **Underground**: granite has joints, some close together and some far apart. Water seeps down the joints and hydrolysis rots the rock along them, producing a thick layer of clay and sand (regolith) with rounded, unweathered **corestones** where the joints are widely spaced.
2. **Stripping**: rivers and rain wash the loose regolith away over a long time.
3. **Exposure**: the hard corestones are left standing as a pile of rounded boulders on a solid base. This is a **kopje** (tor).

Label the joints, the weathered zone, the corestones and the original ground surface.
:::

- **Castle kopje**: a kopje of large, roughly rectangular blocks stacked like the walls of a castle, where vertical and horizontal joints cross.
- **Balancing rocks**: rounded boulders resting on one another at a small point of contact, left after the rock around them has weathered away. The Epworth balancing rocks are a well-known example.
- **Dwala (bornhardt)**: a large, bare, smooth dome of granite with very few joints. It loses curved sheets by exfoliation and pressure release. Domboshava is an example.

The **Matobo Hills** south of Bulawayo, a World Heritage Site, are a whole landscape of dwalas, kopjes and balancing rocks.

## Karst features (limestone and dolomite)

Carbonation dissolves limestone along joints, so the water goes underground.

- **Sinkhole (swallow hole)**: a hollow at the surface where water disappears underground or where a cave roof has collapsed.
- **Cave**: a large underground space dissolved along joints and bedding planes. The Chinhoyi Caves, in dolomite, include a deep water-filled sinkhole.
- **Stalactite**: a column of calcium carbonate hanging from the cave **roof**, formed as dripping water loses carbon dioxide and deposits calcite.
- **Stalagmite**: a column growing **up** from the cave floor where the drips land.
- **Pillar**: forms when a stalactite and stalagmite join.

## Mass wasting

| Type | Speed | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soil creep | Very slow (millimetres a year) | Particles move downhill as soil wets and dries; shown by tilted fence posts, bent tree trunks and small ridges (terracettes) |
| Landslide | Fast | A mass of rock and soil slides along a slip plane, often after heavy rain |
| Mudflow | Fast | Saturated fine soil flows like a liquid down a slope or valley |
| Rockfall | Very fast | Rocks break off a cliff and fall freely, collecting as scree at the foot |

**Causes**: steep slopes; heavy or prolonged rain that saturates the ground and adds weight; removal of vegetation whose roots held the soil; undercutting of slopes by rivers, road cuttings or mining; earthquakes.

:::example{title="Case study: landslides in Chimanimani, Cyclone Idai (March 2019)"}
Very heavy rain from Cyclone Idai fell on the steep slopes of Chimanimani and Chipinge districts. The soils became saturated and heavy, and slopes failed in landslides and debris flows that carried boulders, mud and trees into valleys and settlements. Homes, roads and bridges were destroyed, many people died or went missing, and communities were cut off. Settlements on steep slopes and along stream channels were the most exposed.
:::

### Managing the risk of mass wasting

- **Hazard mapping and land-use planning**: identify unstable slopes and keep homes and schools off them and out of stream channels.
- **Vegetation**: keep or replant trees and grass so roots bind the soil.
- **Drainage**: channels and pipes take water off slopes so they do not become saturated.
- **Engineering**: terraces, retaining walls, gabions (wire baskets of stones) and rock-fall nets on road cuttings.
- **Early warning and education**: warn people when heavy rain is forecast and teach them the signs (cracks, tilting trees, muddy springs).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing weathering with erosion"}
Weathering breaks rock where it is; erosion removes it by a moving agent. A kopje forms by weathering followed by removal of the regolith.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying frost action is common in Zimbabwe"}
Frost occurs on winter nights on the highveld, but freeze-thaw weathering of rock needs repeated freezing of water in cracks; it matters most in cold mountains, not in Zimbabwe.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up stalactites and stalagmites"}
Stalac**t**ites hang from the **t**op (they hold on **t**ight); stalag**m**ites grow up from the ground (they **m**ight reach the roof).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Weathering breaks rock in place: physical (exfoliation, block and granular disintegration, frost action) and chemical (hydrolysis, oxidation, carbonation, solution).
- Climate, rock type, joints, vegetation and time control the type and speed of weathering.
- Granite gives kopjes, castle kopjes, balancing rocks and dwalas (Epworth, Domboshava, Matobo).
- Limestone and dolomite give karst features: sinkholes, caves, stalactites, stalagmites and pillars (Chinhoyi Caves).
- Mass wasting (creep, landslides, mudflows, rockfalls) is triggered by steep slopes, saturation and clearing of vegetation; risk is reduced by planning, vegetation, drainage, engineering and early warning.
:::
