## Introduction

Look around a market such as Mbare Musika in Harare. Tomatoes from nearby farms sit beside rice from Asia, cooking oil made from imported soya and clothes from overseas factories. At the same time, Zimbabwean tobacco, gold and platinum are on their way to buyers in other continents. **Trade** is the buying and selling of goods and services, and it links every village and town to the rest of the world.

Trade matters to Zimbabwe because exports earn the foreign currency needed to buy fuel, machinery, medicines and many other goods the country does not produce. This lesson explains why trade happens, how trade is measured, Zimbabwe's main trade patterns, why trade is unequal, and why countries join together in trading blocs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade"}
The exchange (buying and selling) of goods and services between people, regions or countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Domestic (internal) trade"}
Trade within one country, for example maize sold by farmers in Mashonaland to millers in Bulawayo.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign (international) trade"}
Trade across national borders, involving imports and exports.
:::

:::definition{term="Exports and imports"}
Exports are goods and services sold to other countries; imports are goods and services bought from other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of trade"}
The value of exports minus the value of imports. A **surplus** (favourable balance) means exports are worth more than imports; a **deficit** (unfavourable balance) means imports are worth more.
:::

:::definition{term="Trading bloc"}
A group of countries that agree to reduce or remove barriers to trade between themselves, such as tariffs (taxes on imports) and quotas (limits on the amount imported).
:::

## Why trade happens

1. **Uneven resources:** minerals, fertile soils, energy and water are unevenly spread. Zimbabwe has platinum, gold and chrome but no oil, so it exports minerals and imports fuel.
2. **Different climates:** tropical countries grow crops such as tea and coffee that cooler countries cannot, and the reverse.
3. **Surpluses and shortages:** a country sells what it produces in excess and buys what it lacks.
4. **Specialisation:** countries do best by concentrating on what they produce most efficiently and trading for the rest.
5. **Technology and skills:** some countries make complex goods such as machinery, vehicles and medicines that others cannot yet make.

**Domestic trade** uses one currency and no customs duties; goods move from surplus to shortage areas, for example vegetables from rural areas to urban markets. **Foreign trade** involves border controls, customs duties, exchange rates and longer distances, but opens much larger markets.

## Measuring trade

:::worked-solution[Balance of trade (fictional figures)]
1. A country's exports are worth US\$6.4 billion and its imports US\$7.9 billion.
2. Balance of trade = exports − imports = 6.4 − 7.9 = −1.5.
3. The country has a trade **deficit** of US\$1.5 billion: it spends more on imports than it earns from exports.
:::

**Visible trade** is trade in goods (tobacco, ores, machinery); **invisible trade** is trade in services (tourism, transport, banking, insurance). Money spent by foreign tourists at Victoria Falls is an invisible export for Zimbabwe.

## Trade patterns

**Zimbabwe.** Most of Zimbabwe's exports are primary products: minerals such as gold, platinum-group metals, nickel, chrome (often as ferrochrome), diamonds and lithium, and agricultural products, above all tobacco. Imports are mainly manufactured and processed goods: fuel, machinery, vehicles, chemicals, fertilisers and some foods such as wheat. South Africa is the largest source of Zimbabwe's imports, and its major trading partners include South Africa, China, the United Arab Emirates and Mozambique.

**SADC.** South Africa is the region's largest economy and its main manufacturing exporter, so much trade in southern Africa flows to and from South Africa through corridors such as Beitbridge, Beira and Durban.

**Africa.** Most African countries export a few primary products (crude oil, minerals, cocoa, coffee, cotton) and import manufactured goods. Trade between African countries is a small share of their total trade, partly because many economies produce similar goods and transport links between neighbours are poor.

**The world.** The largest flows of world trade are between and within the major industrial regions of East Asia, Europe and North America. Africa's share of world trade is only a few per cent.

## Trade imbalances

- **Between countries:** countries that export raw materials earn less than those that export manufactured goods, because raw material prices are low and change sharply from year to year, while the prices of manufactured imports tend to rise. This worsens their **terms of trade** and can create persistent deficits and debt.
- **Between regions of the world:** the industrial regions of the world hold most of world trade, while most of Africa remains a supplier of raw materials.
- **Within a country:** in Zimbabwe, Harare and Bulawayo, with their factories, banks and transport links, handle most trade, while remote rural areas trade little beyond local markets. Mining and commercial farming areas near good roads and railways trade more than isolated communal areas.

Ways to reduce imbalances include **adding value** before export (processing minerals, manufacturing tobacco products), diversifying exports, improving transport links and joining trading blocs.

## Trading blocs and producers' organisations

| Organisation | Members and base | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SADC (Southern African Development Community) | Southern African states including Zimbabwe; secretariat in Gaborone, Botswana; formed in 1992 from SADCC (1980) | Regional integration, a free trade area, shared infrastructure such as transport corridors and power pools |
| COMESA (Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa) | Eastern and southern African states including Zimbabwe; secretariat in Lusaka, Zambia | Free trade area and moves towards a customs union and common market |
| ECOWAS (Economic Community of West African States) | West African states; formed in 1975; headquarters in Abuja, Nigeria | Free movement of people and goods; economic integration |
| EU (European Union) | 27 European countries | Single market with free movement of goods, services, capital and people; common external tariff |
| OPEC (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) | Oil-exporting countries including Nigeria, Algeria and Libya; formed in 1960; headquarters in Vienna | A producers' organisation: members agree on oil output to influence world oil prices |

**Why trading blocs form:**

1. To remove tariffs and quotas between members, so goods are cheaper and markets larger.
2. To give small economies a bigger combined market, attracting investment and allowing larger factories.
3. To protect members' industries with a common external tariff on goods from outside.
4. To bargain together with powerful trading partners.
5. To co-operate on shared projects such as roads, railways, power lines and border posts.
6. To reduce dependence on former colonial powers and build regional unity.

Blocs also have disadvantages: weaker members' industries may struggle against stronger neighbours, overlapping memberships (many countries belong to both SADC and COMESA) create confusion, and trade with non-members may fall. The **African Continental Free Trade Area**, under which trading began in 2021, aims to create one market across Africa.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the balance of trade"}
Exports minus imports, not imports minus exports. A negative answer is a deficit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling OPEC a trading bloc like SADC"}
OPEC does not remove tariffs between members; it is a producers' organisation that controls oil output to influence prices.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Zimbabwe exports mainly manufactured goods"}
Zimbabwe's exports are mostly minerals and tobacco (primary products); it imports mainly manufactured goods and fuel.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 Section C"}
Data-response questions give fictional export and import tables: calculate the balance of trade or percentage shares, describe the pattern with figures, then explain it. For "reasons for the formation of trading blocs", give developed reasons ("removing tariffs makes members' goods cheaper, enlarging the market") and name a bloc such as SADC or COMESA.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1"}
Know which blocs Zimbabwe belongs to (SADC and COMESA), where their secretariats are, and that OPEC is an organisation of oil producers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trade happens because resources, climate, skills and technology are unevenly spread; countries specialise and exchange surpluses.
- Domestic trade is within a country; foreign trade crosses borders and involves imports, exports, tariffs and exchange rates.
- Balance of trade = exports − imports; a negative value is a deficit.
- Zimbabwe exports mainly minerals and tobacco and imports fuel, machinery and other manufactured goods; South Africa is its largest source of imports.
- Trading blocs (SADC, COMESA, ECOWAS, EU) reduce barriers and enlarge markets; OPEC is a producers' organisation controlling oil output.
:::
