## Introduction

Drive from Mutare to Chiredzi and you pass tea estates and pine plantations in the mountains, small fields of maize and groundnuts in communal lands, cattle ranches in the dry south and, finally, mile after mile of green, irrigated sugar cane in the Lowveld. Zimbabwe has many farming types side by side because its climate, soils, markets and history vary. This lesson describes the main types, where they are found, and what helps and hinders each.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Communal farming"}
Small-scale farming on communal land, where families have the right to use arable plots and share grazing land, mainly to feed the household with some crops sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Market gardening"}
Intensive growing of vegetables (and sometimes fruit and flowers) on small plots close to urban markets.
:::

:::definition{term="Horticulture"}
The growing of fruit, vegetables, flowers and ornamental plants, often intensively and for high-value markets including exports.
:::

:::definition{term="Plantation agriculture"}
Large-scale commercial growing of a single crop (such as tea, sugar cane or timber) on an estate, with many workers, high capital and processing on or near the estate.
:::

:::definition{term="Irrigation"}
Supplying water to crops artificially, from dams, rivers or boreholes, by furrows, sprinklers, centre pivots or drip lines.
:::

## Subsistence and communal farming

**Characteristics:** holdings of a few hectares; maize, sorghum, millets, groundnuts, round nuts and beans; a few cattle and goats, with cattle used for draught power, milk, manure and as a store of wealth; family labour; hand hoes and ox-drawn ploughs; mostly rain-fed; land held under communal tenure. Many communal lands lie in Regions III, IV and V, often on sandy soils.

**Benefits:** feeds millions of rural households; surplus sold locally; keeps rural communities together; low cash costs.

**Challenges:** low and unreliable rainfall; infertile sandy soils; small, fragmented plots; overgrazing and soil erosion on shared grazing land; little capital for fertiliser and improved seed; poor roads and distant markets.

## Commercial dairying

**Characteristics:** herds of high-yielding breeds such as Friesian (Holstein) and Jersey; planted pastures and bought feed; milking twice a day by machine; milk cooled and collected daily; found mainly in Regions I and II close to towns, because milk is perishable and bulky. Milk is processed by companies such as Dairibord into fresh milk, yoghurt, cheese and other products.

**Benefits:** regular monthly income; jobs; supplies urban markets; manure for crops.

**Challenges:** high cost of stock feed and veterinary care; diseases such as mastitis and tick-borne diseases; need for reliable electricity for milking and cooling; heavy daily labour.

## Horticulture and market gardening

**Market gardening** is common on the edges of towns and in areas well connected to them. Mutoko, for example, is known for supplying tomatoes and other vegetables to Mbare Musika in Harare. **Export horticulture** produces flowers (especially roses), fresh vegetables such as peas and beans, citrus and other fruit, mainly in Region II near Harare, and sends them to Europe by air.

**Benefits:** high value per hectare; intensive use of small plots; jobs, especially for women; foreign currency from exports.

**Challenges:** perishability (needs fast, refrigerated transport); high costs of irrigation, chemicals and packaging; strict quality standards in export markets; price falls when many growers sell the same crop.

## Cattle ranching

**Characteristics:** large ranches of thousands of hectares in Regions IV and V, especially in Matabeleland and the Lowveld; extensive grazing on natural pasture; breeds adapted to heat and ticks, including indigenous Mashona, Tuli and Nkone cattle and crosses with Brahman; beef sold to abattoirs such as those of the Cold Storage Company. Some ranches combine cattle with wildlife and tourism.

**Benefits:** uses land too dry for crops; beef for local and export markets; jobs; hides for leather.

**Challenges:** drought kills grazing and cattle; overgrazing; tick-borne diseases and foot-and-mouth disease, which can stop exports; stock theft; low numbers of workers per hectare.

## Mixed farming

Crops and livestock on the same farm, typical of Regions II and III: maize, soya beans or wheat with beef cattle, dairy cows, pigs or poultry. Crop residues feed animals; manure fertilises fields; risk is spread so a bad season for one enterprise is cushioned by the other.

## Plantation agriculture

| Crop | Main areas | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Tea | Chipinge and the Honde Valley (Eastern Highlands, Region I) | Needs high rainfall and acid, well-drained soils; leaves are plucked by hand and processed in estate factories |
| Sugar cane | Triangle and Hippo Valley near Chiredzi (Lowveld, Region V) | Hot climate; fully irrigated from dams such as Lake Mutirikwi; cane crushed in mills on the estates |
| Timber | Nyanga, Chimanimani and other Eastern Highlands areas | Pine and eucalyptus plantations supply sawmills, poles and paper |

**Benefits:** large-scale employment; export earnings; processing on site adds value; housing, clinics and schools for workers.

**Challenges:** dependence on one crop and on world prices; large capital costs; disease or drought can hit the whole estate; low wages; for timber, fires (and cyclones such as Idai in 2019) can destroy plantations.

## Irrigation farming

:::example{title="Case study: irrigation in the Lowveld and in smallholder schemes"}
Water stored in Lake Mutirikwi and other dams is released into canals that feed the sugar estates around Triangle and Hippo Valley, and the newer Tugwi-Mukosi dam was built partly to expand irrigation in the Lowveld. Irrigation turns a region too dry for rain-fed crops into one of the country's most productive areas. Smallholder schemes, such as Nyanyadzi in Manicaland, let families grow wheat, maize, beans and vegetables in plots supplied by canals, so they can farm in the dry season.
:::

**Methods:** surface (flood and furrow) irrigation; sprinklers; centre pivots (a long rotating arm of sprinklers); drip irrigation, which delivers water to each plant and wastes least.

**Benefits:** farming through the dry season and in drought; two or three crops a year; higher, more reliable yields; jobs.

**Challenges:** high costs of dams, canals, pumps and electricity; siltation of dams; waterlogging and salinisation of soils if drainage is poor; diseases such as bilharzia and malaria in canals; disputes over water.

:::example{title="Worked example: water use (fictional figures)"}
A centre pivot irrigates 40 ha with 6 mm of water per day. 1 mm over 1 ha = 10 m³.

1. Daily water = 40 × 6 × 10 = **2,400 m³**.
2. Over a 90-day season: 2,400 × 90 = **216,000 m³**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting tea in the Lowveld"}
Tea needs high rainfall and cool highlands (Region I). Sugar cane is the Lowveld plantation crop, and it depends on irrigation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all communal farming 'backward'"}
Communal farmers feed most rural families and adapt to difficult conditions. Describe the challenges they face rather than judging the farmers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 Section B"}
For "describe the characteristics", cover size, crops or animals, labour, capital and markets, and name an area. For "challenges", give a cause and an effect (for example "foot-and-mouth disease outbreaks lead to bans on beef exports").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Communal farming: small rain-fed plots, family labour, maize and small grains plus cattle; limited by poor soils, drought and lack of capital.
- Dairying is near towns in Regions I and II; market gardening near urban markets; export horticulture flies flowers and vegetables to Europe.
- Cattle ranching uses the dry Regions IV and V; mixed farming combines crops and livestock in Regions II and III.
- Plantations: tea in Chipinge and the Honde Valley, sugar cane at Triangle and Hippo Valley, timber in the Eastern Highlands.
- Irrigation (Lowveld estates, Nyanyadzi) allows dry-season farming but is costly and can cause salinisation.
:::
