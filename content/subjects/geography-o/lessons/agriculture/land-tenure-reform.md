## Introduction

Who owns or controls land, and on what terms, affects what a farmer can grow, whether they can borrow money and whether they invest in the soil for the future. In Zimbabwe land is also closely tied to history: during the colonial period land was divided by law along racial lines, and the redistribution of land has been one of the main policy issues since independence in 1980. This lesson explains the different ways land is held and describes the land reform programmes, their aims, outcomes and challenges. Views on land reform differ; in an exam you should describe and explain using evidence and present more than one side.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land tenure"}
The system by which land is owned, held or used: who has rights to the land, for how long and on what conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
Changes by government to the ownership, distribution or use of land, such as redistributing land to new farmers.
:::

:::definition{term="Resettlement"}
Moving people onto newly allocated land and providing them with plots and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Land fragmentation"}
The division of a holding into many small, often scattered plots, for example through inheritance as land is shared among children.
:::

:::definition{term="Land consolidation"}
Bringing scattered plots together into larger, compact holdings that are easier to farm.
:::

## Land tenure systems

| System | Who holds the land and how | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Freehold** | The owner has a title deed and holds the land permanently; it can be sold, inherited or mortgaged | Security encourages long-term investment; title deed can be used as collateral for loans | Land can become concentrated in few hands; high cost of buying land; poorer people excluded |
| **Leasehold** | Land is rented from the owner (often the state) for a fixed period under conditions, for example a 99-year lease | Gives access to land without buying it; the state can set conditions on use | Less security than freehold; leases may not be accepted by banks as collateral; land cannot be freely sold |
| **Communal** | Land belongs to the state and is allocated to households by traditional leaders and Rural District Councils; households have the right to use arable plots and share grazing | Every family in the community can get land; low cost; social security for rural people | No title, so land cannot be sold or used as collateral; shared grazing is often overgrazed; plots become fragmented as population grows |
| **State land** | Owned and managed by the state: national parks, forests, research stations, state farms | Protects wildlife, forests and water catchments; land for public purposes | Can be underused; conflicts with neighbouring communities over grazing and resources |

## Fragmentation and consolidation

As families grow, arable land in communal areas is shared among children, so holdings become smaller and more scattered. Fragmentation wastes time walking between plots, makes mechanisation and irrigation harder and reduces yields. **Consolidation** joins plots into compact holdings, making farming and planning (for example of grazing and arable zones) more efficient, but it can be resisted because families value particular plots and graves.

:::example{title="Worked example: fragmentation (fictional figures)"}
A household has 6 ha of arable land. It is divided equally among 3 children, and each child's share is later divided equally among 2 children.

1. First division: 6 ÷ 3 = **2 ha** each.
2. Second division: 2 ÷ 2 = **1 ha** each, a sixth of the original holding.
:::

## Why land reform?

1. **Unequal distribution:** colonial laws, such as the Land Apportionment Act of 1930, reserved much of the best-watered land in Regions I and II for a small number of large commercial farms, while most rural Africans lived in crowded reserves (later communal lands), often in drier regions with poorer soils.
2. **Overcrowding and land degradation** in communal areas: too many people and livestock on too little land.
3. **Land hunger** among landless people and young families.
4. **Social justice and political demands** arising from the liberation struggle.
5. **Raising production and incomes** by giving more people land to farm.

## Phases of land reform

### Phase 1: willing-buyer, willing-seller (from 1980)

Under the independence (Lancaster House) settlement, the government could buy land only from owners who wanted to sell, at market prices. Tens of thousands of families were resettled during the 1980s, but progress slowed because land was costly, funds were limited and much land offered was in drier regions. The Land Acquisition Act of 1992 allowed the state to acquire land compulsorily, with compensation.

### Phase 2: the Fast Track Land Reform Programme (from 2000)

From 2000 the government acquired most large-scale commercial farms rapidly and compulsorily and allocated them under two models:

| Model | Who | Farm size and type | Tenure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Mainly landless people and families from congested communal areas | Small-scale plots, either villagised (homes grouped together, separate arable plots, shared grazing) or self-contained | Permits / offer letters from the state |
| **A2** | People with some capital, skills or farming experience | Small, medium and large-scale commercial farms | Leases from the state (for example 99-year leases) |

## Outcomes and challenges

**Outcomes often reported:**

- Land is far more widely distributed: many more households now farm land in Regions II and III.
- Pressure on some communal areas eased.
- Production patterns changed: commercial output of some crops (such as wheat) and exports of beef and horticultural produce fell sharply in the early 2000s, while smallholder production expanded. Tobacco output fell and later recovered, with small-scale growers producing much of the crop.

**Challenges:**

- **Tenure security:** permits and leases are not always accepted as collateral, which limits borrowing.
- **Inputs and capital:** many new farmers lacked seed, fertiliser, machinery, draught power and credit.
- **Infrastructure:** some irrigation equipment, dams and farm buildings deteriorated.
- **Skills and support:** extension services were stretched.
- **Disputes:** overlapping allocations and boundary disputes on some farms.
- **Compensation:** former owners sought compensation; in 2020 the government and representatives of former farm owners signed an agreement on compensation for improvements made to the farms.
- Loss of farm-worker jobs on former commercial farms.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing A1 and A2"}
**A1** = small-scale plots, mainly for people from crowded communal areas. **A2** = commercial farms for people with capital or skills.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
"Evaluate the outcomes" needs evidence on both sides (wider access to land and growth of smallholder output, but production losses, tenure and input problems) and a supported conclusion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 Section B"}
Compare tenure systems in a table-like way (security, ability to sell, collateral, access). For land reform, use the phases in order, name the models, and give balanced outcomes. Use neutral, factual language.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tenure systems: freehold (title deed), leasehold (fixed-term lease), communal (use rights allocated by traditional leaders and councils) and state land.
- Fragmentation divides holdings into small scattered plots; consolidation joins them.
- Land reform aimed to correct unequal distribution, relieve overcrowding and raise production.
- Phase 1 (from 1980): willing-buyer, willing-seller; Phase 2 (from 2000): fast-track resettlement with A1 (small-scale) and A2 (commercial) models.
- Outcomes include wider access to land and a growing smallholder sector, alongside falls in some commercial output, tenure, input and infrastructure challenges, and compensation issues.
:::
