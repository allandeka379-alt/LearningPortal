## Introduction

Ask any farmer in Zimbabwe about recent seasons and you will hear about rains that came late, long dry spells in January and February, and caterpillars stripping maize leaves. El Niño years such as 2015–16 and 2023–24 brought severe drought to southern Africa, and Cyclone Idai in March 2019 destroyed crops, livestock and plantations in Chimanimani and Chipinge. At the same time, pests such as fall armyworm and diseases such as January disease cut yields every year. Farmers who understand these threats can choose crops, methods and controls that reduce their losses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate change mitigation"}
Actions that reduce the causes of climate change, such as cutting greenhouse gas emissions or storing more carbon in soils and trees.
:::

:::definition{term="Climate change adaptation"}
Actions that help people live with the effects of climate change, such as growing drought-tolerant crops or harvesting rainwater.
:::

:::definition{term="Pest"}
An animal (insect, bird, rodent or other) that damages crops, stored produce or livestock.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation agriculture"}
Farming that disturbs the soil as little as possible, keeps it covered with mulch and rotates crops, to conserve soil moisture and fertility.
:::

## Effects of climate change on agriculture

| Change | Effect on farming |
|---|---|
| Higher temperatures | More evaporation and crop water stress; heat stress in livestock; lower maize yields in hot spells |
| Later and more erratic rains; longer mid-season dry spells | Planting delayed, crops wilt at flowering, harvests fail more often |
| More frequent and severe droughts | Shortage of grazing and water; cattle deaths; food insecurity |
| More intense storms, floods and cyclones | Fields washed away, soil erosion, damaged roads, dams and plantations (for example Cyclone Idai, 2019) |
| Shifting natural regions | Drier regions appear to be expanding, so crops suited to an area under the old map may now fail |
| Changing pest and disease patterns | Warmer conditions can favour some pests and help them spread |

## Mitigation and adaptation

**Mitigation (reducing the causes):**

- Planting trees and woodlots (agroforestry) and reducing deforestation, including wood used to cure tobacco.
- Conservation agriculture and mulching, which keep carbon in the soil.
- Reducing the burning of crop residues and grassland.

**Adaptation (living with the effects):**

1. **Conservation agriculture**, promoted in Zimbabwe through the Pfumvudza/Intwasa programme: small plots with planting basins, minimum tillage, mulch cover and crop rotation, which hold moisture through dry spells.
2. **Drought-tolerant crops and early-maturing varieties:** sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet, cowpeas, and short-season maize.
3. **Hardy livestock breeds** such as Mashona cattle and indigenous goats, and fewer animals per hectare.
4. **Irrigation** from dams and boreholes, especially drip irrigation, which saves water.
5. **Water harvesting:** tied ridges, infiltration pits, small dams and roof catchment tanks.
6. **Crop and income diversification:** several crops, livestock, beekeeping, gardens, so one failure does not ruin the household.
7. **Early warning and weather information**, adjusting planting dates, and crop or livestock insurance.

## Pests and diseases

### Crop pests

| Pest | Crops attacked | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Fall armyworm (caterpillar of a moth; first reported in southern Africa in 2016) | Maize mainly, also sorghum and other cereals | Eats leaves and growing points, holes in leaves and cobs; large yield losses |
| Locusts (swarming grasshoppers) | Almost any green crop and pasture | Swarms can strip fields in hours |
| Quelea birds | Small grains: sorghum, millets, wheat | Huge flocks eat ripening grain |
| Stalk borers | Maize and sorghum | Larvae tunnel inside stems; plants break and yield falls |
| Aphids | Many crops | Suck sap and spread virus diseases |

### Livestock pests and diseases

| Disease or pest | Cause or spread | Effects | Main control |
|---|---|---|---|
| January disease (theileriosis) | Spread by the brown ear tick; most common in the rainy season | High fever and death in cattle | Regular dipping; vaccination where available |
| Heartwater, redwater, gall sickness | Tick-borne | Illness, deaths, lower milk and beef output | Dipping (acaricides), treatment |
| Foot-and-mouth disease | A highly infectious virus; spread by contact, often from buffalo to cattle | Blisters, lameness, weight loss; bans on beef exports | Vaccination, movement controls, quarantine, fencing to separate buffalo and cattle |
| Newcastle disease | A virus of poultry | Rapid deaths of chickens | Vaccination |
| Tsetse fly | Spreads trypanosomiasis in low-lying areas such as the Zambezi valley | Weak, thin cattle that may die | Traps and targets, spraying |

### Methods of control

- **Manual (cultural and physical):** hand-picking caterpillars; destroying infected plants and crop residues; early planting; crop rotation; scaring birds; weeding; ploughing to expose pupae; keeping animals away from infected herds.
- **Biological:** natural enemies (parasitic wasps, birds and ants that eat armyworm larvae); resistant or tolerant crop varieties and livestock breeds; intercropping, for example with legumes.
- **Chemical:** pesticides sprayed on crops; acaricides in dip tanks for ticks; aerial or ground spraying of locust swarms and quelea roosts; veterinary drugs and vaccines.

Chemicals act quickly but are costly, can harm people, bees and natural enemies, and pests can become resistant. **Integrated pest management** combines all three methods and uses chemicals only when needed.

:::example{title="Worked example: yield loss (fictional figures)"}
A farmer expected 3.2 t/ha of maize, but fall armyworm reduced the yield to 2.4 t/ha on 5 ha.

1. Loss per hectare = 3.2 − 2.4 = 0.8 t/ha.
2. Total loss = 0.8 × 5 = **4 t**.
3. Percentage loss = 0.8 ÷ 3.2 × 100 = **25%**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mitigation and adaptation"}
Mitigation tackles the **causes** (emissions, carbon storage). Adaptation copes with the **effects** (drought-tolerant crops, irrigation).
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling vaccination a manual control"}
Vaccination and resistant breeds work through the animal's own defences; treat them as biological or veterinary controls, not manual ones.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 Section B"}
"Deduce the effects of climate change" questions often give rainfall or temperature data: describe the change, then link it to farming (yields, grazing, water). For pests, name the pest, the crop or animal, the effect and at least one control from each of the manual, biological and chemical groups.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Climate change brings higher temperatures, erratic rains, longer dry spells, droughts and stronger storms, lowering yields and livestock output.
- Mitigation reduces causes (trees, less burning, conservation agriculture); adaptation copes with effects (conservation agriculture, drought-tolerant crops and breeds, irrigation, water harvesting, diversification).
- Key pests: fall armyworm, locusts, quelea birds, stalk borers, aphids; key livestock threats: tick-borne diseases such as January disease, foot-and-mouth disease, Newcastle disease, tsetse fly.
- Control methods are manual, biological and chemical; integrated pest management combines them.
:::
