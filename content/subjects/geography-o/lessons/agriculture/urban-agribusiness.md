## Introduction

During the rainy season, much of the open land in and around Harare, Bulawayo, Gweru and Mutare turns green with maize. Families grow crops in backyards, on road verges, under power lines and along streams, and keep chickens and rabbits behind their houses. This is **urban agriculture**. Beyond the farm gate, a whole chain of businesses supplies seed and fertiliser, buys and processes crops and sells food to consumers: this is **agribusiness**. Both create food, jobs and incomes, but both also face real problems.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urban agriculture"}
Growing crops and keeping livestock (and fish) within towns and cities and on their edges, for food or income.
:::

:::definition{term="On-plot and off-plot cultivation"}
**On-plot** farming takes place on a household's own residential stand (backyard gardens, poultry). **Off-plot** farming takes place on open land the household does not own, such as vacant land, road verges, stream banks and wetlands.
:::

:::definition{term="Agribusiness"}
Any business involved in agriculture along the value chain: supplying inputs, producing, processing, transporting, marketing and selling farm products, and providing finance and services to farmers.
:::

:::definition{term="Contract farming"}
An agreement in which a company provides inputs (and often advice) to farmers and agrees to buy their crop at harvest, deducting the cost of the inputs.
:::

## Urban agriculture in Zimbabwe

**Nature:** small plots; mainly maize in the rainy season, with vegetables (rape, covo, tomatoes, onions) all year where water is available; poultry, rabbits, pigs and some fish; mostly family labour; water from rain, taps, wells and streams; produce eaten at home or sold at local markets.

### Advantages

- **Food security:** cheaper food for low-income households, especially when prices rise.
- **Income and jobs:** sale of vegetables, eggs and broilers; work for the unemployed.
- **Use of idle land** that would otherwise be overgrown or used for dumping.
- **Recycling:** kitchen waste becomes compost and manure.
- **Better nutrition** from fresh vegetables and protein.

### Disadvantages and problems

- **Stream-bank and wetland cultivation** causes erosion and **siltation** of rivers and of water-supply dams such as Lake Chivero, which supplies Harare.
- **Pollution:** fertiliser and pesticide run-off; some plots are watered with untreated sewage, which can spread disease.
- **Conflict with councils:** cultivation on unauthorised land breaks by-laws, and crops may be slashed.
- **Insecure tenure:** off-plot farmers can lose their plots at any time, so they do not invest.
- **Theft** of crops and livestock; disputes between neighbours over plots.
- **Tall maize** close to roads and houses blocks visibility and can hide criminals; standing water and blocked drains can breed mosquitoes.
- **Smells and noise** from poultry and pigs in residential areas.

### Solutions

1. **Allocate and register designated plots** away from stream banks and wetlands, with permits or leases.
2. **Enforce by-laws and environmental rules** on stream-bank cultivation, with education rather than only slashing crops.
3. **Train farmers** in conservation methods, safe use of chemicals, composting and drip irrigation.
4. **Use treated waste water** safely, or harvest rainwater.
5. **Form cooperatives** to buy inputs, share water and market produce.
6. **Plan for agriculture** in town planning: allotments and community gardens.

## Agribusiness in Zimbabwe

:::example{title="A diagram in words: the agribusiness value chain"}
Imagine a chain of five links from left to right.

1. **Input supply:** seed companies, fertiliser and chemical suppliers, stockfeed makers, machinery dealers.
2. **Production:** farmers (communal, A1, A2, commercial, urban).
3. **Processing:** maize millers, oilseed crushers, dairies, abattoirs, tobacco processors, sugar mills.
4. **Marketing and distribution:** auction floors and contract buyers for tobacco, the Grain Marketing Board, wholesalers, supermarkets, market traders, exporters.
5. **Consumers** at home and abroad.

Support services (finance, insurance, transport, research and extension) link to every stage.
:::

### Types of agribusiness

| Type | Examples in Zimbabwe |
|---|---|
| Input supply | Seed companies such as Seed Co; agro-dealers selling fertiliser and chemicals |
| Processing | Dairy processors such as Dairibord; maize mills; sugar mills at Triangle and Hippo Valley; peanut butter and cooking-oil makers |
| Marketing | Tobacco auction floors in Harare; the Grain Marketing Board; vegetable traders at Mbare Musika |
| Contract farming | Tobacco and cotton companies supplying inputs to growers; sugar out-grower schemes |
| Small-scale enterprises | Broiler and egg production, horticulture for supermarkets, honey, fish farming, value-added products such as dried vegetables |

### Sources of funding for small-scale agribusiness

- **Own savings** and help from family and friends.
- **Savings clubs** (rotating group savings, often called *mukando* / *round*).
- **Microfinance institutions** and **banks**, including the Agricultural Development Bank of Zimbabwe (Agribank).
- **Contract farming**, where inputs are advanced on credit.
- **Government schemes and grants**, and **NGO or donor** projects.
- **Supplier credit** from agro-dealers.

:::example{title="Worked example: a broiler project (fictional figures)"}
A youth group buys 200 day-old chicks at US\$0.90 each and spends US\$560 on feed and US\$60 on vaccines and other costs. 190 birds survive and are sold at US\$5.00 each.

1. Costs = (200 × 0.90) + 560 + 60 = 180 + 560 + 60 = **US\$800**.
2. Income = 190 × 5.00 = **US\$950**.
3. Profit = 950 − 800 = **US\$150** per batch.
:::

### Importance of agribusiness

**To individuals:** income and self-employment; skills in production, marketing and management; access to inputs and markets (contract farming); better living standards.

**To the economy:** jobs along the whole value chain; value addition (processing raw crops into food and products); export earnings (tobacco, sugar, horticulture); food security; demand for transport, packaging and finance; tax revenue; rural development.

**Challenges:** shortage of capital and high interest rates; price changes; poor roads and power cuts; side-marketing (selling contracted crops to other buyers); climate shocks; competition from imports.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking agribusiness means only large companies"}
A woman selling eggs from her backyard flock, a youth group raising broilers and a vegetable vendor are all agribusinesses.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation of urban agriculture"}
"Evaluate" needs both advantages and problems, and a judgement such as "beneficial if regulated and kept off stream banks".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Urban agriculture (on-plot and off-plot) provides food, income and jobs and uses idle land, but causes siltation, pollution, conflict with councils and health risks.
- Solutions include designated plots, enforced by-laws, training, safe water use, cooperatives and planning.
- Agribusiness covers input supply, production, processing, marketing and contract farming.
- Small-scale agribusiness is funded by savings, savings clubs, microfinance, banks such as Agribank, contract farming, government and NGOs.
- It provides incomes and skills for individuals and jobs, value addition, exports and food security for the economy.
:::
