## Introduction

Why do farmers in Nyanga grow tea, apples and pine trees, farmers near Harare grow maize, tobacco and soya beans, and ranchers near Beitbridge keep cattle and wildlife? The main answer is **rainfall**: how much falls, and how reliably. Around 1960 Zimbabwe was divided into **natural regions** (also called agro-ecological regions) according to rainfall, temperature and soils, and each region was matched with the kind of farming most suited to it. The regions are still used to plan farming, advise farmers and target drought help.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural (agro-ecological) region"}
An area with broadly similar rainfall, temperature and soils, and therefore suited to similar types of farming.
:::

:::definition{term="Rainfall reliability"}
How much rainfall varies from year to year and within the season. Reliable rain arrives on time and in similar amounts each year; unreliable rain brings droughts and long dry spells.
:::

:::definition{term="Mid-season dry spell"}
A period of several weeks without useful rain in the middle of the rainy season, which can wilt or kill crops.
:::

## The five regions

All rainfall figures are approximate mean annual totals; textbooks give slightly different boundaries.

| Region | Mean annual rainfall | Main areas (examples) | Suited farming |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | More than about 1,000 mm; some rain in most months; cool | Eastern Highlands: Nyanga, Vumba, Chimanimani, Chipinge highlands | **Specialised and diversified:** tea, coffee, forestry (pine, eucalyptus, wattle), deciduous fruit (apples), potatoes, dairy |
| IIa | About 750–1,000 mm, reliable; few severe dry spells | Highveld of Mashonaland around Harare, Marondera, Mazowe and Bindura | **Intensive crops and livestock:** maize, tobacco, soya beans, cotton, irrigated winter wheat, horticulture, dairy and beef |
| IIb | About 750–1,000 mm, but with severe dry spells in the season or a short season | Parts of the northern and western highveld | Intensive farming, with more risk; drought-tolerant varieties advised |
| III | About 650–800 mm; frequent mid-season dry spells; high temperatures | Parts of the Midlands (for example around Gweru and Kwekwe) and other middleveld areas | **Semi-intensive:** livestock with fodder crops; drought-tolerant crops; some maize, cotton, groundnuts |
| IV | About 450–650 mm; regular droughts and dry spells | Large parts of Matabeleland, Masvingo and the middleveld communal lands | **Semi-extensive:** livestock; drought-tolerant crops such as sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet |
| V | Less than about 450–650 mm; very erratic | Zambezi valley and the Lowveld (for example Chiredzi, Beitbridge, Binga) | **Extensive:** cattle and game ranching; irrigated sugar cane where dam water is available |

Regions IV and V together cover roughly 60% of the country (an approximate figure), so most of Zimbabwe is better suited to livestock and drought-tolerant crops than to rain-fed maize.

## Why the regions differ

- **Altitude and relief:** rainfall and temperature are linked to height. The Eastern Highlands force moist south-easterly air to rise, giving relief rain (Region I). The highveld (above about 1,200 m) is cooler and wetter than the lowveld and the Zambezi valley (below about 900 m), which are hot and dry (Region V).
- **Distance from the source of moisture:** much rain comes from moist air from the Indian Ocean, so the east generally receives more than the west.
- **Soils:** the fertile red clays of parts of the highveld hold water and nutrients; the granite-derived sandy soils of many communal areas and the Kalahari sands of the west are less fertile.

:::example{title="A diagram in words: sketching the natural regions"}
Draw an outline of Zimbabwe.

1. Along the eastern border, draw a narrow strip from Nyanga south to Chimanimani and Chipinge: **Region I**.
2. Across the north-east and centre-north, around Harare and Marondera and north towards Mazowe, shade a broad band: **Region II** (IIa in the east, IIb further north-west).
3. Around the Region II area, in the middleveld, draw a belt: **Region III**.
4. Shade most of the south and west, around Bulawayo and Masvingo: **Region IV**.
5. Shade the low-lying Zambezi valley in the north and north-west and the Lowveld in the south-east and south: **Region V**.
6. Add a key, a title, a north arrow and labels for a few towns.
:::

:::example{title="Worked example: classifying stations (fictional data)"}
| Station | Mean annual rainfall (mm) | Region suggested |
|---|---|---|
| P (high eastern mountains) | 1,450 | I |
| Q (northern highveld) | 880, reliable | IIa |
| R (southern lowland) | 380 | V |

Station R's mean is far below 450 mm, so it lies in Region V and is best used for ranching or irrigated crops. Note that a station near a boundary (for example 640 mm) could be placed in III or IV, depending on its reliability.
:::

## Farming in each region: reasons

- **Region I:** high rainfall and cool temperatures suit tea, coffee, fruit and fast-growing plantation trees; steep slopes are better for perennial crops and forestry than for ploughed crops.
- **Region II:** reliable rains and fertile soils support intensive cropping; this is the main area for maize, tobacco and soya beans.
- **Region III:** mid-season dry spells make maize risky; farmers combine livestock with fodder and drought-tolerant crops.
- **Region IV:** frequent drought; cattle, goats and small grains (sorghum, millets) are more reliable than maize.
- **Region V:** too dry for rain-fed crops; extensive cattle and wildlife ranching, and large irrigated estates of sugar cane in the Lowveld supplied by dams such as Lake Mutirikwi.

## Climate change and the regions

Rising temperatures increase evaporation, and studies comparing recent rainfall with the original map suggest that the boundaries have shifted: the drier regions (IV and V) appear to have grown and Region II to have shrunk. Rainy seasons may start later and dry spells may be longer. This matters because advice, seed varieties and drought support are planned by region, so maps need regular updating.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting reliability"}
IIa and IIb receive similar totals. The difference is **reliability**: IIb has more severe dry spells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying nothing can be farmed in Region V"}
Rain-fed crops are risky, but Region V supports cattle and game ranching and, with irrigation, huge sugar-cane estates in the Lowveld.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 and Paper 2 Section B"}
Paper 1 often asks which region suits a crop or which farming suits a region. In Paper 2 you may be asked to sketch or annotate a map of the regions: keep the shapes simple, include a key and label the Eastern Highlands, the highveld, the Zambezi valley and the Lowveld. "Explain the farming in Region IV" needs climate facts (low, unreliable rain) linked to the choice of livestock and drought-tolerant crops.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe has five natural regions (I to V, with IIa and IIb) based mainly on rainfall amount and reliability, plus temperature and soils.
- I: Eastern Highlands, more than about 1,000 mm, specialised farming (tea, coffee, fruit, forestry).
- II: highveld, about 750–1,000 mm, intensive cropping (maize, tobacco, soya); IIa more reliable than IIb.
- III: semi-intensive; IV: semi-extensive livestock and drought-tolerant crops; V: extensive ranching and irrigated sugar cane.
- Climate change appears to be shifting the boundaries, with the drier regions expanding.
:::
