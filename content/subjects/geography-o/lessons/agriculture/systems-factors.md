## Introduction

Agriculture employs more people in Zimbabwe than any other activity, and most rural families grow at least part of their own food. A smallholder in a communal area planting maize after the first rains and a company growing lettuce in nutrient solution in a greenhouse look very different, but both are running a **farming system**: they combine inputs, carry out processes and produce outputs. Understanding the system, and what influences it, explains why farming varies so much from place to place.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Agriculture"}
The growing of crops and rearing of livestock (and fish) for food, raw materials and income.
:::

:::definition{term="Farming system"}
A way of looking at a farm as a set of **inputs** that pass through **processes** to give **outputs**, with **feedback** linking outputs back to inputs.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsistence farming"}
Farming mainly to feed the farmer's own household, with little surplus for sale.
:::

:::definition{term="Commercial farming"}
Farming mainly to sell produce for profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Intensive farming"}
High inputs of labour and/or capital per hectare on a small area, giving high yields per hectare.
:::

:::definition{term="Extensive farming"}
Low inputs per hectare spread over a large area, giving low yields per hectare.
:::

## The farm as a system

:::example{title="A diagram in words: a smallholder maize and cattle farm"}
Imagine four boxes joined by arrows from left to right, with an arrow looping back from the last box to the first.

1. **Inputs**
   - *Physical:* rainfall, sunshine, temperature, soil, slope, water.
   - *Human:* family labour, the farmer's knowledge and skills.
   - *Economic:* seed, fertiliser, pesticides, tools, draught oxen, money (capital), transport.
2. **Processes:** ploughing, planting, weeding, fertilising, spraying, harvesting, herding, dipping, milking.
3. **Outputs:** maize, beans and groundnuts, milk and meat, manure, hides, crop residues, income, and waste such as eroded soil.
4. **Feedback:** income buys next season's seed and fertiliser; seed is kept for planting; manure returns to the fields; a poor harvest means fewer inputs next year.
:::

## Types of farming

| Classification | Types | Brief description |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Subsistence / commercial | For the household / for sale and profit |
| What is produced | Arable / pastoral / mixed | Crops only / livestock only / both crops and livestock |
| Inputs per hectare | Intensive / extensive | High inputs on small areas / low inputs over large areas |
| Growing medium | Hydroponics | Plants grow with their roots in a nutrient solution instead of soil |
| | Aquaponics | Fish are raised in tanks; their waste water feeds plants grown hydroponically, and the plants clean the water for the fish |
| | Aeroponics | Plant roots hang in the air in a closed chamber and are sprayed with a nutrient mist |

Hydroponics, aquaponics and aeroponics are usually **intensive** and **commercial**. They use less water and land than soil farming and can be run in greenhouses or in towns, but they need capital, electricity and technical skill.

## Factors influencing agriculture

### Physical factors

- **Rainfall:** the amount, reliability and season of rain decide which crops can be grown without irrigation.
- **Temperature and altitude:** frost on high ground limits some crops; heat in low valleys suits others.
- **Soils:** fertile, deep, well-drained soils suit intensive cropping; thin or sandy soils suit grazing.
- **Relief and slope:** steep slopes erode easily and are hard to plough.
- **Water for irrigation** from rivers, dams or boreholes.

### Political factors

- Government **land policy and tenure** (who owns land and on what terms).
- **Subsidies**, input schemes and loans; **prices** set or guaranteed by the state.
- **Marketing boards** and auction systems; trade agreements and import or export rules.

### Socio-economic factors

- **Capital** for machinery, irrigation, seed and fertiliser.
- **Labour:** its availability, cost and skills.
- **Markets and transport:** distance to buyers and the quality of roads.
- **Technology and research:** improved seed varieties, irrigation, mechanisation.
- **Culture and tradition:** for example the value of cattle as wealth, and food preferences.

:::example{title="Worked example: is the season profitable? (fictional figures)"}
A farmer grows 3 ha of maize. Inputs cost US\$420 per ha. The yield is 2.5 tonnes per ha, sold at US\$300 per tonne.

1. Total input cost = 3 × 420 = **US\$1,260**.
2. Total output = 3 × 2.5 = 7.5 t; income = 7.5 × 300 = **US\$2,250**.
3. Profit = 2,250 − 1,260 = **US\$990**, which can be fed back as inputs next season.
:::

## A communal farming area in Zimbabwe

:::example{title="Case study: elements of a communal farm"}
A typical communal-area holding in Zimbabwe has:

- a **homestead** of houses, a granary, kraals or pens for cattle, goats and chickens;
- **arable fields** (often several hectares) for maize, sorghum, millet, groundnuts and beans;
- a **garden** near a stream, well or borehole for vegetables;
- shared **grazing land** (the commons) used by the whole village;
- **woodland** for firewood, poles and fruit; and shared services such as a **dip tank**, borehole and school.
:::

Land in a community is divided into **land-use zones**: residential, arable, grazing, woodland, water sources and wetlands, and areas for services. **Zoning** is needed to prevent conflict between users (for example cattle straying into crops), protect wetlands and stream banks from cultivation, reduce overgrazing and erosion, keep homes away from fields that are sprayed and plan space for services.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up intensive and commercial"}
**Intensive** describes inputs per hectare; **commercial** describes the purpose. A small vegetable garden can be intensive and subsistence; a huge cattle ranch is extensive and commercial.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing inputs without classifying them"}
If a question asks you to classify inputs, say which are physical (rainfall, soil), human (labour, skills) and economic (seed, fertiliser, capital).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B and Cambridge Paper 2"}
Questions often give a farm description and ask for inputs, processes and outputs. Use the correct terms and give at least one example of each. "Explain how physical factors influence farming" needs the factor, its effect and an example, such as "low, unreliable rainfall in the south limits crops, so cattle ranching is common".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A farm is a system of inputs (physical, human, economic), processes, outputs and feedback.
- Farming is classified as subsistence or commercial; arable, pastoral or mixed; intensive or extensive; hydroponics, aquaponics and aeroponics are soil-free, intensive methods.
- Physical (climate, soil, relief, water), political (policy, subsidies, prices, tenure) and socio-economic factors (capital, labour, markets, technology, culture) influence farming.
- A communal farm has a homestead, arable fields, a garden, shared grazing and woodland; zoning separates land uses to avoid conflict and protect the environment.
:::
