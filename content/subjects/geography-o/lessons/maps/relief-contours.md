## Introduction

A map is flat, but the land is not. Zimbabwe rises from below about 500 m in the Zambezi valley and the Lowveld to about 2,590 m at Mount Nyangani in the Eastern Highlands, and the Highveld plateau across the centre of the country stands mostly above about 1,200 m. Topographical maps show these differences in height, called **relief**, mainly with **contour lines**.

Learning to "see" the shape of the land from contours lets you explain why a road winds, where a dam could be built, why a farm sits on flat ground and which way a river flows. Contour questions appear in every map-work paper.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Contour line"}
A line on a map joining places of the same height above sea level.
:::

:::definition{term="Contour interval"}
The difference in height between two neighbouring contour lines, for example 20 m. It is stated in the map margin and stays the same across one map.
:::

:::definition{term="Spot height"}
A dot with a number beside it giving the exact height of that point in metres.
:::

:::definition{term="Trigonometrical station"}
A surveyed point, usually on a hilltop, shown by a small triangle with its height. Surveyors used these points as fixed references when mapping.
:::

:::definition{term="Relief"}
The shape of the land surface: its heights, slopes and landforms.
:::

## Reading heights from a map

Contours are drawn in brown on most topographical maps. Every fourth or fifth line (an **index contour**) is thicker and labelled; the labels are usually written so that their tops face uphill.

:::worked-solution[Finding the contour interval]
1. Find two neighbouring labelled contours, for example 1500 m and 1600 m.
2. Count the unlabelled contours between them: four.
3. Four lines between make five equal gaps: (1600 − 1500) ÷ 5 = **20 m**.
:::

To find the height of a point **between** two contours, estimate its position: a point halfway between the 1540 m and 1560 m contours is about **1550 m**. A point on a contour line has exactly that height. Spot heights and trigonometrical stations give exact values, so use them whenever a question asks for the highest point.

:::example{title="Height difference"}
A trigonometrical station marked 1462 m stands above a dam whose water surface lies on the 1180 m contour. The difference in height is 1462 − 1180 = **282 m**.
:::

## Contour spacing and types of slope

- Contours **close together** show a **steep** slope; contours **far apart** show a **gentle** slope; no contours at all over a wide area shows almost **flat** land.
- **Even** spacing shows a **uniform** (straight) slope.
- A **concave** slope is steep near the top and gentle near the bottom: contours are crowded near the high ground and spread out lower down.
- A **convex** slope is gentle near the top and steep near the bottom: contours are spread out near the top and crowded lower down.

:::keyfact{title="Intervisibility clue"}
From the top of a convex slope you often cannot see the bottom, because the bulge of the slope blocks the view. From the top of a concave slope you can see all the way down.
:::

## Recognising landforms from contour patterns

Imagine the contours in words, as you might meet them in a map extract:

- **Conical hill**: roughly circular closed contours, one inside the other, evenly spaced, with a spot height or trigonometrical station at the centre. Many granite kopjes on the Highveld appear like this, though usually smaller and steeper.
- **Ridge**: long, narrow closed contours, high ground stretched out in one direction.
- **Plateau**: a large, fairly level area at the top with few or no contours, edged by closely spaced contours (steep sides).
- **Escarpment**: an elongated line of very closely spaced contours on one side (the scarp slope) with widely spaced contours on the other (the gentle dip slope).
- **Spur**: a tongue of high land projecting into lower ground. The contours form a **V or U pointing downhill**, towards lower ground.
- **Valley**: low land between higher ground, usually with a river. The contours form a **V pointing uphill**, towards higher ground, and the river runs through the point of each V.
- **Col (saddle)**: a low point on a ridge between two hilltops. You see two sets of closed contours, each round a summit, with lower ground between them.

:::example{title="Valley or spur?"}
Read the numbers on the contours. If the point of each V lies at the **higher** end (the 1560 m contour bends further upstream than the 1540 m one), the V points uphill, so it is a valley. A river along the V is a strong clue. If the point of the V faces the lower contours, it is a spur.
:::

## Describing relief and drainage

When asked to "describe the relief" of an area, give:

1. The **highest and lowest** points, with heights and grid references.
2. The **general slope** (for example "land falls from about 1400 m in the west to about 1180 m in the east").
3. The main **landforms** (hills, ridges, valleys, plateau) and the **steepness** of slopes, with locations.

For **drainage**, describe the rivers: their direction of flow (always from higher contours to lower), whether they are permanent or seasonal (dashed blue lines), the drainage pattern, and features such as dams, marshes or many small streams.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reading the V the wrong way"}
Valley Vs point upstream (to higher ground); spur Vs point downhill. Always check the contour values before deciding.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the number of lines, not the gaps"}
Four unlabelled lines between 1500 m and 1600 m make five gaps, so the interval is 20 m, not 25 m.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a river flows uphill"}
Rivers always flow from higher contours to lower ones. Use the contour values, not the direction of the page, to decide.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 1, landform questions often describe a contour pattern in words or by grid square. Decide first whether the land rises or falls, then look at the shape and spacing.
:::

:::exam-tip
When you "describe the relief", quote heights and grid references from the map. A general description with no figures earns few marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Contours join places of equal height; the contour interval is the height gap between neighbouring lines.
- Spot heights and trigonometrical stations give exact heights; estimate heights between contours.
- Close contours mean steep slopes; wide spacing means gentle slopes. Concave slopes are steep at the top, convex slopes steep at the bottom.
- Valley Vs point uphill; spur Vs point downhill. A col is a low point between two summits.
- Describe relief with heights, general slope and landforms; describe drainage with river direction, pattern and features.
:::
