## Introduction

When Cyclone Idai struck eastern Zimbabwe and Mozambique in March 2019, aid agencies needed to know quickly which areas were flooded and which roads and bridges were cut. Images taken from aircraft and satellites gave that picture far faster than teams on the ground could. Farmers, foresters, town planners and wildlife managers use the same kinds of images every day.

This lesson covers two linked skills: **interpreting photographs** (from the ground and from the air) and **remote sensing**, which depends on understanding light and the electromagnetic spectrum.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Remote sensing"}
Collecting information about the Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites, aircraft or drones, by recording the electromagnetic radiation that the surface reflects or gives off.
:::

:::definition{term="Electromagnetic spectrum"}
The full range of electromagnetic waves arranged by wavelength: gamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light, infrared, microwaves and radio waves.
:::

:::definition{term="Wavelength"}
The distance between one wave crest and the next. Visible light has wavelengths of roughly 400 to 700 nanometres (nm).
:::

:::definition{term="False-colour image"}
An image in which bands of radiation (often including invisible near-infrared) are displayed using visible colours that do not match what the eye would see.
:::

## Types of photograph

| Type | Viewpoint | What it shows well | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground photograph | Taken at ground level, looking horizontally | Detail of nearby features, people, buildings | Small area; distant features hidden behind near ones |
| Oblique aerial photograph | Taken from the air at an angle | A wide area with recognisable side views of features; **high oblique** views include the horizon, **low oblique** views do not | Scale changes across the photo: near features look larger than far ones |
| Vertical aerial photograph | Taken from directly overhead | A plan view like a map, with a nearly even scale; used to make and update maps | Features look unfamiliar from above; heights are hard to judge |

![Side view of an aircraft taking three kinds of photograph: a vertical photograph looks straight down and shows no horizon (a plan like a map), a low oblique is angled but shows only ground, and a high oblique looks almost level so the horizon appears in the picture.](media:diagrams/aerial-photograph-types.png "Figure: how the camera angle gives vertical, low oblique and high oblique aerial photographs")

### Describing a photograph

Ground and oblique photographs are divided into the **foreground** (nearest the camera, at the bottom), the **middle ground** and the **background** (furthest away, near the top). Describe each part in turn: landforms, vegetation, land use, settlement and any human activity. Use the photograph's clues to judge the **season** (green crops or dry grass), the **time of day** (long shadows mean early morning or late afternoon) and the **direction** if the Sun's position is known.

On a vertical photograph, use shape, size, tone, texture, pattern and shadow to identify features: centre-pivot irrigation shows as circles, plantations as regular blocks with even texture, roads as thin lines, and water as dark smooth areas.

:::example{title="Scale of a vertical aerial photograph"}
Two road junctions are 4 cm apart on a vertical photograph and 1 km apart on the ground. 1 km = 100,000 cm, so the photograph scale is 4 : 100,000 = **1 : 25,000**.
:::

## Light as a wave and the electromagnetic spectrum

Light travels as a wave. Different kinds of electromagnetic radiation differ in their **wavelength**: shorter waves (gamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet) carry more energy; longer waves (infrared, microwaves, radio) carry less. Our eyes detect only a narrow band, **visible light**, from violet (about 400 nm) through blue, green, yellow and orange to red (about 700 nm).

Just beyond red lies **near-infrared**, which we cannot see but which sensors on satellites can record. Further out, **thermal infrared** records heat, and **microwaves** are used by radar.

:::keyfact{title="Order of the spectrum (shortest to longest wavelength)"}
Gamma rays → X-rays → ultraviolet → visible (violet, blue, green, yellow, orange, red) → infrared → microwaves → radio waves.
:::

## How surfaces reflect radiation

Each surface reflects different amounts of each band. This pattern is its **spectral signature**, and it is what makes remote sensing work.

- **Healthy green vegetation** absorbs most blue and red light (chlorophyll uses them for photosynthesis), reflects some green (which is why leaves look green) and reflects **near-infrared very strongly**. Stressed or drying plants reflect less near-infrared.
- **Clear water** absorbs nearly all near-infrared, so it appears very dark in that band. Muddy water reflects more visible light than clear water.
- **Bare soil and rock** reflect fairly evenly across visible and near-infrared bands, so they appear in light, dull tones.
- **Built-up areas** (concrete, tarmac and roofs) show bluish-grey tones in many composites.

### Reading a false-colour image

In the commonly used "standard" false-colour composite, the near-infrared band is displayed as **red**, the red band as **green**, and the green band as **blue**. As a result:

- healthy vegetation appears **bright red**;
- clear water appears **dark blue or black**;
- towns appear **blue-grey or cyan**;
- bare soil appears in pale or brownish tones.

So a bright red patch in a dry-season image of a dry area suggests irrigated crops or riverine forest, and a fading of red between two dates suggests drought stress or clearing.

## Passive and active sensors

Most satellite cameras are **passive**: they record sunlight reflected from the surface, so they need daylight and clear skies. **Active** sensors such as radar send out their own microwave signal and record what returns; they work at night and through cloud, which makes them valuable during floods and storms.

## Uses and limitations of remote sensing

Uses: mapping land use and urban growth, monitoring deforestation and veld fires, checking crop health and drought, mapping floods after storms, tracking wildlife habitats and updating topographical maps.

Limitations: cloud blocks passive sensors; small features may be smaller than one **pixel** (the image's spatial resolution); images need careful checking on the ground (**ground truthing**); some imagery and software are costly; and the date and season of an image must be known before comparing two images.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling an oblique photograph a map"}
Only vertical photographs have a nearly even scale. On an oblique photograph features in the background look smaller than they are.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking near-infrared is visible"}
Near-infrared is invisible to the eye; a false-colour image *shows* it using a visible colour (usually red).
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading red as fire or bare soil in false colour"}
In a standard false-colour composite, bright red means healthy vegetation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe the landscape in the photograph" answers should be organised by foreground, middle ground and background, and should mention relief, vegetation, land use and settlement.
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions on the spectrum often ask for the order of bands or why vegetation reflects near-infrared. Learn the order and the chlorophyll explanation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ground, oblique (high and low) and vertical aerial photographs give different views; only vertical photos have a nearly even scale.
- Describe photographs by foreground, middle ground and background.
- The electromagnetic spectrum runs from gamma rays to radio waves; visible light is about 400–700 nm, with near-infrared just beyond red.
- Healthy vegetation reflects near-infrared strongly and appears bright red in standard false colour; clear water appears dark.
- Remote sensing monitors land use, crops, floods and forests, but is limited by cloud, resolution and the need for ground truthing.
:::
