## Introduction

A map is a drawing of part of the Earth's surface, seen from directly above and reduced to fit on paper or a screen. Bus drivers, farmers planning a new field, district planners choosing a site for a clinic and soldiers on patrol all rely on maps.

In the examinations a whole section of work is based on a **topographical map extract**. Before you can measure distance, give grid references or describe relief, you must be able to *read* the map: find its title, scale and key, and recognise the symbols on it. This lesson gives you that foundation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Map"}
A scaled, simplified representation of all or part of the Earth's surface drawn on a flat surface, usually as seen from above.
:::

:::definition{term="Topographical map"}
A detailed, large-scale map showing both natural features (relief, rivers, vegetation) and human features (settlements, roads, railways, boundaries) of a small area.
:::

:::definition{term="Conventional (universal) symbols"}
Standard signs, colours and letters used on maps to stand for real features, so that maps can be read by anyone who has the key.
:::

:::definition{term="Key (legend)"}
The panel on a map that explains what each symbol, colour and line style means.
:::

:::definition{term="Sketch map"}
A simple map drawn freehand, not exactly to scale, showing only the features needed for a particular purpose, such as the route from home to school.
:::

## The components of a good map

A map is only useful if the reader can interpret it. Most good maps have the following components.

1. **Title** – says what the map shows and where, for example *Land use around a fictional mission station*.
2. **Frame (border)** – a neat line enclosing the map so that it looks complete.
3. **Key or legend** – explains every symbol used.
4. **Scale** – shows how distance on the map relates to distance on the ground (see the lesson on scale).
5. **North point (direction arrow)** – usually an arrow labelled N, pointing to grid north.
6. **Grid lines** – numbered lines (eastings and northings) that let us give grid references.
7. **Source and date** – who made the map and when, so the reader knows how up to date it is.

![A sketch of a map of a school with its seven components numbered: 1 title, 2 frame, 3 key, 4 scale bar, 5 north arrow, 6 numbered grid lines, 7 source and date, so a reader can interpret the symbols, measure distance and find direction.](media:diagrams/good-map-components.png "Figure: the seven components of a good map, labelled on a map of a fictional school")

:::example{title="Checking a map for its components"}
A Form 1 class draws a map of their school grounds. It shows the classrooms, the sports field and the borehole, and has a title and a key. To make it a *good* map they still need a **north arrow** (so a reader can orient it), a **scale** (so distances can be estimated) and a **frame**. Adding the date tells a future reader when the buildings were mapped.
:::

## Types of map

Maps can be grouped by **scale** and by **purpose**.

| Type | What it shows | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topographical | Relief, drainage, vegetation and human features in detail | A 1:50 000 sheet of a district |
| Political | Countries, provinces, boundaries and capitals | A map of the provinces of Zimbabwe |
| Physical (relief) | Height of the land, mountains, rivers and lakes, often with layer colouring | An atlas map of Africa's relief |
| Economic | Activities such as mining, farming and industry | A map of mining centres along the Great Dyke |
| Thematic (statistical) | One topic shown by shading, dots or lines | A map of population density by district |

**Large-scale** maps (such as 1:25 000 or 1:50 000) cover a *small* area in a lot of detail. **Small-scale** maps (such as 1:1 000 000 in an atlas) cover a *large* area with little detail. This catches many students out: a large scale means the fraction 1/25 000 is a larger number than 1/1 000 000.

## Conventional symbols on topographical maps

Topographical maps use three kinds of symbol:

- **Point symbols** for features at one place: a school, a clinic, a borehole, a dip tank, a church, a trigonometrical station, a spot height.
- **Line symbols** for features that run across the map: roads, railways, rivers, power lines, contours, boundaries.
- **Area symbols** (shading or patterns) for features covering ground: forest, cultivation, swamp, a built-up area, a dam.

Colour also carries meaning, and the same broad rules are followed by most map makers:

| Colour | Usually shows |
| --- | --- |
| Blue | Water: rivers, dams, swamps, boreholes |
| Brown | Relief: contour lines and their heights |
| Green | Vegetation: forest, woodland, plantations |
| Black | Many human features: buildings, railways, boundaries, names |
| Red or orange | Main roads (on many series) |

Letters are also used as abbreviations, for example **Sch** (school), **Cl** (clinic), **PO** (post office), **BH** (borehole) and **Mt** (mount). Always check the key of the map in front of you, because series differ.

:::example{title="Reading symbols in words"}
Imagine a square on a map that contains a short blue line ending in a small circle labelled BH, a black square labelled Sch, a dashed black line and green tree symbols. You would read this as: a **borehole** (water supply), a **school** (a service), a **footpath or track** (minor route) and **woodland** (natural vegetation). From these clues you could suggest that people live nearby and use the borehole for water.
:::

## Drawing a sketch map of the local area

A sketch map does not need to be exact, but it must be **clear and useful**.

1. Decide the purpose, for example *how to get from my home to school*.
2. Draw a frame and put a north arrow in one corner.
3. Draw the main route first, then add only the landmarks a stranger would need: a river crossing, a shop, a church, a road junction.
4. Use simple symbols and explain them in a key.
5. Add a title and an approximate scale, such as *about 1 cm to 200 m*.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing large scale with large area"}
A large-scale map shows a **small** area in **great** detail. An atlas map of the whole of Africa is a small-scale map.
:::

:::mistake{title="Guessing symbols"}
Students sometimes guess that a black square is a house when the key says it is a school or a store. Read the key of the extract you are given before naming a feature.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the north point"}
A map without a north point cannot be turned to match the ground, so directions on it are meaningless. Always add one to sketch maps.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Multiple-choice items in ZIMSEC Paper 1 often ask you to *identify* the feature shown by a symbol at a grid reference, or the component missing from a map. In free-response work, *list* or *name* needs single words; *describe* needs the feature and where it is.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for evidence of a function from a map (for example, that a settlement is a service centre), quote the symbols you can see: *a clinic, two schools and a police station*. Evidence must come from the map, not from general knowledge.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A map is a scaled, simplified view of the Earth's surface seen from above.
- A good map has a title, frame, key, scale, north point, grid and source.
- Maps include topographical, political, physical, economic and thematic types; large-scale maps show small areas in detail.
- Conventional symbols are point, line or area symbols; blue usually means water, brown relief and green vegetation.
- Sketch maps are simplified, freehand maps drawn for one purpose, still with a title, key and north arrow.
:::
