## Introduction

Why are the sugar mills at Triangle and Hippo Valley in the far south-east Lowveld, while most clothing and food factories are in Harare and Bulawayo? Why was a steelworks built at Redcliff, near Kwekwe, in the middle of the country? A factory owner wants the place where **total costs** are lowest and **profits** are highest. That depends on where the raw materials, power, workers and customers are, and on how much it costs to move things between them.

This lesson explains the factors, groups industries by the factor that matters most to them, and applies the ideas to Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Industrial location"}
The place where an industry or factory is sited, chosen to keep costs low and reach markets.
:::

:::definition{term="Raw-material-oriented industry"}
An industry sited close to its raw materials, usually because they are bulky, heavy, perishable or lose much weight in processing.
:::

:::definition{term="Market-oriented industry"}
An industry sited close to its customers, usually because the product is heavier, bulkier, more fragile or more perishable than its inputs, or needs close contact with buyers.
:::

:::definition{term="Footloose industry"}
An industry that is not tied to raw materials or markets and can locate in many places, often because its inputs and products are light and high in value (for example electronics assembly or software).
:::

:::definition{term="Agglomeration"}
The clustering of related industries in one area so that they share services, suppliers, skilled workers and infrastructure (agglomeration economies).
:::

## Physical factors

1. **Raw materials:** heavy, bulky or perishable materials are expensive to transport, so processing happens close to them. Sugar cane begins to lose its sugar soon after cutting, so mills must be near the cane fields.
2. **Power:** early industries were tied to coalfields or water power. Today electricity is carried by the national grid, so power is less of a locating factor, but industries that use huge amounts (smelters) still prefer reliable, cheap supplies.
3. **Water:** industries such as paper making, brewing, textiles and steel use large volumes of water for processing or cooling.
4. **Land and site:** large, flat, cheap land with firm foundations is needed for big works; space for expansion is valuable.

## Human and economic factors

1. **Labour:** the right numbers of workers, with the right skills, at the right wage. Clothing factories need many semi-skilled workers; research firms need highly qualified staff.
2. **Capital:** money for buildings and machinery, from banks, investors or government.
3. **Markets:** places where the products are sold. Large cities are large markets.
4. **Transport:** good road, rail, airport and port links cut the cost of moving inputs and products. **Containerisation** (goods packed in standard steel boxes moved between ship, rail and lorry) has made long-distance transport cheaper.
5. **Government policy:** grants, tax relief, special economic zones and planning controls can attract industry to chosen areas, or keep it out of others.
6. **Technology and communications:** reliable electricity, internet and telephone links allow firms to be managed from a distance and help footloose industries.
7. **Agglomeration and linkages:** firms gain from being near suppliers, customers and similar firms (shared skilled labour, repair services, banks).

:::note
Factors change over time. **Industrial inertia** describes an industry that stays in its original location after the first reasons have gone, because moving would be expensive and skilled workers, buildings and links are already there.
:::

## Classifying industries by their main factor

:::example{title="A diagram in words: the weight-loss idea"}
Imagine two arrows drawn on a page, one long and one short.

1. A sugar mill takes in about ten tonnes of cane to make roughly one tonne of sugar (an approximate figure; it varies with the cane).
2. If the mill were in Harare, all ten tonnes would have to travel there. If it is beside the fields, only the one tonne of sugar travels.
3. So industries whose raw materials **lose a lot of weight** in processing locate near the raw materials.
4. Industries that **gain weight**, such as bottling soft drinks (water is added), locate near the market.
:::

| Type | Why | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Raw-material-oriented | Inputs are heavy, bulky or perishable; weight is lost | Sugar milling, cotton ginning, ore smelting, sawmilling, tea processing |
| Market-oriented | Product is bulkier, fragile, perishable or needs customer contact | Bakeries, soft-drink bottling, furniture, printing |
| Footloose | Light, high-value inputs and products; power from the grid | Electronics assembly, software, call centres |

## Applying the factors to Zimbabwe

:::example{title="Case study: sugar milling in the south-east Lowveld"}
The large mills at **Triangle** and **Hippo Valley** near Chiredzi are a clear raw-material-oriented industry.

1. **Raw material:** sugar cane grows on irrigated estates and outgrower farms around the mills; it is heavy and spoils quickly after cutting.
2. **Water:** irrigation water comes from dams in the Runde and Save basins, such as Lake Mutirikwi (Kyle).
3. **Climate and land:** the hot Lowveld with flat land suits cane when irrigated.
4. **Labour:** the estates and mills employ thousands of workers, many living in company settlements.
5. **Transport:** sugar is moved by rail and road to markets in Zimbabwe and for export.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: heavy industry in the Midlands"}
The steelworks at **Redcliff**, near Kwekwe, was built there because iron ore, limestone and coal could be brought to a central site on the main railway, and the area had flat land, water and workers. It stopped producing steel in the late 2000s, but the Midlands still has ferrochrome smelting at Kwekwe, close to the chrome of the Great Dyke. This shows the pull of raw materials for heavy, bulky inputs.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: Harare as a market-oriented centre"}
Harare has the largest concentration of manufacturing in the country (industrial areas such as Workington, Southerton and Msasa). It is the largest market, the capital (government offices, banks, head offices), the hub of road, rail and air links, and has the biggest pool of skilled and semi-skilled labour. Food processing, drinks, printing, furniture and clothing benefit from being close to customers and to each other: this is **agglomeration**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without linking them to the industry"}
"Labour, market, transport" earns little. Say **why** the factor matters for that industry: "cane loses weight and spoils quickly, so the mill is next to the estates".
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking power still ties all industry to coalfields"}
Electricity is now distributed through the national grid, so most factories can locate anywhere on the grid. Only very heavy power users are strongly attracted to power sources.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B"}
Typical tasks: "Explain the factors that influence the location of a named industry in Zimbabwe." Name the industry and its place, then give three or four factors, each developed with a reason and local detail (Triangle sugar: cane, irrigation water, rail).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 9)"}
You may be given a map or table of a factory site and asked to "suggest reasons" for its location. Use the resource first (near a motorway junction, flat land, edge of town), then add general knowledge. Separate physical from human factors when asked.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Industries locate where total costs are lowest and profits highest.
- Physical factors: raw materials, power, water, land; human factors: labour, capital, markets, transport, government policy, technology and agglomeration.
- Raw-material-oriented industries process heavy, bulky or perishable inputs that lose weight (sugar milling, smelting).
- Market-oriented industries make products that gain weight or must be near customers (bottling, baking); footloose industries can locate almost anywhere.
- In Zimbabwe, sugar milling in the Lowveld follows raw materials, Midlands smelting follows minerals, and Harare's varied industry follows the market and agglomeration.
:::
