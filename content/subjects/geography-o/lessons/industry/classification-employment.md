## Introduction

Think about a single cotton shirt. A farmer in Gokwe grows the cotton. A ginnery separates the lint from the seed, a mill spins and weaves it, and a factory cuts and sews the shirt. A lorry driver delivers it, a shop in Harare sells it, and somewhere a designer or a software engineer may have worked on its pattern or on the shop's payment system. Every one of these people works in **industry**, but in very different kinds of industry.

Geographers sort economic activities into four **sectors**. The share of workers in each sector is a country's **employment structure**, and it tells us a great deal about how developed the country is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Industry"}
Any economic activity that produces goods or provides services in return for payment. In everyday speech "industry" often means factories only, but in geography it covers all four sectors.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary industry"}
Extracting or growing raw materials from the land or sea: farming, mining, quarrying, forestry and fishing.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary industry"}
Manufacturing and processing: turning raw materials or parts into finished goods, for example milling sugar, smelting ferrochrome, making cement or assembling vehicles. Construction is usually counted here too.
:::

:::definition{term="Tertiary industry"}
Providing services to people and businesses: retailing, transport, banking, health, education, tourism and government.
:::

:::definition{term="Quaternary industry"}
Knowledge-based services: research and development, information and communication technology, consultancy and specialist data processing.
:::

:::definition{term="Employment structure"}
The percentage of a country's workers employed in each sector of industry.
:::

## The four sectors with examples

| Sector | What it does | Zimbabwean examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracts raw materials | Maize and tobacco farming, gold and platinum mining, timber plantations in the Eastern Highlands, kapenta fishing on Lake Kariba |
| Secondary | Processes and manufactures | Sugar milling in the Lowveld, ferrochrome smelting at Kwekwe, textiles, food and drink processing, construction |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Shops and markets, buses and railways, banks, hospitals, schools, hotels at Victoria Falls |
| Quaternary | Creates and processes knowledge | University research, agricultural research stations, software and mobile-money development |

:::example{title="A diagram in words: the chain of production"}
Draw four boxes in a row joined by arrows.

1. **Primary:** a farmer grows sugar cane near Chiredzi.
2. **Secondary:** a mill crushes the cane and refines sugar.
3. **Tertiary:** lorries and trains carry bags of sugar to wholesalers and supermarkets, which sell it.
4. **Quaternary:** scientists breed better cane varieties and engineers design the software that tracks deliveries.

The same product passes through every sector. Each step **adds value**, which is why processing raw materials at home (beneficiation) earns a country more than exporting them raw.
:::

## Why each sector matters to Zimbabwe

1. **Primary:** farming employs most rural people and feeds the nation; minerals and tobacco earn most of the country's foreign currency.
2. **Secondary:** manufacturing adds value to raw materials, creates urban jobs, reduces the need for imports and supplies other sectors (fertiliser for farmers, equipment for mines).
3. **Tertiary:** services employ a growing share of urban workers; tourism earns foreign currency; transport and banking allow every other sector to function.
4. **Quaternary:** research improves crop yields and mining methods, and ICT supports mobile banking and online trade.

:::note
The four sectors depend on each other. A drought that cuts the maize and cotton harvest also cuts work for millers, ginners and transporters, and lowers spending in shops.
:::

## Employment structures and development

Employment structure is usually shown as a **pie chart**, a **divided bar** or a **triangular graph** with one axis for each of the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors (quaternary is often included in tertiary).

:::example{title="Comparing three fictional countries (fictional data)"}
| Country | Primary (%) | Secondary (%) | Tertiary and quaternary (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Country P (low-income) | 68 | 9 | 23 |
| Country Q (middle-income) | 30 | 27 | 43 |
| Country R (high-income) | 2 | 20 | 78 |

1. Each row adds up to 100%: for Country Q, 30 + 27 + 43 = 100.
2. Country P has most people in primary jobs, mostly small-scale farming.
3. Country Q has a large secondary sector because factories have grown.
4. Country R has very few farmers, because machines do the work, and most people work in services.
:::

### Low-income countries (LICs)

Most people work in **primary** industry, mainly subsistence and small-scale farming. There is little capital to build factories, many people have limited education, and machinery is scarce, so farming needs many hands.

### Middle-income countries (MICs)

The **secondary** sector grows as factories open, often helped by foreign investment and cheap labour. People move from farms to towns for factory work. Farming becomes more mechanised, so the primary share falls. Services also grow.

### High-income countries (HICs)

The **tertiary** and **quaternary** sectors dominate. Farming and mining are highly mechanised and employ very few people. Manufacturing jobs have fallen because of automation and because many factories have moved to countries with lower labour costs (**deindustrialisation**). Rising incomes create demand for services such as health, leisure and finance.

## The Clark-Fisher idea in outline

The Clark-Fisher model describes how a country's employment structure changes over time as it develops.

1. **Pre-industrial stage:** primary employment is very high; secondary and tertiary are small.
2. **Industrial stage:** secondary employment rises to a peak as manufacturing grows; primary falls quickly as farming is mechanised.
3. **Post-industrial stage:** secondary falls (automation, competition from abroad) while tertiary and quaternary keep rising until they employ most people.

Picture a line graph with time along the bottom: the primary line falls steadily, the secondary line rises and then falls (a hump), and the tertiary line rises throughout.

The model is a useful summary, but it does not fit every country. Some countries move from farming straight into services without a large factory stage, and a country's position depends on government policy, trade and investment, not just on time.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling construction or mining a service"}
Mining is **primary** (extraction). Construction and processing are **secondary**. Only activities that provide a service, such as transport, retail or teaching, are tertiary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying HICs have no primary industry"}
HICs still farm and mine, often producing a great deal, but with machines and very few workers. A small **share** of workers is not the same as small output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading a triangular graph the wrong way"}
On a triangular graph each axis is read along its own gridlines. Check that your three readings add up to 100%.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 Section B"}
Classification items are common in Paper 1: decide what the activity **does** (extracts, makes, serves, researches). In Paper 2, "explain the importance of industry to Zimbabwe" needs developed points: say what the sector does **and** what follows (jobs → wages spent locally; exports → foreign currency for imports).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 9)"}
"Describe how employment structures change" questions usually give a table or triangular graph. Quote figures for at least two countries and use comparative words ("Country R has 66 percentage points fewer primary workers than Country P"). For "explain", link each change to a reason such as mechanisation, industrialisation or rising incomes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Industry is any paid economic activity; it is divided into primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary sectors.
- In Zimbabwe, primary industry (farming and mining) earns most foreign currency, secondary adds value and creates urban jobs, and tertiary services such as tourism and transport support everything else.
- Employment structure is the percentage of workers in each sector.
- LICs are dominated by primary jobs, MICs have growing secondary sectors, HICs are dominated by tertiary and quaternary jobs.
- The Clark-Fisher model shows primary falling, secondary rising then falling, and tertiary rising as a country develops.
:::
