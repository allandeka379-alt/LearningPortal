## Introduction

Some companies are bigger than whole countries. A few earn more each year than the total value of everything Zimbabwe produces. These giant firms, with offices, factories, mines and shops spread across many countries, are **transnational corporations (TNCs)**. They are one of the main drivers of globalisation.

In Zimbabwe, TNCs are involved in platinum and other mining, drinks, tobacco, banking, telecommunications and fuel. Whether they help or harm their **host countries** is one of the big debates in economic geography.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transnational corporation (TNC)"}
A company that operates in more than one country. It is also called a multinational corporation.
:::

:::definition{term="Host country"}
A country in which a TNC sets up operations such as a factory, mine, office or shop.
:::

:::definition{term="Headquarters"}
The main office of a TNC, where top decisions are made, usually in the country where the company began (often an HIC).
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign direct investment (FDI)"}
Investment by a company from one country in factories, mines or other businesses in another country.
:::

:::definition{term="Subcontracting"}
Paying another, independent firm to make products or parts on the TNC's behalf, instead of owning the factory.
:::

## How TNCs are organised

:::example{title="A diagram in words: the layers of a TNC"}
Picture a pyramid.

1. **Top: headquarters** in a major city of the home country (usually an HIC). Top managers make strategic decisions, control finance and marketing, and take most of the profit.
2. **Research and development** centres, usually in HICs near universities and highly skilled workers, design new products.
3. **Regional offices** manage operations in a continent or group of countries.
4. **Production:** factories, assembly plants or mines, often in MICs and LICs where labour and land are cheaper or raw materials are found. Some production is subcontracted.
5. **Sales and distribution** everywhere there are customers.

Skilled, well-paid work tends to stay at the top of the pyramid; lower-paid production work is at the bottom.
:::

## Why TNCs locate where they do

1. **Cheap labour:** wages are much lower in many LICs and MICs, which matters for labour-intensive industries such as clothing and footwear.
2. **Raw materials:** mining TNCs must go where the minerals are, for example platinum on the Great Dyke.
3. **Markets:** being inside a large or fast-growing market cuts transport costs and helps a firm understand local customers.
4. **Avoiding trade barriers:** producing inside a trading bloc avoids the tariffs on imports into it.
5. **Government incentives:** tax holidays, cheap land and special economic zones.
6. **Weaker regulations** on wages, working hours or pollution (a reason that is widely criticised).
7. **Infrastructure and stability:** ports, power, communications and a stable government reduce risk.

## Advantages and disadvantages for host countries

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Jobs, often better paid than local alternatives | Jobs may be low-skilled, low-paid, with long hours; managers often from abroad |
| Investment in infrastructure (roads, power, water, housing) | Profits are sent back to the home country (**leakage**) |
| Skills training and transfer of technology | The TNC may leave suddenly if costs rise elsewhere |
| Tax revenue and export earnings | Tax incentives mean less revenue; TNCs may avoid tax |
| Local firms gain contracts as suppliers (**multiplier effect**) | Local firms may be out-competed |
| Wider range of goods for consumers | Environmental damage: pollution, land degradation, water use |
| | TNCs can have great influence over the host government |

:::example{title="Case study (named TNC): Nike"}
1. **Headquarters:** Beaverton, Oregon, in the USA, where design, research, marketing and finance are controlled.
2. **Production:** Nike owns very few factories. It subcontracts the making of shoes and clothing to independent factories, mainly in Asia: Vietnam, China and Indonesia are the leading producers. Hundreds of thousands of workers make Nike products.
3. **Why there:** low labour costs, large workforces, experienced footwear manufacturers and good container ports.
4. **Sales:** worldwide, through its own shops, other retailers and online.
5. **Benefits to host countries:** factory jobs and wages, export earnings, skills and supplier industries.
6. **Problems:** in the 1990s campaigners exposed low pay, long hours and poor conditions in some supplier factories. Nike responded with a code of conduct for suppliers and factory audits, although critics argue that problems continue. Most of the profit goes to the headquarters and shareholders, not to the producing countries.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: platinum mining TNCs on the Great Dyke"}
Platinum mining in Zimbabwe is dominated by foreign-owned companies. **Zimplats**, majority-owned by the South African company Impala Platinum, mines at Ngezi near Chegutu and processes ore at Selous. **Unki** mine near Shurugwi has been owned by Anglo American Platinum.

- **Advantages:** large investment, jobs, export earnings in foreign currency, taxes and royalties, roads, housing, schools and clinics in mining areas, and contracts for local suppliers.
- **Disadvantages:** much of the ore is exported only partly processed, so value is added abroad; profits leave the country; mining disturbs land and uses water; output depends on world prices and on decisions made outside Zimbabwe.
- The government has encouraged **beneficiation** (more processing, including refining, inside Zimbabwe) so the country keeps more of the value.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking TNCs only harm LICs"}
TNCs bring both advantages and disadvantages. A strong answer weighs both and considers who gains (workers, government, consumers) and who loses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing ownership and subcontracting"}
Many clothing and footwear TNCs do not own the factories that make their goods. They subcontract, which makes it easy to move orders elsewhere.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B"}
"Describe TNCs and discuss their advantages and disadvantages": define a TNC, give a Zimbabwean example, then balance at least two advantages and two disadvantages, each developed.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 9)"}
The detailed specific example must name the TNC **and** the country where it operates. For "evaluate the impacts on host countries", finish with a judgement: do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages, and for whom?
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A TNC operates in more than one country; its headquarters and research are usually in an HIC.
- Production is often in MICs and LICs, owned or subcontracted, for cheap labour, raw materials, markets and incentives.
- Host countries gain jobs, investment, skills, taxes and exports.
- Disadvantages include low pay, profit leakage, sudden closure, environmental damage and political influence.
- Nike (headquarters in Beaverton, USA; production subcontracted in Asia) and platinum miners on the Great Dyke show both sides.
:::
