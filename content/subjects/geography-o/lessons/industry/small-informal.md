## Introduction

Walk through Mbare in Harare and you hear hammers on metal before you see the workshops. At **Siyaso** and **Magaba**, hundreds of small workshops make scotch carts, buckets, window frames, stoves and spare parts, often from scrap metal. In Glen View, rows of carpenters make sofas and wardrobes in the open air. Across the country, people sell vegetables, repair phones, sew uniforms and run tuck-shops.

These **small to medium enterprises (SMEs)** and **informal industries** employ a very large share of Zimbabwe's working people, especially since many large factories closed or cut jobs. This lesson explains why they exist, what they are like, where they are, why they matter, and how workers in all industries can be kept safe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small to medium enterprise (SME)"}
A business with a small number of workers and limited capital and turnover. Small enterprises may be formal (registered) or informal.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Economic activities that are not officially registered or regulated, usually do not pay tax and give workers no formal contracts, pensions or social security.
:::

:::definition{term="Home industry"}
Small-scale production carried out at or near the home, or at a council-designated site for small producers, such as sewing, welding or carpentry.
:::

:::definition{term="Occupational safety and health (OSH)"}
Measures that protect workers from injury and disease caused by their work.
:::

## Causes of the growth of SMEs and the informal sector

1. **Job losses in the formal sector:** retrenchments under the Economic Structural Adjustment Programme (ESAP) in the 1990s, and factory closures during later economic decline, pushed many people into self-employment.
2. **Rapid urban population growth:** rural-urban migration and natural increase produce more job seekers than formal jobs.
3. **School leavers** with few formal job openings.
4. **Low entry costs:** many activities need little capital, simple tools and no licence.
5. **Demand for cheap goods and services** from low-income households.
6. **Government and NGO support** through training, small loans (for example from the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Corporation, SMEDCO) and designated sites.
7. **Extra income:** formal workers whose wages are low run small businesses on the side.

## Characteristics

| Feature | SMEs and informal industries |
|---|---|
| Size | Few workers, often family members or apprentices |
| Capital | Little; simple, often second-hand or home-made tools |
| Methods | Labour-intensive; skills learned on the job |
| Raw materials | Often recycled (scrap metal, off-cuts, used tyres) |
| Registration | Many unregistered; no tax paid; no formal contracts |
| Premises | Open-air sites, backyards, market stalls, roadsides |
| Income | Irregular and often low |
| Safety | Usually no protective clothing, guards or first aid |

## Location

:::example{title="A diagram in words: where informal industry clusters in a city"}
Imagine a map of Harare from the city centre outwards.

1. **Around bus termini and markets** (for example Mbare Musika): vendors, food sellers and repairers, because thousands of people pass by each day.
2. **In high-density suburbs** such as Mbare, Glen View and Highfield: clusters of metalwork, furniture making and building materials, where workers live and land is cheaper.
3. **Along busy roads and at road junctions**: car repairs, tyre fitting and stalls that catch passing trade.
4. **At designated home-industry sites** set aside by councils in many towns.
5. **In rural service centres and growth points**: grinding mills, welding, tailoring and bicycle repairs.

Informal industries locate where **customers** are many, **rents** are low or nil, and **raw materials** (scrap, timber off-cuts) can be found.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: the Siyaso and Magaba clusters, Mbare"}
In Mbare, Harare's oldest high-density suburb, large clusters of small workshops make and repair metal goods: scotch carts, ploughs, stoves, buckets, burglar bars and spare parts. Raw material is mainly scrap metal bought from collectors. Workers learn on the job. Products are sold to urban customers and to traders who take them to rural areas. The clusters show **agglomeration** at a small scale: workshops share customers, tools, suppliers and skills.
:::

## The role of informal industries in Zimbabwe

1. **Employment and income** for millions of people who would otherwise have no work.
2. **Cheap goods and services** for low-income households, such as furniture, clothing and repairs.
3. **Skills training** through apprenticeship.
4. **Recycling** of scrap metal, tyres and plastics, reducing waste.
5. **Import substitution:** locally made goods replace some imports.
6. **Links with the formal sector:** informal firms buy from and supply formal firms; successful SMEs can grow into formal companies.

### Problems facing the sector

- Little access to **credit**, because there is no collateral.
- Lack of **secure premises**, power and water; some workshops are demolished or moved.
- **Poor quality** and competition from cheap imports.
- **No social security** or safety protection for workers.
- **Lost tax revenue** for government, and congestion and waste in towns.

Possible solutions include providing serviced work sites, simple registration, microfinance, training in business skills and quality, and linking SMEs to large firms as suppliers.

## Occupational safety and health

Every industry has hazards. In a welding workshop: burns, eye damage from the arc, fumes and electric shock. In a sawmill: cuts and noise. In a mine: dust and rock falls. In a textile mill: noise and cotton dust.

| Measure | Example |
|---|---|
| Personal protective equipment (PPE) | Welding masks, goggles, gloves, overalls, safety boots, ear protectors, dust masks |
| Safe machines | Guards on saws and moving parts; regular maintenance |
| Safe workplace | Ventilation, fire extinguishers, clear exits, first-aid kits, warning signs |
| Training | Safe working methods, handling chemicals, first aid |
| Rules and inspection | Health and safety laws, workplace inspections, safety committees and officers |
| Compensation | Insurance for injured workers through the National Social Security Authority (NSSA) |

In Zimbabwe, NSSA promotes occupational safety and health and runs workers' compensation for registered employers, and laws such as the Factories and Works Act set standards for workplaces. Informal workers are often outside these systems, so awareness campaigns and cheap PPE are especially important for them.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the informal sector has no value"}
Being unregistered does not mean unimportant. The informal sector feeds families, trains workers and supplies cheap goods. Discuss both its role and its problems.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing PPE as the only safety measure"}
PPE is the last line of defence. Good answers also mention guarding machines, training, ventilation, inspections and first aid.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B"}
"Describe the characteristics" wants features (size, capital, tools, registration). "Discuss the role" needs benefits **and** problems. For safety questions, link each measure to a hazard: "goggles protect welders' eyes from sparks".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SMEs and informal industries are small, labour-intensive, low-capital businesses, many unregistered.
- They grew because of formal job losses, urban population growth, low entry costs and demand for cheap goods.
- They cluster near markets, bus termini, high-density suburbs and designated home-industry sites (Siyaso, Magaba, Glen View).
- They provide jobs, income, cheap goods, training and recycling, but lack credit, premises and social security.
- Safety measures include PPE, machine guards, ventilation, training, inspections and compensation through NSSA.
:::
