## Introduction

Look at the labels in a Zimbabwean kitchen: mealie-meal, cooking oil, sugar, tea, matches, soap. Many of these are made in Zimbabwe from Zimbabwean raw materials. Manufacturing and processing turn the products of farms, forests and mines into goods of higher value, provide jobs in towns and save the country money it would otherwise spend on imports.

This lesson describes **where** these industries are, gives examples of the main types, and examines the **problems** facing manufacturing and how they might be solved.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Manufacturing industry"}
Secondary industry that makes finished goods from raw materials or parts, for example clothing, furniture, fertiliser or processed food.
:::

:::definition{term="Processing industry"}
Industry that changes a raw material into a more useful or valuable form, often as a first step: milling grain, ginning cotton, crushing cane, smelting ore.
:::

:::definition{term="Agro-processing"}
Processing agricultural products (crops, livestock, timber) into food, drink, fibre and other goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiation"}
Adding value to a raw material, especially a mineral, inside the country before it is exported.
:::

:::definition{term="Import substitution"}
Making goods at home that were previously imported, to save foreign currency and create jobs.
:::

## Distribution of industry

:::example{title="A diagram in words: Zimbabwe's industrial map"}
Imagine an outline map of Zimbabwe with the main railway from Harare south-west to Bulawayo.

1. **Harare** (north-east end of the line) has the largest and most varied group of industries, in industrial areas such as Workington, Southerton, Graniteside and Msasa.
2. **Bulawayo** (south-west end) is the second centre, historically known for heavy engineering, clothing, food processing and the headquarters and workshops of the National Railways of Zimbabwe.
3. **The Midlands** towns between them, Kadoma, Kwekwe and Gweru, are on the same railway and close to the Great Dyke: metal and mineral processing (ferrochrome smelting, steel in the past), fertiliser, textiles and footwear.
4. **Mutare** in the east has timber processing (sawmills and board making from Eastern Highlands plantations) and food processing, and lies on the route to the port of Beira.
5. **The south-east Lowveld** around Chiredzi has the sugar mills at Triangle and Hippo Valley.

The pattern is **uneven**: most manufacturing is concentrated along the Harare–Bulawayo railway, while large parts of the country, especially the communal lands of the north, west and south, have very little.
:::

Reasons for the concentration along the line of rail:

1. The railway and main roads carry raw materials and finished goods cheaply.
2. The largest towns provide markets, workers, banks and services.
3. Electricity and water supplies were developed first in these areas.
4. Minerals of the Great Dyke and the greenstone belts are close by.
5. Once industries were established, **agglomeration** attracted more.

## Types of manufacturing and processing

### Agro-processing

| Industry | Raw material | Examples of location |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar milling (and ethanol) | Irrigated sugar cane | Triangle and Hippo Valley near Chiredzi |
| Cotton ginning, spinning and textiles | Cotton from small-scale farmers | Ginneries in cotton-growing districts such as Gokwe; textile mills in towns such as Kadoma |
| Grain milling | Maize and wheat | Harare, Bulawayo and most large towns |
| Tea and coffee processing | Tea and coffee estates | Eastern Highlands (Chipinge, Honde Valley) |
| Meat and dairy processing | Cattle and milk | Abattoirs in Bulawayo and Harare; dairies near large towns |
| Timber processing | Pine and eucalyptus plantations | Mutare and the Eastern Highlands |
| Tobacco processing | Flue-cured tobacco | Harare (auction floors and processors) |

:::example{title="Case study: sugar at Triangle and Hippo Valley"}
Cane from irrigated estates and outgrower farms is crushed in the mills, and the juice is boiled and refined into sugar. Molasses, a by-product, is used to make **ethanol** at Triangle, which can be blended with petrol. The industry shows strong **linkages**: it buys irrigation water, fertiliser and transport, employs thousands of people, and supports schools, clinics and shops in the estate settlements.
:::

### Metal and mineral processing

1. **Ferrochrome smelting** in the Midlands, for example at Kwekwe, uses chrome ore from the Great Dyke.
2. **Platinum** ore from the Great Dyke is crushed and concentrated near the mines before smelting.
3. **Gold** is refined in Harare.
4. **Cement** is made from local limestone at several works.
5. **Iron and steel** were made at Redcliff, near Kwekwe, until production stopped in the late 2000s.

## Importance of manufacturing

1. **Adds value** to raw materials, so exports earn more (ferrochrome rather than chrome ore; refined sugar rather than cane).
2. **Creates jobs** in towns, and the wages support shops, transport and housing.
3. **Saves foreign currency** through import substitution (local fertiliser, cooking oil, building materials).
4. **Linkages:** supplies other sectors (fertiliser and packaging for farms, equipment for mines) and buys from them.
5. **Government revenue** from taxes, used for services.

## Problems facing manufacturing

1. **Power shortages:** load-shedding cuts production when Kariba South (hydro) has low water or the ageing units at Hwange break down.
2. **Shortage of foreign currency** to import machinery, spare parts and some raw materials.
3. **Obsolete machinery:** much equipment is old, so costs are high and quality falls.
4. **Competition from imports:** cheaper goods from South Africa and Asia undercut local products, especially clothing and food.
5. **High transport costs:** Zimbabwe is landlocked; road and rail links to ports such as Beira and Durban are long and the railway has declined.
6. **Loss of skilled workers** who emigrate for better pay.
7. **Limited and expensive credit**, and unstable prices, make planning and investment difficult.

## Possible solutions

| Problem | Possible solutions |
|---|---|
| Power shortages | Expand and repair power stations; independent solar projects; energy efficiency; factories installing their own solar |
| Foreign currency | Increase exports (especially processed goods); export retention schemes; foreign investment |
| Obsolete machinery | Long-term loans or credit lines for retooling; joint ventures with foreign firms |
| Cheap imports | Import duties on competing goods; "buy local" campaigns; improve quality and efficiency |
| Transport | Rehabilitate the railway; improve roads and border posts |
| Investment | Special economic zones with tax incentives; stable economic policy |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing distribution with a list of towns"}
Saying "Harare, Bulawayo, Gweru" is not a description. Say where industry is **concentrated** (along the Harare–Bulawayo line of rail), where it is **sparse**, and name examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague solutions"}
"The government should help" earns nothing. Say **how**: "provide long-term loans so firms can replace old machinery".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B"}
Questions often give a map or table of industrial centres and ask you to "describe the distribution", "explain the importance" or "suggest solutions to the problems". Match each solution to a specific problem, and use named examples such as sugar milling at Triangle or ferrochrome at Kwekwe.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Most of Zimbabwe's manufacturing is concentrated along the Harare–Bulawayo railway, with Harare the largest centre.
- Agro-processing includes sugar milling (Triangle, Hippo Valley), cotton ginning and textiles, grain milling, tea, meat and timber processing.
- Mineral processing includes ferrochrome smelting in the Midlands and gold refining.
- Manufacturing adds value, creates jobs, saves foreign currency and links with other sectors.
- Problems: power cuts, foreign-currency shortages, old machinery, cheap imports, transport costs, skills loss; solutions must target each problem.
:::
