## Introduction

Almost all of Zimbabwe's electricity comes from two places: the Kariba South hydro-electric power station on the Zambezi and the Hwange coal-fired power station in the north-west. When Lake Kariba is low after a drought, or when old generating units at Hwange break down, the whole country feels it through load shedding. This lesson explains how stations generate electricity and why they are built where they are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Power station"}
A plant that converts a source of energy (falling water, heat from burning coal, sunlight, wind) into electricity.
:::

:::definition{term="Turbine"}
A wheel with blades that is turned by moving water, steam, gas or wind. It drives a generator.
:::

:::definition{term="Generator"}
A machine in which a coil turning inside a magnetic field produces electricity.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal power station"}
A power station that uses heat, usually from burning coal, oil or gas or from nuclear fission or hot rocks, to turn water into steam that drives turbines.
:::

:::definition{term="National grid"}
The network of high-voltage transmission lines and substations that carries electricity from power stations to towns, mines and farms.
:::

:::definition{term="Installed capacity"}
The maximum output a power station can produce, measured in megawatts (MW). Actual output is often lower.
:::

## How electricity is generated

Most stations work on one principle: something turns a turbine, which turns a generator.

**Hydro-electric power (HEP).** A dam stores water in a reservoir. Imagine a cross-section through the dam: water enters intakes near the base of the reservoir, falls through large pipes called penstocks and strikes the blades of turbines in the power house. The turbines drive generators, transformers raise the voltage for the grid, and the water returns to the river. Output depends on the **height the water falls** (the head) and the **volume of water** released.

**Coal-fired thermal power.** Coal is crushed to a fine powder and burned in a boiler. The heat turns water in the boiler tubes into high-pressure steam, which spins the turbines. The steam is then cooled back into water in a condenser, using large amounts of cooling water (often cooled again in cooling towers).

**Gas-fired power.** Burning gas drives a gas turbine directly.

**Geothermal power.** Steam from wells drilled into hot rocks drives turbines, as at Olkaria in Kenya.

**Nuclear power.** In a reactor, uranium atoms split (fission), releasing heat that turns water into steam to drive turbines. Koeberg near Cape Town is Africa's only nuclear power station.

**Solar power.** Photovoltaic (PV) panels turn sunlight directly into electricity (direct current), and inverters change it to alternating current for the grid. 

**Wind power.** Wind turns the blades of a turbine on a tall tower. The blades turn a generator in the housing at the top. Wind farms group many turbines together.

## Factors that influence the siting of power stations

| Type | Main siting factors |
| --- | --- |
| HEP | A large river with a reliable flow; a narrow, deep gorge so that a short dam holds back a large reservoir; hard, impermeable rock for strong foundations and little leakage; a wide basin upstream to store water; few people to resettle; access to the grid and to markets |
| Coal-fired | Close to a coalfield, because coal is bulky and costly to transport; a large, reliable supply of cooling water; large areas of flat, stable land for the station, coal stockpiles and ash ponds; rail links; away from large settlements because of pollution |
| Nuclear | Huge volumes of cooling water (coasts or large rivers); stable rock away from earthquake zones; far from large towns for safety; flat land |
| Geothermal | Hot rocks near the surface in volcanic or rift areas |
| Solar | High sunshine all year, little cloud; cheap flat land; close to the grid |
| Wind | Strong, steady winds on coasts, plains or exposed high ground; open land; close to the grid |

:::example{title="Case study: the Kariba hydro-electric scheme"}
- **Site.** The Kariba Dam was built across the Kariba Gorge on the Zambezi and completed in 1959. The narrow gorge and solid rock allowed a relatively short dam wall to hold back a huge reservoir, Lake Kariba, roughly 280 km long, one of the largest artificial lakes in the world by volume. The Zambezi's large catchment provides the water.
- **Power.** Kariba South power station on the Zimbabwean bank and Kariba North Bank on the Zambian side share the water. Kariba South was extended with two new generating units, raising its installed capacity to about 1,050 MW. The dam is managed by the Zambezi River Authority, owned jointly by Zimbabwe and Zambia.
- **Benefits.** Cheap electricity without smoke; a commercial kapenta fishery; tourism at Kariba town and on the lake (houseboats, wildlife viewing); water transport.
- **Costs.** Tens of thousands of Tonga people living along the valley were moved to drier, less fertile land, and many feel they never shared the benefits. Large areas of woodland and wildlife habitat were flooded (Operation Noah rescued thousands of animals). Filling the lake was followed by a series of earthquakes, a well-known example of reservoir-induced seismicity. Water falling over the spillways scoured a deep plunge pool below the wall, which engineers have had to reshape.
- **Weakness.** Output depends on the lake level. In drought years such as 2015-2016, 2019 and 2022-2024, water allowed for generation was cut and output fell sharply, causing long power cuts.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: Hwange thermal power station"}
- **Site.** Built next to the Hwange coalfield in north-western Zimbabwe, so coal reaches the boilers by conveyor and short rail links rather than by long-distance transport. Cooling water is piped from the Zambezi. The Bulawayo to Victoria Falls railway passes the town, and transmission lines link the station to the grid.
- **Power.** The original six units (installed capacity about 900 MW) were joined by two larger new units in the early 2020s.
- **Advantages.** A local fuel, so no foreign currency is spent on importing it; output does not depend on rainfall.
- **Problems.** Smoke, dust and sulphur dioxide; large carbon dioxide emissions; ash disposal; coal mining scars the land.
:::

Zimbabwe also has three small, old coal-fired stations at Harare, Bulawayo and Munyati, which produce little power today. Solar farms built by independent power producers are adding to supply.

:::example{title="Worked example: output in a drought (fictional data)"}
A fictional HEP station has an installed capacity of 1,000 MW. During a drought its output falls to 300 MW.

- Fall in output = 1,000 − 300 = **700 MW**.
- Percentage fall = 700 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **70 %**.
- Output as a percentage of capacity = 300 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = **30 %**.
:::

## Environmental impact of generating electricity

| Impact | Main cause | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air pollution | Smoke, dust and sulphur dioxide from coal | Respiratory illness; acid rain damages vegetation, soils and buildings |
| Climate change | Carbon dioxide from burning coal, oil and gas | Rising temperatures, more droughts and floods |
| Land degradation | Coal mining, ash dumps, clearing land | Scarred land, polluted soil and water |
| Thermal and water pollution | Warm cooling water and ash ponds | Less oxygen in rivers, harm to fish |
| Flooding of land | HEP reservoirs | Displaced people, lost farmland, woodland and habitats |
| Deforestation | Cutting fuelwood where electricity is not available | Soil erosion, siltation, loss of biodiversity |
| Radioactive waste | Nuclear stations | Must be stored safely for thousands of years |

**Measures to reduce the impacts:**

- **Cleaner technology at thermal stations**: filters (electrostatic precipitators) to remove dust, scrubbers to remove sulphur dioxide, and tall chimneys to disperse what remains.
- **Cleaner energy**: more solar, HEP and other renewables in the energy mix.
- **Reforestation and woodlots** to replace trees cut for fuel and to absorb carbon dioxide.
- **Rehabilitation** of mined land and ash dumps by levelling, covering with soil and planting.
- **Environmental impact assessment (EIA)** before building a station, with fair compensation and resettlement for displaced people.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying HEP stations burn something"}
HEP uses the energy of falling water. Only thermal stations burn fuel to make steam.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing siting factors without linking them to the type of station"}
"Water supply" means a reliable river flow for HEP but cooling water for a coal or nuclear station. Say which.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Most power stations use a turbine to drive a generator; HEP uses falling water, thermal stations use steam from burning coal, gas, nuclear fission or hot rocks, and wind turbines use moving air; solar PV makes electricity directly.
- HEP needs a reliable river, a narrow gorge and hard rock; coal stations need a coalfield, cooling water and flat land; nuclear stations need cooling water and stable ground away from towns.
- Kariba South (HEP, completed 1959 on the Zambezi) and Hwange (coal) supply most of Zimbabwe's electricity; Kariba's output falls in droughts.
- Generation causes air pollution, climate change, land degradation and displacement; filters, scrubbers, renewables, reforestation, rehabilitation and EIAs reduce the damage.
:::
