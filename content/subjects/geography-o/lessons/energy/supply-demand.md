## Introduction

A family in a high-income country may own two cars, a fridge, a freezer, a washing machine, air conditioning and several computers. A family in a rural village in a low-income country may cook on a wood fire and light the house with a single solar lamp. Multiply these differences by billions of people and you have the global pattern of energy use: very uneven, and growing fast as more countries develop. This lesson explains why demand keeps rising, where energy is produced and used, and why countries worry about the security of their supply.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Energy supply"}
The amount of energy a country produces or obtains from its own resources and imports.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy demand (consumption)"}
The amount of energy used by households, transport, industry, agriculture and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy surplus"}
When a country produces more energy than it consumes, so it can export the rest.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy deficit"}
When a country consumes more energy than it produces, so it must import the difference or go without.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy security"}
Having reliable and uninterrupted access to enough energy at an affordable price.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy mix"}
The combination of different sources (coal, oil, gas, nuclear, HEP, solar and others) that a country uses.
:::

## Why global energy production and consumption are increasing

1. **Population growth.** The world population passed 8 billion in 2022. More people need more energy for cooking, lighting and transport.
2. **Economic development.** As countries industrialise, factories, mines and offices use more energy. The rapid growth of China and India since the 1990s has added enormously to world demand.
3. **Rising living standards.** Higher incomes mean more cars, appliances, air conditioning, travel and consumer goods, all of which use energy to make and to run.
4. **Urbanisation.** City dwellers usually use more commercial energy (electricity, gas and fuel) than rural people, who may rely on fuelwood.
5. **Technology.** New devices (computers, mobile phones and data centres) all need electricity.
6. **More production.** New reserves are found and new techniques (deep-sea drilling, fracking of shale rock) make more oil and gas available, while the cost of solar panels and wind turbines has fallen sharply, adding new supply.

## Global patterns of surplus and deficit

| Energy surplus (net exporters) | Energy deficit (net importers) |
| --- | --- |
| Countries of the Middle East such as Saudi Arabia and Qatar (oil and gas) | Japan and South Korea (few fossil-fuel resources, large industries) |
| Russia (oil, gas and coal) | Many countries in Europe, such as Germany and Italy |
| Norway (oil and gas from the North Sea) | India and China, which now import large amounts of oil, gas and coal despite their own coal reserves |
| Australia (coal and liquefied natural gas) | Many small and land-locked LICs with no fossil fuels |
| Nigeria and Angola (oil) | |

The pattern depends on **resources** (where fossil fuels occur), **population size** and **level of development** (how much energy each person uses). A country can have large reserves but still be in deficit if its demand is very high, as with China.

:::example{title="Worked example: surplus or deficit? (fictional data)"}
Table 1 shows energy production and consumption for three fictional countries, in million tonnes of oil equivalent (Mtoe).

| Country | Production (Mtoe) | Consumption (Mtoe) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 450 | 180 |
| B | 60 | 210 |
| C | 25 | 24 |

- Country A: 450 − 180 = **+270 Mtoe, a large surplus**; it is a major exporter.
- Country B: 60 − 210 = **−150 Mtoe, a deficit**; it must import 150 Mtoe, which is 150 ÷ 210 × 100 = **71.4 %** of the energy it uses.
- Country C is almost balanced (+1 Mtoe).
:::

## Energy security and why it matters

A country is **energy secure** when supply is reliable, enough for its needs and affordable. It is **energy insecure** when supplies are uncertain, too small or too costly.

**Threats to energy security:**

- **Dependence on imports**, especially from a few suppliers. Japan imports almost all of its oil and gas.
- **Political conflict.** After the war in Ukraine began in 2022, gas supplies from Russia to Europe were cut and prices rose steeply.
- **Price changes** on world markets, which hit importing countries hard.
- **Climate and natural hazards.** Droughts cut HEP output (as at Kariba in Zambia and Zimbabwe); storms and floods damage power lines and refineries.
- **Ageing or poorly maintained infrastructure**, causing breakdowns and power cuts.
- **Running out** of domestic reserves.

**Why energy security matters:**

- **The economy.** Factories, mines and farms cannot produce without reliable power; power cuts raise costs and discourage investment.
- **Quality of life.** Hospitals, schools, water pumping and refrigeration of food and medicines all depend on energy.
- **Costs.** Importing energy uses foreign currency and leaves a country exposed to price shocks.
- **Political independence.** Countries that depend on one supplier may be put under pressure by it.

Countries improve energy security by **diversifying** their energy mix and suppliers, developing their own renewables, storing fuel, building interconnectors with neighbours and using energy more efficiently.

## Why energy use varies between countries

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Level of development | HICs use far more energy per person than LICs: more industry, transport, appliances and services |
| Climate | Cold countries use energy for heating, and hot humid ones increasingly for air conditioning |
| Resources | Countries with cheap oil, gas or coal of their own often use more energy per person |
| Population size | Large populations mean large total demand, even if use per person is low |
| Economic structure | Countries with heavy industry (steel, aluminium, chemicals) use more energy than those based on farming |

In many LICs most energy still comes from **fuelwood and charcoal**, burned for cooking. Roughly 600 million people in sub-Saharan Africa have no access to electricity (an approximate recent estimate).

## Why energy use varies within countries

- **Urban and rural.** Towns are connected to the grid and have more businesses; many rural areas are not connected, so households use fuelwood, paraffin or small solar systems.
- **Income.** Richer households can afford electricity, gas and appliances; poorer ones use cheaper fuels.
- **Industrial regions** (mining towns, industrial cities) use far more energy than farming regions.

The **energy ladder** describes how households move from wood and dung, to charcoal and paraffin, then to LPG and electricity as their incomes rise and supplies become available.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing total use with use per person"}
China uses more energy in total than any other country, but energy use per person is much higher in many HICs. Read the units of any table carefully.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking that having reserves means a surplus"}
A country with large reserves can still be in deficit if its demand is larger than its production.
:::

:::mistake{title="Defining energy security as having renewable energy"}
Energy security is about reliable, sufficient and affordable supply. Renewables can help, but a country using imported solar panels and local coal can be secure or insecure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 2 often gives a map or table of energy surplus and deficit. "Describe the pattern" needs named regions or countries and figures; "explain" needs reasons (resources, development, population).
:::

:::exam-tip
Use HIC, MIC and LIC when comparing energy use, and always say whether figures are totals or per person.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- World energy demand is rising because of population growth, industrialisation, rising living standards, urbanisation and new technology; new reserves and cheaper renewables increase supply.
- Surplus countries (Middle East, Russia, Norway, Australia) export energy; deficit countries (Japan, South Korea, much of Europe, India, China) import it.
- Energy security means reliable, sufficient, affordable supply; it is threatened by import dependence, conflict, price changes, hazards and poor infrastructure.
- HICs use much more energy per person than LICs; within countries, urban and richer households use more commercial energy than rural and poorer ones.
:::
