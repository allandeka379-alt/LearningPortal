## Introduction

Every country faces the same two questions about energy: how can we get enough of it, and how can we use less of what we have? Zimbabwe answers both every day. Load shedding schedules, prepaid electricity meters, solar panels on rooftops, families cooking with biogas and new generating units at Hwange are all part of managing energy. Good management makes supply more reliable and affordable while reducing damage to the environment, so that future generations still have the energy they need.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Energy management"}
Planning how energy is supplied and used so that it is reliable, affordable and does as little damage to the environment as possible.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy conservation"}
Reducing the amount of energy used, by avoiding waste or by using it more efficiently.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy efficiency"}
Getting the same result (light, heat, transport) from less energy, for example an LED bulb giving the same light as an old bulb while using far less electricity.
:::

:::definition{term="Interconnector"}
A high-voltage power line that links the grids of two or more countries so that electricity can be traded between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable energy"}
Energy supplied and used in a way that meets today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs: renewable, low-polluting and affordable.
:::

## Strategies to increase energy supply

| Strategy | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Find and develop new reserves | Exploration for oil and gas; new coal mines; fracking of shale rock | Uses local resources; jobs and export income | Non-renewable; emissions; damage to land and water |
| Build or expand power stations | New generating units at existing stations | Large, reliable addition to supply | Very costly; long building time; pollution if coal-fired |
| Develop renewables | Solar farms, wind farms, new HEP dams, geothermal wells, biogas | Will not run out; low emissions; costs of solar and wind have fallen sharply | Output varies with weather; dams displace people; storage is costly |
| Import energy | Fuel imports; buying electricity from neighbours | Quick to arrange | Uses foreign currency; depends on suppliers |
| Build interconnectors | Regional power pools such as the Southern African Power Pool (SAPP) | Countries share surpluses and cover each other's shortages | Neighbours may be short at the same time (for example in a regional drought) |
| Nuclear power | New nuclear stations | Large, reliable, low-carbon supply | Very high cost; radioactive waste; safety concerns |

## Strategies to conserve energy

- **Efficient lighting and appliances**: LED bulbs, efficient fridges and motors.
- **Insulation and building design**: keeping heat in or out so less heating or cooling is needed; solar water heaters instead of electric geysers.
- **Public transport and car sharing**: one bus carries as many people as dozens of cars.
- **Efficient wood stoves**: enclosed stoves burn wood more completely, using much less wood than an open three-stone fire and producing less smoke.
- **Biogas digesters**: turn dung and food waste into cooking gas, saving fuelwood.
- **Pricing and metering**: prepaid meters and higher prices for heavy users encourage people to switch off what they are not using.
- **Reducing waste in industry**: recovering waste heat, maintaining machines, recycling (making aluminium from scrap uses far less energy than from ore).
- **Woodlots and tree planting**: replacing trees cut for fuel so that fuelwood remains renewable.

:::example{title="Worked example: savings from LED bulbs (fictional data)"}
A school replaces 50 bulbs of 60 W with 50 LED bulbs of 9 W. The lights are on for 6 hours a day on 200 school days.

- Power saved = 50 × (60 − 9) = 50 × 51 = 2,550 W = **2.55 kW**.
- Energy saved per day = 2.55 kW × 6 h = **15.3 kWh**.
- Energy saved per year = 15.3 × 200 = **3,060 kWh**.
- Percentage saving = 51 ÷ 60 × 100 = **85 %**.
:::

## Evaluating strategies

When you evaluate a strategy, judge it against clear criteria:

1. **Sustainability**: will it last, and does it limit pollution and emissions?
2. **Cost**: to build, to run and to the people who use it.
3. **Reliability**: does it work day and night, in wet and dry years?
4. **Scale**: how much energy does it supply or save?
5. **Who benefits and who loses**: towns or rural areas, rich or poor, people displaced by a dam.

Renewables and conservation usually score best on sustainability; coal and new oil and gas score well on reliability but badly on emissions. Most countries therefore aim for a **diverse energy mix** with a rising share of renewables, plus conservation.

## Energy policies

Governments shape energy use through laws, targets and incentives: setting targets for renewable energy, allowing private companies (independent power producers) to build and sell power, setting electricity tariffs, setting standards for efficient appliances, funding rural electrification and promoting clean cooking.

:::example{title="Case study: Zimbabwe's energy mix and its management"}
**The energy mix.**

- **Electricity** comes mainly from Kariba South (HEP, about 1,050 MW installed) and Hwange (coal-fired, expanded with two new units in the early 2020s). Three small old coal stations at Harare, Bulawayo and Munyati add little. Solar farms built by independent power producers, and rooftop solar on homes and businesses, are growing quickly. Zimbabwe also imports electricity from neighbours such as Mozambique and South Africa through the Southern African Power Pool.
- **Liquid fuels** (petrol, diesel, paraffin, jet fuel) are all imported, much of it through the pipeline from Beira in Mozambique to Feruka near Mutare. Ethanol made from sugar cane at Chisumbanje in the south-east is blended with petrol.
- **Fuelwood** is still the main cooking fuel for most rural households, and wood is also used to cure tobacco.

**Impacts.**

- In drought years (2015-2016, 2019 and 2022-2024) low water in Lake Kariba cut HEP output, causing long load shedding that hurt industry, mining, farming and households.
- Hwange's ageing units often broke down; burning coal pollutes the air and emits carbon dioxide.
- Cutting trees for fuelwood and tobacco curing contributes to deforestation and soil erosion.
- Fuel imports use scarce foreign currency.

**Management.**

- **Increasing supply**: extending Kariba South and adding units at Hwange; encouraging solar independent power producers; imports through SAPP; a planned joint HEP scheme with Zambia at Batoka Gorge, downstream of Victoria Falls.
- **Rural electrification**: the Rural Electrification Agency connects schools, clinics and business centres, and solar systems serve places far from the grid.
- **Conservation**: prepaid meters, a switch to LED lighting, solar water heaters, and campaigns to save power at peak times.
- **Clean cooking and woodlands**: promotion of biogas digesters and efficient stoves, woodlots and tree planting.
- **Regulation**: the Zimbabwe Energy Regulatory Authority (ZERA) licenses producers and regulates fuel and electricity prices.

**Evaluation.** Expanding Hwange makes supply less dependent on rainfall but increases emissions. Solar suits Zimbabwe's sunny climate and is quick to build, but needs storage for evening demand. Imports help in emergencies, but neighbours are often short of power at the same time during regional droughts. Conservation is the cheapest way to "find" more energy, but depends on households and firms being able to afford efficient equipment.
:::

## Energy in the local area (ZIMSEC project)

ZIMSEC asks you to investigate the energy used and conserved in your own area. A simple project could: survey 20 households about their main fuels for cooking and lighting and the costs; count the solar panels, biogas digesters or efficient stoves in a ward; record how load shedding affects local businesses; and suggest conservation measures. Present results as tables and pie charts, and compare your area with the national picture.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up conservation and increasing supply"}
Building a solar farm increases supply; fitting LED bulbs conserves energy. Exam questions often ask for one or the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaluating without criteria"}
"Solar is good" is not an evaluation. Say why (renewable, cheap to run, suits a sunny climate) and what limits it (night, cost of batteries).
:::

:::mistake{title="A case study without detail"}
Name the stations, the sources and at least one specific problem and management measure for your chosen country.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge "detailed specific example" questions want the energy mix, impacts and management of one named country, with real place names. Zimbabwe works well: Kariba, Hwange, imports through SAPP, fuelwood and solar.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply can be increased by new reserves, new power stations, renewables, imports and interconnectors; each has costs and limits.
- Energy can be conserved through efficient lighting and appliances, insulation, public transport, efficient stoves, biogas, pricing and woodlots.
- Strategies are evaluated by sustainability, cost, reliability, scale and who gains or loses.
- Zimbabwe relies on Kariba HEP, Hwange coal, imported fuel and power, and rural fuelwood, and manages its energy by expanding stations, encouraging solar, rural electrification, conservation and regulation.
:::
