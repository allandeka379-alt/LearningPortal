## Introduction

When the lights go off during load shedding, a whole town feels it: welding shops stop, irrigation pumps fall silent, milk spoils in fridges and pupils revise by candlelight. Energy powers almost every economic activity. In Zimbabwe, electricity comes mainly from the Kariba hydro-electric power station on the Zambezi and the Hwange coal-fired power station, petrol and diesel are imported, and most rural families still cook with firewood. More and more homes, schools and businesses now add solar panels.

This lesson sorts energy sources into renewable and non-renewable types, explains what each is used for, and weighs up their advantages and disadvantages.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Energy resource"}
Anything that can be used to provide energy for heating, lighting, cooking, transport or driving machines, such as coal, sunlight, falling water or wood.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable energy"}
Energy from a source that is naturally replaced and will not run out on a human time scale, such as the sun, wind and flowing water.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-renewable energy"}
Energy from a source that took millions of years to form and is used much faster than it can be replaced, so it will eventually run out: coal, oil, natural gas and uranium.
:::

:::definition{term="Fossil fuels"}
Coal, oil and natural gas, formed from the remains of plants and animals buried and compressed over millions of years. Burning them releases carbon dioxide.
:::

:::definition{term="Fuelwood"}
Wood and charcoal burned for cooking and heating. It is renewable if trees are replanted or regrow as fast as they are cut, and non-renewable if they are not.
:::

## Classifying energy sources

| Renewable | Non-renewable | Either (depends on use) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solar, wind, hydro-electric power (HEP), geothermal, tidal, wave, biogas, biofuels such as ethanol | Coal, oil, natural gas, nuclear (uranium) | Fuelwood and charcoal |

**Why fuelwood can be either:** around many villages in Zimbabwe, trees are cut for firewood and for clearing fields much faster than they regrow. Used like that, wood is effectively non-renewable. In a managed woodlot, where trees are replanted and harvested in rotation, it is renewable.

## The main sources and their uses

- **Coal**: burned in thermal power stations (Hwange) and factory boilers, for curing tobacco and in smelters. Zimbabwe mines coal at Hwange.
- **Oil (petroleum)**: refined into petrol, diesel, paraffin and jet fuel for transport, tractors, generators and lighting. Zimbabwe has no oil wells and imports fuel, much of it through a pipeline from Beira in Mozambique.
- **Natural gas**: used for electricity, cooking and industry; liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) is sold in cylinders for cooking.
- **Nuclear**: uranium fuel produces heat by fission to make steam for electricity. South Africa's Koeberg station near Cape Town is the only nuclear power station in Africa.
- **HEP**: falling water turns turbines. Kariba supplies both Zimbabwe and Zambia.
- **Solar**: photovoltaic (PV) panels make electricity directly; solar water heaters heat water. Zimbabwe has high sunshine levels for most of the year.
- **Wind**: turbines turn generators; best on coasts and exposed high ground with strong, steady winds.
- **Geothermal**: heat from hot rocks underground makes steam. Kenya generates much of its electricity this way at Olkaria in the Rift Valley.
- **Biomass and biogas**: wood, crop waste and dung are burned, or dung and food waste are digested to make methane gas (biogas) for cooking and lighting. Ethanol made from sugar cane can be blended with petrol.
- **Tidal and wave**: the rise and fall of tides or the movement of waves drive turbines, for example the tidal barrage at La Rance in France. They are only possible on coasts.

## Why energy is important to economic activities

| Sector | How energy is used |
| --- | --- |
| Agriculture | Tractors and transport, irrigation pumps, tobacco curing, milking and cooling, processing crops |
| Mining | Drills, hoists, pumps that keep mines dry, ventilation, crushing ore and smelting (for example ferrochrome) |
| Manufacturing | Machines, furnaces and boilers, lighting, refrigeration |
| Transport and services | Fuel for road, rail and air; power for offices, hospitals, schools and telecommunications |

A reliable, affordable supply attracts investment and creates jobs. Power cuts and fuel shortages raise costs, cut output and force businesses to buy expensive generators and diesel.

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coal | Large reserves at Hwange; reliable supply day and night; cheap to mine | Releases carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide (acid rain) and dust; mining scars land; finite |
| Oil | Easy to transport and store; essential for vehicles | Imported, so costly in foreign currency; prices change with world markets; pollution and carbon dioxide |
| Nuclear | Huge output from little fuel; almost no greenhouse gases during operation | Very expensive to build; radioactive waste; risk of accidents |
| HEP | Renewable, cheap to run, no smoke; dam water also used for irrigation, fishing and tourism | Very costly to build; floods land and displaces people; output falls in droughts |
| Solar | Free, renewable, abundant in Zimbabwe; panels can serve remote homes and clinics off the grid | Only works in daylight; batteries are expensive; panels cost money to buy |
| Wind | Renewable and clean once built | Needs steady strong winds, which much of inland Zimbabwe lacks; output varies; noise and visual impact |
| Geothermal | Reliable, renewable, low emissions | Only possible in volcanic or rift areas such as the East African Rift |
| Fuelwood | Free or cheap; no equipment needed | Deforestation and soil erosion; smoke causes respiratory disease indoors; time spent collecting |
| Biogas | Uses waste; leaves slurry that is a good fertiliser; reduces fuelwood use | Needs a steady supply of dung and water; digesters cost money to build |

:::example{title="Worked example: an energy mix (fictional data)"}
Table 1 shows how the electricity used by a fictional country was generated in one year.

| Source | Share (%) |
| --- | --- |
| Coal | 45 |
| HEP | 40 |
| Solar | 10 |
| Imports and others | 5 |

- Share from renewable sources produced at home (HEP + solar): 40 + 10 = **50 %**.
- On a pie chart, the coal sector would have an angle of 45 ÷ 100 × 360 = **162°**.
- In a drought year, if HEP output fell by half, the gap of 20 % would have to be filled by burning more coal, buying more imports, or cutting supply (load shedding).
:::

## Choosing the best source

There is no single "best" source. The right choice depends on what resources a country or area has (coal, rivers with steep gradients, sunshine, wind), on cost, on how reliable the supply must be, and on how much environmental damage is acceptable. A remote rural clinic is better served by solar panels than by an expensive grid line; a smelter needs large, reliable supplies from the grid.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all biomass renewable"}
Fuelwood is renewable only if trees are replanted or allowed to regrow. Say "renewable if managed sustainably".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying HEP has no environmental impact"}
HEP produces no smoke, but building a dam floods land, displaces people and wildlife, and changes the river downstream.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing nuclear with fossil fuels"}
Uranium is non-renewable, but it is not a fossil fuel, and it does not release carbon dioxide when it produces heat.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Justify the choice of a particular type of energy" (ZIMSEC) needs a specific context: who is using it, where, and why the advantages outweigh the disadvantages there.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, geothermal, tidal, wave, biogas and biofuels) are naturally replaced; non-renewable sources (coal, oil, gas, uranium) will run out.
- Fuelwood is renewable only when trees are replaced as fast as they are cut.
- Zimbabwe relies on Kariba HEP and Hwange coal for electricity, imported fuel for transport and fuelwood for most rural cooking, with solar growing fast.
- Energy drives agriculture, mining, manufacturing and services; shortages raise costs and cut output.
- Each source has advantages and disadvantages in cost, reliability and environmental impact, so the best choice depends on local resources and needs.
:::
