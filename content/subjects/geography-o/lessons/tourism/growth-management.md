## Introduction

A century ago, holidays abroad were for the wealthy few. Today well over a billion international tourist trips are made each year (an approximate figure that dipped sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic and then recovered). Tourism earns money, creates jobs and builds airports and roads, but it can also damage the very landscapes, wildlife and cultures that tourists come to see.

This lesson explains why tourism has grown, how resorts change over time, what benefits and problems tourism brings, and how it can be managed sustainably.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tourism"}
Travelling to and staying in places outside a person's usual environment for leisure, business or other purposes, usually for less than a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass tourism"}
Large numbers of tourists visiting the same places, often on package holidays.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable tourism"}
Tourism that meets the needs of today's tourists and host communities while protecting the environment and culture for the future.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecotourism"}
Small-scale, nature-based tourism that conserves the environment, benefits local people and educates visitors.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The largest number of visitors a place can take before its environment, facilities or visitor experience are damaged.
:::

## Why tourism has grown

1. **Rising incomes** give people more disposable income to spend on travel.
2. **More leisure time:** paid holidays, shorter working weeks and longer, healthier retirement.
3. **Cheaper, faster transport:** jet aircraft, low-cost airlines and better roads.
4. **The internet and mobile phones:** online booking, price comparison and reviews make travel easier.
5. **Package holidays** combine flights, hotels and transfers at a low price.
6. **Marketing and the media:** television, films and social media make distant places familiar and desirable.
7. **Government promotion and investment** in airports, visas and attractions.
8. **Demand for new experiences:** adventure, wildlife, culture and ecotourism.

## The Butler model of resort development

The Butler model (1980) describes how the number of visitors to a resort changes over time. Picture an S-shaped curve with time along the bottom and visitor numbers up the side.

1. **Exploration:** a few adventurous visitors; no special facilities; tourists use local services.
2. **Involvement:** local people begin to provide accommodation, food and guides; numbers rise slowly.
3. **Development:** outside companies invest in hotels and attractions; marketing grows; numbers rise rapidly; local control falls.
4. **Consolidation:** growth slows; tourism dominates the local economy; some residents resent tourists.
5. **Stagnation:** carrying capacity is reached; the resort is no longer fashionable; environmental and social problems appear.
6. **After stagnation:** either **decline** (visitors fall, hotels close) or **rejuvenation** (new attractions, new markets or upgrading revive the resort), or numbers stay roughly stable.

### Strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Simple and easy to apply to many resorts | Not every resort passes through every stage, or in the same order |
| Shows that growth does not continue for ever | Stages are hard to identify while they are happening |
| Warns planners to act before stagnation | Lengths of stages vary greatly; it cannot predict timing |
| Highlights the role of outside investment and local control | Ignores sudden shocks such as disasters, pandemics or political crises |

## Benefits and problems at different scales

| Scale | Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| Local | Jobs in hotels, guiding, crafts and transport; improved roads, water and electricity; markets for local farmers | Seasonal, low-paid jobs; higher prices for land and food; litter, noise and congestion; loss of traditional culture; crime |
| National | Foreign currency; tax revenue; the multiplier effect spreads income to other sectors; investment in airports | **Leakage** of profits to foreign-owned hotels and tour companies; over-dependence on one industry; damage to fragile environments |
| Global | Cultural exchange and understanding; funds for conservation of World Heritage Sites | Greenhouse gas emissions from aviation; spread of disease; global brands replacing local character |

## Managing tourism sustainably

| Strategy | How it works | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Limiting visitor numbers (permits, quotas, entry fees) | Keeps numbers within carrying capacity; fees fund conservation | Effective, but may make visits expensive and exclusive |
| Zoning and planning controls | Keeps hotels away from sensitive areas; height limits | Works if enforced; pressure from developers |
| Ecotourism lodges | Small scale, low impact (solar power, local food) | Good for the environment, but serves few visitors and earns less in total |
| Community-based tourism | Local people own and run lodges, guiding, homestays | Keeps income local and reduces leakage; needs training and capital |
| Education and codes of conduct | Visitors told to stay on paths, not to feed animals, to respect customs | Cheap, but depends on visitors' behaviour |
| Spreading visitors | Promote new sites and off-peak seasons | Reduces pressure on hotspots but can spread impacts to new places |

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: Victoria Falls, on the Zambia-Zimbabwe border"}
1. **Growth:** the falls (about 1.7 km wide and about 100 m high, approximate figures) became reachable by rail when the Victoria Falls Bridge opened in 1905, and the town grew around tourism. The site has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site, shared by Zambia and Zimbabwe, since 1989. In the 1990s adventure activities such as bungee jumping from the bridge and white-water rafting in the gorges brought rapid growth.
2. **Butler stages:** exploration and involvement in the early 1900s; development with hotels and adventure tourism; then a fall in visitors on the Zimbabwean side during the economic and political difficulties of the 2000s (a decline caused by outside events, not by over-use); then **rejuvenation**, helped by the joint hosting of the UN World Tourism Organization General Assembly by Zambia and Zimbabwe in 2013, the KAZA visa that lets visitors cross between the two countries on one visa, and an upgraded international airport.
3. **Benefits:** jobs in hotels, lodges, guiding, rafting and craft markets; foreign currency; better roads, airport and services.
4. **Problems:** pressure on paths and the rainforest around the falls; litter and waste; competition with wildlife and local people for land and water; touting; leakage to foreign tour operators.
5. **Management:** national park entry fees and marked paths in the rainforest; World Heritage rules limiting building close to the falls; community and conservation projects that share income with surrounding villages.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating ecotourism as any holiday in nature"}
A safari in a crowded park with large hotels is not ecotourism. Ecotourism must be small scale, conserve the environment, benefit local people and educate visitors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Butler model without applying it"}
For high marks, say which stage a named resort is in and what evidence shows it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 9)"}
Expect a line graph of visitor numbers to label with Butler stages, a table of tourism data, and an extended question on the growth, impacts and management of tourism in a **named country or area**. For "evaluate strategies", say how well each one works and for whom, then give an overall judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tourism has grown because of rising incomes, more leisure time, cheaper transport, the internet, package holidays and marketing.
- The Butler model: exploration, involvement, development, consolidation, stagnation, then decline or rejuvenation.
- Tourism brings jobs, foreign currency and infrastructure but also leakage, seasonal work, cultural change and environmental damage.
- Sustainable strategies include visitor limits, zoning, ecotourism, community-based tourism and visitor education.
- Victoria Falls shows growth, decline due to outside events, and rejuvenation, with management by fees, paths and World Heritage rules.
:::
