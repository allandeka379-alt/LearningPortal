## Introduction

Every morning in Zimbabwe millions of journeys begin: pupils walk or cycle to school, kombis fill up at ranks in Harare and Bulawayo, lorries queue at Beitbridge, and trains haul coal and chrome towards the ports. **Transport** is the movement of people and goods from one place to another. Without it, farmers could not sell their crops, mines could not export minerals and factories could not receive raw materials.

Zimbabwe is **landlocked**: it has no coastline, so almost all of its overseas trade must travel by road, rail or pipeline through neighbouring countries to ports such as Beira, Maputo and Durban. Choosing the right mode of transport is therefore an everyday economic decision for the country. This lesson looks at the five main modes, their strengths and weaknesses, how they have changed, and how to choose between them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transport"}
The movement of people (passengers) and goods (freight) from one place to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Mode of transport"}
A type of transport: road, rail, air, water (inland waterways and sea) or pipeline.
:::

:::definition{term="Terminal costs"}
Fixed costs paid whatever the length of the journey: loading and unloading, building and running stations, ports, airports and depots.
:::

:::definition{term="Line-haul costs"}
Costs that increase with the distance travelled, such as fuel, drivers' wages and wear on vehicles.
:::

:::definition{term="Bulky goods"}
Heavy goods of low value for their weight, such as coal, chrome ore, cement, fertiliser and maize.
:::

:::definition{term="Containerisation"}
Carrying goods in standard-sized steel boxes that can be moved directly between ship, train and lorry without unpacking.
:::

## The five modes

:::example{title="Comparing the modes (general characteristics)"}
| Mode | Best for | Main advantages | Main disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road | Short to medium journeys; door-to-door delivery | Flexible routes and times; reaches farms, villages and mines; low terminal costs | Small loads per vehicle; high fuel cost per tonne over long distances; congestion, accidents and road damage |
| Rail | Bulky goods over long distances; commuters | Large loads; cheaper than road per tonne over long distances; less fuel per tonne | Fixed routes; high cost of building and maintaining track; goods often need a lorry at each end |
| Air | Passengers over long distances; light, valuable or perishable goods | Fastest mode; crosses mountains and seas | Very expensive per tonne; small cargo space; needs large airports; high fuel use per passenger |
| Water | Very heavy, bulky cargo over long distances (sea); local crossings (lakes) | Cheapest per tonne over long distances; very large loads | Slow; needs ports; depends on water level and weather |
| Pipeline | Liquids and gases: fuel, oil, natural gas, water | Very low running cost; continuous flow; takes tankers off the roads | Very expensive to build; carries only fluids; fixed route; leaks harm the environment |
:::

**Road** transport is the most used mode in Zimbabwe for both passengers and freight. Kombis, buses and lorries can stop anywhere along a road and deliver to the door, so no transfer is needed. Its weakness is that each vehicle carries a small load, so over long distances the cost per tonne is high, and heavy lorries damage roads.

**Rail** suits heavy, bulky goods such as coal from Hwange, chrome, fertiliser and grain moving long distances. Zimbabwe's railways are run by the National Railways of Zimbabwe, with its headquarters in Bulawayo. Rail's weaknesses are its fixed routes and the high cost of keeping track, wagons and signals in good repair.

**Air** transport is fast but expensive. It suits passengers travelling long distances (business travel, tourists flying to Victoria Falls) and light, high-value or perishable exports, such as cut flowers and fresh horticultural produce that must reach markets in Europe within a day or two.

**Water** transport inside Zimbabwe is limited to lakes, for example ferries and houseboats on Lake Kariba. Zimbabwe's sea trade uses ports in neighbouring countries. Ships carry the largest loads of all and are cheapest per tonne over long distances, but they are slow.

**Pipeline** transport moves fuel from the port of Beira in Mozambique to Feruka near Mutare, so fuel does not have to travel by road tanker along the whole route. Pipelines cost a great deal to build but very little to run.

## Choosing a mode: cost, distance and the cargo

The total cost of a journey is made up of **terminal costs** and **line-haul costs**. Road has low terminal costs but high line-haul costs; rail and water have higher terminal costs (stations, sidings, ports, loading equipment) but lower line-haul costs. So:

- over short distances road is usually cheapest;
- over longer distances rail becomes cheaper;
- over very long distances (between continents) sea transport is cheapest.

:::worked-solution[Comparing freight costs (fictional rates)]
1. A company must move 500 tonnes of fertiliser 400 km. Road costs US\$0.09 per tonne-km; rail costs US\$0.06 per tonne-km plus US\$2,000 for loading at both ends.
2. Road: 500 × 400 × 0.09 = US\$18,000.
3. Rail: 500 × 400 × 0.06 = US\$12,000, plus US\$2,000 = US\$14,000.
4. Rail is cheaper by US\$4,000, because over 400 km its lower line-haul cost outweighs its higher terminal cost.
:::

Other factors also matter:

- **Nature of the goods:** perishable goods need speed (air or fast road); fragile goods need careful handling; liquids suit pipelines; bulky ores suit rail.
- **Value of the goods:** valuable goods can pay for faster, dearer transport.
- **Urgency and reliability:** a breakdown or delayed train can matter more than a small saving in cost.
- **Access:** a farm or small-scale mine with no railway siding must use road, at least for the first part of the journey.
- **Safety and the environment:** rail and pipelines use less fuel per tonne and take lorries off the roads.

## How transport has changed

Transport in Zimbabwe and across Africa has changed greatly:

1. **Human and animal power:** carrying loads on the head, ox-drawn sledges and scotch carts. Scotch carts and bicycles are still important in many rural areas.
2. **Railways:** from the late 1800s railways linked mines and farms to ports; towns such as Bulawayo grew as railway centres.
3. **Roads and motor vehicles:** tarred trunk roads, buses and lorries spread through the 1900s. Today the road network carries most passengers, and many goods that once went by rail now go by lorry.
4. **Air travel:** international airports at Harare, Bulawayo and Victoria Falls link Zimbabwe to the region and the world.
5. **Containers and pipelines:** containers make transfers between ship, rail and road quick; the Beira to Feruka pipeline carries fuel.
6. **Modern trends:** kombis and ride-hailing in towns, road upgrading, and dry ports and border posts designed to speed up the movement of goods.

:::example{title="Case study: transport choices for a landlocked country"}
A tobacco exporter near Harare sends bales by lorry to a rail depot or straight to a border post, then on to a port by road or rail, and finally overseas by container ship. A flower grower near Harare sends boxes of cut stems by refrigerated lorry to the airport for a flight to Europe. A mine on the Great Dyke may send chrome ore by rail. Each choice reflects the value, weight and perishability of the cargo.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying one mode is always cheapest"}
Road is cheapest over short distances, rail and water over long ones. Always link cost to distance and the type of cargo.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting transfers"}
Rail, air and sea rarely deliver to the door. Goods usually need a lorry at one or both ends, which adds terminal costs and time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague advantages"}
"Rail is good" earns nothing. Say *why*: "rail carries large loads of bulky goods such as coal cheaply over long distances".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 and Paper 2 Section C"}
Multiple-choice items often ask which mode suits a cargo: match bulky to rail or sea, perishable and valuable to air, liquids to pipeline, short door-to-door trips to road. In Paper 2, "explain the advantages" needs a reason for each point, ideally with a Zimbabwean example (Hwange coal by rail, flowers by air, fuel by pipeline from Beira).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Cost = tonnes × distance × rate per tonne-km, plus any fixed terminal charge. Show each step and give the unit (US\$).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The five modes are road, rail, air, water and pipeline; each suits particular cargoes and distances.
- Road is flexible and door-to-door; rail carries bulky goods cheaply over long distances; air is fast but dear; water is cheapest per tonne over very long distances; pipelines carry fluids cheaply once built.
- Total cost = terminal costs + line-haul costs, so road is cheapest for short trips and rail and sea for long ones.
- Zimbabwe is landlocked, so it depends on road, rail and pipeline links to ports in neighbouring countries.
- Transport has changed from human and animal power to railways, roads, air, containers and pipelines.
:::
