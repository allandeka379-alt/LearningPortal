## Introduction

Rivers give life. Farmers in the lower Zambezi valley plant on fertile alluvial soils, towns draw their water from dams, and Kariba's turbines light homes in Zimbabwe and Zambia. Yet the same rivers can destroy. Families in low-lying Muzarabani have repeatedly been moved to higher ground when rivers burst their banks, and in March 2019 Cyclone Idai turned streams in Chimanimani into torrents that swept away homes and roads.

This lesson weighs the **opportunities** of living near rivers against the **hazards**, explains why floods happen and evaluates how they can be managed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Flood"}
When a river's discharge is too great for its channel, so water overflows the banks onto land that is normally dry.
:::

:::definition{term="Flash flood"}
A sudden flood that rises within minutes or a few hours of intense rain, usually in small, steep or urban basins.
:::

:::definition{term="Hard engineering"}
Building structures to control rivers, such as dams, levées and concrete channels.
:::

:::definition{term="Soft engineering"}
Working with natural processes and managing how people use the land, such as afforestation, floodplain zoning, wetland restoration and warning systems.
:::

:::definition{term="Recurrence interval"}
The average time between floods of a given size, calculated from records as (number of years of record + 1) ÷ rank of the flood.
:::

## Opportunities of living near rivers

| Opportunity | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Water supply for homes, industry and irrigation | Harare's water from dams on the Manyame; irrigation in the lowveld |
| Fertile alluvial soils on floodplains | Crops on the lower Zambezi valley floor |
| Flat land for building, roads and railways | Many towns and roads follow river valleys |
| Hydro-electric power | Kariba on the Zambezi |
| Fishing | Kapenta fishing on Lake Kariba |
| Transport | Large rivers such as the Nile and the Rhine |
| Tourism and recreation | Victoria Falls, rafting, canoe safaris at Mana Pools |

## Causes of floods

### Natural (physical) causes

- **Intense or prolonged rainfall**, for example from tropical cyclones such as Idai (2019) or a very wet rainy season.
- **Saturated soils** after weeks of rain, so further rain cannot infiltrate.
- **Steep slopes** and **impermeable rock** that speed run-off.
- **Low-lying, flat land** such as floodplains and deltas.
- Rivers joining at confluences, adding flood peaks together.

### Human causes

- **Deforestation and overgrazing**: less interception, more overland flow, more soil washed into rivers.
- **Urbanisation**: impermeable surfaces and drains shorten lag time and raise peaks.
- **Silting of channels and dams** from soil erosion, reducing their capacity.
- **Settlement on floodplains**, putting people in harm's way.
- **Dam releases or failures** when reservoirs are full.
- **Climate change**, which is expected to make extreme rainfall more intense.

## Impacts of floods

| | Short-term | Long-term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Social** | Deaths and injuries; people displaced; schools closed | Water-borne disease (cholera, diarrhoea); malaria from standing water; trauma; loss of homes |
| **Economic** | Crops, livestock and stored food lost; roads and bridges destroyed | Cost of rebuilding; lost income; food insecurity until the next harvest |
| **Environmental** | Erosion of banks and soil; landslides on steep slopes | Fresh fertile silt on floodplains (a benefit); pollution from sewage spread by floodwater |

:::example{title="Case study (Zimbabwe): Muzarabani and Tokwe-Mukosi"}
**Muzarabani**, in the lower Zambezi valley in the north of the country, is flat and low-lying. Rivers draining off the escarpment slow down and spill onto the valley floor in wet years, so floods have affected the district many times, destroying crops and homes and cutting roads. Responses have included evacuation to higher ground, community warning committees and relief food.

At the **Tokwe-Mukosi dam** in Masvingo Province, very heavy rain in early 2014 filled the basin behind the unfinished dam faster than expected. Thousands of families living in the area that was to be flooded had to be evacuated at short notice, many to a camp at Chingwizi, and there were fears for communities downstream if the incomplete wall failed. The event shows that large engineering projects bring flood risk as well as protection, and that resettlement must be planned well before a reservoir fills.
:::

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: the Limpopo floods, Mozambique, 2000"}
- **Where**: the lower Limpopo valley in southern Mozambique, a low-lying floodplain where the river reaches the Indian Ocean. The Limpopo basin is shared by South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe and Mozambique, so Mozambique receives floodwater from all of them.
- **Causes**: weeks of exceptionally heavy rain from late January 2000 saturated the ground across southern Africa. In late February **Cyclone Eline** brought more heavy rain over the upper basin. Rivers already full rose further, and floodwater from upstream countries arrived on the flat lower valley. Settlement and farming on the floodplain increased exposure.
- **Impacts**: towns such as Chokwe and Xai-Xai were inundated; roads, bridges and the main north-south road link were cut; several hundred people died (about 700 by most estimates) and hundreds of thousands were made homeless; crops and cattle were lost; cholera and malaria cases rose. Thousands of people were stranded on roofs and in trees and were rescued by helicopters, many from the South African Air Force.
- **Management**: emergency rescue and international aid; afterwards Mozambique strengthened its disaster management institute and community early-warning systems along the Limpopo, and encouraged resettlement on higher ground. Problems remained: many farmers returned to the fertile floodplain, and the country depends on rainfall and dam-release information from upstream countries.
:::

## Managing floods

### Hard engineering

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dams and reservoirs** | Store floodwater and release it slowly | Also give water, HEP and irrigation | Very expensive; flood land and displace people; trap sediment; risk of failure or emergency releases |
| **Levées and embankments** | Raise the banks so the channel holds more | Protect towns and farmland | Can be overtopped or breached, causing worse floods; move the problem downstream |
| **Channelisation** | Straighten, deepen or line the channel | Water leaves the area quickly | Faster flow can flood places downstream; destroys habitats |
| **Flood relief channels** | Divert excess water around a town | Protect a built-up area | Costly; need space |

### Soft engineering

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Afforestation and soil conservation** | Trees and contour ridges increase interception and infiltration | Cheap; reduces soil erosion and silting too | Takes years to work; uses farmland |
| **Floodplain zoning** | Keep homes and schools off the highest-risk land; use it for grazing or parks | Reduces damage at low cost | Hard to enforce where land is scarce; people may already live there |
| **Wetland and floodplain restoration** | Let water spread and store naturally | Benefits wildlife; sustainable | Land cannot be built on |
| **Forecasting and early warning** | Rain and river gauges, radio and mobile phone alerts | Saves lives; cheap | Does not prevent damage; needs good communication |
| **Preparation** | Raised houses, flood shelters, evacuation plans, drills | Reduces deaths | Depends on community organisation |

![Cross-section of a river and its floodplain: levées raise both banks so the channel holds the flood level, the floodplain is zoned for grazing and parkland, and homes are built on higher ground away from the river.](media:diagrams/flood-defences-levee-zoning.png "Figure: levées (hard engineering) and floodplain zoning (soft engineering)")

**Evaluating management.** Ask: does it reduce **deaths**, reduce **damage**, at what **cost**, who **benefits** and who **loses**, does it **move the problem** elsewhere, and is it **sustainable** (works for a long time without harming the environment or depending on outside money)? In LICs, low-cost soft methods such as warning systems, zoning and catchment conservation are often more sustainable than large engineering, while dams can help if they are well managed and communities are consulted.

:::example{title="Calculating a recurrence interval (fictional data)"}
A gauging station has 39 years of records. The second-largest flood on record reached 1,250 m³/s.

1. Recurrence interval = (n + 1) ÷ m, where n = number of years of record and m = rank.
2. (39 + 1) ÷ 2 = 20 years.
3. A flood of this size can be expected, on average, once every 20 years (a 1-in-20-year flood). It could still happen in two years running.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining them"}
"Deforestation" earns little on its own. Explain: fewer trees → less interception and infiltration → more overland flow → river rises faster and higher.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling dams completely safe"}
Dams reduce many floods but can fail, must sometimes release water, displace people and trap sediment. A good evaluation mentions both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 asks for a **detailed specific example** of a flood on a named river: give the river, the country, the date, specific causes, impacts in categories, and management. ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section A rewards named Zimbabwean or African examples.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "evaluate" questions, give advantages **and** disadvantages, then a judgement: which method is most effective for this place and why.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rivers offer water, fertile soil, flat land, HEP, fishing, transport and tourism, but floods are a major hazard.
- Natural causes: intense or prolonged rain (cyclones), saturated soils, steep slopes, flat floodplains. Human causes: deforestation, urbanisation, silting, floodplain settlement, dam releases.
- Impacts are social, economic and environmental, short and long term.
- Hard engineering (dams, levées, channelisation) controls the river; soft engineering (afforestation, zoning, warnings) works with it and is often more sustainable.
- Case studies: Limpopo floods in Mozambique (2000); Muzarabani and Tokwe-Mukosi (2014) in Zimbabwe.
:::
