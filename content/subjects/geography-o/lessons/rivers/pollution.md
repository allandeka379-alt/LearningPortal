## Introduction

Most of Harare's water comes from **Lake Chivero**, a reservoir on the Manyame River downstream of the city. Rivers that drain Harare and Chitungwiza, such as the Mukuvisi and the Marimba, flow towards it. When sewage works are overloaded or broken, partly treated waste and industrial effluent enter these rivers. The lake has suffered from algal blooms and mats of water hyacinth, and the city must spend heavily on chemicals to make the water safe to drink. A city can end up drinking its own pollution.

River pollution is a problem of rapid urban growth, industry, mining and farming everywhere. This lesson explains its causes and impacts, how it is measured, and how it can be managed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollution"}
The addition of harmful substances or energy (such as heat) to the environment at a rate faster than it can be broken down, dispersed or made harmless.
:::

:::definition{term="Point and non-point sources"}
A **point source** comes from a single place, such as a sewage outfall or factory pipe. A **non-point (diffuse) source** comes from a wide area, such as fertiliser washed off many farms.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
Over-enrichment of water with nutrients (nitrates and phosphates), leading to rapid growth of algae and plants, loss of oxygen and death of fish and other animals.
:::

:::definition{term="Effluent"}
Liquid waste discharged from a sewage works, factory or mine.
:::

## Human causes of river pollution

| Source | Pollutants | Typical places |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Domestic sewage** | Organic matter, bacteria and viruses, nutrients, detergents (phosphates) | Fast-growing cities with overloaded sewage works; settlements without sewers |
| **Industrial effluent** | Heavy metals (chromium, lead), oils, dyes, acids, hot water | Tanneries, textile mills, chemical plants, power stations |
| **Mining** | Silt, acids (acid mine drainage), cyanide, mercury, heavy metals | Gold, copper and coal mining; small-scale gold panning that uses mercury |
| **Agriculture** | Nitrate and phosphate fertilisers, pesticides, animal waste, soil | Irrigated farms, feedlots, cultivated slopes |
| **Solid waste** | Plastics, litter, dumped rubbish | Informal settlements and towns without collection services |

## Impacts of river pollution

### On people

- **Water-borne disease**: drinking or washing in water contaminated with sewage spreads cholera, typhoid and diarrhoeal diseases, which are especially dangerous for young children.
- **Poisoning**: heavy metals and mercury build up in fish and in people who eat them (bioaccumulation) and can damage the nervous system and kidneys.
- **Cost**: water companies must spend more on treatment; fishing and tourism lose income.

### On ecosystems: eutrophication step by step

1. Fertilisers or sewage add **nitrates and phosphates** to a river or lake.
2. Algae and floating plants (such as water hyacinth) grow rapidly: an **algal bloom**.
3. The surface layer blocks sunlight, so plants below die.
4. **Bacteria** decompose the dead plants and algae, using up **dissolved oxygen**.
5. Oxygen levels fall so low that fish and invertebrates die. Some algae also release toxins.

![Eutrophication in five steps: fertiliser or sewage adds nitrates and phosphates, algae bloom, the surface layer blocks sunlight, bacteria decomposing the dead plants use up the oxygen, and fish die.](media:diagrams/eutrophication-steps.png "Figure: the chain of eutrophication in a river or dam")

Other ecosystem impacts: toxic chemicals kill organisms directly; silt smothers spawning grounds and blocks sunlight; plastic is swallowed by animals; hot water from power stations holds less oxygen.

## Measuring river pollution

| Measure | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Dissolved oxygen** (mg per litre) | Low values show organic pollution; most fish need more than about 5 mg/l |
| **Nitrate and phosphate levels** | Nutrient pollution from farms and sewage |
| **Faecal coliform bacteria** | Sewage contamination; a health risk |
| **Turbidity** (cloudiness) | Silt from soil erosion or mining |
| **Biotic index** (kick sampling of invertebrates) | Clean water supports sensitive species such as mayfly and stonefly nymphs; badly polluted water supports only tolerant ones such as sludge worms and rat-tailed maggots |

:::example{title="Interpreting water-quality data (fictional data)"}
Students measured dissolved oxygen above and below a sewage outfall on a fictional river.

| Site | Position | Dissolved oxygen (mg/l) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | 500 m above outfall | 8.4 |
| Q | 100 m below outfall | 3.6 |
| R | 5 km below outfall | 6.3 |

1. Fall from P to Q = 8.4 − 3.6 = 4.8 mg/l.
2. As a percentage of P: 4.8 ÷ 8.4 × 100 = 57 % (to the nearest whole number).
3. Explanation: bacteria break down the organic waste and use up oxygen. Further downstream the river recovers as waste is diluted and broken down and turbulence adds oxygen.
:::

## Managing river pollution

| Strategy | How it helps | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sewage treatment works** (and repair of sewers) | Removes organic matter, bacteria and some nutrients before discharge | Expensive to build and run; overloaded as cities grow; needs reliable electricity |
| **Laws and fines** ("polluter pays"), effluent permits | Make factories and mines treat their waste | Need inspectors and honest enforcement; fines may be cheaper than treatment |
| **Cleaner technology in industry** | Recycling water, recovering chromium, closed systems | Cost for small firms |
| **Buffer strips and careful fertiliser use** | Vegetation beside rivers traps soil and nutrients | Takes farmland; needs farmer training |
| **Wetland protection** | Wetlands filter nutrients and silt naturally | Under pressure from building and farming |
| **Safe waste collection and sanitation** | Reduces solid waste and sewage reaching rivers | Needs funding and services in informal settlements |
| **Monitoring and education** | Identifies polluters; changes behaviour | Does not clean water by itself |

**Sustainable strategies** deal with pollution at its **source** (less waste, cleaner production, protected wetlands), last for a long time without huge running costs, and involve local communities. Clean-up after pollution has happened is usually more expensive and less effective than prevention.

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: the River Ganges (Ganga), India"}
- **The river**: the Ganges rises in the Himalayas and flows roughly 2,500 km across northern India to the Bay of Bengal. Hundreds of millions of people live in its basin, and it is sacred to Hindus, who bathe in it at places such as Varanasi.
- **Causes**: the largest source is **untreated or partly treated sewage** from cities along the river, because treatment capacity has not kept up with urban growth. Industrial effluent adds heavy metals, notably **chromium from leather tanneries** around Kanpur. Fertiliser and pesticide run-off from the intensively farmed plain, solid waste, and religious practices such as cremation and offerings add further pollution.
- **Impacts**: levels of faecal bacteria in many stretches are far above safe bathing limits, so people who bathe in or drink the water risk diarrhoeal diseases and typhoid. Heavy metals harm fish and enter the food chain. Wildlife such as the endangered Ganges river dolphin is threatened.
- **Management**: the **Ganga Action Plan** (from 1985) built sewage treatment plants but had limited success because capacity was too small and plants often did not run. The **Namami Gange** programme (from 2014) has funded many more treatment plants, river-front clean-up, monitoring of industries and closure of some polluting units.
- **Evaluation**: water quality has improved in some stretches, but sewage generation still outpaces treatment, enforcement on small industries is difficult and the scale of the basin is enormous. Long-term success depends on sewers and treatment in every town, cleaner industry and public participation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing eutrophication as 'nutrients kill the fish'"}
Nutrients do not poison fish. The sequence is nutrients → algal bloom → plants die → **bacteria use up oxygen** → fish die. Include the bacteria and the oxygen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague management answers"}
"Stop pollution" earns nothing. Name a strategy, say **how** it reduces pollution, and give a limitation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 may ask for a **detailed specific example** of pollution in a named river. Give the river and country, at least two specific causes with places, impacts on people and on the ecosystem, and named management with an evaluation.
:::

:::exam-tip
When given water-quality data, describe the change with figures (above and below a source), then explain the cause and the recovery downstream.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Human causes: sewage, industrial effluent, mining (silt, mercury, acids), farm run-off (nitrates, phosphates, pesticides) and solid waste; point and non-point sources.
- Impacts: water-borne disease, poisoning, treatment costs; eutrophication (algal bloom → decomposition → oxygen loss → fish die).
- Pollution is measured by dissolved oxygen, nutrients, bacteria, turbidity and biotic indices.
- Management: sewage treatment, laws and fines, cleaner industry, buffer strips, wetlands, sanitation, monitoring; prevention at source is most sustainable.
- Example: the Ganges (sewage, Kanpur tanneries; Ganga Action Plan and Namami Gange). Zimbabwe: rivers draining Harare towards Lake Chivero.
:::
