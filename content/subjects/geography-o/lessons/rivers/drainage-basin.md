## Introduction

Stand on a bridge over almost any river in Zimbabwe in February and you may see brown water running bank-full. Stand on the same bridge in September and you may see only sand and a few pools. The river has not moved: what has changed is the amount of rain falling on the land that feeds it. That land is the river's **drainage basin**.

Zimbabwe sits astride a great divide. Rain that falls north of the central watershed drains towards the **Zambezi**; rain that falls to the south and east drains to the **Save** or the **Limpopo**. All three rivers end in the Indian Ocean in Mozambique. Understanding how water moves through a basin explains why rivers flood, why boreholes dry up in drought, and why a dam on the Zambezi depends on rain that falls hundreds of kilometres away.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Drainage basin"}
The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Also called a catchment.
:::

:::definition{term="Watershed"}
The boundary of a drainage basin: a line of high ground that separates one basin from the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Source, mouth, tributary and confluence"}
The **source** is where a river begins; the **mouth** is where it ends (in the sea, a lake or another river). A **tributary** is a smaller river that joins a larger one, and the point where they meet is a **confluence**.
:::

:::definition{term="Discharge"}
The volume of water passing a point in a river each second, measured in cubic metres per second (m³/s, or cumecs). Discharge = cross-sectional area × velocity.
:::

:::definition{term="River regime"}
The pattern of a river's discharge through the year.
:::

:::definition{term="Wetted perimeter"}
The length of the channel bed and banks in contact with the water in a cross-section.
:::

## The drainage basin as an open system

The global water cycle is a **closed system**: the amount of water on Earth stays the same. A drainage basin is an **open system**, because water enters it and leaves it.

| Part of the system | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Input** | Precipitation (rain, hail) |
| **Stores** | Interception (water held on leaves), surface storage (puddles, lakes, dams), soil moisture, groundwater (in rocks), the river channel |
| **Flows (transfers)** | Infiltration, percolation, overland flow, throughflow, groundwater flow, channel flow, stemflow |
| **Outputs** | Evaporation, transpiration (together: evapotranspiration), river discharge into the sea or a lake |

**Diagram in words** (draw it as a cross-section of a hillside with arrows):

1. Rain falls on trees and grass. Some is **intercepted** on leaves and evaporates; some drips or runs down stems to the ground.
2. At the surface, water **infiltrates** into the soil. If the soil is saturated or hard-baked, or the rain is very heavy, water runs over the surface as **overland flow**.
3. In the soil, water moves sideways downslope towards the river as **throughflow**.
4. Some water moves deeper into the rock (**percolation**) and joins **groundwater**, which moves slowly to the river as **groundwater flow**. This keeps some rivers flowing in the dry season (**base flow**).
5. All these flows feed the river, which carries the water to the mouth as **channel flow**.
6. Throughout, water returns to the air by **evaporation** and **transpiration** from plants.

![Hillside cross-section of a drainage basin: rain (800 mm a year) is intercepted by trees, infiltrates the soil, runs as overland flow or throughflow, percolates to groundwater and reaches the river as channel flow, while 600 mm is lost to evaporation and transpiration, leaving 200 mm of discharge.](media:diagrams/drainage-basin-system.png "Figure: stores and flows in a drainage basin (water balance of the fictional 800 mm basin)")

:::example{title="A simple water balance"}
A fictional basin receives 800 mm of rain in a year. Evapotranspiration takes 600 mm and there is no change in storage.

1. Water balance: precipitation = discharge + evapotranspiration ± change in storage.
2. 800 = discharge + 600 + 0.
3. Discharge (as a depth over the basin) = 200 mm, so only a quarter of the rain reaches the river.

In hot, semi-arid parts of Zimbabwe evapotranspiration is high, so only a small fraction of the rain becomes river flow.
:::

## Features of a river and its basin

Imagine a map of a basin shaped like a leaf. The **watershed** runs round the edge. Small streams rise at **sources** on high ground, join at **confluences** and grow into the **main river**, which leaves the basin at the **mouth**. Along the **long profile** (a section from source to mouth) the gradient is steep near the source and gentle near the mouth, giving a concave curve.

:::example{title="Case study: Zimbabwe's three great basins"}
- **Zambezi basin**: rivers north of the central watershed, such as the Manyame, Mazowe, Sanyati and Gwayi, drain north or north-west to the Zambezi, which forms the border with Zambia. The Zambezi, roughly 2,600 km long, flows over the Victoria Falls, through Lake Kariba and Cahora Bassa and reaches the Indian Ocean in a delta in Mozambique.
- **Save basin**: the Save rises on the plateau south-east of Harare and flows south-east; the Runde joins it near the Mozambique border before it crosses Mozambique to the sea.
- **Limpopo basin**: rivers in the south-west and south, such as the Shashe and Mzingwane, drain to the Limpopo, which forms the border with South Africa near Beitbridge.

The **central watershed** follows the high ground of the highveld running roughly south-west to north-east across the country.
:::

## How a river changes downstream: the Bradshaw model

The Bradshaw model summarises how channel characteristics change from source to mouth.

| Increases downstream | Decreases downstream |
| --- | --- |
| Discharge | Gradient (slope) |
| Width and depth (so cross-sectional area) | Size of load particles |
| Mean velocity | Channel bed roughness |
| Volume of load | |

The surprise is **velocity**. Upstream water looks fast as it tumbles over boulders, but much of its energy is lost to friction with a rough bed. Downstream the channel is deeper and smoother, so proportionally less water touches the bed and banks, and the mean velocity is higher.

:::example{title="Calculating discharge"}
At a fictional site a channel is 8 m wide, the mean depth is 0.5 m and the mean velocity is 0.6 m/s.

1. Cross-sectional area = width × mean depth = 8 × 0.5 = 4 m².
2. Discharge = area × velocity = 4 × 0.6 = 2.4 m³/s.
3. If the channel is roughly rectangular, wetted perimeter = 0.5 + 8 + 0.5 = 9 m.
:::

## River regimes in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has one rainy season, roughly November to March, so most rivers have a **seasonal regime**: high flow and floods from December to March, falling flow in April and May, and very low flow from about July to October. Many smaller rivers are **seasonal (ephemeral)**: in the dry season they shrink to pools or a dry sandy bed, while water still moves slowly through the sand below. Rivers fed by the wetter Eastern Highlands keep more base flow.

The Zambezi at the Victoria Falls behaves differently. Much of its water comes from rain in Angola and Zambia far upstream, and it is slowed by wide floodplains on the way, so the falls usually carry most water around **April**, after Zimbabwe's rains have ended, and least around **November**.

## Flood hydrographs

A **storm hydrograph** shows how a river's discharge changes after a period of rain. It is drawn with rainfall as bars and discharge as a line against time.

| Part | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Base flow | Normal flow fed by groundwater |
| Rising limb | Discharge increasing as storm water arrives |
| Peak discharge | Highest flow |
| Lag time | Time between peak rainfall and peak discharge |
| Falling (recessional) limb | Discharge decreasing after the peak |

A **"flashy"** hydrograph (short lag time, high peak, steep limbs) results from steep slopes, impermeable rock, saturated or baked soils, little vegetation and urban surfaces. A **gentle** hydrograph (long lag time, low peak) results from gentle slopes, permeable rock, deep soils and forest.

![Storm hydrograph from the lesson's example: rainfall peaks at 10:00, discharge rises from a base flow of 3 m³/s to a peak of 18 m³/s at 22:00 (a lag time of 12 hours, a rise of 15 m³/s) and then falls back along the falling limb.](media:diagrams/storm-hydrograph.png "Figure: a storm hydrograph with base flow, rising limb, peak discharge, falling limb and lag time")

:::example{title="Reading a hydrograph from a table"}
Fictional data: rainfall peaked at 10:00 on day 1 and discharge peaked at 22:00 on day 1, rising from a base flow of 3 m³/s to 18 m³/s.

1. Lag time = 22:00 − 10:00 = 12 hours.
2. Rise in discharge = 18 − 3 = 15 m³/s.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up infiltration and percolation"}
**Infiltration** is water soaking into the soil surface. **Percolation** is water moving further down from the soil into the rock below.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying velocity falls downstream"}
In the Bradshaw model mean velocity **increases** downstream because the channel becomes more efficient, even though the gradient decreases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring lag time from the start of the rain"}
Lag time runs from **peak rainfall** to **peak discharge**, not from when the rain started.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A drainage basin is an open system: input (precipitation), stores, flows (infiltration, throughflow, overland flow, percolation, groundwater flow) and outputs (evapotranspiration, discharge).
- Basin features: watershed, source, tributary, confluence, mouth. Zimbabwe drains to the Zambezi, Save and Limpopo, separated by the central watershed.
- Bradshaw model: discharge, width, depth, velocity and load increase downstream; gradient, particle size and roughness decrease.
- Most Zimbabwean rivers have a seasonal regime with high flow in about December to March; many smaller ones are seasonal.
- Hydrographs: lag time is from peak rainfall to peak discharge; steep, bare, impermeable or urban basins give flashy hydrographs.
:::
