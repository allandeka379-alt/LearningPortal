## Introduction

After a heavy storm in January, the Mazowe or the Mzingwane turns the colour of strong tea. That colour is soil and sand: the river is carrying part of the landscape downstream. In the dry season the same river leaves sandbanks that people dig for building sand. Every river is constantly doing three jobs: **eroding** (wearing away its bed and banks), **transporting** what it has eroded, and **depositing** it somewhere else.

Which job it does at any place depends on its **energy**. This lesson explains the processes; the landforms they create (waterfalls, gorges, meanders, floodplains, deltas) are covered in the two landform lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Erosion"}
The wearing away and removal of rock and soil by a moving agent, here running water and the load it carries.
:::

:::definition{term="Load"}
All the material a river carries: boulders, pebbles, sand, silt, clay and dissolved minerals.
:::

:::definition{term="Transportation"}
The movement of the load downstream by the river.
:::

:::definition{term="Deposition"}
The dropping of load when the river no longer has enough energy to carry it.
:::

:::definition{term="Vertical and lateral erosion"}
**Vertical erosion** deepens the channel (downcutting into the bed). **Lateral erosion** widens it (cutting sideways into the banks).
:::

## Where does a river get its energy?

A river's energy comes from the **mass of water** and the **gradient** it flows down. More water (higher discharge) and faster flow (higher velocity) mean more energy. Much of this energy is used simply to overcome **friction** with the bed and banks. Only the surplus is available to erode and transport. When a river is in flood its discharge and velocity rise sharply, so most erosion and transport happens in a few days of high flow each year. In Zimbabwe that means the rainy season.

## The four processes of erosion

| Process | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Hydraulic action** | The sheer force of moving water hits the bed and banks. Water and air are forced into cracks, compressed, then released, loosening and breaking off rock. |
| **Abrasion (corrasion)** | Load carried by the river is scraped and bounced along the bed and banks, wearing them away like sandpaper. This is the most effective process in most rivers. |
| **Attrition** | Pieces of load knock against each other and against the bed, so they break into smaller, rounder pieces. Attrition wears down the load, not the channel. |
| **Solution (corrosion)** | Slightly acidic water dissolves soluble rocks such as limestone and dolomite and carries the minerals away. |

:::example{title="Diagram in words: abrasion making a pothole"}
1. Swirling water (an eddy) traps a few pebbles in a small hollow in the rock bed.
2. The current spins the pebbles round and round, grinding the hollow deeper and wider (abrasion).
3. The pebbles themselves are worn smooth and round (attrition) and are replaced by new ones.
4. In time a deep, round hole forms in the bed: a **pothole**.
:::

### Vertical or lateral?

- In the **upper course**, the river is high above its base level (the sea or a lake) and its load is large and angular. Bouncing boulders cut **downwards**, so **vertical erosion** dominates, producing deep, narrow V-shaped valleys.
- In the **middle and lower courses**, the river is closer to base level. The current swings from side to side, so **lateral erosion** cuts into the outer banks of bends, widening the valley.

## The four processes of transportation

**Diagram in words**: draw a channel cross-section with the bed at the bottom and show, from the bottom up:

1. **Traction**: the largest boulders and pebbles are rolled and pushed along the bed. This happens mainly in floods.
2. **Saltation**: smaller pebbles and coarse sand are bounced along the bed in a series of hops.
3. **Suspension**: fine sand, silt and clay are carried within the flow, making the water look brown. This is usually the largest part of the load.
4. **Solution**: dissolved minerals are carried invisibly in the water.

The size of particle a river can move rises steeply with velocity. A river in flood can roll boulders that would not move at all at low flow.

:::example{title="Comparing the load at two sites (fictional data)"}
Students measured the long axis of 20 pebbles at two sites on a fictional river.

| Site | Distance from source (km) | Mean long axis (mm) | Mostly angular or rounded? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1 | 140 | Angular |
| B | 30 | 35 | Rounded |

1. Change in mean size = 140 − 35 = 105 mm.
2. As a percentage of the size at A: 105 ÷ 140 × 100 = 75 % smaller.
3. Explanation: **attrition** and **abrasion** during transport break pebbles into smaller, smoother, rounder pieces downstream.
:::

## Deposition: why and where

A river deposits its load when its energy falls, so it can no longer carry everything. This happens when:

- **velocity falls**, for example on the inside of a meander bend, where water is shallow and slow;
- **discharge falls** after a flood or at the end of the rainy season;
- the river **enters a lake, reservoir or the sea**, where the current is checked (deltas form, and dams such as Kariba trap sediment);
- the river **overflows its banks**, so water spreads over the floodplain, slows and drops silt;
- the **load increases** suddenly, for example after soil erosion on bare slopes upstream.

Deposition is **sorted**: the largest, heaviest material is dropped first, then sand, then silt and finally clay, which settles only in very still water.

:::example{title="Case study: silting of rivers and dams in Zimbabwe"}
Where slopes are overgrazed or cleared for cultivation, heavy rain washes soil into rivers. Rivers then carry more sediment than they can move in the dry season, and sandbanks build up in the channel. Small dams in communal areas can fill with silt and lose much of their storage. This is **deposition caused by an increase in load**, and it is one reason why soil conservation upstream protects water supplies downstream.
:::

## How energy and processes change downstream

| | Upper course | Middle course | Lower course |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gradient | Steep | Moderate | Gentle |
| Discharge | Low | Higher | Highest |
| Main erosion | Vertical | Lateral and vertical | Lateral |
| Main transport | Traction and saltation of large, angular load (in floods) | Saltation and suspension | Suspension and solution of fine load |
| Deposition | Little (large boulders after floods) | On the inside of bends | Much: floodplain, levées, delta |

Although the gradient is steep upstream, much of the energy is lost to friction with a rough, shallow channel. Downstream, discharge and channel efficiency increase, so the river has more total energy, but it uses it mainly to carry a large volume of fine load in suspension.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing attrition with abrasion"}
**Abrasion** wears away the **bed and banks**. **Attrition** wears down the **load itself**. If the question asks how pebbles become smaller and rounder, the answer is attrition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying deposition happens because the river is 'tired'"}
Explain it in terms of energy: deposition happens when **velocity or discharge falls**, so the river cannot carry its load.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up solution in erosion and in transport"}
Solution is both a process of **erosion** (dissolving rock) and of **transportation** (carrying dissolved minerals). Say which one you mean.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Questions such as "describe two processes of river erosion" (ZIMSEC Paper 2, Cambridge Paper 1) need the **name plus how it works**. "Abrasion" alone earns one mark at most; "abrasion: load scraped along the bed wears it away" earns two.
:::

:::exam-tip
If you are asked to draw the processes of transportation, a labelled channel cross-section with four arrows (rolling, bouncing, carried, dissolved) is quick and earns full marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A river's energy depends on its discharge and velocity; most is used to overcome friction, and most erosion happens in floods.
- Erosion: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition (wears the load) and solution. Vertical erosion dominates upstream, lateral erosion downstream.
- Transport: traction (rolling), saltation (bouncing), suspension (carried) and solution (dissolved).
- Deposition happens when velocity or discharge falls, at lakes, reservoirs and the sea, on floodplains and on the inside of bends; it is sorted by size.
- Downstream, load becomes smaller and rounder, and deposition becomes more important.
:::
