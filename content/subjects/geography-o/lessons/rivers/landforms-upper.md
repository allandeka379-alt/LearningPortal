## Introduction

Zimbabwe's most famous landform is a river landform. At the **Victoria Falls** the whole Zambezi, about 1.7 km wide, drops roughly 100 m into a narrow chasm, throwing up spray seen from far away; the local name *Mosi-oa-Tunya* means "the smoke that thunders". Below the falls the river races through a zigzag of deep gorges. The falls and gorges are a World Heritage Site and the centre of a major tourist industry.

In this lesson you will learn how river erosion produces the landforms of a river's **upper course**, and why the Victoria Falls do not quite follow the textbook model. You need to know the processes of erosion (hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution) from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="V-shaped valley"}
A steep-sided, narrow valley with a V-shaped cross-section, formed by vertical erosion and the weathering and mass movement of the valley sides.
:::

:::definition{term="Interlocking spurs"}
Ridges of higher land that project alternately from each side of a valley, so that they overlap like the teeth of a zip when viewed upstream.
:::

:::definition{term="Waterfall"}
A sudden, steep drop in the course of a river, where water falls vertically over a rock step.
:::

:::definition{term="Gorge"}
A narrow, steep-sided valley, often deeper than it is wide, frequently formed as a waterfall retreats upstream.
:::

:::definition{term="Rapids"}
A stretch of fast, turbulent water flowing over a steep, uneven bed, often where bands of hard and soft rock alternate.
:::

## V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs

**Formation in words** (draw three cross-sections side by side):

1. In the upper course the river is high above base level and has a steep gradient. In floods it carries large, angular boulders.
2. These are dragged and bounced along the bed, cutting downwards by **abrasion** and **hydraulic action**: **vertical erosion**.
3. As the channel deepens, the valley sides are exposed. **Weathering** (for example by rain, frost or plant roots) loosens rock and soil.
4. Loosened material slides or creeps down the slope (**mass movement**) into the river, which carries it away.
5. The result is a valley with steep, straight sides and a narrow floor almost filled by the river: a **V shape**.

Because the river is small and vertical erosion is dominant, it has little energy to cut sideways. It winds around the more resistant ridges of rock rather than through them, leaving **interlocking spurs**.

![A V-shaped valley in cross-section, with steep straight sides and a narrow floor cut by vertical erosion, and in plan view where a small river winds between ridges (interlocking spurs) that project alternately from each side.](media:diagrams/v-valley-interlocking-spurs.png "Figure: a V-shaped valley in cross-section and interlocking spurs in plan view")

:::example{title="Reading a V-shaped valley on a map"}
On a topographical map, a V-shaped valley appears as contours forming tight V shapes (or U shapes) pointing **upstream**, towards higher ground. Closely spaced contours on both sides show the steep valley sides. Interlocking spurs show as contours bulging alternately from each side of the river.
:::

## Potholes

Potholes are round hollows drilled into a rocky river bed. Eddies spin pebbles inside small hollows, and the pebbles grind the hollow deeper by **abrasion**. They are common in rocky beds in the upper course and in gorges.

## Rapids

Where a river flows over alternating bands of hard and soft rock, the soft rock is eroded faster, leaving a series of small steps. The water becomes shallow, fast and turbulent: **rapids**. Rapids also form where a river crosses a jumble of boulders. Rapids in the gorges below the Victoria Falls are used for white-water rafting.

## Waterfalls and gorges: the general model

**Formation in words** (draw four stages in a long section):

1. A band of **hard, resistant rock** lies on top of **softer, less resistant rock** across the river's path.
2. The river erodes the softer rock downstream faster than the hard rock, so a **step** forms in the long profile.
3. Water falls over the step. Its force (hydraulic action), and the rocks it swirls round (abrasion), scour a deep **plunge pool** at the foot.
4. Spray and swirling water **undercut** the soft rock at the back of the plunge pool, leaving an **overhang** of hard rock.
5. The overhang has nothing to support it and eventually **collapses** into the plunge pool. The broken blocks are used to erode the pool further.
6. The process repeats, and the waterfall **retreats upstream**, leaving a steep-sided **gorge** in front of it.

![Long section of a waterfall: a hard rock cap lies over softer rock; the soft rock is eroded faster, a plunge pool is scoured and the hard rock overhangs, then collapses, so the falls retreat upstream and leave a gorge downstream.](media:diagrams/waterfall-retreat-gorge.png "Figure: how a waterfall forms, retreats and leaves a gorge")

:::example{title="How fast does a waterfall retreat? (fictional data)"}
A fictional waterfall has retreated 450 m upstream in 1,500 years, leaving a gorge.

1. Rate of retreat = 450 ÷ 1,500 = 0.3 m per year.
2. At the same rate, the extra retreat in the next 200 years = 0.3 × 200 = 60 m.

Real rates vary greatly with rock type and discharge, and collapse happens in sudden steps rather than smoothly.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: the Victoria Falls and the Batoka Gorge"}
- **Setting**: the Zambezi flows across a wide plateau of **basalt**, a hard rock formed from ancient lava flows, on the border of Zimbabwe and Zambia.
- **Lines of weakness**: as the basalt cooled and was later stressed, it cracked along **joints and faults**. Some cracks were filled with softer, weaker material.
- **Formation**: the river found a weakness running across its course and eroded it into a deep, narrow chasm. The whole river now pours over the lip of hard basalt into this crack: the present falls, about 1.7 km wide and about 100 m high.
- **Retreat**: water leaves the chasm through a narrow outlet and has eroded back along the next line of weakness. Over a very long time the falls have retreated upstream in a series of steps, each leaving a gorge. That is why the gorges below the falls form a **zigzag**: each section marks a former position of the falls. The Batoka Gorge continues the zigzag downstream.
- **Future**: at the western (Zimbabwean) end, the Devil's Cataract is lower than the rest of the lip, and the river is thought to be cutting back along a new line of weakness there.
- **Importance**: tourism (viewing, rafting on the rapids, bungee jumping from the bridge), a World Heritage Site, a rainforest kept damp by the spray.

So the Victoria Falls show the same processes as the general model (hydraulic action, abrasion, retreat and gorge formation), but the weakness exploited is a line of **joints and faults**, not a layer of soft rock under hard rock.
:::

Other waterfalls in Zimbabwe are found where rivers drop off the edge of the plateau, especially in the Eastern Highlands, for example the Mutarazi Falls in the Nyanga area.

## Comparing upper-course landforms

| Landform | Main process | Shape and features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V-shaped valley | Vertical erosion plus weathering and mass movement | Steep straight sides, narrow floor |
| Interlocking spurs | River winding round resistant spurs | Overlapping ridges |
| Potholes | Abrasion by swirling pebbles | Round hollows in the bed |
| Rapids | Differential erosion of hard and soft bands | Turbulent, shallow, fast water |
| Waterfall | Undercutting and collapse at a rock step | Vertical drop, plunge pool, overhang |
| Gorge | Retreat of a waterfall | Narrow, very steep sides, deep |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the river carves the whole V shape on its own"}
The river cuts **down**. The sloping sides of the V are produced by **weathering and mass movement** of the valley sides. Mention both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Missing the sequence in a waterfall answer"}
Examiners reward the **order**: hard over soft rock → soft rock eroded → plunge pool → undercutting → overhang → collapse → retreat → gorge. Skipping steps loses marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying the hard-over-soft model to the Victoria Falls"}
The Victoria Falls formed along **joints and faults** in basalt. Use the case study to show you know this.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Upper-course landforms are formed mainly by vertical erosion (abrasion and hydraulic action).
- V-shaped valleys: river cuts down; weathering and mass movement shape the sides. Interlocking spurs form as the river winds round resistant ridges.
- Potholes are drilled by swirling pebbles; rapids form over alternating hard and soft rock.
- Waterfalls form at a step of hard rock over soft; undercutting, overhang collapse and retreat leave a gorge.
- The Victoria Falls formed along joints and faults in basalt; their retreat left the zigzag gorges below the falls.
:::
