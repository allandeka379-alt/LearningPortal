## Introduction

Physical fieldwork measures the natural environment: how fast a stream flows, how a beach slopes, which plants grow where, and how temperature changes across a school grounds. A stream near your school, such as a tributary that feeds a local dam, is a perfect site for practice, and the same methods apply to any river or beach in an examination question.

Cambridge Paper 4 often describes a river or coastal study, gives you a table of fictional results and asks about equipment, method, calculations and accuracy. This lesson explains the standard methods and the calculations you must be able to do.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Discharge"}
The volume of water passing a point in a river each second, measured in cubic metres per second (m³/s, also called cumecs). Discharge = cross-sectional area × velocity.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-sectional area"}
The area of the river's cross-section at one site: width × mean depth, in m².
:::

:::definition{term="Wetted perimeter"}
The length of the river bed and banks in contact with the water across one cross-section.
:::

:::definition{term="Quadrat"}
A square frame (often 0.5 m or 1 m across, sometimes divided into a grid) used to sample vegetation or pebbles at a point.
:::

:::definition{term="Transect"}
A line across an area along which measurements are taken at regular intervals, for example across a river channel, up a beach or through vegetation.
:::

## Rivers

| Measurement | Equipment | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Width | Tape measure | Stretch tape from bank to bank at water level, at right angles to the flow |
| Depth | Metre rule | Measure at equal intervals across the channel (for example every 0.5 m); calculate the mean |
| Velocity | Float (orange or dog biscuit) and stopwatch, or a flow meter | Time the float over a measured distance (for example 10 m) three times; velocity = distance ÷ mean time |
| Bedload size | Ruler or calipers | Measure the long (a) axis of pebbles picked at points along the transect |
| Bedload roundness | Powers' roundness chart | Match each pebble to a scale from very angular to very rounded |
| Gradient | Clinometer and two ranging poles | Measure the angle of the water surface or bed between poles a set distance apart |

:::worked-solution[Discharge at one site (fictional data)]
1. Width of channel = 3.0 m.
2. Depths at six equally spaced points: 0.10, 0.22, 0.30, 0.34, 0.24 and 0.12 m. Mean depth = 1.32 ÷ 6 = 0.22 m.
3. Cross-sectional area = width × mean depth = 3.0 × 0.22 = 0.66 m².
4. Mean velocity from float times = 0.45 m/s.
5. Discharge = area × velocity = 0.66 × 0.45 = 0.297 m³/s (about 0.30 m³/s).
:::

Downstream, results usually show width, depth, cross-sectional area, velocity and discharge increasing, while pebbles become smaller and more rounded through attrition. This pattern is described by the Bradshaw model. Real streams often have anomalies: a weir, a bridge, a pool or a riffle, or a tributary joining between two sites.

:::example{title="Accuracy problems in river studies"}
- A float measures only **surface** velocity, and wind or weeds can slow or speed it.
- Starting and stopping the stopwatch adds reaction-time errors: repeat three times and average.
- The metre rule can sink into soft mud, or water can pile up against it, giving false depth readings.
- Choosing "interesting" pebbles rather than sampling systematically biases size and roundness results.
- Rain the day before raises discharge, so all sites should be measured on the same day.
:::

## Beaches

A **beach profile** shows how the beach slopes from the sea to the back of the beach. Two ranging poles are placed a set distance apart (or at each **break of slope**) along a transect at right angles to the sea. A **clinometer** is used to read the angle from one pole to the same height on the next. Distances and angles are then drawn as a profile.

**Longshore drift** can be studied by placing a float or painted pebble in the swash and measuring how far it moves along the beach in a set time, or by measuring pebble size and the height of sand against groynes at points along the beach.

:::example{title="Reading a profile table (fictional)"}
| Section | Distance (m) | Angle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sea edge to pole 2 | 5 | 4° up |
| Pole 2 to pole 3 | 5 | 9° up |
| Pole 3 to pole 4 (berm) | 5 | 2° up |
| Pole 4 to back of beach | 5 | 15° up |

The beach is gentle near the sea, steepens, then flattens at a berm before rising steeply to the back of the beach. The steepest section (15°) is at the back.
:::

## Vegetation and microclimate

**Quadrats** are placed at regular points along a **transect** (systematic sampling) or at random coordinates in an area (random sampling). In each quadrat record the plant species, the **percentage cover** of each and sometimes the height of vegetation. With a 100-square gridded quadrat, each square covered counts as 1%.

**Microclimate** studies compare small places, such as a car park, a lawn and under trees. Use a thermometer (temperature, read in the shade), an anemometer (wind speed), a hygrometer or wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (humidity) and a rain gauge, all at the **same height** and at the **same time** at each site.

:::example{title="A microclimate prediction"}
Hypothesis: the tarred car park is warmer than the area under trees at 13:00. The tar absorbs and re-radiates heat and has no shade, while trees shade the ground and release moisture that cools the air, so the hypothesis is likely to be supported.
:::

## Recording and accuracy

Design a recording sheet before going into the field, with columns for site number, distance, each repeat reading and the mean. Record units in the column headings. Note the weather and anything unusual, such as a fallen tree, because these explain anomalies later.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using one depth reading"}
The deepest point is not the mean depth. Measure at equal intervals across the channel and average.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing units in discharge"}
Width and depth must both be in metres and velocity in m/s; then discharge is in m³/s. Depths measured in centimetres must be converted first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equipment named but not used"}
"Use a clinometer" earns little. Say how: "stand ranging poles 5 m apart, sight from the eye-level mark on one to the same mark on the next and read the angle".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show every step of a calculation"}
For discharge, write the mean depth, the area and then the discharge, with units. Method marks are given for correct steps even if the final figure is wrong.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining results"}
Link river results to processes: discharge rises downstream because tributaries add water; pebbles get smaller and rounder because of attrition and abrasion over distance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- River width (tape), depth (metre rule at intervals), velocity (float or flow meter over a set distance) give cross-sectional area and discharge.
- Discharge = cross-sectional area × velocity, in m³/s.
- Beach profiles use ranging poles and a clinometer along a transect; longshore drift is traced with floats or pebble surveys.
- Quadrats along transects record percentage cover; microclimate readings need the same height and time at every site.
- Repeat readings, sample systematically and measure all sites on the same day to improve reliability.
:::
