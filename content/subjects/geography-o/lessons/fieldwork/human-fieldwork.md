## Introduction

Human geography studies people: where they live, shop, work and travel, and how they feel about their surroundings. You cannot ask everyone in Bulawayo where they shop, or count every car that passes through Masvingo, so human fieldwork depends on careful **sampling** and well-designed **recording sheets**.

ZIMSEC asks you to collect and present population data from your school or local community, for example a school census of ages and home areas. Cambridge Paper 4 regularly sets questionnaires, pedestrian and traffic counts, environmental quality surveys and land-use surveys. This lesson covers all of them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sample"}
A small part of a whole population that is studied so that conclusions can be drawn about the whole. A larger sample is usually more reliable.
:::

:::definition{term="Random sampling"}
Every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen, for example by drawing names or using random numbers.
:::

:::definition{term="Systematic sampling"}
Choosing at a regular interval, for example every 10th house or every 5th person passing.
:::

:::definition{term="Stratified sampling"}
Dividing the population into groups (strata) and sampling each group in proportion to its size, for example by age group or Form.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunistic (convenience) sampling"}
Choosing whoever is available and willing. It is quick but likely to be biased.
:::

:::definition{term="Pilot survey"}
A small trial run of a questionnaire or method, used to find and fix problems before the main survey.
:::

## Sampling in practice

:::worked-solution[Systematic sampling interval]
1. A street has 240 houses and you want a sample of 30.
2. Interval = 240 ÷ 30 = 8.
3. Choose a random starting house between 1 and 8 (say house 3), then survey houses 3, 11, 19, 27 and so on.
:::

:::worked-solution[A stratified sample of a school (fictional numbers)]
1. A school has 600 pupils: Form 1 = 180, Form 2 = 150, Form 3 = 150, Form 4 = 120. You want a sample of 60.
2. The sample is 60 ÷ 600 = 10% of the school.
3. Take 10% of each Form: 18 from Form 1, 15 from Form 2, 15 from Form 3 and 12 from Form 4 (total 60).
4. Within each Form, choose pupils randomly, for example by drawing register numbers.
:::

Stratified sampling makes sure that every group is fairly represented. Opportunistic sampling, such as questioning only people who stop to talk, may over-represent some groups (for example people who are not in a hurry).

## Questionnaires and interviews

A questionnaire is a list of questions asked in the same way to every person. **Closed questions** give fixed answers to choose from (easy to count and graph); **open questions** let people answer in their own words (richer, but harder to analyse).

:::example{title="Part of a shoppers' questionnaire (fictional study)"}
1. Where have you travelled from today? (name of suburb or village)
2. How did you travel? Walk / kombi / bus / car / other
3. How often do you shop here? Daily / weekly / monthly / less often
4. What is the main reason you shop here? (open question)

Good practice: introduce yourself and the purpose; keep it short; do not ask for names or other personal details; avoid leading questions such as "Don't you agree the market is too dirty?"; thank the person; work in pairs in public places.
:::

Answers to question 1 can be plotted as **desire lines** from each home area to the shopping centre, showing its **sphere of influence** (the area it serves).

## Pedestrian and traffic counts

Counts measure how busy a place is. Use a **tally sheet** with columns for each site and time, and count for the same length of time at every site (for example 10 minutes) and at the same times of day. Traffic counts can separate vehicle types (cars, kombis, buses, lorries). Results are shown as **bar graphs**, **located bar graphs** on a map, or **flow-line maps** whose line widths are proportional to the counts.

:::example{title="Pedestrian count results (fictional)"}
| Site | Distance from town centre (m) | Count (10 min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 | 186 |
| 2 | 250 | 121 |
| 3 | 450 | 64 |
| 4 | 650 | 72 |
| 5 | 850 | 23 |

Counts fall with distance from the centre, from 186 to 23, except site 4 (72), which is higher than site 3 (64), perhaps because of a bus stop or clinic nearby.
:::

## Environmental quality and bi-polar surveys

An environmental quality survey scores a place on several criteria, each between two opposite descriptions (a **bi-polar** scale), for example from −2 (very poor) to +2 (very good).

:::example{title="Bi-polar scores for one street (fictional)"}
| Criterion | −2 | −1 | 0 | +1 | +2 | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Litter: lots … none | | ✓ | | | | −1 |
| Noise: loud … quiet | | | | ✓ | | +1 |
| Building condition: poor … good | | | | | ✓ | +2 |
| Green space: none … plenty | | ✓ | | | | −1 |
| Total | | | | | | +1 |
:::

Scores are subjective (two people may judge the same street differently), so the same pair should score every site, using agreed descriptions for each number, or several people score and the mean is used.

## Land-use surveys, the CBD and population data

A **land-use survey** records the use of each building (shops, offices, housing, industry, open space) on a base map, often with a colour or letter key. To **delimit the CBD**, students record land use and building height along transects out from the centre; the edge of the CBD is where shops and offices give way to housing and building height falls.

For **population data** in a school or village, a short census form records age, sex, Form or household size and place of birth. The results can be drawn as a **population pyramid** (age-sex bars) or bar graphs and compared with national data from a census report.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a big sample is automatically unbiased"}
Asking 200 people at one bus stop at 07:00 gives a large but biased sample of commuters. The sampling method and timing matter as much as size.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leading or personal questions"}
Questions that suggest an answer, or ask for names and incomes, produce unreliable answers and raise ethical problems. Keep questions neutral and anonymous.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting for different lengths of time"}
A 5-minute count at one site cannot be compared with a 15-minute count at another. Keep the time and the time of day the same.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describing a method"}
Say what is recorded, where, when, for how long and how sites or people are chosen. For example: "Count pedestrians walking past in both directions for 10 minutes at each of eight sites, at 10:00 on a weekday, using a tally sheet."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Sampling questions"}
If asked to justify a sampling method, link it to the population: stratified for groups of different sizes, systematic for evenly spread houses, random to avoid bias. Show the calculation for interval or proportions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Samples can be random, systematic, stratified or opportunistic; a pilot survey tests the method first.
- Questionnaires use closed and open questions, must be neutral and anonymous, and answers can be mapped as desire lines.
- Pedestrian and traffic counts use tally sheets, equal counting times and comparable times of day.
- Bi-polar surveys score environmental quality on opposite descriptions; they are subjective, so keep scorers consistent.
- Land-use surveys delimit the CBD; school or community census data can be drawn as population pyramids.
:::
