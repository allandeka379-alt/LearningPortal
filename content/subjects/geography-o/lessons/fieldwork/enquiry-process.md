## Introduction

Geographers do not only read about places: they go out and measure them. A class might ask whether a stream gets faster downstream, whether shoppers travel further to a town centre than to a local shopping centre, or whether litter is worse near a bus terminus. To answer such a question fairly, every investigation follows the same series of stages, called the **route to geographical enquiry**.

In the Cambridge examination, Paper 4 (Geographical Investigations) is built entirely on this route, and Coursework (Component 3) asks you to carry it out yourself. ZIMSEC continuous assessment projects follow the same logic. Learn the stages once and you can apply them to any river, beach, street or village.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Aim"}
A short statement of what the investigation sets out to find out, for example "to investigate how the river channel changes downstream".
:::

:::definition{term="Hypothesis"}
A statement that can be tested with data and shown to be true or false, for example "the river's velocity increases with distance downstream". The plural is hypotheses.
:::

:::definition{term="Null hypothesis"}
A statement that there is no relationship between the two things being measured, for example "there is no relationship between distance downstream and velocity".
:::

:::definition{term="Primary and secondary data"}
Primary data are collected by the investigator in the field (counts, measurements, questionnaires). Secondary data were collected by someone else (census reports, maps, websites, weather station records).
:::

:::definition{term="Reliability and accuracy"}
Results are reliable if repeating the method gives similar results. They are accurate if they are close to the true value.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk assessment"}
A list of the hazards in the field, how likely and serious each is, and the precautions that reduce the danger.
:::

## The stages of an enquiry

| Stage | What you do | Example (a stream study) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Question, aim and hypotheses | Choose a question that can be answered with data; write testable hypotheses | "Channel width increases downstream." |
| 2. Planning | Choose sites, equipment, sampling, timing; write a risk assessment; design recording sheets | Six sites, 200 m apart; tape measure, metre rule, float, stopwatch |
| 3. Data collection | Collect primary data; gather secondary data | Width, depth and float time at each site |
| 4. Presentation | Choose suitable graphs, maps and tables | Scatter graph of width against distance; cross-sections |
| 5. Analysis and interpretation | Describe patterns, quote figures, explain them, note anomalies | Width rises from 1.2 m to 3.8 m, apart from site 4 |
| 6. Conclusion | Say whether each hypothesis is accepted, rejected or partly accepted, using evidence | Hypothesis accepted |
| 7. Evaluation | Judge the method, accuracy and reliability; suggest improvements and further work | Repeat float times three times; add more sites |

### Writing good hypotheses

A good hypothesis names **two variables** and the expected **direction** of the relationship, so that the data can prove it right or wrong. "Rivers are interesting" cannot be tested. "Pebble size decreases downstream" can.

:::example{title="Turning an aim into hypotheses"}
Aim: to find out how land use changes away from the centre of a town (fictional study).

- Hypothesis 1: the percentage of shops and offices decreases with distance from the town centre.
- Hypothesis 2: building height decreases with distance from the town centre.
- Null hypothesis for 2: there is no relationship between building height and distance from the centre.

Each names something measured (percentage of shops; number of storeys) and something it depends on (distance).
:::

### Planning and risk assessment

Planning decides **where, when and how** data are collected. You choose sampling methods (see the human and physical fieldwork lessons), make a recording sheet with columns for every measurement, and agree a time so that conditions are comparable (traffic counts at 08:00 and 13:00 are not comparable).

:::example{title="Part of a risk assessment for a river study (fictional)"}
| Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fast or deep water | Falling in, being swept away | Do not enter water above knee depth; work in groups; check rain forecast |
| Slippery rocks | Twisted ankle | Wear boots with grip; walk, do not run |
| Polluted water, bilharzia | Infection | Cover cuts; wear waterproof boots or waders where possible; wash hands afterwards |
| Crossing roads to sites | Traffic accident | Cross only at agreed points under supervision |
:::

### Presenting and analysing data

Choose a method that suits the data: **line graphs** for continuous change (traffic through the day), **bar graphs** for categories (land-use types), **scatter graphs** to test a relationship between two variables, **pie charts** for proportions, **cross-sections** for river channels and beach profiles, and **located graphs or maps** to show where results came from.

When you analyse, follow **describe, quantify, explain, anomalies**:

1. Describe the overall trend ("velocity generally increases downstream").
2. Quote data ("from 0.21 m/s at site 1 to 0.64 m/s at site 6").
3. Explain it using geographical knowledge ("less friction as the channel becomes deeper and smoother").
4. Point out anomalies ("site 4 is slower because a fallen tree slowed the flow").

### Conclusions and evaluation

The conclusion answers the hypothesis directly: **accepted**, **rejected** or **partly accepted**, with evidence. Results rarely prove a hypothesis everywhere, so "partly true" with reasons is often the best answer.

The evaluation asks how far the results can be trusted. Common weaknesses are too few sites or people, a single reading at each site, measurements on only one day, equipment errors (a float caught by weeds), and human bias (only asking friendly-looking people). Improvements must fix a named weakness: "take three float readings at each site and calculate the mean".

:::example{title="Evaluating a fictional pedestrian count"}
A group counted pedestrians for 5 minutes at eight sites, all on one Saturday morning.
- Weakness: one short count per site, so a single bus arriving could distort the result.
- Improvement: count for 10 minutes at each site, three times during the day, on a weekday and a Saturday, and use the mean.
- Further work: add sites in all directions from the centre, not only along one road.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing an untestable hypothesis"}
"Traffic is a problem in Mutare" cannot be measured. Name variables: "traffic flow is greatest on roads nearest the town centre".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the hypothesis is proved"}
Small fieldwork samples cannot prove anything absolutely. Say the hypothesis is accepted or supported by the evidence, and quote the evidence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague improvements"}
"Be more careful" or "use better equipment" earns nothing. Name the weakness and the specific change, for example "use a flow meter instead of a float, which is blown by the wind".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing accuracy with reliability"}
Repeating a reading improves reliability (consistency). Using a more precise instrument improves accuracy.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 4 is about method"}
Cambridge Paper 4 questions take you through a fictional investigation stage by stage. Read the hypothesis first and keep referring back to it; every "describe the results" and "conclusion" answer must use the figures in the table.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
**Describe** a method: say what, where, how and how often, as numbered steps. **Suggest** an improvement: one change plus why it helps. **Explain** a result: give the geographical reason.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Conclusion answers"}
A strong conclusion states the decision, quotes two pieces of data and mentions an exception. For example: "Partly accepted: width increased from site 1 to site 6, but site 4 was narrower than site 3."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The route to enquiry: question and hypotheses, planning, data collection, presentation, analysis, conclusion, evaluation.
- A hypothesis is a testable statement naming two variables and the expected relationship; a null hypothesis says there is no relationship.
- Planning includes sampling, recording sheets, timing and a risk assessment with precautions.
- Analysis describes the trend, quotes data, explains it and identifies anomalies.
- Conclusions accept, reject or partly accept the hypothesis with evidence; evaluations name weaknesses, specific improvements and further work.
:::
