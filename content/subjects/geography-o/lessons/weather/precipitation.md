## Introduction

On a hot November afternoon in Harare the morning sky is clear, by 14:00 towering clouds have built up, and by 16:00 a thunderstorm is drumming on the roofs. In the Eastern Highlands, mist and drizzle can hang over the hills for days in June while Harare stays dry. Both are precipitation, but they form in different ways.

Every type of rain has the same basic cause: **moist air rises, cools and condenses**. What differs is *what makes the air rise*. In this lesson you will learn the forms of precipitation, the steps by which rain forms, and the three types of rainfall that ZIMSEC asks you to draw and explain.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Precipitation"}
Any water, liquid or solid, that falls from the atmosphere to the ground: rain, drizzle, hail, sleet and snow. Dew and frost form directly on surfaces and are usually studied with them.
:::

:::definition{term="Dew point"}
The temperature at which air becomes saturated, so that further cooling makes water vapour condense into droplets.
:::

:::definition{term="Condensation"}
The change of water vapour (a gas) into liquid water droplets, usually around tiny particles of dust, salt or smoke called condensation nuclei.
:::

:::definition{term="Adiabatic cooling"}
Cooling of rising air because it expands as air pressure falls with height; no heat is taken away, the air simply spreads its energy over a larger volume.
:::

:::definition{term="Rain shadow"}
An area on the sheltered (leeward) side of high ground that receives much less rain than the windward side, because the air descending it is warming and drying.
:::

## Forms of precipitation

| Form | What it is | How it forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rain | Liquid drops, larger than about 0.5 mm | Cloud droplets join together until they are heavy enough to fall |
| Drizzle | Very small, closely spaced drops | Falls from low, thin layer cloud (stratus) with weak uplift |
| Hail | Balls or lumps of ice | Strong up-currents in cumulonimbus clouds carry drops up and down, adding layers of ice |
| Snow | Ice crystals joined into flakes | Forms when the cloud and the air below are below freezing |
| Dew | Water droplets on grass and surfaces | Ground cools on clear, calm nights; air touching it cools below the dew point |
| Frost | Ice crystals on surfaces | As dew, but the dew point is below 0 °C, so vapour turns straight to ice |

In Zimbabwe, rain and hail fall in the hot wet season, while dew and frost are common on clear winter nights in the cool dry season, especially in low-lying hollows on the highveld where cold air collects.

## How rain forms: the basic sequence

Think of a "parcel" of air near the ground.

1. **Evaporation**: the sun heats land and water; water evaporates and the air becomes moist.
2. **Uplift**: something forces the air to rise (heating, a mountain or a front).
3. **Adiabatic cooling**: as it rises the air expands and cools, at roughly 1 °C per 100 m while it is unsaturated (a simplified textbook rate).
4. **Dew point and condensation**: when it cools to its dew point, vapour condenses on nuclei and cloud forms. The height where this happens is the **condensation level** (the flat base of the cloud).
5. **Growth of droplets**: tiny droplets collide and join (or ice crystals grow in cold cloud tops) until they are heavy enough to fall against the up-currents.
6. **Precipitation**: the drops fall as rain, or as hail or snow if they are frozen.

:::example{title="Finding the condensation level"}
Air at the ground is 28 °C and its dew point is 18 °C. It rises and cools at about 1 °C per 100 m.

1. Cooling needed = 28 − 18 = 10 °C.
2. Height = 10 × 100 m = 1,000 m.
3. Cloud base forms at about 1,000 m above the ground.

Once the air is saturated it keeps cooling as it rises, but more slowly (often taken as about 0.5 °C per 100 m), because condensation releases heat.
:::

## Convectional rainfall

**Diagram in words** (draw it as a cross-section with arrows):

1. Strong sun heats the ground in the morning; the ground heats the air above it.
2. The warm air becomes lighter and rises in up-currents (convection currents), carrying moisture from evaporation and transpiration.
3. It cools adiabatically, reaches dew point and forms towering **cumulonimbus** cloud.
4. By mid-afternoon heavy showers or thunderstorms fall, often with lightning and sometimes hail.
5. Rain is heavy but short-lived, and the sky often clears in the evening.

**Where**: most of Zimbabwe's summer rain (November to March) is convectional, with afternoon storms. It also falls almost daily in equatorial regions such as the Congo basin and the Amazon.

## Relief (orographic) rainfall

**Diagram in words**:

1. Moist air blowing from the sea meets a mountain range.
2. It is forced to rise up the **windward** slope, cools and condenses: cloud and rain form on the windward side.
3. Having lost much of its moisture, the air crosses the summit and sinks down the **leeward** slope.
4. Sinking air is compressed and warms, so it can hold more vapour; clouds evaporate and little rain falls. This drier area is the **rain shadow**.

:::example{title="Case study: the Eastern Highlands of Zimbabwe"}
Moist air from the Indian Ocean, carried by the south-east trade winds, is forced to rise over the Eastern Highlands (Nyanga, Vumba and Chimanimani). The windward, eastern-facing slopes are the wettest part of the country, with more than about 1,000 mm a year in many places. In the cool season the same moist air brings days of low cloud, mist and drizzle known locally as **guti**. Lower ground to the west and south-west, such as parts of the Save valley, is much drier, partly because it lies in a rain shadow and partly because it is lower and hotter.
:::

Elsewhere, the Drakensberg of South Africa and the windward slopes of the Himalayas in the monsoon are classic examples.

## Frontal (cyclonic) rainfall

**Diagram in words**:

1. A mass of warm, moist air meets a mass of cold, dense air. The boundary between them is a **front**.
2. The lighter warm air is forced to rise over the wedge of cold air.
3. It cools, condenses and forms layers of cloud and rain along the front.
4. At a warm front the slope is gentle, so rain is steady and long-lasting; at a cold front the slope is steep, so rain is short and heavy.

**Where**: frontal rain is typical of the mid-latitudes, for example Britain all year and Cape Town in winter. In Zimbabwe it is uncommon: in the cool season, cold fronts passing across South Africa sometimes bring cloud, cold winds and light rain to the south of the country.

## Comparing the three types

| | Convectional | Relief | Frontal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause of uplift | Heating of the ground | High ground in the path of wind | Warm air rising over cold air |
| Typical cloud | Cumulonimbus | Stratus and cumulus on slopes | Layered (nimbostratus) and cumulonimbus at cold fronts |
| Rain | Heavy, short, afternoon storms | Steady on windward slopes | Steady (warm front) or short and heavy (cold front) |
| Example | Zimbabwe in summer; equatorial regions | Eastern Highlands; Drakensberg | Britain; Cape Town in winter |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying warm air rises and then 'turns into rain'"}
Rising air does not rain on its own. You must name the steps: it **cools** (adiabatically), reaches **dew point**, **condenses** into cloud droplets, and the droplets **grow** until they fall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the rain shadow on the windward side"}
The windward side faces the wind and gets the rain. The rain shadow is on the **leeward** (sheltered) side, where air sinks and warms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hot air holds less water"}
Warm air can hold **more** water vapour than cold air. That is why cooling leads to condensation, and why sinking, warming air in a rain shadow dries out.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 often asks you to "draw an annotated diagram" of a rainfall type. Use a simple cross-section, arrows for air movement, and labels on each stage: heating or uplift, cooling, dew point/condensation, cloud, rain. A diagram with no labels earns few marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "describe how rain forms", give the sequence in order: evaporation, rising, cooling, dew point, condensation on nuclei, droplets growing, falling. Each correct step is usually one mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
In Paper 1 multiple choice, watch for the leeward/windward trap and for the type of cloud: cumulonimbus goes with convectional storms and hail.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Precipitation includes rain, drizzle, hail, sleet and snow; dew and frost form directly on cold surfaces.
- Rain forms when moist air rises, cools adiabatically to its dew point, condenses on nuclei into cloud droplets that grow and fall.
- Convectional rain: ground heating makes air rise; heavy afternoon storms; most of Zimbabwe's summer rain.
- Relief rain: air forced over high ground; wet windward slopes (Eastern Highlands) and a dry rain shadow on the leeward side.
- Frontal rain: warm air rises over cold air at a front; common in mid-latitudes, rare in Zimbabwe.
:::
