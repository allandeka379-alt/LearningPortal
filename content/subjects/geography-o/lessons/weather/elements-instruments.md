## Introduction

Before a farmer near Chinhoyi plants maize, she wants to know whether the rains have really started. A pilot landing at Harare needs the wind direction and the air pressure. A school timetables its sports day for the dry season. All of them depend on careful **measurements of the weather**.

Geographers measure weather with simple instruments kept at a **weather station**. Many Zimbabwean schools have one. In this lesson you will learn what each element of weather is, which instrument measures it, how to read the instrument, and where a weather station should be placed. Later lessons use these readings to draw climate graphs and forecast the weather.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weather"}
The condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time, for example "hot, sunny and calm in Masvingo this afternoon". Weather changes from hour to hour and day to day.
:::

:::definition{term="Climate"}
The average weather of a place worked out from records over a long period, usually at least 30 years, for example "hot wet summers and cool dry winters".
:::

:::definition{term="Element of weather"}
One measurable part of the weather: temperature, precipitation (rainfall), humidity, air pressure, wind (speed and direction), sunshine and cloud cover.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative humidity"}
The amount of water vapour in the air compared with the most it could hold at that temperature, given as a percentage. Saturated air has a relative humidity of 100 %.
:::

## The elements and their instruments

| Element | Instrument | Unit | How it is read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Maximum-minimum (Six's) thermometer | °C | Read the bottom of each metal index; reset daily |
| Rainfall | Rain gauge | mm | Pour the water into the measuring cylinder; read at eye level |
| Humidity | Wet-and-dry bulb thermometer (hygrometer) | % | Find the difference between the two thermometers; use a humidity table |
| Air pressure | Barometer (aneroid or mercury); a barograph records it | hPa (millibars) | Read the pointer or mercury height |
| Wind direction | Wind vane | Compass point | The arrow points to where the wind comes **from** |
| Wind speed | Cup anemometer | m/s or km/h | Count the turns of the cups or read the dial |
| Sunshine | Campbell-Stokes sunshine recorder | hours | Measure the length of the burnt trace on the card |
| Cloud cover | Observation by eye | oktas (eighths of the sky) | Estimate how many eighths of the sky are covered |

### Temperature

A **maximum-minimum thermometer** (Six's thermometer) is a U-shaped glass tube containing alcohol and mercury. As the temperature rises, the mercury pushes a small metal **index** up the maximum side; as it falls, it pushes the other index up the minimum side. The indexes stay where they were pushed, so one reading each morning gives the highest and lowest temperatures of the past 24 hours. The indexes are then reset with a magnet or a push button.

From the two readings you can work out:

- **Mean daily temperature** = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2
- **Daily (diurnal) range** = maximum − minimum

:::example{title="Working with a day's readings"}
On a day in October a station near Beitbridge (fictional readings) records a maximum of 37 °C and a minimum of 22 °C.

1. Mean daily temperature = (37 + 22) ÷ 2 = 59 ÷ 2 = 29.5 °C.
2. Daily range = 37 − 22 = 15 °C.

A large daily range like this is typical of clear, dry weather: the sun heats the ground strongly by day and heat escapes quickly at night with no cloud to trap it.
:::

### Rainfall

A **rain gauge** is a funnel over a collecting jar inside a metal cylinder. It is sunk into the ground so that the rim stands about 30 cm above the surface: high enough that rain splashing off the ground does not enter, low enough that wind does not blow the rain past it. Each morning the water is poured into a **measuring cylinder** graduated in millimetres and read at eye level. Daily readings are added to give monthly and annual totals.

![Rain gauge: rain enters a funnel set with its rim 30 cm above the ground, collects in the jar inside a can sunk in the ground, and is poured into a measuring cylinder read in millimetres at eye level at the same time each day.](media:diagrams/rain-gauge-and-cylinder.png "Figure: a rain gauge and its measuring cylinder")

### Humidity

The **wet-and-dry bulb thermometer** has two ordinary thermometers side by side. The bulb of one is wrapped in muslin that dips into a small container of water.

1. Water evaporates from the muslin. Evaporation uses heat, so the wet bulb cools.
2. In dry air, evaporation is fast, so the wet bulb reads much lower than the dry bulb.
3. In moist air, evaporation is slow, so the difference is small.
4. When the air is saturated, there is no evaporation and both thermometers read the same: relative humidity is 100 %.

The difference between the readings is looked up in a **humidity table** to find the relative humidity.

### Pressure and wind

A **barometer** measures the weight of the air above. Pressure is given in hectopascals (hPa), which are the same as millibars; average sea-level pressure is about 1013 hPa, and readings are lower at high altitude. A falling barometer often warns of cloud and rain; a rising one of settled weather.

A **wind vane** has an arrow that swings into the wind, so it points to the direction the wind blows **from**. A wind blowing from the south-east towards the north-west is a south-easterly wind. The **cup anemometer** has three or four cups that spin faster as the wind gets stronger. Both should be mounted high on a pole, clear of buildings and trees.

### Sunshine and cloud

The **Campbell-Stokes sunshine recorder** is a glass sphere that focuses the sun's rays onto a card, burning a line whenever the sun shines. The total length of burnt trace gives the hours of bright sunshine. **Cloud cover** is estimated by eye in **oktas**: 0 oktas is a clear sky, 8 oktas is completely overcast.

## The weather station and the Stevenson screen

Thermometers are kept in a **Stevenson screen**, a wooden box designed to measure the true temperature of the air.

![Stevenson screen: a white, louvred wooden box with a double roof on legs so that the thermometers sit 1.2 m above short grass, in shade with free air flow; the door opens to the south so the sun never shines on the thermometers when it is opened.](media:diagrams/stevenson-screen.png "Figure: a Stevenson screen and the reason for each feature")

| Feature | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Painted white | Reflects the sun's rays so the box itself does not heat up |
| Louvred (slatted) sides | Let air circulate freely while keeping out direct sunlight and rain |
| On legs, thermometers about 1.2 m above the ground | Avoids heat given off by the ground surface |
| Double roof | Insulates against the sun's heat from above |
| Door facing away from the sun (south in Zimbabwe) | Sun does not shine on the thermometers when the door is opened |

:::example{title="Siting a school weather station"}
A Form 1 class in Gweru chooses between three places: beside the tarred car park, under a large msasa tree, and an open grassy area on the sports field. The sports field is best:

- It is **open**, away from buildings and trees that would block wind, shade the rain gauge and shelter the instruments.
- It is **grass-covered and level**: tar and concrete heat up and give off heat, raising temperature readings.
- It can be **fenced** to stop the instruments being damaged, and it is **easy to reach** every morning.
:::

Readings are taken at the **same time each day**, usually in the morning, so that records can be compared fairly.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing weather and climate"}
"Today it is raining in Mutare" describes weather. "Mutare has wet summers" describes climate. Climate is a long-term average, not one day.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the wind vane points to where the wind is going"}
The arrow points into the wind, to where it comes **from**. Winds are named by the direction they come from.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the thermometers in the sun"}
A thermometer in direct sunshine measures the heat of the sun's rays on the glass, not the air temperature. Air temperature is always measured in the shade, inside the Stevenson screen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a big wet-and-dry bulb difference means humid air"}
It is the other way round. A big difference means fast evaporation, so the air is dry. No difference means the air is saturated.
:::

Paper 1 often asks you to match an instrument with its element or unit. Learn the table above, including the units: mm for rainfall, hPa (mb) for pressure, oktas for cloud cover.
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Weather is the day-to-day state of the atmosphere; climate is the average weather over at least 30 years.
- Elements and instruments: temperature (maximum-minimum thermometer), rainfall (rain gauge), humidity (wet-and-dry bulb), pressure (barometer), wind direction (wind vane) and speed (anemometer), sunshine (sunshine recorder), cloud cover (by eye, in oktas).
- Mean daily temperature = (max + min) ÷ 2; daily range = max − min.
- A bigger wet-and-dry bulb difference means drier air; equal readings mean 100 % relative humidity.
- A weather station needs an open, level, grassy, secure site; the white, louvred Stevenson screen keeps thermometers in the shade with free air flow.
:::
