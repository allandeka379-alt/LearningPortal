## Introduction

Why does the rain arrive in Zimbabwe in November and not in June? Why do the eastern slopes get days of drizzle in winter while Bulawayo stays clear? The answers lie in huge bodies of air, thousands of kilometres across, that take on the character of the land or sea beneath them and then move. Where they meet, and where the main rain belt of the tropics lies, decide whether a week is wet or dry.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Air mass"}
A very large body of air, often over 1,000 km across, whose temperature and humidity are roughly the same horizontally because it stayed for a long time over one kind of surface.
:::

:::definition{term="Source region"}
The large, uniform area of land or sea where an air mass forms and gains its characteristics, usually under calm high pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Air stream"}
A broad, steady flow of air from a source region towards another area, such as the south-east trade winds.
:::

:::definition{term="Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)"}
A belt of low pressure near the equator where the trade winds of the two hemispheres meet (converge), forcing air to rise and bringing cloud and heavy rain.
:::

## Classifying air masses

Air masses are named by two features of their source region:

- **Latitude** (temperature): **tropical** (T), from low latitudes, warm; **polar** (P), from high latitudes, cold. (Arctic or Antarctic air is colder still.)
- **Surface** (humidity): **maritime** (m), formed over the sea, moist; **continental** (c), formed over land, dry.

| Air mass | Source region | Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tropical maritime (mT) | Warm oceans in low latitudes | Warm and moist; gives cloud and rain when it rises |
| Tropical continental (cT) | Hot deserts and dry interiors (for example the Sahara, the Kalahari in summer) | Hot and dry; clear skies, haze and dust |
| Polar maritime (mP) | Cold oceans in high latitudes | Cool and moist; showers |
| Polar continental (cP) | Cold land masses in winter (for example Siberia) | Very cold and dry; clear, frosty weather |

:::example{title="How an air mass changes as it moves"}
An air mass is **modified** by the surfaces it crosses.

1. Moist tropical maritime air leaves the Indian Ocean on the south-east trades.
2. Over Mozambique and up the Eastern Highlands it rises, cools and drops much of its moisture as relief rain or drizzle.
3. By the time it reaches western Zimbabwe it is warmer and drier, so it brings little rain there.
:::

## The air streams that affect Zimbabwe

**1. South-east trade winds** blow from high pressure over the southern Indian Ocean. The air is moist (maritime) when it reaches the coast.
- In the **hot wet season** it feeds moisture into storms, especially in the east.
- In the **cool dry season** it is shallow and cool; forced up the Eastern Highlands it gives **guti** (mist and drizzle) on the east-facing slopes, while the plateau further west stays dry under high pressure.

**2. North-east monsoon** air comes from the northern Indian Ocean. It crosses the equator in the southern summer and is deflected, reaching northern Mozambique, Malawi, Zambia and northern Zimbabwe as warm, moist air that brings rain.

**3. North-west airflow (Congo air)** is warm, very moist and unstable air drawn from the Congo basin. When it reaches Zimbabwe in the middle of the rainy season it brings widespread, heavy and prolonged rain, especially in the north and west.

**4. Dry continental air.** In the cool dry season, a large area of high pressure over southern Africa produces sinking, dry air. This is why winter skies are clear, days are sunny and nights cold.

**January**: draw arrows from the north-east (monsoon air) and the north-west (Congo air) meeting south-east trade wind arrows over or close to Zimbabwe. Label the meeting zone "ITCZ: low pressure, rising air, cumulonimbus, heavy rain".

**July**: draw a large "H" over the southern African interior with arrows spiralling outward, labelled "high pressure, sinking dry air, clear skies". Draw the ITCZ far to the north, across West Africa and the Sahel, labelled "ITCZ, about 15-20° N".
:::

## The ITCZ and its seasonal shift

The trade winds of both hemispheres blow towards the equatorial low. Where they meet, the air **converges and rises**, cools, condenses and forms towering cumulonimbus clouds with heavy rain and thunderstorms. This belt is the ITCZ.

The ITCZ follows the **overhead sun**, but about a month or so behind it, and swings further over the large land masses than over the oceans.

| | Position of the ITCZ over Africa | Effect on Zimbabwe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| January | Well south of the equator, over or near southern Africa (roughly 10-20° S over the interior) | Hot wet season: convergence, rising air, heavy rain |
| July | North of the equator, across West Africa and the Sahel (roughly 15-20° N) | Cool dry season: high pressure, clear skies |

When the ITCZ moves south late or does not reach as far as usual, the south of Zimbabwe can have a poor season; when it lingers over the country, rains can be heavy and floods possible.

:::example{title="How far does the ITCZ move?"}
One degree of latitude is about 111 km. If the ITCZ lies at 15° N in July and 15° S in January:

1. Total shift = 15 + 15 = 30° of latitude.
2. Distance ≈ 30 × 111 = 3,330 km.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming air masses by where they go"}
Air masses are named by their **source region**, not their destination. Air reaching Zimbabwe from the Indian Ocean is maritime because it formed over the sea.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the ITCZ exactly on the equator all year"}
The ITCZ moves with the overhead sun, with a lag. It lies far south of the equator over Africa in January and north of it in July.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the south-east trades always bring rain"}
In the cool season they bring drizzle only to the east-facing slopes. Elsewhere high pressure and sinking air keep the plateau dry.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 may ask you to "describe the position of the ITCZ in January and July and the weather associated with it". Give approximate latitudes and name the weather: convergence, rising air, cumulonimbus, heavy rain.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to classify an air mass, give both parts (tropical/polar and maritime/continental) and link each to the source region's latitude and surface.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An air mass is a huge body of air with uniform temperature and humidity, named after its source region.
- Tropical (warm) or polar (cold); maritime (moist) or continental (dry): mT, cT, mP, cP.
- Zimbabwe's summer rain comes from moist air streams (south-east trades, north-east monsoon, north-west Congo air) converging at the ITCZ.
- In winter high pressure and dry continental air bring clear skies; guti drizzle affects only the eastern slopes.
- The ITCZ follows the overhead sun with a lag: south over southern Africa in January, north over the Sahel in July.
:::
