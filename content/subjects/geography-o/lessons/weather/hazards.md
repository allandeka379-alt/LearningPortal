## Introduction

Weather becomes a **hazard** when it threatens people, property, crops or livestock. In Zimbabwe a single season can bring both extremes: a dry spell that wilts the maize in January and a flood that sweeps away a bridge in February. Lightning kills people and cattle every rainy season, hail can shred a tobacco crop in minutes, and frost can blacken vegetables on a July morning.

In this lesson you will study the main weather hazards of Zimbabwe and southern Africa, their causes and effects, and the measures that reduce their impact. You will also see how people themselves change local weather. Tropical cyclones are covered in their own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weather hazard"}
A weather event that has the potential to cause loss of life, injury, damage to property or disruption of economic activity.
:::

:::definition{term="Drought"}
A long period with much less rainfall than normal, so that water supplies, crops and pasture suffer.
:::

:::definition{term="Flood"}
The covering of normally dry land by water, for example when a river overflows its banks or heavy rain cannot drain away.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions taken to reduce the harm caused by a hazard, before or during the event.
:::

## Floods

**Causes**

- Very heavy or prolonged rain, especially from tropical cyclones and storms over several days.
- Rain falling on ground that is already saturated, or on hard, bare or paved ground, so more water runs off.
- Low-lying, flat land beside large rivers, where water spreads out slowly.
- Deforestation and overgrazing in catchments, which increase run-off and silt up river channels.

**Effects**: loss of life and livestock, destroyed homes, roads and bridges, crops washed away, contaminated water and outbreaks of diseases such as cholera and diarrhoea, and children unable to reach school.

:::example{title="Case study: flood-prone areas in Zimbabwe"}
Low-lying areas such as **Muzarabani** in the lower Zambezi valley and **Tsholotsho** in Matabeleland North flood in many wet seasons. Flat land, rivers that overflow and heavy storms combine. In 2014 people living in the basin of the Tokwe-Mukosi dam, which was still being built in Masvingo Province, had to be evacuated after heavy rains filled the reservoir. Floods in the east of the country after Cyclone Idai (2019) are studied in the tropical cyclones lesson.
:::

**Reducing the impact**: early warnings by radio and phone; moving people to higher ground; building homes on raised sites; dams and dykes; keeping drains and culverts clear; planting trees and grass in catchments; land-use rules that stop building on floodplains.

## Lightning

**Causes**: tall cumulonimbus clouds in convectional storms build up electrical charges as ice and water particles collide. A discharge between the cloud and the ground is lightning; the sudden heating of the air causes thunder.

**Effects**: deaths and injuries to people working in fields, herding cattle or sheltering under trees; livestock killed; huts set on fire; damage to electrical equipment and power lines.

**Reducing the risk**:

- Fit lightning conductors to schools, clinics and tall buildings.
- Shelter inside a solid building or a closed car, not under an isolated tree.
- Keep away from open fields, water, metal fences and hilltops during a storm.
- If caught in the open, crouch low with feet together; do not lie flat.
- Wait some time after the last thunder before going back outside.

## Drought

**Causes**

- Late onset or early end of the rains, or long **mid-season dry spells**.
- **El Niño**: warming of the eastern Pacific Ocean that is often linked to below-normal rainfall over southern Africa. The droughts of 1991-92 and 2015-16 were El Niño years.
- High pressure (sinking, dry air) staying over southern Africa for long periods.
- Low rainfall reliability in regions IV and V, where the average total is already low.

**Effects**: crop failure and food shortages; deaths of cattle from lack of water and pasture; falling water levels in dams (less water for towns and irrigation, less hydro-electric power from Kariba); wildlife deaths; loss of income for farmers and agro-based industries; people migrating to towns.

:::example{title="Planning for a dry season"}
A farmer in natural region IV can reduce the risk of drought by:

1. growing drought-tolerant crops such as sorghum and millet instead of relying only on maize;
2. using conservation farming (mulching, planting basins) to keep moisture in the soil;
3. harvesting rainwater from roofs and storing it in tanks;
4. keeping fewer, hardier animals and storing fodder;
5. listening to the seasonal forecast before choosing what and when to plant.
:::

Governments and organisations reduce the impact with grain reserves, food aid, drilling boreholes, small dams and irrigation schemes, and early warning systems.

## Other hazards: hail, heatwaves and frost

| Hazard | Cause | Main effects | Reducing the impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hailstorm | Strong up-currents in cumulonimbus clouds | Shredded crops (especially tobacco and vegetables), broken roofs and windows, injured animals | Crop insurance; hail nets over high-value crops |
| Heatwave | Long spells of very high temperatures, often in the hot dry season or with high pressure | Heat stress and dehydration, especially for the old and young; crops wilt; more evaporation from dams | Drinking water, shade, avoiding work at midday |
| Frost | Clear, calm winter nights when the ground cools below 0 °C, worst in valley bottoms | Damage to wheat, vegetables, coffee and young trees | Choosing sites on slopes, covering plants, irrigating before a frost night |

## How people change local weather

| Activity | Effect on local weather |
| --- | --- |
| Urbanisation | Buildings and tarmac absorb heat and factories and cars give off heat, so towns are warmer than the countryside (an **urban heat island**); more dust can mean more fog and local showers |
| Deforestation | Less transpiration, so drier air and possibly less local rainfall; bare ground heats up more by day |
| Dam building | Large lakes such as Kariba add moisture to the air and moderate temperatures close to the shore |
| Industrialisation | Smoke and gases cause smog and acid rain; particles act as condensation nuclei |
| Afforestation and irrigation | Cooler, moister local air |
| Cloud seeding | Spraying chemicals such as silver iodide into suitable clouds to encourage rain; it has been tried in Zimbabwe, but results are uncertain |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying drought is just 'no rain'"}
Drought is rainfall well **below normal** for long enough to cause water shortages. Region V is normally dry, so a dry month there is not necessarily a drought; a failed rainy season is.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing effects without saying who is affected"}
"It destroys things" earns little. Name the effect and the people or sector: "floods washed away bridges, cutting off rural clinics".
:::

:::mistake{title="Sheltering under a tree in a storm"}
An isolated tree attracts lightning. A building or a closed vehicle is the safe place.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section A often asks you to "describe the causes", "explain the effects" and "suggest measures". Keep the three separate, and for effects say whether they are social, economic or environmental.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "suggest" questions, give practical measures that fit the place: boreholes and drought-tolerant crops in region V, conductors and safe shelter for lightning, early warning and relocation for floods.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Floods follow heavy or prolonged rain, especially in low-lying areas such as Muzarabani and Tsholotsho; reduce with warnings, relocation, dams and catchment care.
- Lightning comes from cumulonimbus storms; reduce deaths with conductors and safe shelter.
- Drought follows failed or late rains, dry spells and El Niño years; reduce its impact with drought-tolerant crops, water harvesting, irrigation and grain reserves.
- Hail, heatwaves and frost damage crops and threaten people.
- Towns, deforestation, dams and industry change local weather.
:::
