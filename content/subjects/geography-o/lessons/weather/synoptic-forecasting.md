## Introduction

Every evening the television news shows a map of southern Africa covered in lines, arrows and symbols. That map is a simplified **synoptic chart**: a picture of the weather over a large area at one moment. Forecasters build it from thousands of readings taken at the same time at weather stations, on ships and by satellites.

In this lesson you will learn to read the symbols on a synoptic chart, to recognise high and low pressure and fronts, and to explain why forecasts matter to farmers, pilots and disaster managers. You will also look at the traditional signs that many Zimbabwean communities use to judge when the rains will come. You should already know the elements of weather and their instruments.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Synoptic chart"}
A weather map that summarises the weather over a large area at one time, using standard symbols and isobars. "Synoptic" means "seen together".
:::

:::definition{term="Isobar"}
A line on a map joining places with the same air pressure (reduced to sea level).
:::

:::definition{term="Station model"}
The group of symbols drawn around a weather station's position on a synoptic chart to show its cloud cover, wind, temperature, pressure and present weather.
:::

:::definition{term="Weather forecasting"}
Predicting the weather for a place for the coming hours, days or season, using present observations, records of past patterns and computer models.
:::

## Reading the station model

Each station is a small circle. The symbols around it show:

| What is shown | Symbol | How to read it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cloud cover | The circle is shaded in parts | Empty circle = clear (0 oktas); quarter shaded = 2 oktas; half = 4 oktas; fully shaded = overcast (8 oktas); a cross in the circle = sky hidden (for example by fog) |
| Wind direction | A shaft (line) attached to the circle | The shaft points towards the direction the wind blows **from** |
| Wind speed | Feathers (barbs) at the end of the shaft | Each full feather = 10 knots, a half feather = 5 knots, a solid triangle (pennant) = 50 knots; a circle around the station = calm |
| Temperature | A number at the top left | Degrees Celsius |
| Present weather | A symbol to the left of the circle | Dot = rain; comma = drizzle; star (asterisk) = snow; triangle = hail; three horizontal lines = fog or mist; a zigzag arrow like lightning = thunderstorm |

:::example{title="Decoding one station"}
A station circle is three-quarters shaded. A shaft sticks out to the north-east with two full feathers. The number 21 is at the top left and two dots are beside the circle.

1. Cloud cover: three-quarters of the circle is shaded, so 6 oktas.
2. Wind: the shaft points north-east, so the wind blows **from** the north-east (a north-easterly).
3. Wind speed: two full feathers = 2 × 10 = 20 knots.
4. Temperature: 21 °C.
5. Present weather: dots mean rain.
:::

## Isobars, highs and lows

Isobars are drawn at a regular interval, for example every 4 hPa (1008, 1012, 1016 and so on). They form patterns:

- **Low pressure (a depression or cyclone)**: closed isobars with the lowest value in the centre. Air rises in a low, cools and forms cloud, so lows bring **cloudy, wet, often windy** weather.
- **High pressure (an anticyclone)**: closed isobars with the highest value in the centre. Air sinks, warms and dries, so highs bring **clear, dry, settled** weather; in the Zimbabwean winter they also bring cold nights and frost.
- **Spacing**: isobars close together mean a steep pressure gradient and **strong winds**; isobars far apart mean **light winds**.
- **Direction of turning**: wind does not blow straight from high to low pressure; the Earth's rotation turns it. In the **southern hemisphere** winds blow **clockwise** around a low and **anticlockwise** out of a high. In the northern hemisphere the directions are reversed.

### Fronts

A **front** is the boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity. On a chart:

| Front | Symbol |
| --- | --- |
| Cold front | A line with **triangles** pointing the way the front moves (blue on coloured charts) |
| Warm front | A line with **semicircles** pointing the way it moves (red) |
| Occluded front | Triangles and semicircles alternating on the **same** side (purple) |

Fronts are linked to cloud and rain because one air mass is forced to rise over another. You will study them in detail with temperate depressions.

:::example{title="Interpreting a simple chart"}
A chart of southern Africa in July (fictional) shows a high of 1028 hPa centred over the Highveld of South Africa, with isobars spreading widely over Zimbabwe, which lies between the 1020 and 1024 hPa isobars.

- High pressure means sinking air: clear skies over Zimbabwe.
- Widely spaced isobars mean light winds.
- Clear skies and calm air allow heat to escape at night, so expect cold nights and possible frost in low-lying areas, with sunny, mild days.
:::

## Why forecasting matters

Zimbabwe's national weather service, the **Meteorological Services Department**, issues daily forecasts, warnings and seasonal outlooks. Forecasts are used by:

- **Farmers**: when to plant, spray, irrigate or harvest; whether a season is expected to be wetter or drier than normal.
- **Aviation**: pilots and air traffic control need wind, visibility, cloud and thunderstorm warnings for safe take-off and landing.
- **Fishing and boating** on Lake Kariba, where sudden storms raise dangerous waves.
- **Disaster management**: early warnings of cyclones, floods, heavy rain or heatwaves allow people to move to safety and save lives.
- **Construction, sport, tourism and transport**: planning outdoor work and events.

Forecasts have limits. They become less accurate the further ahead they look, and local storms in the wet season are hard to predict exactly.

## Indigenous knowledge in forecasting

Long before modern instruments, rural communities in Zimbabwe used signs in nature to judge the coming season. Examples that are widely reported include:

- **Trees**: many trees, such as msasa, put out new leaves in the hot dry season before the rains arrive.
- **Animals and insects**: the arrival of migratory birds, ants moving their eggs to higher ground, and swarms of flying termites after the first good rain.
- **The sky and wind**: a halo around the moon, a change in wind direction, and hot, humid, still days that build up before storms.

These indicators are cheap, understood locally and passed from elders to younger people, and some match scientific explanations (a halo is caused by thin, high cloud that often comes ahead of rain). However, their reliability varies from place to place and they cannot give exact amounts or dates. Many extension officers now encourage farmers to combine them with official forecasts.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reading the wind shaft backwards"}
The shaft points to where the wind comes **from**, exactly like a wind vane. A shaft pointing south shows a southerly wind.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying closely spaced isobars mean calm weather"}
Close isobars mean a steep pressure gradient and strong winds. Calm conditions go with widely spaced isobars, usually near the centre of a high.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using northern-hemisphere wind directions"}
In the southern hemisphere, winds blow clockwise around a low and anticlockwise around a high.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Paper 1 you may be given a station model and asked for cloud cover, wind direction or speed. Count the oktas from the shading and the knots from the feathers carefully.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why weather forecasting is important" questions in Paper 2 want a user **and** how the forecast helps them, for example "Farmers can delay planting if a dry spell is forecast, so seed is not wasted."
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to evaluate indigenous knowledge, give strengths (cheap, local, available) and weaknesses (unreliable, not quantified, being lost), then conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A synoptic chart shows the weather over a large area at one time, using station models, isobars and front symbols.
- Station model: shading = cloud in oktas; shaft points to where the wind comes from; full feather = 10 knots, half = 5; dot = rain, comma = drizzle, triangle = hail, lines = fog.
- Lows: rising air, cloud and rain; highs: sinking air, clear and dry; close isobars = strong winds. Southern hemisphere: clockwise around lows.
- Fronts: triangles = cold, semicircles = warm, both on one side = occluded.
- Forecasts help farmers, aviation, boating, disaster management and tourism; indigenous indicators are useful locally but less reliable.
:::
