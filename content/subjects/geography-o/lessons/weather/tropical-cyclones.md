## Introduction

In March 2019 a storm that had formed over the Mozambique Channel crossed the coast near Beira and moved inland. Within days, rivers in Chimanimani and Chipinge districts had become torrents of mud and boulders, and whole settlements were buried. That storm was **Cyclone Idai**, the deadliest weather disaster in Zimbabwe's recent history. Tropical cyclones are the most powerful storms on Earth, and Zimbabwe, though landlocked, is not beyond their reach.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tropical cyclone"}
A large, intense, rotating storm with very low pressure at its centre that forms over warm tropical seas. It is called a **hurricane** in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a **typhoon** in the north-west Pacific and a **cyclone** in the Indian Ocean and around Australia.
:::

:::definition{term="Eye"}
The calm, often cloud-free centre of a tropical cyclone, where air sinks; it is often a few tens of kilometres across.
:::

:::definition{term="Eye wall"}
The ring of towering cumulonimbus cloud around the eye, with the strongest winds and heaviest rain.
:::

:::definition{term="Storm surge"}
A rise in sea level, far above the normal tide, caused by strong onshore winds piling water against the coast and by the low pressure letting the sea surface rise.
:::

:::definition{term="Landfall"}
The moment the centre of a cyclone crosses from the sea onto the coast.
:::

## Distribution and seasons

Tropical cyclones form between about **5° and 20-30° north and south** of the equator, over the warm western parts of the oceans. They do not form right on the equator, because the spin given by the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect) is too weak there.

| Region | Name | Main season (approximate) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| North Atlantic, Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico | Hurricane | June to November |
| North-west Pacific (Philippines, Japan, China) | Typhoon | Most of the year, peak July to October |
| North Indian Ocean (Bay of Bengal) | Cyclone | Around the monsoon, April-May and October-November |
| South-west Indian Ocean (Madagascar, Mozambique) | Cyclone | November to April |
| Around northern Australia | Cyclone | November to April |

The season in each hemisphere is late summer and autumn, when the sea is warmest. Storms usually move westwards with the trade winds, then curve away from the equator.

## Conditions for formation

1. Sea-surface temperature of about **26.5 °C or more**, with warm water to a depth of tens of metres to supply heat and moisture.
2. Location at least about 5° from the equator, so the Coriolis effect can make the air spin.
3. Moist, unstable air that can rise to great heights.
4. Little change in wind speed or direction with height (low **wind shear**), so the storm is not torn apart.
5. An existing area of low pressure or disturbance to start the process.

## How a cyclone forms (stages)

1. Warm sea heats the air above it; moist air **rises** rapidly, creating low pressure at the surface.
2. As the air rises it cools and **condenses**, releasing **latent heat**, which warms the air further and makes it rise faster: the storm feeds itself.
3. More air rushes in at the surface to replace it, and the Coriolis effect makes it **spiral** (clockwise in the southern hemisphere, anticlockwise in the northern).
4. A tropical depression strengthens into a tropical storm, which is given a name, and then, when sustained winds pass about 119 km/h, a tropical cyclone (hurricane).
5. Over land or cooler water the storm loses its supply of heat and moisture, and friction slows it, so it **weakens**. It can still bring torrential rain far inland.

## Structure

Imagine a cross-section through the storm:

- **Eye**: calm, clear or lightly cloudy, sinking air, lowest pressure.
- **Eye wall**: a ring of cumulonimbus with the strongest winds (in the most intense storms over about 250 km/h) and torrential rain.
- **Spiral rain bands**: bands of storm cloud spiralling outwards, separated by drier gaps.
- **Outflow**: at the top, air spreads outwards, forming a circle of high cirrus cloud.

The whole system can be several hundred kilometres across.

## Hazards

- **Strong winds** destroy buildings, crops, power lines and trees, and turn debris into missiles.
- **Storm surge** floods low-lying coasts and is often the biggest killer.
- **Torrential rain** causes river floods and flash floods, even hundreds of kilometres inland.
- **Landslides and mudflows** on steep, saturated slopes.
- **After-effects**: contaminated water and diseases such as cholera, food shortages and loss of homes and jobs.

:::example{title="Case study: Cyclone Idai, March 2019 (Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Malawi)"}
**Formation and track**: the system first formed as a tropical depression off Mozambique in early March 2019, brought floods to Malawi and Mozambique, moved back over the warm Mozambique Channel and strengthened into an intense cyclone. It made landfall near **Beira** in mid-March and moved inland towards eastern Zimbabwe, weakening but bringing torrential rain.

**Impacts in Mozambique**: strong winds and a storm surge damaged most of the city of Beira; huge areas of farmland around the Buzi and Pungwe rivers were flooded; a cholera outbreak followed.

**Impacts in Zimbabwe**: the heaviest effects were in **Chimanimani and Chipinge** districts. Intense rain on steep, saturated slopes caused flash floods, mudslides and rock falls that buried homes, destroyed roads and bridges and cut communities off for days. Several hundred people died in Zimbabwe and many were never found; more than 1,000 died across the three countries. Crops, livestock, schools and clinics were lost.

**Responses**: rescue by the army, police and volunteers, including helicopters; emergency food, shelter and medical aid from the government, neighbouring countries, churches and international organisations; later rebuilding of roads and bridges and moving some families from dangerous slopes.

**Lessons**: the need for earlier, clearer warnings that reach remote communities; avoiding settlement on steep slopes and river banks; stronger bridges and drainage; and community evacuation plans.
:::

## Managing cyclones: the four Ps

| P | What it means | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Prediction** | Tracking storms with satellites, aircraft and computer models to forecast path and strength | Regional meteorological centres issue warnings days ahead |
| **Preparation** | Getting people and property ready before the storm | Emergency kits, stockpiles of food and water, evacuation drills, education |
| **Planning** | Long-term land-use and building decisions | No building on floodplains, low coasts or steep slopes; evacuation routes; building codes |
| **Protection** | Structures that reduce damage | Cyclone shelters on stilts (as in Bangladesh), sea walls, strengthened roofs and bridges, mangroves to absorb surges |

Countries with more money (HICs) can afford satellites, strong buildings and fast evacuation, but even they can suffer badly, as when **Hurricane Katrina** (2005) flooded New Orleans after flood defences failed. In LICs, warnings may not reach rural people, houses are weaker and recovery takes longer.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cyclones form on the equator"}
They need the Coriolis effect to spin, so they form at least about 5° away from it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the danger is over when the eye passes"}
After the calm of the eye the winds return from the opposite direction, often just as strong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying landlocked countries are safe"}
Cyclones weaken over land but can bring extreme rain far inland, as Idai did in eastern Zimbabwe.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tropical cyclones form over seas of about 26.5 °C or more, between about 5° and 20-30° N and S, in late summer and autumn.
- Rising moist air, condensation and latent heat drive a spiralling low-pressure storm with an eye, eye wall and rain bands.
- Hazards: winds, storm surge, torrential rain, floods and landslides, then disease and food shortages.
- Cyclone Idai (March 2019) hit Beira, then Chimanimani and Chipinge, with floods and landslides that killed hundreds in Zimbabwe.
- Management uses prediction, preparation, planning and protection.
:::
