## Introduction

Zimbabwe's weather is ruled by the tropics, but in winter a cold snap with grey skies and a biting southerly wind sometimes reaches the south of the country. The cause lies far to the south, where cold polar air and warm subtropical air meet along a line of storms. The same kind of weather system, the **temperate depression**, brings most of the rain to Cape Town in winter and to Britain all year. Understanding it explains the "fronts" you see on weather maps.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Depression (temperate cyclone)"}
An area of low pressure in the mid-latitudes, usually about 1,000-3,000 km across, in which warm and cold air masses meet, bringing cloud, wind and rain. Isobars form closed rings with the lowest pressure in the centre.
:::

:::definition{term="Front"}
The boundary between two air masses of different temperature and humidity.
:::

:::definition{term="Warm front"}
The leading edge of advancing warm air, which rises gently over the colder air ahead of it.
:::

:::definition{term="Cold front"}
The leading edge of advancing cold air, which undercuts the warm air and forces it to rise steeply.
:::

:::definition{term="Occlusion"}
The stage at which the faster cold front catches up with the warm front and lifts the warm air off the ground completely.
:::

:::definition{term="Anticyclone"}
An area of high pressure in which air sinks, giving light winds and settled, usually dry weather.
:::

## Where and why depressions form

Depressions form along the **polar front**, the boundary at roughly 40-60° of latitude where cold, dense polar air meets warm, moist tropical air. Fast high-level winds (jet streams) above the polar front help to draw air upwards and steer the depressions. They generally move from **west to east** in both hemispheres, carried by the westerly winds.

**Stages of formation (diagram in words)**:

1. **Polar front**: warm tropical air and cold polar air lie side by side, moving in opposite directions along the front.
2. **Wave (kink)**: a small bulge develops; warm air begins to push into the cold air and rise over it. Pressure falls at the tip of the bulge.
3. **Mature depression**: the bulge becomes a **warm sector** of warm air, bounded in front by a **warm front** and behind by a **cold front**. Air spirals in towards the low centre.
4. **Occlusion**: the cold front moves faster and catches up with the warm front, lifting the warm air off the ground. An **occluded front** forms.
5. **Decay**: with no more warm air at the surface, the depression fills (pressure rises) and dies away.

Air circulates **anticlockwise** around a depression in the northern hemisphere and **clockwise** in the southern hemisphere.

## Structure and cross-section

Imagine a cross-section from west to east through a mature depression, with the system moving eastward:

| Part (east to west) | Cloud | Weather |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Far ahead of the warm front | Thin, high cirrus, then thicker cirrostratus (haloes around the sun or moon) | Fine but clouding over; pressure starts to fall |
| Approaching the warm front | Thick altostratus, then low nimbostratus | Steady, prolonged rain; pressure falling steadily |
| Warm sector | Low stratus | Mild and humid; drizzle or light rain; pressure steady |
| Cold front | Cumulonimbus | Short, heavy downpours, possibly thunder and hail; gusty winds; sudden fall in temperature; pressure starts to rise |
| Behind the cold front | Cumulus | Colder, clearer air with scattered showers; pressure rising |

The warm front slopes gently (rain over a wide belt ahead of it); the cold front slopes steeply (narrow band of heavy rain). In the northern hemisphere the wind changes direction clockwise (**veers**) as each front passes.

:::example{title="How long before the front arrives?"}
A cold front is 450 km west of a town and moving east at 30 km/h.

1. Time = distance ÷ speed = 450 ÷ 30 = 15 hours.
2. If it is now 06:00, the front should arrive at about 21:00.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a barograph (fictional record)"}
Pressure at a coastal station: 09:00 1,016 hPa; 15:00 1,004 hPa; 21:00 1,004 hPa; 03:00 1,012 hPa.

1. Falling 12 hPa in six hours: the warm front is approaching.
2. Steady at 1,004 hPa: the station is in the warm sector.
3. Rising again: the cold front has passed.
:::

## Where depressions affect the world

- **North-west Europe and Britain**: depressions arrive from the Atlantic all year, bringing changeable weather.
- **Mediterranean lands, the south-western Cape and south-west Australia**: in **winter**, when the pressure belts move towards the equator, depressions bring the rainy season.
- **Southern Africa's interior**: cold fronts passing south of the continent can push cold air northwards in winter, giving cold snaps, cloud and occasional light rain in southern Zimbabwe.

## Anticyclones in contrast

| | Depression | Anticyclone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure | Low, lowest in the centre | High, highest in the centre |
| Air movement | Converges and rises | Sinks and spreads out |
| Isobars | Often close together | Widely spaced |
| Winds | Strong | Light or calm |
| Weather | Cloud, rain, changeable | Dry and settled; hot sunny days in summer; cold, clear nights with frost or fog in winter |
| Circulation (southern hemisphere) | Clockwise | Anticlockwise |

The cool dry season in Zimbabwe is dominated by high pressure over southern Africa, which is why winter weather is so settled.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking 'cyclone' always means a tropical storm"}
A temperate depression is also called a mid-latitude cyclone, but it is a different, colder, frontal system. Tropical cyclones have no fronts and form over warm seas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Swapping the slopes of the fronts"}
The warm front is gentle (long, steady rain); the cold front is steep (short, heavy rain).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the northern-hemisphere circulation in the south"}
Around a low, air turns clockwise in the southern hemisphere and anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Depressions form on the polar front where warm tropical air meets cold polar air, and move west to east.
- Stages: polar front, wave, mature depression with warm sector, occlusion, decay.
- Warm front: gentle slope, layered cloud, steady rain. Cold front: steep, cumulonimbus, heavy short rain, sudden cooling.
- They bring winter rain to Mediterranean lands and the south-western Cape and changeable weather to north-west Europe.
- Anticyclones: high pressure, sinking air, light winds, settled weather.
:::
