## Introduction

Zimbabwe lies wholly within the tropics, between roughly 15.5° and 22.5° south of the equator, yet Harare in July can have frosty mornings while Chiredzi in the lowveld stays warm. The Eastern Highlands are often green and misty while the far south is dry bush. Understanding why helps explain where tea, tobacco, maize, cattle and sugar cane are produced, and why one season can be good and the next a disaster.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate"}
The average weather of a place, worked out from records over a long period (usually about 30 years).
:::

:::definition{term="Climate variability"}
The way the climate of a place differs from its long-term average from one season or year to the next, for example a wetter or drier season than normal.
:::

:::definition{term="Anomaly"}
The difference between a measured value and the long-term average: a rainfall anomaly of −150 mm means 150 mm less than normal.
:::

:::definition{term="Mid-season dry spell"}
A period of several weeks without useful rain in the middle of the rainy season, often in January or February.
:::

## Zimbabwe's three seasons

| Season | Approximate months | Weather |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hot wet season | November to March | Hot days, convectional thunderstorms, almost all the year's rain |
| Cool dry season | May to August | Clear skies, warm days, cold nights; frost in places; guti drizzle on the eastern slopes |
| Hot dry season | September to October | Very hot, dry, dusty and hazy; often the hottest time of year before the rains |

April is a short change-over month as the rains end. In the cool season, high pressure lies over southern Africa and the air sinks, so skies are clear. In the hot wet season the rain belt (the Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone) moves south over Zimbabwe and moist air streams arrive.

## What controls Zimbabwe's climate?

**1. Latitude.** Being in the tropics, Zimbabwe has a high midday sun all year. In the southern summer (about November to February) the midday sun is overhead or nearly overhead, so summers are hot. In June the sun is lower in the northern sky and days are shorter, so winters are cooler.

**2. Altitude.** Temperature falls with height, by about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m on average. Much of central Zimbabwe is a plateau (the highveld, mostly above about 1,200 m), which is why Harare and Gweru are pleasant rather than hot. The low-lying areas, such as the lowveld of the south-east and the Zambezi valley, are much hotter.

:::example{title="Using the lapse rate"}
A fictional station in the Zambezi valley at 500 m has a mean January temperature of 28 °C. Estimate the January temperature at a highveld station at 1,500 m.

1. Height difference = 1,500 − 500 = 1,000 m.
2. Fall in temperature ≈ 6.5 °C per 1,000 m × 1 = 6.5 °C.
3. Estimated temperature = 28 − 6.5 = 21.5 °C.
:::

**3. Distance from the sea.** Zimbabwe is landlocked: Mozambique lies between it and the Indian Ocean. Moist air from the ocean loses moisture as it crosses Mozambique and the Eastern Highlands, so rainfall generally decreases westwards. Inland places have a larger range of temperature than coasts (continentality): hot days, cold winter nights.

**4. Relief and aspect.** The Eastern Highlands force moist air to rise, giving relief rain on their east-facing slopes (more than about 1,000 mm a year) and a drier rain shadow beyond.

## The pattern across the country

- **Rainfall** decreases from the north-east and east (Eastern Highlands, Mashonaland) towards the south and south-west (Matabeleland, Masvingo lowveld). As rough guides, Harare receives about 800 mm a year, Bulawayo about 600 mm and parts of the far south below 400 mm.
- **Reliability** decreases in the same direction: the drier the area, the more the totals vary from year to year.
- **Temperature** depends mostly on altitude: cool on the Eastern Highlands, moderate on the highveld, hot in the lowveld and Zambezi valley.

These patterns are the basis of the five natural farming regions, studied in agriculture.

| Climatic zone | Climate | Activities it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eastern Highlands | Cool, wet, misty; rain over 1,000 mm | Tea, coffee, timber plantations, fruit; tourism (hiking, scenery) |
| Highveld | Moderate temperatures, reliable summer rain | Maize, tobacco, soya beans, dairy; most of the population and industry |
| Middleveld | Warmer, less reliable rain | Cotton, small grains, cattle |
| Lowveld and Zambezi valley | Hot, dry, unreliable rain | Cattle ranching, wildlife and safari tourism; sugar cane under irrigation |

:::example{title="Climate and tourism"}
In the cool dry season the bush thins out and animals gather at water holes, so game viewing in Hwange National Park and the Zambezi valley is easier. Clear, dry weather also suits sightseeing. The volume of water over the Victoria Falls is highest some weeks after the rains, when spray can hide the view, and lowest late in the dry season.
:::

## Climate variability

Zimbabwe's rainfall changes greatly from year to year:

- **Late onset** of the rains (after mid-November), which shortens the growing season.
- **Mid-season dry spells** that wilt crops at the flowering stage.
- **Early end** of the rains before crops mature.
- **Drought years**, often linked to **El Niño**, for example 1991-92 and 2015-16.
- **Very wet years**, sometimes linked to La Niña or tropical cyclones, with floods.
- **Temperature anomalies**: unusually hot spells (heatwaves) or cold snaps with frost.

:::example{title="Calculating a rainfall anomaly"}
A fictional station has a long-term mean of 750 mm. One season it receives 600 mm.

1. Anomaly = 600 − 750 = −150 mm.
2. As a percentage of the mean: −150 ÷ 750 × 100 = −20 %, so the season was 20 % below normal.
:::

**Effects** include crop failure and food shortages, livestock deaths, low dam levels for towns, irrigation and hydro-electric power, and in wet years floods and damaged roads.

**Responses**

- Seasonal forecasts and early warning systems, broadcast before planting.
- Drought-tolerant and early-maturing crop varieties; small grains in drier regions.
- Conservation farming (mulching, planting basins) and rainwater harvesting.
- Irrigation from dams and boreholes.
- Grain reserves and food aid; crop and livestock insurance.
- Staggered planting so that one dry spell does not ruin the whole crop.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Zimbabwe is cool because it is far from the equator"}
Zimbabwe is in the tropics. Its moderate temperatures on the highveld are mainly due to **altitude**, not latitude.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up variability and climate change"}
Variability is the normal year-to-year swing around the average. Climate change is a long-term shift in the average itself.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the hottest month in December everywhere"}
Many Zimbabwean stations are hottest in October or November, before cloud and rain cool the wet season.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Three seasons: hot wet (November-March), cool dry (May-August), hot dry (September-October).
- Latitude gives a tropical climate; altitude keeps the highveld moderate; distance from the sea and relief make the west and south drier.
- Rainfall and reliability decrease from north-east to south-west; temperatures are highest in the lowveld and Zambezi valley.
- Climatic zones support different activities: tea in the Eastern Highlands, maize and tobacco on the highveld, ranching and wildlife in the lowveld.
- Variability (late rains, dry spells, El Niño droughts, wet years) is reduced by forecasts, suitable crops, water storage and grain reserves.
:::
