## Introduction

Zimbabwe has no coastline, but many Zimbabweans know the sea from holidays at Beira or Vilanculos in Mozambique, whose coast runs for roughly 2,500 km along the Indian Ocean. Even on Lake Kariba, strong winds blowing across open water raise waves that wear away the shore and pile up sand in sheltered bays. The same processes, on a much larger scale, shape every sea coast in the world.

The coast is where land, sea and air meet, and it is one of the most rapidly changing environments on Earth. This lesson explains the **processes**: how waves form and work, how they erode, transport and deposit material, and how weathering and mass movement attack cliffs from above. The landforms that result are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fetch"}
The distance of open water over which the wind blows to form waves. The longer the fetch, the bigger the waves can grow.
:::

:::definition{term="Swash and backwash"}
**Swash** is the rush of water up the beach after a wave breaks. **Backwash** is the water flowing back down the beach under gravity.
:::

:::definition{term="Longshore drift"}
The movement of sediment along a coast by waves approaching the shore at an angle.
:::

:::definition{term="Wave refraction"}
The bending of waves as they approach an irregular coastline, so that their energy is concentrated on headlands and spread out in bays.
:::

:::definition{term="Sub-aerial processes"}
Weathering and mass movement acting on the land above the waves, especially on cliff faces.
:::

## How waves form and break

Waves are formed by **wind blowing over the sea**. Friction between the wind and the water surface transfers energy, so the water moves in circular orbits while the wave energy travels forward. The size and energy of a wave depend on:

- **wind speed**: stronger winds give bigger waves;
- **duration**: how long the wind blows;
- **fetch**: how far the wind has blown over open water. Coasts facing a long fetch, such as those exposed to a whole ocean, receive powerful waves.

**How a wave breaks** (draw a side view in four stages):

1. In deep water the wave moves forward with water particles turning in circles.
2. As it reaches shallow water, the base of the wave is slowed by **friction** with the sea bed.
3. The top of the wave keeps moving faster, so the wave becomes higher and steeper.
4. The crest topples forward and the wave **breaks**, sending **swash** up the beach. The water then returns as **backwash**.

## Constructive and destructive waves

| Feature | Constructive waves | Destructive waves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Low, long wavelength | High, steep, short wavelength |
| Frequency | Fewer, often about 6-8 a minute | More, often about 10-14 a minute |
| Swash and backwash | Strong swash, weak backwash | Weak swash, strong backwash |
| Effect | Push material **up** the beach; build it up | Drag material **down** the beach; erode it |
| Typical beach | Wide and gently sloping, often with a berm | Steep and narrow, often with shingle |
| Typical weather | Calm conditions, distant storms | Local storms, strong winds |

Destructive waves are often **plunging** waves: they break almost vertically, so their energy is directed downwards and their backwash pulls sediment out to sea. Constructive waves **spill** gently forward, so the swash travels far up the beach and much of the water soaks into the sand before it can return.

:::example{title="Counting waves (fictional data)"}
A student counted 39 waves breaking in 3 minutes on a fictional beach.

1. Waves per minute = 39 ÷ 3 = 13.
2. 13 a minute is a high frequency, typical of destructive waves.
3. Supporting evidence would be a steep beach with strong backwash.
:::

## Wave refraction

Imagine waves approaching a coast where a hard-rock **headland** sticks out between two **bays**.

1. Water is shallower off the headland, so waves are slowed there first by friction with the sea bed.
2. In the deeper water of the bays, waves keep moving faster.
3. The wave fronts therefore **bend** (refract) to wrap round the headland.
4. Wave **energy is concentrated** on the sides and front of the headland, causing erosion.
5. In the bays, wave energy is **spread out** over a longer shore, so waves are lower and deposition occurs, forming bay-head beaches.

Over time refraction tends to straighten the coastline, as headlands are worn back and bays are filled with sand.

## Processes of marine erosion

| Process | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| **Hydraulic action** | The force of waves hitting the cliff. Air trapped in cracks is compressed by the water and then expands explosively as the wave retreats, widening the cracks. |
| **Abrasion (corrasion)** | Waves throw sand and pebbles against the cliff, wearing it away like sandpaper. Also grinds the rocky shore platform. |
| **Attrition** | Rocks and pebbles carried by waves knock against each other and become smaller and rounder. |
| **Solution (corrosion)** | Seawater dissolves soluble rocks such as limestone and chalk. |

Erosion is greatest where waves are powerful (long fetch, storms, destructive waves), where rocks are weak or heavily jointed, and where there is no beach to absorb wave energy.

## Transport: longshore drift

Material on the coast is moved by the same processes as in rivers (traction, saltation, suspension and solution), but the most important movement **along** the coast is longshore drift.

**Longshore drift in words** (draw a plan view of a beach):

1. The **prevailing wind** blows at an angle to the coast, so waves approach the beach at an angle.
2. The **swash** carries sand and pebbles up the beach at that same angle.
3. The **backwash** returns straight down the beach, at right angles to the shoreline, under gravity.
4. Each wave moves the material a little further along the beach in a **zigzag** path.
5. Over time large amounts of sediment move along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind.

:::example{title="Measuring longshore drift (fictional data)"}
Students placed a marked float in the sea and recorded how far it moved along the beach.

1. The float moved 36 m in 6 minutes.
2. Rate = 36 ÷ 6 = 6 m per minute.
3. In metres per second: 6 ÷ 60 = 0.1 m/s.
:::

## Deposition

Waves deposit material when they lose energy. This happens:

- in **sheltered bays**, where wave refraction spreads energy out;
- where **constructive waves** dominate, with strong swash and weak backwash;
- where the coastline **changes direction** suddenly, so longshore drift carries material into deeper, calmer water (the start of a spit);
- where **rivers** bring large amounts of sediment to the coast;
- in the shelter of **islands, reefs or groynes**.

## Sub-aerial processes

Cliffs are attacked from above as well as at their base.

- **Weathering**: salt crystals grow in cracks and break the rock (salt weathering); rainwater dissolves limestone (chemical weathering); plant roots and burrowing animals open cracks (biological weathering); in cold climates freeze-thaw breaks the rock.
- **Mass movement**: rockfalls from steep cliffs of hard rock; **slumping** of soft clay cliffs after heavy rain, when saturated material slides along a curved surface.
- **Run-off**: rain washing over the cliff top removes loose material.

Marine erosion at the base (forming a wave-cut notch) and sub-aerial processes above work together to make cliffs retreat.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing the backwash at the same angle as the swash"}
In longshore drift the **swash** follows the angle of the waves, but the **backwash** runs straight down the beach under gravity. That is why material zigzags along the shore.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing attrition and abrasion"}
**Abrasion** wears away the cliff and the shore platform; **attrition** wears down the pebbles themselves.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 often asks you to "describe the differences between constructive and destructive waves" or to "explain longshore drift with the help of a labelled diagram". Use a two-column comparison and label prevailing wind, swash, backwash and the direction of drift.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining wave refraction, say clearly where energy is **concentrated** (headlands) and where it is **dispersed** (bays), and link each to erosion or deposition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Waves are formed by wind; their energy depends on wind speed, duration and fetch. Friction with the sea bed makes waves break.
- Constructive waves (low, infrequent, strong swash) build beaches; destructive waves (high, frequent, strong backwash) erode them.
- Wave refraction concentrates energy on headlands and spreads it out in bays.
- Marine erosion: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition and solution.
- Longshore drift moves sediment along the coast in a zigzag: swash at an angle, backwash straight down.
- Deposition happens where waves lose energy; sub-aerial weathering and mass movement attack cliffs from above.
:::
