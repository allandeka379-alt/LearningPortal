## Introduction

Much of Zimbabwe's imported fuel and many of its exports pass through **Beira**, a port city on the low-lying coast of Mozambique. In March 2019 Cyclone Idai made landfall near Beira, and its winds, storm surge and rain damaged a large share of the city's buildings before the storm moved inland to cause disaster in Chimanimani. Coasts are among the most valuable places on Earth to live and work, and among the most exposed.

This lesson weighs the **opportunities** of the coast against its **hazards**, and evaluates how people try to protect coasts from erosion and flooding. You should know the coastal processes and landforms from the previous two lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hard engineering"}
Building artificial structures to resist or control the sea, such as sea walls, groynes, rock armour and gabions.
:::

:::definition{term="Soft engineering"}
Working with natural processes to reduce erosion and flooding, such as beach nourishment, dune regeneration and managed retreat.
:::

:::definition{term="Storm surge"}
A temporary rise in sea level caused by low air pressure and strong onshore winds during a storm, which can flood low-lying coasts.
:::

:::definition{term="Managed retreat"}
Deliberately allowing the sea to erode or flood an area of low value, often creating salt marsh that then acts as a natural defence.
:::

## Opportunities of living on the coast

| Opportunity | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Ports and trade | Beira and Maputo serve landlocked Zimbabwe, Zambia and Malawi |
| Tourism | Beaches, coral reefs and diving (e.g. Vilanculos and the Bazaruto archipelago) |
| Fishing and aquaculture | Prawns, fish, seaweed farming |
| Flat land for farming, industry and cities | Deltas and coastal plains |
| Energy | Offshore oil and gas, wind and tidal power |
| Mild climate and recreation | Sea breezes; water sports |

## Hazards of living on the coast

- **Coastal erosion**: cliffs and beaches retreat, threatening homes, roads, farmland and infrastructure.
- **Coastal flooding**: storm surges, very high tides and tsunamis flood low-lying land.
- **Tropical storms**: strong winds, storm surges and heavy rain (see the tropical cyclones lesson).
- **Sea-level rise** linked to climate change, which increases flooding and erosion risk.

**Causes of rapid erosion**: soft rock (clay, sand), a long fetch and frequent storms, narrow or missing beaches, sub-aerial processes such as slumping, and people removing sand or building defences that starve beaches further along the coast.

**Impacts**: loss of homes and land, damage to roads and services, loss of tourism income, falling property values, salt water ruining farmland and water supplies, and loss of habitats.

## Managing the coast

### Hard engineering

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sea wall** | Concrete wall, often curved, that reflects wave energy | Strong, long-lasting protection for towns; promenade | Very expensive; reflected waves scour the beach in front; ugly |
| **Groynes** | Wooden or rock barriers built out at right angles to the beach | Trap sand moved by longshore drift, building a wider beach that absorbs wave energy | Starve beaches further along the coast of sand, increasing erosion there |
| **Rock armour (rip-rap)** | Large boulders piled at the foot of a cliff or in front of a wall | Absorbs wave energy; cheaper than a sea wall | Can be moved in big storms; blocks access; looks out of place |
| **Gabions** | Wire cages filled with rocks | Cheap; absorb energy | Short life; wire rusts and breaks |

![Plan view of groynes built at right angles to a beach: sand carried by longshore drift piles up against the up-drift side of each groyne, widening the beach there, while the beach beyond the last groyne is starved of sand and erodes.](media:diagrams/groynes-trap-sand.png "Figure: how groynes trap sand and starve the beach down-drift")

### Soft engineering

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Beach nourishment** | Adding sand or shingle to a beach | Natural-looking; wider beach absorbs energy and helps tourism | Must be repeated; sand may be dredged from elsewhere |
| **Dune regeneration** | Planting marram grass, fencing, boardwalks | Cheap; creates habitat; dunes act as a natural barrier | Takes time; restricts access |
| **Managed retreat** | Allow low-value land to flood; create salt marsh | Cheap; sustainable; salt marsh absorbs wave energy | Loss of farmland or homes; compensation disputes |
| **Mangrove and reef protection** | Conserve natural buffers in tropical coasts | Absorb waves and storm surges; support fishing | Need protection from clearance and pollution |

### Deciding what to protect

Coastal managers usually choose between four options: **hold the line** (keep the coast where it is), **advance the line** (build out to sea), **managed retreat**, or **do nothing** (no active intervention). Decisions depend on the value of what is at risk compared with the cost, the effect on neighbouring stretches of coast, and sustainability.

:::example{title="Cost per metre of protection (fictional data)"}
A fictional town plans 800 m of rock armour costing US\$2.4 million.

1. Cost per metre = 2,400,000 ÷ 800 = US\$3,000 per metre.
2. A sea wall costing US\$9,000 per metre would cost 9,000 × 800 = US\$7.2 million for the same length.
:::

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: the Holderness coast, England (an HIC)"}
- **Where**: about 60 km of coast in eastern England, from Flamborough Head south to Spurn Head.
- **Why it erodes fast**: the cliffs are made of soft **boulder clay** (till), which slumps when wet; waves from the North Sea have a long fetch from the north-east; beaches are narrow and do not absorb much wave energy. The coast retreats on average by roughly 1.5 to 2 m a year, among the fastest rates in Europe, and many villages recorded in medieval times have been lost to the sea.
- **Impacts**: farmland and homes lost, roads ending at cliff edges, caravan parks moved back, falling property values.
- **Management**:
  - **Hornsea** and **Withernsea**: sea walls, groynes and rock armour protect the towns.
  - **Mappleton**: in 1991 two rock groynes and rock armour at the cliff foot were built (costing about £2 million) to protect the village and the coast road.
  - **Easington**: rock armour protects the gas terminal, which is of national importance.
  - Most of the rest of the coast is left to erode (no active intervention), because the cost would be greater than the value of the land.
- **Evaluation**: the defences have protected the towns and Mappleton, but by trapping sediment they have **starved beaches to the south**, so erosion just south of Mappleton increased (a terminal groyne effect). Less sediment also reaches Spurn Head. Homeowners on unprotected stretches receive little help, which many see as unfair, but protecting the whole coast would be very expensive and would starve other areas of sediment. The approach is a compromise between cost, fairness and sustainability.
:::

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: protecting Beira from tropical storms (an LIC)"}
Beira lies on low, flat land near sea level at the mouth of the Pungwe River, and its coast has been eroding for decades. Old sea walls and groynes had fallen into disrepair. When **Cyclone Idai** struck in March 2019, the storm surge and winds damaged many buildings, port facilities and power lines, and rain and surge water flooded low-lying neighbourhoods. Since then, international partners have helped fund repairs to coastal defences, drainage channels and storm-water systems, and early warning has been improved. The challenge for an LIC is that hard defences are costly to build and maintain, while sea-level rise and stronger storms increase the risk each year.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every method 'good' or 'bad'"}
Evaluation needs **both sides**: say what a method protects, what it costs, and what side effects it has on other places or the environment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the effect along the coast"}
Groynes and sea walls change sediment movement. Always consider what happens **downdrift**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Coasts offer ports, tourism, fishing, flat land and energy, but face erosion, flooding, storms and sea-level rise.
- Hard engineering (sea walls, groynes, rock armour, gabions) gives strong local protection but is costly and can increase erosion elsewhere.
- Soft engineering (beach nourishment, dune regeneration, managed retreat, mangroves) works with nature and is usually more sustainable.
- Options: hold the line, advance the line, managed retreat, do nothing; chosen by comparing costs, benefits and effects on neighbouring coasts.
- Holderness: soft boulder clay retreating roughly 1.5-2 m a year; towns protected, Mappleton groynes starved beaches to the south. Beira: low-lying port hit by Cyclone Idai in 2019.
:::
