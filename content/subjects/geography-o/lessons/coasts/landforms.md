## Introduction

Photographs of famous coasts often show a tall pillar of rock standing in the sea, a natural arch, or a long finger of sand reaching across a bay. Each of these is the result of the processes in the previous lesson: waves eroding the coast where energy is high, and depositing material where energy is low. Because the processes are well understood, geographers can explain each landform as a sequence of stages, and even predict how it will change.

This lesson covers landforms of **erosion** and of **deposition**, and the two main types of coastline. You need to know hydraulic action, abrasion, wave refraction and longshore drift.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Headland and bay"}
A **headland** is a section of resistant rock that juts out into the sea. A **bay** is a curved inlet of the sea between two headlands, eroded in weaker rock.
:::

:::definition{term="Wave-cut platform"}
A gently sloping, rocky surface at the foot of a cliff, exposed at low tide, left behind as the cliff retreats.
:::

:::definition{term="Stack and stump"}
A **stack** is an isolated pillar of rock standing in the sea, separated from a headland. A **stump** is the low remains of a stack after it has collapsed, often covered at high tide.
:::

:::definition{term="Spit"}
A long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle joined to the land at one end and extending into the sea or across a river mouth.
:::

:::definition{term="Concordant and discordant coastlines"}
On a **concordant** coastline, bands of different rock run **parallel** to the coast. On a **discordant** coastline, they run at **right angles** (or a steep angle) to the coast.
:::

## Landforms of erosion

### Headlands and bays

Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the sea at right angles (a discordant coast), the soft rock (for example clay or sand) is eroded faster and is worn back into **bays**. The hard rock (for example chalk, limestone or granite) is eroded more slowly and is left jutting out as **headlands**. Once headlands form, wave refraction concentrates energy on them, while bays become sheltered and collect sandy **bay-head beaches**.

### Cliffs and wave-cut platforms

**Formation in words** (draw a cross-section in four stages):

1. Waves attack the base of a cliff between high and low tide by hydraulic action and abrasion.
2. A **wave-cut notch** is eroded at the base.
3. The notch deepens until the rock above is unsupported and collapses. Sub-aerial weathering above helps.
4. The fallen debris is broken down and removed by the sea, and the cliff **retreats** inland.
5. As it retreats it leaves behind a gently sloping **wave-cut platform**, smoothed by abrasion and exposed at low tide. As the platform widens, waves break further out and lose energy before reaching the cliff, so erosion slows.

:::example{title="Rate of cliff retreat (fictional data)"}
A fictional clay cliff top was measured at 42 m from a road in 1995 and at 15 m from the road in 2025.

1. Retreat = 42 − 15 = 27 m.
2. Time = 2025 − 1995 = 30 years.
3. Mean rate = 27 ÷ 30 = 0.9 m per year.
4. At this rate the road would be reached in about 15 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 17 years.
:::

### Caves, arches, stacks and stumps

**Formation in words** (draw five stages of a headland):

1. Waves exploit a **line of weakness** (a joint or fault) in the headland by hydraulic action and abrasion. The crack widens.
2. The crack is enlarged into a **cave**.
3. The cave is eroded right through the headland, often where caves on both sides meet, forming an **arch**.
4. The arch is widened by waves at its base and weakened by weathering above until the roof **collapses**, leaving an isolated **stack**.
5. The stack is undercut at its base and eventually collapses into a low **stump**.

:::example{title="Named examples: the Dorset coast, England"}
The Jurassic Coast of Dorset is a World Heritage Site with classic examples. **Durdle Door** is a limestone arch. **Old Harry Rocks**, at the end of a chalk headland near Studland, are a stack and a stump; a neighbouring stack collapsed in the 1890s. At **Lulworth Cove** the sea broke through a narrow band of resistant limestone running parallel to the coast and then eroded the softer clays behind it into an almost circular cove: a concordant coast. Further east, **Swanage Bay** (soft clays) between the chalk of the Foreland and the limestone of Durlston Head is a discordant coast of bays and headlands.
:::

## Landforms of deposition

### Beaches

Beaches form where waves deposit sand and shingle, especially where **constructive waves** dominate and in **sheltered bays**. Sandy beaches have a gentle gradient; shingle beaches are steeper because shingle is permeable and the backwash is weakened. Features include a **berm** (a ridge at the high-tide mark) and **storm ridges** higher up, built by large storm waves.

### Spits

**Formation in words** (draw a plan view):

1. **Longshore drift** carries sand and shingle along the coast in the direction of the prevailing wind.
2. Where the coastline **changes direction** (for example at a river mouth or a bend in the coast), drift continues to carry material out into the open water.
3. The water there is calmer, so material is deposited and builds up, extending the ridge into the sea.
4. Changes in wind direction and wave refraction curve the end of the spit, forming a **recurved (hooked) end**.
5. In the sheltered water behind the spit, mud is deposited and **salt marshes** develop.
6. A spit across a river mouth cannot close it completely because the river's current keeps the channel open.

**Example**: **Spurn Head**, a curved spit about 5 km long at the mouth of the Humber estuary in eastern England, is built of material eroded from the Holderness cliffs to the north and carried south by longshore drift.

### Bars and tombolos

If a spit grows right across a bay where there is no strong river current, it joins two headlands and forms a **bar**, trapping a **lagoon** behind it. **Slapton Sands** in Devon, England, is a shingle bar with a freshwater lagoon (Slapton Ley) behind it. A ridge of sediment that joins an island to the mainland is a **tombolo**.

### Sand dunes

**Formation in words**:

1. A wide beach dries out at low tide; onshore winds blow dry sand inland.
2. The sand is trapped by obstacles such as driftwood or seaweed and forms small **embryo dunes**.
3. Hardy plants, especially **marram grass**, colonise; their roots bind the sand and they grow up through new layers, so dunes grow higher (**fore dunes or yellow dunes**).
4. Further inland, older dunes gain more plants and humus, turning grey (**grey dunes**), and hollows between them may hold water (**dune slacks**).

Large coastal dunes occur, for example, on the islands of the Bazaruto archipelago off Mozambique. Dunes protect the land behind them from the sea, but trampling and vehicles can destroy the vegetation and start blow-outs.

## Concordant and discordant coastlines

| | Concordant | Discordant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rock bands | Parallel to the coast | At right angles to the coast |
| Typical shape | Fairly straight, with occasional coves where the outer hard band is breached | Indented: alternating headlands and bays |
| Example | Lulworth Cove area, Dorset | Swanage Bay area, Dorset |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the line of weakness"}
Caves do not appear at random. They start where waves exploit a **joint, crack or fault**. Mention it as the first stage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a spit forms because waves push sand straight out to sea"}
A spit is built by **longshore drift** continuing past a **change in the direction of the coast**, into calmer water where material is deposited.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain the formation of a stack" needs the full sequence: line of weakness → cave → arch → collapse → stack (→ stump). Use process names (hydraulic action, abrasion) at each step.
:::

:::exam-tip
If a resource shows a geology map, look at the direction of the rock bands relative to the coast to decide whether it is concordant or discordant, then explain which bands form headlands and which form bays.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Headlands (resistant rock) and bays (weak rock) form on discordant coasts.
- Cliffs retreat through notching and collapse, leaving wave-cut platforms.
- Line of weakness → cave → arch → stack → stump (e.g. Durdle Door, Old Harry Rocks).
- Beaches form in low-energy places; spits form where longshore drift continues past a change in coastline direction (Spurn Head); bars close off lagoons (Slapton Sands).
- Sand dunes develop as marram grass traps wind-blown sand.
- Concordant coasts: rock bands parallel to the coast (Lulworth Cove). Discordant: at right angles (Swanage Bay).
:::
