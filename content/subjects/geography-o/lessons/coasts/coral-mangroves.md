## Introduction

Off the coast of Mozambique, around the islands of the Bazaruto archipelago, coral reefs support turtles, dugongs and a dazzling variety of fish, and they draw divers from around the world, including many from Zimbabwe. Along the same coast, in sheltered estuaries and the Zambezi delta, dense forests of **mangrove** trees grow in salty mud flooded twice a day by the tide. Coral reefs and mangroves are two of the most productive ecosystems on Earth, and two of the most threatened.

This lesson explains where they are found, why they matter, what threatens them and how they can be protected. You should know what an ecosystem is (producers, consumers, food webs).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Coral reef"}
A ridge or mound built from the limestone skeletons of tiny marine animals called coral polyps, which live in colonies in warm, shallow, clear tropical seas.
:::

:::definition{term="Zooxanthellae"}
Microscopic algae that live inside coral polyps. They photosynthesise and supply the coral with food and colour; the coral gives them shelter and nutrients. This is a **symbiotic** relationship.
:::

:::definition{term="Coral bleaching"}
The whitening of coral when stress, especially unusually warm water, makes the polyps expel their zooxanthellae. Bleached coral is not yet dead, but it will starve if the stress lasts too long.
:::

:::definition{term="Mangrove"}
A tree or shrub adapted to grow in salty, waterlogged mud in the intertidal zone of sheltered tropical and subtropical coasts.
:::

## Coral reefs: where and why

Reef-building corals need:

| Condition | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Warm water**, usually above about 18 °C, best roughly 23-29 °C | Polyps grow slowly in cooler water; too hot causes bleaching |
| **Shallow water** (most reef-building corals grow within a few tens of metres of the surface) | Zooxanthellae need sunlight to photosynthesise |
| **Clear water** with little sediment | Mud blocks sunlight and smothers polyps |
| **Salt water** of normal salinity | Fresh water from large rivers kills coral, so reefs are often absent near river mouths |
| **Well-oxygenated, unpolluted water** with wave action | Supplies oxygen and food |

So coral reefs lie mainly **between about 30° N and 30° S**, especially on the **eastern** sides of continents where warm ocean currents flow, for example the Great Barrier Reef (Australia), the Caribbean, the Red Sea, the Indian Ocean (including Mozambique, Kenya and the Maldives) and South-East Asia. They are largely absent from western coasts, such as west Africa and western South America, where cold currents flow.

There are three main types: **fringing reefs** grow close to the shore; **barrier reefs** are separated from the shore by a lagoon; **atolls** are rings of reef around a lagoon, often where a volcanic island has sunk.

## Mangroves: where and why

Mangroves grow on **sheltered**, low-energy tropical and subtropical coasts, mainly between about 30° N and 30° S: estuaries, deltas and lagoons where fine mud is deposited. They have special adaptations:

- **Prop (stilt) roots** anchor the tree in soft mud and slow the water;
- **Breathing roots (pneumatophores)** stick up out of the mud to take in oxygen, because waterlogged mud has little air;
- **Salt tolerance**: some species filter salt at the roots, others excrete it through their leaves;
- **Seedlings** grow while still attached to the parent, then drop into the mud or float to new sites.

The largest mangrove forest in the world is the **Sundarbans** in the Ganges delta of Bangladesh and India. In Africa, Mozambique has large areas of mangroves, notably in the Zambezi delta.

## Why coral reefs and mangroves matter

| Value | Coral reefs | Mangroves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Biodiversity** | Cover a tiny share of the ocean floor yet support a large proportion of marine species | Home to fish, crabs, birds, monkeys and, in the Sundarbans, tigers |
| **Coastal protection** | Break waves offshore, reducing erosion and storm damage | Roots absorb wave energy and storm surges and trap sediment, building land |
| **Fisheries** | Food and income for coastal communities | Nurseries for young fish and prawns that later live offshore |
| **Tourism** | Diving and snorkelling | Eco-tourism, boat trips |
| **Other** | Medicines from reef organisms; sand for beaches | Timber, fuel, honey; store large amounts of carbon ("blue carbon") |

## Threats

**To coral reefs**

- **Ocean warming**: periods of unusually high sea temperature cause mass **bleaching**. Ocean acidification (seawater absorbing carbon dioxide) makes it harder for corals to build skeletons.
- **Pollution**: sediment and fertiliser washed from farms; sewage; oil spills. Nutrients encourage algae that smother coral.
- **Overfishing** and destructive fishing with dynamite or poison; removal of grazing fish allows algae to spread.
- **Tourism**: trampling, anchors, souvenir collecting.
- **Coastal development** and dredging that stir up sediment.
- **Natural threats**: tropical cyclones; outbreaks of coral-eating crown-of-thorns starfish.

**To mangroves**

- **Clearance** for shrimp (prawn) farms, rice fields, salt pans, tourist resorts, ports and housing.
- **Cutting** for firewood, charcoal and building poles.
- **Pollution** from oil, sewage and pesticides.
- Changes in river flow and sediment supply (for example, dams upstream) and **sea-level rise**.

:::example{title="Measuring change in coral cover (fictional data)"}
Surveys at a fictional reef recorded live hard coral covering 42 % of the sea bed in 2014 and 27 % in 2024.

1. Change = 42 − 27 = 15 percentage points.
2. As a percentage of the 2014 cover: 15 ÷ 42 × 100 = 35.7 %, so about 36 % of the live coral was lost.
:::

## Protecting and managing coral reefs and mangroves

- **Marine protected areas** with **zoning**: no-take zones where fishing is banned, plus zones for tourism and limited fishing.
- **Reducing pollution at source**: better farming practices to cut run-off, sewage treatment.
- **Sustainable fishing**: quotas, bans on dynamite and poison fishing, community-managed fishing areas.
- **Sustainable tourism**: mooring buoys instead of anchors, limits on visitor numbers, education of divers.
- **Mangrove replanting and protection**: community forestry, laws against clearance, payments for blue carbon.
- **Tackling climate change**: reducing greenhouse gas emissions is the only long-term answer to bleaching.

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: the Great Barrier Reef, Australia"}
- **Where and what**: the world's largest coral reef system, stretching more than 2,300 km along the coast of Queensland in north-east Australia and made up of thousands of individual reefs and hundreds of islands. It became a World Heritage Site in 1981.
- **Importance**: thousands of species of fish, corals and molluscs; nesting sites for turtles and seabirds; protection of the coast from waves; tourism worth billions of Australian dollars a year and supporting tens of thousands of jobs; commercial and recreational fishing.
- **Threats**: repeated **mass bleaching** during marine heatwaves (for example in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and 2024); run-off of sediment, fertiliser and pesticides from sugar-cane and cattle farms in river catchments; outbreaks of **crown-of-thorns starfish**, which may be linked to nutrient run-off; tropical cyclones; shipping, coastal development and tourism pressure.
- **Management**: the reef is managed as a marine park by the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority. A **zoning plan** introduced in 2004 made about a third of the park no-take "green zones". Programmes pay and advise farmers to reduce run-off, divers cull crown-of-thorns starfish, and a long-term sustainability plan (Reef 2050) coordinates action.
- **Evaluation**: zoning has helped fish populations recover in protected areas, and local threats are better controlled than on most reefs in the world. But local management cannot stop ocean warming: bleaching events are becoming more frequent, leaving less time for coral to recover. The reef's future depends heavily on global action on climate change.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying bleached coral is dead"}
Bleached coral has lost its algae and is stressed, but it can **recover** if temperatures fall quickly. Prolonged bleaching kills it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting mangroves on exposed coasts"}
Mangroves need **sheltered**, muddy, low-energy coasts such as estuaries and deltas. They then help protect the coast behind them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Coral reefs need warm (above about 18 °C), shallow, clear, salty, unpolluted water; most lie between about 30° N and 30° S on eastern coasts.
- Mangroves grow on sheltered, muddy tropical coasts and have prop roots, breathing roots and salt tolerance.
- Both support high biodiversity, protect coasts, sustain fisheries and tourism; mangroves store carbon.
- Threats: warming and bleaching, acidification, pollution, overfishing, tourism, clearance for shrimp farms and development.
- Management: marine protected areas and zoning, pollution control, sustainable fishing and tourism, replanting, cutting emissions.
- Great Barrier Reef: repeated bleaching, farm run-off and crown-of-thorns starfish; managed by zoning (about a third no-take since 2004) and Reef 2050, but climate change remains the greatest threat.
:::
