## Introduction

In 2010 two earthquakes of similar size struck two island nations. In January, a magnitude 7.0 earthquake near Port-au-Prince in Haiti killed a very large number of people; estimates range from about 100,000 to over 200,000. In September, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake near Christchurch in New Zealand caused heavy damage but no deaths directly from the shaking. The physical event was similar. The outcome was not.

A **natural hazard** only becomes a **disaster** when it affects people. This lesson asks why millions of people live close to active faults and volcanoes, what impacts tectonic events have, and why the same size of event can be a minor emergency in one place and a catastrophe in another.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural hazard"}
A natural event, such as an earthquake or eruption, that has the potential to cause loss of life, injury or damage to property and the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Disaster"}
A hazard event that seriously disrupts a community, causing widespread human, economic or environmental losses that the community cannot cope with using its own resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Vulnerability"}
How likely people and places are to suffer harm from a hazard, depending on factors such as poverty, building quality, preparedness and access to help.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary and secondary impacts"}
Primary impacts are caused directly by the event (buildings collapse from shaking, lava burns houses). Secondary impacts follow from it (fires, disease, homelessness, loss of jobs).
:::

## Why do people live in hazardous areas?

| Reason | Explanation and example |
| --- | --- |
| Fertile soils | Weathered volcanic ash and lava give rich soils for crops, for example on the slopes of Kilimanjaro and Vesuvius |
| Geothermal energy | Heat from volcanic rocks generates cheap electricity and heating, as in Iceland and Kenya's rift valley |
| Minerals and jobs | Volcanic areas can hold sulphur and metal ores; large cities on fault zones, such as Tokyo, Los Angeles and Kathmandu, offer work and services |
| Tourism | Volcanoes, hot springs and scenery attract visitors and create jobs |
| Attachment to place | Families have lived there for generations; land, homes, graves and culture tie them to it |
| Poverty | Poorer people cannot afford to move or buy land elsewhere |
| Confidence | People trust monitoring, warnings and strong buildings, or believe a big event will not happen in their lifetime because events are rare |

## Impacts of earthquakes

| Type | Short term | Long term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social | Deaths and injuries; people trapped; homelessness; shortages of water, food and shelter | Disease in crowded camps (cholera spread in Haiti later in 2010); trauma; loss of schools and health services |
| Economic | Destroyed buildings, roads, ports and power lines; businesses closed | Cost of rebuilding; lost jobs and income; tourism falls; insurance costs rise; government debt |
| Environmental | Landslides; fires; tsunami flooding; liquefaction | Damaged ecosystems and farmland; pollution, for example from the Fukushima nuclear plant after the 2011 tsunami in Japan |

## Impacts of volcanic eruptions

| Type | Short term | Long term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social | Deaths from pyroclastic flows, lahars and gases; evacuation; breathing problems from ash | Communities abandoned; people migrate away |
| Economic | Crops and livestock lost; buildings, roads and airports damaged; air travel disrupted | Farmland unusable for years; tourism lost; but later fertile soils and new tourism |
| Environmental | Forests burned or buried; rivers blocked; acid rain | Large eruptions can cool the climate for a year or two; new land and soils form |

:::example{title="Example: the Soufrière Hills volcano, Montserrat (from 1995)"}
Montserrat is a small island in the Caribbean, a British Overseas Territory. Eruptions that began in 1995 produced pyroclastic flows and ash; in June 1997 pyroclastic flows killed 19 people who had stayed in the danger zone. The capital, Plymouth, was buried and abandoned, the southern part of the island became an exclusion zone, and many residents left the island permanently. The airport was destroyed and the economy shrank. Later, the volcano itself became a tourist attraction.
:::

## Why do some events cause more damage than others?

**Physical factors**

- **Magnitude** (energy released) and, for volcanoes, the size and type of eruption (VEI).
- **Depth of focus**: shallow earthquakes cause stronger surface shaking.
- **Distance** from the epicentre or volcano to towns.
- **Ground conditions**: soft, waterlogged ground amplifies shaking and liquefies.
- **Secondary hazards**: tsunamis, landslides and lahars.

**Human factors**

- **Population density**: more people exposed in cities than in remote areas.
- **Building quality**: unreinforced concrete and heavy roofs collapse; reinforced and flexible buildings survive.
- **Level of development**: high-income countries (HICs) have stronger building codes, monitoring, trained emergency services and money to rebuild; low-income countries (LICs) often lack these.
- **Preparedness**: drills, warnings and emergency plans save lives.
- **Time of day**: an earthquake during working or school hours, or at night when people are asleep in weak houses, changes the death toll.

:::example{title="Comparison: Haiti (LIC) and Canterbury, New Zealand (HIC), 2010"}
| | Haiti, January 2010 | Canterbury, September 2010 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnitude | 7.0 | 7.1 |
| Location | Shallow, close to the capital, Port-au-Prince | Shallow, about 40 km from Christchurch |
| Buildings | Many poorly built concrete homes, no enforced code | Strict building codes |
| Deaths | Estimates from about 100,000 to over 200,000 | None directly from the shaking |
| Response | Government buildings, hospitals and the port damaged; heavy reliance on foreign aid | Well-organised national emergency services and insurance |

The magnitudes were almost the same; differences in vulnerability explain most of the difference in deaths.
:::

## Measuring the size of events (reminder)

Earthquake **magnitude** is measured on the moment magnitude scale (Richter values are similar), which is logarithmic; **intensity** (effects at a place) is measured on the Mercalli scale from I to XII. The size of an eruption is measured on the **Volcanic Explosivity Index**, from 0 to 8, also roughly logarithmic.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming bigger magnitude always means more deaths"}
Vulnerability often matters more than magnitude. Compare Haiti and Canterbury.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing only negative impacts of volcanoes"}
Eruptions also bring long-term benefits: fertile soils, geothermal energy and tourism.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling people who stay 'ignorant'"}
Most people who live near hazards have good reasons: jobs, land, family, poverty or trust in protection. Explain these reasons.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Organise impacts as social, economic and environmental, and short and long term; a table in your head helps you cover everything in a 6-7 mark answer.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- People live in hazardous areas for fertile soils, geothermal energy, minerals, jobs, tourism, attachment to place, poverty and confidence in protection.
- Impacts are social, economic and environmental, and short and long term; primary impacts come directly from the event, secondary impacts follow.
- Physical factors (magnitude, depth, distance, ground, secondary hazards) and human factors (population density, building quality, development, preparedness, time of day) decide the damage.
- Similar earthquakes in Haiti and Canterbury (2010) had very different death tolls because of differences in vulnerability.
- Size is measured by magnitude (moment magnitude, Richter), intensity (Mercalli) and, for eruptions, the VEI.
:::
