## Introduction

We cannot stop earthquakes or eruptions. We can, however, decide where to build, how to build, how to warn people and how to respond. Japan, which has some of the world's most frequent strong earthquakes, holds national disaster drills every year and builds its tall buildings to sway rather than snap. In 1991, scientists watching Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines persuaded tens of thousands of people to leave before one of the largest eruptions of the twentieth century.

This lesson looks at how tectonic hazards are managed, how to judge which methods work best, and two detailed examples you can use in exams.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary (immediate) responses"}
Actions taken in the hours and days after an event to save lives and meet urgent needs: search and rescue, first aid, emergency shelter, food and clean water.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary (long-term) responses"}
Actions taken in the following months and years to rebuild and reduce future risk: rebuilding homes and infrastructure, restoring jobs and services, and improving building codes.
:::

:::definition{term="Disaster risk management (DRM)"}
A continuous cycle of reducing the risk of disasters and dealing with them: mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery.
:::

:::definition{term="Retrofitting"}
Adding strengthening features to existing buildings so that they can withstand earthquakes.
:::

## The disaster risk management cycle

1. **Mitigation**: reduce the risk before an event (zoning, strong buildings).
2. **Preparedness**: plans, drills, warning systems and emergency supplies.
3. **Response**: rescue, medical care, shelter and relief immediately after the event.
4. **Recovery**: rebuilding, ideally "building back better" so the next event does less harm; this leads back to mitigation.

## Monitoring and prediction

**Volcanoes** usually give warning signs, so eruptions can often be forecast:

- **Seismometers** detect swarms of small earthquakes as magma rises.
- **Tiltmeters** and **GPS** measure the ground swelling as magma collects.
- **Gas sampling** detects rising sulphur dioxide and carbon dioxide.
- **Satellites** measure ground movement and detect heat (thermal imaging).

**Earthquakes cannot be predicted** to an exact time. Scientists can **forecast** the probability of a large earthquake on a fault over decades, using past records and measured stress. **Early-warning systems**, such as Japan's, detect the fast P waves and automatically send alerts seconds before the damaging waves arrive, enough to stop trains, open fire-station doors and let people take cover.

## Protection: building to survive

:::example{title="Diagram in words: an earthquake-resistant building"}
Imagine a tall office block in cross-section.

1. **Deep foundations** anchored in solid rock, not loose soil.
2. **Rubber or spring base isolators** between the foundations and the building, which absorb shaking.
3. A **steel or reinforced-concrete frame** with **cross-bracing** that lets the building sway without breaking.
4. A heavy **counterweight (damper)** near the roof that moves against the sway.
5. **Shatterproof glass**, and furniture and shelves fixed to walls.
6. **Automatic shut-off** valves on gas and electricity supplies to prevent fires.
7. **Open space** around the building for safe evacuation.
:::

In poorer areas, cheaper methods help: lighter roofs, timber frames, bands of reinforcement around walls, and better-mixed concrete. **Retrofitting** strengthens schools, hospitals and bridges already standing.

For volcanoes, protection is harder: barriers and channels can divert some lava flows and lahars, and roofs can be sloped and strengthened against ash, but evacuation is the main defence.

## Planning

- **Hazard mapping**: maps show areas at risk from shaking, liquefaction, lava, pyroclastic flows and lahars.
- **Land-use zoning**: keep homes, schools and hospitals out of the most dangerous zones; use them for parks or farmland.
- **Evacuation plans** and routes, with alert levels for volcanoes.
- **Drills and education**: "drop, cover and hold on"; practising evacuation in schools.
- **Emergency kits**: water, food, torch, radio, first-aid kit and medicines for at least three days.

## Evaluating the strategies

| Strategy | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monitoring and prediction | Saves many lives in eruptions; allows evacuation | Expensive equipment and experts; false alarms reduce trust; earthquakes cannot be predicted |
| Earthquake-resistant buildings | Saves lives and property in every future event | High cost; retrofitting old or informal housing is difficult in LICs |
| Hazard mapping and zoning | Cheap once done; reduces exposure long term | Hard to enforce where land is scarce or people already live in danger zones |
| Drills, education, kits | Cheap; quick to introduce; saves lives | Needs repetition and trust; does not protect property |
| Early-warning technology | Seconds of warning reduce injuries and stop transport | Expensive networks; very little warning near the epicentre |

Generally, HICs can afford all of these; LICs gain most from low-cost measures such as education, simple building improvements and community preparedness, often with international support.

## Detailed specific example: the Nepal earthquake, 25 April 2015

- **Cause**: Nepal lies on the collision boundary where the Indo-Australian plate (carrying India) pushes north into the Eurasian plate. Stress built up on a major fault and was released in a **magnitude 7.8** earthquake with its epicentre in Gorkha district, between Kathmandu and Pokhara, at a shallow depth. A strong aftershock (about magnitude 7.3) followed on 12 May.
- **Impacts**: about 9,000 people died and many thousands were injured. Hundreds of thousands of homes were destroyed, many of them traditional stone and brick buildings in villages and old buildings in Kathmandu, including historic temples. Landslides blocked roads to remote mountain villages, and an avalanche struck Everest base camp. Tourism, a key source of income, fell sharply.
- **Primary responses**: search and rescue by the Nepali army and teams from many countries; helicopters reached cut-off villages; field hospitals, tents, food and water were supplied with international aid.
- **Secondary responses**: a national reconstruction programme and grants for rebuilding homes with earthquake-resistant designs; repairs to roads and heritage sites. Rebuilding was slow in remote areas because of the terrain, cost and organisational problems.
- **Lesson**: as a low-income country with steep relief and many weak buildings, Nepal was highly vulnerable, and access to remote areas limited the response.

## Detailed specific example: Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, June 1991

- **Cause**: Pinatubo lies on a destructive boundary where oceanic crust is subducted beneath the Philippine islands. After centuries of quiet, magma rose and the volcano reawakened in early 1991. Its main eruption on 15 June 1991 was one of the largest of the twentieth century (VEI 6).
- **Monitoring and response**: Philippine and United States scientists installed seismometers and measured gas and ground movement. Using alert levels, the authorities evacuated tens of thousands of people, and a nearby US air base, before the main eruption. This is estimated to have saved many thousands of lives.
- **Impacts**: about 800-900 people died, many when roofs collapsed under ash made heavy by the rain of a typhoon that struck at the same time. Ash buried farmland; lahars swept down river valleys during the rainy seasons of the following years, burying villages. The eruption sent so much sulphur dioxide into the upper atmosphere that global average temperature fell by roughly 0.5 °C for about a year.
- **Lesson**: monitoring and evacuation worked, but secondary hazards (lahars) and long-term recovery remained serious problems for years.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Claiming scientists can predict earthquakes"}
Scientists can forecast probabilities and give seconds of warning once an earthquake starts, but they cannot predict the exact time and place in advance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up primary and secondary responses"}
Primary = immediate (rescue, aid). Secondary = long term (rebuilding, new codes).
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaluating without judgement"}
"Evaluate" needs strengths, limitations **and** a conclusion about which method is most effective in a given place.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 extended parts (6-7 marks) ask for a **detailed specific example**: give the place, date, cause, figures and named responses. Learn Nepal 2015 and Pinatubo 1991 (or the examples your teacher used).
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 asks for "measures to reduce the effects": for each measure say **how** it reduces deaths or damage.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link each method to the hazard: monitoring works well for volcanoes; for earthquakes, buildings and planning matter most.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Primary responses save lives immediately; secondary responses rebuild and reduce future risk; the DRM cycle runs mitigation → preparedness → response → recovery.
- Volcanoes can be monitored (seismometers, tiltmeters, GPS, gas, satellites) and eruptions forecast; earthquakes can only be forecast as probabilities, with seconds of early warning.
- Protection: earthquake-resistant design and retrofitting; planning: hazard maps, zoning, evacuation plans, drills and emergency kits.
- Each method has costs and limits; low-cost education and simple building improvements matter most in LICs.
- Nepal 2015 (magnitude 7.8, about 9,000 deaths) shows high vulnerability; Pinatubo 1991 shows how monitoring and evacuation save lives.
:::
