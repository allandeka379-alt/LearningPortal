## Introduction

On 22 February 2006 a strong earthquake, of magnitude about 7, struck central Mozambique. The shaking was felt across eastern Zimbabwe, hundreds of kilometres away. Most Zimbabweans never feel an earthquake, because the country lies far from the main plate boundaries, but southern Africa is not completely quiet.

An **earthquake** is a sudden shaking of the ground caused by the release of energy stored in rocks. This lesson explains why earthquakes happen, how the energy travels, how earthquakes are measured and the secondary effects that often cause more damage than the shaking itself.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Earthquake"}
A sudden, violent shaking of the ground caused by the release of built-up stress when rocks break or slip along a fault.
:::

:::definition{term="Focus (hypocentre)"}
The point underground where the rocks first break and the earthquake starts.
:::

:::definition{term="Epicentre"}
The point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus. Shaking is usually strongest here.
:::

:::definition{term="Seismic waves"}
Waves of energy that travel out from the focus through the Earth and along its surface.
:::

:::definition{term="Seismometer (seismograph)"}
An instrument that detects and records ground movement; the record is a seismogram.
:::

## What causes earthquakes?

1. Plates move, but along faults the rocks are locked together by **friction**.
2. **Stress** builds up and the rocks bend slightly, storing energy, often for many years.
3. When the stress becomes greater than the friction, the rocks **suddenly slip** along the fault.
4. The stored energy is released as **seismic waves** that spread out from the focus.

Most earthquakes occur at **plate boundaries**. Others happen on old faults within plates, near volcanoes as magma moves, and some are **triggered by people**: the filling of large reservoirs (earthquakes followed the filling of Lake Kariba in the early 1960s) and deep mining (for example in South Africa's gold-mining areas).

**Depth of focus**: shallow earthquakes (less than about 70 km deep) usually cause more surface damage than deep ones, because the waves lose less energy before reaching the surface.

## Seismic waves

| Wave | Speed | Movement | Travels through | Damage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P waves (primary) | Fastest; arrive first | Push and pull (compress and stretch the rock) in the direction of travel | Solids and liquids | Least |
| S waves (secondary) | Slower; arrive second | Shake the rock side to side or up and down | Solids only | Moderate |
| Surface waves | Slowest; arrive last | Roll and sway the ground surface | Along the surface | Most; largest movement |

Because P and S waves travel at different speeds, the gap between their arrival times tells scientists how far away the earthquake was: the longer the gap, the further the epicentre. Readings from three or more stations locate the epicentre.

:::example{title="Worked example: distance from the time gap"}
At a fictional station the S waves arrive 25 seconds after the P waves. Using a simple rule that each second of gap represents about 8 km:

1. Distance ≈ 25 × 8 km.
2. Distance ≈ 200 km.

A circle of radius 200 km drawn round the station passes through the epicentre; circles from two other stations cross at the epicentre.
:::

## Measuring earthquakes

**Magnitude** measures the **energy released** at the source. There is one magnitude for each earthquake.

- **Richter scale**: developed in the 1930s from the height (amplitude) of waves on a seismogram. It is **logarithmic**: each whole number is **ten times** greater ground movement and about **32 times** more energy. A magnitude 6 earthquake therefore shakes the ground ten times more than a magnitude 5.
- **Moment magnitude scale (Mw)**: used by scientists today, especially for large earthquakes. It is based on the area of the fault that slipped and how far it moved. Its numbers are similar to Richter values for moderate earthquakes. News reports often still say "Richter scale" when they give a moment magnitude.

**Intensity** measures the **effects** at a particular place.

- **Modified Mercalli scale**: from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction), based on what people felt and saw and the damage to buildings. One earthquake has many intensities: high near the epicentre, lower further away, and higher on soft ground.

| Mercalli level (selected) | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| II-III | Felt by a few people indoors; hanging objects swing |
| V | Felt by nearly everyone; dishes break; unstable objects fall |
| VII | Difficult to stand; damage to poorly built buildings |
| IX | Serious damage even to well-designed buildings; ground cracks |
| XII | Total destruction; objects thrown into the air |

## Secondary effects

| Effect | How it happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tsunami | An undersea earthquake suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor, pushing up the water above; waves cross oceans at high speed and grow tall in shallow coastal water | Indian Ocean, 26 December 2004: about 230,000 deaths in many countries; Japan, 11 March 2011 |
| Liquefaction | Shaking makes water-saturated sand and silt behave like a liquid, so buildings tilt and sink | Christchurch, New Zealand, 2011 |
| Landslides | Shaking loosens slopes in mountainous areas | Nepal, 2015 |
| Fires | Broken gas pipes and electricity cables start fires; broken water mains make them hard to fight | San Francisco, 1906; Kobe, Japan, 1995 |

## Earthquakes in southern Africa

- The **East African Rift** has frequent small and moderate earthquakes as the African plate stretches; its southern end extends towards Lake Malawi and central Mozambique, the area of the 2006 earthquake.
- **Zimbabwe** lies inside the plate. It has occasional small earthquakes, some linked to faults in the Zambezi valley and to Lake Kariba, and it feels larger earthquakes from Mozambique and the rift.
- **South Africa** experiences tremors linked to deep gold mining as well as occasional natural earthquakes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing focus and epicentre"}
The focus is underground, where the rupture starts; the epicentre is on the surface directly above it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating magnitude 6 as twice magnitude 3"}
The scales are logarithmic. Magnitude 6 has 1,000 times more ground movement than magnitude 3 (10 × 10 × 10), not twice as much.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up magnitude and intensity"}
Magnitude is one number for the energy released; intensity (Mercalli) describes effects at each place and changes with distance.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 often asks you to "describe the focus, epicentre and seismic waves" from a diagram. Label the focus below ground, the epicentre directly above it and waves spreading out in circles.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining the cause of an earthquake, use the sequence: plates move → rocks lock by friction → stress builds → sudden slip → energy released as seismic waves.
:::

:::exam-tip
In calculations on logarithmic scales, count the steps and multiply: two steps of the Richter scale is 10 × 10 = 100 times the ground movement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Earthquakes happen when stress built up along faults is suddenly released; most occur at plate boundaries, some within plates or triggered by reservoirs and mining.
- The focus is underground; the epicentre is directly above it. Shallow-focus earthquakes are usually more damaging.
- P waves arrive first, S waves next and surface waves last; surface waves cause most damage.
- Magnitude (Richter, moment magnitude) measures energy and is logarithmic; intensity (Mercalli I-XII) measures effects at a place.
- Secondary effects include tsunamis, liquefaction, landslides and fires; southern Africa's earthquakes are linked to the East African Rift, faults, reservoirs and mining.
:::
