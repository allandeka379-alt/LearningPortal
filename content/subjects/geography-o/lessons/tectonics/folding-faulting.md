## Introduction

From Kariba, the road to Harare climbs steeply up the Zambezi escarpment, rising several hundred metres in a short distance. The Zambezi valley below is not only carved by the river: it is a block of the crust that has sunk between faults. Far to the north, the same kind of process has created the Great Rift Valley, with its deep lakes and volcanoes, which runs from Ethiopia to Mozambique.

Plate movements put rocks under enormous **stress**. When rocks are squeezed they bend into **folds**; when they are stretched or squeezed beyond their strength they crack and move along **faults**. This lesson explains both processes and the landforms they create.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Compression"}
Forces that push rocks together, shortening and thickening the crust. It causes folding and reverse faults.
:::

:::definition{term="Tension"}
Forces that pull rocks apart, stretching and thinning the crust. It causes normal faults.
:::

:::definition{term="Fold"}
A bend in layers of rock produced by compression.
:::

:::definition{term="Fault"}
A crack (fracture) in the crust along which the rocks on either side have moved.
:::

:::definition{term="Rift valley (graben)"}
A long, steep-sided valley formed when a block of land sinks between two roughly parallel faults.
:::

:::definition{term="Block mountain (horst)"}
A block of land left standing high, or pushed up, between two faults.
:::

## Folding

Sedimentary rocks are laid down in flat layers. When plates push together, the layers buckle like a tablecloth pushed across a table.

**Parts of a fold**

- **Anticline**: an upfold, shaped like an arch; the layers slope down away from its crest.
- **Syncline**: a downfold, shaped like a trough.
- **Limbs**: the two sloping sides of a fold.
- **Axis (axial plane)**: an imaginary line or plane dividing the fold into two halves.

**Types of fold** (as compression increases)

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Simple (symmetrical) | Both limbs slope at about the same angle; gentle, even pressure |
| Asymmetrical | One limb is steeper than the other; pressure is stronger from one side |
| Overfold | One limb is pushed right over the other, so both limbs slope the same way |
| Recumbent fold | The fold is pushed over until it lies almost flat |
| Overthrust fold (nappe) | Pressure is so great that the fold breaks along a low-angle fault and is pushed forward, sometimes many kilometres |

![Cross-section of folded rock layers showing an anticline (arch) and a syncline (trough) with their limbs and axes, and sketches of four fold types as compression increases: simple, asymmetrical, overfold and recumbent.](media:diagrams/fold-parts-types.png "Figure: parts of a fold and the main types of fold")

:::example{title="Diagram in words: how fold mountains form"}
1. Sediments are laid down in layers on the floor of a sea between two plates.
2. The plates move together. The sea floor sediments are squeezed between them.
3. The layers buckle into anticlines and synclines, which become overfolds and nappes as compression increases.
4. The crust is thickened and pushed up thousands of metres, forming a range of fold mountains.

Fossil sea shells found high in the Himalayas are evidence that the rocks were once on a sea floor.
:::

**Fold mountains** of the world include the Himalayas, the Alps, the Andes and the Rockies (young fold mountains, still rising), and the Atlas Mountains of north-west Africa and the Cape Fold Mountains of South Africa (older ones).

**Importance of fold mountains**: water supply from rivers and snowmelt, HEP, minerals, tourism, forestry and grazing, but also steep slopes, thin soils, avalanches and landslides, earthquakes, and barriers to transport.

## Faulting

When stress is too great for rocks to bend, they break.

| Fault type | Stress | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal fault | Tension | The block on one side slips down; the crust is stretched |
| Reverse fault | Compression | The block on one side is pushed up and over the other; the crust is shortened |
| Tear (strike-slip) fault | Sideways shear | Blocks move horizontally past each other |

The vertical distance a block has moved is called the **throw**. A steep slope left by the movement is a **fault scarp**; a long, high one is an **escarpment**.

### Landforms of faulting

:::example{title="Diagram in words: a rift valley and a block mountain"}
**Rift valley**: draw two parallel normal faults sloping towards each other. Tension pulls the crust apart and the central block sinks between the faults. Label the steep escarpments on each side, the valley floor and any lake.

**Block mountain (horst)**: draw two parallel faults with the central block left high while the land on both sides sinks (or the central block is pushed up by compression). Label the flat or gently tilted top and the steep fault scarps.
:::

- **Fault-line valley**: rocks shattered along a fault are weaker and erode easily, so rivers often cut valleys along fault lines.
- **Tilted block**: a block raised on one side, with a steep scarp face and a gentle back slope.

![Four fault structures in cross-section: a normal fault where tension makes one block slip down, a reverse fault where compression pushes one block up and over the other, a rift valley where a central block sinks between two faults, and a block mountain (horst) where a central block stands high between two faults.](media:diagrams/fault-types-rift-horst.png "Figure: normal fault, reverse fault, rift valley and block mountain")

:::example{title="Case study: the East African Rift Valley"}
The Great Rift Valley runs for thousands of kilometres from the Red Sea and Ethiopia south through Kenya and Tanzania to Lake Malawi and Mozambique. An eastern branch crosses Kenya; a western branch holds Lakes Albert, Edward, Kivu and Tanganyika. It formed as the African plate began to split, stretching the crust so that long blocks sank between faults.

- **Landforms**: steep escarpments, long deep lakes (Tanganyika and Malawi are among the deepest lakes in the world), block mountains such as the Ruwenzori, and volcanoes nearby such as Kilimanjaro and Nyiragongo.
- **Uses**: lake fishing and water supply, fertile volcanic soils, geothermal power in Kenya, tourism and wildlife.
- **Problems**: earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and steep escarpments that make road and rail building difficult.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: the faulted mid-Zambezi valley"}
In northern Zimbabwe, the middle Zambezi valley is a long, low trough bounded by faults, with the steep Zambezi escarpment on its southern side. Over geological time blocks of crust sank here between faults, and younger sedimentary (Karoo) rocks, including coal-bearing layers, were preserved in the trough. Lake Kariba lies in part of this valley. The escarpment makes the climb from the hot valley floor to the plateau steep, and the valley is hot and dry, used mainly for wildlife (Mana Pools), tourism, fishing and hydro-electric power.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying rivers carved rift valleys"}
A river may flow along a rift valley, but the valley was formed by faulting: blocks sank between faults. Rivers then modify it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing anticline and syncline"}
Anticline = arch (A for arch, layers go up); syncline = sink (layers go down into a trough).
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up normal and reverse faults"}
Normal faults come from tension (pulling apart); reverse faults come from compression (pushing together).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Compression folds rocks into anticlines and synclines; as pressure increases, folds become asymmetrical, overfolds, recumbent folds and nappes.
- Fold mountains (Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Atlas) form where plates push sea-floor sediments together and upwards.
- Faults are fractures with movement: normal (tension), reverse (compression) and tear (sideways).
- Faulting produces rift valleys (graben), block mountains (horsts), escarpments, tilted blocks and fault-line valleys.
- Examples: the East African Rift Valley with its lakes and the faulted mid-Zambezi valley with the Zambezi escarpment and Lake Kariba.
:::
