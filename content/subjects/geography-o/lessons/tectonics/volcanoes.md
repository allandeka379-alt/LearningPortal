## Introduction

Kilimanjaro, Africa's highest mountain at about 5,895 m, is a volcano. So is Nyiragongo in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, whose lava lake has sent fast-moving lava into the city of Goma. Zimbabwe has no active volcanoes, but its landscape contains the roots of ancient ones: basalt lavas around Victoria Falls and dark dolerite dykes that cut across the granite.

A **volcano** is an opening in the Earth's crust through which **magma**, gases and rock fragments erupt, together with the landform built around it. This lesson explains why volcanoes form, their types and features, the hazards they bring and why people still value them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Volcano"}
An opening (vent) in the crust through which magma, gases and fragments erupt, and the landform built up around it.
:::

:::definition{term="Viscosity"}
How thick and sticky magma is. Viscous (thick) magma traps gas and erupts explosively; runny magma lets gas escape and erupts gently.
:::

:::definition{term="Pyroclastic flow"}
A fast-moving, extremely hot avalanche of gas, ash and rock fragments that rushes down the slopes of a volcano.
:::

:::definition{term="Lahar"}
A volcanic mudflow formed when ash and debris mix with water from heavy rain, melting snow or a crater lake.
:::

## Why volcanoes form where they do

Magma forms in three main settings:

1. **Constructive boundaries**: plates move apart, pressure falls and the mantle partly melts, producing runny **basalt** magma (Iceland, the East African Rift).
2. **Destructive boundaries**: water released from the subducted oceanic plate lowers the melting point of the mantle above it; the magma is **viscous and gas-rich** (Andes, Japan, Montserrat).
3. **Hot spots**: plumes of hot mantle rise beneath the middle of a plate (Hawaii).

## Types of volcano

| Type | Shape | Magma and eruptions | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strato-volcano (composite cone) | Steep, cone-shaped, tall; alternate layers of ash and lava | Viscous, gas-rich; explosive eruptions with long quiet gaps | Mount Fuji (Japan), Mount St Helens (USA), Kilimanjaro |
| Shield volcano | Very wide with gentle slopes, like a warrior's shield lying on the ground | Runny basalt; frequent, gentle (effusive) eruptions; lava flows travel far | Mauna Loa (Hawaii), volcanoes of Iceland |
| Cinder cone | Small, steep cone of loose cinders around one vent | Gas-rich bursts throw out fragments that pile up; often erupt only once | Parícutin (Mexico), which grew from a farmer's field in 1943 |

:::example{title="Diagram in words: a cross-section of a strato-volcano"}
1. Deep below: a **magma chamber**, a reservoir of molten rock.
2. Rising from it: the **main vent** (pipe), leading to the **crater** at the summit.
3. Inside the cone: alternate **layers of ash and lava** from many eruptions.
4. On the flank: a **side vent** feeding a smaller **secondary (parasitic) cone**.
5. Above the crater: an **ash cloud**; on the slopes, **lava flows** and the path of a **pyroclastic flow**.

If a huge eruption empties the magma chamber, the summit can collapse into a wide basin called a **caldera**.
:::

## Active, dormant and extinct

- **Active**: has erupted in recent times and is likely to erupt again (Nyiragongo, Etna, Mauna Loa).
- **Dormant**: has not erupted for a long time but could erupt again (Kilimanjaro is usually described as dormant).
- **Extinct**: not expected ever to erupt again, because its magma supply has been cut off (Mount Kenya).

These classes are not certain: some "extinct" or long-dormant volcanoes have surprised people by erupting.

## Volcanic hazards

| Hazard | What it is | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lava flows | Molten rock flowing downhill | Usually slow enough to walk away from, but they burn and bury buildings, roads and fields. Runny lava can move fast on steep slopes |
| Pyroclastic flows | Avalanche of hot gas and ash | Can exceed 100 km/h and several hundred °C; kill everything in their path; the deadliest hazard |
| Ash fall (tephra) | Fragments thrown into the air, from fine ash to large "bombs" | Roofs collapse under the weight; crops and grazing are buried; water is polluted; breathing problems; aircraft engines can fail |
| Lahars | Volcanic mudflows | Travel fast down river valleys far from the volcano; bury towns |
| Gases | Carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and others | Suffocation, acid rain, breathing problems |

:::example{title="Examples of volcanic hazards"}
- **Lahar**: in 1985 lahars from Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia buried the town of Armero, tens of kilometres from the summit, killing over 20,000 people.
- **Gas**: in 1986 a sudden release of carbon dioxide from Lake Nyos, a volcanic crater lake in Cameroon, suffocated about 1,700 people in nearby villages.
- **Ash cloud**: in 2010 ash from Eyjafjallajökull in Iceland closed much of Europe's airspace for several days.
:::

### Why speed, size, frequency and spread matter

- **Speed of onset**: slow lava flows allow evacuation; pyroclastic flows and lahars give almost no warning.
- **Size (magnitude)**: larger eruptions eject more material, measured on the **Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)** from 0 to 8; each step up is about ten times more material.
- **Frequency**: frequent small eruptions (Hawaii) are easier to plan for than rare large ones, when people may have forgotten the danger.
- **Spread**: lava affects a narrow path; ash can spread over thousands of square kilometres and even across continents.

## Intrusive landforms (in outline)

Not all magma reaches the surface. When it cools underground it forms **intrusions**, which are exposed later by erosion.

- **Dyke**: a vertical or steep sheet that cuts **across** rock layers.
- **Sill**: a horizontal sheet squeezed **between** rock layers.
- **Batholith**: a huge dome-shaped mass, usually of granite, that cooled slowly deep underground. Much of Zimbabwe's granite plateau and its kopjes are exposed parts of ancient granite masses.
- **Laccolith**: a dome-shaped intrusion that has pushed the layers above it upwards.

## Benefits of volcanoes

- **Fertile soils**: weathered volcanic ash and lava produce rich soils, for example on the slopes of Kilimanjaro, where coffee and bananas are grown.
- **Geothermal energy**: hot rocks heat water for electricity and heating (Iceland; Olkaria in Kenya's rift valley).
- **Minerals**: sulphur, and diamonds from ancient volcanic pipes (kimberlites).
- **Tourism**: climbing Kilimanjaro, visiting Iceland's geysers.
- **New land and building stone**: lava builds islands such as Hawaii and Iceland; basalt is used for building and roads.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking lava is the biggest killer"}
Pyroclastic flows, lahars and ash collapse kill far more people than lava flows, which usually move slowly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up shield and strato-volcanoes"}
Shield = gentle slopes, runny basalt, gentle eruptions. Strato = steep, layered, viscous magma, explosive eruptions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing dykes and sills"}
A **d**yke cuts **d**own across layers; a **s**ill lies **s**ideways between them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 often asks you to "describe the main features of a volcano" from a diagram: name and locate at least four features (magma chamber, vent, crater, secondary cone, ash and lava layers).
:::

:::exam-tip
When describing a hazard, say **what** it is and **why** it is dangerous (speed, temperature, distance travelled or area covered).
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 may ask for the **effects** of volcanic activity: give both positive (soils, energy, tourism) and negative effects.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Volcanoes form at constructive boundaries, destructive boundaries and hot spots.
- Strato-volcanoes are steep and explosive (viscous magma); shield volcanoes are broad and gentle (runny basalt); cinder cones are small cones of loose fragments.
- Features: magma chamber, vent, crater, layers of ash and lava, secondary cones, calderas. Volcanoes are active, dormant or extinct.
- Hazards: lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash fall and tephra, lahars and gases; their impact depends on speed, size (VEI), frequency and spread.
- Intrusions include dykes, sills, batholiths and laccoliths; volcanoes also bring fertile soils, geothermal energy, minerals and tourism.
:::
