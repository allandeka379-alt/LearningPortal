## Introduction

Look at a map of the Atlantic Ocean and the bulge of South America seems to fit into the hollow of West Africa like two pieces of a jigsaw. That is not a coincidence. The continents have moved, and they are still moving, by a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

To understand earthquakes, volcanoes, fold mountains and the Great Rift Valley, you first need to know what the inside of the Earth is like and why its outer shell is broken into moving **plates**. This lesson covers the Earth's layers, the theory of continental drift and the theory of plate tectonics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Crust"}
The thin, solid, outermost layer of the Earth. Continental crust is thick and less dense; oceanic crust is thin and denser.
:::

:::definition{term="Lithosphere"}
The rigid outer shell of the Earth, made of the crust and the uppermost part of the mantle. It is broken into tectonic plates.
:::

:::definition{term="Asthenosphere"}
A weak, partly molten layer of the upper mantle beneath the lithosphere. It can flow very slowly, so the plates can move over it.
:::

:::definition{term="Tectonic plate"}
A large, rigid slab of lithosphere that moves slowly over the asthenosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Continental drift"}
The theory, put forward by Alfred Wegener in the early twentieth century, that the continents were once joined in a single supercontinent (Pangaea) and have slowly drifted apart.
:::

## The layers of the Earth

The Earth's radius is about 6,370 km. Imagine cutting it in half like a peach: a thin skin (crust), a thick flesh (mantle) and a stone (core).

| Layer | Approximate depth or thickness | State | Key characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Crust | Continental about 25-70 km thick (average about 35 km); oceanic about 5-10 km | Solid | Continental: older, less dense, granite-type rocks. Oceanic: younger, denser, basalt |
| Mantle | To about 2,900 km depth | Mostly solid, but the asthenosphere flows slowly | Hot, dense rock; heat drives slow convection currents |
| Outer core | About 2,900-5,100 km | Liquid | Iron and nickel; its movement creates the Earth's magnetic field |
| Inner core | About 5,100 km to the centre | Solid | Iron and nickel; very hot (several thousand °C) but kept solid by enormous pressure |

The **lithosphere** (crust plus the rigid top of the mantle, roughly 100 km thick) floats on the **asthenosphere**.

## Continental drift: Wegener's theory and evidence

Wegener argued that about 250-300 million years ago all the continents formed one supercontinent, **Pangaea**, which later split into Laurasia in the north and **Gondwana** in the south (Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica).

**Evidence**

1. **Jigsaw fit**: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit closely, especially at the edge of the continental shelf.
2. **Fossils**: the same fossils, such as the small reptile *Mesosaurus* and the plant *Glossopteris*, are found on continents now separated by oceans; they could not have crossed them.
3. **Rocks and mountains**: matching rock types and mountain belts line up when the continents are fitted together.
4. **Past climates**: traces of ancient glaciers are found in southern Africa, India and South America, now warm places, and coal (formed from tropical swamps) is found in Antarctica. These places must once have been at different latitudes.

Wegener could not explain **what force** moved the continents, so many scientists rejected his idea. In the 1950s and 1960s, studies of the ocean floor (young rocks at mid-ocean ridges, older rocks further away, and magnetic stripes) showed that the sea floor spreads. This led to the theory of **plate tectonics**.

## Plate tectonics

The lithosphere is divided into about seven large plates and many smaller ones.

| Large plates | Some smaller plates |
| --- | --- |
| Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic | Nazca, Cocos, Caribbean, Philippine, Arabian, Juan de Fuca |

Plates carry continents, oceans or both. Zimbabwe lies near the middle of the **African plate**, far from the main plate boundaries, which is why it has few strong earthquakes and no active volcanoes.

### How plates move

- **Convection currents**: heat from the core warms the mantle; hot rock rises slowly, spreads out under the lithosphere, cools and sinks again. The traditional explanation is that these currents drag the plates along.
- **Ridge push**: at mid-ocean ridges, new hot lithosphere stands high; as it cools and becomes denser it slides away from the ridge under gravity, pushing the plate.
- **Slab pull**: where old, cold, dense oceanic lithosphere sinks into the mantle at a trench, its weight pulls the rest of the plate behind it. Many scientists now think this is the strongest force.

Plates move at about 1-10 cm a year. That seems tiny, but over 100 million years 5 cm a year adds up to 5,000 km.

:::example{title="Worked calculation: how far does a plate move?"}
A plate moves at 4 cm per year. How far does it move in 1 million years?

1. Distance = 4 cm × 1,000,000 = 4,000,000 cm.
2. 4,000,000 cm ÷ 100 = 40,000 m.
3. 40,000 m ÷ 1,000 = 40 km.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the mantle is liquid magma"}
The mantle is almost all solid rock. The asthenosphere is weak and partly molten, so it can flow very slowly, but it is not an ocean of liquid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing crust and lithosphere"}
The lithosphere includes the crust **and** the rigid top of the mantle. Plates are pieces of lithosphere, not just crust.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two cores"}
The outer core is liquid; the inner core is solid because of the huge pressure, even though it is hotter.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Layers: crust (continental thick and light, oceanic thin and dense), mantle (with the slowly flowing asthenosphere), liquid outer core and solid inner core.
- The lithosphere (crust plus top of the mantle) is broken into plates that move over the asthenosphere.
- Wegener's continental drift: Pangaea split up; evidence from the jigsaw fit, fossils, matching rocks and past climates.
- Sea-floor spreading supplied the mechanism, leading to plate tectonics.
- Plates move a few centimetres a year, driven by mantle convection, ridge push and slab pull; Zimbabwe lies inside the African plate.
:::
