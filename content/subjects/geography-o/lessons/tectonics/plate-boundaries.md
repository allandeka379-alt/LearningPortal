## Introduction

If you plotted every large earthquake and every active volcano of the last hundred years on a world map, you would not get a random scatter of dots. You would get **narrow lines**: a great ring around the Pacific Ocean, a line down the middle of the Atlantic, a belt from the Mediterranean through the Himalayas, and a branching line through East Africa. These lines are the **plate boundaries**.

Zimbabwe lies inside the African plate, so it rarely feels strong earthquakes. But only a few hundred kilometres to the north-east, the East African Rift is slowly tearing the continent apart. This lesson explains what happens where plates meet.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plate boundary (plate margin)"}
The zone where two tectonic plates meet and move relative to each other.
:::

:::definition{term="Subduction"}
The sinking of a denser oceanic plate beneath another plate into the mantle, where it melts.
:::

:::definition{term="Ocean trench"}
A long, narrow, very deep part of the ocean floor where one plate is subducted beneath another.
:::

:::definition{term="Hot spot"}
A place where an unusually hot plume of mantle rock rises beneath a plate, away from any boundary, producing volcanoes.
:::

## The four types of boundary

### 1. Constructive (divergent) boundaries

Two plates **move apart**. Pressure falls in the mantle below, rock partly melts and **basalt magma rises** to fill the gap, forming new crust.

- **Under the ocean**: a **mid-ocean ridge** with a central rift, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge between the North American and Eurasian plates. Iceland is part of this ridge that rises above sea level.
- **On a continent**: the land stretches and blocks sink between faults, forming a **rift valley**, such as the East African Rift Valley, where the African plate is splitting.
- **Hazards**: frequent but usually **shallow, small earthquakes**; **gentle eruptions** of runny basalt lava (shield volcanoes and fissure eruptions).

### 2. Destructive (convergent) boundaries: oceanic and continental

An oceanic plate moves **towards** a continental plate. The oceanic plate is **denser**, so it is **subducted**.

:::example{title="Diagram in words: the Nazca and South American plates"}
Draw a cross-section from west to east.

1. The **Nazca plate** (oceanic) moves east towards the **South American plate** (continental).
2. Where they meet, the Nazca plate bends down into the mantle, forming the deep **Peru-Chile trench**.
3. Friction between the plates builds stress that is released in **earthquakes**, some shallow and some very deep along the sinking plate.
4. At about 100 km depth, water released from the sinking plate helps the mantle above it melt. The **magma** is thick and gas-rich; it rises to form **explosive strato-volcanoes**.
5. The edge of the continent is squeezed and crumpled into **fold mountains**, the **Andes**.
:::

### 3. Destructive boundaries: oceanic and oceanic

When two oceanic plates meet, the older, denser one is subducted. A deep trench forms and a curved chain of volcanic islands, an **island arc**, grows on the other plate. Japan, the Mariana Islands (beside the Mariana Trench, the deepest point of the oceans at about 11 km) and the islands of the eastern Caribbean, such as Montserrat, formed this way.

### 4. Collision boundaries

Two **continental** plates move towards each other. Continental crust is too light to be subducted deeply, so the crust is crumpled, thickened and pushed up into great **fold mountains**. The **Himalayas** formed, and are still rising, where the Indo-Australian plate (carrying India) pushes into the Eurasian plate. There are **powerful earthquakes** but **few or no volcanoes**, because no oceanic plate sinks to melt.

### 5. Conservative (transform) boundaries

Two plates **slide past each other**. Crust is neither made nor destroyed. The plates stick, stress builds, and then they jerk past each other in an **earthquake**. There are **no volcanoes**. The **San Andreas Fault** in California, between the Pacific and North American plates, is the best-known example.

## Why earthquakes and volcanoes occur where they do

- **Earthquakes** occur at all plate boundaries, because plates moving against or away from each other build up and release stress.
- **Volcanoes** occur where magma is produced: at constructive boundaries (decompression melting), destructive boundaries (melting above the sinking plate) and hot spots. Collision and conservative boundaries have few or none.
- The **Pacific "Ring of Fire"** is a horseshoe of destructive boundaries around the Pacific Ocean; it has most of the world's active volcanoes and strongest earthquakes.

## Hot spots

A hot spot is a fixed plume of hot mantle rock beneath the middle of a plate. As the plate moves over it, a **chain of volcanoes** forms: the active volcano sits over the plume and older, extinct volcanoes are carried away. The **Hawaiian Islands**, in the middle of the Pacific plate, are the classic example: the island of Hawaii (the Big Island) is active, and the islands get older towards the north-west.

## Plate movement and climate over geological time

Plates move slowly, but over millions of years they change climates.

1. **Continents move through latitudes**: southern Africa carries rocks left by ancient glaciers from a time when it lay near the South Pole, and Antarctica has coal formed in warm swamps.
2. **Mountains change winds and rainfall**: the uplift of the Himalayas affects the Asian monsoon and creates rain shadows.
3. **Ocean currents**: as plates opened and closed seaways, ocean currents changed. When Antarctica separated from South America and Australia, a current formed around it, helping it to become cold and ice-covered.
4. **Volcanic gases and ash**: large eruptions add ash and gases to the atmosphere and can cool the climate for a year or two.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying volcanoes occur at all boundaries"}
Collision and conservative boundaries have earthquakes but few or no volcanoes, because no plate is melting there.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the continental plate is subducted"}
At an ocean-continent boundary the **oceanic** plate sinks because it is denser.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the San Andreas Fault a destructive boundary"}
Plates slide past each other there: it is conservative. No crust is destroyed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Constructive: plates move apart, magma rises; ridges and rift valleys; small earthquakes, gentle eruptions (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift).
- Destructive: denser oceanic plate is subducted; trench, fold mountains or island arcs; strong earthquakes and explosive volcanoes (Andes, Japan).
- Collision: two continental plates crumple into fold mountains; strong earthquakes, few volcanoes (Himalayas).
- Conservative: plates slide past; earthquakes, no volcanoes (San Andreas Fault).
- Hot spots such as Hawaii form volcanoes inside plates; plate movement has changed climates through latitude, mountains, ocean currents and eruptions.
:::
