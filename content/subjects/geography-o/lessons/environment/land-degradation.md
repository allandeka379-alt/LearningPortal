## Introduction

Drive through many communal areas at the end of the dry season and you will see bare, trampled ground around villages, deep gullies cutting across grazing land, and rivers that are wide ribbons of sand instead of flowing water. Soil that took thousands of years to form can be washed away in a few storms. Once it is gone, crops fail, dams fill with silt and families must walk further for firewood and water.

**Land degradation** is a fall in the quality and productivity of land. It happens everywhere, but it hits hardest where people depend directly on farming, grazing and fuelwood. This lesson explains its forms and causes, its effects, and the ways land can be protected or restored.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land degradation"}
A long-term decline in the ability of land to support plants, animals and people, caused by soil erosion, loss of vegetation, loss of nutrients, pollution or changes in water supply.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil erosion"}
The removal of topsoil by water or wind faster than new soil can form.
:::

:::definition{term="Siltation"}
The build-up of eroded soil (silt and sand) on the beds of rivers, dams and wetlands.
:::

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The permanent clearing of trees and woodland, for farmland, fuelwood, timber, settlement or mining.
:::

:::definition{term="Desertification"}
The degradation of land in dry areas (semi-arid and dry sub-humid) until it becomes desert-like, caused by climatic variations such as drought and by human activities such as overgrazing.
:::

## Forms of soil erosion by water

1. **Sheet erosion**: a thin layer of topsoil is removed evenly from a gentle slope by water flowing over the surface. It is hard to notice until stones and roots are exposed.
2. **Rill erosion**: water concentrates into many small channels, a few centimetres deep, which a plough can still smooth over.
3. **Gully erosion**: rills join and deepen into steep-sided channels, sometimes several metres deep, that cannot be ploughed across. Gullies grow headwards (back up the slope) during each rainy season.

![Cross-sections of three forms of water erosion: sheet erosion removes a thin even layer of topsoil, rill erosion cuts many small channels a few centimetres deep that a plough can smooth, and gully erosion cuts a deep steep-sided channel that cannot be ploughed across.](media:diagrams/erosion-sheet-rill-gully.png "Figure: sheet, rill and gully erosion compared")

Wind erosion is most serious on dry, bare, loose soils, especially at the end of the dry season.

## Causes of land degradation

| Cause | How it degrades the land |
| --- | --- |
| Overgrazing | Too many livestock remove the grass cover and trample the soil, so rain splashes and washes it away |
| Over-cultivation | Growing crops year after year without fallow or fertiliser exhausts nutrients and weakens soil structure |
| Poor ploughing | Ploughing up and down slopes creates channels for runoff; cultivating close to stream banks causes bank collapse |
| Deforestation | Clearing for fields and settlement, cutting fuelwood and curing tobacco remove roots that bind soil and leaves that intercept rain |
| Veld fires | Burn grass and young trees, leaving bare ground just before the first heavy storms |
| Sand abstraction and gold panning | Digging river beds and banks for building sand or gold widens channels, destroys banks and adds silt downstream |
| Wetland destruction | Draining, cultivating or building on vleis and dambos dries them out, so they no longer store water and filter silt |
| Drought and rainfall variability | Plants die back, and the first heavy storms fall on unprotected ground |

These causes are linked. Population growth in communal areas increases the demand for land, grazing and fuelwood, which leads to more clearing, which exposes more soil.

:::example{title="Diagram in words: from bare soil to a silted dam"}
1. Grass is grazed to the ground and the last trees on a hillside are cut for firewood.
2. The first storm of November falls on bare soil; raindrops splash soil particles loose.
3. Runoff carries the loose soil down the slope as sheet and rill erosion.
4. Runoff concentrates in a cattle track, which deepens into a gully.
5. The eroded soil reaches the river, which becomes shallow and sandy, and is deposited in a dam downstream.
6. The dam holds less water, so irrigation and water supplies fail sooner in the dry season.
:::

## Effects of land degradation

- **Physical:** loss of fertile topsoil, gullies, siltation of rivers, dams and wetlands, lower water tables, more flash floods, loss of habitats and biodiversity.
- **Economic:** lower crop yields and poorer grazing, higher fertiliser costs, loss of dam storage for irrigation and urban water, damage to roads and bridges by gullies and floods.
- **Social:** food insecurity, longer walks to collect firewood and water (often by women and girls), migration from degraded areas to towns, conflict over remaining good land.

:::example{title="Worked example: dam capacity lost to siltation (fictional data)"}
A small irrigation dam was built with a capacity of 2,400,000 m³. A survey 20 years later found that silt now fills 600,000 m³ of it.

- Percentage of capacity lost: 600,000 ÷ 2,400,000 × 100 = **25 %**.
- Mean rate of siltation: 600,000 ÷ 20 = **30,000 m³ per year**.
- If this rate continues, the remaining 1,800,000 m³ would fill in 1,800,000 ÷ 30,000 = **60 years**, unless erosion upstream is reduced.
:::

## Managing land degradation

| Method | How it works | Evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contour ridges and contour ploughing | Ridges and furrows follow the contours, slowing runoff and trapping soil | Cheap and effective on gentle slopes; ridges must be maintained or they break and form gullies |
| Terracing | Steep slopes are cut into level steps | Very effective on steep land, but needs a lot of labour to build and repair |
| Afforestation and woodlots | Trees bind soil, intercept rain and supply fuelwood | Takes years to grow; seedlings die without care and protection from livestock |
| Conservation agriculture (e.g. Pfumvudza/Intwasa) | Minimum tillage, planting basins, mulching with crop residues, crop rotation | Protects soil and holds moisture; needs mulch, labour and training |
| Controlled and rotational grazing | Limits stock numbers and rests paddocks so grass recovers | Sustainable if herders cooperate; difficult on communal land with no fences |
| Fireguards and fire laws | Cleared strips stop fires spreading; EMA fines for starting veld fires | Effective when guards are cut before the dry season; enforcement is difficult |
| Gully reclamation | Stone or brushwood check dams, gabions, grass planting slow water and trap silt | Works on small gullies; large gullies are expensive to repair |
| Stone lines and planting pits (Sahel) | Lines of stones along contours and small pits filled with manure trap water and soil | Low-cost, uses local materials and has helped restore degraded farmland in the Sahel |

![Side views of two soil conservation methods: contour ridges across a gentle slope slow the runoff and trap soil but must be maintained, while terraces cut a steep slope into level steps that are very effective but need much labour to build and repair.](media:diagrams/contour-ridges-terraces.png "Figure: contour ridges and terraces")

A **sustainable** method protects the land for the future, is affordable for local people, uses local materials and labour, and is supported by the community so that it continues after outside funding ends.

:::example{title="Desertification in the Sahel"}
The Sahel is the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara, stretching across Africa from Senegal to Sudan. Long droughts, such as those of the 1970s and 1980s, combined with rapid population growth, overgrazing, over-cultivation and fuelwood cutting, degraded large areas. Responses include stone lines and planting pits in Burkina Faso, letting tree stumps regrow on farmland (farmer-managed natural regeneration) in Niger, and the African Union's Great Green Wall initiative, launched in 2007, to restore land in a belt across the region. Local, low-cost methods have generally spread further than large tree-planting projects, where many seedlings have died.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying 'drought causes desertification' and stopping there"}
Desertification usually results from drought **and** human misuse together. Explain how each human activity removes vegetation or exposes soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a method without evaluating it"}
For "evaluate" or "assess", say how well the method works, what it costs, who must maintain it and whether it is sustainable.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land degradation is a long-term fall in the productivity of land: soil erosion (sheet, rill, gully), siltation, deforestation, veld fires, sand abstraction, wetland destruction and desertification.
- Main causes: overgrazing, over-cultivation, poor ploughing, deforestation for fields and fuel, fires and drought, all made worse by population pressure.
- Effects are physical (lost topsoil, silted dams), economic (lower yields, lost water storage) and social (food insecurity, migration).
- Management: contour ridges, terracing, afforestation, conservation agriculture, controlled grazing, fireguards, gully reclamation, stone lines.
- The most sustainable methods are cheap, use local materials and labour, and have community support.
:::
