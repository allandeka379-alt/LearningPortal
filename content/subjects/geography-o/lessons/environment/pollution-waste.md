## Introduction

Walk past a busy bus terminus in any Zimbabwean town and you will see the problem: plastic bags caught in fences, heaps of uncollected rubbish, smoke from burning waste, the roar of kombis and music from shops. In 2008 and 2009 a cholera outbreak spread through Harare, Chitungwiza and many other areas, causing about 4,000 deaths. Burst sewers, broken water supplies and uncollected refuse helped the disease spread. Pollution is not only an "environmental" issue: it decides whether people stay healthy, whether a town attracts investors and how much a council must spend.

This lesson covers the main kinds of pollution, their sources and effects, how they can be controlled, and how towns manage solid waste. River pollution as a case study is covered in its own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollution"}
The release of harmful substances or energy (such as noise or heat) into the environment faster than the environment can disperse, break down or absorb them, so that people, animals, plants or property are harmed.
:::

:::definition{term="Pollutant"}
Any substance or form of energy that causes pollution, for example sewage, smoke, plastic, pesticides or loud noise.
:::

:::definition{term="Solid waste"}
Unwanted solid material thrown away by households, shops, industry and institutions, such as food scraps, paper, plastics, glass, metal and building rubble.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanitary landfill"}
A planned waste-disposal site where waste is spread in layers, compacted and covered with soil each day, on a base lined to stop leachate reaching groundwater. An **open dump** has none of these controls.
:::

:::definition{term="Graffiti"}
Writing or drawings painted or scratched on walls, buses and other public surfaces without permission. It is a form of visual pollution.
:::

## Types of pollution, their sources and effects

| Type | Main sources | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Raw or partly treated sewage, industrial effluent, mine waste (mercury, acid, silt), fertiliser and pesticide run-off, leachate from dumps | Cholera, typhoid and diarrhoea; eutrophication and water hyacinth; fish deaths; higher water-treatment costs |
| Air | Coal-fired power stations and boilers, smelters, vehicle exhausts, burning refuse and tyres, veld fires, dust from roads and mines | Respiratory diseases (asthma, bronchitis); acid rain; smog; reduced visibility; greenhouse gases |
| Land | Litter and illegal dumping, plastics, mine dumps and tailings, overuse of agrochemicals, oil spills at garages | Ugly surroundings; blocked drains and flooding; livestock choke on plastics; poisoned soils; breeding sites for flies, rats and mosquitoes |
| Noise | Kombis and hooting, loudspeakers at shops, bars and churches, industry, aircraft, generators | Stress, loss of sleep, hearing damage, poor concentration in schools |
| Visual (graffiti) | Vandalism of walls, bridges and buses | Run-down appearance, lower property values, costs of repainting |

:::example{title="Diagram in words: how a blocked drain spreads disease"}
1. Plastic bags and bottles are dumped in a storm drain in a high-density suburb.
2. The drain blocks; when the first heavy rains fall in November, water floods the street.
3. A nearby sewer also overflows, and the floodwater mixes with sewage.
4. Children play in the water and families draw water from shallow wells that the floodwater has entered.
5. Bacteria such as those causing cholera and typhoid spread from person to person.

The chain shows why one form of pollution (land) can lead to another (water) and then to a health effect.
:::

## Controlling pollution

- **Laws and enforcement.** The Environmental Management Agency (EMA) issues licences for effluent and emissions, inspects factories and mines, and fines polluters ("the polluter pays"). Councils can fine people for littering and illegal dumping.
- **Treatment at source.** Sewage treatment works, effluent ponds at factories, dust filters and scrubbers on chimneys, and catalytic converters on vehicles.
- **Bans and regulations.** In 2017 Zimbabwe banned expanded polystyrene food containers (often called kaylite), which were a large part of litter at takeaways and bus stations. Noise limits and by-laws control loudspeakers and work at night.
- **Education and community action.** Clean-up campaigns, school environmental clubs and the monthly national clean-up day encourage people not to litter.
- **Planning.** Zoning keeps noisy, polluting industry away from homes and schools (see the lesson on EIA and land-use planning).

## Managing solid waste in Zimbabwean towns

Urban councils are responsible for collecting refuse and running disposal sites. Harare's main disposal site, Pomona, has long been an open dump rather than a properly engineered landfill. Many councils lack enough refuse trucks and fuel, so collection is irregular, and residents in high-density suburbs and informal settlements often dump or burn waste.

The **waste hierarchy** puts the most sustainable methods first:

1. **Reduce**: buy fewer packaged goods, carry reusable shopping bags.
2. **Reuse**: refill containers, repair items, use old tyres for building or planting.
3. **Recycle**: collect paper, cans, glass and plastics for processing. Many informal waste pickers earn a living by selling plastics and metal to recyclers.
4. **Recover energy or compost**: compost food and garden waste; biogas from organic waste.
5. **Dispose**: a sanitary landfill as the last choice.

:::example{title="Worked example: a waste survey (fictional data)"}
A Form 3 class weighed the rubbish thrown away by 20 households in one week.

| Type of waste | Mass (kg) |
| --- | --- |
| Food and garden waste | 96 |
| Plastics | 36 |
| Paper and card | 24 |
| Glass and metal | 18 |
| Other | 6 |
| **Total** | **180** |

- Percentage that is food and garden waste: 96 ÷ 180 × 100 = **53.3 %**.
- Mean per household per week: 180 ÷ 20 = **9 kg**.
- If food and garden waste were composted, the waste sent to the dump would fall by more than half. This is why composting is a key strategy for Zimbabwean councils, whose waste is mostly organic.
:::

## Effects of environmental deterioration

- **Social:** disease (cholera, typhoid, respiratory illness), loss of sleep and stress from noise, loss of pride in the neighbourhood, conflicts between residents and councils.
- **Economic:** higher costs of water treatment and health care, lost working days, lower tourism and property values, money spent on clean-ups and repainting instead of development.
- **Physical (environmental):** polluted rivers and dams, eutrophication, soil contamination, loss of wildlife, blocked drains and urban flooding, greenhouse gases from burning and rotting waste.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing pollution types without sources"}
"Air pollution causes asthma" is incomplete. Examiners want a source, a pollutant and an effect: "smoke from burning refuse at dumps releases particles that cause asthma and bronchitis."
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every dump a landfill"}
An open dump is uncontrolled. A **sanitary landfill** is lined, compacted and covered daily, and its leachate and gas are managed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting noise and graffiti"}
The ZIMSEC syllabus includes noise pollution and graffiti. Have one source, effect and control ready for each.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Paper 2, "describe the effects" questions often say "social, economic and physical". Organise your answer under those three headings and give at least one developed point for each.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Outline measures" answers score best when each measure is matched to a specific pollutant: "fines for factories that release untreated effluent into rivers" rather than "make laws".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pollution is the release of harmful substances or energy faster than the environment can absorb them: water, air, land, noise and visual (graffiti).
- Main sources in Zimbabwe: sewage and effluent, mining, vehicles and power stations, burning and dumping of refuse, loudspeakers and traffic.
- Effects are social (disease, stress), economic (treatment and health costs, lost tourism) and physical (eutrophication, contaminated soils, flooding).
- Control: laws enforced by EMA and councils, treatment at source, bans (polystyrene containers, 2017), education and zoning.
- The waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, recover or compost, and dispose in a sanitary landfill only as the last option.
:::
