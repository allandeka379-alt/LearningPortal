## Introduction

Imagine a company wants to open a quarry beside a river, upstream of a village and a school. Will the dust make children ill? Will blasting crack houses? Will silt reach the river the villagers drink from? Who decides, and on what evidence? An **environmental impact assessment (EIA)** is the process that answers these questions **before** work starts. **Land-use planning** goes further: it decides in advance which land should be used for homes, industry, farming, roads and conservation, so that conflicts and hazards are avoided in the first place.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Environmental management planning"}
Deciding in advance how human activities in an area will be organised and controlled so that resources are used sustainably and harm to people and the environment is avoided or reduced.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental impact assessment (EIA)"}
A study, carried out before a project is approved, that predicts its likely effects on the environment and people and proposes ways to avoid, reduce or make up for the harmful ones.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions taken to reduce harmful impacts, for example dust suppression, replanting, treating effluent or resettling and compensating affected people.
:::

:::definition{term="Land-use zoning"}
Dividing an area into zones, each reserved for particular uses (for example residential, industrial, commercial, agricultural, recreational or conservation) with rules on what may be built in each.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk-informed land-use planning"}
Planning that takes account of hazards such as floods, landslides and pollution, keeping homes and important services out of the most dangerous places.
:::

## The EIA process

In Zimbabwe the Environmental Management Act requires an EIA for the types of project listed in the Act, such as mines and quarries, dams, roads, large farms and irrigation schemes, factories, and waste-disposal sites. The developer pays for the study, and the Environmental Management Agency (EMA) reviews it. A project on the list may not start without EMA's approval.

1. **Screening**: decide whether the project needs a full EIA (is it on the list, and how large and sensitive is it?).
2. **Scoping**: identify the main issues to study (for example dust, water, wildlife, graves, relocation of households) and the people who will be affected.
3. **Baseline study**: record the present state of the environment (water quality, soils, vegetation, wildlife, population, land use) so that later change can be measured.
4. **Impact prediction**: predict positive and negative, short- and long-term impacts and how serious each will be.
5. **Mitigation and an environmental management plan**: propose measures to reduce each harmful impact, alternatives (such as a different site), and how the site will be rehabilitated.
6. **Public consultation**: affected communities, councils and traditional leaders are informed and give their views (this happens at several stages).
7. **EIA report**: the findings are written up and submitted to EMA.
8. **Review and decision**: EMA approves the project (usually with conditions) or rejects it.
9. **Monitoring and auditing**: once the project runs, it is checked to see that conditions are being met.

:::example{title="Diagram in words: an EIA for a fictional quarry"}
Imagine a cross-section from west to east: the village and school on high ground (west), a proposed quarry on a granite hill (centre), and a river with a dam downstream (east).

- **Impacts predicted:** dust carried by the prevailing wind onto the village and school; noise and vibration from blasting; silt washing from waste heaps into the river; a gravel road crossing grazing land; jobs for 60 local people (fictional figure).
- **Mitigation proposed:** blasting only between 12:00 and 14:00 with warnings; spraying roads with water; a belt of trees between the quarry and the school; silt traps between the waste heaps and the river; fencing old pits and backfilling them when work ends.
- **Decision:** approval with conditions, monitored twice a year.
:::

### Why EIA is important

- It prevents costly damage that would be expensive or impossible to repair later.
- It gives communities a voice and protects their health, water and livelihoods.
- It makes developers plan rehabilitation, which matters greatly in **mining**, where open pits, waste dumps and polluted water can last for decades.
- It supports sustainable development by balancing economic benefits with environmental protection.

**Weaknesses:** EIAs can be expensive and slow; reports are paid for by the developer and may play down impacts; consultation may be rushed; and many small-scale and illegal operations, such as artisanal gold mining, never carry out an EIA. Monitoring after approval is often weak.

## Land-use planning in Zimbabwe

- **Urban areas:** the **Regional, Town and Country Planning Act** (Chapter 29:12) requires local authorities to prepare **master plans** (long-term plans for a whole town) and **local plans** (detailed plans for parts of it). A **development permit** is needed before land is subdivided or its use changed. Plans zone land for residential, industrial, commercial and recreational use, with green belts and buffer zones between industry and homes.
- **Rural areas:** **rural district councils** (under the Rural District Councils Act) plan land use in communal and resettlement areas, deciding where villages, arable land and grazing should go and protecting wetlands, stream banks and woodland.

Land-use planning is a strategy for **sustainable environmental management** because it keeps polluting activities away from homes and water sources, protects wetlands and steep slopes, reduces the distances people travel, and keeps land available for food production.

:::example{title="Worked example: a fictional layout plan"}
A fictional 40 ha layout plan for a new suburb allocates land as follows.

| Land use | Area (ha) |
| --- | --- |
| Residential stands | 24 |
| Roads | 6 |
| Schools, clinic and shops | 4 |
| Open space and wetland buffer | 6 |

- Residential share: 24 ÷ 40 × 100 = **60 %**.
- If each residential stand measures 20 m by 30 m, it covers 600 m². One hectare is 10,000 m², so 24 ha = 240,000 m², enough for 240,000 ÷ 600 = **400 stands**.
:::

## Challenges of land-use planning and solutions

| Challenge | Suggested mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| Rapid urban growth and housing shortages lead to illegal settlements on wetlands and land reserved for other uses | Provide serviced, affordable stands in suitable places; enforce plans fairly |
| Plans become out of date and councils lack planners and funds | Train and employ more planners; use GIS to update plans |
| Political interference and corruption in allocating land | Transparent allocation and public consultation |
| Settlement in hazardous places (floodplains, steep slopes) | Risk-informed planning: hazard maps and no-build zones |

:::example{title="Case study: Cyclone Idai and risk-informed planning"}
In March 2019 Cyclone Idai brought extreme rain to Chimanimani and Chipinge districts. Floods and landslides swept down steep valleys, destroying homes built close to stream channels and on unstable slopes, and hundreds of people died. Rebuilding has focused on moving homes away from stream channels and steep slopes and on mapping hazard zones before land is allocated, which is risk-informed land-use planning.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating an EIA as a permit to pollute"}
An EIA does not approve damage. Its purpose is to avoid, reduce and make up for harm, and EMA can reject a project.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing the stages out of order"}
Screening and scoping come first, then the baseline study, prediction, mitigation and the report; monitoring comes after the decision.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Environmental management planning decides in advance how activities are organised to protect people and the environment.
- An EIA predicts a project's impacts before approval: screening, scoping, baseline study, prediction, mitigation and management plan, consultation, report, review and decision, monitoring.
- EIA is vital for mining, dams and roads, but it can be costly, biased or poorly monitored, and illegal operations avoid it.
- Land-use planning in Zimbabwe uses master and local plans (Regional, Town and Country Planning Act) and rural district councils to zone land.
- Challenges include rapid urban growth, corruption, shortages of planners and settlement in hazardous places; risk-informed planning keeps people out of danger zones.
:::
