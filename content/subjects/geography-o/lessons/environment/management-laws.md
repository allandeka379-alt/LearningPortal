## Introduction

Long before there were environmental laws in Zimbabwe, many communities protected certain forests, pools and hills as sacred places, and taboos stopped people from cutting fruit trees or killing animals out of season. Today the same aims (keeping land, water and air healthy so that they can support people in the future) are pursued through Acts of Parliament, government agencies and international treaties. This lesson looks at what the environment is, what managing it means, the laws and institutions Zimbabwe uses, and the international agreements it has signed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Environment"}
The surroundings in which people, animals and plants live: the land (rocks, soils, vegetation), water (rivers, lakes, groundwater) and air (the atmosphere), together with the human-made features in them.
:::

:::definition{term="Pristine environment"}
An environment that is still in its natural state, little changed by human activity, such as parts of Mana Pools or the remote Chimanimani mountains.
:::

:::definition{term="Degraded environment"}
An environment whose quality has been reduced by human activity, for example an eroded, overgrazed hillside, a polluted river or a mined-out area left with open pits.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental management"}
Planning and controlling the use of natural resources and human activities so that the environment is protected and resources are available for present and future generations (sustainable use).
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous knowledge systems (IKS)"}
The knowledge, beliefs and practices developed by local communities over many generations, such as taboos, totems and sacred sites, that guide how people use the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Domestication of a treaty"}
Turning the obligations of an international agreement into the country's own laws so that they can be enforced at home.
:::

## Aspects of the environment

| Aspect | Examples | Signs of degradation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Soils, vegetation, wildlife, minerals | Erosion, gullies, deforestation, open mine pits, litter |
| Water | Rivers, dams, wetlands, groundwater | Siltation, pollution, eutrophication, drained vleis |
| Air | The atmosphere, climate | Smoke, dust, greenhouse gases, acid rain |

## Indigenous knowledge in environmental management

- **Sacred sites:** forests, hills and pools where cutting trees, hunting or fishing was forbidden, so they acted as natural reserves.
- **Taboos:** for example forbidding the cutting of fruit trees, or forbidding the drawing of water from a spring with a dirty or blackened container, which protected springs from contamination.
- **Totems (mutupo/isibongo):** people do not kill or eat their totem animal, which gives some species protection.
- **Chiefs and spirit mediums:** traditional leaders enforced these rules and could fine offenders.

IKS is cheap, respected locally and fits local conditions. However, it is weakening as people move to towns, convert to new religions and face poverty, and it is not always recognised by modern law.

## Zimbabwe's environmental laws and institutions

| Law | Institution | Main responsibilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environmental Management Act (Chapter 20:27), 2002 | Environmental Management Agency (EMA) | Protects the environment as a whole; requires environmental impact assessments; licenses effluent, emissions and waste; controls veld fires, sand abstraction and wetlands; can fine polluters |
| Water Act (1998) and ZINWA Act (1998) | Zimbabwe National Water Authority (ZINWA) and catchment councils | Allocates water through permits, manages dams and water quality, and plans water use in the seven catchment areas (Gwayi, Manyame, Mazowe, Mzingwane, Runde, Sanyati, Save) |
| Parks and Wild Life Act | Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (ZimParks) | Manages national parks and other protected areas, controls hunting, and fights poaching |
| Forest Act | Forestry Commission | Protects state forests, licenses timber cutting and promotes tree planting |

Local authorities (urban and rural district councils) also make by-laws on refuse, noise and land use.

### How effective are the laws?

**Strengths:** a clear legal framework; EMA can stop projects that have not had an EIA; fines and licences apply the "polluter pays" principle; awareness campaigns on veld fires and littering reach schools and communities.

**Challenges:**

- Limited funding, vehicles and staff, so many areas are rarely inspected.
- Fines that are low compared with the profits of illegal activity, such as gold panning or sand abstraction.
- Poverty: people who depend on firewood, gold panning or streambank cultivation have few alternatives.
- Overlapping responsibilities between agencies and councils, and in some cases corruption.
- Weak public knowledge of the laws.

**Possible solutions:** higher and consistently enforced fines; more inspectors and community monitors; involving traditional leaders and IKS; providing alternative livelihoods; and education.

## International treaties and protocols

| Agreement | Year | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ramsar Convention | 1971 | Conservation and wise use of wetlands of international importance |
| CITES | 1973 | Controls international trade in endangered wild animals and plants |
| Montreal Protocol | 1987 | Phases out chemicals such as CFCs that destroy the ozone layer |
| Basel Convention | 1989 | Controls movements of hazardous waste between countries |
| Bamako Convention | 1991 | African agreement banning the import of hazardous waste into Africa |
| UNFCCC | 1992 | Framework for reducing greenhouse gases and adapting to climate change |
| Kyoto Protocol | 1997 | Binding emission targets for industrialised countries under the UNFCCC |

Zimbabwe also shares the Zambezi basin with its neighbours. The Zambezi River Authority, owned jointly by Zimbabwe and Zambia, manages the Kariba dam, and the basin states signed an agreement in 2004 to set up the Zambezi Watercourse Commission (ZAMCOM) to plan the shared use of the river.

:::example{title="Case study: Ramsar sites in Zimbabwe"}
Zimbabwe became a party to the Ramsar Convention in 2013 and listed wetlands such as Mana Pools on the Zambezi and Monavale Vlei in Harare. Listing brings international recognition and can attract support for conservation, but the wetlands still face pressure from cultivation, building and pollution, so listing only helps if local laws protecting wetlands are enforced.
:::

### Domesticating treaties: pros and cons

**Advantages:** access to international funding and technology; shared action on problems that cross borders (climate, ozone, shared rivers, wildlife trade); a better international reputation and cooperation; stronger national laws.

**Disadvantages:** the cost of meeting obligations (monitoring, reporting, new equipment); rules that may limit some economic activities, for example CITES restrictions on selling stockpiled ivory, which Zimbabwe has argued should be allowed; treaties drawn up mainly by richer countries may not fit local conditions; and weak enforcement can make them "paper" laws.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the institutions"}
EMA protects the environment generally; ZINWA manages water; ZimParks manages wildlife and parks; the Forestry Commission manages forests. Name the right one for the right job.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Basel and Bamako"}
Both deal with hazardous waste. Basel is a global agreement that controls movements of waste; Bamako is the African agreement that bans its import into Africa.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "assess the effectiveness of environmental legislation", give strengths **and** challenges, then a short judgement. One-sided answers rarely reach the top marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked about a treaty, give its name, what it controls, and one advantage and one disadvantage of Zimbabwe domesticating it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The environment has three aspects (land, water and air); pristine environments are little changed, degraded ones are damaged by human activity.
- Environmental management plans resource use sustainably; indigenous knowledge (sacred sites, taboos, totems) has long protected resources.
- Key laws and institutions: the Environmental Management Act and EMA; the Water Act, ZINWA and catchment councils; the Parks and Wild Life Act and ZimParks; the Forest Act and the Forestry Commission.
- Laws are weakened by limited funding, low fines, poverty, overlapping mandates and poor awareness.
- Treaties include Ramsar, CITES, Montreal, Basel, Bamako, UNFCCC and Kyoto; domesticating them brings funding and cooperation but also costs and restrictions.
:::
