## Introduction

In a good rainy season, granaries across rural Zimbabwe are full of maize, sorghum and groundnuts. After a drought, the same families may run out of food months before the next harvest. Whether a household eats enough depends not only on the weather but also on income, prices, roads, markets and government policy.

This lesson explains what food security is, what reduces food supply, why food insecurity matters, which strategies can increase food supply, whether food aid helps, and how Zimbabwe's small-scale farmers feed the nation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Food security"}
When all people, at all times, can obtain enough safe and nutritious food to lead an active and healthy life.
:::

:::definition{term="Food insecurity"}
When people do not have reliable access to enough affordable, nutritious food.
:::

:::definition{term="Famine"}
A severe, widespread shortage of food in an area, causing hunger, disease and deaths.
:::

:::definition{term="Food aid"}
Food, or money to buy food, given by governments, the UN World Food Programme or charities to people who do not have enough to eat.
:::

:::definition{term="Small-scale farmer"}
A farmer who works a small area of land, mainly with family labour, growing food for the household and selling any surplus. In Zimbabwe this includes communal-area, resettled and small-scale commercial farmers.
:::

## Factors that reduce food supply

| Natural factors | Human factors |
|---|---|
| **Drought** and unreliable rainfall | **Conflict**: crops destroyed, farmers displaced, trade blocked |
| **Floods** and tropical cyclones | **Poverty**: farmers cannot afford seed, fertiliser or irrigation |
| **Pests**: locusts, fall armyworm, quelea birds | **Rapid population growth** outpacing production |
| **Crop and animal diseases** | **Land degradation**: overgrazing, deforestation, soil erosion |
| **Infertile or eroded soils** | **Poor infrastructure**: roads, storage, markets; post-harvest losses |
| **Climate change**: higher temperatures, shorter seasons | **Economic and policy problems**: inflation, high input prices, low producer prices |
| | **Cash crops and biofuels** using land that could grow food |
| | **Disease in people** (for example HIV and AIDS) reducing farm labour |

:::example{title="Case study: Cyclone Idai, March 2019"}
Cyclone Idai brought extreme rain and floods to Chimanimani and Chipinge districts in eastern Zimbabwe. Landslides and floods destroyed homes, roads, crops, livestock and stored food, and cut off communities for days. It shows how a single natural hazard can turn a food-secure area into one that needs emergency help.
:::

## Problems of food insecurity at different levels of development

**In LICs:**

1. **Undernutrition** and deficiency diseases (kwashiorkor from lack of protein, marasmus from lack of energy, anaemia from lack of iron).
2. **Stunting** of children's growth and lower ability to learn at school.
3. Weak immune systems, so more illness and **higher child mortality**.
4. Adults too weak to work, so **production falls** further (a vicious circle).
5. Selling livestock and tools to buy food; **migration** to towns or other countries; sometimes conflict over resources.

**In MICs and HICs:**

1. **Food poverty**: low-income households cannot afford healthy food and may rely on food banks.
2. Cheap, energy-dense processed food leads to **obesity**, diabetes and heart disease.
3. Dependence on imported food makes countries vulnerable to world price rises.

## Strategies to increase food supply

| Strategy | How it helps | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Irrigation** (dams, boreholes, drip and canal schemes) | Allows crops in the dry season and in dry regions; reduces drought risk | Expensive; salinisation and waterlogging if poorly managed |
| **High-yielding and drought-tolerant varieties** (Green Revolution seeds, small grains) | More output per hectare; survive dry spells | Seed and fertiliser cost money each season |
| **Mechanisation** | Prepares land quickly and timely planting | Expensive; fuel; may reduce farm jobs |
| **Genetic modification** | Crops resistant to pests, disease or drought | Cost; concerns about safety, biodiversity and control by companies; not permitted in all countries |
| **Fertilisers and pesticides** | Raise yields; protect crops | Cost; water pollution; harm to beneficial insects |
| **Land reform** | Redistributes land to more farmers | Output depends on support, skills, inputs and secure tenure |
| **Support for small-scale farmers** (credit, extension, inputs, markets) | Raises productivity of the majority of farmers | Needs sustained funding and good organisation |
| **Conservation agriculture** (minimum tillage, mulching, planting basins) | Holds water and soil; higher yields in dry years | Labour-intensive at first |

## Evaluating food aid

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Saves lives quickly in emergencies (drought, floods, war) | Can create **dependency**, reducing the incentive to farm |
| Prevents people selling livestock and tools to survive | Free imported food can **lower prices** for local farmers |
| Food-for-work projects build dams, roads and terraces | May arrive late, be stolen, or not reach the most needy |
| Can be bought locally to support nearby farmers | **Tied aid** may have to be bought from the donor country |
| | Does not tackle the causes of food insecurity |

Food aid works best as **short-term emergency relief** combined with **long-term development** such as irrigation, better seed, storage and roads. Cash transfers or vouchers can let people buy food from local traders, which supports local markets.

## Small-scale farmers and food security in Zimbabwe

Small-scale farmers in communal and resettlement areas produce a large share of the country's staple foods.

1. **Household food:** they grow maize, sorghum, millet, groundnuts, beans and vegetables and keep cattle, goats and poultry.
2. **National supply:** surplus grain is sold to the Grain Marketing Board (GMB) and local traders, feeding towns and the strategic grain reserve.
3. **Drought resilience:** in drier natural regions IV and V, small grains such as sorghum and millet are more drought-tolerant than maize.
4. **Conservation agriculture:** the Pfumvudza/Intwasa programme (from 2020) supports farmers to plant in prepared basins with mulch to conserve moisture and soil.
5. **Cash crops** such as cotton and tobacco provide income to buy food.

Constraints include dependence on rainfall, small plots, shortage of draught power, high input costs, limited credit, poor roads and storage, and climate change.

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: food insecurity in Zimbabwe"}
1. **Background:** most rural households depend on rain-fed farming; maize is the staple food.
2. **Causes of insecurity:** recurring droughts, including the severe drought of 1991-92 and El Niño droughts such as those of 2015-16 and 2023-24; cyclones such as Idai (2019); economic instability raising input and food prices; land degradation; and the loss of farm labour to migration.
3. **Effects:** poor harvests, rising food prices, households cutting meals, child undernutrition and reliance on food aid in bad years.
4. **Responses:** government grain distribution through the GMB, input support for small-scale farmers, conservation agriculture, irrigation rehabilitation, promotion of small grains, and food assistance from the World Food Programme and NGOs in drought years.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating food security with growing more food"}
Food security is also about **access**: people need money, markets and roads to obtain food. A country can produce enough food while poor households still go hungry.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers on food aid"}
"Food aid is bad because it causes dependency" ignores lives saved in emergencies. Evaluate both sides and say when it works best.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section B"}
"Outline the contribution of small-scale farmers to food security" needs several points, each linked to food: household supply, surplus to the GMB, small grains in dry regions, conservation farming.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 10)"}
The extended question often asks for a detailed specific example of food insecurity in a named country. Give causes (natural and human), effects and responses with real detail, then evaluate how successful the responses have been.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Food security means everyone, at all times, can obtain enough safe, nutritious food.
- Natural factors (drought, floods, pests, disease, poor soils) and human factors (conflict, poverty, population growth, degradation, poor infrastructure) reduce food supply.
- Food insecurity causes undernutrition, stunting, disease and lost productivity in LICs, and food poverty and obesity in richer countries.
- Strategies include irrigation, better seeds, mechanisation, GM crops, fertilisers, land reform, support for small farmers and conservation agriculture.
- Food aid saves lives in emergencies but can cause dependency and lower local prices; Zimbabwe's small-scale farmers are central to national food security.
:::
