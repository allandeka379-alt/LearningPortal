## Introduction

The world produces enough food to feed everyone, yet hundreds of millions of people go hungry while hundreds of millions of others are overweight. A supermarket in Harare sells apples from South Africa and rice from Asia, while peas and roses grown near Harare are flown to Europe. Food is a global business.

This lesson describes where people eat too little or too much, why food production and diets are changing, how food supply can be increased, and what the globalisation of food means for farmers, shoppers and the environment.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Calorie intake"}
The amount of energy a person obtains from food each day, measured in kilocalories (kcal). Adults need very roughly 2,000 to 2,500 kcal a day (approximate guide values that vary with age, sex and activity).
:::

:::definition{term="Undernutrition"}
Not eating enough food, especially energy and protein, for good health.
:::

:::definition{term="Malnutrition"}
An unbalanced diet: too little of some nutrients (undernutrition, vitamin or mineral deficiency) or too much energy (leading to obesity).
:::

:::definition{term="Food miles"}
The distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
:::

:::definition{term="Agribusiness"}
Farming run as a large commercial business, often owned or controlled by large companies that also process, transport and sell the food.
:::

## Global patterns of calorie intake

When average daily calorie intake per person is shown on a world choropleth map, a clear pattern appears (all values approximate):

1. **High intake** (often above about 3,000 kcal): North America, Europe, Australia and some Middle Eastern and East Asian countries.
2. **Medium intake** (about 2,500–3,000 kcal): much of Latin America, North Africa and East and South-East Asia.
3. **Low intake** (below about 2,500 kcal, and in some cases close to 2,000): many countries of sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia, and countries affected by conflict.

### Reasons for the variations

| Factor | Effect on calorie intake |
|---|---|
| Income and development | Richer people can buy enough food and more processed, energy-rich food |
| Food production | Fertile soil, reliable rain or irrigation, machinery and fertiliser raise output |
| Climate and natural hazards | Drought, floods and pests cut harvests in vulnerable areas |
| Conflict | War destroys crops, blocks trade and forces farmers to flee |
| Population growth | Rapid growth can outpace food production |
| Infrastructure and trade | Poor roads and storage cause losses; imports can make up shortfalls if a country can pay |
| Culture and diet | Diets rich in meat, sugar and fat raise calorie intake |

:::example{title="Reading a table of calorie data (fictional data)"}
| Country | Daily calorie intake (kcal) |
|---|---|
| Country A | 3,450 |
| Country B | 2,180 |

1. Difference = 3,450 − 2,180 = **1,270 kcal**.
2. Country A is above the rough 2,500 kcal guide, Country B below it.
3. Country B's intake as a percentage of A's = 2,180 ÷ 3,450 × 100 = **63.2%** (to one decimal place).
:::

## Changes in global food production and consumption

### Why production has increased

1. **The Green Revolution** (from the 1960s): high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, with fertiliser and irrigation, greatly increased yields in Asia and Latin America.
2. **Mechanisation, irrigation and agrochemicals** raised output per hectare and per worker.
3. **Expansion of farmland** into forests and grasslands, for example for soya and cattle ranching.
4. **New technology:** genetically modified crops, hydroponics, better storage and refrigeration.
5. **Fish farming (aquaculture)** has grown as wild fish catches levelled off.

### Why consumption has changed

1. **Population growth** means more mouths to feed.
2. **Rising incomes** in MICs such as China, India and Brazil increase demand for meat, dairy, oil and processed foods. Producing meat needs much more grain and water than plant foods.
3. **Urbanisation:** city dwellers buy more packaged, processed and fast food.
4. **Global tastes:** foods such as pasta, rice, burgers and pizza are eaten worldwide.
5. **Waste:** a large share of food is lost in storage and transport in LICs, and thrown away by shops and households in HICs.

## Strategies to increase food supply

- Irrigation schemes, from large dams to small drip systems.
- High-yielding and drought-tolerant seed varieties, including GM crops where they are permitted.
- Fertilisers, pesticides and mechanisation.
- Land reform and support for small-scale farmers (credit, training, inputs).
- Better storage and roads to reduce post-harvest losses.
- New methods such as hydroponics and aquaculture.

The challenges of food supply, and these strategies, are examined in detail in the food-security lesson.

## The globalisation of food supplies

### Reasons

1. **Refrigerated transport and containerisation** allow fresh food to travel long distances; air freight carries high-value, perishable crops.
2. **Consumer demand** in HICs for a wide range of foods all year round, including tropical and out-of-season produce.
3. **Lower production costs** (labour, land, sunshine) in many LICs and MICs.
4. **Supermarkets and agribusiness TNCs** organise global supply chains.
5. **Trade liberalisation** has reduced tariffs on many food products.

:::example{title="Example: fresh vegetables and flowers from East and southern Africa"}
Kenya grows green beans, mange-tout peas and cut flowers for European supermarkets; Zimbabwe also exports horticultural produce such as fresh vegetables, flowers and citrus. Produce is picked, packed and chilled, then flown overnight to Europe, so it arrives fresh within a day or two.
:::

### Impacts

| Group | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Producers in LICs and MICs | Jobs and export earnings; new skills and technology | Low wages and seasonal work; dependence on a few foreign buyers; land and water used for exports rather than local food |
| Consumers | Wider choice; food all year round; sometimes lower prices | Loss of local and seasonal foods; more processed food and diet-related disease |
| Environment | Efficient large farms can raise output per hectare | Food miles and air freight add greenhouse gases; heavy water use in dry areas; deforestation for soya, palm oil and cattle; pesticide pollution |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming low calorie intake is caused only by climate"}
Drought matters, but poverty, conflict, poor infrastructure and population growth are often more important. Give a range of human and physical factors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying globalisation of food only harms LICs"}
Export horticulture provides jobs and foreign currency. Balance the benefits and the problems for each group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 10)"}
For a choropleth map or table of calorie intake, describe the pattern using continents and named countries, and quote values with units (kcal per person per day). "Explain the variations" needs reasons linked to income, production, conflict and climate.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Calorie intake is highest in HICs (often above about 3,000 kcal) and lowest in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- Variations reflect income, food production, climate and hazards, conflict, population growth and infrastructure.
- Production has risen through the Green Revolution, mechanisation, irrigation, new land and technology; diets are shifting towards meat and processed food.
- Food supplies are globalised by refrigerated transport, air freight, consumer demand, supermarkets and trade liberalisation.
- Impacts on producers, consumers and the environment are both positive and negative (jobs and choice versus low wages, food miles and water use).
:::
