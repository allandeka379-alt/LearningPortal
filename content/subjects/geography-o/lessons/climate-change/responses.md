## Introduction

There are two ways to deal with a leaking roof: fix the hole, or put buckets under the drips. Responses to climate change work the same way. **Mitigation** fixes the hole by cutting the greenhouse gases that cause warming. **Adaptation** puts out the buckets by helping people cope with changes that are already happening or cannot be avoided. Both are needed. A farmer in Chivi planting drought-tolerant sorghum is adapting; a country building solar farms instead of coal power stations is mitigating.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions that reduce the causes of climate change, by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or removing greenhouse gases from the atmosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
Actions that reduce the harm caused by climate change, or take advantage of new conditions, without tackling its causes.
:::

:::definition{term="Carbon sink"}
Something that absorbs and stores more carbon than it releases, such as a growing forest, soil or the oceans.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable energy"}
Energy from sources that are naturally replaced and do not run out, such as solar, wind, hydro-electric and geothermal power.
:::

## Mitigation techniques

| Technique | How it reduces warming | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Renewable energy (solar, wind, hydro) | Replaces coal, oil and gas, so less CO₂ is released | High start-up costs; solar and wind vary with weather; hydro depends on rainfall |
| Energy efficiency | Efficient bulbs, appliances, buildings and stoves use less energy | Needs money and awareness |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Growing trees absorb CO₂ (carbon sinks) and protect soils | Takes years; needs land; trees can be cut or burned |
| Carbon capture and storage | CO₂ from power stations or factories is captured and stored underground | Expensive; few working schemes; storage must not leak |
| Cleaner transport | Public transport, cycling, electric vehicles, efficient engines | Infrastructure costs; electricity may still come from coal |
| Less methane from farms and waste | Better livestock feed, capturing gas from landfills | Hard to apply on millions of small farms |
| Carbon taxes and trading | Make polluters pay, encouraging cleaner choices | Can raise prices; needs strong enforcement |

## Adaptation techniques

| Technique | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Water storage and harvesting | Dams, tanks and rainwater harvesting supply water in droughts |
| Drought-tolerant and early-maturing crops | Sorghum, millet and short-season maize varieties survive dry spells |
| Conservation agriculture | Minimum tillage, mulching and planting basins keep moisture in the soil |
| Irrigation | Drip and solar-powered irrigation reduce dependence on rain |
| Flood defences | Embankments, sea walls, raised houses, mangrove planting |
| Early warning systems | Forecasts and alerts give people time to act before droughts, storms and floods |
| Insurance | Crop and livestock insurance help farmers recover from bad seasons |
| Changing land use | Moving homes off floodplains and unstable slopes; switching from crops to livestock in dry areas |
| Health measures | Malaria control, heat-health warnings |

## International agreements

**UNFCCC (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change), 1992.** Agreed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro. Countries accepted that climate change is a serious problem and agreed to work together to stabilise greenhouse gas levels. Its members meet every year at a "COP" (Conference of the Parties).

**Kyoto Protocol, 1997** (in force from 2005). Set legally binding targets for richer, industrialised countries to cut emissions, on average by about 5 % below 1990 levels in its first period (2008-2012). It introduced carbon trading. **Weaknesses**: developing countries had no targets, and some large emitters (such as the USA) did not ratify it.

**Paris Agreement, 2015.** Almost every country agreed to keep the rise in global temperature **well below 2 °C** above pre-industrial levels and to try to limit it to **1.5 °C**. Each country sets its own targets (**nationally determined contributions**, NDCs) and is meant to strengthen them every five years. Richer countries promised finance to help poorer countries mitigate and adapt. **Weaknesses**: targets are voluntary, and the pledges made so far are not enough to meet the 1.5 °C goal.

## National strategies: Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe produces a very small share of world emissions but is highly vulnerable to drought and heat. Its responses include:

- A national climate change response strategy and climate policy, and NDCs submitted under the Paris Agreement.
- **Adaptation in farming**: promoting conservation agriculture (the Pfumvudza programme of planting basins and mulching), drought-tolerant small grains, small-scale irrigation and seasonal forecasts.
- **Mitigation**: encouraging solar energy (solar home systems and solar-powered irrigation pumps), planting trees and encouraging tobacco farmers to grow woodlots instead of cutting indigenous woodland for curing.
- **Disaster risk reduction**: early warning and civil protection planning, especially after Cyclone Idai.

:::example{title="Detailed specific example: Bangladesh"}
**Impacts**: a large share of Bangladesh's people live on the low delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra. Sea-level rise makes cyclone storm surges more dangerous, salt water spreads into rice fields and wells, and river floods are severe in the monsoon.

**Responses**:
1. **Cyclone shelters**: thousands of raised concrete shelters, often also used as schools.
2. **Early warning**: a large volunteer network spreads cyclone warnings by loudspeaker and radio, helping to evacuate people. Death tolls from cyclones have fallen greatly since the 1970s and 1990s.
3. **Embankments and mangroves**: earth embankments protect farmland; mangrove planting absorbs wave energy.
4. **Farming changes**: salt-tolerant rice varieties and "floating gardens" (rafts of water plants on which vegetables are grown) in flooded areas.

**Evaluation**: early warning and shelters have saved many lives at fairly low cost, but embankments need constant repair, and the country depends on other nations cutting emissions, since its own emissions are tiny.
:::

## Evaluating responses

| | Mitigation | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tackles | The causes | The effects |
| Scale | Needs global cooperation; one country alone has little effect | Works locally and quickly |
| Benefits | Long-term, for everyone | Immediate, for the community that adapts |
| Costs | High up-front costs; may slow some industries | Can be cheap (mulching) or very expensive (sea walls) |
| Limits | Warming continues for decades even if emissions fall | Some changes are too large to adapt to (e.g. small islands drowning) |

A sensible approach combines both: cutting emissions to limit future warming, while adapting to changes that are already locked in.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mitigation and adaptation"}
If an action reduces greenhouse gases, it is **mitigation**. If it helps people cope with the effects, it is **adaptation**. Planting trees is mitigation; growing drought-tolerant crops is adaptation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing agreements without evaluating them"}
For "evaluate", say what an agreement achieved and what limited it (binding or voluntary targets, who took part, whether pledges are enough).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting scale"}
State whether a response is local, national or international.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 often ends the climate change question with a 6-7 mark part on a detailed specific example of a named country or region. Give impacts and responses with place names and facts, then judge how effective the responses are.
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 may ask you to "identify ways of adapting to and mitigating climate change" in Zimbabwe. Give farm-level, community and national examples.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mitigation tackles causes: renewables, efficiency, afforestation, carbon capture, cleaner transport, carbon pricing.
- Adaptation copes with effects: water storage, drought-tolerant crops, conservation agriculture, irrigation, flood defences, early warning, insurance.
- UNFCCC (1992) began global cooperation; Kyoto (1997) set binding targets for rich countries; Paris (2015) set the well-below-2 °C goal with national pledges.
- Zimbabwe focuses mainly on adaptation (Pfumvudza, small grains, irrigation, forecasts) with some mitigation (solar, trees).
- Both approaches are needed; their success depends on cost, cooperation and scale.
:::
