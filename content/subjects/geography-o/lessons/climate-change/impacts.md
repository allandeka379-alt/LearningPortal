## Introduction

A rise of about 1 °C in the world's average temperature sounds small. But averages hide extremes: hotter heatwaves, heavier downpours, longer droughts and higher seas. For a maize farmer in Mwenezi, a fisherman at Kariba or a family on the low coast of Bangladesh, these changes already affect daily life. Impacts differ from place to place, and they are not all negative. This lesson works through them from the global to the local scale.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Impact"}
An effect of climate change on people, the economy or the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Sea-level rise"}
The increase in average sea level caused mainly by **thermal expansion** (water expands as it warms) and **melting ice on land** (glaciers and ice sheets).
:::

:::definition{term="Extreme weather event"}
Weather that is unusually severe for a place, such as a record heatwave, a very intense storm or a long drought.
:::

:::definition{term="Vulnerability"}
How likely a community is to be harmed by a hazard, which depends on its exposure, wealth, health and ability to cope.
:::

## Global impacts

| Area | Present and predicted impacts |
| --- | --- |
| **Temperature** | More frequent and intense heatwaves; fewer cold nights; warming fastest in the Arctic |
| **Sea level** | About 20 cm of rise since 1900 (approximate); further rise this century of tens of centimetres to around a metre depending on future emissions; flooding and erosion of low coasts, salt water entering farmland and wells |
| **Rainfall** | Wet regions tend to get wetter and many dry regions drier; heavier downpours; less predictable seasons |
| **Extreme events** | More intense droughts, floods and wildfires; the strongest tropical cyclones expected to become more intense |
| **Ice** | Shrinking Arctic sea ice and mountain glaciers; rivers fed by glacier melt (for example from the Himalayas) may first increase and later decrease in flow |
| **Ecosystems** | Coral bleaching in warmer seas; species moving towards the poles or uphill; some unable to move fast enough and at risk of extinction |
| **Food** | Lower yields of crops such as maize in hot regions; heat stress in livestock; some cooler regions may gain longer growing seasons |
| **Health** | Heat stress; malaria and other insect-borne diseases spreading to higher, previously cooler areas; more water-borne disease after floods |
| **Economy** | Damage to infrastructure, crops lost, higher insurance costs; tourism affected (less snow, bleached reefs, low waterfalls) |

:::example{title="Case study: sea-level rise in low-lying countries"}
Most of the land of the **Maldives** in the Indian Ocean is less than about 1 m above sea level, so rising seas threaten homes, fresh water and tourism. In **Bangladesh**, a large share of the people live on the low delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra; higher seas make storm surges from cyclones more dangerous and push salt water into rice fields and drinking-water wells.
:::

## Impacts in southern Africa and Zimbabwe

Southern Africa is expected to become **hotter** and, in many areas, **drier**, with more variable rainfall.

**Farming and food security**
- Higher temperatures and longer, more frequent dry spells reduce yields of rain-fed maize, the staple crop.
- Rainy seasons may start later and end earlier, shortening the growing season.
- Drier conditions may push the boundaries of the natural regions, so areas once suited to maize may become better suited to drought-tolerant small grains and livestock.
- Livestock suffer from heat, water shortages and poorer grazing.

**Water and energy**
- Lower river flows and higher evaporation reduce water stored in dams for towns and irrigation.
- In dry years the level of **Lake Kariba** falls, cutting hydro-electric power output, which has contributed to power cuts in Zimbabwe and Zambia.
- In drought years the flow over the **Victoria Falls** can be unusually low late in the dry season, affecting the view for tourists.

**Ecosystems and wildlife**
- Water holes dry up in parks such as Hwange, putting pressure on elephants and other animals.
- More frequent fires and changes in vegetation.

**Health and people**
- Heat stress in towns and the lowveld; malaria may spread to higher areas that were once too cool for mosquitoes.
- Floods after intense storms (as with Cyclone Idai, 2019) bring deaths and water-borne disease.
- Rural households lose income; some migrate to towns.

:::example{title="Working with a temperature projection (fictional figures)"}
A fictional district has a mean hot-season maximum of 31.5 °C. A model projects a rise of 0.4 °C per decade.

1. Rise over 5 decades = 0.4 × 5 = 2.0 °C.
2. Projected maximum = 31.5 + 2.0 = 33.5 °C.
3. Days above 35 °C would become more common, with more heat stress for crops, cattle and people.
:::

## Positive impacts?

Not all effects are harmful everywhere:
- Some cold regions (for example parts of Canada, Russia and northern Europe) may gain longer growing seasons and new crops.
- Melting Arctic sea ice opens shorter shipping routes and access to resources.
- Milder winters reduce heating costs and cold-related deaths in high latitudes.
- Some places may receive more rain, helping farming and reservoirs.

However, scientists judge that negative impacts outweigh positive ones overall, and that the poorest people, who have done least to cause the problem, are often the most vulnerable.

## Why impacts vary

| Factor | Effect on vulnerability |
| --- | --- |
| Wealth | Richer countries (HICs) can afford irrigation, sea walls, insurance and health care; LICs often cannot |
| Dependence on farming | Rain-fed farmers suffer most from droughts and changing seasons |
| Location | Low coasts, dry lands and small islands are most exposed |
| Governance and planning | Early warning, good land-use planning and emergency services reduce losses |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all places will get drier"}
Some places will get wetter and others drier. Many dry regions, including much of southern Africa, are expected to get drier, but the pattern varies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sea level rises only because sea ice melts"}
Floating sea ice does not raise sea level much when it melts. The rise comes from **thermal expansion** and **ice on land** (glaciers and ice sheets) melting into the sea.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing impacts without a scale or place"}
Say where: "lower maize yields in natural region IV", "salt water entering wells in coastal Bangladesh".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 asks for impacts "at a range of scales". Organise answers as global, regional (southern Africa) and national or local (Zimbabwe), and include present and predicted impacts.
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 may ask for the "advantages and disadvantages of climate change". Give at least one positive impact, and say where it applies.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Global impacts: hotter extremes, sea-level rise (thermal expansion and melting land ice), changing rainfall, more intense droughts, floods and storms.
- Effects on food, water, ecosystems (coral bleaching, species shifts), health (heat, malaria) and the economy.
- Zimbabwe: lower maize yields, shorter and less reliable seasons, low Kariba levels and power cuts, stress on wildlife, heat and disease.
- Some positive impacts in cold regions, but negative impacts dominate, especially for the poorest.
- Vulnerability depends on wealth, dependence on farming, location and planning.
:::
