## Introduction

Ask older farmers in Masvingo or Gokwe and many will say the rains are less dependable than when they were young and the hot season feels hotter. Measurements around the world tell a similar story: the planet is warmer now than at any time since accurate records began. The climate has always changed naturally, but scientists are confident that the rapid warming of the last century is mainly caused by people. This lesson looks at the evidence and at the natural and human causes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate change"}
A long-term change in the average climate of a place or of the whole Earth, lasting decades or longer, such as a rise in average temperature or a shift in rainfall patterns.
:::

:::definition{term="Global warming"}
The rise in the average temperature of the Earth's surface and lower atmosphere, especially since the late 1800s.
:::

:::definition{term="Greenhouse effect"}
The natural process by which gases in the atmosphere absorb heat given off by the Earth's surface and re-radiate some of it back, keeping the Earth much warmer than it would otherwise be.
:::

:::definition{term="Enhanced greenhouse effect"}
The extra warming caused by human activities adding more greenhouse gases to the atmosphere.
:::

## Evidence for climate change

| Evidence | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Temperature records** | Thermometer records since the mid-1800s show global average temperature has risen by roughly 1.1-1.2 °C; most of the warmest years on record have occurred since 2000 |
| **Ice cores** | Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic and Greenland ice show carbon dioxide levels and temperatures going back hundreds of thousands of years; CO₂ was about 280 parts per million (ppm) before industrialisation and is now over 400 ppm, higher than at any point in the ice-core record |
| **Arctic sea ice** | Satellites since 1979 show the area of summer sea ice in the Arctic has shrunk sharply |
| **Glaciers** | Most mountain glaciers are retreating; the ice on Mount Kilimanjaro has shrunk greatly since the early 1900s |
| **Sea level** | Global sea level has risen by about 20 cm since 1900, as water expands when it warms and ice on land melts |
| **Historical records** | Diaries, harvest dates, paintings and ships' logs describe past climates, for example the very cold winters of Europe's "Little Ice Age" (about 1300-1850) |
| **Natural records** | Tree rings, pollen in lake sediments and coral growth bands record past conditions |

:::example{title="Reading a temperature record (fictional data)"}
| Decade | Temperature compared with the 1951-1980 average (°C) |
| --- | --- |
| 1961-1970 | −0.04 |
| 1981-1990 | +0.18 |
| 2001-2010 | +0.55 |
| 2011-2020 | +0.82 |

1. Change from 1961-1970 to 2011-2020 = 0.82 − (−0.04) = 0.86 °C.
2. Over five decades that is about 0.86 ÷ 5 ≈ 0.17 °C per decade, and the rise is faster in the later decades.
:::

## Natural causes

**1. Orbital changes (Milankovitch cycles).** The Earth's orbit and tilt change slowly in regular cycles:
- the shape of the orbit changes from more circular to more elliptical (about every 100,000 years);
- the tilt of the Earth's axis varies (about every 41,000 years);
- the axis wobbles like a spinning top (about every 20,000-26,000 years).

These change how much sunlight reaches different latitudes and seasons and help to explain the cycle of ice ages and warmer interglacials over hundreds of thousands of years. They are far too slow to explain the warming of the last century.

**2. Sunspots and solar output.** Dark spots on the sun increase and decrease in a cycle of about 11 years. More sunspots go with slightly more energy from the sun. A long period with very few sunspots (about 1645-1715) happened during the cold Little Ice Age. Measurements show no upward trend in the sun's output over recent decades while temperatures have kept rising, so the sun cannot explain recent warming.

**3. Volcanic eruptions.** Large eruptions blast ash and sulphur dioxide high into the atmosphere, where tiny droplets reflect sunlight and **cool** the Earth for one to three years. After the eruption of Mount Pinatubo (Philippines, 1991) global temperatures fell for about two years. Eruptions also release CO₂, but far less than human activities each year.

## The natural greenhouse effect

**Diagram in words**:

1. Short-wave radiation from the sun passes through the atmosphere and heats the Earth's surface.
2. The warm surface gives off long-wave (infra-red) heat radiation.
3. Greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb much of this heat and send some back towards the surface.
4. This keeps the Earth's average temperature about 15 °C instead of about −18 °C. Without it, life as we know it could not exist.

## Human causes: the enhanced greenhouse effect

| Activity | Gas added | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) for power, industry and transport | Carbon dioxide | Coal-fired power stations such as Hwange, vehicles, factories |
| Deforestation | Carbon dioxide | Trees store carbon; burning or rotting releases it, and fewer trees remain to absorb CO₂ (clearing for tobacco curing, firewood and farmland in Zimbabwe) |
| Farming | Methane and nitrous oxide | Cattle digestion and rice paddies give off methane; nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide |
| Cement making | Carbon dioxide | Heating limestone releases CO₂ |
| Waste in landfills | Methane | Rotting rubbish without air |
| Refrigerants and some industrial gases | CFCs and similar gases | Very powerful heat-trapping gases |

More greenhouse gas means more heat is trapped, so the average temperature rises. The main human gas is carbon dioxide, but methane traps far more heat per molecule.

## People and local climates

People also change climate at a **local** scale:
- **Towns** form urban heat islands, warmer than the countryside.
- **Large dams** such as Kariba raise local humidity and moderate temperatures near the shore.
- **Deforestation** makes the local climate hotter by day and drier, with less transpiration.
- **Desertification**: overgrazing and clearing on dry margins leave bare soil that reflects more sunlight and holds less moisture, which can reduce local rainfall further.
- **Afforestation and irrigation** cool and moisten the local air.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the greenhouse effect is bad"}
The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth habitable. The problem is the **enhanced** greenhouse effect from extra gases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing climate change with the ozone hole"}
Ozone depletion (caused mainly by CFCs) lets in more ultraviolet light. It is a different problem from the build-up of greenhouse gases, although CFCs are also greenhouse gases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying volcanoes cause global warming"}
Big eruptions usually cause short-term **cooling** because their sulphur droplets reflect sunlight.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 1 often asks you to "explain how human activity causes the enhanced greenhouse effect". Link each activity to a named gas and to more heat being absorbed and re-radiated.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "describe the evidence", give the type of evidence, what it shows and, where possible, a figure or a place (Kilimanjaro glaciers, Arctic sea ice, ice cores).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Evidence: rising temperature records, ice cores, shrinking Arctic sea ice, retreating glaciers, rising sea level, historical and natural records.
- Natural causes: Milankovitch orbital cycles (very slow), sunspot cycles and volcanic eruptions (short-term cooling).
- The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth warm; human activities enhance it.
- Human causes: burning fossil fuels, deforestation, farming (methane, nitrous oxide), cement and landfill.
- People also change local climates through towns, dams, deforestation and desertification.
:::
