## Introduction

Is the river faster downstream? Are pedestrian counts higher on market day? Did the district's birth rate fall? Geographers answer questions like these with numbers, and simple statistics turn a page of raw data into a clear conclusion. Fieldwork reports, data-response questions and Cambridge Paper 4 all expect you to calculate averages and spreads, use percentages and rates correctly, and say how far the data can be trusted.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mean"}
The sum of all values divided by the number of values.
:::

:::definition{term="Median"}
The middle value when the data are placed in order. With an even number of values, it is the mean of the two middle values.
:::

:::definition{term="Mode"}
The value (or class) that occurs most often.
:::

:::definition{term="Range"}
The highest value minus the lowest value: a simple measure of spread.
:::

:::definition{term="Interquartile range (IQR)"}
The upper quartile minus the lower quartile: the spread of the middle half of the data, which is not affected by extreme values.
:::

## Averages and spread

:::worked-solution[Seven daily traffic counts at a junction (fictional data, vehicles per 10 minutes): 12, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22, 15]
1. Order the data: 12, 15, 15, 15, 18, 20, 22 (n = 7).
2. **Mean** = 117 ÷ 7 ≈ 16.7 vehicles.
3. **Median** = the 4th value = 15.
4. **Mode** = 15 (it occurs three times).
5. **Range** = 22 − 12 = 10.
6. **Quartiles**: in this course, the lower quartile (LQ) is the median of the values below the median, and the upper quartile (UQ) the median of the values above it. Lower half 12, 15, 15 → LQ = 15. Upper half 18, 20, 22 → UQ = 20.
7. **IQR** = 20 − 15 = 5.
:::

Which average is best? The **mean** uses every value but is pulled by extremes; the **median** is better when there are a few very large or small values (such as household incomes); the **mode** suits categories (most common type of shop). The **range** is easy but depends only on two values; the **IQR** describes the middle half and ignores outliers.

## Percentages, ratios, proportions and rates

- **Percentage** = part ÷ whole × 100.
- **Percentage change** = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A positive answer is an increase, a negative one a decrease.
- **Ratio** compares parts: 18 boys and 12 girls give 18 : 12, which simplifies to **3 : 2**.
- **Proportion** compares a part with the whole: 12 girls out of 30 pupils is 12/30 = 0.4 (40%).
- **Rates** compare events with the population, usually **per 1,000** (‰) for births and deaths, or **per km²** for density.

:::worked-solution[Percentage change and a rate (fictional district)]
1. A growth point's population rose from 4,500 to 5,400. Change = (5,400 − 4,500) ÷ 4,500 × 100 = **+20%**.
2. The district recorded 1,260 births in a population of 42,000. Birth rate = 1,260 ÷ 42,000 × 1,000 = **30 per 1,000**.
:::

## Significant figures and standard notation

Round to **significant figures** (s.f.) by counting from the first non-zero digit: 15,678 to 2 s.f. is 16,000; 0.004 56 to 2 s.f. is 0.0046. Keep the same number of significant figures as your least precise measurement; a mean river depth of 0.432857 m from a metre rule is better reported as 0.43 m.

**Standard notation (standard form)** writes very large or small numbers as a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of 10: 15,200,000 = 1.52 × 10⁷; 0.000 3 = 3 × 10⁻⁴.

## Relationships, interpolation and extrapolation

When two data sets are compared, describe the **direction** of the relationship (positive or negative), its **strength** (how closely the points follow a line) and any **anomalies**. A relationship is not proof of cause: two variables may both depend on a third.

- **Interpolation** estimates a value **between** measured points (for example the temperature at 13:00 from readings at 12:00 and 14:00). It is usually reliable.
- **Extrapolation** extends the trend **beyond** the data (for example predicting next year's population). It is risky because conditions may change.

## Anomalies and limitations of data

An **anomaly** is a value that does not fit the pattern. Check whether it is a recording error (a misread instrument, a slip in writing) or a genuine result with a geographical cause (a local obstacle, a special event). Do not simply delete it; explain it.

Data has limitations: **small samples** may not represent the whole; data from **one day or season** may be untypical; **instruments** have limited precision; **secondary data** may be out of date or collected differently; and **presentation** can mislead, for example by starting an axis above zero or using a pictogram with symbols of different sizes. Good conclusions say how reliable the data is.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to order the data"}
The median and quartiles come from ordered data. Sort first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the new value in percentage change"}
Always divide the change by the **original** value.
:::

:::mistake{title="Too many decimal places"}
A mean of 43.090909 from whole-millimetre measurements suggests false precision. Round sensibly (43.1 mm).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the formula and each step. In data-response and Paper 4 questions, a correct method earns marks even if the final figure slips.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Comment on the reliability" asks about sample size, timing, repeat readings, equipment and possible human error. Name specific problems, not "it might be wrong".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mean, median and mode are averages; range and IQR measure spread (IQR = UQ − LQ).
- Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; rates are usually per 1,000 people.
- Round to sensible significant figures; write very large or small numbers in standard form.
- Interpolation estimates within the data and is safer than extrapolation beyond it.
- Explain anomalies and state the limitations of the data before drawing conclusions.
:::
