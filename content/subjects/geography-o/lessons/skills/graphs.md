## Introduction

Geographers collect numbers: rainfall, traffic counts, crop yields, river velocities, census figures. A table of numbers hides its pattern; a well-chosen graph shows it at a glance. Every geography paper gives you graphs to read, and fieldwork questions ask you to choose, complete and describe them. Two skills matter equally: **picking the right graph for the data** and **describing what it shows** with figures.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Discrete data"}
Data in separate categories or whole counts, such as the number of shops of each type. Shown with bar graphs, pie charts and pictograms.
:::

:::definition{term="Continuous data"}
Data that can take any value in a range and changes smoothly, such as temperature over a day or distance along a transect. Shown with line graphs and histograms.
:::

:::definition{term="Correlation"}
A relationship between two variables. Positive: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls. None: no clear pattern.
:::

:::definition{term="Line of best fit"}
A straight line drawn through the middle of the points on a scatter graph, with roughly equal numbers of points on each side, to show the trend.
:::

## Bar graphs, pictograms and pie charts

- **Simple bar graphs** compare categories; bars are the same width with gaps between them.
- **Compound (stacked) bar graphs** divide each bar into parts, so you can compare totals and the parts that make them up.
- **Divided bar graphs** show one total as 100%, split into percentages.
- **Pictograms** use symbols (for example one cow = 100 cattle); eye-catching but hard to read precisely.
- **Pie charts** show the parts of a whole. Each sector angle = (value ÷ total) × 360°, or percentage × 3.6.

:::worked-solution[Pie chart of employment (fictional town)]
Agriculture 45%, industry 20%, services 35%.
1. Agriculture: 45 × 3.6 = 162°.
2. Industry: 20 × 3.6 = 72°.
3. Services: 35 × 3.6 = 126°.
4. Check: 162 + 72 + 126 = 360°. Draw the sectors clockwise from 12 o'clock, largest first, and add a key.
:::

## Line graphs and histograms

**Line graphs** show change over time or distance, with time on the horizontal (x) axis. Join the plotted points with straight lines, and use several lines on one graph to compare places. **Histograms** show the frequency of continuous data in classes (for example pebble sizes 0–9 mm, 10–19 mm...): the bars touch because there are no gaps between classes.

## Scatter graphs

Scatter graphs test whether two variables are related, for example distance from a town centre and land value. Put the **independent** variable (the one thought to cause change) on the x-axis. Draw a line of best fit and describe the correlation, then name any **anomalies** (points far from the line) and suggest reasons for them.

## Specialised geographical graphs

- **Triangular graphs** plot three components that add up to 100% (for example employment in primary, secondary and tertiary sectors, or the sand, silt and clay in a soil). Each side is a scale from 0 to 100%; read each value along the lines that run parallel to the matching side.
- **Radial graphs** plot values around a circle, for example by month or compass direction.
- **Rose charts (wind roses)** show how often the wind blows from each direction: the longer the bar, the more frequent that direction.
- **Kite graphs** show how the abundance of each plant species changes along a transect; the kite's width at each point is the percentage cover there.
- **Dispersion graphs** plot every value as a dot in a column, showing the spread, clusters and the median and quartiles.
- **Population pyramids** are horizontal bar graphs of age groups, males on the left and females on the right, usually as a percentage of the total population.
- **Proportional symbols** are circles, squares or bars drawn in size to the value at each place.
- **Flow diagrams** show stages in a process, such as the steps from a mine to an export port.
- **Systems diagrams** show inputs, processes and outputs, such as a farm system (inputs: land, labour, seed; processes: ploughing, weeding; outputs: crops, income).

## Constructing a good graph

1. Choose the type that suits the data (categories, parts of a whole, change over time, relationship).
2. Give a **title**, label both **axes** with units, and choose an even scale that uses most of the space.
3. Plot accurately in pencil; use a key where there is more than one data set.
4. For bar graphs leave equal gaps; for histograms let the bars touch.

## Describing graphs: trend, evidence, anomaly

A full description has three parts:

1. **Trend**: the overall pattern ("rainfall increases from January to a peak in...", "as distance increases, land value falls").
2. **Evidence**: quote figures from the graph with units (highest, lowest, range, rate of change).
3. **Anomaly**: any exception to the trend, with its value.

:::example{title="Describing a scatter graph"}
"There is a negative correlation between distance from the CBD and land value: values fall from about US\$ 900 per m² at 0.5 km to about US\$ 100 per m² at 5 km (fictional survey). One site at 3 km has an unusually high value of US\$ 600 per m², perhaps because it lies on a main road."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Wrong graph for the data"}
A line graph joining unrelated categories (maize, tobacco, cotton) suggests a trend that does not exist; use bars.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing without numbers"}
"It goes up then down" earns little. Give values, dates and units.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forcing the best-fit line through the origin"}
A best-fit line follows the points; it does not have to start at zero.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Complete the graph" questions usually carry one mark per bar or sector plotted correctly, so use a ruler and protractor and match the existing shading.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to "suggest a suitable graph", name it and give a reason linked to the data (for example "a pie chart, because the data are parts of a whole").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bars, pictograms and pies suit categories and parts of a whole; line graphs and histograms suit continuous data.
- Pie sector angle = value ÷ total × 360° (or percentage × 3.6).
- Scatter graphs with a best-fit line show positive, negative or no correlation and reveal anomalies.
- Triangular graphs show three parts summing to 100%; rose charts show wind direction; kite graphs show species along transects.
- Describe graphs with the trend, figures as evidence, and any anomaly.
:::
