## Introduction

How many classrooms will a district need in ten years? How many nurses should a new clinic employ? Where should a borehole be drilled? Every one of these decisions depends on population data. Zimbabwe counts its people in a national **census** every ten years, and clinics, schools and registry offices collect information all the time.

This lesson defines the key population terms, shows how to calculate the main rates, and explains where population data comes from and how to present and interpret it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Crude birth rate (CBR)"}
The number of live births per 1,000 people in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Crude death rate (CDR)"}
The number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural increase"}
The difference between the birth rate and the death rate. If deaths exceed births it is a **natural decrease**.
:::

:::definition{term="Infant mortality rate (IMR)"}
The number of babies who die before their first birthday per 1,000 live births in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Total fertility rate (TFR)"}
The average number of children a woman would have during her reproductive years (about 15-49) if current birth rates continued. A TFR of about 2.1 is the **replacement level**.
:::

:::definition{term="Life expectancy"}
The average number of years a newborn baby can expect to live if current death rates continue.
:::

:::definition{term="Net migration rate"}
Immigrants minus emigrants per 1,000 people in a year.
:::

## Calculating population rates

The rates use "per 1,000" (the symbol ‰ means per thousand).

:::example{title="Worked example: a fictional district"}
A fictional district has 250,000 people. In one year there were 8,750 live births, 2,500 deaths, 420 infant deaths (under one year), 1,200 immigrants and 3,200 emigrants.

1. **Birth rate** = 8,750 ÷ 250,000 × 1,000 = **35 per 1,000**
2. **Death rate** = 2,500 ÷ 250,000 × 1,000 = **10 per 1,000**
3. **Natural increase** = 35 − 10 = **25 per 1,000**, which is **2.5%** a year (divide by 10 to change per 1,000 into a percentage).
4. **Infant mortality rate** = 420 ÷ 8,750 × 1,000 = **48 per 1,000 live births** (note: divide by *live births*, not by the whole population).
5. **Net migration** = 1,200 − 3,200 = −2,000 people, so the **net migration rate** = −2,000 ÷ 250,000 × 1,000 = **−8 per 1,000**.
6. **Population change** = natural increase + net migration = (8,750 − 2,500) + (−2,000) = **+4,250 people**.
:::

### Doubling time

A quick estimate of how long a population takes to double is **70 ÷ growth rate (%)**. At 2.5% a year: 70 ÷ 2.5 = **28 years**.

### Population density

**Density = number of people ÷ area (km²)**, measured in people per km². This is covered in detail in the lesson on population distribution and density.

## Sources of population data

| Source | What it is | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **National census** | A count of every person on a set night, with questions on age, sex, education, work, housing and migration. Zimbabwe holds one every ten years (1982, 1992, 2002, 2012, 2022), run by the Zimbabwe National Statistics Agency (ZimStat) | Complete coverage; detailed data for small areas; allows comparison over time | Very expensive; only every ten years; undercounting of mobile, homeless or remote people; errors in age reporting |
| **Vital registration** | Official recording of births, deaths and marriages at registry offices | Continuous; legal records | Many births and deaths, especially rural ones, go unregistered in LICs |
| **Sample surveys** | Questionnaires to a sample of households (for example demographic and health surveys) | Cheaper and quicker; can ask detailed health and fertility questions | Results are estimates; sampling errors |
| **Local and school surveys** | A school census or community survey (number of pupils by age and sex, family size, birthplace) | Easy to carry out; practises fieldwork skills | Small samples; may not represent the wider population |

### Why censuses are difficult in LICs

1. **Cost** and shortage of trained enumerators.
2. **Remote and inaccessible areas** with poor roads, and seasonal flooding.
3. **Mobile people**: migrants, cross-border traders, nomadic herders, the homeless.
4. **Mistrust**: people may fear the census is for tax or political reasons.
5. **Inaccurate ages** where birth records are missing.
6. **Language and literacy** barriers.

## Presenting and interpreting population data

- **Population pyramids** (age-sex bar charts) show structure (see the population structure lesson).
- **Line graphs** show change over time (birth and death rates, total population).
- **Bar charts** compare places or groups (for example family size in different villages).
- **Pie charts** show proportions (for example the share of urban and rural population).
- **Choropleth and dot maps** show distribution and density.

:::example{title="Collecting data: a school census"}
A Form 3 class records, for every learner, age, sex, number of siblings and where their parents were born.

1. **Tally** the results and calculate totals and averages (mean family size).
2. **Present** family size with a bar chart and birthplaces with a pie chart.
3. **Interpret:** for example "Learners from rural homes have a mean of 4.2 siblings, compared with 2.6 for learners from town homes" (fictional results), and suggest reasons (cost of raising children in town, education of parents, access to family planning).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the whole population for infant mortality"}
IMR = infant deaths ÷ **live births** × 1,000, not ÷ total population.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing per 1,000 with per cent"}
A natural increase of 25 per 1,000 is 2.5%, not 25%. Divide by 10 to convert.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
Calculation parts (birth rate, natural increase, IMR) carry marks for working. Write the formula, substitute, and give the unit ("per 1,000"). "Describe the problems of conducting a census" needs specific reasons, not just "it is difficult".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 6)"}
Explain population change with all its components: births, deaths and migration. "Natural increase" ignores migration; "population growth" includes it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Birth and death rates are per 1,000 people per year; natural increase = birth rate − death rate (divide by 10 for %).
- IMR = infant deaths per 1,000 live births; TFR = average children per woman (replacement about 2.1); life expectancy = average expected lifespan.
- Population change = natural increase + net migration; doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (%).
- Data comes from censuses (Zimbabwe: every ten years, by ZimStat), vital registration, sample surveys and local surveys, each with strengths and weaknesses.
- Present data with pyramids, line graphs, bar and pie charts and maps, then describe patterns with figures and explain them.
:::
