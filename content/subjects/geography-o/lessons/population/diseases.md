## Introduction

Disease shapes populations. It lowers life expectancy, raises infant mortality, keeps workers away from fields and factories and costs families and governments money. In Zimbabwe, malaria in the hot low-lying valleys, the cholera outbreak of 2008-2009 and the HIV and AIDS epidemic have all affected the country's population. In rich countries the main killers are different: heart disease, strokes, diabetes and cancers linked to lifestyle and old age. Geographers ask **why** diseases differ from place to place and **how** they affect people and development.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water-borne disease"}
A disease spread by drinking or using water contaminated with germs, usually from human waste (for example cholera, typhoid, dysentery).
:::

:::definition{term="Vector-borne disease"}
A disease carried from one person or animal to another by a living carrier (a **vector**) such as a mosquito or tsetse fly.
:::

:::definition{term="Nutritional (deficiency) disease"}
A disease caused by a diet lacking enough energy, protein, vitamins or minerals (for example kwashiorkor, marasmus, pellagra).
:::

:::definition{term="Lifestyle (non-communicable) disease"}
A disease that is not passed from person to person and is linked to diet, lack of exercise, smoking, alcohol and ageing (for example heart disease and type 2 diabetes).
:::

:::definition{term="Epidemic"}
A rapid spread of a disease affecting many people in an area at the same time.
:::

## Diseases common in developing countries

| Group | Disease | Cause and spread | Where / when common |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water-borne | Cholera | Bacteria in water or food contaminated by faeces; severe diarrhoea and dehydration | Overcrowded areas without safe water or sewers; rainy season |
| Water-borne | Typhoid, dysentery | Contaminated water and food | Poor sanitation |
| Water-related | Bilharzia (schistosomiasis) | Worm larvae released by water snails enter skin in slow-moving water | Dams, irrigation canals, rivers |
| Vector-borne | Malaria | Parasite carried by the female *Anopheles* mosquito | Hot, wet, low-lying areas with stagnant water |
| Vector-borne | Sleeping sickness (trypanosomiasis) | Parasite carried by the tsetse fly | Bush areas with tsetse, e.g. parts of the Zambezi valley |
| Nutritional | Kwashiorkor; marasmus | Lack of protein (kwashiorkor) or of food energy overall (marasmus) | Young children in poor or drought-hit households |
| Nutritional | Pellagra | Lack of niacin (a B vitamin), linked to diets based almost only on maize | Poor households eating little else |
| Viral, sexually transmitted | HIV and AIDS | Virus spread by unprotected sex, infected blood, and from mother to child | Worldwide; highest rates in southern Africa |
| Bacterial, airborne | Tuberculosis (TB) | Spread by coughing in crowded, poorly ventilated places; strongly linked to HIV | Crowded housing |

:::example{title="Case study: malaria in Zimbabwe"}
1. **Where:** malaria risk is highest in hot, low-lying areas such as the Zambezi valley, the Lowveld and the eastern and northern border areas, and lowest on the cooler highveld around Harare.
2. **Why:** *Anopheles* mosquitoes breed in stagnant water and the parasite develops faster at high temperatures, so risk peaks in the hot wet season (about November to April).
3. **Control:** indoor residual spraying of homes, insecticide-treated bed nets, draining stagnant water, rapid diagnostic testing and treatment at clinics.
:::

:::example{title="Case study: the 2008-2009 cholera outbreak in Zimbabwe"}
1. **Scale:** a major epidemic with roughly 100,000 cases and more than 4,000 deaths (approximate figures).
2. **Causes:** breakdown of water treatment and sewage systems in towns such as Harare, burst sewers, uncollected refuse and shortages of treatment chemicals during a severe economic crisis; people used shallow wells and unsafe water.
3. **Lessons:** safe piped water, working sewers and refuse collection prevent cholera better than treatment after an outbreak.
:::

## HIV and AIDS

HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) weakens the immune system; without treatment it leads to AIDS, when other infections such as TB become deadly. In Zimbabwe adult HIV prevalence was among the highest in the world in the late 1990s, at well over a fifth of adults (approximate), and has since fallen to roughly one adult in ten (approximate), helped by behaviour change, condoms, testing and the wide use of **antiretroviral therapy (ART)**.

## Diseases common in developed countries

| Disease | Main causes |
|---|---|
| Heart disease and strokes | Diets high in fat, salt and sugar; lack of exercise; smoking; stress; ageing |
| Type 2 diabetes | Obesity and inactivity |
| Cancers (lung, bowel and others) | Smoking, diet, alcohol, ageing, pollution |
| Dementia | Ageing populations |

Lifestyle diseases are now also rising fast in towns in developing countries, including Zimbabwe, as diets change and people exercise less.

## Why disease patterns differ

| Factor | Developing countries | Developed countries |
|---|---|---|
| Water and sanitation | Many lack safe water and sewers → water-borne disease | Treated water and sewers almost everywhere |
| Climate | Many lie in the tropics, where mosquitoes and tsetse thrive | Mostly temperate; cold winters limit vectors |
| Wealth and diet | Poverty, drought → undernutrition | Plenty of food, often too much fat and sugar → obesity |
| Health care | Few doctors, distant clinics, drug shortages | Many doctors, vaccines, screening |
| Age structure | Youthful populations; infectious diseases kill the young | Ageing populations; diseases of old age dominate |
| Education | Less awareness of hygiene and prevention | High awareness, but unhealthy habits persist |

## Social and economic effects of disease

| Social effects | Economic effects |
|---|---|
| Lower life expectancy; higher infant mortality | Workers absent or less productive; lower farm output |
| Orphans, often cared for by grandparents; child-headed households | Families spend savings on treatment and funerals |
| Children (often girls) leave school to care for the sick | Government spends heavily on health care |
| Stigma and grief | Loss of skilled workers (teachers, nurses) |
| Pressure on clinics and hospitals | Tourism and investment may fall during epidemics |

:::example{title="Worked example: effect of a disease on a workforce (fictional data)"}
A fictional farm employs 250 workers. During the malaria season 35 workers are off sick for an average of 6 days each.

1. **Working days lost** = 35 × 6 = **210 days**.
2. **Percentage of workers affected** = 35 ÷ 250 × 100 = **14%**.
3. If each worker produces goods worth US\$12 a day, the farm loses 210 × 12 = **US\$2,520** in output, which explains why employers often pay for bed nets and spraying.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling malaria water-borne"}
Malaria is **vector-borne**: the mosquito carries it. Water matters only because mosquitoes breed in it. Cholera and typhoid are water-borne because you catch them from the water itself.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying developed countries have no disease problems"}
They have fewer infectious-disease deaths but many deaths from heart disease, cancers and other lifestyle diseases, and care for many elderly people.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"Explain the differences in diseases between developed and developing countries" needs factors (sanitation, climate, wealth, health care, age structure) linked to named diseases on both sides. "Explain the social and economic effects" needs both types, with examples such as HIV and AIDS or cholera in Zimbabwe.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Developing countries: water-borne (cholera, typhoid, dysentery), water-related (bilharzia), vector-borne (malaria, sleeping sickness), nutritional (kwashiorkor, marasmus, pellagra), HIV and AIDS and TB.
- Developed countries: lifestyle and old-age diseases such as heart disease, strokes, type 2 diabetes, cancers and dementia.
- Differences come from sanitation, climate, wealth and diet, health care, education and age structure.
- Zimbabwe: malaria in hot low-lying areas; the 2008-2009 cholera epidemic linked to failing water and sewage systems; HIV prevalence high but falling with ART.
- Disease lowers life expectancy, creates orphans, reduces productivity and costs families and governments money.
:::
