## Introduction

Almost every Zimbabwean family has someone who has moved: a cousin who left the village for a job in Harare, an uncle working in Johannesburg, a nurse in the United Kingdom, a grandparent who retired back to the rural home. Migration changes the size and structure of populations as much as births and deaths do, and it reshapes the places people leave and the places they go to.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Migration"}
The movement of people from one place to another to live, usually for at least a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal migration"}
Migration within one country, for example from a communal area to Harare.
:::

:::definition{term="International migration"}
Migration across a national border, for example from Zimbabwe to South Africa.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic migrant"}
A person who moves voluntarily, usually to find work or earn a higher income.
:::

:::definition{term="Refugee"}
A person forced to flee their country because of war, violence or persecution, who cannot safely return.
:::

:::definition{term="Asylum seeker"}
A person who has fled to another country and applied for protection as a refugee, but whose claim has not yet been decided.
:::

:::definition{term="Internally displaced person (IDP)"}
A person forced to leave their home who stays within their own country, for example after a flood or cyclone.
:::

## Categories of migration

| Basis | Categories | Zimbabwean examples |
|---|---|---|
| Distance / border | Internal; international | Gokwe to Harare; Masvingo to Johannesburg |
| Direction (internal) | Rural-urban; urban-rural; urban-urban; rural-rural | School leaver to Bulawayo; retiree back to the rural home; Gweru to Harare for promotion; families moving to resettlement or gold-panning areas |
| Choice | Voluntary; forced (involuntary) | Job seekers; people displaced by Cyclone Idai (2019) |
| Time | Permanent; temporary or seasonal; daily commuting | Settling abroad; seasonal farm work; travelling from Chitungwiza to Harare each day |

:::example{title="Example of forced migration in Zimbabwe's history"}
When Lake Kariba filled after the dam was completed in 1959, tens of thousands of Tonga people living in the Zambezi valley were moved away from the river to make way for the lake. This was forced, internal and permanent migration; Tonga communities on the Zambian side of the river were moved too.
:::

## Causes: push and pull factors

People move because of **push factors** (things that make them want to leave) and **pull factors** (things that attract them to a destination). **Intervening obstacles** such as cost, distance, borders and visas can stop them.

| | Push factors (origin) | Pull factors (destination) |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low wages, poor harvests, land shortage | Jobs, higher wages, chance to send money home |
| Social | Few schools, clinics or services | Better education, health care, entertainment |
| Environmental | Drought, floods, cyclones, soil erosion | Reliable water, safer environment |
| Political | War, violence, persecution | Peace, safety, freedom |

:::example{title="Diagram in words: Lee's model of migration"}
Imagine two circles, an **origin** on the left and a **destination** on the right. Each circle contains plus signs (attractions) and minus signs (things people dislike). Between them is a wall of **intervening obstacles**: transport cost, distance, border controls. A person moves only if the pull of the destination and the push of the origin are strong enough to overcome the obstacles.
:::

## Impacts of migration

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Migrants** | Higher income; skills; education for children; safety (refugees) | Exploitation, poor housing, discrimination or xenophobia; separation from family |
| **Origin (sending area)** | **Remittances** pay school fees and build houses; less pressure on land and jobs; returning migrants bring skills and savings | Loss of young, skilled workers (**brain drain**), e.g. nurses and teachers; ageing and female-headed rural households; farm labour shortages |
| **Destination (receiving area)** | Labour for farms, mines, construction, care; skills; new businesses; cultural variety | Pressure on housing and services; competition for jobs; possible tension between groups; growth of unplanned settlements in cities |

Rural-urban migration also changes population structure: towns gain young adults, while rural areas keep more children, women and older people.

## Case study: migration from Zimbabwe to South Africa

1. **Type:** mainly voluntary international economic migration, with some people seeking asylum. Much of it crosses the Limpopo at **Beitbridge**, the main border post.
2. **Push factors:** economic decline after about 2000, very high unemployment, hyperinflation in 2007-2008, falling real wages, shortages of goods and services, and droughts that hit rural incomes.
3. **Pull factors:** South Africa is next door, is the largest economy in southern Africa, has higher wages, and offers jobs in construction, farming, mining, retail, hospitality and domestic work; family networks already there help newcomers.
4. **Impacts on Zimbabwe:** remittances sent in cash, by mobile money and as goods (groceries, building materials, cars carried by cross-border transporters) support many households; loss of skilled professionals such as teachers, nurses and doctors (many also went to the UK); some rural areas lost young men.
5. **Impacts on South Africa:** extra labour and skills, and new small businesses; but also competition for low-paid jobs and outbreaks of **xenophobic violence** against foreign nationals (notably in 2008 and 2015).
6. **Management:** South Africa introduced special permit schemes for Zimbabweans from about 2010, allowing many to live and work legally; border controls and deportations of undocumented migrants; both governments work through the Southern African Development Community (SADC).

## Managing international migration

| Strategy | How it works | Evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Points-based or skills-based visas | Admit people with needed skills | Fills skill gaps, but drains skills from origin countries |
| Work permits and amnesties | Give legal status to migrants already present | Reduces exploitation; may be politically unpopular |
| Border controls and deportation | Stop irregular entry | Costly; pushes migrants to dangerous routes |
| Refugee camps and settlement (e.g. Tongogara camp in Chipinge district) | Shelter and food for refugees | Life-saving, but camps can last for years with few jobs |
| Aid and investment in origin countries | Reduce push factors | Slow; works only with good governance |
| Integration programmes | Language classes, recognition of qualifications | Help migrants contribute; need funding |

:::example{title="Worked example: net migration (fictional data)"}
A fictional district had 2,400 in-migrants and 3,150 out-migrants in one year, and a population of 150,000.

1. **Net migration** = in-migrants − out-migrants = 2,400 − 3,150 = **−750** (a net loss).
2. **Net migration rate** = −750 ÷ 150,000 × 1,000 = **−5 per 1,000**.
3. The district is a net **sending** area, typical of a rural district losing young people to towns.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every migrant a refugee"}
A refugee is forced to flee war or persecution. A person who moves for a better job is an economic migrant, even if life at home is hard. An asylum seeker is waiting for a decision on refugee status.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing impacts without saying where"}
Always say whether an impact affects the **migrant**, the **origin** or the **destination**, and whether it is positive or negative.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"Describe the causes of migration" needs both push and pull factors. "Explain the effects of migration" needs effects on both sending and receiving areas, ideally with Zimbabwean examples.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 6)"}
Learn one **detailed specific example** of an international migration with named places, causes, impacts on both countries and how it is managed. "Evaluate strategies" needs a judgement on which works best and why.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Migration can be internal or international, voluntary or forced, permanent or temporary; internal flows are rural-urban, urban-rural, urban-urban or rural-rural.
- Economic migrants choose to move for work; refugees are forced to flee war or persecution; asylum seekers await a decision on refugee status.
- Push factors (unemployment, drought, conflict) and pull factors (jobs, services, safety) cause migration; intervening obstacles may stop it.
- Impacts: remittances and less pressure at the origin but brain drain; labour and skills at the destination but pressure on services and possible tension.
- Zimbabwe to South Africa: economic push factors after about 2000, pull of jobs next door; remittances, brain drain, xenophobia; managed by permits, border controls and SADC.
:::
