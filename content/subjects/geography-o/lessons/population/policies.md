## Introduction

Some governments worry that their populations are growing too fast for schools, jobs and food to keep up. Others worry that too few babies are being born to replace an ageing workforce. Both try to influence how many children families have, through **population policies**. Some of these policies have been gentle and voluntary; others have been strict and controversial.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population policy"}
A set of measures by a government to influence the size, growth, structure or distribution of its population.
:::

:::definition{term="Anti-natalist policy"}
A policy that aims to **reduce** the birth rate, for example by providing family planning or limiting family size.
:::

:::definition{term="Pro-natalist policy"}
A policy that aims to **increase** the birth rate, for example by paying families to have children.
:::

:::definition{term="Family planning"}
Deciding how many children to have and when, with the help of information and contraception.
:::

## Why countries have population policies

**Reasons for anti-natalist policies:**

1. Rapid population growth outpacing food supply, jobs, schools, clinics and housing.
2. A high dependency ratio of children.
3. Pressure on land and the environment (overgrazing, deforestation).
4. To improve the health of mothers and children by spacing births.

**Reasons for pro-natalist policies:**

1. Birth rates below replacement level (about 2.1 children per woman).
2. An ageing population with rising pension and care costs.
3. A shrinking workforce and fewer taxpayers.
4. Concern about national strength or long-term population decline.

## Measures used

| Anti-natalist measures | Pro-natalist measures |
|---|---|
| Free or cheap contraception and family planning clinics | Cash payments for each birth ("baby bonus") |
| Education about family planning; community health workers | Monthly child allowances, larger for third and later children |
| Educating girls and empowering women | Long, paid maternity and paternity leave |
| Raising the legal age of marriage | Subsidised childcare and nursery places |
| Incentives for small families (bonuses, priority housing) | Tax cuts and cheaper housing for large families |
| Penalties for large families (fines, loss of benefits) | Restricting contraception or abortion (in some past cases) |

## Case study: China's one-child policy (anti-natalist)

1. **Reason:** by the late 1970s China had nearly 1 billion people and feared that rapid growth would prevent economic development and food security.
2. **Policy (introduced 1979-1980):** most urban couples were allowed only one child. Couples who complied could receive benefits such as salary bonuses and priority for housing and schooling; those who broke the rules faced fines and loss of jobs. There were exceptions, for example for many rural families whose first child was a girl and for some ethnic minorities. There were reports of forced abortions and sterilisations.
3. **Effects on birth rates:** fertility fell sharply, and China's population grew much more slowly than it otherwise might have.
4. **Other effects:** a strong preference for sons led to a large **gender imbalance**, with many more boys than girls born; a rapidly **ageing population**; the "**4-2-1**" problem, where one child may have to support two parents and four grandparents; and a shrinking workforce.
5. **Changes:** the policy was replaced by a **two-child policy in 2016** and a **three-child policy in 2021**. Birth rates have nevertheless stayed low, because of the cost of housing and education and changing attitudes.

**Evaluation:** effective at lowering births, but with serious human-rights concerns and long-term structural problems. Other countries reduced fertility through education and voluntary family planning without such strict controls.

## Case study: family planning in Zimbabwe (anti-natalist, voluntary)

1. **Organisation:** the **Zimbabwe National Family Planning Council (ZNFPC)** provides contraception, education and advice through clinics and community-based distributors who visit rural homes.
2. **Approach:** voluntary; family planning is presented as **child spacing** to protect the health of mothers and children, and is linked to maternal health, HIV prevention and girls' education.
3. **Effects:** contraceptive use in Zimbabwe is among the highest in sub-Saharan Africa, and the total fertility rate has fallen from more than 6 children per woman in the early 1980s to about 4 (approximate values). Urbanisation, girls' education and economic pressures have also contributed.
4. **Limitations:** fertility remains higher in rural areas; access can be disrupted by shortages of supplies; cultural and religious views in some communities favour large families.

## Case study: France (pro-natalist, developed country)

France has encouraged births for many decades with:

- **family allowances** paid for the second and later children;
- **tax reductions** that increase with the number of children;
- **subsidised childcare** and nursery schools from an early age;
- **paid parental leave**, and discounts for large families (for example on public transport).

**Effects:** France's fertility rate has stayed among the highest in the European Union, close to but below replacement level. It is hard to separate the effect of the policy from other factors, such as attitudes to working mothers and immigration.

## Example: Singapore (a policy that changed direction)

In the 1970s Singapore ran an anti-natalist "**Stop at Two**" campaign. Fertility fell below replacement, so from 1987 the policy changed to "**Have three or more, if you can afford it**", later adding baby bonuses, tax relief and childcare support. Fertility has nevertheless remained very low, showing that it is easier to reduce birth rates than to raise them.

## Evaluating population policies

| Question | Points to consider |
|---|---|
| Did the birth rate change? | Compare before and after; other factors (education, urbanisation) may explain changes too |
| Was it fair and voluntary? | Coercive policies raise human-rights issues |
| What were the side effects? | Gender imbalance, ageing, unwanted children |
| Was it affordable? | Pro-natalist payments are expensive |
| Did it last? | Some policies had to be reversed |

:::example{title="Worked example: judging a pro-natalist policy (fictional data)"}
A fictional country introduced a baby bonus in Year 0.

| Year | Total fertility rate | Births (thousands) |
|---|---|---|
| −5 | 1.45 | 410 |
| 0 | 1.40 | 396 |
| 5 | 1.52 | 428 |
| 10 | 1.47 | 415 |

1. **Change in births, Year 0 to Year 5:** 428 − 396 = 32 thousand, a rise of 32 ÷ 396 × 100 ≈ **8.1%**.
2. **But** by Year 10 fertility had slipped back to 1.47, still far below replacement (2.1).
3. **Judgement:** the bonus may have brought some births forward in time rather than raising family size for good. Other factors (housing costs, careers) still limit births, so the policy had only a small and short-lived effect.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up pro- and anti-natalist"}
*Anti*-natalist means *against* births (reduce). *Pro*-natalist means *for* births (increase). China's one-child policy was anti-natalist; France's is pro-natalist.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the policy caused all the change"}
Birth rates also fall with education, urbanisation and wealth. When evaluating, say how much change was likely due to the policy.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
"Explain the effects of population policies in named developed and developing countries" needs two named countries (for example France and China, or Zimbabwe) with measures and effects for each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 6)"}
"Evaluate the impacts of pro-natalist and anti-natalist policies on birth rates" needs evidence that rates changed, other possible causes and a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Anti-natalist policies aim to lower birth rates (rapid growth, high dependency); pro-natalist policies aim to raise them (low fertility, ageing).
- China's one-child policy (1979-1980 to 2015) cut births but caused gender imbalance, ageing and human-rights concerns; it became a two-child (2016) and three-child (2021) policy.
- Zimbabwe's voluntary family planning, led by the ZNFPC, helped reduce fertility from over 6 to about 4 children per woman (approximate).
- France uses allowances, tax cuts, childcare and leave, keeping fertility relatively high for Europe; Singapore switched from "Stop at Two" to encouraging births.
- Evaluate policies by their effect on birth rates, fairness, side effects, cost and durability.
:::
