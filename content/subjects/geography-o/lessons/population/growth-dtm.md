## Introduction

For most of human history the world's population grew very slowly. Then, in about two centuries, it multiplied roughly eight times, passing 8 billion in 2022 (United Nations estimate). Today some countries, many of them in Africa, are still growing fast, while others, such as Japan, are shrinking. Why? The **demographic transition model** (DTM) offers an explanation that links population change to economic and social development.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population growth"}
An increase in the number of people in an area, from natural increase and/or net in-migration.
:::

:::definition{term="Population explosion"}
A very rapid increase in population, as happened worldwide in the 20th century when death rates fell faster than birth rates.
:::

:::definition{term="Demographic transition model (DTM)"}
A model showing how birth rates, death rates and total population change over time as a country develops, in five stages.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural decrease"}
When the death rate is higher than the birth rate, so the population falls (unless there is in-migration).
:::

## Patterns and trends in world population growth

| Year (approximate) | World population |
|---|---|
| about 1800 | 1 billion |
| about 1927 | 2 billion |
| about 1960 | 3 billion |
| about 1974 | 4 billion |
| about 1987 | 5 billion |
| about 1999 | 6 billion |
| about 2011 | 7 billion |
| 2022 | 8 billion |

(United Nations estimates.)

- **Trend:** growth was slow until about 1800, then accelerated, becoming exponential in the 20th century. The time taken to add each billion fell from over a century to about 12 years.
- **Rate:** the annual growth rate peaked at about 2% in the late 1960s and has since fallen to below 1%, but the large base population means numbers still rise.
- **Projections:** the UN projects about 9.7 billion by 2050 (approximate) and a peak later this century.
- **Pattern:** most growth now takes place in **sub-Saharan Africa** and **South Asia**; populations in much of Europe and East Asia are stable or falling.

## Factors affecting population growth and decline

| Factor | Effect on birth rate | Effect on death rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Health care** (vaccination, clinics, antibiotics) | Lower infant mortality → families need fewer births over time | Falls |
| **Clean water and sanitation** | | Falls (less cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea) |
| **Food supply and nutrition** | | Falls |
| **Family planning** (contraception) | Falls | |
| **Education of women** | Falls (later marriage, careers, knowledge of family planning) | Falls (better child care) |
| **Economic development and urbanisation** | Falls (children cost more, less needed as farm labour) | Falls |
| **Culture and religion** | May keep high (value of large families, opposition to contraception) | |
| **Government policy** | Pro- or anti-natalist policies (see population policies) | Health spending |
| **Disease, war, famine** | | Rises (e.g. HIV and AIDS) |
| **Migration** | In-migration of young adults raises births; out-migration lowers them | |

## The demographic transition model

Imagine a graph with time along the bottom. Two lines start high on the left: the birth rate and death rate, both about 35-40 per 1,000. A third line, total population, starts low.

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Total population | Reasons | Examples today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 High fluctuating** | High | High, fluctuating | Very low | Low, stable | Little health care, famine, disease; children needed for labour | No countries; a few isolated groups |
| **2 Early expanding** | High | Falling rapidly | Very high | Rising rapidly | Better health care, water and food reduce deaths; births stay high | Niger, South Sudan |
| **3 Late expanding** | Falling | Falling more slowly, then low | High but falling | Rising more slowly | Family planning, women's education, urbanisation, lower infant mortality | India, Zimbabwe, Kenya |
| **4 Low fluctuating** | Low | Low | Low | High, stable | Small families by choice; good health care | USA, UK, France |
| **5 Declining** | Very low | Low but rising slightly (ageing) | Negative | Slowly falling | Careers, cost of children; ageing population | Japan, Germany, Italy |

![Demographic transition model: birth and death rates start high at about 38 per 1,000 in stage 1; the death rate falls first in stage 2 so the gap (natural increase) and population grow fast; in stage 3 the birth rate falls; in stage 4 both rates are low and population is high and stable; in stage 5 the birth rate dips below the death rate and population falls slowly.](media:diagrams/demographic-transition-model.png "Figure: the five stages of the demographic transition model (shape only; values illustrative)")

:::example{title="Worked example: placing countries on the DTM (fictional data)"}
| Country | Birth rate (per 1,000) | Death rate (per 1,000) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 46 | 15 |
| Q | 24 | 8 |
| R | 8 | 11 |

1. **P:** natural increase = 46 − 15 = 31 per 1,000 = 3.1% a year. High births, falling deaths: **stage 2**. Doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ 3.1 ≈ 23 years.
2. **Q:** 24 − 8 = 16 per 1,000 = 1.6%. Births falling but still well above deaths: **stage 3**.
3. **R:** 8 − 11 = −3 per 1,000 = −0.3%. Births below deaths: **stage 5** (natural decrease).
:::

## Applying the DTM to Zimbabwe

1. Before the 20th century Zimbabwe's population probably resembled **stage 1**.
2. During the 20th century health care, vaccination and better water lowered death rates while birth rates stayed high: **stage 2**, with rapid growth.
3. Since the 1980s birth rates have fallen markedly, helped by widespread use of family planning, girls' education and urbanisation: **stage 3**.
4. **An exception:** in the 1990s and early 2000s HIV and AIDS raised the death rate and reduced life expectancy, which the model does not predict. Antiretroviral treatment has since brought deaths down again.
5. Emigration of many young adults to South Africa and elsewhere has also affected growth, which the DTM does not include.

## Effects of rapid population growth

| Negative | Positive |
|---|---|
| Pressure on schools, clinics, housing, water and food | Large future labour force |
| Unemployment, especially of young people | Larger market for goods and services |
| Land shortage, overcultivation, overgrazing and deforestation | More people to innovate and develop resources |
| Rapid urban growth and unplanned settlements | |
| High dependency ratio | |

## Effects of population decline

| Negative | Positive |
|---|---|
| Shrinking workforce and labour shortages | Less pressure on land, housing and resources |
| Ageing population: pension and health costs | Lower unemployment |
| Closure of schools and services; rural villages abandoned | Possible rise in wages and investment per worker |
| Smaller market; slower economic growth | Less environmental pressure |

## Strengths and limitations of the DTM

**Strengths:**

- Simple and easy to understand; fits the history of many HICs (for example the UK).
- Allows countries to be compared and future changes predicted.
- Shows the link between development, health and birth and death rates.

**Limitations:**

1. Based on the experience of European countries; transition in LICs has been **faster** and driven by imported medicine rather than industrialisation.
2. Does **not include migration**, which strongly affects many countries.
3. Does not show the impact of **diseases** such as HIV and AIDS, or of **wars**, which can raise death rates in later stages.
4. Does not include **government policies** (for example China's one-child policy) or **cultural and religious** influences.
5. Gives **no time scale** for each stage.
6. Some countries may not move beyond stage 2 or 3 without economic development.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying death rates rise in stage 2"}
In stage 2 the death rate **falls** sharply while the birth rate stays high; that gap causes rapid growth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a stage without evidence"}
"Zimbabwe is in stage 3" needs evidence: falling birth rate, low death rate, still positive natural increase.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 6)"}
"Describe the DTM and its strengths and limitations" needs each stage's birth rate, death rate and growth, then at least two strengths and two limitations. Use a named country at each stage.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C"}
For "explain the effects of rapid population growth", group effects as social, economic and environmental and refer to Zimbabwe or another named country.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- World population grew slowly until about 1800, then rapidly, passing 8 billion in 2022; growth rates have fallen since the late 1960s, and most growth is now in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.
- Birth rates fall with family planning, women's education, urbanisation and lower infant mortality; death rates fall with health care, clean water and food.
- The DTM has five stages: high fluctuating, early expanding, late expanding, low fluctuating and declining.
- Zimbabwe moved from stage 2 to stage 3, but HIV and AIDS and emigration show the model's limitations.
- Rapid growth strains services, jobs and land; decline brings labour shortages and ageing.
:::
