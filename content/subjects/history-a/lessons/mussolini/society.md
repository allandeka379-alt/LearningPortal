## Introduction

Fascists said that they would create a "new Italian": disciplined, patriotic, fertile and obedient, a citizen-soldier who believed in the nation and the Duce. They set out to shape the family, the school, the Church and leisure. This lesson asks how far they succeeded. It covers the Battle for Births and the position of women, the youth organisations and schools, the Lateran Treaties of 1929 and relations with the Church, the mass organisations that brought Italians into Fascist life, and the extent of opposition. The overall question is one of transformation: did the regime really change the way Italians lived and thought?

You should know the nature of the regime and its economic policy from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Battle for Births"}
The campaign launched in 1927 to raise Italy's population (from about 40 million towards 60 million) by encouraging marriage and large families and penalising bachelors.
:::

:::definition{term="Opera Nazionale Balilla (ONB)"}
The Fascist youth organisation founded in 1926, which enrolled boys and girls by age group for physical, military and political training. It was merged into the Gioventù Italiana del Littorio in 1937.
:::

:::definition{term="Lateran Treaties"}
The agreements of 11 February 1929 between Mussolini's government and the Pope, which created the Vatican City State and settled the dispute between the Italian state and the papacy that had lasted since 1870.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1923: the Gentile reforms of education. 1925: the OND; 1926: the ONB.
- 1927: the Battle for Births launched; a tax on bachelors.
- 11 February 1929: the Lateran Treaties. 1931: dispute with the Church over Catholic Action.
- 1930: a single state textbook in primary schools; 1931: the oath for professors; 1937: youth organisations merged.
:::

## Women and the family

The regime wanted women as mothers and homemakers. The **Battle for Births**, announced in 1927, aimed to raise the population, which Mussolini linked to military strength. A tax on bachelors, marriage loans, family allowances, tax advantages for large families and prizes for the most fertile mothers were introduced, and a national Day of the Mother and Child was celebrated. The National Organisation for Maternity and Infancy (ONMI, 1925) provided clinics. Contraception and abortion were banned in the 1930 penal code.

Women's work was restricted: women were paid about half of men's wages, were barred from certain posts and subjects (for example from teaching history and philosophy in the upper schools), and in the later 1930s quotas limited their share of office jobs and the professions. Fascist women's groups (the *Fasci Femminili*) provided organisation and training, but not political power.

**Results.** The Battle for Births failed in its main aim: the birth rate continued to fall during the Fascist years, as in other industrial countries. Many women, particularly in poor families, had to work regardless of the regime's ideals. Historians conclude that Fascism strengthened an existing conservative view of women but did not reverse the trend towards smaller families.

## Young people and education

**Schools.** The philosopher Giovanni Gentile, minister of education in 1922-24, reformed schooling: religion became compulsory in primary schools, the classical lycée became the route to university, and schooling was extended to age 14 in theory. The reforms favoured the elite and did little for vocational training. Later changes made teaching serve the regime: from 1930 a single state textbook was used in primary schools, teachers had to join the Fascist Party, and in 1931 university professors had to swear an oath of loyalty (only a handful refused, out of well over a thousand). History and geography were rewritten to glorify Rome and Fascism.

**Youth organisations.** The **ONB** (1926) enrolled children and young people by age: *Balilla* and *Piccole Italiane* for the youngest, *Avanguardisti* and *Giovani Italiane* for teenagers, and *Giovani Fascisti* for young men. They offered sport, camps, uniforms and military drill, and taught the slogan "Believe, Obey, Fight". By 1937 membership was effectively required in schools. At university, Fascist student groups offered a route into careers.

**Results.** The youth organisations reached millions and gave some young people leisure opportunities they would not otherwise have had. But many joined for convenience or because membership helped schooling and jobs. Church-run schools and families remained influential, and by the time of the war many young people were disillusioned. Historians therefore regard indoctrination as only partly successful.

## Religion and the Church

The Liberal state and the papacy had been in dispute since the capture of Rome in 1870, when the Pope declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican". Mussolini, who had been anticlerical, saw that the support of the Church would give his regime legitimacy among millions of Catholics. The **Lateran Treaties** of 11 February 1929 had three parts: a treaty creating the sovereign Vatican City State and recognising the Italian kingdom with Rome as capital; a Concordat that made Catholicism the religion of the state, gave religious instruction in schools and gave civil effect to church marriage; and a financial agreement compensating the papacy. The settlement was popular among Catholics and strengthened Mussolini's prestige.

Tensions remained. In 1931 the regime tried to restrict Catholic Action, the Church's lay organisation and its youth groups, and Pope Pius XI protested in an encyclical; a compromise left Catholic Action in place under limits. Later the Church was uneasy about the racial laws of 1938 and the alliance with Nazi Germany. Even so, the Church gave Mussolini valuable support throughout most of the regime.

## Mass organisations and leisure

Alongside the party, the regime created organisations that tried to bring all Italians into Fascist life. The **Opera Nazionale Dopolavoro** (OND), founded in 1925, offered clubs, sports, cinema, excursions and cheap holidays to millions of workers, and was among the regime's most popular institutions. Fascist unions, women's groups, veterans' organisations and party events added to the sense of belonging. But many Italians joined because membership helped to get a job, and participation did not always mean commitment.

## Opposition

Open opposition was small and dangerous. The Communist Party continued underground; its leader Antonio Gramsci was imprisoned in 1926. Some Liberals and Socialists went abroad: Don Sturzo, the historian Gaetano Salvemini, and the Rosselli brothers, who founded the movement *Giustizia e Libertà* in 1929. Carlo Rosselli was murdered in France in 1937 by assassins linked to Italian intelligence. Inside Italy the philosopher Benedetto Croce criticised the regime in print, and some Catholics resisted quietly through Catholic Action. Most Italians, however, conformed. Historians debate the question: some, such as Renzo De Felice, argued that the regime enjoyed real consent in the early 1930s; others emphasise coercion and conformity driven by fear and self-interest. The balance changed when the war brought defeat, and after 1943 a partisan resistance arose.

:::example{title="Judging how far society was transformed"}
For transformation: millions of children passed through the youth movements; the OND reached workers; the Lateran Treaties changed the relationship of Church and state; propaganda was everywhere. Against: the birth rate fell, the Church and families remained powerful, the south hardly changed, many people joined only for convenience, and the regime's hold weakened in the war. A judgement could be that the regime changed the outward forms of public life more than the beliefs of most Italians.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing two policies"}
Compare the Battle for Births (the birth rate kept falling, so it failed) with the Lateran Treaties (the Church was won over and Mussolini's prestige rose, so it largely succeeded). Contrasting a failure with a success is a good way to reach a judgement on how far society was transformed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming the Battle for Births worked"}
The birth rate fell over the period. Rewards were small and could not reverse a long-term trend.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the Lateran Treaties as a Catholic dictatorship at the Pope's command"}
They created a settlement of mutual advantage. The Church kept its independence, and the two sides quarrelled over youth and race.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating mass membership as proof of belief"}
Millions joined the ONB, the OND and the party. Many did so for jobs, benefits or fear. Comment on motives, not just numbers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Battle for Births and the restrictions on women's work tried to confine women to the home; the birth rate nevertheless fell.
- The Gentile reforms and the ONB brought schools and young people under Fascist influence, but many joined for convenience and the Church and families kept influence.
- The Lateran Treaties of 1929 settled the quarrel with the papacy and strengthened Mussolini, though tensions remained over Catholic Action and later racial policy.
- The OND and other organisations drew millions into Fascist life, but participation did not equal belief.
- Opposition was small and repressed, with exiles abroad; historians debate how far Italians consented or simply conformed.
:::
