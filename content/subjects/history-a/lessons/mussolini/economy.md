## Introduction

Mussolini promised a strong and prosperous Italy. Economically he faced real difficulties: a poor country in the south, little coal, iron or oil, a large peasant population and an industrial base concentrated in the north. His economic policy changed with his aims. In the early years he worked with free-market financiers; from the mid-1920s he launched a series of "battles" to strengthen the nation; after the Great Depression the state took a larger role; and from the later 1930s he pushed autarky (self-sufficiency) as he prepared for war. This lesson asks how successful these policies were and what they meant for ordinary Italians.

You should know the nature of the regime from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Autarky"}
An economic policy of self-sufficiency, in which a state tries to produce at home what it would otherwise import, usually to prepare for war or to avoid dependence on others.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate State"}
The Fascist idea of organising the economy through "corporations" in which employers, workers and the state would cooperate, replacing class struggle and independent unions.
:::

:::definition{term="Quota 90"}
The target of 90 lire to the pound sterling that Mussolini set for the value of the lira in 1926, as a matter of national prestige (in practice the lira was stabilised at about 92).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1925: the Battle for Grain. 1926-27: the Battle for the Lira (Quota 90). 1928: the Battle for Land.
- 1926-27: the Rocco Law and the Labour Charter. 1934: 22 corporations established.
- 1931: IMI. 1933: IRI. 1935-36: sanctions and autarky.
:::

## The battles of the 1920s

**The early years.** Until 1925 the finance minister, Alberto De Stefani, followed liberal policies: he balanced the budget, reduced taxes and cut the numbers of state employees. The economy recovered, and unemployment fell. But Mussolini wanted a strong lira as a symbol of national strength.

**The Battle for the Lira (1926-27).** The lira had been weak, and in August 1926 Mussolini announced that he would defend it. The government deflated the economy, cut wages and raised interest rates to bring the lira to about 90 to the pound, and the lira was stabilised at about 92 in December 1927. The result was prestige, and some benefits to people with savings and to importers of raw materials. But Italian exports were now dearer, so export industries suffered and unemployment rose. Wages were cut.

**The Battle for Grain (1925).** Italy imported much of its wheat. Tariffs, subsidies and propaganda campaigns encouraged farmers to grow more wheat, and production rose substantially in the following years, so that imports fell. But the cost was high: land was used for wheat that was better suited to fruit, olives, vines or livestock, bread was more expensive for consumers, and the large landowners gained most. Historians see the policy as a political success and an economic mistake.

**The Battle for Land (1928).** The regime drained marshes and reclaimed land, most famously the Pontine Marshes near Rome, where new towns such as Littoria (1932) and Sabaudia (1934) were built and families settled. This created farmland and jobs for a time and was widely publicised. But the amount of land reclaimed was limited compared with the claims made, the settlers were few, and the large estates of the south were not touched.

**Autarky.** After the League imposed sanctions in 1935-36, and as Mussolini prepared for war, the regime tried to make Italy self-sufficient in food, fuel and materials. Synthetic fibres were produced, production of electricity and steel was encouraged, and imports were limited. But Italy lacked the resources: it still had to import coal, oil and metals, and the policy raised costs and made industry less efficient.

| Policy | Aim | Results |
|---|---|---|
| Battle for the Lira (1926-27) | Strong lira for prestige | Lira stabilised near Quota 90, but exports fell, wages were cut |
| Battle for Grain (1925) | Self-sufficiency in wheat | Output rose, imports fell, but other crops suffered, bread dearer |
| Battle for Land (1928) | Reclaim land, resettle families | Pontine Marshes drained, new towns, limited overall effect |
| Autarky (1930s) | Self-sufficiency for war | Substitutes and some growth, but heavy costs and reliance on imports |

## The Great Depression

The Depression hit Italy from 1929. Exports fell, industrial output dropped and unemployment rose from about 300,000 in 1929 to over a million by the early 1930s (official figures probably understated it). Mussolini responded by increasing the role of the state.

- **Public works**: roads and motorways, railway electrification, land reclamation, building programmes and stadia provided jobs and propaganda.
- **IMI (1931)**: the Istituto Mobiliare Italiano, a state bank, lent to industry.
- **IRI (1933)**: the Istituto per la Ricostruzione Industriale took over the shares of the banks and companies that had collapsed. As a result the state came to own a large part of Italian banking, steel, shipbuilding and other industry, a state sector far larger than in most other countries, though Mussolini did not call it socialism.
- **Wage cuts and work-sharing**: wages were reduced and working hours spread to avoid dismissals.

The measures prevented a collapse, and some historians argue that the IRI was one of the regime's most lasting achievements, since it lasted until the end of the twentieth century. But recovery was slow and unemployment remained high.

## The Corporate State

The regime claimed to have solved the conflict between capital and labour through the **Corporate State**. In 1925 the Palazzo Vidoni Pact recognised the Fascist unions as the only workers' representatives; the Rocco Law of 1926 banned strikes and independent unions; and the Labour Charter of 1927 set out the principles of cooperation. In 1934 twenty-two corporations were created, each covering a branch of production, and in 1939 the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations replaced the Chamber of Deputies. In practice the corporations had little real power. Employers kept control of wages and hiring, the workers had no way of bargaining, and the system mostly served as propaganda and a means of control. Most historians conclude that the Corporate State benefited employers and the state more than workers.

:::example{title="Assessing the success of economic policy"}
Aim by aim: the lira was strengthened but at a cost to exports and wages (partial success); grain output rose but at the expense of other farming (mixed); the Pontine drainage was real but small (limited); the IRI rescued industry (success); the Corporate State gave workers no voice (failure). Overall, a strong answer says that the regime achieved some of its aims, such as the stronger lira and state control of industry, but at the expense of living standards, and that the policy was driven by prestige and war preparation as much as by economics.
:::

## Living standards

Real wages fell in the 1920s and early 1930s, and consumption of food such as meat, wheat and sugar per head did not rise. The poor south remained poor, and migration, which had been a safety valve, was restricted. The regime did improve some benefits: the *Opera Nazionale Dopolavoro* (OND) offered leisure activities and cheap holidays to workers, social insurance for maternity, accidents and old age was extended, and from 1934-35 the regime introduced family allowances, a shorter working week and a free Saturday afternoon. But the benefits were modest and linked to the party. Most historians conclude that living standards did not rise significantly.

:::example{title="Assessing one battle"}
On the Battle for Grain: output rose and imports fell (success on its own terms); but land was taken from better-suited crops, bread cost more and large landowners gained most (cost). Judging a policy against its aim and then against its cost is the model for every battle.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the Corporate State a form of socialism or workers' control"}
The corporations left employers in control and banned independent unions. Workers did not gain a voice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the battles failed completely"}
Grain output rose, the lira was strengthened and land was reclaimed. The point is the cost and the limits; give both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the IMI and the IRI"}
The IMI (1931) was a state bank that lent to industry. The IRI (1933) took over failing banks' holdings in firms and so brought a large part of industry under state ownership.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4"}
Typical questions: "Assess the success of Mussolini's economic policies", "How far did the Corporate State change relations between workers and employers?" and "Evaluate the view that the battles were a failure." Treat each policy separately and then give an overall judgement. Name the policy, say what it aimed to do, give a result with a date or detail, and say who gained. Avoid generalising without evidence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mussolini's battles (the lira, grain and land) and autarky served prestige and war preparation more than economic efficiency.
- The strong lira (Quota 90) hurt exports and wages; the grain battle raised output but harmed other farming; land reclamation was limited.
- After the Depression the state created the IMI (1931) and the IRI (1933) and ran public works; the IRI gave the state a large share of industry.
- The Corporate State banned independent unions and served employers and the regime rather than workers.
- Living standards did not rise much, although some benefits such as the OND and family allowances were introduced.
:::
