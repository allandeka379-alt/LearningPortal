## Introduction

Mussolini called his regime "totalitarian", and the word became part of the language, but historians argue about how far it deserves the name. The regime used violence, spies and censorship, built a powerful cult of the leader and sought to remake Italians, yet it left the monarchy, the Church and the army in place and relied on compromise with the established elites. This lesson examines how Mussolini governed (terror, propaganda and institutions), how effective that government was, and how his foreign policy, which began with ambition and showmanship, ended with a disastrous alliance with Hitler.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Totalitarian"}
A state that tries to control every part of public and private life, with one party and one leader, mass propaganda and terror. Historians debate whether Fascist Italy was fully totalitarian or only partly so.
:::

:::definition{term="Il Duce"}
"The Leader": the title that Mussolini took. The cult of the Duce presented him as an infallible man of destiny.
:::

:::definition{term="OVRA"}
The Fascist secret police, set up in 1927, which used informers and spies to track opponents.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1926: the Special Tribunal; 1927: OVRA. 1937: the Ministry of Popular Culture. 1938: the antisemitic laws.
- 1923: the Corfu incident. 1924: Fiume awarded to Italy. 1926-27: Albania becomes a protectorate.
- 3 October 1935: Italy invades Abyssinia (Ethiopia); May 1936: Addis Ababa taken.
- October 1936: the Rome-Berlin Axis. May 1939: the Pact of Steel. 10 June 1940: Italy enters the Second World War.
:::

## The terror state

The Fascist dictatorship used fear but, compared with Nazi Germany or Stalin's USSR, relatively little killing. The **Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State** (1926) tried political crimes, and in total issued only a few dozen death sentences before 1943, though thousands received prison terms. The secret police **OVRA** (1927) and a network of informers watched opponents. Many opponents were sent into **internal exile** (*confino*) in remote villages and islands, including the Communist leader Antonio Gramsci, who was imprisoned in 1926 and died in 1937. Others, such as Don Sturzo, went into exile abroad. Newspapers were controlled, and offices of the opposition were destroyed. The regime also used violence abroad, in Libya (where harsh repression of the resistance in the 1920s and early 1930s cost many thousands of lives) and, from 1935, in Ethiopia.

In 1938 Italy introduced racial laws against Jews, who numbered about 45,000 in Italy. Foreign Jews were ordered to leave, and Italian Jews were barred from state jobs, schools and universities and from marriage with non-Jews. Historians disagree on the causes: Mussolini's closer links with Germany, racist ideas from the Ethiopian war, and his wish to make Italians a more disciplined "race". The laws were unpopular with many Italians, and the Church and some officials protested, but they were enforced. The deportation of Jews to death camps began only after the German occupation in 1943.

## Propaganda and the cult of the Duce

Mussolini controlled the press from the mid-1920s: editors were appointed and journalists given daily orders. In 1937 the Ministry of Popular Culture (*Minculpop*) took charge of press, radio, film and the arts. The LUCE Institute made newsreels, and radio broadcast his speeches. Mussolini was shown as sportsman, aviator, soldier and father of the nation. Crowds were summoned to hear him from the balcony of the Palazzo Venezia. Fascist architecture and the glorifying of ancient Rome (*romanità*) taught Italians that they were heirs of an empire. Sport was used for prestige: Italy won the football World Cup in 1934 and 1938, and the airman Italo Balbo led mass flights across the Atlantic in 1933. Schools and youth movements spread the message (see the lesson on society).

How effective was propaganda? It gave Mussolini genuine popularity in the early 1930s, especially after the Lateran Treaties and the Abyssinian conquest, but historians note that many Italians took part without commitment, and that the regime's grip weakened as the war brought defeat.

## Fascist institutions

Mussolini's title was Head of Government and **Duce**; the party (PNF), led by a secretary, controlled local branches. The **Grand Council of Fascism**, made an organ of the state in 1928, advised on major matters and in 1943 voted to remove him. In 1939 the Chamber of Deputies was replaced by the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations. In the provinces appointed prefects, who were state officials, and podestà ran local government, and party bosses often competed with them. The party grew into a mass organisation, but it was often bureaucratic and corrupt.

The regime was never fully totalitarian. The **King** remained head of state and commander of the armed forces, and in 1943 it was he who dismissed Mussolini. The **Church** kept its schools and its influence, and the army, civil service and big business remained powerful. Mussolini made many compromises, and his personal rule left unclear who made decisions.

:::example{title="Is 'totalitarian' the right word?"}
For: one party, the cult of the Duce, control of the press, secret police and the attempt to shape youth and culture. Against: the monarchy and the Church survived with real independence, big business kept its autonomy, and repression was less murderous than in Germany or the USSR. A good answer says that Mussolini aimed at totalitarian control but achieved it only partly, giving evidence on both sides before judging.
:::

## Foreign policy

Mussolini wished to make Italy a great power and to build a new Roman empire around the Mediterranean ("our sea"), to overturn the "mutilated victory", and to win glory. His early actions were opportunistic. In the **Corfu incident** (1923), after an Italian general was murdered on the Greek-Albanian border, he bombarded and occupied Corfu; the Greeks paid compensation and Italy withdrew. In 1924 Fiume was recognised as Italian. In the later 1920s he made Albania an Italian protectorate and supported the Locarno agreements of 1925. In April 1935 he joined Britain and France at Stresa to oppose German rearmament.

Then he turned to Africa. After a clash at Wal Wal in December 1934, Italy invaded **Abyssinia** (Ethiopia) on 3 October 1935, an independent African state that had defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896. Italian forces used aircraft and poison gas against Ethiopian armies and civilians, and Addis Ababa fell in May 1936. Emperor Haile Selassie appealed to the League of Nations, which imposed limited sanctions that excluded oil and failed to stop the war. For Italy the conquest brought popularity at home and an empire, but it also cost money and men and ended the Stresa partnership. For Africans and people of African descent elsewhere, it became a symbol of colonial aggression and the League's weakness.

Isolated from Britain and France, Mussolini drew closer to Hitler: the **Rome-Berlin Axis** (October 1936), Italian intervention in the Spanish Civil War on Franco's side, the Anti-Comintern Pact (1937) and, after accepting the German Anschluss of Austria, the **Pact of Steel** (May 1939). He invaded Albania in April 1939. Italy stayed out of the war in 1939 because it was unprepared, but entered on 10 June 1940, when France was falling.

**Assessment.** Foreign policy brought prestige and some territory, but by 1940 Italy was dependent on Germany and militarily weak. Most historians therefore judge the later foreign policy, above all the Axis and the war, a failure that destroyed the regime.

:::example{title="Judging foreign policy by its results"}
Judge the conquest of Abyssinia on two timescales: in 1936 it brought an empire and popularity; by 1940 it had cost the friendship of Britain and France and pushed Italy into dependence on Germany. A strong answer says which timescale the question asks you to use.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Comparing Mussolini's terror with Hitler's without nuance"}
Fascist Italy used repression, but with far fewer executions than Nazi Germany or Stalin's USSR. Explain the methods, not only the numbers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the League stopped the Abyssinian invasion"}
The League imposed limited sanctions that did not include oil, and the conquest was completed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4"}
Questions such as "Assess how effectively Mussolini governed Italy", "How far was Italy a totalitarian state?" and "Evaluate the success of Mussolini's foreign policy" need a judgement across areas. Separate terror, propaganda, institutions and foreign policy, give evidence of success and failure in each, and conclude on overall effectiveness. For the Abyssinian war use dates and the part of the League.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The terror state relied on the Special Tribunal, OVRA, internal exile and censorship, with less killing than other dictatorships.
- The cult of the Duce, the Ministry of Popular Culture and rallies spread the regime's message and gave Mussolini real popularity.
- The regime was not fully totalitarian: the King, the Church and the army kept powers.
- Foreign policy moved from Corfu and Albania to the conquest of Abyssinia (1935-36), and then to the Axis and the Pact of Steel.
- The antisemitic laws of 1938 and the alliance with Germany show the regime's final direction; the war of 1940 destroyed it.
:::
