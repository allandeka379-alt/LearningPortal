## Introduction

In October 1922 Benito Mussolini, the leader of a movement that had won only 35 seats at the election of the year before, became prime minister of Italy. Four years later he ruled as a dictator, with other parties and free unions banned. This lesson asks why a liberal state gave way to him: the problems after the war, the growth of the Fascist movement and squads, the circumstances of the March on Rome, and the steps from office to dictatorship.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fascism"}
A movement of the extreme right in Italy led by Mussolini. It was nationalist, hostile to socialism and liberalism, glorified violence and the state, and demanded obedience to a single leader. The name comes from the *fasces*, the bundle of rods that was the symbol of authority in ancient Rome.
:::

:::definition{term="Squadrismo"}
The violence of Fascist squads (*squadre*, the "blackshirts"), who attacked socialists, trade unions and cooperatives, often with the help of landowners and the tolerance of the police.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 23 March 1919: Mussolini founds the Fascist movement in Milan. September 1919-December 1920: D'Annunzio holds Fiume.
- Autumn 1920: factory occupations (Biennio Rosso, 1919-20). November 1921: the National Fascist Party.
- 28 October 1922: the March on Rome; 31 October: Mussolini becomes prime minister.
- 1923: Acerbo Law. April 1924: Fascist "National List" wins the election. 10 June 1924: Matteotti murdered.
- 3 January 1925: Mussolini takes responsibility and moves to dictatorship. 1925-26: the laws that end liberal government.
:::

## Italy after the First World War

**The mutilated victory.** Italy entered the war in 1915 after the secret Treaty of London promised it Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria and part of Dalmatia. The war cost an estimated 600,000 or more Italian lives. At the peace conference Italy gained Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste and Istria, but not Dalmatia or the port of Fiume, which Yugoslavia claimed. Nationalists such as the poet **Gabriele D'Annunzio** denounced this as a "mutilated victory".

**Fiume.** In September 1919 D'Annunzio seized Fiume with a force of ex-soldiers and ruled it for fifteen months with theatrical rituals, such as the balcony speech and the raised-arm salute, which Fascists later copied. The government expelled him in December 1920, but the affair showed that nationalist adventurers could defy a weak state.

**Economic and social crisis.** After the war about two million soldiers returned to a country with high unemployment, inflation and food shortages. Strikes spread in 1919-20, and in the countryside peasants occupied land. In September 1920 about half a million workers occupied factories in Turin and Milan. This **Biennio Rosso** ("two red years") alarmed landowners, industrialists and the middle classes, who feared a Russian-style revolution, even though the occupations ended without a revolution.

**Political weakness.** The election of 1919, held under proportional representation, produced a Chamber in which the Socialists (the largest party) and the Catholic Popular Party could not agree to work with the Liberals or one another. Governments (Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi, Facta) rose and fell quickly.

## The Fascist movement and squadrismo

Mussolini, born in 1883, had been a Socialist journalist but was expelled from the party in 1914 for supporting Italy's entry into the war. In March 1919 he founded the *Fasci italiani di combattimento* in Milan, with a radical programme (for example votes for women and an eight-hour day) that attracted ex-servicemen and nationalists. It won no seats in the election of 1919.

From late 1920 Mussolini moved to the right. Fascist squads, led by local bosses called *ras* (such as Balbo, Farinacci and Grandi), attacked Socialist offices, union halls and cooperatives in the Po valley, Tuscany and other regions, often with weapons supplied by landowners and with the police and army looking away. Industrialists and landowners gave money. In the elections of May 1921 Giolitti brought the Fascists into his National Bloc and they won 35 seats. In November 1921 the movement became the **National Fascist Party** (PNF), with hundreds of thousands of members. Mussolini presented himself both as leader of a violent movement and as a respectable politician who could restore order.

In August 1922 the Socialists called a general strike. Fascists broke it by taking over services, which won applause from the middle classes and showed that the state could not keep order.

## The March on Rome and the appointment of 1922

Mussolini used the threat of a march on the capital to bargain for power. In October 1922 Fascist squads, perhaps 25,000 to 30,000 men and mostly poorly armed, gathered at points near Rome on 27-28 October, while Mussolini himself waited in Milan. Prime Minister Facta wanted to proclaim martial law, but **King Victor Emmanuel III** refused to sign the decree on 28 October. The King then asked Mussolini to form a government. Mussolini travelled to Rome by train, and the Fascists paraded in the capital afterwards. The "March" was therefore a bluff that was used to claim a revolution which had not occurred.

Why did the King refuse to resist? He feared a civil war and disloyalty in the army, doubted that the government could survive, and thought he could tame Mussolini by including him in a coalition. Many Liberals, conservatives, the Church, industrialists and newspapers also expected that office would moderate the Fascists or that they could be used against the left. Historians stress this role of the elites: Mussolini was appointed, not simply a seizer of power. His first cabinet included Liberals, Nationalists and Catholics.

## From office to dictatorship, 1922-1926

**The Acerbo Law (1923)** gave two thirds of the seats to the party that won the most votes, as long as it had at least 25 per cent. In the election of April 1924 the Fascist-led "National List" won about 65 per cent of the vote, helped by violence, intimidation and fraud.

**The Matteotti murder.** On 30 May 1924 the Socialist deputy **Giacomo Matteotti** denounced the violence and fraud in the Chamber. On 10 June he was kidnapped and killed by Fascists with links to Mussolini's circle. The opposition deputies left parliament in protest (the "Aventine Secession"), hoping the King would dismiss Mussolini, but he did nothing. In a speech of 3 January 1925 Mussolini accepted political responsibility and challenged his opponents to act, which marks the move to open dictatorship.

**The laws of 1925-26.** The press was brought under control, and the Palazzo Vidoni Pact (1925) made the Fascist unions the only recognised bargaining body. A law of December 1925 made the head of government responsible to the king alone, and another of January 1926 allowed him to make laws by decree. Elected mayors were replaced by appointed *podestà*. After assassination attempts, laws of November 1926 banned opposition parties, set up a Special Tribunal and restored the death penalty for attempts on the life of the Duce. By the end of 1926 Italy was a one-party dictatorship, though the monarchy, the Church, the army and the courts remained.

:::example{title="Explaining why Mussolini became prime minister"}
Build a chain. First, **conditions**: the mutilated victory, the Biennio Rosso and unstable governments created fear. Second, **the movement**: the squads, tolerated by landowners and officials, destroyed the Socialists' local power. Third, **the elites**: Liberal leaders hoped to use him, and the King refused to use the army. A strong answer ranks them, for example by arguing that the weakness and calculations of the establishment mattered more than Fascist strength, since a determined government could have defeated the March.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing violence and consent"}
To assess the Fascist squads, note that they broke the Socialists' local power through violence (supporting the view of Fascist strength) but did so with the tolerance of police and landowners (supporting the view that the establishment allowed it). The same evidence can therefore support both sides of the argument.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Believing the March on Rome was a successful uprising"}
It was a show of force. Mussolini came to power by the King's invitation, after the King refused to declare martial law.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that Mussolini first governed in coalition"}
In 1922 he led a coalition, and the dictatorship was built over four years, not in a day.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The mutilated victory, economic crisis and the Biennio Rosso, together with weak governments, damaged Liberal Italy's credibility.
- Mussolini's Fascists used squad violence against the left from 1920, with the support or tolerance of landowners, industrialists and officials.
- The March on Rome (October 1922) succeeded because the King refused martial law and invited Mussolini to form a government.
- The Acerbo Law, the election of 1924 and the Matteotti murder weakened parliament, and the speech of 3 January 1925 began the open dictatorship.
- The laws of 1925-26 banned opposition and put the press, unions and local government under Fascist control.
:::
