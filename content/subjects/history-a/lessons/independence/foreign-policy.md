## Introduction

A country's foreign policy is the set of aims and actions by which it deals with other states. Independent Zimbabwe was born in the middle of the Cold War and next to apartheid South Africa, which was powerful, hostile to majority rule and ready to use force against its neighbours. The new government therefore had two tasks: to protect its own security and development, and to keep faith with the liberation of the rest of southern Africa, which it had promised to support.

This lesson explains the principles of Zimbabwe's foreign policy between 1980 and 2000, its main relationships, and the aims and results of its actions in Mozambique, South Africa and the Democratic Republic of Congo. You need to know how Zimbabwe became independent and how the liberation movements were supported by neighbouring states. As in all lessons on the period, aims, outcomes and different views are set out so that you can judge.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Non-alignment"}
A policy of not joining either of the Cold War blocs, led by the United States and the Soviet Union, and of judging issues on their merits. The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) brought together states that followed this policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Frontline States"}
The independent states of southern Africa that supported the liberation of Rhodesia, Namibia and South Africa and bore the cost of South African and Rhodesian attacks: originally Angola, Botswana, Mozambique, Tanzania and Zambia, joined by Zimbabwe in 1980 and Namibia in 1990.
:::

:::definition{term="Destabilisation"}
The policy of apartheid South Africa in the 1980s of weakening neighbouring states by raiding them, backing rebel movements such as RENAMO in Mozambique, and attacking transport routes and economies.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- April 1980: SADCC founded at Lusaka; Zimbabwe a founder member and joins the Frontline States.
- 1980: Zimbabwe joins the UN, the OAU, the Commonwealth and the Non-Aligned Movement.
- 1982-1992: Zimbabwean troops protect the Beira Corridor in Mozambique; the Rome peace agreement is signed in October 1992.
- 1-6 September 1986: the Non-Aligned Summit in Harare.
- October 1991: Commonwealth Heads of Government meeting in Harare (the Harare Declaration).
- August 1998: Zimbabwe sends troops to the DRC.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## Principles and aims

The government stated a number of guiding aims: to defend independence and territorial integrity; to support the end of colonialism and apartheid; to promote economic development through trade, aid and regional co-operation; to pursue **non-alignment**; and to work through the OAU, the UN and the Commonwealth. The new government also followed a policy of reconciliation with its neighbours and with Western states, and kept trade links with South Africa because Zimbabwe's economy depended on its railways and ports, even while it condemned apartheid.

## Non-alignment and international organisations

Zimbabwe joined the **Non-Aligned Movement** in 1980 and hosted its summit in Harare in September 1986, when the Prime Minister, Robert Mugabe, became chairman of the movement until 1989. Through NAM Zimbabwe called for an end to apartheid, for a fairer world economic order and for nuclear disarmament. It also joined the **United Nations** in 1980, served on the Security Council in the 1980s and 1990s, and contributed troops to some UN peacekeeping operations. It joined the **OAU** in 1980, where it supported the Liberation Committee's work for Namibia and South Africa, and Zimbabwe hosted the OAU summit in 1997. Its membership of the **Commonwealth** was marked by the Commonwealth Heads of Government meeting at Harare in October 1991, which adopted the Harare Declaration committing members to democracy, human rights and sustainable development.

Critics of non-alignment note that Zimbabwe in practice received most of its aid, loans and trade from Western countries and the IMF, and from the early 1980s also had friendly relations with socialist states such as China, North Korea and the USSR, so policy was more flexible than the label suggests. Supporters say that this showed pragmatism: Zimbabwe took help from all sides without taking orders from any.

## The Frontline States and SADCC

Zimbabwe joined the Frontline States in 1980. In April 1980 it was a founder of the **Southern African Development Coordination Conference (SADCC)**, which was set up by nine states in Lusaka to reduce their dependence on South Africa for transport and trade, and to promote development together. Zimbabwe took charge of the food security sector. SADCC is the subject of a separate lesson, and became SADC in 1992.

## Opposition to apartheid and the war in Mozambique

Zimbabwe condemned apartheid in the UN, NAM, the Commonwealth and the OAU, supported sanctions in principle, and gave political and humanitarian support to the African National Congress, but it did not allow armed ANC bases on its soil. It was careful because South Africa could close its borders, cut rail links or attack. South Africa was accused of backing sabotage and armed dissidents in Zimbabwe and carried out raids on targets in the region.

The most serious test was Mozambique. After its independence in 1975 Mozambique was attacked by RENAMO, a rebel movement that began under the Rhodesian security services and was supported by South Africa after 1980. The war cut the roads, railways and oil pipeline that linked Zimbabwe to the port of Beira, on which landlocked Zimbabwe depended to reduce its use of the South African ports. From 1982 Zimbabwean troops protected the **Beira Corridor** and in 1985-86 helped the Mozambican army in larger operations. The cost was heavy in money and lives. Some critics at home argued that the money would have been better spent on development; the government replied that securing the corridor was necessary for the country's trade. The Rome General Peace Agreement of October 1992 ended the war, and Zimbabwean troops withdrew by 1993.

:::example{title="Explaining why Zimbabwe intervened in Mozambique"}
1. Zimbabwe is landlocked, and its exports and imports had to travel by rail, road and pipeline to a port.
2. The route to Beira was shorter than that to South African ports, and using it reduced dependence on South Africa.
3. RENAMO, supported by South Africa, attacked the corridor, so Zimbabwe's security and trade were threatened.
4. The Frontline States also had a duty to support Mozambique, whose government had helped Zimbabwe's liberation struggle.
5. Therefore Zimbabwe sent troops to guard the corridor from 1982 and joined fighting in 1985-86.
:::

## The Democratic Republic of Congo, 1998

In August 1998 rebel forces, backed by Rwanda and Uganda, rose against President Laurent Kabila of the DRC. Zimbabwe, with Angola and Namibia, sent troops to support his government. Zimbabwe argued that it acted at the request of a fellow member of SADC and was defending the sovereignty of a member state against invasion. South Africa and some other SADC members disagreed about whether SADC had authorised the action. Critics, including some inside Zimbabwe, asked about the cost to a weak budget and about the business interests that arose. A ceasefire agreement was signed in Lusaka in July and August 1999, and Zimbabwean forces were withdrawn by 2002. This is a case where historians and commentators differ over the motives and the results.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Zimbabwe cut all links with South Africa"}
Zimbabwe condemned apartheid but still traded with South Africa and used its railways. Mention the dependence as the reason.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up SADCC and SADC"}
SADCC (1980) was a co-ordination conference aimed at reducing dependence on South Africa. SADC (1992) is the community with a treaty, which South Africa joined in 1994.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting one view only"}
On the DRC and Mozambique, state the government's reasons and the criticisms, and avoid praise or blame.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe's foreign policy aimed to protect independence, end apartheid and colonialism, develop the economy and remain non-aligned.
- It joined the UN, OAU, Commonwealth and NAM in 1980, hosted the NAM summit in 1986 and the Commonwealth summit in 1991.
- It was a Frontline State and a founder of SADCC, to reduce dependence on South Africa.
- Troops protected the Beira Corridor in Mozambique from 1982 until the peace of 1992.
- The DRC intervention of 1998 was defended as a SADC duty and criticised for its cost; views differ.
:::
