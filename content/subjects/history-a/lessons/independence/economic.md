## Introduction

When Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980 it inherited an economy that was strong in some ways and deeply unequal in others. It had mines, large commercial farms, factories and a good road and rail network, yet most Africans lived in poor rural areas, and the benefits of growth had gone mainly to the white minority. After fourteen years of sanctions and war, the new government had to rebuild, to reduce inequality and to avoid frightening away investment, all at the same time.

This lesson follows the main economic blueprints between 1980 and 2000, asks what they achieved and explains why the 1990s were so much harder than the 1980s. You should already know how the Lancaster House Agreement limited what the new government could change, because those limits shaped its choices. Social services are covered in the next lesson and land reform in a later one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blueprint"}
A government document that sets out aims, policies and targets for the economy over a number of years. It is a plan, so the question for a historian is always how far it was carried out.
:::

:::definition{term="Structural adjustment"}
A package of policies, usually agreed with the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank, that aims to move an economy from state control towards the market. Typical measures are cutting government spending, removing price and import controls, devaluing the currency and selling state businesses.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of payments deficit"}
A situation in which a country spends more foreign currency on imports and debt payments than it earns from exports and other sources.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1981: *Growth with Equity*, the first policy statement; the ZIMCORD donors' conference in March 1981.
- 1982-85: Transitional National Development Plan (TNDP).
- 1986-90: First Five-Year National Development Plan.
- 1991: the Economic Structural Adjustment Programme (ESAP) begins.
- 1996-2000: ZIMPREST, the Zimbabwe Programme for Economic and Social Transformation.
- 14 November 1997: "Black Friday", when the Zimbabwe dollar lost a large part of its value in a few hours.
:::

## The inheritance of 1980

The economy that the new government took over had several features. It was diverse for an African country, with mining, manufacturing and commercial agriculture, but it was dualistic: a modern sector owned largely by whites and foreign companies, and a communal sector of poor land, low output and little investment. Wages, land, skills and services were divided by race. Sanctions had encouraged local industry but had also left the country short of foreign currency and old machinery. Once sanctions ended, there was a strong rebound in 1980-81, helped by good rains and a return to world markets, but the underlying inequality remained.

## Growth with Equity, 1981

In March 1981 the government published *Growth with Equity*, a policy statement that tried to balance two aims. It promised to keep the economy growing, so that investors and white farmers and industrialists would stay, and to spread the benefits through spending on education, health, rural development and a minimum wage. It spoke of a "socialist" direction, but in practice the economy remained mixed, with private business still dominant. In the same month the Zimbabwe Conference on Reconstruction and Development (ZIMCORD) in Salisbury (soon renamed Harare) brought together donor countries, which pledged well over a billion US dollars in aid and loans, although much of it came slowly and as loans.

## The development plans of the 1980s

The Transitional National Development Plan (1982/83-1984/85) set targets for growth, resettlement, social spending and jobs. It was launched just as the drought and the world recession were biting, and many of its targets were not met. The first Five-Year National Development Plan (1986-90) aimed at more modest, realistic growth and at creating jobs, but again it was held back by shortages of foreign currency. The state also took a larger part in the economy through price and wage controls, foreign-currency allocation, a minimum wage and state-owned enterprises, which gave workers some protection but created queues for import licences and made businesses cautious.

:::example{title="Weighing an achievement of the 1980s"}
**Claim:** the 1980s brought real gains to smallholder agriculture.

1. Before 1980 most marketing depots, loans and advice served white farmers; communal farmers were poorly served.
2. After independence new Grain Marketing Board depots were opened in or near communal areas, the Agricultural Finance Corporation lent to small farmers, and extension officers were sent to the communal areas.
3. With good rains in the mid-1980s, communal farmers' share of the marketed maize crop rose from a small fraction to roughly half in the best years, and the country became a net exporter of maize in some seasons.
4. Judgement: this was a genuine achievement, but it depended on good rainfall, on subsidies and cheap credit, and it did not remove poverty in the poorest communal areas.
:::

## Why did the economy struggle?

Several factors combined. **Droughts** in 1982-84 and again in 1991-92 cut harvests and exports, and the state had to import food. **Foreign-currency shortages and debt** limited imports of raw materials and spare parts, so factories ran below capacity. Government spending on social services and the public service rose faster than revenue, and budget deficits were large. **The world recession** of the early 1980s lowered prices for exports such as minerals. Critics also point to inefficiency in state enterprises, controls that discouraged exporters, and slow job creation, because the labour force grew faster than the number of formal jobs. Defenders of the government answer that the drought, the war in Mozambique (which disrupted trade routes), and South African destabilisation of the region imposed heavy costs, and that its priority of spreading services was right.

## ESAP, 1991-95

By 1990 growth was low, unemployment rising and the deficit large. The government, under pressure from lenders and from local business, adopted the Economic Structural Adjustment Programme in 1991, with IMF and World Bank support. It involved:

- removal of price and import controls and **trade liberalisation**;
- devaluation of the Zimbabwe dollar;
- cuts in the government deficit and in the civil service;
- **cost recovery**, meaning fees for some health and education services;
- selling or restructuring state enterprises.

The results are debated. Supporters said ESAP was needed to restore growth, to reduce the deficit and to attract investment. In practice, the severe drought of 1991-92 struck just as ESAP began, and the programme was followed by a fall in output in many industries as cheaper imports competed with local goods, by job losses in textiles, clothing and other manufacturing, and by higher prices for food and transport. Real wages fell, and the cost of user fees fell hardest on poor families. Some sectors, including horticulture and tourism, expanded. Many observers, including some inside government, concluded that ESAP did not achieve its aims for growth and employment and had a high social cost.

## ZIMPREST and the crisis of the late 1990s

ZIMPREST (1996-2000) replaced ESAP and promised faster growth and a bigger role for indigenous business. It was soon overtaken by events. Trade unions and consumers protested against rising prices and taxes, and food riots broke out in January 1998. In 1997 the government agreed to pay compensation and pensions to war veterans that had not been budgeted for, and on 14 November 1997 the Zimbabwe dollar lost much of its value in a few hours ("Black Friday"). In 1998 Zimbabwe sent troops to the Democratic Republic of Congo, which many analysts argued increased the strain on public finances, though the government defended the intervention. By 2000 inflation, unemployment and debt were high and growth had stalled.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating ESAP as a Zimbabwean plan only"}
ESAP was designed with the IMF and World Bank. A balanced answer says why the government chose it (deficits, debt, pressure from lenders and business) and why it was criticised (unemployment, user fees, deindustrialisation).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing plans without judging them"}
Writing out TNDP, Five-Year Plan, ESAP and ZIMPREST is narrative. Say what each aimed at, what it achieved, and what prevented success.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using statistics you are not sure of"}
Give a description ("roughly half the maize sold", "a large fall in the value of the dollar") unless you are certain of the exact figure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Describe the economic policies of the Zimbabwean government between 1980 and 1990", "Why was ESAP introduced?", "To what extent did the economy achieve its aims between 1980 and 2000?" For "to what extent" answers, group successes (growth in the early 1980s, smallholder output, social spending) and failures (drought, debt, unemployment, ESAP's costs), use dates and plan names, and finish with a clear judgement. Remember that the economy was shaped by both internal and external factors.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- At independence Zimbabwe had a diverse but unequal, dualistic economy; the government wanted growth and greater equality.
- Growth with Equity (1981), the TNDP and the first Five-Year Plan set the aims of the 1980s; much donor aid was pledged at ZIMCORD.
- Achievements of the 1980s included more smallholder maize sold, more credit and extension for communal farmers, and higher social spending.
- Droughts, debt, shortages of foreign currency and deficits limited success; ESAP (1991) and ZIMPREST (1996) were followed by job losses, higher prices and crisis in 1997-98.
- Judging policies requires balance: internal choices, external pressures and weather all mattered.
:::
