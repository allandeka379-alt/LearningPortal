## Introduction

For most Zimbabweans, independence meant the hope of a school place, a clinic within reach, and the ending of laws that treated people differently because of race or sex. The government of 1980 made education and health its first social priorities, and in the 1980s Zimbabwe built a reputation in Africa for rapid progress. In the 1990s, however, the same services were strained by structural adjustment, drought and, above all, the HIV/AIDS epidemic.

This lesson explains the expansion of education, the new approach to health, other social changes, and the challenges that followed, always comparing with the colonial period. You should know from earlier lessons that colonial education and health services were divided by race, and from the previous lesson how the economy changed after 1991. Human rights are covered in outline here and in detail in the constitution lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary health care"}
A policy that puts basic, low-cost services such as clinics, immunisation, clean water and health education close to people, especially in rural areas, instead of concentrating money on large hospitals.
:::

:::definition{term="Immunisation"}
Giving vaccines to protect people, especially children, from diseases such as measles, polio and tuberculosis.
:::

:::definition{term="User fees"}
Charges paid by people who use a public service such as a clinic or a school. They were introduced or raised under the cost-recovery policy of ESAP.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1980: fees abolished for primary schooling; a policy of expansion in secondary education.
- 1980-81: the policy document on primary health care, *Planning for Equity in Health*.
- 1982: the Legal Age of Majority Act, giving people adult legal status at 18, including women.
- 1985: the first cases of AIDS were identified in Zimbabwe.
- 1991: user fees for health and education under ESAP; NUST opens in Bulawayo.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Social developments after 1980 are ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine them.
:::

## Education: from a racial system to mass schooling

Before 1980 the education system was divided by race. White children had compulsory, well-funded schooling; most African children attended mission or council schools with fewer places, larger classes and fees, and only a small minority reached secondary school.

After independence the government removed the racial division and **abolished fees at primary level** and set out to build more schools, mostly through communities and councils working with the state. Primary enrolment rose very quickly, and secondary enrolment grew many times over within a decade. Many new secondary schools were opened, often in rural areas, with local people building classrooms and the state paying teachers. New teacher-training programmes, such as the Zimbabwe Integrated National Teacher Education Course (ZINTEC), trained thousands of teachers while they taught. The government also promoted **education with production**, which combined academic work with practical subjects, although it was not always well resourced. At higher level, the University of Zimbabwe expanded, new colleges and polytechnics opened, and later the National University of Science and Technology (NUST, 1991) and Africa University (1992) were established. By the end of the 1980s Zimbabwe had one of the highest adult literacy rates in Africa.

There were weaknesses. Rapid expansion strained the supply of qualified teachers, books and equipment; rural secondary schools often lacked laboratories; and the number of school leavers grew faster than formal jobs, so unemployment among the educated became a problem. In the 1990s fees were reintroduced for some levels and economic hardship made it harder for poor families to pay.

## Health: primary health care

At independence the health system was concentrated in towns, and in rural areas many people lived far from a clinic. Disease, poor nutrition and the effects of war added to the burden. The government adopted a policy of **primary health care**, set out in *Planning for Equity in Health* (1980-81), which stressed that services should reach the rural majority. It built and upgraded rural health centres and clinics, trained village health workers, increased the number of nurses and promoted clean water and sanitation. A national programme of **immunisation** was launched, and coverage of children for diseases such as measles, polio and tuberculosis rose substantially. Infant mortality fell and life expectancy rose during the 1980s.

:::example{title="Comparing before and after 1980"}
**Question:** how far did health care improve for rural Africans after independence?

1. Before 1980 hospitals and doctors were concentrated in the towns and served mainly whites; the war closed many rural clinics.
2. After 1980 the government built and reopened rural health centres, trained village health workers and began a national immunisation programme.
3. Child immunisation coverage rose and infant mortality fell during the 1980s.
4. But distances to the clinic were still long in remote areas, drugs and staff were often in short supply, and from the early 1990s user fees and budget cuts reduced access.
5. Judgement: the improvement in the 1980s was real and large, though the gains were later threatened by cuts and by HIV/AIDS.
:::

## Other social developments

**Women's status.** Under customary and colonial law women were treated as legal minors. The **Legal Age of Majority Act (1982)** gave women adult legal status at 18 and the right to enter contracts and marry without a guardian's consent. Later laws, such as the Matrimonial Causes Act (1985), improved women's rights over property on divorce, and minimum-wage laws helped low-paid workers. Many rural women still had little control of land, and customary practice often differed from the law.

**Housing and workers.** The government set minimum wages (1980), encouraged housing cooperatives and built houses and services in some townships, but a serious housing shortage remained in Harare, Bulawayo and other towns, and overcrowding continued.

**Human rights in outline.** The Declaration of Rights of the Lancaster House constitution protected freedoms, and racial discrimination in law was removed. Critics noted that the state of emergency inherited from the Smith era was kept until 1990 and that laws such as the Law and Order (Maintenance) Act restricted freedoms, and that there were serious disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands between 1983 and 1987, ended by the Unity Accord of 22 December 1987. Supporters of the government stressed the need for security and national unity. These events are explained in the political lessons.

## Challenges of the 1990s

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 1980 | Primary fees abolished; racial barriers in schools and hospitals removed |
| 1980-81 | Primary health care policy launched |
| 1982 | Legal Age of Majority Act |
| 1985 | First AIDS cases identified |
| 1991 | ESAP and cost recovery; NUST opens |
| Late 1990s | HIV/AIDS at its peak, with a very high share of adults infected |

**HIV/AIDS** was the greatest social challenge. By the second half of the 1990s Zimbabwe had one of the highest rates of infection in the world; estimates suggest that roughly a quarter of adults were infected in the worst years. The epidemic reduced life expectancy, left many orphans, strained hospitals and removed teachers, nurses and farm workers from the workforce. **ESAP** brought fees for clinics and schools, cuts in real spending, and the departure of experienced nurses and teachers for better pay. **Unemployment** and poverty grew in towns. Some of these problems were beyond any government's control; others, critics argue, resulted from policy choices.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nothing was done before 1980"}
Mission and government schools and clinics existed, but they were unequal and racially divided. The point is to compare the pattern of access, not to say that there was no service.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only achievements"}
Examiners expect balance. Add quality problems, shortages, unemployment, the effect of ESAP fees and HIV/AIDS.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing statistics"}
Say "enrolment grew many times over" unless you are certain of the exact number.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After 1980 racial barriers were removed, primary fees were abolished and education expanded greatly at every level; literacy became among the highest in Africa.
- The primary health care policy built rural clinics, trained village health workers and began national immunisation, improving child health.
- Women gained adult legal status in 1982 and further rights in marriage law; housing and unemployment remained difficult.
- From the 1990s user fees under ESAP, falling real spending and HIV/AIDS reversed some gains.
- A balanced answer weighs real achievements against quality problems, cost and disease.
:::
