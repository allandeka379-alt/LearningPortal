## Introduction

Independence on 18 April 1980 ended minority rule but opened new questions. How could a country that had just fought a long war, with a white minority that still owned much of the best land and an armed movement divided into rival parties, be governed in peace? The new government promised reconciliation. In the 1980s that promise was tested by the integration of three armies, by rivalry between ZANU (PF) and PF-ZAPU, and by violence in Matabeleland and the Midlands that left a lasting mark. The Unity Accord of 1987 brought the two parties together and ended the worst of the violence. Later in the 1990s, new challenges to the ruling party appeared.

This lesson covers political developments from 1980 to 2000 in order. Economic and social developments, land reform and foreign policy are in other lessons. The disturbances of the 1980s are painful and still debated; this lesson states what is widely accepted and notes where accounts differ. You should know about the Lancaster House settlement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reconciliation"}
The policy of the new government, announced by Robert Mugabe on 4 March 1980, to build a nation by forgiving the past and including all races and parties in the new state.
:::

:::definition{term="Gukurahundi"}
A Shona word meaning the early rain that washes away the chaff before the spring rains. It is the name that is used for the military operations in Matabeleland and the Midlands in the 1980s, and for the period of violence.
:::

:::definition{term="Unity Accord"}
The agreement of 22 December 1987 by which ZANU (PF) and PF-ZAPU merged to form a single party, ZANU (PF), under Robert Mugabe, with Joshua Nkomo later one of two vice-presidents.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 4 March 1980: Mugabe sworn in as prime minister; reconciliation speech.
- November 1980 and February 1981: clashes between ZIPRA and ZANLA units at Entumbane.
- February 1982: Nkomo dismissed from the cabinet; arms found on PF-ZAPU properties.
- January 1983: Fifth Brigade deployed in Matabeleland North. 1982-87: unrest and violence.
- 22 December 1987: Unity Accord. 31 December 1987: Mugabe becomes executive president.
- 1990: elections and a state of emergency lifted; 1999: MDC formed.
:::

## The government of national unity and reconciliation

Mugabe formed a government that included Joshua Nkomo and other PF-ZAPU members as ministers (Nkomo became Minister of Home Affairs), and two white ministers (for agriculture and for trade and industry). In his first address as prime minister Mugabe urged Zimbabweans to forget the past and to build a new nation. The government kept white civil servants, judges and many officers in their posts, retained the existing public service, and accepted the Lancaster House terms on land and property. Reconciliation helped to keep white farmers, industries and skills in the country and prevented a collapse of the economy, and it was widely praised abroad. Critics at home argued that it protected the old economic order and delayed land redistribution, and that it did less for the victims of the war.

## Integration of the armies

At independence there were three armies: ZANLA, ZIPRA and the former Rhodesian forces. A British military training team helped to merge them into a new Zimbabwe National Army. Integration was difficult, since ZANLA and ZIPRA fighters distrusted each other. In November 1980 and February 1981, fighting broke out between ZIPRA and ZANLA units at Entumbane, near Bulawayo, and elsewhere, leaving many dead. Tension between the parties grew, and some former ZIPRA soldiers deserted and returned to the bush as "dissidents".

## Dissidents, Nkomo's dismissal and the Fifth Brigade

In early 1982 the government announced the discovery of large arms caches on farms owned by PF-ZAPU, and said that they showed a plot against the state. In February 1982 Mugabe dismissed Nkomo and other PF-ZAPU ministers from the cabinet. PF-ZAPU leaders denied planning a rebellion. In the following years armed dissident groups, some of them former ZIPRA fighters, attacked farms, buses and villages in Matabeleland, and some, according to reports, received support or training from South Africa. In January 1983 the government deployed the Fifth Brigade, an army unit trained by North Korean instructors and loyal to the ruling party, to Matabeleland North, and later to Matabeleland South.

Reports from the Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace and others recorded killings, beatings, detentions and the destruction of homes by the army, and food restrictions in some areas, as well as killings by dissidents. Estimates of the number of civilians killed, such as the figure of about 20,000 given in a 1997 report by the Commission, are disputed and the true number is not known. The government at the time described the operations as a response to dissident violence; later official statements have acknowledged that wrongs were done and called for healing. The Constitution of 2013 provided for a National Peace and Reconciliation Commission, and the events remain a subject of public debate, with many survivors and families asking for acknowledgement, burials and information.

:::example{title="Presenting a sensitive topic fairly"}
1. State what happened: the arms discoveries, Nkomo's dismissal, dissident attacks, the Fifth Brigade's operations and the reports of civilian deaths.
2. State the government's account: a security response to armed dissidents.
3. State the other accounts: reports by the Catholic Commission and survivors of widespread abuses against civilians.
4. Say clearly that numbers are estimates and are disputed.
5. Avoid naming blame for individuals you cannot prove, and avoid graphic detail. This is the approach examiners reward.
:::

## The Unity Accord

By 1987 both parties had reason to seek agreement. The violence had cost lives and harmed the economy; PF-ZAPU had been weakened and Nkomo had been in talks with Mugabe since 1985. The parties signed the **Unity Accord** on 22 December 1987. ZANU (PF) and PF-ZAPU merged into one party, ZANU (PF), with Mugabe as president and provision for two vice-presidents, Simon Muzenda and Joshua Nkomo (Nkomo took the post in 1990); the dissidents were offered an amnesty in 1988; the parties agreed to work towards a united, non-racial Zimbabwe. A constitutional amendment of 1987 also created an executive presidency, with Mugabe as the first executive president from 31 December 1987; the 20 reserved white seats had been abolished earlier in the same year. The Accord ended the dissident conflict, but it created a dominant-party state in which opposition was weak, and many Matabeleland people felt that their grievances had not been addressed.

## Developments to 2000

- **1990 elections and the one-party question.** ZANU (PF) won overwhelmingly; Edgar Tekere's Zimbabwe Unity Movement was the main opponent. Some party leaders proposed a one-party state, but the party rejected the idea in 1991, and the state of emergency in force since 1965 was ended in 1990.
- **Elections of 1995 and 1996.** ZANU (PF) won most seats in 1995, and Mugabe won the presidency in 1996 after rival candidates withdrew.
- **Pressure from new groups.** In the 1990s trade unions, students and civic groups criticised the government over economic hardship, corruption and the slow pace of land reform; war veterans were granted compensation in 1997. From 1997 the National Constitutional Assembly campaigned for a new constitution.
- **1999-2000.** The Movement for Democratic Change (MDC) was formed in 1999 under Morgan Tsvangirai, from the trade union movement and civic groups. In February 2000 voters rejected a government-backed draft constitution in a referendum, the first major defeat for the ruling party, which led into the events of 2000 described in the lessons on land and the Constitution.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the date of the Accord and the dismissal"}
Nkomo was dismissed in February 1982; the Unity Accord was signed on 22 December 1987.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using precise casualty figures or one-sided blame"}
State estimates as estimates, give the main accounts, and avoid blaming or praising individuals.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the post-Accord changes"}
The Accord led to a merged party and an executive presidency, and to a dominant-party system. Include these when asked about its results.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In 1980 Mugabe's government of national unity included PF-ZAPU and white ministers, and stressed reconciliation.
- Integration of the three armies was difficult; clashes at Entumbane (1980-81) and dissident activity followed.
- Nkomo was dismissed in February 1982; the Fifth Brigade's operations in Matabeleland and the Midlands (from 1983) caused many civilian deaths, which are estimates and remain debated.
- The Unity Accord of 22 December 1987 merged ZANU (PF) and PF-ZAPU, with Nkomo later vice-president, and was followed by an amnesty and an executive presidency.
- In the 1990s ZANU (PF) dominated, but new opposition grew, leading to the MDC (1999) and the referendum defeat of February 2000.
:::
