## Introduction

When Germany attacked the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941, it began the largest land war in history. For four years the Eastern Front tied down most of the German army and cost the Soviet Union more dead than any other country suffered in the Second World War. In the Soviet Union the war is called the Great Patriotic War, a name that deliberately recalled Russia's resistance to Napoleon in 1812. For ZIMSEC the questions are why the Soviet Union nearly collapsed in 1941, how it recovered, what role it played in the defeat of Germany and what the war did to Stalin's rule.

You should know about Stalin's rule in the 1930s (the purges and the Five Year Plans) and about Hitler's aims, which are covered in the lessons on Nazi Germany and the causes of the Second World War.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Operation Barbarossa"}
The German code name for the invasion of the Soviet Union that began on 22 June 1941, in which about three million German troops, with allies, attacked along a front of more than a thousand miles.
:::

:::definition{term="Scorched earth"}
A policy of destroying crops, factories, railways and supplies in an area to prevent an invader from using them. The Soviet Union applied it to the areas that it had to abandon in 1941.
:::

:::definition{term="Lend-Lease"}
The United States programme, begun in 1941, of supplying Britain, the Soviet Union and other allies with weapons, vehicles, food and raw materials without immediate payment.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 23 August 1939: Nazi-Soviet Pact. 22 June 1941: German invasion (Barbarossa). Autumn 1941: siege of Leningrad begins; December 1941: Soviet counter-attack at Moscow.
- August 1942 to 2 February 1943: Battle of Stalingrad. July 1943: Battle of Kursk. June 1944: Operation Bagration.
- 8 May 1945 (9 May in Moscow): German surrender; the Red Army had taken Berlin in the last days of April and early May.
- Marshal Georgy Zhukov was the most successful Soviet commander.
:::

## Why was the Soviet Union unprepared?

Stalin had signed the **Nazi-Soviet Pact** in 1939 to buy time and to gain territory (eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and land taken from Finland after the Winter War of 1939-40). He refused to believe warnings that Hitler would attack, from his own spies and from Britain, partly because he mistrusted the British and partly because he thought Hitler would not fight on two fronts. The Red Army had also been weakened by the purges of 1937-38, in which thousands of officers were removed, and it had done badly against Finland. Much of its air force was destroyed on the ground in the first days. The new frontier in Poland had few prepared defences, and the old fortified line behind it was partly dismantled.

## Disaster and survival, 1941

The Germans advanced in three army groups, towards Leningrad, Moscow and Ukraine. Within weeks they surrounded and captured hundreds of thousands of Soviet troops at Minsk and Smolensk, and by September they took Kiev with a very large number of prisoners. Millions of Soviet soldiers were captured in 1941, and a great many died in German captivity. Stalin appeared in a radio address on 3 July, called on the people to defend the motherland, and took over as supreme commander.

Several factors saved the country. Soviet factories were evacuated in a huge operation: more than a thousand industrial enterprises were moved east to the Urals and Siberia and restarted production within months. The vast distance, the autumn mud and the severe winter slowed the German army, which had not planned for a long war. The siege of **Leningrad** began in September and lasted until January 1944; historians estimate that around 800,000 or more civilians died, mostly of hunger. In December 1941 fresh Siberian divisions under Zhukov counter-attacked at Moscow and drove the Germans back. The German hope of a quick victory had failed.

## The turning point, 1942-43

In 1942 Germany attacked in the south, towards the Volga and the oil of the Caucasus. In July Stalin's Order No. 227, "Not one step back", demanded that no retreat be made without orders. At **Stalingrad** the Soviet 62nd Army fought a house-by-house battle in the ruins of the city. In November 1942 Soviet armies launched Operation Uranus and encircled the German Sixth Army; its remnants, about 90,000 men, surrendered on 2 February 1943, a heavy defeat for Germany. At **Kursk** in July 1943, in one of the largest tank battles in history, the Germans failed to break through, and from then on the Red Army was on the offensive. In 1944 Operation Bagration destroyed Germany's Army Group Centre, and the Red Army moved through Poland and the Balkans. It took Berlin in the spring of 1945.

:::example{title="Why was Stalingrad a turning point?"}
A strong paragraph gives evidence and a limit. Stalingrad was the first time that a whole German army was destroyed and forced to surrender; it ended German hopes in the south, boosted Soviet morale and showed that Hitler's army could be beaten. However, historians argue that Moscow in 1941 had already ended the chance of a quick victory, and Kursk in 1943 removed Germany's ability to attack again. A balanced judgement is that Stalingrad was the most dramatic turning point, but it was part of a series.
:::

## Reasons for Soviet victory

- **Resources and geography:** vast territory, a large population and the industry in the east, producing tanks (notably the T-34), aircraft and rockets in large numbers.
- **Leadership and organisation:** Stalin's control of the war economy and the growing skill of commanders such as Zhukov, as officers gained more freedom in 1942-43.
- **Patriotism and coercion:** many fought for the motherland, helped by an appeal to Russian history and by the Church's support from 1943; others obeyed because deserters and "traitors" were severely punished.
- **German mistakes and brutality:** Hitler's treatment of the occupied peoples and prisoners turned possible supporters into resisters, and his decisions over Stalingrad overstretched his army.
- **Allied help:** Lend-Lease trucks, food and raw materials mattered, though historians disagree about its weight. The bombing of Germany, the campaigns in Africa and Italy and the Normandy landings in June 1944 forced Germany to divide its forces.

## The cost of the war

Historians estimate that the Soviet Union lost about 25 to 27 million people, more than half of them civilians, in the war; the figure is uncertain and is widely accepted as an approximation. Soviet sources reported that some 1,700 towns and 70,000 villages were destroyed, and industry and farming were badly damaged. The Soviet state also deported whole peoples, such as the Volga Germans (1941), the Chechens and Ingush (1944) and the Crimean Tatars (1944), accusing them collectively of collaboration, an act later recognised as a grave injustice.

## The effect on Stalin's regime and society

The war strengthened Stalin's standing: victory gave him prestige at home, and the Soviet Union emerged as one of two superpowers, with control of Eastern Europe. During the war the regime made concessions, easing the pressure on the Church (the Patriarchate was restored in 1943), dissolving the Comintern in 1943 and appealing to nationalism. Many people hoped that the sacrifice would bring a more open society. Instead the regime tightened its grip after 1945. Soldiers and prisoners of war who had been in the West were suspected, and many were sent to camps; cultural control returned in 1946; and the cult of Stalin grew. Women, who had worked in factories and served as medics, snipers and pilots, lost many of the roles they had gained as men returned. The collective farms were left poor. In the longer term the war shaped the Cold War, which brought the Soviet Union into rivalry with the United States, and in Africa the Soviet Union later supported movements for independence.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving the Soviet Union all the credit, or none"}
The Red Army defeated the largest part of the German army, but the Allies' help, their campaigns in the west, and German mistakes also mattered. Balance is needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Stalin was prepared for the invasion"}
He ignored warnings and the army was weakened by the purges. Soviet success in 1942-45 came after the early disaster.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting casualty figures as exact"}
Soviet losses are estimates (about 25 to 27 million). Say so.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Soviet Union was unprepared in 1941 because Stalin ignored warnings, the Red Army was weakened by the purges, and the frontier had been moved forward after the Nazi-Soviet Pact.
- Early German victories captured vast territory and millions of prisoners, but distance, winter, the evacuation of industry and the defence of Leningrad and Moscow prevented a quick German victory.
- Stalingrad (surrender on 2 February 1943) and Kursk (July 1943) turned the war; the Red Army took Berlin in 1945.
- Victory came from resources, organisation, leadership, patriotism and coercion, German errors and Allied help.
- The Soviet Union lost about 25 to 27 million people (an estimate) and suffered huge damage; Stalin emerged stronger and the repression resumed after 1945.
:::
