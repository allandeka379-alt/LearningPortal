## Introduction

Seizing the Winter Palace was easy; keeping Russia was not. In November 1917 the Bolsheviks controlled little more than Petrograd and Moscow, they had no experience of government, and they faced a hostile Europe, a collapsing economy and an unelected claim to rule. Within seven years they had survived a civil war, built a one-party state and adapted their economic policy twice. This lesson asks how they did it and what it cost, and how far the decisions of 1917-24 shaped the Soviet Union that Stalin later ruled. From now on the Western calendar is used throughout, because Russia adopted it on 14 February 1918.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="War Communism"}
The economic policy of 1918-21 in which the state took over industry, requisitioned grain from the peasants, banned private trade and conscripted labour in order to supply the Red Army and the towns during the Civil War.
:::

:::definition{term="Cheka"}
The All-Russian Extraordinary Commission, the Bolshevik secret police founded in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky, which arrested and executed opponents. The Red Terror from September 1918 was carried out largely by the Cheka.
:::

:::definition{term="New Economic Policy (NEP)"}
The policy introduced in March 1921 which ended grain requisitioning, allowed peasants to sell surplus grain and small traders and businesses to operate for profit, while the state kept control of heavy industry, banks and foreign trade.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}

:::

## Establishing the dictatorship

The new government issued decrees designed to win support: the **Decree on Land** (private ownership of land abolished and the land handed over to the peasants' committees, in effect confirming what the peasants had already begun to do), workers' control of factories, and the nationalisation of banks. A **Decree on the Press** closed hostile newspapers. The Church was separated from the state in January 1918, and women gained equal civil rights.

The **Constituent Assembly**, elected in November 1917, had been the demand of every revolutionary party. The Social Revolutionaries won the largest share (about 40 per cent of the vote) and the Bolsheviks about a quarter. When the Assembly met in January 1918 it refused to accept Soviet power, and the Bolsheviks closed it after a single sitting. In this way they chose rule through the soviets, which they controlled, over rule through an elected parliament. The Cheka, founded in December, hunted opponents; the Kadets were banned, and the other parties were gradually pushed out of political life. In March 1921 the Tenth Party Congress banned factions inside the Bolshevik (Communist) Party itself.

## The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk

Peace was Lenin's main promise. After an armistice, German terms at Brest-Litovsk were harsh. Trotsky tried "no war, no peace", and Germany resumed its advance in February 1918. Lenin persuaded a reluctant Central Committee to sign, because the army could not fight and the revolution needed a breathing space. By the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** (3 March 1918) Russia gave up Poland, the Baltic provinces, Finland and Ukraine, and with them about a third of its population and much of its industry and coal. Opponents, including the Left Social Revolutionaries, who then left the government, called it a betrayal. Lenin moved the capital to Moscow. The treaty lost its force when Germany was defeated in November 1918.

:::example{title="Was Brest-Litovsk a defeat or a success for Lenin?"}
A balanced paragraph can argue both sides. It was a heavy loss of land and resources and it brought opposition from within the party. But it ended Russia's war, kept the Bolsheviks in power, fulfilled the promise of peace and allowed Lenin to build the Red Army. Since it was annulled eight months later, its price turned out to be shorter-lived than it had seemed. A judgement: a humiliating but necessary choice that saved the regime.
:::

## The Civil War, 1918-1921

Opposition to the Bolsheviks came from many sides, known together as the **Whites**: officers, monarchists, liberals and moderate socialists. The main White armies were led by Admiral Kolchak in Siberia, General Denikin in the south, General Yudenich near Petrograd and General Wrangel in the Crimea. The Czech Legion rose in May 1918. Britain, France, the United States and Japan sent troops or supplies to northern Russia and the Far East, partly to stop German use of supplies and partly to help the Whites. The war was fought with great cruelty by both sides. After the murder of the Bolshevik Uritsky and an attempt on Lenin's life in August 1918, the Cheka launched the **Red Terror**, in which tens of thousands were killed (estimates vary widely). In July 1918 the Bolsheviks killed the Tsar and his family at Ekaterinburg. Historians estimate that the war, with the famine and disease that went with it, killed several million people.

**Why did the Reds win?** The reasons are usually grouped as follows.

- **Geography and resources.** The Reds held the centre (Moscow and Petrograd), the railway network and most of the arms factories, and could move troops between fronts. The Whites were scattered around the edges and could not co-ordinate.
- **Leadership.** Trotsky as Commissar for War built the Red Army by conscription, used former Tsarist officers under the watch of political commissars, and was a forceful organiser. The Whites had capable generals but disagreed on aims.
- **Unity of purpose.** The Whites included monarchists, liberals and socialists who agreed only that they opposed the Bolsheviks. They had no clear policy on land, and peasants feared that they would restore the landlords.
- **War Communism.** It supplied the army and the towns, however harshly.
- **Propaganda and the national cause.** The Bolsheviks presented themselves as defenders of Russia against foreign intervention, while the Whites, who relied on foreign help, looked like tools of foreign powers. The intervention was in fact limited, and many historians think it did the Whites more harm than good.

## War Communism

From mid-1918 the state nationalised all large and then most small industry, took over trade, rationed food and clothing, introduced labour discipline and sent detachments to **requisition grain** from peasants by force. It saved the army, but the cost was very high. Peasants planted less or hid grain, industrial production fell far below pre-war levels, towns lost population as people went to the countryside for food, and the black market grew. A severe famine in 1921-22, worsened by drought, killed millions (estimates vary).

## Kronstadt and the New Economic Policy

By early 1921 there were peasant risings (notably in Tambov province) and strikes in Petrograd. In March 1921 the sailors of the Kronstadt naval base, who had been strong supporters of the Bolsheviks in 1917, rose in revolt. They demanded fresh elections to the soviets, freedom of speech and of the press for workers and socialist parties, and an end to grain requisitioning. The government called the rising a counter-revolutionary plot. After a bitter assault across the ice (17-18 March) the Red Army crushed it; hundreds were killed in the fighting and many more executed or imprisoned.

The shock persuaded Lenin to retreat. The Tenth Party Congress introduced the **NEP** in March 1921: requisitioning was replaced by a fixed tax on grain, peasants could sell the rest, small businesses and trade were legal again, and the state kept the "commanding heights" of heavy industry, banks and foreign trade. Food output and trade recovered; by the mid-1920s farm and industrial output had come back close to the pre-war level. The "Nepmen" and richer peasants prospered. Many communists disliked it as a retreat from socialism, and in 1923 the "scissors crisis" (industrial prices high and grain prices low) showed its strains. Lenin called it a temporary retreat. After he suffered strokes from 1922 he died on 21 January 1924.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the Civil War as Reds versus one White army"}
The Whites were several separate forces without a common command or policy; that division helps to explain their defeat.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing War Communism and NEP"}
War Communism (1918-21) was forced requisitioning and state control; NEP (from 1921) was the retreat to a tax in kind and private trade.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Bolsheviks built a one-party dictatorship by closing the Constituent Assembly (January 1918), creating the Cheka and banning other parties.
- Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918) ended the war with Germany at a heavy price in land and resources, but kept the regime in power.
- The Reds won the Civil War through control of the centre, Trotsky's Red Army, White disunity and War Communism, with the Red Terror as a weapon.
- War Communism supplied the army but led to economic collapse, peasant resistance and famine.
- Kronstadt (March 1921) persuaded Lenin to adopt NEP, a temporary retreat that revived the economy while the party kept political control.
:::
