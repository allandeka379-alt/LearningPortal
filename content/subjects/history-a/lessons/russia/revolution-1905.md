## Introduction

On a Sunday in January 1905 thousands of working people walked through St Petersburg to ask their Tsar for help, carrying icons and singing hymns. Soldiers fired on them. Within a year Russia had seen strikes, mutinies, peasant risings, a general strike, the creation of workers' councils and a promise of a parliament. Yet Nicholas II remained on his throne, and nine years later he took Russia into a world war. Lenin later called 1905 a rehearsal for 1917, but it also shows how a regime can survive a crisis, so a good answer explains both the causes of the revolt and the reasons it failed.

You should know the structure of the autocracy and the problems of Alexander III's reign from the previous lesson. Dates here follow the Western calendar; the old Russian calendar was thirteen days behind (so Bloody Sunday, 22 January, was 9 January in Russia).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soviet"}
A council. In 1905 workers in St Petersburg and other cities elected soviets to lead strikes; the idea returned in 1917.
:::

:::definition{term="October Manifesto"}
The Tsar's declaration of 30 October 1905 (17 October in the old calendar) promising civil liberties, an elected Duma with a share in law-making, and a wider franchise.
:::

:::definition{term="Duma"}
The elected lower house of the Russian parliament from 1906. The Tsar could dissolve it, and it did not control the ministers.
:::

:::definition{term="Fundamental Laws"}
The constitution issued in April 1906 that set limits on the Duma and kept the Tsar's powers over the army, foreign policy and the appointment of ministers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- February 1904: war with Japan begins; 5 September 1905: Treaty of Portsmouth.
- 22 January 1905: Bloody Sunday, led by Father Gapon. 30 October 1905: the October Manifesto (Witte).
- April 1906: Fundamental Laws and First Duma. Stolypin, prime minister 1906-11, murdered September 1911.
:::

## Causes of the 1905 Revolution

**Long-term discontent.** Peasants were short of land; workers had long hours and no unions; educated people wanted a constitution; and the minorities resented Russification. Nicholas II (from 1894) rejected calls for reform.

**Opposition organised.** In the early 1900s opposition parties formed. The Social Revolutionaries (about 1901-02) looked to the peasants and used terror, killing ministers such as Plehve in 1904. Liberals formed the Union of Liberation (1903-04) and, in the winter of 1904, held a "banquet campaign" calling for a constitution. The Social Democrats, the Marxist party, split in 1903 into the Bolsheviks under Lenin and the Mensheviks.

**Economic problems.** An industrial depression from 1900 caused unemployment, and poor harvests in some years brought hardship. Food and housing were costly in the cities. In December 1904 the dismissal of workers at the Putilov engineering works led to a strike in St Petersburg.

**Defeat in war.** The Russo-Japanese War began in February 1904 as a contest over Manchuria and Korea. Russia expected a quick victory. Instead Japan took Port Arthur (January 1905), won the land battle of Mukden (February-March 1905) and destroyed the Russian Baltic Fleet at Tsushima (May 1905). Defeat exposed the incompetence of the regime, ruined confidence in the Tsar and was a trigger of the unrest.

:::example{title="Long-term causes, triggers and the crisis"}
A candidate who wants a high-level answer to "Why did revolution break out in 1905?" should separate three layers. The deep causes were land hunger, the lack of political rights and the regime's refusal to reform. The conditions that raised the temperature were depression, the growth of organised opposition and the war. The immediate trigger was Bloody Sunday, because the massacre turned loyal petitioners into enemies of the Tsar. A strong answer argues which layer mattered most: without the deep causes the war would have caused only a crisis of government, but without the war the discontent might not have exploded in 1905.
:::

## Events of 1905

**Bloody Sunday.** Father Georgy Gapon led a workers' organisation, which the police had tolerated, and after the Putilov strike he organised a procession to the Winter Palace on 22 January 1905 to present a petition. It asked for an eight-hour day, a constituent assembly and civil liberties. Troops fired on the crowds. The official figure of about 130 dead is now regarded as too low by many historians, though numbers remain uncertain. The Tsar was not in the city. The massacre destroyed the popular image of the "Little Father".

**The spread of unrest.** Strikes spread across the empire; peasants seized land and burned manor houses; students and professionals formed unions; in the non-Russian regions national movements rose. In June sailors on the battleship *Potemkin* mutinied, although the Black Sea Fleet did not follow them.

**October.** Railway workers struck in early October and the strike spread to a general strike that stopped the country. The St Petersburg Soviet was formed to direct the strike, with Leon Trotsky among its leaders. The Tsar was advised to choose between repression and concession. His minister Sergei Witte, who had just negotiated the Treaty of Portsmouth, urged a manifesto. On 30 October the Tsar issued the **October Manifesto**.

**The end of the crisis.** The Manifesto split the opposition. Moderate liberals who were satisfied formed the Octobrist party. The Kadets (Constitutional Democrats) wanted more. The Soviet was arrested in December, and an armed rising in Moscow was crushed by the army. By early 1906 the revolutionary wave had ended.

## Why did the regime survive?

Historians list several reasons. **The army stayed loyal** in the main, and troops brought back from Manchuria after the peace with Japan were used against the rebels. **Concessions divided the opposition**: liberals accepted the Manifesto, workers and peasants were separated, and the Octobrists and Kadets disagreed. **The opposition lacked unity and leadership**: strikes, peasant risings and mutinies were not coordinated, and the leaders of the main socialist parties were in exile or arrested. **Foreign loans** (a large French loan in 1906) helped the government to pay its troops and its debts. And **repression worked**: courts-martial and executions of rebels, especially under Stolypin's regime, and the arrest of the Soviet.

## Change to 1914

**The Duma and the Fundamental Laws.** The first two Dumas were hostile and were dissolved (in July 1906 and June 1907). The Tsar then changed the electoral law, which gave landowners and the rich most of the weight, and the Third Duma (1907-12) completed its term and the Fourth met in 1912. The Fundamental Laws kept the Tsar's powers over ministers, the army and foreign affairs, gave him the right to issue emergency decrees and a veto. Tsarism changed in form, but not in substance.

**Stolypin.** Pyotr Stolypin was prime minister from 1906 until he was shot in September 1911. He used harsh measures to suppress unrest (the courts-martial of 1906-07, and the hangings known as "Stolypin's neckties") and also tried to win the peasants over. His land reforms allowed peasants to leave the commune and consolidate their holdings, with loans from the Peasants' Land Bank, to create a class of prosperous independent farmers, and encouraged settlement in Siberia. A substantial minority of households left the commune before 1914, but most peasants did not, and Stolypin himself said that the reforms needed 20 years of peace.

**Industry and society.** Industrial output grew strongly between 1909 and 1914. Trade unions and a freer press were allowed in a limited way. But strikes revived from 1912, after troops shot many workers at the Lena goldfields in Siberia, and by the summer of 1914 there were large strikes in St Petersburg. From about 1912 the influence of Rasputin over the Empress harmed the court's reputation.

:::example{title="Judging the extent of change"}
A balanced paragraph can set out three points. Politically, the Duma and the freer press were real changes, but the Tsar kept control of ministers, the army and the power to dissolve the Duma, so Russia was not a constitutional monarchy. Economically, growth and Stolypin's land reforms offered hope, but the reforms reached only part of the peasantry, and strikes revived. Socially, repression and discontent continued. The best judgement is that Russia changed in form more than in fact: the regime was stronger than in 1905, but the basic causes of discontent remained.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the October Manifesto with the October Revolution"}
The Manifesto was the Tsar's concession in 1905. The October Revolution was the Bolshevik seizure of power in 1917.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The 1905 Revolution grew from long-term discontent, organised opposition, economic depression and defeat by Japan, and was triggered by Bloody Sunday (22 January 1905).
- Strikes, peasant risings, mutinies and the general strike of October led to the October Manifesto (30 October 1905) and the St Petersburg Soviet.
- The regime survived because the army stayed mostly loyal, concessions divided the opposition, the rebels were not united, foreign loans helped, and repression was effective.
- The Fundamental Laws (1906) and a manipulated franchise kept the Tsar's power largely intact; the Duma gave a limited voice.
- Stolypin combined repression with land reform, and industry grew, but strikes revived by 1914.
- Russia changed more in form than in substance, so the causes of 1905 remained unresolved.
:::
