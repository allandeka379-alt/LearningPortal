## Introduction

In March 1917 a dynasty that had ruled Russia for over three hundred years fell in less than a week. Eight months later a small party, little known in the spring, took power in the capital and began to build the world's first communist state. The events of 1917 reached Africa too, because the Soviet Union later gave training and weapons to liberation movements in southern Africa, among them ZAPU. This lesson asks why the Tsar fell, why the liberal Provisional Government failed, and why the Bolsheviks won.

You should know the problems of the regime before 1914 from the lessons on tsarist Russia and the 1905 Revolution. **Dates:** Russia used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind the Western calendar. Dates here are Western (new style) with the Russian date noted, which is why the "February" and "October" revolutions fall in March and November.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Dual power"}
The situation from March to October 1917 in which two authorities existed: the Provisional Government (formed by the Duma) and the Petrograd Soviet (elected by workers and soldiers). The soldiers obeyed the Soviet's Order No. 1, so the Government could not command them on its own.
:::

:::definition{term="Provisional Government"}
The government set up on 15 March 1917 by members of the Duma, to rule until a Constituent Assembly could be elected. Its first head was Prince Georgy Lvov; Alexander Kerensky led it from July.
:::

:::definition{term="April Theses"}
Lenin's programme on his return in April 1917: no support for the Provisional Government, "all power to the Soviets", an end to the war, land to the peasants. Its slogan was "Peace, Land and Bread".
:::

:::definition{term="Military Revolutionary Committee (MRC)"}
A body set up by the Petrograd Soviet in October 1917, chaired by Trotsky, which organised the armed seizure of the capital for the Bolsheviks.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 8 March 1917 (23 February old style): women strike in Petrograd. 15 March: the Tsar abdicates.
- 16 April 1917: Lenin returns; April Theses. 16-17 July: the July Days. Early September (late August old style): the Kornilov affair.
- 7 November 1917 (25 October old style): Bolsheviks seize power; Sovnarkom formed with Lenin as chairman.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

ZIMSEC treats February and October 1917 as separate stages in the revolution (causes, events, results). Both boards expect dates with the calendar noted.
:::

## The impact of the First World War

The war began with patriotic enthusiasm, but it soon exposed the regime's weakness. The army lost heavily at Tannenberg in 1914 and retreated from Poland in 1915, with serious shortages of shells and rifles. Historians estimate that Russia suffered millions of casualties by 1917. In September 1915 the Tsar took personal command of the army, which tied his name to its defeats and left the government in the hands of the Empress Alexandra and her adviser Rasputin, who were unpopular. 

The home front suffered too. Millions of peasants had been called up, so farm output fell; the railways, overloaded by military traffic, could not bring enough grain and fuel to the towns; and prices rose faster than wages. In Petrograd (the capital's name from 1914) bread queues grew in the winter of 1916-17. The Duma's Progressive Bloc (1915) asked for a government that the country trusted, but the Tsar refused, which alienated the liberals and even some generals.

## The February (March) Revolution

On 8 March 1917 (23 February in Russia), International Women's Day, women textile workers in Petrograd marched for bread and were joined by men from the factories. Within three days the strike became general. The Tsar, at army headquarters, ordered the troops to restore order. On 11 March soldiers fired on demonstrators, but the next day the Volynsky regiment and others refused to fire and joined the crowd. The Duma formed a committee, the workers and soldiers formed the **Petrograd Soviet**, and on 15 March, advised by his generals, Nicholas II abdicated. His brother Michael declined the throne the next day, and the Romanov monarchy ended. No party led it: it was a revolt of hungry workers and tired soldiers that the army would not suppress.

:::example{title="Why did the Tsar fall in 1917 and not in 1905?"}
A strong answer compares. In 1905 the army stayed loyal, the war was nearly over and the opposition was divided. In 1917 the war had broken the economy and the army's loyalty, the liberals and the generals no longer supported the Tsar, and the soldiers in the capital were peasants in uniform who sympathised with the workers. The decisive difference was the army: in 1905 it saved the regime; in 1917 it deserted it.
:::

## The Provisional Government and its failure

The Provisional Government promised civil liberties, an amnesty and elections to a Constituent Assembly. It shared power with the Petrograd Soviet in a system of dual power, and its authority depended on the Soviet's cooperation. Its fatal problems were these:

- **The war.** The government kept Russia in the war to honour its alliances. When Foreign Minister Milyukov restated Russia's war aims in a note to the Allies, there were demonstrations and he resigned. The "Kerensky offensive" in the summer failed, and the army began to disintegrate.
- **Land.** Peasants wanted the landowners' estates. The government said that land reform must wait for the Constituent Assembly, and from the summer peasants began to seize land themselves.
- **Weak leadership.** Kerensky, who led the government from July, lost the confidence of both left and right.

Lenin returned to Petrograd on 16 April (3 April old style), after Germany allowed him to cross its territory by train because it hoped he would weaken Russia's war effort. His April Theses rejected cooperation with the Government. The Bolsheviks gained members and support in the Soviets by promising "Peace, Land and Bread". In the **July Days** (16-17 July) soldiers, workers and Kronstadt sailors demonstrated in Petrograd against the war and the Government. The Bolsheviks supported but did not plan the rising; the Government suppressed it, accused Lenin of being a German agent, drove him into hiding in Finland and arrested Trotsky.

The **Kornilov affair** changed everything. In late August (September in the Western calendar) General Lavr Kornilov, the commander-in-chief, moved troops towards Petrograd. Historians disagree about whether he meant to seize power or had been misled about Kerensky's wishes; Kerensky, in fear, armed the workers' Red Guards and released Bolsheviks from prison. Railway workers stopped Kornilov's troops, and the revolt collapsed. Kerensky was now distrusted by officers and the left alike, and the Bolsheviks regained credit as defenders of the revolution. By September they had a majority in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets, and in early October Trotsky became chairman of the Petrograd Soviet.

## The October (November) Revolution

Lenin, writing from Finland, pressed his party to act before the Constituent Assembly could meet. On 23 October (10 October old style) the Bolshevik Central Committee voted for an armed rising, over the objections of Zinoviev and Kamenev, who thought it premature. Trotsky's Military Revolutionary Committee prepared the seizure of key points while appearing to defend the Soviet. On 6 November (24 October) Kerensky tried to close down Bolshevik newspapers and moved troops, which gave the Bolsheviks a reason to strike. Red Guards, soldiers and sailors occupied bridges, stations and the telephone exchange, and on the night of 7-8 November (25-26 October) they took the Winter Palace, where the ministers were meeting; Kerensky had left to find troops. Very few people were killed. The Second Congress of Soviets, which opened on 7 November, approved the seizure after the Mensheviks and Social Revolutionaries walked out in protest. It created the first Bolshevik government, the **Council of People's Commissars (Sovnarkom)**, with Lenin as chairman, and issued decrees on peace and on land.

## Why did the Bolsheviks win?

Explanations differ in emphasis. **Weakness of the Provisional Government**: it kept the war going, delayed land reform and lost the army. **Lenin's leadership**: clear slogans and the decision to act. **Trotsky's organisation**: the MRC and the Red Guards. **Party discipline**, compared with the divided Mensheviks and Social Revolutionaries. **Popular support**: majorities in the soviets of the main cities. Historians differ on the character of October. Some Western historians call it a coup by a minority party, helped by chaos; Soviet historians presented it as an inevitable popular revolution. Many since the 1970s stress both: a well-organised party took power in a city where its slogans had mass support, though the rest of the country was still to be won.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing the dates"}
The "February Revolution" happened in March (Western calendar) and the "October Revolution" in November. Say which calendar you use.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The First World War brought defeat, shortages and the loss of the army's loyalty, and the Tsar's personal command and Rasputin's influence discredited the dynasty.
- The February (March) Revolution began with bread strikes on 8 March 1917; the soldiers joined the crowds and Nicholas II abdicated on 15 March.
- Dual power (the Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet) left authority divided; the Government kept the war going and delayed land reform.
- Lenin's April Theses ("Peace, Land and Bread"), the July Days and the Kornilov affair shaped the Bolsheviks' rise.
- On 7-8 November (25-26 October) the Bolsheviks seized the capital through the Military Revolutionary Committee and formed Sovnarkom.
- Historians differ on whether October was a coup or a popular revolution; most now stress both the Bolsheviks' organisation and the weakness of the Government.
:::
