## Introduction

In 1881 the Russian Empire stretched from the Baltic to the Pacific, and fewer than one in four of its people could read. It was ruled by one man, the Tsar, who answered to no parliament, and it was about to be tested by a rapid but uneven economic change. Within 36 years that regime would be gone. Historians still argue whether the Romanov monarchy was already doomed in 1881 or whether it was brought down later by war and by its own mistakes; the first step in judging is to understand the system that Alexander III inherited and the challenges that it faced.

This lesson explains how Russia was governed, what Alexander III did after his father's murder, how the economy and society changed, and how opposition took shape. The Russian Revolution as a whole is covered in the next lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Autocracy"}
Rule by one person whose power is not limited by law, parliament or any other body. The Fundamental Laws of the Empire described the Tsar as an autocrat whose authority came from God.
:::

:::definition{term="Russification"}
A policy of making the non-Russian peoples of the empire (Poles, Ukrainians, Finns, Baltic Germans, Jews, Muslims and others) use the Russian language and adopt Russian institutions and, often, the Orthodox faith.
:::

:::definition{term="Populism (Narodnik)"}
A movement of educated Russians who believed that the peasant commune could lead Russia to a just society without capitalism. Some Populists chose terrorism; the group called the People's Will murdered Alexander II.
:::

:::definition{term="Marxism"}
The theory of Karl Marx that the industrial working class (the proletariat) would overthrow capitalism. In Russia it grew from the 1880s as factories grew.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 13 March 1881 (1 March in the old Russian calendar): Alexander II assassinated by the People's Will; Alexander III becomes Tsar (reigned to 1894).
- 1881: the Statute on Measures to Preserve State Order. 1882: the May Laws against Jews. 1883: Plekhanov founds Emancipation of Labour.
- 1887: Alexander Ulyanov, Lenin's elder brother, hanged for a plot against the Tsar. 1889: land captains. 1891-92: famine.
- 1 November 1894 (new style): Alexander III dies and Nicholas II succeeds him.
:::

## How Russia was ruled

The Tsar was the source of all law. Under the Fundamental Laws of 1832 he was described as an "autocratic and unlimited" monarch. Beneath him stood the **State Council** (an appointed body that reviewed draft laws and could only advise), the **Committee of Ministers** and the **ministries**, which each reported to the Tsar rather than to a prime minister, and the **Senate**, the highest court. In the provinces, governors ruled with the police. The rural councils called **zemstva** (created 1864) were dominated by landowners and dealt only with local roads, schools and clinics.

Two other institutions supported the throne. The **army** was the Tsar's main weapon in a country with poor communications, and the **Orthodox Church** taught obedience to the Tsar. Its chief lay official, the Procurator of the Holy Synod from 1880, was Konstantin Pobedonostsev, a close adviser to Alexander III who distrusted parliaments, a free press and religious toleration.

## From reform to repression

Alexander II (1855-81) ended serfdom in 1861 and made other reforms. His murder on 13 March 1881 convinced his son that reform had encouraged revolutionaries, not satisfied them. The new Tsar turned back.

**Police powers.** In August 1881 the Statute on Measures to Preserve State Order and Public Tranquillity allowed governors to declare an area in a state of emergency, ban meetings, close newspapers and send suspects into exile without trial. It was meant to be temporary but was renewed until 1917. The secret police, known as the Okhrana, watched suspected revolutionaries.

**Control of ideas.** Censorship was tightened in 1882, the University Statute of 1884 ended the universities' independence, and a circular of 1887 discouraged children of the poorer classes from entering secondary schools.

**Control of the countryside and the towns.** In 1889 **land captains**, appointed from the local nobility, were given judicial and administrative power over the peasants. In 1890 the zemstvo law gave the nobles an even larger majority in those councils, and in 1892 the town councils' franchise was narrowed.

**Russification.** Russian became the language of administration and, increasingly, of education in Poland and the Baltic provinces. Jews, who were confined mostly to the Pale of Settlement, suffered most. Waves of pogroms followed Alexander II's murder in 1881-82; the May Laws of 1882 restricted where Jews could live and work, and quotas in education followed. Other faiths also faced restrictions.

:::example{title="Weighing Alexander III's policy"}
A strong paragraph on "how successful was repression?" starts with a judgement and then supports it. In the short term repression worked: the People's Will was destroyed and organised terrorism faded for a time. Yet it did not solve the underlying problems. Russification embittered Poles, Finns and Jews, who were later to give the revolutionary movements many leaders and followers, and the exclusion of educated Russians from any role in government left them with nowhere to go but opposition. A balanced conclusion is that repression bought the regime time rather than security.
:::

## Economic development

The state, not private business, drove modernisation, because Russia needed railways, weapons and industry to remain a great power. Three finance ministers shaped policy.

- **Nikolai Bunge** (minister 1881-87) cut the peasants' redemption payments, founded the Peasants' Land Bank (1882) to help them buy land, and introduced factory inspectors and the first limits on child labour (1882) and on night work by women and children (1885).
- **Ivan Vyshnegradsky** (1887-92) raised taxes and tariffs and pushed grain exports to pay for imports and to balance the budget. 
- **Sergei Witte** (appointed 1892) took a more ambitious path with railways, a tariff wall, foreign loans (France was the main lender) and the gold standard. Construction of the Trans-Siberian Railway began in 1891.

Industry grew quickly in coal, iron and steel, oil at Baku and textiles, helped by foreign capital. Yet most of Russia remained poor and agricultural. In 1891-92 a harvest failure hit the Volga region and famine and epidemics followed; historians estimate that hundreds of thousands died. The government was criticised for being slow to act, and educated Russians organised relief through the zemstva and local committees, finding a public voice.

## Society

About four in five Russians were peasants. After 1861 many still paid redemption dues for land, most lived in communes (the *mir*) that redistributed strips of land, and population growth made plots smaller. The nobility declined in wealth, although nobles dominated the administration, and a small **middle class** of merchants and professionals grew in the towns. A new **industrial working class** lived in crowded barracks and rented rooms in St Petersburg, Moscow and the southern coalfields, worked long hours and had no legal right to form unions or to strike.

## The growth of opposition

**Populists.** The Populists had a long record from the 1870s. The People's Will turned to terrorism and killed Alexander II, but the police arrested most of its leaders and the peasants did not rise. In 1887 a small group of students, including Alexander Ulyanov, was arrested for a plot to kill the Tsar; he was hanged. The Social Revolutionary party grew from Populist ideas in 1901-02.

**Liberals.** Moderate nobles and professionals, many working through the zemstva, wanted a constitution and an elected assembly, but had no empire-wide organisation.

**Marxists.** George Plekhanov, living in exile, founded the Emancipation of Labour group in 1883 and argued that Russia must pass through capitalism to a socialist revolution led by workers. Marx's *Capital* had already been translated into Russian (1872). Study circles formed in St Petersburg and other cities and later reached workers. The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party was founded in 1898, and the revolutionaries who followed Lenin split from it in 1903.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Alexander II and Alexander III"}
Alexander II freed the serfs (1861) and was assassinated (1881). Alexander III (1881-94) reversed the trend with repression. Nicholas II (1894-1917) was the last Tsar.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Russia was an autocracy: the Tsar ruled with ministers, the State Council, the army, the police and the Orthodox Church, and without a parliament.
- After the murder of Alexander II in 1881, Alexander III reversed reform with emergency powers, censorship, land captains and Russification, and persecuted Jews and other minorities.
- Bunge, Vyshnegradsky and Witte drove industrial growth and railway building, at a heavy cost for peasants; the famine of 1891-92 exposed the weakness of the system.
- Most Russians were poor peasants; a small middle class and a growing working class had no political voice.
- Opposition survived underground: Populists, liberals, national minorities and, increasingly, Marxists such as Plekhanov.
- Repression gave the regime time but did not remove the causes of discontent.
:::
