## Introduction

The risings of 1896-97 were led by people whose authority came from different sources. Spirit mediums spoke for ancestors and gave a spiritual meaning to the struggle. Chiefs and headmen commanded followers and strongholds. Military leaders among the Ndebele organised the regiments that had survived the war of 1893-94. Women took part as mediums, as supporters and as fighters' families. Yet in the end the Company and its allies won. This lesson looks at the leaders and asks why they were defeated and why the risings were remembered.

You need the previous two lessons, on the causes and the course of the risings. A good answer to a question on this topic names specific leaders, says what each did, and weighs several reasons for defeat rather than listing them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Spirit medium"}
A person believed to be possessed by the spirit of a dead ancestor or of a guardian spirit of the land (*mhondoro* in Shona), who speaks with that spirit's voice and gives guidance to chiefs and people.
:::

:::definition{term="Mwari cult"}
The religion centred on the high god Mwari, with shrines in the Matopo Hills served by priests and messengers. It was respected by both Ndebele and Shona.
:::

:::definition{term="Divide and rule"}
The policy of using differences among rivals, such as chiefs who do not join a rising, in order to defeat them one at a time.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names to learn"}
- Nehanda (Nyakasikana): medium of the Mazowe region, Mashonaland.
- Kaguvi: medium linked with the spirit of Chaminuka, Mashonaland.
- Mkwati: priest of the Mwari cult who helped to spread the rising to Mashonaland.
- Mashayamombe, Makoni, Mangwende: Shona chiefs who led resistance.
- Somabulana and Sekombo: Ndebele leaders in the Matopos who took part in the peace talks.
:::

## Spirit mediums and religious leaders

**Nehanda.** The medium of the spirit Nehanda, who lived in the Mazowe area, was a woman of great influence among the Shona. She encouraged chiefs to resist, and she was captured in 1897 and executed in April 1898. According to tradition her personal name was Charwe, and she is remembered in independent Zimbabwe as *Mbuya* (grandmother) Nehanda. Traditions about her last words are uncertain.

**Kaguvi.** Kaguvi, said to be the medium of the spirit of Chaminuka, was based in central Mashonaland and called on chiefs to join. He worked with Nehanda, was captured in 1897 and was executed with her.

**Mkwati and the Mwari cult.** Mkwati, an official of the Mwari cult in Matabeleland, took part in the Ndebele rising and later went into Mashonaland, where he helped to spread the revolt and links between regions. The Mwari shrines gave the movement a spiritual centre that crossed ethnic lines. The priest known as the Mlimo, whom settlers regarded as the main Ndebele leader, was claimed by the scout Frederick Burnham to have been shot in June 1896, although historians dispute whom he killed.

**Why religion mattered.** The mediums gave a spiritual explanation for the sufferings of 1895-96 and told people that the ancestors had told them to resist. They had authority over chiefs who could not otherwise be commanded. They gave confidence and claimed that bullets would turn to water, a belief which could not survive repeated defeat. Their role was strongest where chiefs had been weakened by colonial rule.

## Chiefs and military leaders

Chiefs such as **Mashayamombe** in the Hartley area, **Makoni** in the east and **Mangwende** in Mrewa led communities in the Shona rising, used strongholds in hills and caves, and fought as long as they could. Among the Ndebele, indunas such as Somabulana and Sekombo directed the fighting in the Matopos. They tried to continue the war with the old regiments' leaders but had no king. The Shona fought district by district; each chief decided for himself when to join and when to stop.

## The part played by women

Women took part in several ways. Nehanda, as a medium, was one of the two most respected figures in the Shona rising, and she was a woman. Women prepared and carried food, passed messages, hid supplies and cared for the wounded. Women also suffered in the hunger, the burning of villages and the displacement that followed. The Company's treatment of Nehanda shows that the authorities saw women leaders as threats.

:::example{title="Evaluating the role of spirit mediums"}
**Strengths:** they united chiefs who might otherwise have acted separately; they supplied moral authority and an explanation of events; they were the voice of the ancestors on the land. **Weaknesses:** they had no military command; their claims, such as the promise that bullets would turn to water, failed when tested; and their capture removed the heart of the rising. A balanced answer says that they were essential to the unity and spirit of the risings but that unity and spirit were not enough to win against modern weapons.
:::

## Reasons for African defeat

1. **Weapons.** The Company's forces had Maxim guns and modern rifles, together with artillery and dynamite for the caves. Most African fighters used spears, axes and old muzzle-loaders, with some modern rifles.
2. **Lack of unity and central command.** There was no single leader for all the fighters. The Ndebele and Shona fought separately, and Shona chiefs fought in their own districts. When the Ndebele made peace at the indabas, the Company could turn its forces to Mashonaland.
3. **Famine and disease.** Rinderpest, drought and the loss of crops weakened the people. Fighting in the farming season meant that fields were not planted, and hunger forced many to make terms.
4. **Colonial reinforcements.** The Company brought in troops from the Cape, Natal and Britain and volunteers from the settlers, and used the new telegraph and wagons. It could replace men and supplies, which the fighters could not.
5. **Divide and rule.** Some chiefs did not join the risings, and the Company used African guides and police against those who did. It negotiated separately with the Ndebele.
6. **Tactics.** The Company's patrols destroyed crops and villages, which cut off food supplies, and they took strongholds one at a time with dynamite.

| Reason | Effect |
|---|---|
| Weapons | Modern arms and dynamite overcame strongholds |
| No central command | Chiefs were defeated one by one |
| Famine and disease | The people could not keep fighting |
| Reinforcements | The Company could replace losses |
| Divide and rule | African unity was broken |

## Why the risings were remembered

The risings did not restore independence, but they left a legacy. The executed mediums became martyrs, and their names were passed on in song, story and tradition. Writers and nationalists of the twentieth century called the war of liberation the Second Chimurenga, drawing a line from 1896. Some historians, such as Terence Ranger, stressed this continuity; others warn against reading later nationalism back into 1896 and point out that the earlier movement had its own causes and aims. In independent Zimbabwe, Nehanda and Kaguvi are honoured as national heroes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying defeat was caused by weapons only"}
Weapons mattered, but so did the lack of unity, hunger and divide and rule. Weigh them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying spirit mediums commanded the armies"}
They gave spiritual authority and called people to resist. Military leadership came from chiefs and indunas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the Ndebele"}
Give examples from both Matabeleland (Mkwati, Mwari cult, Somabulana, Sekombo) and Mashonaland (Nehanda, Kaguvi, Mashayamombe, Makoni).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nehanda, Kaguvi, Mkwati and the Mwari cult gave religious leadership; chiefs and indunas provided military leadership; women played many roles.
- The Africans were defeated by superior weapons, lack of central command, famine and disease, colonial reinforcements and divide and rule.
- Kaguvi and Nehanda were executed in 1898, and the risings did not restore independence.
- They were remembered as an inspiration for the later nationalists of the Second Chimurenga.
:::
