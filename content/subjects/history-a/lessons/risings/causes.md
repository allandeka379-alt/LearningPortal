## Introduction

In March 1896 Ndebele communities in the south-west of the country rose against the British South Africa Company. In June the Shona rose in Mashonaland. The two movements are called the **Umvukela** (Ndebele, "the rising") and the **First Chimurenga** (Shona, "the struggle"); together they are the first large-scale African resistance to colonial rule in Zimbabwe. Within a year thousands had died, many of them African fighters and civilians, and about 450 settlers also lost their lives, according to the usual estimates.

Why did people who had lived under the Company for only a few years take the risk of an uprising? This lesson explains the causes, the part of religious leaders, and the debate over how far the risings were planned together. The course of the fighting is in the next lesson, and the defeat of the risings in the lesson after it. You should know the occupation of 1890 and the war of 1893-94.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Umvukela"}
An Ndebele word meaning "the rising", used for the Ndebele rebellion of 1896. The Shona word *chimurenga* means a struggle or war of resistance.
:::

:::definition{term="Rinderpest"}
A deadly cattle disease that spread through southern Africa in the 1890s and killed a very large share of the cattle on which people depended for food, wealth and transport.
:::

:::definition{term="Native Police"}
African police recruited by the Company, under white officers, to keep order and collect taxes. Many people resented their conduct.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- December 1895 - January 1896: the Jameson Raid takes many Company police out of the country.
- March 1896: the Ndebele rise (the Umvukela).
- June 1896: the Shona rise in Mashonaland.
- Religious leaders: Mkwati and the Mwari cult (Matabeleland); Kaguvi and Nehanda (Mashonaland).
:::

## Causes in Matabeleland and Mashonaland

**1. Loss of land.** After 1893-94 settlers pegged farms on the best Ndebele land, and Africans were moved into reserves such as Gwaai and Shangani, which were poor and dry. In Mashonaland farms and mining claims were pegged around Salisbury, Hartley and Mazowe, and chiefs lost the use of their land and the right to say who might settle.

**2. Loss of cattle.** Cattle were wealth, the means of paying bridewealth and the link between families and ancestors. The Company and settlers took most of the Ndebele herds after the war. The Shona lost cattle through fines, seizures and disease.

**3. Forced labour.** The Company and settlers needed labourers for mines, farms and roads. Chiefs were pressed to provide workers, and men who refused were punished. Women and children also had to work. Conditions were harsh and pay was low.

**4. Taxation.** A hut tax and other taxes forced Africans to earn money. The only way to pay was often to work for settlers, so tax was a means of recruiting labour as well as raising revenue.

**5. The Native Police.** The police, who often came from other regions, were accused of cruelty, theft of cattle and the abuse of women. They were backed by the Company's authority, and the people had little means of complaint.

**6. Rinderpest, drought and locusts.** In 1895-96 the rains failed and locusts destroyed crops. Rinderpest reached the country in early 1896. To stop its spread the Company shot cattle, including healthy herds, which Africans saw as a new theft. Many people blamed the settlers for the disasters, and religious leaders said that the ancestors were angry and that the whites must be driven out.

**7. Loss of political independence.** The Ndebele had lost their king and state. In Mashonaland chiefs had lost power to Native Commissioners, who could dismiss and appoint them. Rulers who had been respected were now treated as agents of the Company.

:::example{title="Grouping causes in an essay"}
Group the causes: **long-term grievances** (land, cattle, labour, taxes, police, loss of independence); **short-term triggers** (rinderpest, the shooting of cattle, drought and locusts in 1895-96); and the **opportunity** (the Jameson Raid had taken many Company police out of the country in late 1895). A strong paragraph then says which mattered most. For example, you might argue that the long-term grievances made rising possible, but that the disasters of 1896 and the departure of the police made it happen then.
:::

## The role of religious leaders

Religious leaders linked the grievances with ancestral authority and gave the movement a sense of purpose. In Matabeleland, the Mwari cult at the Matopos shrines, through its priests (one of them known to settlers as the Mlimo) and the priest **Mkwati**, told people that the whites had brought the disasters and that they could be removed. In Mashonaland, the spirit mediums **Kaguvi**, who was linked with the spirit of Chaminuka, and **Nehanda** (Nyakasikana) of the Mazowe region were respected and could call chiefs to resist. Mkwati later moved to Mashonaland and helped to spread the rising. The leaders spoke about the ancestors, the land and the return of rain and prosperity, and they gave people confidence that resistance could succeed.

## Were the risings coordinated?

This is a long-standing debate. Terence Ranger, in *Revolt in Southern Rhodesia, 1896-97* (1967), argued that the two risings were closely connected, with the Mwari cult and the mediums linking Ndebele and Shona into a movement for the recovery of independence, and that this tradition later inspired nationalists. Other historians, notably Julian Cobbing (in an article of 1977), have questioned the evidence for a central priesthood and plan, and stressed local grievances and decisions, so that the movements were separate responses with leaders acting for their own reasons. Many historians now accept a middle view: there were contacts and shared ideas, especially through the spread of Mkwati's influence, but each rising had its own causes and leaders. Whichever view is taken, the common grievances explain why the risings came so close together.

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1893-94 | War and loss of Ndebele independence |
| 1894 | Reserves and Native Commissioners set up |
| 1895 | Drought and locusts |
| December 1895 - January 1896 | Jameson Raid |
| Early 1896 | Rinderpest reaches the country |
| March 1896 | Umvukela begins in Matabeleland |
| June 1896 | Rising in Mashonaland |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving one cause only"}
Rinderpest alone, or religion alone, cannot explain the risings. Show a set of connected causes and weigh them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Ndebele and Shona grievances as identical"}
Both lost land and cattle and were forced to labour. The Ndebele had also lost their state; the Shona were angry about Native Commissioners and the taxes introduced after 1890. Mention both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the risings a purely religious movement"}
Religion gave leadership and unity, but the grievances were political, economic and social.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Account for the Ndebele and Shona risings of 1896-97", "Why did the Shona and Ndebele rise at the same time?" and "To what extent were the risings coordinated?". Group causes, give specific detail, and show how they link. For a debate question, state both views and give a supported judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Loss of land and cattle, forced labour, taxes, the Native Police and loss of independence were the long-term grievances.
- Drought, locusts and rinderpest in 1895-96, and the shooting of cattle, made life worse.
- The Jameson Raid removed many police and gave an opportunity.
- Mkwati, Kaguvi, Nehanda and the Mwari cult gave leadership and purpose.
- Historians disagree about how far the risings were planned together; common grievances explain their timing.
:::
