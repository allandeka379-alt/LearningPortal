## Introduction

The risings of 1896-97 were not one battle but two campaigns that lasted more than a year. In Matabeleland the Ndebele began to attack Company posts and settlers in March 1896, and in the Matopo Hills they held out until a negotiated peace in October. In Mashonaland the fighting began in June and continued into 1897, with Shona leaders using hills and caves as strongholds. The Company's response was a mixture of military force, siege and, in Matabeleland, talks. In the end it won, but it paid a price, and it learned that it had to change the way it ruled.

This lesson narrates the course of the fighting and explains the results. The causes are in the previous lesson. The reasons for African defeat and the role of the spirit mediums are in the lesson after this one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Laager"}
A defensive circle of wagons, or a fortified camp, used by settlers for protection. Bulawayo and Salisbury had laagers in 1896.
:::

:::definition{term="Indaba"}
A Nguni word for a meeting or discussion between leaders. The Matopos indabas of 1896 were the peace talks between Rhodes and the Ndebele leaders.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- March 1896: the Umvukela begins in Matabeleland.
- Late March 1896: Bulawayo is placed in a state of defence; the laager is formed.
- June 1896: the Shona rise in Mashonaland (Mazowe, Hartley and other districts).
- August - October 1896: Cecil Rhodes meets Ndebele leaders in the Matopos (the indabas).
- 1897: fighting continues in Mashonaland, with strongholds attacked and chiefs captured.
- April 1898: Kaguvi and Nehanda are executed.
:::

## The rising in Matabeleland

In late March 1896 Ndebele groups attacked settlers and mines in the Insiza, Filabusi and Gwanda districts and other places. About 130 settlers were killed in the first week, according to the usual estimates, and the survivors went to Bulawayo, Gwelo and other laagers. Ndebele impis gathered near Bulawayo, and the town was under threat. The Company could not at first send help from Mashonaland, and it asked for volunteers and for reinforcements from the Cape and Bechuanaland. A column from Bulawayo fought and won a battle at the Umguza River in April, and a relief force under Colonel Herbert Plumer reached the town in May, but the Ndebele withdrew to the rocky Matopo Hills, where Company forces could not easily defeat them. The Ndebele fought as small, mobile groups, using cover, and forced the Company into costly patrols and failed attacks in the Matopos in the winter.

**The Mlimo.** Settlers believed that the priest known as the Mlimo was the main leader. The American scout Frederick Burnham claimed that in June 1896 he and a companion entered the Matopos and shot the priest he took to be the Mlimo. Historians dispute whether the man shot was the Mwari oracle at all, and the fighting did not end.

**The indabas.** By August 1896 the Company's attempts had failed, the cost was high and the imperial government was concerned. Rhodes, who had come to Bulawayo, took a bold decision. With a few companions he went unarmed into the Matopos to meet Ndebele leaders, and met their indunas in August and again in October, with Native Department interpreters. The leaders, including Somabulana and Sekombo, had also grown weary after the planting season failed and hunger spread. Rhodes promised to listen to their grievances, to appoint chiefs with salaries and to restrain the Native Police. The fighting ended in the Matopos in October 1896.

## The rising in Mashonaland

On 17 June 1896 Shona groups attacked settlers and mines in the Hartley district and near Mazowe, among other places, and spread to many areas. Settlers were killed, and others fled to the laagers at Salisbury and elsewhere. The movement was led by chiefs and mediums: Mashayamombe in the Hartley area, Makoni in the east, Mangwende, Kunzwi-Nyandoro and Gutu in other districts, and the mediums Kaguvi and Nehanda in the central region. Shona fighters used the broken hill country and caves, with muzzle-loading guns and some modern rifles obtained earlier by trade.

The Company, supported by troops from Britain and the Cape under Imperial command, attacked chiefs one by one. It burned villages and crops, took cattle and used dynamite against fortified caves and hill strongholds. Makoni was captured and executed in August 1897. Mashayamombe held out until 1897. By the end of that year most of the strongholds had fallen.

**Kaguvi and Nehanda.** Kaguvi was captured in 1897 and Nehanda later that year. They were tried for the murder of a Native Commissioner and were executed in Salisbury in April 1898. Shona tradition remembers that Nehanda refused to accept Christianity and said her bones would rise again; this is oral tradition, and the exact words are uncertain. Kaguvi is recorded as accepting baptism before his death.

:::example{title="Explaining why the Matopos indabas succeeded"}
1. **The military position:** the Company could not win quickly in the Matopos and the cost was high.
2. **The Ndebele position:** hunger, the lost harvest and exhaustion made peace attractive.
3. **Rhodes's offer:** he promised redress of grievances, salaries for the chiefs and control of the Native Police.
4. **The result:** peace was made with the Ndebele leaders, but the Company's rule remained.
A good answer says that both sides had reasons to settle, and that peace did not restore what had been lost.
:::

## Results of the risings

- **Loss of life.** About 450 settlers died, according to the usual figures. African deaths are not known exactly, but historians accept that many thousands died in fighting, hunger and disease, and the numbers on the African side were far larger than those on the settlers' side.
- **Hardship.** Fields and granaries were destroyed, and cattle were lost. Famine followed the lost harvest of 1896 and 1897.
- **Greater colonial control.** The Company strengthened its army and police, and the Shona and Ndebele could no longer expect to restore independence by force.
- **Administrative changes.** The Native Department was reorganised, with Native Commissioners in each district and salaried chiefs and headmen working under them. The imperial government obtained more say through the Southern Rhodesia Order in Council of 1898, which also set up a Legislative Council with an elected settler element.
- **Land and taxes.** The reserves were extended, but the Company kept the best land, and taxes continued.
- **Memory and inspiration.** The risings became a source of pride and a model for later nationalists, who called their own struggle the Second Chimurenga.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing only the settlers' losses"}
The African losses were much greater. Include both, and use words such as "estimates" for figures.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the risings ended at the indabas"}
The indabas ended the Ndebele fighting in the Matopos in 1896. In Mashonaland the war continued into 1897, and the leaders were executed in 1898.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that nothing changed after 1897"}
Company rule remained, but the administrative changes of 1898, the Native Commissioner system and the paid chiefs came from the risings.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Ndebele rose in March 1896 and fought on from the Matopos until the indabas of August to October 1896.
- The Shona rose in June 1896 and held strongholds until 1897; the Company used siege, dynamite and burning.
- Kaguvi and Nehanda were captured in 1897 and executed in April 1898.
- About 450 settlers and many thousands of Africans died; famine followed.
- Company control tightened and the administration was reorganised in 1898; the risings became an inspiration for later nationalists.
:::
