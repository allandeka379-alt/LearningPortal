## Introduction

Between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries, historians estimate that well over twelve million Africans were taken onto ships and carried across the Atlantic as slaves, and millions more were taken across the Sahara and the Indian Ocean. Many died in the journey and in the wars that fed the trade. It is a central and painful part of African history, and its end, which came gradually between 1807 and the end of the nineteenth century, changed the lives of African societies as much as the trade itself had.

This lesson explains why abolition came, how it was carried out, and what effects it had on Africa. The growth of "legitimate trade" that followed is the subject of the next lesson. When you answer questions on this topic, remember that abolition did not happen all at once or for one reason, and that Africans were not only victims but also, as rulers, traders and resisters, actors in the story.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Abolition"}
The legal ending of the slave trade (the buying, selling and transporting of people) or of slavery itself (the ownership of people). The two were separate steps: Britain ended the trade in 1807 and slavery in its colonies in 1833-34.
:::

:::definition{term="West Africa Squadron"}
A fleet of Royal Navy ships based at Freetown from 1808 and later, whose task was to stop slave ships and free the people on board.
:::

:::definition{term="Recaptive"}
A person freed from a captured slave ship, who was often settled at Freetown rather than returned home.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1787: the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade is founded in London.
- 1807: Britain abolishes the slave trade; 1808: the United States ends the import of slaves and the West Africa Squadron begins.
- 1822: Liberia is founded for freed African Americans (independent in 1847).
- 1833-34: Britain abolishes slavery in its colonies (effective 1 August 1834; apprenticeship to 1838).
- 1848: France finally abolishes slavery.
- 1865: the 13th Amendment ends slavery in the United States.
- 1873: the Sultan of Zanzibar closes the slave market under British pressure.
- 1888: Brazil abolishes slavery.
:::

## Reasons for abolition

**Humanitarian.** Quakers, evangelical Christians and former slaves campaigned against the trade. Thomas Clarkson gathered evidence; William Wilberforce led the campaign in Parliament; Granville Sharp used the courts; and writers such as Olaudah Equiano, a former slave who published his life story in 1789, made the suffering real to readers. Mass petitions, boycotts of slave-grown sugar and public meetings showed that many ordinary people supported ending the trade.

**Economic.** Historians debate how far economic change caused abolition. Eric Williams argued in *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) that the West Indian sugar economy was in decline and that industrial capitalists wanted free trade and free labour, so abolition suited British interests. Seymour Drescher, in *Econocide* (1977), argued that the sugar colonies were still profitable around 1807 and that abolition went against economic interest, so moral and political forces must have been decisive. Many historians accept that both humanitarian and economic arguments played a part.

**Political.** The French Revolution and the Haitian Revolution (1791-1804), in which enslaved people won their freedom and independence, showed that slavery could be overthrown and frightened slave owners. Slave resistance, such as the Baptist War in Jamaica in 1831-32, pushed Parliament towards ending slavery. Britain also wished to win moral prestige and to damage rivals.

:::example{title="Weighing the reasons for abolition"}
1. **Humanitarian:** a mass movement shaped public opinion and put pressure on MPs.
2. **Economic:** free-trade ideas and the shift of industry made planters less powerful, but their decline is disputed.
3. **Political:** revolutions and slave resistance made slavery seem unsafe.
4. **Judgement:** most historians agree that no single reason is enough. A strong answer ranks the reasons and explains why the humanitarian movement could succeed only when the political and economic situation made it possible.
:::

## The process of abolition

- **Britain.** The Slave Trade Act of 1807 made it illegal for British ships to carry slaves. The Slavery Abolition Act of 1833 freed slaves in the British colonies from 1 August 1834, with an apprenticeship period to 1838 and compensation of about £20 million paid to the owners, not to the freed people.
- **Enforcement.** The Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron, from 1808, patrolled the coast and captured slave ships. Historians estimate that it freed around 150,000 people over the century. Britain also made treaties with other states, such as Portugal, Spain and Brazil, and with African rulers, giving up the export of slaves. In 1851 Britain bombarded Lagos to remove King Kosoko, who supported the trade, and installed a ruler who signed an anti-slavery treaty.
- **The United States.** The import of slaves was banned in 1808, but slavery continued until the Civil War and the 13th Amendment of 1865.
- **France.** Abolished slavery in 1794, restored it under Napoleon in 1802, and ended it finally in 1848.
- **Brazil.** The Atlantic trade to Brazil was largely stopped by 1850-51, and slavery was abolished in 1888.
- **East Africa and Zanzibar.** The trade across the Indian Ocean continued longer. After pressure from Britain, the Sultan of Zanzibar closed the slave market in 1873, and slavery was legally ended in Zanzibar in 1897.

## Impact on Africa

**Economic dislocation.** States that had grown rich by selling slaves, such as Dahomey and Oyo, lost an important source of income. Rulers and merchants who had imported guns, cloth and spirits by selling captives had to find other exports. The change was painful, and some societies suffered years of disorder.

**Political change.** Some states weakened and fell, such as the Oyo empire, which declined in the early nineteenth century, partly for internal reasons, leading to wars among the Yoruba. New states appeared where leaders successfully switched to other trades. Rulers who clung to the old trade were pressed by Britain, and the naval patrols began a pattern of European involvement.

**Resettlement.** Freed people were landed at **Freetown** in Sierra Leone, which became a colony in 1808, and formed a society of recaptives, settlers from Britain and Nova Scotia, and returnees, who spoke English and often became Christian. **Liberia** was founded in 1822 by the American Colonization Society for freed African Americans, and became independent in 1847. Both settlements showed the hope of a "return to Africa", but they also caused tension with local peoples and were sometimes treated by Europeans as bases for influence.

**Shift to other trades.** Palm oil, groundnuts, rubber and ivory replaced slaves as exports, as the next lesson explains. This created new wealth and new problems.

**Slavery inside Africa.** The end of the export trade did not end slavery in Africa. In some places, slave labour was used to produce palm oil and other crops, so domestic slavery grew. Colonial rulers later ended it slowly, and often reluctantly.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying abolition ended slavery everywhere in 1807"}
1807 ended the British slave trade. Slavery in British colonies ended in 1833-34, and in other countries much later (1848, 1865, 1888). The trade across the Indian Ocean continued into the 1870s.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only humanitarian reasons, or only economic reasons"}
Historians debate how the reasons fit together. Explain several and weigh them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Africans as passive"}
Africans resisted enslavement, rulers made choices about the trade, and Africans such as Equiano took part in the campaign.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Questions include "Why was the slave trade abolished?", "Describe how the Atlantic slave trade was ended" and "Assess the impact of abolition on West African societies". Give reasons in groups with dates and names, rank them, and for impact cover political, economic and social effects, with an example such as Freetown, Liberia, Oyo or Dahomey.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Abolition came from a mix of humanitarian pressure, changing economic interests and political events such as the Haitian Revolution and slave resistance.
- Britain abolished the trade in 1807 and slavery in 1833-34; the West Africa Squadron, treaties and later pressure enforced it; other states followed over many decades.
- Africa's slave-exporting states lost revenue and some declined; Freetown and Liberia were founded for freed people.
- Trade shifted to palm oil, groundnuts, rubber and ivory, while domestic slavery persisted.
:::
