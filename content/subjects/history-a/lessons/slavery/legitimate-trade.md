## Introduction

When Britain and other states abolished the slave trade in the early nineteenth century, they hoped that Africans would find something else to sell. They called the trade in goods such as palm oil, groundnuts, rubber and ivory "legitimate trade", to distinguish it from the trade in people. Industrial Europe needed these goods: palm oil for soap, candles and the lubrication of machinery, groundnuts for oil and food, rubber for tyres and cables after the 1880s, and ivory for combs, piano keys and ornaments. African rulers and merchants who could supply them found new sources of wealth.

The change was not smooth. Some states made the move successfully and became powerful; others failed to adjust and fell into decline; and the new trade brought rivalries, wars and eventually European interference. This lesson explains why the transition took place, who succeeded and who did not, and the impact on African societies. You should have studied the lesson on the abolition of the slave trade first.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Legitimate trade"}
The trade in goods other than slaves, mainly agricultural and forest products, between African producers and European merchants in the nineteenth century.
:::

:::definition{term="Middleman"}
An African merchant or state that bought goods from producers inland and sold them to European traders on the coast, taking a profit and controlling access to the interior.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade house"}
A commercial unit in the Niger Delta states, made up of a family head, kin and dependants, including slaves, who traded together. Powerful houses competed for control of markets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
- 1807 onwards: the end of the British slave trade increases the push towards other exports.
- 1869: Jaja founds Opobo after leaving Bonny.
- 1873: Britain recognises Jaja as king of Opobo.
- 1873: Zanzibar's slave market is closed.
- 1884-85: the Berlin Conference declares free trade in the Niger basin.
- 1887: Jaja is arrested and exiled by the British.
:::

## Reasons for the transition

1. **Industrial demand.** British industry needed oils for soap, candles and machinery. Palm oil exports from West Africa grew from a few thousand tonnes to tens of thousands of tonnes in the mid-century. Rubber demand rose rapidly later.
2. **Abolition and pressure.** The Royal Navy patrols and treaties raised the risks of slave trading, and British merchants wanted to buy oil instead. Britain also encouraged rulers to sign treaties turning to legitimate commerce.
3. **African interests.** Many rulers and merchants found that oil and groundnuts were profitable and less risky. Some were also discouraged by falling prices for slaves. Others preferred the older trade, which they could continue illegally to Brazil and Cuba for many years.
4. **Cheaper transport.** Steamships on the rivers, from the 1830s and 1850s, and better ocean transport lowered costs.

## Successes and failures

**The Niger Delta states.** Bonny, Brass, Calabar and New Calabar had been important slave ports. They turned to palm oil, which was brought down by canoe from the Igbo hinterland. Their trade houses grew rich by controlling the markets as middlemen.

**Opobo and Jaja.** Jaja, who was born in Igboland and sold into slavery in Bonny as a boy, rose to be head of the Anna Pepple House. After conflict with rival houses in Bonny, he moved with followers in 1869 and founded Opobo, close to the palm-oil markets. He controlled the trade, sold oil directly to Liverpool merchants, and built up an army and fleet. In 1873 Britain recognised him as king of Opobo. His example shows how an African state could use legitimate trade to build power. But he refused to let British traders go inland, and his control conflicted with the principle of free trade in the Niger area agreed at Berlin in 1885. In 1887 the British consul invited him to talks, then arrested him, and he was tried and exiled to the West Indies, where he died in 1891 on the way home. Opobo's decline followed.

**Dahomey.** Under King Gezo (1818-58) Dahomey made a partial change. Palm oil was produced on royal plantations, using slave labour, but the kingdom depended on war and the sale of captives, and it continued to export slaves while it could. Rivalry with Britain and then France, and the loss of its main trade, weakened it, and it was conquered by France in 1892-94.

**Asante.** After the abolition of the trade, Asante had trouble replacing the revenue from selling slaves. It traded gold, kola nuts and rubber, but wars with Britain (including that of 1873-74) and competition on the coast hurt it, and it came under British control by 1901.

**Zanzibar and East Africa.** In East Africa the Sultan of Zanzibar built wealth from cloves, produced on plantations with slave labour, and from ivory brought from the interior by caravan, including by traders such as Tippu Tip. The Atlantic abolition did not end the Indian Ocean trade at once, and Zanzibar's slave market was closed only in 1873 after British pressure. Slavery in Zanzibar continued legally until 1897.

:::example{title="Explaining a case: why was Jaja's Opobo successful and why did it fall?"}
1. **Success:** Jaja chose a site next to the palm-oil markets and controlled the trade through his own fleet and house.
2. **Direct sales:** he exported oil to Liverpool without going through other middlemen.
3. **Recognition:** Britain recognised his kingdom in 1873.
4. **Conflict:** British traders wanted to trade directly with the inland markets, and the Berlin principle of free trade was used against him.
5. **Fall:** he was arrested in 1887 and exiled; Opobo lost its independence.
A strong answer links the rise to the opportunities of legitimate trade and the fall to European interference.
:::

## Impact on African societies

- **Growth of towns and ports.** Freetown, Lagos, Bonny, Opobo, Calabar and Zanzibar grew, with merchants, missionaries and new groups of educated Africans.
- **New wealth and power.** Middleman states and trade houses became rich and built armies. Women traders in some places became wealthy.
- **Instability.** Rivalry for control of markets led to wars among trade houses and states. The need for labour to produce oil and cloves increased domestic slavery in some places.
- **European interference.** Consuls, gunboats and traders pressed for free access and security. Britain bombarded towns, signed treaties and removed rulers such as Kosoko of Lagos and Jaja. These interventions prepared the way for colonisation, since the merchants and consuls asked for more protection and then for annexation.
- **Dependence on world markets.** Prices of oil and rubber fell and rose, so African economies were exposed to forces they could not control.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying legitimate trade replaced the slave trade at once and without problems"}
The transition took decades, and some states continued the slave trade illegally. It brought new rivalries and dangers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying legitimate trade benefited everybody in Africa"}
Rulers and merchants benefited most. Ordinary producers often worked for low returns, and slave labour increased in some places.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Opobo and Bonny"}
Opobo was founded by Jaja in 1869 after he left Bonny. It was a rival state.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Industrial demand for palm oil, groundnuts, rubber and ivory, plus abolition and African interests, led to the growth of legitimate trade.
- The Niger Delta states and Opobo under Jaja succeeded as middleman powers; Dahomey and Asante adjusted poorly; Zanzibar grew rich on cloves and ivory.
- Towns and ports grew, wealth and power shifted to new rulers and merchants, and wars and domestic slavery increased.
- European consuls, traders and gunboats interfered, which led to the removal of rulers such as Jaja and prepared the way for colonisation.
:::
