## Introduction

Land was the central issue of Zimbabwe's history from the 1890s to the present. Colonial laws, above all the Land Apportionment Act of 1930 and the Land Tenure Act of 1969, had divided the country by race and left a small number of white farmers with about two-fifths of the land, mostly the best-watered, while hundreds of thousands of African families crowded into Communal Areas on poorer soil. The liberation movements had promised land, and the wish to recover it was one of the main reasons many people supported the struggle.

This lesson covers the first twenty years of independence. It explains what the government inherited, what the Lancaster House Agreement allowed, the laws and models used for resettlement, the results by 1999, and the criticisms of different groups. Land reform after 2000 is the subject of the next lesson. You should know how land was taken in the colonial period and the terms of the 1979 settlement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Resettlement"}
The movement of families from overcrowded Communal Areas onto land acquired by the government, usually former commercial farms, with plots and sometimes help with services.
:::

:::definition{term="Willing-buyer willing-seller"}
The approach that the government followed in the 1980s, in line with the Lancaster House constitution: it bought land that owners offered for sale, paying a fair price in full, and used compulsion only rarely.
:::

:::definition{term="Compulsory acquisition"}
The taking of land by the state, with compensation, even if the owner does not wish to sell.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1979: Lancaster House Agreement; for ten years (to April 1990) the property clauses were entrenched and land was in practice bought from willing sellers.
- 1980-90: resettlement under Models A, B, C and D; about 52,000 families settled by 1990.
- 1985: Land Acquisition Act, giving the state the first right to buy farms offered for sale.
- 1990: Constitution Amendment No. 11; 1992: Land Acquisition Act allowing compulsory acquisition with fair compensation.
- 1993-94: the Rukuni Commission on land tenure.
- 1997: about 1,500 farms listed for acquisition. 9-11 September 1998: the donors' conference in Harare.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## The land question in 1980

At independence about 6,000 white commercial farmers held a very large share of the farmland, roughly two-fifths of the country, mostly in the regions with the best rainfall and soil, while some 700,000 African families lived in the Communal Areas, which were overcrowded, eroded and often in dry regions. The war had also displaced many people. The people therefore expected rapid redistribution. The government's aims were to reduce overcrowding, to improve living standards, to settle landless people and war-displaced families, and to do so without damaging commercial agriculture, on which exports and food supplies depended.

## The Lancaster House terms

The 1979 constitution protected private property. Land could be acquired compulsorily only in limited cases, such as under-utilised land for resettlement, and compensation had to be prompt and adequate and could be sent out of the country. The relevant clauses could not be amended for ten years without the agreement of all members of the House, which included twenty reserved white seats until 1987. The British government said that it would help to fund land purchase, but no fixed fund was agreed. These terms limited the pace of change: in practice the government relied on buying farms that owners offered ("willing-buyer willing-seller"), and had to find the money to pay for them.

## Resettlement in the 1980s

The government set a target that grew from 18,000 families to 162,000 families by 1982. Settlers were chosen from the landless, the unemployed and war-displaced, and later from overcrowded Communal Areas. The state used four resettlement models:

| Model | Description |
|---|---|
| A | Individual family plots (about five hectares of arable land) with shared grazing, in villages |
| B | Collective farms run as cooperatives |
| C | A state estate with outgrowers on smaller plots |
| D | Ranching schemes for communal grazing in dry areas |

Model A was by far the most common, because people wanted their own land. The cooperatives of Model B were less popular, and Models C and D were rarely used. By 1990 about **52,000 families** had been settled on roughly 3 million hectares, and by 1997-98 about 70,000 families, well below the target of 162,000. Studies of the settled families found that many improved their incomes and food security, grew more maize and cotton, and gained schools and clinics.

:::example{title="Explaining why resettlement was slower than planned"}
1. The target of 162,000 families was large and was set quickly.
2. In practice the government bought mainly what owners offered, and many offered poor land at high prices.
3. Money was short, because Britain's help was limited and because other spending, including drought relief, competed for funds.
4. Surveying, building roads, wells, clinics and schools on newly bought farms took time.
5. Therefore by 1990 only about a third of the target had been reached.
:::

## Laws and amendments

The **Land Acquisition Act of 1985** gave the government the right of first refusal on any commercial farm offered for sale, and it could be bought at a price valued by the state. After the ten-year protection ended in 1990, the government passed **Constitution Amendment No. 11 (1990)** and the **Land Acquisition Act of 1992**, which allowed compulsory acquisition of land for resettlement, with "fair compensation" to be decided by the courts, and which allowed the state to set a maximum farm size; a land tax was introduced soon afterwards. The Rukuni Commission (1993-94) studied land tenure and recommended reform of systems of tenure in communal areas. In 1997 the government listed about 1,500 farms for compulsory acquisition; many owners challenged this in court and the process was slow.

## Difficulties, criticisms and the 1998 conference

**Difficulties.** Prices were high, many offered farms were poor, funds were limited and the cost of services high. Land was in practice also affected by drought and ESAP.

**Criticisms from different sides.** Landless people and war veterans said progress was too slow and that most farms remained in white hands. Commercial farmers argued that they were productive and that acquisitions should respect the law and fair compensation. Some analysts and some donors questioned whether some farms went to people with political connections rather than to the needy, and whether the government had a clear plan. The government said that the Lancaster House terms and the unwillingness of Britain to fund the programme as promised had delayed the change. In November 1997 the British Secretary for International Development, Clare Short, wrote that the new British government did not accept a special responsibility to meet the costs of land purchase in Zimbabwe, a letter that angered the Zimbabwean government.

**The 1998 donors' conference.** In September 1998 the government held a conference in Harare with donors, which agreed in principle on the need for a land programme (Phase II of the Land Reform and Resettlement Programme) with an inception phase, but funding and details were not settled.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nothing was done before 2000"}
About 70,000 families were resettled before 2000. The point is that progress fell short of targets, not that there was none.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting precise statistics from memory"}
Give round figures ("about 52,000 families by 1990", "roughly two-fifths of the land") and say they are estimates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming one cause only"}
Explain slow progress through the combination of Lancaster House terms, cost, the quality of land offered, limited aid and administration.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Describe the land reform policy of 1980-1990", "Why was progress with land reform slow in the 1980s?", "To what extent was resettlement successful between 1980 and 1999?" Use the model names and dates, include the Lancaster House constraint and the laws of 1985 and 1992, give successes and difficulties, and reach a balanced judgement. Do not assign praise or blame to individuals.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- At independence about 6,000 white farmers held roughly two-fifths of the land, while hundreds of thousands of African families lived in crowded Communal Areas.
- Lancaster House required willing-buyer willing-seller for ten years; resettlement under Models A-D settled about 52,000 families by 1990 and about 70,000 by the late 1990s.
- The Land Acquisition Acts of 1985 and 1992 and Constitution Amendment No. 11 widened the state's powers.
- Progress was slowed by cost, poor land, limited aid and administration; landless people, farmers, donors and the government judged the programme differently.
- The 1998 donors' conference agreed in principle on a land programme, but the issue remained unresolved.
:::
