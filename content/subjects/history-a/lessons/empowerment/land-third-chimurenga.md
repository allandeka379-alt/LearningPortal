## Introduction

From 2000 Zimbabwe carried out one of the fastest and most controversial land redistributions in modern African history. The government called it the **Third Chimurenga**, linking it to the First Chimurenga of 1896-97 and the Second Chimurenga of the liberation war, and presented it as the completion of the struggle for land. Its critics, at home and abroad, challenged the way it was done and its effects. Historians, economists and ordinary Zimbabweans still disagree about it, so this lesson sets out the facts, the aims and the different views.

It follows from the previous lesson, which shows that by 1999 about 70,000 families had been resettled, against a target of 162,000, and that most large commercial farms were still owned by whites. You should also know about the Lancaster House terms and the 1998 donors' conference. Questions on this topic need dates, laws, models and balanced judgements, without slogans.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fast-track land reform"}
The accelerated programme of compulsory acquisition and redistribution of commercial farmland that began in 2000, officially called the Fast Track Land Reform Programme. The government called it the Third Chimurenga.
:::

:::definition{term="A1 and A2 models"}
Two models of settlement under the fast-track programme. A1 gave smaller plots to families, in villages or as self-contained farms, for household farming. A2 gave larger farms to people who were expected to run commercial enterprises.
:::

:::definition{term="Land tenure"}
The terms on which land is held, such as freehold title, lease, permit or customary right. Security of tenure affects whether farmers can borrow and invest.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 12-13 February 2000: referendum on a draft constitution; the "No" vote won; farm occupations followed.
- April 2000: Constitution Amendment No. 16 and amendments to the Land Acquisition Act.
- July 2000: the Fast Track Land Reform Programme is launched.
- September 2001: the Abuja Agreement between Zimbabwe, Britain and Commonwealth states.
- 2003: the Presidential Land Review Committee (Utete) reports.
- 2005: Constitution Amendment No. 17 vests acquired land in the state.
:::

## Why the fast-track programme began

Several explanations are given, and most historians combine them.

1. **Slow progress and unmet expectations.** By 1999 most commercial farmland had not been redistributed, and many people in Communal Areas still lacked land.
2. **The Lancaster House constraints and dispute with Britain.** The government argued that Britain had not honoured promises to help pay for land, pointing to the British letter of November 1997 on responsibility for the costs. Britain replied that it had funded land purchase in the 1980s and that its aid should be directed at poverty reduction and a transparent programme.
3. **The referendum of February 2000.** A draft constitution put to a vote on 12-13 February 2000 would have allowed acquisition without compensation, with Britain responsible for paying. Voters rejected the draft, by about 55 per cent to 45 per cent, in a low turnout. Soon afterwards war veterans and others began to occupy farms. Critics argue that the loss of the referendum, and the political competition from a new opposition party, the Movement for Democratic Change, was a spur to the programme; the government and its supporters say that the occupations expressed long-standing frustration at the failure to redistribute land.
4. **Pressure from war veterans and landless people.** War veterans' payments in 1997 and demands for land were part of the same pressure.

## Laws and the course of the programme

In April 2000 Parliament passed Constitution Amendment No. 16, which stated that Britain, as the former colonial power, should pay for land taken, and that the government would pay only for improvements, and amended the Land Acquisition Act to speed up acquisition. From July 2000 the government designated farms under the fast-track programme, many of them already occupied. Courts, including the Supreme Court in December 2000, ruled that parts of the process were unlawful, and the government amended the law in response. The **Abuja Agreement** of September 2001, signed with Britain and Commonwealth foreign ministers, linked an end to unlawful occupations and respect for the rule of law to British funding for a land programme, but it was not implemented. In 2005 Amendment No. 17 vested acquired land in the state and removed the right to challenge acquisition in court.

By 2003 the Utete Committee reported that about 11 million hectares had been acquired, with more than 140,000 families on A1 plots and about 16,000 farmers on A2 farms. These are government figures, and independent studies give different totals, but all agree that the redistribution was large. Many farm workers, who were not offered land, lost homes and jobs, with estimates of those affected in the hundreds of thousands. There was violence during the occupations, and some farmers, workers and others were killed or injured.

:::example{title="Analysing the 2000 referendum as a turning point"}
1. A draft constitution was put to the vote on 12-13 February 2000, including a clause on acquiring land without compensation.
2. The result was a "No" vote of about 55 per cent, which was the first electoral defeat of the government since independence.
3. Within weeks farm occupations began, and in April the government changed the Constitution and Land Acquisition Act.
4. The sequence shows that the land question and political competition were closely connected, although historians differ on how far the occupations were planned by the state and how far they came from below.
5. Therefore the referendum explains the timing of the fast-track programme, while slow progress after 1980 explains why the issue was so powerful.
:::

## Impact

| Area | Effects reported |
|---|---|
| Agriculture | Output of maize, wheat and tobacco fell sharply in the early 2000s as large farms changed hands; later recovery in some crops, notably tobacco, came mainly from small-scale growers on contract |
| Land and people | Hundreds of thousands of families obtained land; many farm workers and their families were displaced |
| Tenure | Permits and offer letters gave weaker security than title, limiting borrowing; leases for A2 farmers were introduced later |
| Economy | Loss of export earnings, shortages of food and foreign currency; the economy contracted and inflation rose to hyperinflation by 2008, to which many factors contributed |
| International relations | Britain, the European Union and the United States criticised the programme and imposed targeted measures on individuals; the government called these sanctions harmful to the whole economy, while Western governments said that they were targeted at individuals |

The government and its supporters stress that the programme corrected a historical injustice, gave land to many landless Zimbabweans and ended the dominance of a small group of large farmers. Critics stress the loss of production and jobs, the violence, the lack of clear tenure, the allocation of several farms to a few well-connected people, and the damage to investment. Independent researchers have found both gains for many resettled households and wide differences in how well farmers have done. In 2020 the government signed an agreement to pay compensation to former farmers for improvements to their land.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reducing the programme to one cause"}
The programme had several causes: slow progress, the Lancaster House terms, the dispute with Britain, the referendum and the pressure from war veterans. Use at least three and link them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting the outcome as all good or all bad"}
Both sides have evidence. Give gains for many families and the loss of production and jobs, and conclude with a reasoned judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sanctions with the land dispute"}
The measures against individuals came after the programme began (from 2001-02). Give the government's view and the Western view, and avoid assuming either.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- By 1999 about 70,000 families had been resettled, and most commercial farms were still in white hands.
- After the "No" vote in the referendum of February 2000, farm occupations began, and the government amended the Constitution and the Land Acquisition Act; the Fast Track Land Reform Programme began in July 2000.
- A1 plots went to smaller households and A2 farms to commercial farmers; government figures show some 11 million hectares redistributed.
- Output fell sharply in the early 2000s, farm workers were displaced and tenure was insecure; many families gained land.
- Views differ: justice and completion of the struggle against loss of production, violence and economic damage.
:::
