## Introduction

A farmer owns a field, and a business owns its factory. But a musician owns a song, an inventor owns an invention, and a business owns the name by which customers know it, even though none of these can be touched. These creations of the mind are called **intellectual property**. For empowerment to mean more than the transfer of land and shares, Zimbabweans also need to own and profit from their ideas, brands and creative work, whether they are a musician in Harare, a small manufacturer in Bulawayo, a developer of a mobile application or a rural community with knowledge of medicinal plants.

This lesson explains what intellectual property is, the laws and institutions that protect it in Zimbabwe and the region, and the successes and difficulties. It links to the previous lessons because indigenous enterprise needs protection of brands and inventions to grow. You do not need to remember section numbers of the Acts; you need to understand the types of protection, the main institutions and the reasons for and against enforcement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Intellectual property (IP)"}
Legal rights over creations of the mind, such as inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, symbols, names and images used in commerce.
:::

:::definition{term="Patent"}
An exclusive right, granted for a limited period (generally 20 years), to make, use and sell a new, useful and inventive product or process. The inventor must describe the invention publicly in return.
:::

:::definition{term="Copyright"}
The automatic right of authors, composers, artists, film makers and software writers to control the copying and public performance of their works, generally for the author's life and a long period after death. Registration is not needed in most countries, though it can help as evidence.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade mark"}
A sign, name, logo or shape that distinguishes the goods or services of one business from another. Registration gives the owner the exclusive right to use it, for a renewable period.
:::

:::definition{term="Industrial design"}
The appearance of a product, such as the shape of a bottle or the pattern on a cloth, which can be registered to stop copying.
:::

:::definition{term="Traditional knowledge"}
Knowledge, skills and practices developed over generations by communities, such as the use of plants for medicine, craft techniques, songs and dances. Protecting it is difficult because it belongs to a community and is often unwritten.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names to learn"}
- ZIPO: the Zimbabwe Intellectual Property Office, which administers patents, trade marks and industrial designs.
- ZIMURA: the Zimbabwe Music Rights Association, a collecting society for musicians' royalties.
- ARIPO: the African Regional Intellectual Property Organization, created by the Lusaka Agreement of 1976 and headquartered in Harare.
- WIPO: the World Intellectual Property Organization, a UN specialised agency in Geneva.
- Swakopmund Protocol (2010): ARIPO's agreement on protecting traditional knowledge and expressions of folklore.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Intellectual property is ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine it.
:::

## Why protect intellectual property?

1. **Reward and incentive.** If anyone can copy a song or an invention freely, creators will not earn a living and may stop creating.
2. **Business growth.** A registered trade mark builds customers' trust and lets a small firm sell its own brand; a patent allows an inventor to find investors.
3. **Fairness to communities.** Without protection, outsiders can profit from local knowledge and plants without sharing benefits.
4. **Economic development and trade.** International agreements such as the TRIPS Agreement of the World Trade Organization set minimum standards; countries that protect IP can attract investors and licensing.

There is a balance. Too little protection discourages creators; too much protection can raise the price of medicines and books and limit access to knowledge. Developing countries usually argue for rules that allow for public health and education.

## Laws and institutions in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has separate Acts for each type of right: the **Patents Act**, the **Trade Marks Act**, the **Industrial Designs Act**, the **Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act**, and an Act on **Geographical Indications**, which protects names that show the origin of a product. The **Zimbabwe Intellectual Property Office (ZIPO)**, in the Ministry of Justice, registers patents, trade marks and designs and keeps the registers. Copyright arises automatically, and the Copyright Act is enforced mainly through the courts, the police and collecting societies such as **ZIMURA**, which gather royalties from radio stations, venues and other users and pay them to musicians.

Zimbabwe is a member of the **World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO)**, which administers major treaties, such as the Berne Convention on copyright and the Paris Convention on patents and trade marks, and of the **ARIPO**, whose Harare Protocol (1982) allows an inventor to apply for a patent in several member states through one application, and whose Banjul Protocol (1993) does the same for trade marks. The Swakopmund Protocol of 2010 provides a regional framework for protecting traditional knowledge and folklore. Zimbabwe is also a member of the WTO, so it has accepted the TRIPS standards.

:::example{title="Applying the types of IP to a business"}
**Case:** Tendai, who runs a small firm in Gweru, develops a new type of low-cost water filter, calls it "Nzou Clean" and designs a distinctive bottle.

1. The **invention** (the filtering method) can be protected by a **patent**, if it is new and inventive, by registering with ZIPO or through ARIPO.
2. The **name and logo** "Nzou Clean" can be registered as a **trade mark**.
3. The **shape of the bottle** can be registered as an **industrial design**.
4. The **instructions and the advertising jingle** are protected by **copyright** automatically.
5. If another firm copies her name, she can sue for infringement, but she will need evidence of registration or prior use.
:::

## Government efforts, successes and challenges

The government has tried to raise awareness, to update laws, to support registration for small businesses and artists, to combat piracy through the police and customs, and to help ZIMURA collect royalties. Zimbabwe hosts ARIPO, giving it a role in regional policy. Local musicians, publishers and manufacturers have benefited from royalties and registered brands.

The challenges are considerable. **Piracy** of music, films and software is widespread, particularly with digital copying. Many small businesses, creators and rural communities do not know about their rights or find registration costly and slow. Enforcement agencies have limited resources, and the courts take time. Traditional knowledge is difficult to protect, because it is collectively held, and benefit-sharing rules are still developing. Few Zimbabweans file patents, so much innovation remains unprotected.

| Type of IP | Protects | Usual method | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patent | New inventions | Registration | A new water filter |
| Copyright | Creative works | Automatic | A song or book |
| Trade mark | Brand names and logos | Registration | A company logo |
| Industrial design | Look of a product | Registration | A bottle shape |
| Traditional knowledge | Community knowledge | Regional and national frameworks | Herbal remedies, crafts |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing copyright with patents"}
Copyright protects the expression of ideas (the song, the book) automatically; a patent protects an invention and must be applied for. You cannot patent a song or copyright a machine's method.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that intellectual property is only about musicians"}
It also covers inventions, brand names, designs and traditional knowledge, and affects businesses and communities.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the law without the problems"}
Include difficulties such as piracy, cost, low awareness and limited enforcement, and say what the government and institutions have tried.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Intellectual property covers patents, copyright, trade marks, industrial designs and traditional knowledge; it rewards creators and supports enterprise.
- Zimbabwe has an Act for each type of right; ZIPO registers patents, trade marks and designs, and ZIMURA collects music royalties.
- Zimbabwe belongs to WIPO and ARIPO (headquartered in Harare); ARIPO's Harare, Banjul and Swakopmund Protocols give regional patents, marks and protection for traditional knowledge.
- Piracy, low awareness, costs of registration and limited enforcement are the main challenges.
- A balanced answer weighs creators' rights against public access and links protection to empowerment.
:::
