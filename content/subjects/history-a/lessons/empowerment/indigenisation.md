## Introduction

At independence in 1980, Zimbabwe's economy was strongly divided by race. Most large farms, mines, factories and shops were owned by whites or by foreign companies, and Africans had been kept out of skilled jobs, credit and business by law and custom. **Indigenisation** and **empowerment** are the names of the policies by which successive governments tried to change this, by bringing black Zimbabweans into the management and ownership of the economy. They remain among the most debated issues in the country's recent history.

This lesson explains the reasons for these policies, how they were applied from 1980 to the present, the business models that promote local ownership, and the arguments about their results. Early land reform and the economic blueprints are explained in other lessons. The lesson presents aims, outcomes and criticisms so that you can reach your own judgement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indigenisation"}
The transfer of ownership, control and jobs in the economy to indigenous Zimbabweans, meaning (in the 2007 Act) people disadvantaged by unfair discrimination before independence on 18 April 1980, and their descendants.
:::

:::definition{term="Empowerment"}
Policies and programmes that give people the skills, finance, opportunities and decision-making power to take part fully in the economy. It is wider than ownership alone.
:::

:::definition{term="Affirmative action"}
Deliberate measures to help groups that were disadvantaged in the past, for example by giving priority in recruitment, training or contracts.
:::

:::definition{term="Community share ownership trust (CSOT)"}
A trust that holds shares in a company, such as a mine, for the benefit of the surrounding community, whose members receive projects such as clinics and schools from the dividends.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1980s: Africanisation of the public service; support for small business through SEDCO (1983); lobbying by black business groups such as the Indigenous Business Development Centre (1990).
- 2007-08: the Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act, setting a 51 per cent target for indigenous ownership in large firms.
- 2010: Indigenisation regulations and the National Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Board (NIEEB).
- 2011-12: the first community share ownership trusts.
- 2018 onwards: the law is narrowed to a few resources and later reformed.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Indigenisation and empowerment are ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine them.
:::

## Why were these policies adopted?

1. **Historical inequality.** Laws such as the Land Apportionment Act, the job reservation practices and the lack of loans for Africans had kept ownership in white hands. The nationalist movements had promised to change this.
2. **Political legitimacy.** Having won independence on the promise of liberation, the government needed to show ordinary people a material change.
3. **Constraints of the Lancaster House settlement.** The agreement protected property for ten years, so early policy focused on the public service, training and new business rather than on seizing assets.
4. **Economic arguments.** Supporters believed that local ownership would keep profits in the country, build skills and strengthen national pride. Critics replied that ownership alone would not produce skills or capital.

## The 1980s and 1990s: affirmative action and support for small business

In the 1980s the main thrust was Africanisation of the **public service**, local councils, the army and police, and the state-owned enterprises, with training programmes and scholarships to supply skilled people. The government supported small business through the **Small Enterprises Development Corporation (SEDCO)**, founded in 1983, and black business groups, such as the Indigenous Business Development Centre formed in 1990, lobbied for more access to loans, premises and contracts. Women's business groups were also assisted. Under ESAP in the 1990s trade liberalisation made life harder for many small producers, and indigenous business complained of lack of capital and of access to land, premises and loans. At the same time the government wished to attract foreign investors, through bodies such as the Zimbabwe Investment Centre (1989), so empowerment of local business and the welcome to outside capital pulled in different directions.

## The Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act

The Act of 2007 (signed in 2008), with regulations in 2010, required that at least **51 per cent** of the shares in businesses valued above a threshold (US\$500,000 in the 2010 regulations) be held by indigenous Zimbabweans, and it set up the **National Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Board (NIEEB)** to oversee the process. Foreign-owned mines, banks and other firms submitted plans and were allowed to meet the target in stages through sales to employees, communities, the state or indigenous investors. For example, large mining companies set up **community share ownership trusts**, such as those at Mhondoro-Ngezi-Chegutu, which received a share of the dividends to pay for local services, and **employee share ownership schemes (ESOPs)** gave workers shares.

:::example{title="Weighing the community share ownership trusts"}
**Claim:** CSOTs advanced empowerment.

1. They gave communities near mines a legal stake in the company and part of the profit, which had rarely happened before.
2. Money was used for clinics, schools, boreholes and roads in some districts.
3. But the funds depended on company profits and dividends, which fell when mineral prices fell; some trusts were slow to pay out; and disagreements arose about how funds were managed and who benefited.
4. Judgement: the model widened participation and brought visible projects, but its value depended on honest management, transparency and the profitability of the mine.
:::

## Changes after 2013

The 51 per cent rule was applied unevenly and attracted much criticism for the uncertainty it created, and from about 2013 the government softened it in practice for many sectors. An amendment in 2018 narrowed the requirement to the platinum and diamond sectors, and later legislation moved the policy towards case-by-case deals and a broader approach of empowerment through opportunities, finance and skills. The government also promoted **cooperatives**, **small and medium enterprises (SMEs)** and support for youth and women in business, presented as models of ownership that build national pride.

## Successes, challenges and different views

| Supporters say | Critics say |
|---|---|
| It corrected historic racial inequality and gave black Zimbabweans a stake | The 51 per cent rule frightened investors and reduced capital and jobs |
| CSOTs, cooperatives and SMEs gave communities and small producers new income | Benefits went mainly to a small, politically connected group |
| Local ownership keeps profits in the country and builds skills | Ownership without capital or management skills can lead to failure |
| Resource-rich countries often seek a greater share of the benefits of their minerals | Unclear rules and corruption undermined the programme; empowerment needed finance and training, not just share transfers |

Historians and economists therefore judge indigenisation differently: the **aim** is widely accepted, but the **method** and **results** are debated.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing indigenisation with land reform"}
Both address inequality, but indigenisation concerns ownership of businesses, mines and jobs, while land reform concerns farmland. Keep the two apart.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the 2007 Act"}
The policy began in the 1980s with the public service and small business support. Give a sequence of stages.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing for one side only"}
Examiners expect aims, successes, criticisms and a judgement. Avoid slogans and praise or blame of individuals.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Describe the policy of indigenisation in Zimbabwe", "Why did the government adopt empowerment policies?", "To what extent has indigenisation been successful?" Define terms, give a sequence (1980s, 2007-08, 2018), use a business model as an example (CSOT, cooperative or SME), and balance achievements and difficulties before you conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenisation and empowerment aim to correct racial inequality in ownership and jobs inherited at independence.
- The 1980s brought Africanisation of the public service and support for small business; the 2007 Act required 51 per cent indigenous ownership in large firms and created the NIEEB.
- Business models include community share ownership trusts, employee share schemes, cooperatives and SMEs.
- The law was narrowed after 2013 and in 2018; results include community projects but also investor uncertainty and uneven benefits.
- The aim is widely accepted; the method and results are debated.
:::
