## Introduction

For the first four decades of the Cold War, Western historians wrote with almost no access to the records of the other side. The Soviet Union's archives were closed, and its own historians worked under strict political control. The collapse of the Soviet Union in December 1991 changed this. Documents from Moscow and from the former communist states of Eastern Europe, and later from China, became available, though never fully. They provided new evidence on the thinking of the leaders on the other side, and led to what is called the "New" Cold War history. This lesson explains how Russian perspectives differ from Western ones, what the new sources have added, and how far they have changed the answers to the question of who or what was to blame. It continues the debate in the previous lesson.

You should know the events of 1941-49 and the three schools of interpretation from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Post-post-revisionism"}
A name given to the 'New' Cold War history of the 1990s, which used newly opened archives of the Soviet bloc and China to re-examine the origins of the Cold War, often giving more weight to Soviet ideology and to Stalin's role than the revisionists and post-revisionists had done.
:::

:::definition{term="Archive"}
A collection of original documents (state papers, letters, minutes of meetings, diaries) kept for research. Historians treat such documents as primary evidence, but must judge who wrote them, why, and what is missing.
:::

:::definition{term="Empire by invitation"}
A phrase used by the Norwegian historian Geir Lundestad (1986) to argue that the American presence in Western Europe grew largely because European governments asked for it, unlike the Soviet hold on Eastern Europe, which was imposed.
:::

:::

## Russian and Soviet perspectives

**The official Soviet view.** Until the late 1980s, Soviet historians were expected to follow the Communist Party's line. They explained the Cold War as the result of American "imperialism": after 1945 the United States, with its atomic monopoly and capitalist interests, tried to dominate the world, to encircle the Soviet Union with bases and to crush socialism. The Soviet Union, on this account, defended peace and the independence of the peoples of Eastern Europe. This view resembles some revisionist arguments in the West, but it was part of an official ideology and allowed no public criticism of Stalin's policies.

**Russian historians since 1991.** There is no single Russian view. Some historians have used the archives to be critical of Stalin: Vladislav Zubok and Constantine Pleshakov, in *Inside the Kremlin's Cold War* (1996), argued that Stalin and his circle were driven by a mixture of Marxist-Leninist ideology, the ambitions of a revolutionary power that was also an heir to the Russian empire, and fear of the West. Stalin, on this account, was a cautious opportunist who exploited chances where he could, not a planner of world revolution, but one whose belief in the inevitable hostility of capitalism made compromise very difficult. Other Russian writers, especially in recent years, stress Western hostility and the expansion of NATO, and present Soviet policy as a defence of legitimate security interests. Because access to some Russian archives has been limited again since the early 2000s, the picture remains incomplete, and historians must be aware of political pressure on historical writing in any country.

## New sources and what they showed

Several sets of evidence changed the debate.

- **Soviet archives**, opened in the early 1990s, and the Cold War International History Project (founded in 1991 in the United States), which collected and translated documents. They revealed discussions among Soviet leaders about Germany, Poland and the atomic bomb, and Stalin's dealings with the communist leaders of Eastern Europe, China and Korea.
- **Eastern European and Chinese records**, which showed that local communist leaders had their own aims and that relations with Moscow were more complex than the idea of simple Soviet control.
- **Intelligence records**: the American "Venona" decrypts of Soviet cables, released in 1995, and a Soviet archivist's notes (the Mitrokhin archive, published in 1999), revealed the extent of Soviet espionage, including espionage on the atomic bomb.
- **Soviet admissions**: in 1990-92 the Soviet and then Russian governments acknowledged that the NKVD had killed Polish officers at Katyn in 1940, confirming the view that the Soviet Union had long denied.

## The 'New' Cold War history

John Lewis Gaddis summed up the argument in *We Now Know: Rethinking Cold War History* (1997). Using the new evidence, he argued that Stalin's ideology and personality were central to the origins of the Cold War: Stalin was a dictator whose suspicion and ambition left little room for compromise, and who needed a hostile outside world to justify his own system at home. In this view, the earlier revisionist claims that American economic expansion was the main cause were not supported by what the Soviet documents revealed. Gaddis also argued that the American position in Western Europe was, in Lundestad's phrase, an "empire by invitation", unlike the Soviet hold on the east. Other historians in this tradition, such as Vojtech Mastny, studied Stalin's mixture of insecurity and ambition.

The New Cold War history was not only about blame. It also widened the subject. Odd Arne Westad, in *The Global Cold War* (2005), argued that the superpowers' competition for influence in the Third World, including Africa, was at the heart of the Cold War, and that interventions by both sides (and by China and Cuba) in places such as Angola, Mozambique and Ethiopia had lasting effects. For students in Zimbabwe, this is not remote: the liberation movements received support from rival patrons, and the Cold War shaped the Rhodesian state's own claim to be defending the West against communism.

**Strengths.** It uses a wider range of primary sources, includes the view from the "other side", and draws attention to the roles of ideology and of individual leaders. **Weaknesses.** Critics argue that it can be triumphalist, written from the viewpoint of the victor, and that it may overstate Stalin's personal role. The archives are incomplete, and documents from a dictatorship are hard to read: records of what leaders said may not show what they believed, and some key files remain closed. Some historians (for example, those of the post-revisionist tradition such as Melvyn Leffler) warn that the new sources do not remove the need to examine American policy critically.

:::example{title="Weighing new evidence"}
Imagine a newly found record of a private Soviet meeting in 1946 in which leaders discuss fear of American bases and the need for a buffer zone. A student can ask four questions: Who wrote it, and for whom? Does it show intention or just discussion? Does it match other sources, such as Western documents or Soviet actions? And how far does it change an existing interpretation? Such a record might support the view that Soviet policy was driven by security, but a historian would also check whether it explains the methods used in Poland or Czechoslovakia, and would not rely on it alone.
:::

:::example{title="Using Katyn as evidence"}
The Soviet admission in 1990-92 that its secret police had killed Polish officers at Katyn shows two things. It confirms that official Soviet claims could be false, so earlier Western suspicions were not baseless. But it does not by itself prove that all Soviet policy was aggressive. A historian uses it as one piece of evidence about Soviet methods, not as a proof of overall aims.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming that new documents settle the argument"}
Archives add evidence but do not remove disagreement. Interpretations still differ about what the evidence means and which parts matter.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Russian historians as a single group"}
Soviet historians before 1991 were under official control, and since 1991 views have varied. Name the period and the argument.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling post-post-revisionism simply anti-revisionist"}
It uses new sources and often gives more weight to Soviet ideology, but it also widens the subject to the global Cold War and to the role of other states.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Official Soviet history blamed American imperialism; Russian historians since 1991 have held varied views and are sometimes constrained by access and politics.
- The opening of Soviet, East European and Chinese archives, Venona and Katyn admissions gave new evidence about the other side.
- The 'New' Cold War history (Gaddis, We Now Know, 1997) gave more weight to Stalin's ideology and personality and to Western Europe's 'empire by invitation'.
- It also widened the study to the global Cold War, including Africa (Westad, 2005).
- Critics warn of triumphalism, incomplete archives and overstating Stalin's role.
:::
