## Introduction

In May 1945 the armies of the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union met on the Elbe in Germany as allies who had destroyed Nazism. Four years later, in April 1949, the Western powers signed the North Atlantic Treaty to defend themselves against the Soviet Union, and Germany had been divided into two states. The rivalry between the Soviet Union and the United States and their allies, which fell short of direct war between the superpowers, is called the Cold War. This lesson sets out the events of 1941-49 in Europe, which form the context for the Paper 3 debate on who or what was to blame. The debate itself is in the next two lessons. The Cold War also shaped the liberation wars in southern Africa, where the two sides backed rival movements.

You should know how the Second World War ended in 1945 and the main results of the war from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cold War"}
The state of tension and rivalry between the Soviet bloc and the Western bloc after 1945, fought by propaganda, economic pressure, alliances, spying, an arms race and conflicts by proxy rather than by direct war between the main powers.
:::

:::definition{term="Iron Curtain"}
The phrase used by Winston Churchill in a speech at Fulton, Missouri, on 5 March 1946, for the dividing line between the Soviet-dominated east and the west of Europe.
:::

:::definition{term="Containment"}
The American policy of preventing the spread of communism to new countries, not by rolling it back where it was already established.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 4-11 February 1945: Yalta. 17 July-2 August 1945: Potsdam.
- 5 March 1946: Churchill's "Iron Curtain" speech. 12 March 1947: the Truman Doctrine. June 1947: the Marshall Plan announced.
- February 1948: the communist takeover in Czechoslovakia. 24 June 1948-12 May 1949: the Berlin Blockade and Airlift.
- 4 April 1949: NATO formed.
:::

## The wartime alliance and its strains

The United States, Britain and the Soviet Union fought together because of a common enemy, not common beliefs. Britain and the USA were capitalist democracies; the USSR was a communist dictatorship under Stalin, and its leaders had been suspicious of the West since the Russian Revolution. Even during the war there were tensions.

![Timeline of the origins of the Cold War: Yalta in February 1945, Potsdam, Churchill's Iron Curtain speech in March 1946, the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan of 1947, the Berlin Blockade from June 1948 and NATO in April 1949.](media:diagrams/cold-war-origins-timeline.png "Figure: timeline of the origins of the Cold War, 1945-1949")

- **The second front.** Stalin asked the Western Allies from 1941 to invade France, to relieve the pressure on the Red Army, which suffered the heaviest losses. They landed in Italy in 1943 and in Normandy only in June 1944, and Soviet leaders suspected that the delay was meant to weaken the USSR.
- **Poland.** The Soviet Union kept the eastern part of Poland that it had taken in 1939. In 1943 the Germans found the graves at Katyn of Polish officers killed by the Soviet secret police in 1940, and the USSR broke off relations with the Polish government in exile in London. During the Warsaw Uprising of 1944 the Red Army halted on the river and the Home Army was defeated.
- **The atomic bomb.** The Americans and British developed it in secret and did not share the secret fully with Stalin, who learned of it through spies and whom Truman told only briefly at Potsdam.
- **Aims for the peace.** Roosevelt and Churchill wanted free elections and open trade in Europe; Stalin wanted friendly governments and a buffer of states on the western border of the USSR, which had been invaded twice in thirty years.

## Yalta and Potsdam

At **Yalta** (4-11 February 1945) Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin agreed to divide Germany into zones, to hold free elections in liberated Europe (the Declaration on Liberated Europe), to move Poland's frontiers westward and to form a Polish government that included democratic leaders, and to create the United Nations. Stalin promised to enter the war against Japan. Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945 and was succeeded by Harry Truman, who was more suspicious of Stalin.

At **Potsdam** (17 July-2 August 1945) Truman, Stalin and Churchill (replaced on 28 July by the new Prime Minister, Clement Attlee) confirmed the zones and the aims of disarming Germany and trying war criminals, agreed that each power would take reparations mainly from its own zone, and set the Polish-German frontier on the Oder and Neisse rivers provisionally. They quarrelled about reparations, the governments of eastern Europe and the fate of Germany, and the atomic bomb, tested on 16 July, made the Americans more confident.

## The division of Europe, 1945-47

Red Army occupation allowed local communists, helped by Moscow, to take power in the countries of eastern Europe: Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary and later Czechoslovakia. Elections were rigged, as in Poland in 1947, rival parties were weakened and opponents were jailed. Churchill's speech in 1946 described an iron curtain across Europe, running from Stettin in the north to Trieste in the south. In February 1946 the American diplomat George Kennan sent his "Long Telegram" from Moscow, arguing that the Soviet Union was bound to be hostile and had to be contained. In 1946 the Soviet diplomat Nikolai Novikov sent a report from Washington warning that the United States was seeking world domination. Both reports show how each side read the other's actions.

## The Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan

In Greece a civil war between the government and communist guerrillas was in progress, and Britain, which could no longer afford to support the Greek government, asked the United States to take over. On **12 March 1947** Truman told Congress that the United States would help "free peoples" resisting subjugation, and asked for US$400 million for Greece and Turkey. This is the **Truman Doctrine**. In June 1947 Secretary of State George Marshall offered economic aid to all of Europe. The **Marshall Plan** (1948-52) gave over US$13 billion to Western Europe. Stalin forbade the countries of eastern Europe to accept it, regarding it as a means of American influence. He set up the Cominform in September 1947 and the Comecon economic council in January 1949, and Soviet spokesmen described the world as divided into two camps.

## Germany, Berlin and NATO

Disagreements about Germany led the Western powers to combine their zones and, in June 1948, to introduce a new currency (the Deutschmark) in them. Stalin saw this as a step towards a separate West German state. On **24 June 1948** the Soviet Union blocked road, rail and canal routes into West Berlin, which lay inside the Soviet zone, hoping to force the Western powers out. The Western powers answered with the **Berlin Airlift**, flying in food and fuel to over two million people; more than two million tonnes of supplies were delivered in about eleven months. The Blockade ended on 12 May 1949 without war. 

The crisis pushed the West to unite. In April 1949 twelve states, including the United States, Britain, France and Canada, signed the North Atlantic Treaty, creating **NATO**, a defensive alliance in which an attack on one was an attack on all. In May 1949 West Germany was formed and in October East Germany followed. The Soviet Union exploded its first atomic bomb in August 1949.

:::example{title="Using Yalta and Potsdam"}
A candidate asked whether the Cold War was inevitable can use the two conferences both ways: at Yalta the leaders agreed principles that kept the alliance together, so cooperation had been possible; by Potsdam new leaders, quarrels over Poland and Germany and the atomic bomb showed conflicting aims. Both belong in a balanced answer.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a famous speech"}
Churchill's speech at Fulton (5 March 1946) can be read in two ways. As evidence for Soviet expansion, it described a division of Europe that already existed. As evidence for Western attitudes, it was a call for Anglo-American firmness made by a former Prime Minister then in opposition, which Stalin condemned as a declaration of hostility. A good answer notes who spoke, to whom, and what effect it had.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the Cold War as starting on a single date"}
Historians date its start variously (wartime tensions, 1945, 1946-47, 1948-49). Give your own dating and reasons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan"}
The Doctrine (March 1947) promised support to states resisting communism, with military and economic aid to Greece and Turkey. The Plan (June 1947) offered economic aid to all of Europe.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the blame on one side in a context lesson"}
The context lesson sets out events. Judgements about responsibility belong in the debate, with evidence on both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 3"}
The extract questions test whether you know the facts well enough to judge the historian's interpretation: Yalta and Potsdam decisions, the control of eastern Europe, the Doctrine and Plan, the Blockade and NATO. Learn the sequence and the dates, and be able to say how a given event could be read as Soviet expansion or as Soviet defence, American leadership or American economic ambition.

## Summary

:::summary
- The wartime alliance was held together by a common enemy, and was strained by the second front, Poland, the atomic bomb and rival aims.
- Yalta and Potsdam reached agreements on Germany and Europe, but disagreement grew over Poland, reparations and elections.
- Soviet influence spread across eastern Europe by 1948, while the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan committed the United States to Western Europe.
- The Berlin Blockade (June 1948-May 1949) and the Airlift were the first major crisis; the West responded with NATO in April 1949.
- Germany was divided into two states in 1949, and Europe into two blocs.
:::
