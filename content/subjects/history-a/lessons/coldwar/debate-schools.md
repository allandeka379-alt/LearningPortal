## Introduction

Few questions in modern history have been argued over as fiercely as "Who or what was to blame for the Cold War?" Historians who write about it are also citizens of the countries they study, and their answers have changed with the times, the evidence available and their own experience. The Cambridge Paper 3 syllabus asks you to explain how far tensions were inherent in 1945, to weigh the importance of leaders, ideology, security and economics, and to understand the three main schools of interpretation. This lesson explains each, paraphrases the arguments, and shows how to test an extract against the facts. It builds on the narrative in the previous lesson.

You should know the events of 1941-49 from the lesson on the origins of the Cold War.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Historiography"}
The study of how history has been written: the different interpretations of an event and the reasons they differ, including the time, place and evidence available to the historians.
:::

:::definition{term="Orthodox (traditionalist) school"}
The interpretation, dominant in the West in the 1940s and 1950s, that blamed Soviet expansion and communist ideology for the Cold War.
:::

:::definition{term="Revisionist school"}
The interpretation, prominent from the 1960s, that blamed American economic and political expansion, or at least stressed American responsibility.
:::

:::definition{term="Post-revisionist school"}
The interpretation that emerged in the 1970s and 1980s, which looked at the actions of both superpowers, and at security fears and misperception, rather than blaming one side.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 3"}
This lesson supports the Paper 3 debate on the causes of the Cold War (A Level only). It is not a ZIMSEC topic. In the extract question, identify which school the extract's argument resembles, but remember that real historians do not always fit a label.
:::

## Were tensions inherent in 1945?

Some historians argue that conflict was almost bound to occur. The war destroyed the old balance of power: Germany and Japan were crushed, Britain and France were weakened, and two powers with different systems were left far stronger than the rest. This is the **bipolar** world. The Soviet Union was a communist state that believed capitalism would give way to socialism, and the United States was a capitalist democracy that feared the spread of communism. With a power vacuum in Europe, and with each side certain that its own security required the other's weakness, rivalry seemed likely. Against this, other historians point out that cooperation in 1945 was still possible, that the alliance had survived a hard war, and that the actual path of the Cold War depended on decisions that could have been different. A good answer notes that "inherent" tensions do not explain the timing and form of the Cold War.

## Factors in the debate

**Personalities.** Stalin has been seen as a ruthless and suspicious dictator who trusted nobody, and who after the war feared being weakened by the West. Truman was less experienced than Roosevelt in foreign affairs, and some historians argue that his blunt approach, and his advisers' mistrust, hardened relations. Others suggest that Roosevelt's approach of trust could not have lasted and that the structure of the situation mattered more than the men. Churchill's speech of 1946 helped to make the language of division public.

**Ideology.** Marxism-Leninism taught that capitalism and communism were in conflict, and American leaders saw communism as a threat to freedom and trade. Traditionalists stress the effect of communist ideology on Soviet aims; revisionists stress the American belief in free markets and an "open door" for American goods and investment. Post-revisionists note that both sides used ideology to justify what they did for other reasons.

**Security.** The Soviet Union had been invaded twice in thirty years and had lost millions of people; it sought a buffer zone in Eastern Europe. The United States held the atomic bomb until 1949 and wished to prevent Soviet domination of Europe and Asia. Each side's measures for security were seen as threats by the other, which is called the security dilemma.

**Economics.** The USSR needed to rebuild and sought reparations from Germany. The American economy needed markets in Europe, and Marshall Plan aid served both the recovery of Europe and the export of American goods. Revisionists see economic expansion as central; critics say that this exaggerates American aims.

## The three schools

**The traditionalist (orthodox) school.** Writing in the 1940s and 1950s, when the Cold War was at its height, historians such as Herbert Feis argued that Stalin's expansionism and communist ideology were the main causes, and that the United States responded reluctantly to Soviet aggression in Eastern Europe, Greece and Berlin. Arthur Schlesinger Jr, in a famous article of 1967 in *Foreign Affairs*, recognised American mistakes but still held that the Soviet Union's ideology and Stalin's character made the conflict unavoidable. **Strengths:** it explains the Soviet takeover of Eastern Europe and the rigged elections. **Weaknesses:** it takes American claims at face value, and gives little weight to Soviet security fears or the American use of the atomic bomb. **Context:** it reflected the Western view at the height of the Cold War.

**The revisionist school.** From the late 1950s, and especially in the 1960s, the Vietnam War and a more critical view of American foreign policy prompted new questions. William Appleman Williams, in *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959), argued that the American search for open markets overseas led to expansion. Gar Alperovitz, in *Atomic Diplomacy* (1965), argued that Truman used the bomb partly to intimidate the Soviet Union. Gabriel Kolko stressed the economic interests behind American policy. Revisionists suggested that the Soviet Union acted defensively, and that the United States, with its economic strength and nuclear weapons, pushed the pace. **Strengths:** it challenged the assumptions of the traditionalists and used American archives. **Weaknesses:** critics say that it neglected Stalin's repression and the brutal methods used in Eastern Europe, and that the evidence for American economic motives is weaker than for security fears. **Context:** it reflected disillusion with American foreign policy over Vietnam.

**The post-revisionist school.** From the 1970s historians such as John Lewis Gaddis, in *The United States and the Origins of the Cold War, 1941-1947* (1972), argued that both sides share responsibility, that leaders acted from security fears and domestic pressures, and that mistakes and misperception hardened the division. Melvyn Leffler, in *A Preponderance of Power* (1992), argued that American officials were driven mainly by a search for security and wished to ensure that no hostile power controlled the resources of Eurasia, which led them to build up strength; in this view the United States was neither a mere victim nor a ruthless aggressor. **Strengths:** it uses more evidence and avoids one-sided blame. **Weaknesses:** critics argue that it sometimes balances responsibility too evenly, even when one side's actions were more aggressive. **Context:** it appeared in the period of détente, when relations between the superpowers eased and more documents were released.

:::example{title="Testing an extract against the schools"}
Consider this statement: "By holding on to the atomic bomb and pressing for open markets, the United States set the terms of the postwar order, and Stalin's measures in Eastern Europe were a defensive reaction." It resembles the revisionist school. To test it, find support (American atomic monopoly, the Marshall Plan's economic benefits, the Soviet experience of invasion) and challenges (the speed and brutality of the Soviet takeover in Poland and Czechoslovakia, the Soviet role in the Greek crisis, Stalin's own ambitions). Then decide: the statement is useful in raising American power, but it understates Soviet responsibility for the division of Europe.
:::

:::example{title="Placing a historian in context"}
If asked why revisionist historians were critical of the United States in the 1960s, give the time and a reason. The Vietnam War made many Americans doubt their government's motives, and historians began to ask whether earlier policy had also been less defensive than claimed. This explains why the argument appeared when it did, without proving that it is correct.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the schools as fixed labels for every historian"}
Many historians combine ideas, or changed their views. Say that an argument "resembles" a school, and name historians only with their arguments.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a list of historians instead of an argument"}
Listing names earns little credit. Use the school to explain the argument of the extract, and test it with evidence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the context of each school"}
Explain why each school arose when it did: the high Cold War, the Vietnam era and détente. This shows that you understand why interpretations change.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 3"}
The extract question asks about the historian's interpretation and approach: identify the main argument, say whose responsibility it stresses and which factors (ideology, security, economics, personalities), and test it using your knowledge. For the essay, "To what extent was the Soviet Union to blame for the Cold War?", give evidence of Soviet responsibility and of American responsibility, show how the schools differ, and reach a clear judgement. Never quote; paraphrase.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Some tensions were inherent in 1945, because the war left two rival superpowers, but the form and timing of the Cold War depended on decisions.
- Personalities, ideology, security and economics all played a part, and historians weigh them differently.
- Traditionalists (Feis, Schlesinger) blamed Soviet expansion and ideology; revisionists (Williams, Alperovitz, Kolko) blamed American economic and political expansion.
- Post-revisionists (Gaddis, Leffler) stressed security fears, misperception and the responsibility of both sides.
- Each school reflects its time: the high Cold War, the Vietnam era and détente.
:::
