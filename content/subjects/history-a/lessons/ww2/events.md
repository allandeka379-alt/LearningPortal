## Introduction

The Second World War lasted from 1939 to 1945 and was fought in Europe, Africa, the Atlantic, Asia and the Pacific. A syllabus cannot cover every campaign, and an examiner does not expect it. You are asked to understand turning points: moments when the balance of power changed and the final result became more likely. This lesson follows the war in order, picks out the turning points and shows where Africa and Africans fit into the story. Thousands of Zimbabweans served in the armed forces of Southern Rhodesia, and African soldiers from British, French and Belgian colonies fought in Europe, North Africa, East Africa and Burma.

You should know from the lesson on the causes how the war began in September 1939.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blitzkrieg"}
"Lightning war": the German method of fast attacks combining tanks, aircraft and infantry to break through enemy lines and surround armies, used in Poland in 1939 and France in 1940.
:::

:::definition{term="Turning point"}
An event after which the course of the war changed in a lasting way. Which events deserve the name is a matter of judgement, and examiners reward answers that give reasons.
:::

:::definition{term="Total war"}
War in which a state mobilises its whole economy and population, including civilians, for the war effort, and in which civilians become targets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- 1940: the fall of France (armistice 22 June); the Battle of Britain (summer-autumn).
- 22 June 1941: Barbarossa. 7 December 1941: Pearl Harbor.
- June 1942: Midway. Autumn 1942: El Alamein. August 1942-2 February 1943: Stalingrad.
- 6 June 1944: D-Day. 8 May 1945: victory in Europe. 6 and 9 August 1945: Hiroshima and Nagasaki. 2 September 1945: Japan formally surrenders.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC only"}

:::

## 1939-40: German victories in Europe

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 with fast tank and air attacks (blitzkrieg), and the Soviet Union entered from the east on 17 September under the secret protocol of the Pact. Poland was divided. In April 1940 Germany took Denmark and Norway; on 10 May it attacked the Netherlands, Belgium and France, bypassing the strong Maginot Line through the Ardennes. Between 26 May and 4 June about 338,000 British and allied soldiers were evacuated from Dunkirk. France asked for an armistice on 22 June 1940; the north and the Atlantic coast were occupied, and a French government at Vichy ruled the south. General de Gaulle in London led the Free French. Italy entered the war on 10 June 1940.

Britain, now led by Winston Churchill, stood alone. In the Battle of Britain (summer to autumn 1940) the Royal Air Force defeated the German Luftwaffe's attempt to win control of the air, and Hitler postponed the invasion of Britain. The bombing of British cities continued through the winter (the Blitz).

## Africa and the Mediterranean

North and East Africa were battlefields. Italian forces in Libya attacked Egypt in 1940, and in 1941 Rommel's German Afrika Korps arrived to help them. The war swung back and forth until the Second Battle of El Alamein (October-November 1942), when General Montgomery's Eighth Army won a decisive victory. In November 1942 American and British forces landed in French North Africa (Operation Torch), and the Axis forces in Tunisia surrendered in May 1943. In East Africa, British Empire forces, with African troops from Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria, the Gold Coast and South Africa, and with Ethiopian patriots, defeated the Italians in 1940-41; Emperor Haile Selassie returned to Addis Ababa in May 1941. Chad, under the Black governor Félix Éboué, declared for Free France in August 1940.

Africa also supplied people and raw materials: African soldiers served in the Mediterranean and in Burma, where the Rhodesian African Rifles and West and East African divisions fought the Japanese; African labourers built roads and airfields; the Copperbelt provided copper; and Southern Rhodesia hosted air training schools for Commonwealth pilots. These contributions were large, and many veterans came home with new ideas, a point returned to in the lesson on results.

## 1941: the war widens

On 22 June 1941 Hitler launched **Operation Barbarossa**, the invasion of the Soviet Union, breaking the Pact. It was by far the largest land invasion in history. German armies advanced hundreds of kilometres, but the attack on Moscow stalled in the winter of 1941. On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked the American fleet at **Pearl Harbor** in Hawaii and, within weeks, British, Dutch and American possessions in South-East Asia; Singapore fell on 15 February 1942. Germany declared war on the United States on 11 December, so that two great industrial powers joined the Allies. Most historians see 1941 as the year when the war became a world war and Germany's chances of winning a long war shrank.

## 1942-43: the tide turns

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| June 1942 | Battle of Midway: US Navy sinks four Japanese aircraft carriers |
| August 1942 - 2 February 1943 | Battle of Stalingrad |
| October-November 1942 | Second Battle of El Alamein |
| November 1942 | Allied landings in North Africa (Torch) |
| July 1943 | Battle of Kursk; Allies invade Sicily |
| September 1943 | Italy surrenders after Mussolini's fall in July |

At **Midway** (4-7 June 1942) the United States Navy sank four Japanese aircraft carriers and ended Japan's ability to attack across the Pacific. At **Stalingrad** the German Sixth Army was surrounded in the winter of 1942-43 and surrendered on 31 January-2 February 1943, with enormous losses. It was the first great German defeat on land and the Soviet Union began to advance. At El Alamein and in Tunisia the Axis lost North Africa. In July 1943 the Soviets won the tank battle of Kursk, and the Allies invaded Sicily, which led to Mussolini's fall and Italy's surrender.

:::example{title="Was Stalingrad the main turning point?"}
A strong paragraph can argue that Stalingrad was the decisive turning point in Europe because Germany lost an entire army and could no longer win in the east; the Soviet Union then fought its way to Berlin. A rival view would give weight to the entry of the United States in December 1941 (its factories made Allied victory almost certain in a long war), or to Midway for the Pacific, or to the Battle of Britain, which kept the island free as a base. The best answers compare these cases, define what a turning point is, and decide which event changed the outcome most.
:::

## 1944-45: the defeat of the Axis

On **6 June 1944** (D-Day) the Western Allies landed in Normandy, the largest seaborne invasion in history, and Paris was liberated in August. In the east the Soviet summer offensive of 1944 (Operation Bagration) destroyed German Army Group Centre. A German counter-attack in the Ardennes in December 1944 (the Battle of the Bulge) failed. In February 1945 Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin met at Yalta. Soviet troops entered Berlin in April, and Hitler took his own life on 30 April. Germany surrendered on 7-8 May (VE Day, 8 May).

In the Pacific the Americans advanced by "island-hopping", taking Guadalcanal, the Philippines, Iwo Jima and Okinawa, and bombed Japanese cities. On **6 August 1945** an American aircraft dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima and on 9 August another on Nagasaki; the Soviet Union declared war on Japan on 8 August. Estimates are that about 140,000 and 70,000 people had died in the two cities by the end of 1945. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August, and signed the formal surrender on 2 September 1945. Historians continue to debate whether the bombs were needed to end the war or whether Japan was already near surrender and the Soviet entry would have been enough.

:::example{title="Building a chain of consequences"}
Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) brought the United States into the war; Germany then declared war on the United States on 11 December; American production supplied Britain and the Soviet Union; and together with Stalingrad and the second front this led to Germany's defeat. Writing the links in order turns a list of events into an explanation of a turning point.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing campaigns instead of explaining turning points"}
Do not retell battles. State why an event changed the course of the war and compare it with others.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting Africa"}
North and East Africa were theatres of war, and African soldiers and workers contributed a great deal. Use at least one African example when the question allows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Midway and Pearl Harbor, or D-Day and VE Day"}
Pearl Harbor (December 1941) was a Japanese attack on the US; Midway (June 1942) was an American victory. D-Day (6 June 1944) was the Normandy landing; VE Day was 8 May 1945.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Which was the most important turning point of the Second World War?", "Account for the defeat of Germany", "Describe the role of Africa in the war." Give dates, state criteria for importance, compare two or three events and reach a reasoned judgement. For "defeat of Germany", combine Soviet power, American production, Allied cooperation and German mistakes such as attacking the USSR.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Germany's blitzkrieg conquered Poland and France in 1939-40; Britain survived the Battle of Britain.
- North and East Africa were battlefields; African troops, workers and raw materials contributed to Allied victory.
- Barbarossa and Pearl Harbor in 1941 widened the war and brought the Soviet Union and the United States against the Axis.
- Midway, Stalingrad and El Alamein in 1942-43 turned the tide; D-Day and the Soviet advance defeated Germany by May 1945.
- The atomic bombs of August 1945 and the Soviet declaration of war ended the war with Japan on 2 September 1945.
:::
