## Introduction

When the guns fell silent in 1945, Europe and much of Asia lay in ruins, tens of millions were dead and millions more were homeless. Yet the war also produced a new world order: two superpowers, a new world organisation, a divided Germany and, within two decades, the end of most European empires in Africa. This lesson sets out the results of the war and asks which were most important. For a student in Zimbabwe the last of these matters most, because the war changed how Africans and Europeans saw colonial rule.

You should know from the lesson on events how the war ended in 1945.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Crimes against humanity"}
A category of crime defined at Nuremberg: murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation and other inhuman acts committed against civilian populations, before or during a war.
:::

:::definition{term="Superpower"}
A state with such military and economic strength that it can influence events across the world. After 1945 the term described the United States and the Soviet Union.
:::

:::definition{term="Displaced persons"}
People forced from their homes by war: survivors of camps, forced labourers and refugees, of whom millions were in Europe in 1945.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 26 June 1945: the UN Charter signed at San Francisco; in force 24 October 1945.
- 20 November 1945-1 October 1946: the Nuremberg trial of the major war criminals.
- 1947: the Truman Doctrine (12 March) and the Marshall Plan announced (June). 1949: the Federal Republic of Germany (May) and the German Democratic Republic (October).
- October 1945: the Fifth Pan-African Congress meets in Manchester.
:::

## Human and material losses

The numbers are uncertain and every figure is an estimate. Historians estimate that about 70 to 85 million people died as a result of the war, most of them civilians, in battle, bombing, massacres, famine and disease. Among the worst-hit countries (estimates):

| Country or group | Estimated deaths |
|---|---|
| Soviet Union | about 24-27 million |
| China | about 15-20 million |
| Germany | about 6-8 million |
| Poland | about 5-6 million |
| Japan | about 2.5-3 million |
| Jews murdered in the Holocaust | about 6 million (also counted in the national totals above) |

Britain lost under half a million people and the United States about 400,000. Africans served and died in large numbers, but the records are poor and no reliable overall figure exists. Cities such as Warsaw, Berlin, Stalingrad and Hiroshima were destroyed, and industry, railways and farmland were wrecked. The war also left millions of displaced persons.

## Punishing the guilty: Nuremberg

The Allies set up an International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg. Between 20 November 1945 and 1 October 1946 twenty-two leading Nazis were tried for crimes against peace, war crimes, crimes against humanity and conspiracy. Twelve were sentenced to death, seven to prison and three acquitted; Göring killed himself before he could be hanged. Further trials judged doctors, judges and industrialists. A similar tribunal tried Japanese leaders in Tokyo (1946-48). Nuremberg established the principle that individuals, including leaders, can be held responsible for crimes under international law and that "following orders" is not a defence, which later influenced human rights law. Critics said it was "victors' justice", since Allied actions such as the bombing of cities were not judged, and the Soviet Union, which had signed the Pact with Germany in 1939, sat among the judges.

## Germany divided

At Potsdam in July-August 1945 the victors agreed to disarm Germany, destroy Nazism and try war criminals. Germany lost territory in the east to Poland and the USSR, and was divided into four zones of occupation (American, British, French and Soviet), with Berlin, deep inside the Soviet zone, also divided among the four. Disagreements, especially over reparations and economic policy, led the three Western zones to combine. In May 1949 they became the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) and in October the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic (East Germany). Germany was divided for forty years.

## The United Nations

The war convinced many leaders that a stronger world body was needed than the League of Nations. The UN Charter was signed by fifty states at San Francisco on 26 June 1945 and took effect on 24 October. It had a General Assembly of all members and a Security Council with five permanent members (the USA, USSR, Britain, France and China), each holding a veto, so that the major powers could not be forced to act against their will. The UN also created agencies for refugees, health, food and children. Its limits became clear when the permanent members disagreed during the Cold War.

## The beginning of the Cold War

The wartime alliance between the Soviet Union and its Western partners was an alliance against a common enemy. Once Germany was beaten, differences over eastern Europe, Germany and ideology led to rivalry. By 1947-49 Europe was split between a Soviet-dominated east and a Western bloc supported by the United States. The causes and the debate about responsibility are examined in the Cold War lessons.

## Recovery in Europe

Western Europe's economies were damaged, and many feared that hunger would favour communism. The American **Marshall Plan** (announced in June 1947 and run from 1948 to 1952) gave Western European countries over US\$13 billion in aid, which helped them to rebuild and trade. The Soviet Union rejected the Plan for itself and its allies, taking resources from its zone and eastern Europe instead. West Germany's recovery was rapid in the 1950s. The Bretton Woods agreements of 1944 had already created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank to support a stable trading system.

## The effect on empires and African nationalism

:::example{title="Explaining the link between the war and decolonisation"}
A good answer separates the war's effects into steps. First, Britain and France were weakened financially and militarily and could no longer easily afford their empires. Second, the Atlantic Charter of August 1941 spoke of the right of peoples to choose their government, and African leaders asked whether it applied to them; the colonial powers had also fought a war against racial domination. Third, hundreds of thousands of African soldiers had served overseas, learned new skills and seen Europeans in defeat, and returned expecting reward. Fourth, the United States and the Soviet Union, the new superpowers, were both officially against old-style empires. Finally, the Fifth Pan-African Congress in Manchester (October 1945), with leaders such as Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta, demanded African independence. A balanced answer adds that independence took years of struggle and that settler colonies such as Southern Rhodesia followed a different path.
:::

In Southern Rhodesia the post-war years brought industrial growth, more white immigration and a rise in African urban workers' strikes: the railway workers' strike of 1945 and the general strike of 1948 showed a new labour movement. Elsewhere independence came to India and Pakistan in 1947, Ghana in 1957 and many African states around 1960. Italy lost its colonies, and Libya became independent in 1951. Some historians argue that the war was the main cause of decolonisation; others stress African nationalism, which was growing before 1939, so that the war accelerated rather than created the movement.

:::example{title="Using estimates honestly"}
To describe the cost of the war, write: "Historians estimate that about 70 to 85 million people died, among them an estimated 24 to 27 million in the Soviet Union." The words "estimate" and the ranges show that you know the figures are uncertain. An exam answer that gives one exact total without qualification is less careful than one that names the range.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving exact death tolls as if they were certain"}
All casualty figures are estimates and vary. Write "about" and give a range, as historians do.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the UN with the League"}
The UN had American and Soviet membership and a Security Council with a veto. The League had no US membership and no standing force.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Germany was divided at Potsdam"}
Zones of occupation were agreed in 1945; the two German states were created in 1949.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Assess the consequences of the Second World War", "Why was the United Nations formed in 1945?", "How did the war help African nationalism?" Pick three or four results, explain them and rank them. Separate immediate results (deaths, trials, zones) from long-term ones (the Cold War, decolonisation).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Between about 70 and 85 million people died, and the Soviet Union and China suffered the greatest losses (estimates).
- The Nuremberg trials (1945-46) tried leading Nazis and set the principle of individual responsibility.
- Germany was occupied in four zones and divided into two states in 1949.
- The United Nations replaced the League in 1945 with a Security Council and a veto for five great powers.
- The war weakened European empires, raised African expectations and hastened decolonisation.
:::
