## Introduction

The Second World War began in Europe on 1 September 1939, only twenty-one years after the end of the First. In Asia it had begun earlier, with Japan's invasion of China. The war became global, drew in millions of Africans as soldiers, labourers and victims of shortages, and changed the world order. Historians do not agree on a single cause; they weigh the long-term conditions that made war likely against the decisions of 1938-39 that made it certain. For a student in Zimbabwe, the question has a particular importance, because the weakness of the League of Nations over Ethiopia and the failure of the European powers to keep peace shaped the post-war move towards the United Nations and the claim of Africans for self-determination.

You should know the main features of the Treaty of Versailles, the League of Nations and the policies of Hitler and Mussolini from earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Collective security"}
The idea that peace could be kept if all states promised to act together against an aggressor. It was the basis of the League of Nations, but it worked only if the major powers were willing to act.
:::

:::definition{term="Appeasement"}
The policy, followed above all by Britain under Neville Chamberlain (Prime Minister 1937-40), of settling Germany's grievances by negotiation and concession in the hope of avoiding war.
:::

:::definition{term="Diktat"}
A "dictated peace": the German name for the Treaty of Versailles, which Germans had to accept without negotiation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1931: Japan invades Manchuria. 1935: Italy invades Ethiopia. 1936: the Rhineland. 1937: Japan's war with China widens.
- 12 March 1938: the Anschluss. 29-30 September 1938: the Munich Agreement. 15 March 1939: German troops occupy Prague.
- 31 March 1939: Britain and France guarantee Poland. 23 August 1939: the Nazi-Soviet Pact. 1 September 1939: Germany invades Poland. 3 September: Britain and France declare war.
:::

## Long-term causes

**The Treaty of Versailles.** Germany lost territory, its colonies (including Namibia, then German South West Africa, and Tanganyika), had to limit its army, accepted blame for the war and paid reparations. Many Germans thought the treaty a humiliating diktat and politicians of all parties wished to revise it, which Hitler used. At the same time Italy was disappointed with its gains and Japan felt that it had not been treated as an equal. Some historians argue that the treaty was too harsh to be accepted and too weak to prevent Germany's revival.

**The Depression.** The collapse of world trade after 1929 brought mass unemployment and helped the Nazis and the Japanese military to power. It also made democratic governments cautious about spending on arms, and states raised tariffs and looked after themselves, weakening cooperation.

**The dictators and aggression.** Fascism and militarism glorified war and expansion. Mussolini wished to build an empire, Hitler wanted Lebensraum and to undo Versailles, and the Japanese military sought raw materials and territory in Asia.

**The League and collective security.** The League lacked the United States, which never joined, and was unable to use force. It failed to stop Japan in Manchuria (1931-33), when it condemned Japan's invasion of Chinese territory but Japan left the League in 1933. It failed again over Ethiopia in 1935-36, when sanctions excluded oil and Britain and France preferred to keep Italy as a possible ally against Germany. The Disarmament Conference collapsed when Germany left in 1933. Each failure encouraged the aggressors.

**Appeasement.** Britain and France had many reasons to avoid confrontation: memories of the slaughter of 1914-18, fear of bombing, economic difficulty and weak armies, a belief that parts of Versailles were unfair, fear of communism and the need to protect their empires in Africa and Asia. Chamberlain also believed that Hitler's demands could be satisfied. Critics argue that giving way strengthened Hitler and lost the chance to stop him earlier when Germany was weaker. Defenders argue that appeasement gave Britain time to rearm and showed the public that every effort for peace had been made.

## Japan in Asia

The Japanese army seized Manchuria after the Mukden incident of September 1931 and set up the puppet state of Manchukuo. In July 1937 a clash near Beijing started a full-scale war with China, in which Japanese forces captured Nanjing in December 1937 and committed massacres of civilians there. Japan wanted resources and markets. Its alliance with Germany and Italy in the Tripartite Pact (1940) and its move into French Indo-China in 1940-41 brought it into conflict with the United States and Britain.

## The short-term sequence, 1938-39

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| March 1936 | German troops enter the Rhineland |
| 12 March 1938 | Anschluss: Germany and Austria united |
| September 1938 | Sudeten crisis; Munich Agreement (29-30 September) |
| 15 March 1939 | Germany occupies Bohemia and Moravia (Prague) |
| 31 March 1939 | Britain and France guarantee Poland |
| 23 August 1939 | Nazi-Soviet Pact |
| 1 September 1939 | Germany invades Poland |
| 3 September 1939 | Britain and France declare war on Germany |

**The Anschluss.** In March 1938 Hitler forced the Austrian government to accept German troops, and union was approved by a plebiscite in April. The Western powers protested but did nothing.

**Munich.** Hitler demanded that the Sudetenland, a region of Czechoslovakia with a large German-speaking population, be given to Germany. At Munich (29-30 September 1938) Chamberlain, Daladier, Mussolini and Hitler agreed to the transfer; the Czechoslovak government was not invited. Chamberlain returned announcing "peace for our time".

**Prague.** In March 1939 Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, breaking the Munich promises. It showed that Hitler's aims went beyond uniting Germans, and Britain and France then changed policy: they promised to defend Poland and began to rearm faster.

**The Nazi-Soviet Pact.** On 23 August 1939, Germany and the Soviet Union signed a non-aggression pact with a secret protocol that divided eastern Europe into spheres of influence. Stalin had been alarmed by the Munich Agreement, from which the USSR was excluded, and doubted that the West would fight; Hitler wanted to avoid a war on two fronts. The pact made the attack on Poland possible.

**Poland.** Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939 and two days later Britain and France declared war.

:::example{title="Weighing responsibility"}
In 1961 the historian A.J.P. Taylor argued that Hitler was an opportunist who took advantage of other states' mistakes and that the war grew from the failures of statesmen as much as from a Nazi plan. Most historians have rejected the stronger claims in his argument, pointing to Hitler's consistent aims of expansion and race war and the central role of the Nazi regime, but they accept that appeasement and the weakness of the League made his success easier. A strong essay assigns the main responsibility to Germany, but explains how the other states' choices allowed it.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining a cause step by step"}
Take the remilitarisation of the Rhineland in March 1936. Step one: Hitler broke Versailles and Locarno. Step two: Britain and France did not act. Step three: Hitler concluded that they would not use force. Step four: this encouraged the Anschluss and the Sudeten demands in 1938. A paragraph that links each step shows why the event mattered to the outbreak of war, instead of only describing it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming appeasement or Versailles alone"}
Both mattered, but so did the Depression, the dictators' aims and the failure of collective security. Rank the causes and justify the ranking.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dating the Anschluss and Munich wrongly"}
The Anschluss was in March 1938 and concerned Austria; Munich was in September 1938 and concerned the Sudetenland. Prague (March 1939) followed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring Asia"}
The war in Asia began with Manchuria in 1931 and the invasion of China in 1937. Mention Japan when the question is about the causes of the war as a whole.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Account for the outbreak of the Second World War", "To what extent was appeasement responsible?" and "How far was the Treaty of Versailles a cause of the war?" Plan three or four causes in order of importance, give dates, link each cause to the outbreak (not only describe it), and end with a clear judgement. Remember that the League's failure over Ethiopia is also African history.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Treaty of Versailles left grievances in Germany, Italy and Japan, and the Depression brought extremists to power.
- Fascist and militarist leaders pursued expansion, and the League failed to stop Japan in Manchuria or Italy in Ethiopia.
- Britain and France practised appeasement because of fear of war, weak forces and belief that parts of Versailles were unjust.
- The Anschluss, Munich and the occupation of Prague showed that Hitler's aims were unlimited, and the Nazi-Soviet Pact made the attack on Poland possible.
- Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939; Britain and France declared war on 3 September.
:::
