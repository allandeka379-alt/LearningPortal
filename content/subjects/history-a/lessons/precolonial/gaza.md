## Introduction

The Mfecane did not end at the Limpopo. In the 1820s a Nguni general named Soshangane led his followers away from the wars between the Ndwandwe and the Zulu and built a new kingdom in what is now southern Mozambique and the south-east of Zimbabwe. This was the **Gaza state**, named after Soshangane's grandfather Gasa. For about seventy years it dominated the lower Limpopo and Save valleys. It ended in 1895, when a Portuguese army defeated its last king, Ngungunyana (also written Gungunyana or Gungunhana).

This lesson covers the founding and organisation of the Gaza state, its trade and its dealings with the Portuguese, and the reasons for its fall. The Mfecane background is in the lesson on the Zulu state and the neighbouring Ndebele state is in its own lesson, so you can compare how Nguni migrants founded states in different places.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tributary"}
A people who are not part of the ruling group but pay tribute to a more powerful ruler. The Tsonga, Chopi and Ndau were tributaries of Gaza.
:::

:::definition{term="Vassal"}
A ruler who accepts the authority of a stronger ruler, often paying tribute or promising loyalty in return for protection.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- About 1819-1821: after the Zulu defeat of Zwide's Ndwandwe, Soshangane leads his followers north (approximate).
- 1820s-1830s: Soshangane founds the Gaza state between the Limpopo and Save rivers.
- About 1858: Soshangane dies; his sons Mawewe and Mzila (Muzila) fight for the throne, and Mzila wins about 1862 with Portuguese help.
- About 1884: Ngungunyana succeeds Mzila.
- 7 November 1895: Portuguese forces defeat the Gaza army at Coolela; 28 December 1895: Ngungunyana is captured at Chaimite.
:::

## Origins and expansion

Soshangane (Soshangane Manukuza) was a general in Zwide's Ndwandwe army. When Shaka defeated the Ndwandwe about 1819, Soshangane led his followers north with their cattle. They passed through southern Mozambique, defeating or absorbing the Tsonga, Chopi and other peoples they met, and they moved up the Save valley towards south-eastern Zimbabwe. Soshangane's armies raided widely and in 1833 attacked the Portuguese fort at Delagoa Bay. He set up his chief capital in central southern Mozambique, and his kingdom reached the Limpopo in the south and the Save and the eastern highlands of Zimbabwe in the north. In the 1830s he also came into conflict with the Nguni leader Zwangendaba and Nxaba, who were moving north.

## Organisation of the state

**Political.** The king (*inkosi*) held supreme power, aided by *izinduna*. The core of the state was the Nguni ruling group, who held the highest positions. The conquered Tsonga, Chopi and Ndau were governed by appointed officials or local chiefs who paid tribute and sent young men to serve. The capital moved from time to time, and under Ngungunyana the main town was at Mandlakazi.

**Military.** As in the Zulu and Ndebele states, young men were organised in age regiments (*amabutho*) under the king's officers. The army raided neighbours for cattle and captives, collected tribute and defended the state. Captured young men were absorbed into the army, so the Nguni identity spread over time.

**Economic.** Gaza economy depended on cattle, farming by conquered peoples and women, tribute in grain, cattle and ivory, and trade. Rulers traded ivory and, later, other goods to the Portuguese on the coast. In the 1880s many Gaza men went to work in the South African mines, and the wages bought guns and cloth. This shows that the state both drew wealth from its subjects and became linked to the new economy of southern Africa.

**Social.** The Nguni elite ruled over a mix of peoples, and in time Nguni and local customs mixed. Intermarriage and the use of the Nguni language by subject people were common.

## Relations with the Portuguese

For most of the nineteenth century the Portuguese were weak on the Mozambique coast and in the Zambezi valley and could not control the interior. The Gaza kings dealt with them from strength: Soshangane attacked their forts, and the Portuguese often paid tribute to the king for permission to trade or to keep their posts. In the succession struggle after Soshangane's death, Mzila obtained Portuguese help against his brother Mawewe, which made the Portuguese influential, but Mzila remained independent.

**Ngungunyana** became king about 1884. In his reign the Gaza state was threatened by Portuguese, British and Boer interests, all of whom wanted the lands between Delagoa Bay and the Zambezi. He tried to protect his independence by playing one power against another: he accepted Portuguese symbols of friendship and sent messages to the British seeking protection. He also moved his capital south to Mandlakazi, nearer to the coast and the South African mines. After the Scramble for Africa, the Portuguese decided that they must occupy the interior to keep it from Britain. They sent a modern army with rifles and machine guns under officers such as António Enes and Mouzinho de Albuquerque.

## Fall of the Gaza state

The Portuguese campaign of 1895 ended the state. At Coolela on 7 November 1895 the Gaza army was defeated by Portuguese fire, and Ngungunyana was captured at Chaimite on 28 December 1895 and sent into exile in Portugal and the Azores, where he died in 1906.

Reasons for the fall:

- **Superior Portuguese weapons.** Rifles and machine guns defeated massed regiments.
- **Internal divisions.** The succession dispute of the 1860s, discontent among the tributary peoples, and divisions within Ngungunyana's family weakened the state, and some subject groups helped the Portuguese.
- **International rivalry.** After the Berlin Conference, Portugal needed to show "effective occupation", which gave it a reason to act.
- **Dependence on tribute and raiding.** The state's wealth rested on tribute from discontented peoples and on cattle, which left it vulnerable once Portuguese posts and traders competed for the same peoples.
- **Loss of allies.** Ngungunyana failed to obtain British protection.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes of Gaza's fall"}
Weapons were the immediate reason for defeat, but historians point out that several African states had earlier defeated European forces and then fallen later, so the question is why Gaza could not unite against Portugal. The answer lies in internal divisions and discontented tributaries, combined with the Portuguese decision to act after 1885. A balanced answer treats external force as decisive and internal division as the condition that made it succeed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Gaza with the Ndebele state"}
Both were founded by Nguni migrants, but Mzilikazi's Ndebele settled in south-western Zimbabwe and were conquered by the British South Africa Company in 1893-94. Soshangane's Gaza state lay in southern Mozambique and south-eastern Zimbabwe and was conquered by the Portuguese in 1895.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Ngungunyana was the founder"}
The founder was Soshangane. Ngungunyana was the last important king.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Questions may ask you to "describe the origins and organisation of the Gaza state" or "explain why the Gaza state fell in 1895". Give names and dates (Soshangane, Mzila, Ngungunyana, 1895) and compare Gaza with another state. For causes of decline, separate internal and external factors and judge between them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Soshangane, a general under Zwide, fled the Zulu about 1819-1821 and founded the Gaza state in southern Mozambique and south-eastern Zimbabwe.
- The state was ruled by a king and izinduna, with age regiments, tribute from the Tsonga, Chopi and Ndau, cattle and trade.
- The Portuguese were weak for most of the century, and Gaza kings dealt with them from strength.
- Under Ngungunyana, rivalry among Portugal, Britain and the Boers threatened the state.
- A Portuguese army with modern weapons defeated Gaza at Coolela in 1895 and captured Ngungunyana at Chaimite.
:::
