## Introduction

The soapstone bird of Great Zimbabwe appears on the national flag and the coat of arms, and the country takes its name from the stone city near Masvingo. Great Zimbabwe was the greatest achievement of the Iron Age farmers of southern Africa, but it was not the first state on this stage. About two hundred years earlier, near the meeting of the Limpopo and Shashe rivers, the rulers of **Mapungubwe** had already built a wealthy state on cattle, gold and trade. Understanding Mapungubwe helps explain how Great Zimbabwe arose.

This lesson covers their location, origins, organisation and trade, the controversy about who built them, and the debate over why they declined. The later states of the plateau, the Mutapa, Torwa and Rozvi, are separate lessons. You should know the Bantu migrations, the theories of state formation and the types of evidence used in the sources lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Dry-stone walling"}
Building with shaped stones laid without mortar. At Great Zimbabwe, builders split granite by heating and cooling the rock, then laid the blocks in courses to make walls that have stood for centuries.
:::

:::definition{term="Elite"}
The ruling and wealthy group in a society. At Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe, the elite lived on or inside the stone-walled areas, apart from the commoners.
:::

:::definition{term="Prestige goods"}
Rare, valuable items, such as imported beads, cloth and ceramics, which rulers obtained through trade and used to show status and reward followers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and facts to learn"}
- Mapungubwe: Limpopo-Shashe region; its main period, on the hill, is roughly 1220-1290 (approximate); abandoned by about 1300.
- Great Zimbabwe: near present Masvingo; stone building mainly from about the thirteenth to the fifteenth centuries; abandoned by the end of the fifteenth century (approximate).
- The gold burials at Mapungubwe, including the gold rhinoceros, were found in 1933.
- Great Zimbabwe and Khami were made World Heritage Sites in 1986; the Mapungubwe cultural landscape in 2003.
- The name "Zimbabwe" is usually explained as coming from Shona words for "stone houses" (*dzimba dza mabwe*) or "venerated houses".
:::

## Mapungubwe

Mapungubwe lies in the Limpopo valley where South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe meet. The earlier settlement at the site called K2 dates to about the eleventh and early thirteenth centuries, and Mapungubwe Hill became the royal centre afterwards. The people were Iron Age farmers who kept large herds of cattle and grew sorghum and millet. Archaeology shows an important change from older villages: **the rulers lived on the top of the hill, separated from the commoners who lived below**. This is the earliest clear evidence in the region of a class division expressed in the layout of a settlement.

Mapungubwe grew rich by trade. Ivory and gold were exchanged for glass beads, cloth and other goods that arrived from the Indian Ocean coast. Burials on the hill contained gold beads, bangles and the small gold rhinoceros, some of the finest gold work found in southern Africa. Power rested on cattle, ivory and gold, and on ritual authority.

By about 1300 the hill had been abandoned. Archaeologists suggest that a change to cooler and drier conditions made farming hard in the Limpopo valley, and that trade routes and political centres were shifting north to the Zimbabwe plateau. These explanations are inferences and are debated.

## Great Zimbabwe

### Location and growth

Great Zimbabwe stands on a granite hill and the valley below it, in a well-watered area of the plateau. The land was good for cattle and crops, lay close to gold-bearing areas and to routes towards the coast, and had building stone close at hand. People settled there from about the eleventh century, and the stone walls were built mainly from the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries. At its height its population is estimated by archaeologists at roughly ten to eighteen thousand people, although estimates differ.

### The stone city

- **The Hill Complex**, the oldest part, probably the royal residence and ritual centre. The soapstone birds were found here.
- **The Great Enclosure**, a large oval wall, up to about eleven metres high and over 240 metres around, with a solid conical tower and narrow passages. It probably housed the king's household, relatives, and initiation or ritual activity.
- **The Valley Ruins**, smaller enclosures where other members of the elite lived.

The walls are of dry-stone masonry, and the pole-and-daga (clay) houses of commoners stood outside the stone enclosures.

### Organisation

**Political.** A king ruled with the help of councillors and officials, and the state included districts governed from smaller stone-walled sites. Tribute in cattle, grain and ivory was delivered to the centre.

**Economic.** Cattle were the principal wealth. Farmers grew sorghum and millet, and gold was mined and panned in the region. Craftsmen worked iron, gold and copper and made pottery and soapstone carvings. The rulers profited from the long-distance trade in gold and ivory.

**Social.** The king and the elite lived inside the stone enclosures, commoners outside. Kinship, totems and marriage structured the community, and craftsmen and traders formed specialised groups.

**Religious.** The people believed in ancestral spirits and a high god, later known as Mwari. The king was believed to have a special link with the royal ancestors and with rain. The soapstone birds, shown with human features, may represent royal ancestors or messengers between the king and the spirit world.

### Trade

Gold and ivory travelled from the plateau to the Indian Ocean coast through ports such as Sofala, and on to Kilwa and the wider network of Arab, Persian and Indian merchants. Excavations at Great Zimbabwe have recovered glass beads, Chinese celadon, Persian pottery and copper coins from Kilwa. This is strong evidence of links with the Indian Ocean trade. The imports did not make the city: its cattle wealth and farming base were the foundation, and trade added prestige goods that rulers could give away to followers.

## Who built Great Zimbabwe?

When Europeans reached the ruins in the nineteenth century, some declared that Africans could not have built them. Karl Mauch (1871) suggested the Queen of Sheba, and Theodore Bent (1891) argued for Phoenician or Arab builders. The idea suited the settlers' claims. **Archaeology disproved it.** David Randall-MacIver (1905) found only African objects and medieval imported goods in the lowest levels, and Gertrude Caton-Thompson (1929) confirmed it. Radiocarbon dating, Shona oral tradition and later research agree: Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona.

:::example{title="A source analysed: why the foreign builder claim was weak"}
1. **The claim:** the walls were too grand for Africans.
2. **The evidence against:** excavation found African pottery, tools and iron-working, with imports only from the medieval Indian Ocean trade; the walls developed from simpler African building.
3. **The motive:** the claim was made by people who assumed Africans were incapable of such work and who benefited from denying African achievement.
4. **Judgement:** the claim rested on prejudice, not evidence, and archaeology showed it to be false.
:::

## The decline of Great Zimbabwe

The reasons are debated, and no single explanation is proved.

1. **Environmental exhaustion.** A large population may have exhausted firewood, grazing and soil.
2. **Shift of trade.** The gold and ivory routes moved north towards the Zambezi valley, reducing the rulers' wealth.
3. **Water and salt shortages.** Some traditions mention a search for salt.
4. **Political division.** Succession disputes and the emergence of rival centres such as Khami and the Mutapa state.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes"}
A balanced answer might argue that environmental pressure made the city hard to sustain, but that the loss of trade turned a problem into a collapse, since the king could no longer reward followers. The northern Mutapa and southern Torwa states then took over the leadership of the plateau. Because the evidence is limited, the judgement must be tentative.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Great Zimbabwe was built by the Shona kings of the Mutapa"}
The Mutapa state arose later, in the north, and Mutota and Matope are traditionally linked to a movement from Great Zimbabwe. Great Zimbabwe belongs to an earlier period.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the decline as certain"}
Say "theories" or "historians argue" and weigh several. Do not state that salt, trade or environment alone is the cause.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the stone walls"}
Examiners want political, economic, social and religious organisation, and trade, not only a description of the buildings.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mapungubwe (about 1220-1290) was an early class-divided state on cattle, gold and ivory in the Limpopo valley.
- Great Zimbabwe, near Masvingo, grew from about the thirteenth century on cattle wealth, farming and the gold and ivory trade with the Indian Ocean.
- Political, economic, social and religious organisation centred on the king, his tribute, the stone enclosures and the cult of the royal ancestors.
- The myth of foreign builders was disproved by archaeology and radiocarbon dating: the builders were the ancestors of the Shona.
- Causes of decline, including environmental exhaustion, a shift of trade and political division, are debated.
:::
