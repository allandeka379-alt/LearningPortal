## Introduction

When Great Zimbabwe lost its place in the fifteenth century, two new centres of power grew on the plateau. In the north, the Mutapa state rose in the Zambezi valley. In the south-west, a state called **Torwa** built its capital at **Khami**, near present-day Bulawayo, whose terraces and decorated walls are protected today as a World Heritage Site. In the late seventeenth century a new power, the **Rozvi**, led by Changamire Dombo, overthrew the Torwa, defeated the Mutapa and the Portuguese and ruled most of the plateau for about a century and a half.

This lesson covers the Torwa and Rozvi states, their organisation, their stone-building tradition, the religious importance of Mwari, and their decline in the nineteenth century. The coming of the Ndebele, who ended Rozvi power, is dealt with more fully in the lesson on the Ndebele state, and the Mfecane that drove Nguni groups northwards is in the Zulu lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Changamire"}
A title borne by the ruler of the Rozvi state and, in some traditions, by earlier governors in the south. The founder of the Rozvi state is known as Changamire Dombo.
:::

:::definition{term="Mambo"}
The Shona title of a king. The Rozvi rulers were known as Mambo, and are traditionally believed to have carried the sacred authority of the ancestors.
:::

:::definition{term="Mwari cult"}
A form of religion centred on the high god Mwari, with shrines in the Matobo Hills and elsewhere, whose priests and messengers were consulted for rain and guidance. The Rozvi rulers supported the cult.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- About 1450: Khami becomes the capital of the Torwa state (approximate).
- 1680s: Changamire Dombo overthrows the Torwa and builds the Rozvi state (approximate).
- Late 1600s: the Rozvi defeat Mutapa forces and expel the Portuguese from the plateau.
- Rozvi capitals and stone sites: Danangombe (Dhlo-Dhlo), Naletale and Khami.
- 1830s: Nguni invasions by the groups of Zwangendaba and Nxaba break Rozvi power; Mzilikazi's Ndebele complete the conquest about 1838-40.
- 1986: Khami is made a World Heritage Site.
:::

## The Torwa state

The Torwa state arose in the south-west of the plateau, in the region the Portuguese called Butua. Its rulers, known as the Torwa dynasty, belonged to the Shona-speaking society that had built Great Zimbabwe. Tradition links them to the royal house of the older state. Their capital, **Khami**, was occupied mainly between about 1450 and 1650 and is an outstanding stone site. Its walls stand on terraced platforms with decorated patterns such as chevrons and checks, and it contained houses, courts and large quantities of imported Chinese porcelain and glass beads that show trade links.

The Torwa grew rich on cattle, farming and the gold and ivory trade, and used part of the wealth to build in stone. Portuguese sources of the sixteenth century mention Butua and its ruler, and show that they were interested in the gold and cattle of the region. By the seventeenth century, however, the Torwa were weakened by disputes within the ruling house and by pressure from the north, which opened the way for a rival.

## The rise of the Rozvi

**Changamire Dombo** (the name Dombo means "rock" or "boulder") first appears in traditions as a herder or governor serving the Mutapa or the Torwa, depending on the source. He built up an army, and in the late seventeenth century he defeated the Torwa and took over their territory. The name **Rozvi** is usually explained as meaning "destroyers" or "plunderers". Dombo's forces then defeated the Mutapa and attacked the Portuguese trading fairs and settlements on the plateau, forcing the Portuguese to retreat to the Zambezi valley and the coast. This ended the hope of Portuguese conquest of the interior and made the Rozvi the dominant power. Dombo is thought to have died in the 1690s, but the Rozvi state continued under later Mambo.

The details of dates and events come from oral tradition and the records of the Portuguese, and historians do not agree on all of them, such as the exact date of the fall of Khami.

## Organisation of the Rozvi state

**Political.** The Mambo was the head of the state, a sacred ruler who was believed to link the people with the ancestors. Governors, often relatives of the ruler, controlled provinces and collected tribute. A strong army, organised under the Mambo, defended the state and enforced tribute from conquered peoples. Succession was a risk, since several houses could claim the throne.

**Economic.** Farming and cattle-keeping were the basis of life, and cattle were the main wealth. Tribute in cattle, grain and ivory flowed to the capital. Gold mining and ivory hunting supported trade, which at first went through Portuguese fairs and later through African traders. Craftsmen worked iron, copper and gold and made pottery.

**Social.** The Mambo and his nobles lived in stone-walled capitals; the commoners lived in villages. Social ties were arranged by clan, totem and kinship.

**Religious.** The Rozvi supported the **Mwari cult**. The shrines in the Matobo Hills, whose priests spoke for the high god, were consulted for rain, for the harvest and in times of crisis. The cult linked many peoples across the plateau to the ruler and survived the end of the Rozvi state; it later played a role in the Umvukela of 1896 (see the lesson on the risings).

**Stone building.** Rozvi capitals such as **Danangombe** (Dhlo-Dhlo) and **Naletale**, as well as the older Khami, show the continuing tradition of dry-stone building, with platforms, decorated walls and enclosures. The Mambo's courts were built on a smaller scale than Great Zimbabwe but displayed the power of the state.

:::example{title="A cause explained: how the Rozvi became strong enough to defeat the Portuguese"}
1. **Military force:** Dombo built a disciplined army that could defeat the Torwa and the Mutapa.
2. **Wealth:** control of cattle, tribute and gold gave him the means to keep an army.
3. **Weakness of rivals:** the Mutapa state was divided by civil war, and the Portuguese were few and spread out over the Zambezi valley.
4. **Result:** the Rozvi destroyed Portuguese fairs and drove them from the plateau.
:::

## The decline of the Rozvi

By the early nineteenth century Rozvi power had weakened, and the state was destroyed within a few decades.

- **Internal weakness.** Succession disputes divided the ruling house and governors of provinces became independent, so the Mambo could not call on the whole state.
- **Nguni invasions.** After the Mfecane in the south, groups led by Nxaba and Zwangendaba crossed the Limpopo in the 1830s and attacked the Rozvi, seizing cattle and capturing towns. Tradition says the last Mambo, Chirisamhuru, was killed in this period, though the exact circumstances are uncertain.
- **The Ndebele.** Mzilikazi's followers moved into the south-west of the plateau about 1838-40, defeated the remnants of Rozvi power and took over the region.
- **Economic pressure.** Raiding and disruption of trade and farming weakened the chiefs on whom the state relied.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes: internal or external?"}
Some historians stress the Nguni invasions, which were the immediate cause of collapse. Others argue that a strong Rozvi state would have resisted them, and that it was the long-standing internal weakness that left it open. A balanced judgement is that internal decline made the state vulnerable and the Nguni invasions delivered the final blow.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Torwa and the Rozvi"}
The Torwa were the older south-western dynasty at Khami; the Rozvi under Changamire Dombo overthrew them in the late seventeenth century.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Ndebele founded the Rozvi state"}
The Rozvi state was founded by Changamire Dombo in the seventeenth century. The Ndebele arrived in the 1830s and replaced Rozvi power in the south-west.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Khami and Great Zimbabwe as the same place"}
Khami lies near Bulawayo and was occupied in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries; Great Zimbabwe lies near Masvingo and belongs mainly to the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Torwa state was based at Khami in the south-west from about 1450 and prospered on cattle, gold and trade.
- Changamire Dombo overthrew the Torwa in the late seventeenth century and founded the Rozvi state, which defeated the Mutapa and drove the Portuguese from the plateau.
- The Rozvi were ruled by the Mambo, with provincial governors, tribute, a strong army, cattle wealth and support for the Mwari cult.
- Stone sites such as Khami, Danangombe and Naletale show the continuing tradition of stone building.
- The Rozvi declined through succession disputes and independent provinces, then fell to Nguni invaders in the 1830s and the Ndebele about 1838-40.
:::
