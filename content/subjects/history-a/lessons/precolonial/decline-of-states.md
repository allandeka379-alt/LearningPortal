## Introduction

You have now met seven states: Great Zimbabwe, the Mutapa, the Rozvi, the Ndebele, the Zulu, Gaza and the Mandinka of Samori Toure. Each rose, flourished and fell. Examiners rarely ask you to repeat the story of one state. They ask a comparative question: "Which mattered more in the decline of pre-colonial African states, internal weakness or outside pressure?" To answer it you need to organise what you know into a framework, compare cases, and reach a judgement that you can defend.

This is an essay-skill lesson. It does not teach the decline of each state again; revise those in the single-state lessons. It teaches three new frameworks, shows how to use them, and gives a model plan.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal factor"}
A cause of decline that arises within the state: succession disputes, rebellion by subject peoples, economic or environmental strain, divisions in the army or the ruling group.
:::

:::definition{term="External factor"}
A cause of decline that arises from outside the state: invasion by neighbours, migration, changes in trade routes, European intervention.
:::

:::definition{term="Long-term condition and trigger"}
A **condition** is a weakness that builds up over years and makes collapse possible. A **trigger** is the event that starts the collapse. A good answer separates the two.
:::

:::keyfact{title="One-line summaries to learn"}
- **Great Zimbabwe:** environmental pressure, shift of trade north, political division (all debated).
- **Mutapa:** civil wars and succession disputes, Portuguese interference, loss of provinces, the Rozvi.
- **Rozvi:** succession disputes and independent provinces, then Nguni invasions and the Ndebele.
- **Ndebele:** divided leadership; concession pressure and conquest by the BSAC, 1893-94.
- **Zulu:** succession disputes and civil war; Boer and British pressure; defeat in 1879.
- **Gaza:** tributary discontent; Portuguese conquest in 1895.
- **Mandinka:** French army, loss of arms supply, revolts of subjects; capture in 1898.
:::

## Framework 1: internal and external factors

The basic frame sorts causes into two columns. Here is a summary of the states in the table.

| State | Main internal factors | Main external factors | Ended by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Great Zimbabwe | Environmental exhaustion; political division | Shift of gold and ivory trade north | Gradual abandonment (15th century) |
| Mutapa | Succession disputes; civil war; revolt of provinces | Portuguese interference; the Rozvi | Loss of independence in the 17th century |
| Rozvi | Succession disputes; independent provinces | Nguni invasions (1830s); the Ndebele | Conquest in the 1830s |
| Ndebele | Divided army; resentful Shona tributaries | Concession hunters; the BSAC | Conquest, 1893-94 |
| Zulu | Succession disputes; civil war | Boers; Britain | Defeat in 1879; annexation 1887 |
| Gaza | Discontented tributaries; family division | Portuguese army | Conquest, 1895 |
| Mandinka | Revolts of conquered peoples; harm of scorched earth | French army; loss of arms | Capture, 1898 |

A first pattern appears at once: **every state had both kinds of cause**. A strong answer never says "only internal" or "only external". It says which was more important and why.

## Framework 2: long-term conditions and triggers

Using this frame turns a list into an argument.

| State | Long-term condition | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Rozvi | Weak succession and independent provinces | Nguni invasions in the 1830s |
| Ndebele | Divided leadership; reliance on raiding | Rudd Concession, Royal Charter, Fort Victoria incident and the 1893 war |
| Zulu | Succession disputes, civil war | Boer defeat of the army (1838); the Anglo-Zulu War (1879) |
| Gaza | Resentful tributaries | Portuguese campaign of 1895 |
| Mutapa | Disputed succession | Portuguese support for rival claimants; Rozvi expansion |

:::example{title="Applying the frame in one paragraph"}
*"In the Rozvi case, the condition was internal and the trigger external. Succession disputes and the independence of provincial governors meant that the Mambo could not call on the whole state. The Nguni invasions of the 1830s were the trigger, because they struck a state that could not unite to resist. A strong Rozvi state, such as the one that expelled the Portuguese in the seventeenth century, might have survived. Therefore internal weakness was the deeper cause and the invasion was the immediate cause."*
:::

## Framework 3: African states against Europeans and against Africans

Another useful comparison is **who brought the state down**.

- **Ended within Africa (by gradual decline or by other African powers):** Great Zimbabwe (gradual abandonment; the Mutapa and Torwa later led the plateau), the Torwa (overthrown by the Rozvi), the Mutapa (reduced by the Rozvi and by Portuguese interference), the Rozvi (broken by Nguni groups and the Ndebele).
- **Defeated by Europeans in the late nineteenth century:** the Ndebele (BSAC), Gaza (Portugal), Mandinka (France), the Zulu (Britain).

This comparison raises a good question: why did the states that fell to Europeans fall? The common answer is **superior weapons and organisation**: rifles, machine guns, artillery and supplies. But the cases show that this is not the whole answer. Samori and the Zulu at Isandlwana both defeated or delayed European forces. What matters is the combination: European firepower and the willingness of imperial governments to support wars, together with African disunity, the loss of arms supplies and revolts of tributaries.

:::example{title="Weighing Europeans' weapons against internal weakness"}
1. **For the weapons argument:** at Coolela and in the Ndebele war, massed regiments were destroyed by rifles and machine guns.
2. **Against:** Samori resisted for sixteen years; the Zulu won at Isandlwana. Weapons alone did not decide the outcome.
3. **Further evidence:** in Gaza and the Mandinka state, some conquered peoples helped the Europeans, and in the Ndebele state the army was divided.
4. **Judgement:** weapons were decisive in the final battles, but the capacity to use them depended on weaknesses inside the states.
:::

## Building the judgement

A comparative essay on "internal or external?" can follow this plan.

1. **Introduction.** Define the two kinds of factor and state your line: "Both mattered, but internal weakness was the deeper cause in the African-conquered states while external force was decisive in the states conquered by Europeans."
2. **Paragraph 1: internal factors.** Use two or three states (Rozvi, Mutapa, Zulu) with specific evidence.
3. **Paragraph 2: external factors.** Use two or three states (Ndebele, Gaza, Mandinka).
4. **Paragraph 3: link or interaction.** Show how the two worked together (for example, Portuguese support for rival claimants in the Mutapa).
5. **Conclusion.** Judge which mattered more, giving the reason, and recognise limits: some evidence is oral tradition or comes from the conquerors.

:::example{title="Testing a judgement"}
*Claim:* "External factors were more important than internal ones." To test it, ask: Would the state have survived without the external pressure? For Gaza, the Ndebele and the Mandinka, probably yes for several more years, which supports the claim. For the Rozvi and the Mutapa, internal weakness left them open to any attack, which challenges it. A reasoned conclusion would be that external force ended the nineteenth-century states, but internal weakness ended the earlier ones.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Telling the story of each state in turn"}
A comparative question needs themes. Group the states under internal and external factors and compare, rather than narrating each state's decline separately.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all states as having fallen for the same reason"}
Different periods had different causes. Earlier states fell to other African powers; later ones to Europeans with modern weapons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reaching no judgement"}
End by saying which type of factor mattered more and why, using evidence from the cases you have named.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2"}
Typical questions: "To what extent were internal factors responsible for the decline of pre-colonial states you have studied?", "Compare the decline of two states" and "Assess the factors that brought about the collapse of the Rozvi and Ndebele states". Name at least three states with dates and specific evidence, and keep your line of argument visible in every paragraph.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Every pre-colonial state declined through a mixture of internal and external factors; the task is to judge which mattered more.
- Distinguish long-term conditions (succession disputes, tributary discontent) from triggers (invasion, European campaigns).
- Earlier states fell to African neighbours or gradual decline; nineteenth-century states fell to European armies with modern weapons.
- European weapons were decisive in the final battles, but African disunity, loss of arms supplies and revolts of tributaries made them effective.
- A strong essay compares themes, uses named evidence from several states and ends with a supported judgement.
:::
