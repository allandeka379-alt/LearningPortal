## Introduction

In the first decades of the nineteenth century, much of south-eastern Africa was turned upside down. Chiefdoms fought, merged or broke apart; whole peoples moved hundreds of kilometres; and new kingdoms appeared from the Drakensberg to Lake Malawi. Nguni speakers call this period the **Mfecane** ("the crushing"); Sotho speakers call it the **Difaqane** ("forced migration"). At its centre stood the Zulu kingdom of Shaka. Its effects reached Zimbabwe: the Ndebele, the Gaza and the Nguni groups of Zwangendaba and Nxaba are all part of the story.

This lesson explains the causes of the Mfecane and the argument over them, the rise of the Zulu state under Shaka, its organisation, the consequences across the region, and the decline of the Zulu kingdom. Many names and dates in this period are known only approximately, and historians dispute some of them, so they are given only where well established.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mfecane / Difaqane"}
Terms for the period of wars, migrations and state-building among the peoples of south-eastern Africa from the 1810s to the 1830s, linked with the rise of the Zulu kingdom.
:::

:::definition{term="Impi"}
A Zulu fighting force. Under Shaka the *impi* was an organised army of age regiments.
:::

:::definition{term="Confederacy"}
A group of chiefdoms joined under the leadership of one strong chief, usually by alliance or conquest, without being fully merged.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- Early 1800s: rival chiefdoms (Mthethwa under Dingiswayo, Ndwandwe under Zwide) grow stronger in what is now KwaZulu-Natal.
- About 1816: Shaka becomes chief of the Zulu; about 1818, after Dingiswayo's death, he leads the Mthethwa and defeats Zwide's Ndwandwe.
- 1828: Shaka is assassinated; Dingane becomes king.
- 16 December 1838: Boers defeat Dingane's army at Blood River.
- 1879: the Anglo-Zulu War; Zulu victory at Isandlwana (22 January), British victory at Ulundi (4 July).
:::

## Causes of the Mfecane

Historians debate the causes, and the debate is itself an exam topic.

1. **Population pressure and competition for land and cattle.** The older view, set out by J. D. Omer-Cooper in *The Zulu Aftermath* (1966), stresses growing populations on limited grazing and farming land, and competition between chiefdoms that were turning into larger states.
2. **Drought and famine.** A long drought around the beginning of the century intensified competition for food and cattle.
3. **Trade.** Growth in the ivory trade through Delagoa Bay (Maputo) gave chiefs who controlled it an advantage and fed rivalry between the Ndwandwe, Mthethwa and others.
4. **Leadership and military change.** Capable leaders, notably Dingiswayo and Shaka, reorganised their societies for war.
5. **Slave raiding and colonial pressure.** The historian Julian Cobbing, in an article called "The Mfecane as alibi" (1988), argued that the main cause was the slave trade from Delagoa Bay and slave raiding from the Cape, and that the idea of Shaka's wars as the central cause was a convenient story that hid European responsibility.

:::keyfact{title="A debated question"}
Historians now generally agree that the Zulu kingdom was one important factor, and that the older picture of a vast Zulu-driven catastrophe has been overstated. They disagree about how much weight to give to Zulu expansion, drought, trade and slave raiding, and about how many people were killed or displaced. A good answer presents the competing views and judges them.
:::

## The rise of the Zulu state

**Dingiswayo and the Mthethwa.** Dingiswayo (died about 1818) built the Mthethwa confederacy into the leading power by conquest and alliance. He reorganised young men into age regiments and made his army a loyal force rather than a collection of clan fighters.

**Shaka.** Shaka, a son of the chief of the small Zulu clan, served in Dingiswayo's army and, with his support, became chief of the Zulu about 1816. When Dingiswayo was killed by Zwide, the Ndwandwe leader, Shaka took charge of the Mthethwa and defeated Zwide's forces in two battles, at Gqokli Hill (about 1818) and the Mhlatuze river (about 1819). He then built a kingdom that dominated what is now KwaZulu-Natal.

### Shaka's reforms

- **Weapons and tactics.** The short stabbing spear (*iklwa*) and a large cowhide shield replaced throwing assegais, so fighting was close and decisive. The "buffalo horns" formation sent flanking "horns" round the enemy while the "chest" attacked from the front and a reserve (the "loins") stood back.
- **Regiments.** Young men were enrolled in age regiments (*amabutho*) living in royal military towns (*amakhanda*), and could marry only with the king's permission.
- **Discipline and training.** Regiments trained hard and marched long distances fast.
- **Political centralisation.** Shaka reduced the power of old hereditary chiefs, appointed loyal officials (*izinduna*) and took the cattle of defeated peoples for the king.
- **Incorporation.** Defeated groups were absorbed into the kingdom rather than destroyed, so the state grew.

**Organisation.** The king stood at the head, supported by *izinduna*, with the royal cattle and the annual first-fruits ceremony strengthening his authority. The state raised its army from the regiments, fed it from royal herds and the produce of conquered peoples, and tied the nation together by loyalty to the king.

:::example{title="A cause explained: why Shaka's army won"}
1. **A new weapon:** the stabbing spear forced the enemy into close combat, where discipline decided battles.
2. **A new formation:** the "horns" surrounded smaller armies.
3. **A new loyalty:** regiments served the king, not their clan chiefs, so they could be sent anywhere.
4. **A new supply:** cattle and food taken from the defeated supported more regiments.
5. **Result:** victory over Zwide and the growth of the kingdom.
:::

## Consequences across the region

As the Zulu kingdom grew, groups defeated by or opposed to it moved away, spreading its methods and causing new conflicts.

The effects included the destruction of some societies, such as parts of the Rozvi state; the creation of new states; the spread of Nguni languages and military organisation; depopulation of some areas, which later Boer trekkers occupied; and the mixing of peoples.

## Decline of the Zulu kingdom

Shaka was killed in 1828 by his half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana. Dingane ruled until 1840. Factors in the decline were:

- **Succession disputes and civil war.** Dingane was overthrown by his half-brother Mpande in 1840, with Boer help, and in 1856 the sons of Mpande fought at Ndondakusuka, which weakened the kingdom.
- **Boer pressure.** In 1838 Boer trekkers defeated the Zulu army at Blood River.
- **British expansion.** Britain annexed Natal in 1843 and later wanted to end the Zulu army and its independence.
- **The Anglo-Zulu War (1879).** Cetshwayo's army destroyed a British column at Isandlwana, but the British won at Ulundi, captured the king and divided the kingdom among chiefs. Britain annexed Zululand in 1887.

:::example{title="Weighing the Mfecane debate"}
A student could argue as follows: *Omer-Cooper's view puts the Zulu state at the centre and explains it by population pressure; Cobbing's view argues that the slave trade has been ignored. The evidence for drought and for the slave trade at Delagoa Bay is strong, so the Zulu kingdom was probably one cause among several. The strongest answer treats the Mfecane as the product of several factors.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Shaka alone caused the Mfecane"}
Shaka was one important actor, but drought, trade, population pressure and slave raiding also played a part, and the older chiefdoms of Dingiswayo and Zwide had already begun the change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Mfecane with the Bantu migrations"}
The Bantu migrations happened over thousands of years. The Mfecane happened mainly between about 1815 and 1840.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving exact numbers of deaths"}
Estimates of those killed or displaced vary widely and are disputed. Write "historians estimate" and note that the scale is debated.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Mfecane (Difaqane) was a period of wars and migrations in south-eastern Africa from the 1810s to the 1830s; its causes and scale are debated.
- Causes include population pressure, drought, the ivory and slave trades, strong leaders and Zulu expansion.
- Shaka built a powerful kingdom through the stabbing spear, the "horns" formation, age regiments, centralisation and incorporation.
- The Mfecane created the Ndebele, Gaza, Ngoni, Swazi, Kololo and Basotho states and spread Nguni military ideas.
- The Zulu kingdom declined through succession disputes, Boer defeat at Blood River and British conquest in 1879.
:::
