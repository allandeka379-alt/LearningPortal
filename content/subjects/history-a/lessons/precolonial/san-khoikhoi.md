## Introduction

Long before Shona, Ndebele or Zulu farmers arrived, southern Africa was home to people who lived by hunting, gathering and herding. Their paintings still cover rock shelters in the Matopo Hills, and their words survive as clicks in the Nguni languages. Historians call the two peoples studied here the **San** (hunter-gatherers) and the **Khoi-Khoi** (herders), and together the **Khoisan**. Older books use other names that the peoples themselves did not choose and that are now regarded as insulting; we use the names San and Khoi-Khoi.

This lesson explains how these societies were organised, what they knew about their environment and how that knowledge still matters, and how they dealt with the Bantu-speaking farmers who moved into their lands. The migrations of the farmers themselves belong to the next lesson. You should already know that early people made stone tools and lived in small groups, but you do not need the detailed tool sequence of the Stone Age.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hunter-gatherer"}
A person who gets food by hunting wild animals and collecting wild plants, honey and insects, rather than by farming or herding.
:::

:::definition{term="Pastoralist"}
A person whose way of life is based on herding domestic animals, such as cattle and sheep, and moving them to find grazing and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous knowledge system"}
The knowledge, skills and beliefs that a community has built up over many generations about its environment, medicine, food and survival, and passes on by example and word of mouth.
:::

:::definition{term="Band"}
The small, flexible group of related families that made up San society, usually numbering a few dozen people, which moved to follow food and water.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key points to learn"}
- The San were hunter-gatherers living in small bands; the Khoi-Khoi kept sheep and cattle and lived in larger groups led by chiefs.
- Herding reached southern Africa roughly 2,000 years ago; Bantu-speaking farmers entered the region in the first millennium CE.
- San rock art survives in the Matopo Hills and across southern Africa.
- Khoisan languages contributed click sounds to some Nguni languages such as Zulu and Xhosa.
:::

## The San

### Organisation of society

San society was built on **kinship**. A band was a group of related families, typically a few dozen people, camped around a water source for part of the year and moving as plants ripened and game migrated. Bands were linked by marriage and by sharing across a wider territory. This meant that when drought struck one area, relatives elsewhere could host them.

The San had no kings or hereditary chiefs. Leadership was informal: an older man or woman respected for experience or skill in hunting, healing or knowledge of the land guided decisions, but others were free to disagree and to leave. Important decisions were made by discussion. Resources such as water holes and hunting grounds were shared within the group, and **sharing meat** was a strong rule that bound the band together.

There was a **division of labour**. Men usually hunted, using bows with poisoned arrows, snares and traps, sometimes tracking one animal for hours. Women gathered roots, bulbs, fruits, nuts and insects, and these plant foods often provided a larger share of the diet than meat. Children learned by watching and helping.

### Beliefs and rock art

San religion saw the spirit world as close to daily life. Healers entered a trance, often during all-night dances, to heal the sick, bring rain or make hunting successful. Many rock paintings in the Matopo Hills and elsewhere show animals such as eland and kudu, and human figures in postures that researchers connect with trance ritual. The paintings are hard to date exactly and their meanings are debated, but they are a major source for San beliefs and experience.

## The Khoi-Khoi

The Khoi-Khoi were **pastoralists**, living chiefly in the south-western parts of southern Africa and Namibia. They kept fat-tailed sheep and cattle and moved in search of grazing and water. Their societies were larger than San bands, organised in **clans** headed by chiefs whose authority rested on the number of cattle and followers they had. Cattle were wealth: they provided milk, meat, hides and status. The Khoi-Khoi also hunted, gathered and traded, and they exchanged livestock and goods with the San and with neighbouring farmers. Their portable, mat-covered houses were suited to moving camps. Women milked the animals and looked after the household, while men herded and defended the stock.

The Khoi-Khoi were later badly affected by European settlement at the Cape after 1652: they lost grazing land, and a smallpox epidemic in 1713 killed a large part of the population. That story belongs to a later period, but it shows how fragile their way of life was once outside pressures arrived.

## Indigenous knowledge

Both peoples developed detailed knowledge of their environment. This **indigenous knowledge** included:

- **Medicine.** Knowledge of plants for healing wounds, relieving pain and fighting illness; for example, certain succulent plants were used to ease hunger and thirst on long hunts. Healers also treated illness through ritual.
- **Hunting methods.** Tracking skills, knowledge of animal behaviour and poisons for arrow tips made from plants, and from the larvae of certain beetles.
- **Paints and tools.** Pigments from minerals and plant and animal materials used for rock art, and tools of bone, stone and ostrich shell.
- **Environmental knowledge.** Where to find water, which plants were safe, how to read weather and seasons.

:::example{title="Why indigenous knowledge matters today"}
1. **The knowledge:** San healers and hunters knew which plants reduced hunger and thirst.
2. **The use:** modern researchers and companies have studied some of these plants for medicines and foods.
3. **The issue:** disputes arose about whether the communities that first used the plants should share in the benefits.
4. **The lesson:** indigenous knowledge is real science developed through observation, and it raises questions of fairness in the present.
:::

Their legacy also lies in language (click sounds in Zulu and Xhosa), in the rock art that is protected as heritage, and in cultural knowledge that has passed into the wider population through intermarriage and contact.

## Relations with Bantu-speaking farmers

When Bantu-speaking farmers arrived, with iron tools and cattle and crops, they cleared land and settled in the better-watered areas. The farmers' numbers and tools gave them an advantage. Relations were not simply conflict. In some places San hunters traded honey, skins and ivory for iron, grain and tobacco; some San families became clients or servants of farmers; there was intermarriage; and the contact left clicks in Nguni languages. In other places farmers took over water and grazing, and San bands were pushed into drier or more remote areas such as the Kalahari. Hunting by San in territory the farmers regarded as their own led to clashes. Many historians therefore describe the process as one of both **exchange and displacement**.

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| Many thousands of years ago | San-ancestor hunter-gatherers live across southern Africa |
| About 2,000 years ago | Sheep, and then cattle, reach southern Africa with herding peoples |
| First millennium CE | Bantu-speaking farmers settle in the region |
| 1652 | Dutch settlement at the Cape begins |
| 1713 | Smallpox epidemic devastates Khoi-Khoi communities at the Cape |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating San and Khoi-Khoi as the same people"}
The San were hunter-gatherers in small bands without chiefs; the Khoi-Khoi were herders in clans with chiefs. They were related in language and appearance but their ways of life were different.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that the San had no organisation"}
San society was organised by kinship, shared rules and informal leadership. Absence of a king does not mean absence of order.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the farmers' arrival only as conquest"}
Contact involved trade, intermarriage and cooperation as well as displacement. A good answer shows both.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Questions ask you to "describe the social, economic and religious organisation of the San" or to "examine the contribution of the San and Khoi-Khoi to indigenous knowledge systems". Give the three or four features with an example for each, and link knowledge to its use today. "Assess the impact of Bantu-speaking people on the San" needs both contact and displacement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Using evidence"}
Name your evidence: rock paintings in the Matopo Hills, click sounds in Nguni languages. Examiners reward specific examples over general statements.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The San lived in small kin-based bands, hunting and gathering, with informal leadership, sharing and a belief in a spirit world expressed in rock art.
- The Khoi-Khoi were pastoralists organised in clans under chiefs, with cattle and sheep as wealth.
- Both developed indigenous knowledge of medicine, plants, hunting and the environment, which still matters today.
- Bantu-speaking farmers traded and intermarried with the San but also took land, pushing many San into drier areas.
- The legacy of the Khoisan includes rock art, click sounds in some Nguni languages and traditional knowledge.
:::
