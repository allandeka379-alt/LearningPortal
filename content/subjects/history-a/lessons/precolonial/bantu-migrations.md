## Introduction

Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Zulu, Swahili, Kikuyu and hundreds of other languages are related. Together they are called the **Bantu languages**, spoken today by hundreds of millions of people across the southern half of Africa. Related words in distant places, such as the stem *-ntu* meaning "person" in many of them, show that the speakers share a common ancestor language. How did one language family spread over so large an area? Historians and linguists answer with the story of the **Bantu migrations**, one of the largest movements of people in African history.

This lesson explains where Bantu speakers came from, how we know, how they spread, why, and what difference they made. It builds on the evidence in the sources lesson and on the San and Khoi-Khoi lesson, and it leads to the Iron Age states that follow. Note that "Bantu" is a language label, not a race.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Bantu languages"}
A large family of related languages spoken from Cameroon and Kenya southwards, which share a common ancestor ("proto-Bantu"). The word comes from the stem meaning "people" found in many of them.
:::

:::definition{term="Iron Age"}
The period in which farming communities made and used iron tools. In southern Africa archaeologists speak of the **Early Iron Age** (roughly the first millennium CE) and the **Late Iron Age** (after about 1000 CE), when larger, more centralised societies developed.
:::

:::definition{term="Migration"}
The movement of people from one place to settle in another. Historians distinguish a mass movement from a slow spread of people, ideas and skills over many generations.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key facts"}
- The Bantu languages probably began in the Nigeria-Cameroon borderlands of west-central Africa several thousand years ago.
- Speakers spread by two broad routes, through the equatorial forest and along its eastern edge, then south.
- Farming communities with iron-working were living in Zimbabwe by about the third century CE.
- The details of the movement are debated.
:::

## Origins and evidence

Most scholars place the original home of the Bantu languages in the region where Nigeria and Cameroon meet, probably four to five thousand years ago. Three kinds of evidence support this.

1. **Linguistics.** Comparing hundreds of Bantu languages shows that the related languages are most varied near Cameroon, which suggests that is where they have been spoken longest. Shared vocabulary for farming, iron, pottery and cattle shows what the ancestors knew.
2. **Archaeology.** Distinctive pottery styles and the remains of iron-smelting furnaces and villages can be traced, with radiocarbon dates, from west-central Africa to the east and south.
3. **Other evidence.** Studies of crops, human biology and genetics also support a movement out of west-central Africa, although they are complex.

There is disagreement on the detail. Early twentieth-century writers imagined a "Bantu invasion" by a conquering race, which is not supported by the evidence. The linguist Joseph Greenberg argued for a western origin around Nigeria and Cameroon, while Malcolm Guthrie located the "core" area farther south in the savanna belt south of the forest. The Cameroon view is now most widely accepted, but dating and routes remain under discussion.

## Routes and dating

Two main streams are usually described, though they were probably many small movements.

- A **western stream** moved south through the equatorial forest along rivers into the Congo basin and towards Angola.
- An **eastern stream** moved along the forest edge to the Great Lakes region and then south and east, into eastern and southern Africa.

Dates are approximate. Bantu speakers reached the Great Lakes about 2,500 years ago, and communities with distinct pottery traditions entered Zimbabwe and neighbouring areas in the early centuries CE.

| Period | Development |
|---|---|
| About 3000 BCE | Bantu languages begin in the Nigeria-Cameroon borderlands (approximate) |
| First millennium BCE | Speakers spread through and around the equatorial forest |
| About 2,500 years ago | Speakers reach the Great Lakes region |
| By about 300 CE | Early Iron Age farming communities settled in Zimbabwe |
| After about 1000 CE | Late Iron Age: larger and more centralised societies, including Great Zimbabwe |

:::keyfact{title="Debate: wave or trickle?"}
Some historians imagined large waves of migrants sweeping south. Others argue that movement was gradual: small groups of farmers moved to new land when villages grew, married local people and passed on skills. A third view stresses that ideas and technology, such as iron-working, may have spread by contact as well as by the movement of people. Most scholars accept that a real movement of people took place but that it was slow and varied.
:::

## Causes

Historians suggest several linked causes, and they disagree about their weight.

- **Population growth.** Farming and iron tools let communities grow, so some people split off to find new land.
- **Search for land and resources.** Fertile soil, grazing, iron ore and salt drew settlers to new areas.
- **Iron and farming.** Iron axes and hoes made it easier to clear woodland and farm the savanna, giving farmers an advantage.
- **Environmental change.** Climate changes, including drying in parts of the Sahara and shifts in the forest, may have opened routes through the savanna and pushed communities to move.
- **Conflict and politics.** Disputes in growing villages could lead a faction to leave with its followers.

:::example{title="Explaining one cause step by step: iron and farming"}
1. **The technology:** iron tools were harder and sharper than stone, so farmers could clear trees and break the soil.
2. **The effect on food:** more land under crops such as sorghum and millet supported more people.
3. **The effect on movement:** larger populations and the ability to farm new woodland encouraged some groups to settle further away.
4. **The effect on others:** hunter-gatherers could not compete for the best farmland, so farmers spread.
5. **Limit:** iron alone does not explain it; people also moved because of land shortage and conflict.
:::

## Impact

**On the San and Khoi-Khoi.** Farmers took the best-watered land, so many hunters and herders were absorbed, made dependants or pushed into drier regions. But there was also trade, intermarriage and borrowing, such as the clicks found in Zulu and Xhosa.

**On southern African society.** The migrants brought:

- **farming** with crops such as sorghum, millet and later maize, and permanent villages;
- **iron-working**, which produced hoes, axes, spears and ornaments;
- **cattle-keeping** and ways of using cattle as wealth;
- **pottery traditions**, which archaeologists use to trace communities;
- the **Bantu languages**, which became the main languages of the region.

Over time larger populations, surplus food, cattle and trade allowed rulers to emerge, which led to the states of the Late Iron Age: Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, the Mutapa, the Torwa and the Rozvi.

:::example{title="Weighing an interpretation"}
A student reads that "Bantu invaders conquered the country". To weigh it: (1) The evidence shows farmers spreading slowly over many centuries, not a single conquest. (2) Archaeology and language show mixing with earlier peoples. (3) The word "invader" carries an unproven assumption of violence. A better description is a gradual spread of farming communities.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the migrations as one event"}
They lasted many centuries and included many small movements in many directions. Use phrases such as "gradual" and "over centuries" and avoid dating the migration to a single year.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Bantu migrations with the Mfecane or the Ndebele migration"}
The Bantu migrations began thousands of years ago. The Mfecane and the Ndebele crossing of the Limpopo belong to the nineteenth century.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting contested points as certain"}
The origin in west-central Africa is well supported, but the routes, speed and causes are debated. Say "most scholars" or "the evidence suggests".
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Bantu languages began in the Nigeria-Cameroon region and spread over most of the southern half of Africa over many centuries.
- Evidence comes from linguistics, archaeology and other studies; details of the routes and dating are debated.
- Causes included population growth, search for land, iron and farming, environmental change and conflict.
- The migrations brought farming, iron-working and cattle-keeping, and displaced or absorbed San and Khoi-Khoi, though trade and intermarriage also took place.
- The Iron Age farming communities laid the foundations for later states such as Great Zimbabwe.
:::
