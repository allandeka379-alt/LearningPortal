## Introduction

In the 1830s an army of Nguni people, with their families and cattle, crossed the Limpopo and settled on the south-western plateau, where the Rozvi state had already been weakened. They became the Ndebele, whose kingdom lasted until the British South Africa Company invaded it in 1893. In a little over half a century, the Ndebele turned a refugee group into a powerful military state that dominated Matabeleland and exacted tribute from many Shona communities.

This lesson covers the Ndebele migration and the founding of the state, its organisation under **Mzilikazi** and **Lobengula**, and the pressures that undermined it. The war of 1893-94 is a separate lesson. The Mfecane that produced the migration is explained in the lesson on the Zulu state, and you should know how the Rozvi state declined.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inkosi"}
The Ndebele word for king or chief. The Ndebele king held supreme power in war, justice and ritual.
:::

:::definition{term="Induna (plural izinduna)"}
A senior officer or councillor who commanded a regiment or governed a district for the king and advised him.
:::

:::definition{term="Ibutho (plural amabutho)"}
An age regiment, a group of young men enrolled together who served the king as soldiers, herders and labourers until they were allowed to marry.
:::

:::definition{term="Incorporation"}
The absorption of conquered peoples into the state, giving them a place in its society. The Ndebele incorporated Sotho, Tswana and Shona people.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- About 1820s: Mzilikazi kaMashobane, a Khumalo chief, breaks away from Shaka's Zulu kingdom (approximate).
- 1830s: the Ndebele fight Boers, Griqua and Zulu in the Transvaal.
- 1837-1839: the Ndebele move north across the Limpopo.
- About 1840: they settle in south-western Zimbabwe, around Bulawayo.
- 1868: Mzilikazi dies; 1870: Lobengula is installed as king.
- 1888: Rudd Concession; 1890: the Pioneer Column enters Mashonaland.
:::

## Migration and founding

Mzilikazi was the leader of the Khumalo, a small Nguni clan that was a vassal of Shaka. According to tradition he quarrelled with Shaka over the cattle captured in a raid and led his followers away, in the early 1820s. They moved north-west into the highveld of the Transvaal, where they raided neighbouring Sotho and Tswana communities, took their cattle and absorbed many people. In 1837 Boer trekkers defeated them and Zulu forces under Dingane attacked from the east, and the Ndebele decided to go farther north. They crossed the Limpopo, and around 1840 settled in south-western Zimbabwe, in the country around the Matobo Hills and the later site of Bulawayo.

## Organisation of the state

**Political.** The king (*inkosi*) was supreme: he led the army, judged serious cases and performed rituals. He ruled with a council of *izinduna*, who advised and governed districts, and he used regional chiefs to manage subject peoples. The capital was the royal town, around which regimental towns were set. The king's powers were limited by custom and by the need to carry the council with him.

**Military.** The state was built on the *amabutho*. Young men from all groups were enrolled in regiments and lived in regimental towns, where they were trained and served as warriors, herders and tribute collectors. Regiments raided neighbours for cattle and tribute and defended the kingdom. Discipline was strict, and the king rewarded successful regiments with cattle.

**Social.** Ndebele society was divided into three broad groups, with a layer of status depending on origin. The **Zansi** were the original Khumalo Nguni, the highest group. The **Enhla** were those absorbed in the Transvaal, such as Sotho and Tswana. The **Holi** (*Amaholi*) were the local people incorporated after the settlement in Zimbabwe, mainly Shona, who had lower status. Over time, however, intermarriage and service in the army blurred the lines, and many Holi gained high positions.

**Economic.** The Ndebele were herders and farmers. Cattle were the main wealth and a sign of status, and the king owned very large herds. Women and subject peoples worked the fields. Raiding supplied cattle and **tribute** from the Shona, who gave grain, cattle and labour. The state also traded ivory and later hides with white hunters and traders who came to Bulawayo.

**Religious.** The Ndebele honoured their ancestors, especially the royal ancestors, and held the first-fruits ceremony. In their new land they came to consult the **Mwari cult** shrines in the Matobo Hills for rain, adopting a belief of the Shona and Kalanga peoples.

:::example{title="Explaining a feature: how the regiments held the state together"}
1. **Recruitment:** young men of every group (Zansi, Enhla, Holi) were enrolled in the same regiment.
2. **Loyalty:** living together under the king's officers and serving him weakened the older loyalties of clan and origin.
3. **Function:** regiments defended the state, enforced tribute and brought in cattle that the king distributed.
4. **Result:** a mixed refugee group became one nation with a shared loyalty to the king.
:::

## The reign of Lobengula

On Mzilikazi's death there was a period of uncertainty, and a regent governed for about two years, as some groups doubted whether the chosen heir, Lobengula, was truly Mzilikazi's son. In 1870 Lobengula was installed after he had defeated his rivals. He ruled from Bulawayo for nearly a quarter of a century. He kept the kingdom intact, maintained the regimental system and dealt cautiously with the white hunters, traders and missionaries who came to his court. To protect his kingdom he tried to balance the claims of foreigners against each other.

## Pressures that undermined the state

**Internal.**
- **Succession disputes** after Mzilikazi's death showed that the throne could be contested.
- **Divisions within the army:** young regiments wished to fight, while older leaders, and Lobengula himself, wished to avoid a war with a powerful opponent.
- **Resentment of Shona subjects** who paid tribute and suffered raiding, and who might side with a stronger power.
- **Dependence on raiding** and cattle, which ended when raids were stopped.

**External.**
- **Boer and British pressure from the south.** In 1887 the Transvaal (Grobler) treaty, and in February 1888 a treaty with Britain (the Moffat Treaty), limited Lobengula's freedom to deal with other powers.
- **Concession hunters.** After gold was discovered in the Transvaal in 1886, many seekers of mineral rights came to Bulawayo. On 30 October 1888 Lobengula's representatives signed the Rudd Concession, in which, according to the English text, he gave Rudd's partners the exclusive right to minerals in his territory in return for money, guns and a steamboat. Lobengula later claimed that he had been misled about its meaning.
- **The Royal Charter (1889) and the Pioneer Column (1890)**, which placed a British company with armed police on the borders of Matabeleland.

These external pressures, rather than any single internal weakness, brought the independence of the state to an end, as the lesson on the Anglo-Ndebele War shows.

:::example{title="Weighing internal and external causes of the state's fall"}
Some historians stress external factors, since the British South Africa Company's guns, money and political backing made resistance very hard. Others draw attention to the divisions in the Ndebele leadership and the discontent of Shona tributaries. A strong answer explains each and argues that external pressure was decisive, while internal divisions made the response weaker.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Ndebele with the Rozvi"}
The Rozvi were Shona-speaking rulers of the plateau until the 1830s. The Ndebele were Nguni newcomers who took over the south-west.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Ndebele state as only a raiding state"}
Raiding was important, but the state also farmed, herded, incorporated peoples and traded. Always show how the army and the economy fitted together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing the dates of Mzilikazi and Lobengula"}
Mzilikazi led the migration and ruled until 1868; Lobengula ruled from 1870 to 1894.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mzilikazi, a Khumalo chief, broke from Shaka in the 1820s, moved to the Transvaal, and about 1840 settled in south-western Zimbabwe.
- The state was ruled by the king with izinduna and held together by age regiments, cattle, tribute from the Shona and the incorporation of peoples (Zansi, Enhla, Holi).
- Lobengula became king in 1870 and ruled until 1894, trying to keep the kingdom independent.
- Internal pressures (succession dispute, divided army, resentful tributaries) and external pressures (treaties, concession hunters, the Rudd Concession and the Chartered Company) undermined the state.
- External pressure was decisive, but internal divisions weakened the response.
:::
