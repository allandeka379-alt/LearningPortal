## Introduction

By the middle of the fifteenth century the great stone city of Great Zimbabwe was losing its leading place on the plateau. To the north, in the lower Zambezi valley, a new power was growing. Its rulers took the title *Mwene Mutapa* (often written Mwenemutapa or Munhumutapa), and the Portuguese who reached the coast in the early sixteenth century called the state "Monomotapa". For about two centuries it was the leading state of the region, and it controlled much of the gold that reached the Indian Ocean coast.

This lesson explains where the Mutapa state came from, how it was organised, how its relations with the Portuguese developed and why it declined. The activities of the Portuguese themselves, such as their fortresses and missionaries, are covered in the lesson on Portuguese contacts; here the focus is the African state. You should know the lesson on Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe, because the Mutapa state's traditions begin there.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mwene Mutapa"}
The title of the ruler of the Mutapa state. It is usually explained as "lord of the conquered lands", a reference to the territories he ruled and to the tribute he received.
:::

:::definition{term="Prazo"}
A large estate leased by the Portuguese Crown in the Zambezi valley. The holders (*prazeros*) ruled over African inhabitants and, with their own armed followers, became a power in the valley in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.
:::

:::definition{term="Feira"}
A trading fair, the Portuguese word for the markets, such as Luanze and Masapa, where gold and ivory were exchanged for cloth and beads under the protection of the Mutapa.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- About 1430-1450: Mutota (Nyatsimba Mutota), according to tradition, leads his followers from Great Zimbabwe to the Zambezi valley (dates are approximate).
- About 1450-1480: his son Matope expands the state (approximate).
- 1505-1506: the Portuguese reach Sofala; Diogo de Alcacova writes about the Mutapa.
- 1561: the Jesuit missionary Gonçalo da Silveira is killed at the Mutapa's court.
- 1569-1572: Francisco Barreto leads a Portuguese expedition up the Zambezi that fails.
- 1607: treaty between the Mutapa Gatsi Rusere and the Portuguese.
- 1629: the Portuguese support Mavura Mhande, who accepts vassal status.
:::

## Origins and expansion

According to oral tradition, Mutota left Great Zimbabwe with followers, perhaps in search of salt and new land. He settled in the Dande region of the Zambezi valley and conquered the neighbouring peoples, such as the Tavara and Tonga, taking tribute from them. His son **Matope** continued the expansion, bringing under Mutapa control much of northern Zimbabwe and parts of Mozambique, and reaching towards the Indian Ocean. Portuguese reports of the early sixteenth century describe a large kingdom whose ruler received tribute from many chiefs.

These stories come from oral tradition and need care. The movement north is supported by the decline of Great Zimbabwe and the rise of the Mutapa in the same period, but the details, dates and the reasons for the journey (salt, land, trade or politics) are debated by historians. The Mutapa traditions also connect the new rulers to the Great Zimbabwe dynasty and to the Shona-speaking peoples of the plateau.

## Organisation of the state

**Political.** The *Mwene Mutapa* stood at the head of the state, regarded as sacred and linked to the royal ancestors. He ruled from a capital that moved with each reign. He was advised by a court of relatives and officials, and governed distant provinces through chiefs and governors, some of whom were relatives and others conquered chiefs who were left in place if they paid tribute and sent troops when called. Royal wives and sisters held positions of influence at court. Because there was no fixed rule of succession, several royal houses claimed the throne, which later weakened the state.

**Economic.** Most people farmed and kept cattle. The state's wealth came from **tribute** (gold, ivory, grain, cattle, cloth and service), from **mining** and panning of gold, and from the **trade** in gold and ivory to the coast. The Mutapa allowed foreign merchants to trade in his land under his control, taxed them and gave them a place at his court. Imported cloth and beads were distributed to reward followers.

**Social.** Society was divided between the royal family and nobles, commoners, and dependants including captives. Clan and kinship ties organised marriage and obligations.

**Religious.** The ruler had a special link with the ancestors. **Spirit mediums** (*mhondoro*) spoke for the royal ancestors, helped to legitimise a new ruler and could influence succession. The state sponsored ceremonies for rain and harvest. These religious ties gave authority to the ruler even over peoples he had conquered.

:::example{title="A cause explained step by step: why the Mutapa state expanded"}
1. **A base:** a new ruling family with cattle and followers settled in a fertile, well-watered valley on routes to the coast.
2. **Conquest:** Mutota and Matope subdued neighbouring peoples and made them pay tribute.
3. **Tribute and trade:** tribute and the gold and ivory trade brought wealth and goods that rewarded followers and paid soldiers.
4. **Ritual authority:** support from spirit mediums and royal ancestors gave the ruler legitimacy.
5. **Result:** a state that covered a large part of northern Zimbabwe and Mozambique.
:::

## Relations with the Portuguese

Before the Portuguese came, Muslim and Swahili merchants had traded with the Mutapa for gold. After the Portuguese took Sofala (1505) and entered the Zambezi (Sena and Tete) they tried to control the gold trade and win influence at the Mutapa's court. The relationship moved through stages.

![Timeline of the Mutapa state: Mutota's move north in about 1430-1450, Portuguese at Sofala in 1505, the treaty of 1607, Mavura's vassal status in 1629 and the Rozvi expulsion of the Portuguese in the late 1600s.](media:diagrams/mutapa-timeline.png "Figure: timeline of the Mutapa state and the Portuguese")

1. **Trade and diplomacy (sixteenth century).** The Portuguese were allowed to trade at fairs, and missionaries were received. In 1561 Gonçalo da Silveira baptised the Mutapa, but Muslim merchants and court advisers, afraid of Portuguese power, persuaded the king that Silveira was dangerous, and he was killed. The Portuguese used his death as a reason to intervene.
2. **Military intervention (1569-72).** The expedition of Francisco Barreto aimed to seize the gold mines but was ruined by disease and by the difficulty of the terrain, and failed.
3. **Growing influence (seventeenth century).** Portuguese settlers took advantage of disputes among the Mutapa's rivals. In 1607 Gatsi Rusere made a treaty with them that gave them certain rights, including over mines, in return for help against his enemies. In 1629 the Portuguese helped to put Mavura Mhande on the throne, and he accepted vassal status. Portuguese prazeros and traders became powerful in the valley.

The treaties are known mainly from Portuguese documents, and their wording and meaning are debated: the Portuguese claimed more than the Mutapa rulers may have intended to grant.

## Reasons for decline

- **Civil war and succession disputes.** The lack of a fixed rule for succession led to rival claimants and civil wars, and the Mutapa's authority weakened.
- **Portuguese interference.** The Portuguese took sides in disputes, extracted concessions and encouraged rebellions, so that the Mutapa lost independence over mines and trade.
- **Revolt of subject peoples.** Provinces such as Kiteve and Manyika, and the southern governor who became the Rozvi ruler Changamire, broke away.
- **The rise of the Rozvi.** Changamire Dombo's armies defeated Mutapa and Portuguese forces in the late seventeenth century.
- **Economic problems.** Output of gold fell, trade was disrupted and the population in the valley suffered from wars, drought and disease.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes of decline"}
Some historians stress Portuguese interference as the main cause, since the treaties tied the Mutapa to Portugal. Others point out that the Portuguese were few and weak in the interior, and that it was the internal weakness of the state, with disputed successions and rebellion by powerful provinces, that gave them their chance. A fair judgement is that internal weakness opened the door and Portuguese interference widened the cracks, while the Rozvi gave the decisive blow.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the Mutapa the builders of Great Zimbabwe"}
The Mutapa state arose after Great Zimbabwe had declined. Tradition links it to Great Zimbabwe through Mutota, but it was a separate state in the north.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the Portuguese as conquerors of the Mutapa"}
The Portuguese gained influence through treaties, support for rival claimants and local power in the Zambezi valley. They never conquered the plateau and were expelled from it by Changamire Dombo's army.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving exact dates for Mutota and Matope"}
Their reigns are dated only approximately from oral tradition and Portuguese documents; use "about" and "fifteenth century".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Mutapa state arose in the fifteenth century in the Zambezi valley under Mutota and Matope, according to tradition after a move from Great Zimbabwe.
- It was ruled by the Mwene Mutapa, with tributary chiefs, a court, spirit mediums and wealth from tribute, farming and the gold and ivory trade.
- Relations with the Portuguese moved from trade and diplomacy to intervention and, by 1629, vassal status for some Mutapa rulers.
- Decline came from succession disputes and civil war, Portuguese interference, the break-away of provinces and the rise of the Rozvi.
- Many details come from oral tradition and Portuguese documents and are debated.
:::
