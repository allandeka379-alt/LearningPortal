## Introduction

Southern Africa in 1200 CE was a land of villages, but within two centuries a stone-walled capital stood at Great Zimbabwe, ruling a large part of the plateau. About six centuries later a young Zulu chief built an army and a kingdom within a few years. Why do some communities become **states**, with a ruler, officials, an army and a system of tribute, while others stay as separate villages or chiefdoms? Historians offer several theories, and no single one explains every case.

This lesson gives you the frame for the lessons on individual states (Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, Rozvi, Ndebele, Zulu, Gaza and the Mandinka). It also gives you the vocabulary of organisation that examiners expect. Use a few contrasting examples here; each state has its own lesson. You should already know the Bantu migrations and the arrival of Iron Age farmers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="State"}
A society with a central authority that can make decisions, collect tribute or taxes, settle disputes and organise defence over a territory and its people. A **chiefdom** is smaller, with a chief ruling a limited area.
:::

:::definition{term="Tribute"}
Goods or labour (cattle, grain, ivory, gold, hunting or building service) that subjects or conquered peoples were obliged to give to a ruler.
:::

:::definition{term="Centralisation"}
The gathering of political power in the hands of a ruler and his officials at the centre, instead of leaving it with local chiefs.
:::

:::definition{term="Surplus"}
Food or goods produced beyond what a community needs for its own use. A surplus can be stored, exchanged or used to support people who do not farm, such as rulers, soldiers and craftsmen.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The six theories at a glance"}
Trade; surplus from farming and cattle; iron technology; religion and ritual leadership; conquest and migration; environment.
:::

## Theories of state formation

### 1. Trade

Control of long-distance trade gave rulers wealth and prestige goods, which they used to reward followers. The Zimbabwe plateau supplied gold and ivory to the Indian Ocean coast, and Great Zimbabwe's rulers are thought to have grown rich on this trade. *Weakness:* trade often followed state growth rather than causing it, and some states, such as the Zulu, grew with little long-distance trade at first.

### 2. Surplus from farming and cattle

Farming and cattle-keeping produced surplus that could support a ruling family and specialists. Cattle were stored wealth that could be lent to followers to create loyalty. *Weakness:* surplus alone does not explain why some farming communities, but not others, produced a state.

### 3. Iron technology

Iron tools improved farming, and iron weapons strengthened armies. Those who controlled iron-working had power. *Weakness:* iron-working was widespread, including in communities that never formed states.

### 4. Religion and ritual leadership

Rulers who could link the people with ancestors and with a high god, and who were believed to control rain, had authority. In the Shona region the cult of Mwari and the role of spirit mediums supported rulers. *Weakness:* religion supports authority but does not by itself create armies or a tribute system.

### 5. Conquest and migration

Some states were founded by leaders who conquered neighbours or by immigrants who took control of existing communities. The Ndebele under Mzilikazi and the Gaza under Soshangane are examples. *Weakness:* conquest explains the expansion of some states but not the origin of others, which grew peacefully.

### 6. Environment

Regions with good grazing, water, minerals and trade routes could support larger populations. The plateau between the Zambezi and Limpopo had gold, good pasture and relative freedom from the tsetse fly. *Weakness:* the same environment existed before and after states, so it cannot be the only cause.

| Theory | Example | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Trade | Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa | Trade may follow rather than cause a state |
| Surplus | Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe | Many farming societies stayed small |
| Iron | Early farming chiefdoms | Iron was widespread |
| Religion | Great Zimbabwe, Rozvi (Mwari) | Does not explain armies and tribute |
| Conquest | Ndebele, Zulu, Gaza | Does not explain peaceful growth |
| Environment | Plateau states | Same environment existed without states |

Most historians therefore combine theories: an environment and economy that produced a surplus, a ruler who could control trade or iron, a religion that supported him and, sometimes, an army.

## Common features of internal organisation

**Political.** A ruler (called *mambo*, *mwene*, *inkosi* or by other titles) stood at the head, often seen as sacred or as the link to the ancestors. He was advised by a **council** of relatives and senior men, and he used officials, regional chiefs and governors to rule distant areas. The capital was the political and ceremonial centre. States collected **tribute**, kept an **army** and settled disputes through courts. The ruler's authority was usually checked by customs and the need for the support of leading men.

**Economic.** Most people were farmers and herders. Cattle were the main form of wealth. Mining, iron-working, pottery and weaving were specialised crafts. Rulers controlled tribute and often a monopoly of key trade, such as gold and ivory. Surplus supported the court and army.

**Social.** Society was divided between a royal family and nobles, commoners and dependants (including captives). Kinship, clans and **totems** organised daily life and marriage. Age-sets or regiments were important in some states.

**Religious.** Beliefs in ancestors (especially royal ancestors) and in a high god guided community life. Rituals for rain and harvest, spirit mediums and shrines gave the ruler legitimacy.

:::example{title="Applying the theories: the Zulu and Great Zimbabwe compared"}
1. **Great Zimbabwe:** gold and ivory trade with the Indian Ocean (trade), cattle and farming surplus, ritual leadership and a rich environment explain its growth; conquest was probably less important.
2. **The Zulu state:** under Shaka military reorganisation and conquest (conquest) were central, supported by cattle wealth and by disputes over land and grazing in the region; long-distance trade mattered much less.
3. **Comparison:** the same theories appear but in different proportions. A strong answer states which theory is most convincing for each case and why.
:::

## How organisation helped states to expand

- **Tribute** funded the court and army and tied subject peoples to the centre.
- **A permanent army** or regiments allowed conquest and defence.
- **Marriage alliances** and the appointment of relatives or loyal officials as governors tied regions together.
- **Incorporation** of conquered peoples, who were given a place in the state, increased manpower.
- **A shared religion** or ritual centre gave unity.
- **Control of trade routes** brought wealth that rewarded followers.

But the same features created weaknesses. Succession disputes could split the state, subject peoples could rebel when tribute was heavy, and a state that depended on one ruler or one trade could collapse if that ruler died or the trade shifted. These weaknesses return in the lesson on the decline of states.

:::example{title="A paragraph of an essay answer"}
*"Trade was an important cause of the rise of the Mutapa state, but not the only one. Control of the gold and ivory trade with the coast gave the rulers wealth to reward followers and to buy imported cloth and beads. However, the Mutapa also relied on cattle and farming surplus, on military conquest of neighbouring peoples and on religion, as the king was linked to the ancestors. Trade therefore helped the state to grow, but it worked together with these other factors."*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming one cause as if it were proved"}
These are theories. Write "one theory argues" or "many historians combine". A single cause rarely explains the rise of a state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing organisation as a list"}
Do not only list "king, council, army". Explain how each feature helped the state to work or expand, for example how tribute supported the army.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up a chiefdom and a state"}
A chiefdom is a small unit under a chief; a state has a central ruler, officials, an army and a tribute system over a wider area.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2"}
Typical questions ask you to "account for the rise of states in pre-colonial Africa" or "analyse the political and economic organisation of a state you have studied". Link each theory to a named state, evaluate it, and finish with a judgement about the combination of causes. Questions on organisation are marked on both description and explanation of how organisation helped the state.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Theories for the rise of states include trade, surplus, iron technology, religion, conquest and environment; each has limitations.
- Most historians combine theories, with different weights in different states.
- Common features included a ruler with councillors and governors, tribute, an army, cattle wealth, social ranks and religious beliefs that supported the ruler.
- Organisation helped expansion through tribute, armies, marriage alliances, incorporation and shared religion.
- The same features created weaknesses, such as succession disputes, rebellions and dependence on one ruler.
:::
