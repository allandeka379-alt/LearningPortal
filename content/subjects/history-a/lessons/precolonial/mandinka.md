## Introduction

Most of the pre-colonial states you have studied lie in southern Africa. To judge them properly you need a comparison from another part of the continent. West Africa offers a very good one: the Mandinka state of **Samori Toure** was created by a trader's son who built a modern army, used Islam as a unifying force and for about sixteen years resisted the French army, which was among the best equipped in the world. Samori's story shows both the strength of nineteenth-century African states and the reasons why they fell.

This lesson covers the Mande background, Samori's state, its army, economy and religion, its resistance to French expansion and its fall. The partition of Africa as a whole is in the lesson on the Berlin Conference and the Scramble. Compare Samori with Mzilikazi, Ngungunyana and Lobengula as you read.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mande"}
The peoples of the upper Niger region of West Africa who speak related Manding languages, including the Mandinka (Malinke). Their ancestors built the empire of Mali in the thirteenth century.
:::

:::definition{term="Dyula"}
Mande traders, often Muslim, who carried goods such as kola nuts, gold, salt and cloth over long distances in West Africa. Samori's family belonged to this group.
:::

:::definition{term="Almamy"}
A title meaning "the Imam" or Muslim religious and political leader, taken by Samori in the 1880s.
:::

:::definition{term="Sofa"}
The professional infantry soldiers of Samori's army.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- Thirteenth century: Sundiata Keita founds the empire of Mali (the Mande heritage).
- About 1830: Samori Toure is born in the region now in Guinea.
- 1860s-1870s: Samori builds an army and conquers neighbouring chiefdoms; he sets up his state, often called the Wassoulou empire, with its capital at Bissandugu.
- About 1884: he takes the title Almamy.
- 1880s-1890s: wars with the French.
- 29 September 1898: Samori is captured by the French; he dies in exile in 1900.
:::

## The Mande heritage

The Mali empire (thirteenth to fifteenth centuries) grew from the Mande lands around the upper Niger. It was founded by Sundiata Keita and became famous for its gold, its trade across the Sahara and the pilgrimage of its ruler Mansa Musa to Mecca in 1324-25. The empire declined in the fifteenth century, but its traditions of kingship, the memory of Sundiata, and the networks of Dyula traders survived. Samori could draw on this heritage when he built his own state.

## Samori Toure's state

**Origins.** Samori was born into a Dyula family in the region of present-day Guinea. According to tradition he served as a soldier and then built a small army of his own. In the 1860s and 1870s he conquered his neighbours and built a state in the savanna and forest edge. He took the title of Almamy about 1884, making himself a religious as well as political leader.

**The army.** Samori's greatest achievement was his army. He organised a standing force of professional soldiers (*sofa*), trained and paid, together with cavalry and levies from conquered peoples. His army reached tens of thousands of men at its height, though exact numbers are estimates. He divided it into units under trusted commanders, and used discipline, regular drill and good intelligence. Importantly, he obtained **modern firearms**, especially breech-loading rifles, through trade with the British colony of Sierra Leone, and he set up workshops where blacksmiths repaired guns and copied some of them.

**The economy.** The state paid for weapons and soldiers through the gold of the Buré region, trade in kola nuts, cattle, ivory and cloth, and sales of captives taken in war, which were exchanged for horses and firearms. Agriculture and tribute from conquered peoples fed the army. Samori also relied on the Dyula traders' networks to move goods.

**Government and religion.** The state was divided into provinces governed by loyal commanders and officials, with Samori holding supreme authority. Islam was a unifying force: he encouraged mosques and Qur'anic schools and required Islamic law, which gave the state a common ideology above clan loyalties. But forced conversion in some conquered districts angered non-Muslim peoples and caused revolts.

:::example{title="Explaining a strength: why Samori's army was effective"}
1. **Organisation:** a standing army of paid professionals under trusted commanders.
2. **Weapons:** rifles obtained from Sierra Leone, repaired and copied in his own workshops.
3. **Finance:** gold, kola, cattle and captives paid for weapons and horses.
4. **Ideology:** Islam gave soldiers a common cause.
5. **Result:** a force that could defeat neighbouring states and for years delay the French.
:::

## Resistance to the French

From the 1880s the French army advanced from Senegal towards the upper Niger, seeking to build an empire in West Africa. Samori fought the French in a series of campaigns from about 1882 to 1898. He alternated between fighting and diplomacy: he signed treaties with the French in 1886-87 to gain time, and he tried to get British support from Sierra Leone. When the French pressed him, he moved his state eastwards, towards what is now Côte d'Ivoire, and he used scorched-earth tactics, burning the land in the path of the French so that they could not live off it. This policy also harmed the local people who suffered from his army's demands.

## The fall of Samori's state

- **French military superiority.** The French had artillery, machine guns, steamboats and better supplies, and an organised colonial army.
- **Loss of arms supply.** As the French and British agreed on borders in the 1890s, Samori lost access to the Sierra Leone arms market.
- **Revolts by subject peoples.** Forced conversion and heavy demands for food and men turned many peoples against him.
- **Lack of African unity.** Neighbouring rulers, such as the king of Sikasso, were rivals who sometimes helped the French.
- **Scorched earth.** His policy destroyed the economic base on which his army depended.
- **Imperial rivalry.** The Berlin Conference (1884-85) set rules for European claims, and France felt obliged to occupy its claims.

Samori was captured in September 1898 at Gelemou and exiled to Gabon. His resistance made him a national hero in Guinea.

:::example{title="Weighing the causes: military or political?"}
Some historians stress the French military advantage. Others point out that Samori's army held out for sixteen years, which shows that he was not powerless, and that internal opposition and the loss of arms supply were what made French technology decisive. A balanced judgement is that the French weapons gave victory, but the weaknesses within the state allowed them to win.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Samori Toure with Mansa Musa or Sundiata"}
Sundiata and Mansa Musa belong to the medieval Mali empire; Samori ruled in the nineteenth century.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the harm his rule caused"}
Samori was a gifted organiser and resister, but his wars, enslavement of captives and forced conversion also caused suffering. A balanced answer notes both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting his defeat as inevitable"}
He held out for years. Explain why the French eventually won rather than assuming it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Mandinka (Mande) heritage came from the Mali empire; Samori Toure, a Dyula trader's son, built a new state in the nineteenth century.
- His strength lay in a professional army (sofa), modern rifles from Sierra Leone, workshops, trade in gold and kola, and the unifying force of Islam.
- He resisted the French from the 1880s with fighting, treaties and scorched earth.
- The state fell because of French military superiority, loss of arms supplies, revolts of subject peoples and lack of African unity.
- Samori was captured on 29 September 1898 and died in exile in 1900.
:::
