## Introduction

Three years after the Pioneer Column raised its flag at Fort Salisbury, the British South Africa Company went to war with the Ndebele kingdom. The war lasted from October 1893 to the end of that year and ended with the destruction of the Ndebele state as an independent power. Ndebele fighters outnumbered the Company's force many times, but they lost the main battles in a few hours. The war shows how the new technology of the machine-gun, and the Company's careful preparation, outweighed numbers.

This lesson covers the causes, the events and the results of the war. You should know the Rudd Concession and the occupation of Mashonaland. The later risings of 1896-97 are in another lesson, and so is the Ndebele state itself in the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Maxim gun"}
An early machine-gun, invented in the 1880s, that fired hundreds of rounds a minute. It was carried by the Company's force and gave it the power to stop massed Ndebele charges.
:::

:::definition{term="Impi"}
A Nguni word for an army or fighting force, used for the Ndebele regiments.
:::

:::definition{term="Matabeleland Order in Council"}
The British order of 1894 that set up the Company's administration in Matabeleland.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- July 1893: the Victoria incident near Fort Victoria.
- 14 August 1893: the Victoria Agreement, which promised rewards to volunteers.
- 25 October 1893 (Shangani) and 1 November 1893 (Bembesi): the main battles.
- 3-4 November 1893: Bulawayo is found burnt and occupied.
- 4 December 1893: the Shangani Patrol under Major Allan Wilson is wiped out.
- Early 1894: Lobengula dies; Ndebele leaders submit.
- 1894: the Matabeleland Order in Council.
:::

## Causes of the war

**The Company's wish for Matabeleland.** By 1893 Mashonaland had not delivered the gold that investors expected. Settlers and shareholders looked to Matabeleland, with its pastures and cattle and its rumoured wealth, and the Company's administrator, Leander Starr Jameson, and Rhodes wished to remove the last African power in the country. The Company argued that the Concession allowed it to work all Lobengula's lands.

**Differing views of the Concession.** The king claimed that the Concession gave the Company only mineral rights of limited scope and that he remained ruler of his people. The Company treated it as the basis of its rule over the whole country. It also said that Lobengula had no right to rule Mashonaland, where his regiments had taken tribute and cattle from the Shona; it presented itself as a protector of the Shona. Some historians see this as a pretext, since the Company had little concern for the Shona in other ways.

**Ndebele raiding and Company policy.** The Ndebele continued to raid Shona communities near the Company's settlements. The Company's agents demanded that this stop and that the Ndebele stay west of an agreed line. Lobengula said that the Shona were his subjects and were stealing from his cattle posts.

**The Victoria incident (July 1893).** Shona people near Fort Victoria were said to have stolen cattle and cut telegraph wire. Lobengula sent an impi to punish them. Jameson ordered the Ndebele to withdraw beyond the Company's boundary; when they did so, a Company patrol fired on them and killed a number, according to several reports. Jameson used the event to claim that the Ndebele had attacked the Company. The incident was the trigger for war, but historians agree that Jameson and Rhodes had already decided that Matabeleland was to be taken.

:::example{title="Weighing causes: trigger or underlying?"}
Separate **underlying causes** (the wish for land and gold, Rhodes's ambition, the clash over the Concession) from the **trigger** (the Victoria incident). A strong paragraph explains that the incident would not have led to war if the Company had wished to settle the matter peacefully. A Company which had already prepared its forces and promised rewards to volunteers was seeking a reason, not accidentally finding one.
:::

## The Victoria Agreement

In August 1893 the Company agreed with volunteers that each man would receive a farm of about 6,000 acres, 20 gold claims and a share of the cattle captured. About 700 men were raised, and Khama's Ngwato soldiers from the Bechuanaland side supported the Company. Lobengula sent messages to the British asking for peace, but they came to nothing.

## The course of the war

Three columns advanced towards Bulawayo from the east and south. On **25 October 1893** the Ndebele attacked the Company's laager at the **Shangani** river and were driven off with heavy losses. On **1 November**, at **Bembesi**, a larger Ndebele force of several thousand men attacked the laager in the open. The Maxim guns and rifles of the column, perhaps five Maxims and the rifles of the Company's men, cut the charges down. Perhaps a thousand or more Ndebele were killed, while the Company lost very few. Lobengula had left Bulawayo, set fire to it and moved north. The Company's columns entered the burnt town on 3-4 November 1893.

The Company pursued the king. In early December a patrol of 34 men under Major Allan Wilson crossed the Shangani river, was cut off from the main column and was wiped out. The **Shangani Patrol** became a symbol for settlers; for the Ndebele it was proof of their courage. Lobengula continued to move north and died early in 1894, probably of smallpox; the place of his burial is not known.

## Why the Ndebele lost

- **Weapons:** the Maxim gun and modern rifles against spears and some old rifles.
- **Tactics:** the Company's laager defence and mounted men.
- **Preparation:** the Company had raised volunteers and supplies and had allies.
- **Ndebele weaknesses:** Lobengula hesitated, did not unite the whole nation, and some of his indunas wished to avoid war; some Shona groups gave help to the Company out of resentment of Ndebele raids.

## Results for the Ndebele

- **Loss of independence.** The kingdom ceased to exist. In 1894 the Matabeleland Order in Council put Matabeleland under the Company, with a Native Department and Native Commissioners.
- **Loss of land.** Settlers took farms in the fertile areas around Bulawayo and elsewhere. The Ndebele were later put on reserves, such as the Gwaai and Shangani reserves, on poorer land.
- **Loss of cattle.** The Company and settlers seized very large numbers of cattle, the main wealth of the people. Estimates vary, but the loss was severe.
- **Social effects:** the regiments were broken up, and many Ndebele had to work for wages or labour for settlers. Those who had held authority lost it. These grievances helped to bring about the rising of 1896.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Victoria incident was the only cause"}
It was the trigger. The underlying causes were the Company's wish for land and gold, Rhodes's aims and the dispute about the Concession.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Ndebele were defeated because they were cowardly or backward"}
They fought bravely and in large numbers. They lost because of the Maxim gun and rifles, tactics, preparation and divided leadership.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing 1893-94 with 1896-97"}
The war of 1893-94 destroyed the Ndebele state. The risings of 1896-97 were the Ndebele and Shona response to Company rule.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Company wanted Matabeleland's land and wealth and disputed Lobengula's reading of the Concession.
- The Victoria incident of July 1893 was the trigger; the Victoria Agreement promised farms, claims and cattle to volunteers.
- Maxim guns won the battles of Shangani (25 October) and Bembesi (1 November), and Bulawayo was occupied in early November.
- The Shangani Patrol was lost on 4 December, and Lobengula died in early 1894.
- The Ndebele lost independence, land and cattle, which prepared the ground for 1896.
:::
