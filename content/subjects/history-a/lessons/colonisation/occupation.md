## Introduction

On 12 September 1890 a small party of men raised the British flag on a hill beside a stream in Mashonaland and named their camp Fort Salisbury, after the British prime minister. They had marched for three months from the south with several hundred police and settlers. No battle was fought that day, but the date marks the start of white settler rule in the country that became Rhodesia and later Zimbabwe. Behind it lay two years of diplomacy and a document, the Rudd Concession, whose meaning is still debated.

This lesson explains why the country was wanted and how occupation was achieved. It ends with the flag at Fort Salisbury; the war against the Ndebele in 1893-94 is a separate lesson. You should know the treaties of 1887 and 1888 from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Concession"}
A written grant by an African ruler of rights, in this case to minerals. The Rudd Concession gave Rhodes's agents the exclusive right to metals and minerals in Lobengula's kingdom.
:::

:::definition{term="Royal Charter"}
A document from the British Crown that gave the British South Africa Company (BSAC) the right to administer and develop a large area north of the Limpopo, to make treaties, to raise police, and to hold land and mining rights.
:::

:::definition{term="Pioneer Column"}
The party of about 180 settlers, escorted by about 500 BSAC police, that entered Mashonaland in 1890 to occupy the country on behalf of the Company.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- February 1888: Moffat Treaty.
- 30 October 1888: the Rudd Concession, signed at Bulawayo.
- 29 October 1889: the Royal Charter to the British South Africa Company.
- June 1890: the Pioneer Column leaves Bechuanaland.
- 12 September 1890: the flag is hoisted at Fort Salisbury.
:::

## Why Zimbabwe was wanted

- **Minerals.** Gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886 led many to believe that a second Rand lay to the north, in Matabeleland and Mashonaland. Old workings and the stories of earlier visitors supported the belief.
- **Rhodes's ambitions.** Cecil John Rhodes, a diamond magnate at Kimberley and later prime minister of the Cape (1890-96), wanted to extend British rule northward, "from the Cape to Cairo", and to make fortunes from the land.
- **Rivalry with other states.** Britain wished to keep the Portuguese from joining Mozambique to Angola across the interior, and to prevent the Transvaal and Germany from reaching the north.
- **Land and settlement.** Settlers expected good farmland and an area free of Boer control.

## The Rudd Concession

In October 1888 a party sent by Rhodes, Charles Rudd, Rochfort Maguire and Francis Thompson, with the help of missionary Charles Helm as interpreter and with the support of Moffat and British officials, came to Bulawayo. After weeks of discussion Lobengula made his mark on 30 October 1888. The document gave the party exclusive rights over all metals and minerals in his kingdom and the right to do whatever was necessary to win them. In return Lobengula was to receive £100 each month, 1,000 Martini-Henry rifles with 100,000 rounds of ammunition, and a gunboat on the Zambezi (or £500 instead).

**The dispute.** Lobengula afterwards said that he had understood that only a few white men would dig in his country and that they would obey his laws, and that the written terms did not say this. He said that he had been tricked, and he denied that he had given away the country. In 1889 he sent two councillors, Babayane and Mshete, to London to see Queen Victoria. Rhodes's agents, on the other hand, said that the written text was clear and that Lobengula had signed with his eyes open. The interpreter, the lack of a witness on the king's side and the unequal knowledge of the parties are the main points in the debate.

:::example{title="Analysing a source: the king's complaint"}
Suppose a British newspaper report of 1889 says that Lobengula complained that he had been promised that only a few white men would dig and that they would obey his laws (this is a paraphrase, not the king's words). Ask four questions. **Who** is speaking, and were they present? **What** does it say, and does it match the document? **Why** was the complaint made (to persuade the British to cancel the concession)? **How** can it be checked? The Concession's text, reports of the negotiations and the later behaviour of the Company are the evidence to weigh. The best judgement is that the king and the Company understood the transaction in different ways, and the written text favoured the Company.
:::

## The Royal Charter, 1889

Armed with the Concession, Rhodes went to London in 1889. He joined rival groups into one company and persuaded the British government, under Lord Salisbury, that a chartered company could occupy the north at little cost to taxpayers. On **29 October 1889** the British government granted a Royal Charter to the British South Africa Company. It could make treaties, raise a police force, hold land and mineral rights, and administer the territory north of the Limpopo, though its borders were not exactly defined. The government kept the right to cancel the Charter and to approve the Company's choice of administrator. In return the Company promised to respect African customs and to end slavery, promises that were often ignored.

## The Pioneer Column

The Company planned to enter Mashonaland, not Matabeleland, in order to avoid a fight with the Ndebele. The column was led by Frank Johnson, with Frederick Selous as guide and a police escort under Lieutenant-Colonel Pennefather. About 180 pioneers and some 500 police left Bechuanaland in June 1890. They cut a road north-east, built forts (Tuli, Victoria and Charter), and reached the site of Harare on 12 September 1890, where they hoisted the flag. Lobengula protested but did not attack, and the column was disbanded within weeks, the pioneers being promised farms and mining claims.

## Rivalry with the Portuguese and the Boers

The Portuguese claimed a belt of land from Mozambique to Angola. Their claims alarmed Rhodes, who sent agents to make treaties with Shona chiefs on the eastern side. In January 1890 Britain's ultimatum forced Portugal to withdraw its forces from the Shire highlands and from Mashonaland, and in 1891 the boundary between Portuguese and British claims was agreed. The Transvaal, for its part, still talked of trekking north; a small Boer trek in 1891 was prevented. Rivalry encouraged haste.

## The role of Chaminuka in Shona tradition

Shona oral tradition remembers **Chaminuka**, a spirit medium of the Zezuru, as a prophet who foretold the arrival of the whites. In the stories he said that strangers would come in wagons and take the land. Traditions also say that he was killed on Lobengula's order. The account comes from oral tradition, not from documents, and the details differ from teller to teller. It shows how the Shona remembered colonisation as something that was foreseen by their spiritual leaders.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Lobengula sold his country"}
The Rudd Concession gave mineral rights, not ownership of the land. The Company later claimed more than the document said, and the king disputed its meaning.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Concession, the Charter and the Pioneer Column"}
The Concession (1888) gave rights. The Charter (1889) gave the Company authority. The Column (1890) put men in the country. Keep them in order and say what each added.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Pioneers fought the Ndebele"}
They avoided Matabeleland and fought no war in 1890. The war came in 1893.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hopes of gold, Rhodes's ambitions and rivalry with Portugal, the Transvaal and Germany drove the occupation.
- The Rudd Concession of 30 October 1888 gave exclusive mineral rights; Lobengula disputed what he had agreed.
- The Royal Charter of 29 October 1889 gave the British South Africa Company authority north of the Limpopo.
- The Pioneer Column, avoiding Matabeleland, reached Harare and raised the flag at Fort Salisbury on 12 September 1890.
- Shona tradition remembers Chaminuka as foretelling the coming of the whites.
:::
