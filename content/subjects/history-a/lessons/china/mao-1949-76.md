## Introduction

When Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic on 1 October 1949, China had been at war almost continuously for decades. Mao ruled until he died in September 1976. In those twenty-seven years the Communist Party ended foreign domination, reorganised land and industry, raised literacy and health care and made China a nuclear power. The same period also saw one of the worst famines in history and a decade of political turmoil. The question for students is how to weigh the achievements against the costs, and how far Mao's methods were successful. There is also a link to Zimbabwe: the Sino-Soviet split of the 1960s was reflected in the liberation movements, with ZANU and ZANLA looking to China and ZAPU and ZIPRA to the Soviet Union.

You should know from the previous lesson how the Communists won power in 1949.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="People's commune"}
A large unit of rural organisation created in 1958 which combined farming, small industry, schools, health care and local government and replaced the earlier co-operatives. Families ate in communal canteens.
:::

:::definition{term="Great Leap Forward"}
The campaign of 1958-62 to raise industrial and farm output very rapidly through mass mobilisation of labour rather than by machinery and expertise.
:::

:::definition{term="Cultural Revolution"}
The Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution, launched by Mao in 1966, in which students (Red Guards) and others attacked "capitalist roaders", intellectuals and traditional culture, in order to renew revolutionary spirit and to restore Mao's authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Red Guards"}
Groups of students and young people, mobilised from 1966, who attacked teachers, officials and the "Four Olds" (old ideas, culture, customs and habits), and fought each other in factions.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}

## Consolidation, 1949-1957

The Party moved quickly to control the country. The **Agrarian Reform Law** (1950) took land from landlords and gave it to some 300 million landless and poor peasants. Mass meetings denounced landlords, and many were killed; estimates run from hundreds of thousands to over a million. Campaigns against "counter-revolutionaries" (1950-51) and against corruption and the capitalists (the Three-anti and Five-anti campaigns, 1951-52) removed opponents and brought business under control. The Marriage Law of 1950 banned arranged marriage and gave women equal rights. In the Korean War (1950-53) China sent its army against the United Nations forces, and the war strengthened the regime's hold and its ties to the Soviet Union.

![Timeline of Mao's China from the 1950 Agrarian Reform Law and the First Five-Year Plan to the Great Leap Forward of 1958-62, the famine of 1959-61, the Cultural Revolution from 1966, Mao's death in September 1976 and the arrest of the Gang of Four.](media:diagrams/mao-china-timeline.png "Figure: timeline of Mao's China, 1950-1976")

The **First Five-Year Plan** (1953-57) followed the Soviet model, with heavy industry, large state projects and Soviet advisers and loans. Industry grew fast. Farmers were pushed from mutual-aid teams into co-operatives by 1956, and private business was nationalised. In 1956 the "Hundred Flowers" campaign invited intellectuals to criticise the Party; when the criticism went further than Mao expected, the Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1957 punished hundreds of thousands.

## The Great Leap Forward, 1958-1962

Mao believed that China could skip stages and overtake Britain in steel within about fifteen years, by mobilising the masses. In 1958 the Party set up **people's communes**, which by the end of the year included almost all the rural population, and called for steel to be made in backyard furnaces. Peasants were taken from the fields to build dams and make steel, and the iron was often useless. Local officials, fearing punishment, reported wildly exaggerated harvests, so the state took more grain than the peasants could spare. Bad weather in some regions in 1959-61 made things worse. At the Lushan Conference of 1959 Marshal Peng Dehuai criticised the Leap and was dismissed, which silenced others.

The result was a catastrophic famine in 1959-61. Estimates of the number of excess deaths vary very widely. Official Chinese statistics imply about 16 million; many scholars estimate between 15 and 45 million or more. The disagreement arises from poor records, and the true number will probably never be known. By 1961 the leadership under Liu Shaoqi and Deng Xiaoping reversed the worst policies: communes were reduced, private plots were restored, and grain was imported. Mao stepped back from the daily running of government.

:::example{title="Why did the Great Leap Forward fail?"}
A good answer separates causes. The aims were unrealistic: mass labour could not replace machines and skill. The methods were harmful: communal canteens, steel from backyard furnaces and the neglect of the harvest. The political system made the problem worse: officials exaggerated results, and critics like Peng Dehuai were punished, so Mao heard only good news. Natural disasters made things worse, but historians generally see policy as the main cause. A judgement: the Leap failed because a system without honest information was driven by an unrealistic goal.
:::

## The Sino-Soviet split

China at first depended on the USSR. But after Stalin's death (1953), disagreements grew. Mao disliked Khrushchev's criticism of Stalin in 1956 and his policy of "peaceful coexistence" with the West, and the Soviet Union would not give China nuclear weapons technology. In 1960 the USSR recalled its advisers, which ended many projects. China condemned Soviet "revisionism" and competed for the leadership of world communism and of anti-colonial movements in Africa and Asia. In 1969 Chinese and Soviet troops fought on the Ussuri River. China tested its first atomic bomb in 1964. The split opened the way for improved relations with the United States, and President Nixon visited China in February 1972.

## The Cultural Revolution, 1966-1976

Mao felt that his authority had been weakened after the Leap and that the Party was becoming bureaucratic and was moving towards capitalist ideas, as he thought the Soviet Union had. In 1966 he called on young people to "bombard the headquarters". **Red Guards** attacked teachers, officials and intellectuals, destroyed temples, books and works of art and held struggle meetings in which victims were humiliated and sometimes killed. Schools and universities closed. Liu Shaoqi was removed and died in detention in 1969, and Deng Xiaoping was sent to the countryside. A cult of Mao spread, with the "Little Red Book" of his quotations. By 1967-68 rival Red Guard factions were fighting each other, and Mao used the army under Lin Biao to restore order. Millions of urban youth were sent "down to the countryside". Lin Biao himself fell: he died in a plane crash in 1971 after an alleged plot. Radical leaders, including Mao's wife Jiang Qing (the later "Gang of Four"), kept influence while Zhou Enlai tried to keep government and the economy working.

Estimates of the number of deaths in the Cultural Revolution range from several hundred thousand to two million or more; many more were persecuted or lost education. The economy was disrupted but not wrecked, and rural health clinics and basic schooling expanded.

## China at Mao's death, and an assessment

When Mao died on 9 September 1976, China was united and independent, with a nuclear weapon, a basic industrial base and far higher literacy and life expectancy than in 1949. But living standards had grown slowly, millions had suffered in the Leap and the Cultural Revolution, and the Party was divided. Within a month the Gang of Four was arrested. Deng Xiaoping, restored to power by 1978, began economic reforms, and in 1981 the Party declared that the Cultural Revolution had been a disaster.

Historians divide on Mao's record. Supporters point to national unity, land reform, women's rights, health and education. Critics point to the famine, the persecutions and the cult, and argue that the costs outweighed the gains. A balanced view recognises that the gains and the costs both came from Mao's policy of mass mobilisation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving a single death figure for the Great Leap famine"}
The figures range from about 15 to 45 million or more. Give the range and say why it varies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing the Hundred Flowers, the Great Leap and the Cultural Revolution"}
The Hundred Flowers (1956-57) was a campaign to invite criticism; the Leap (1958-62) was an economic campaign; the Cultural Revolution (1966-76) was a political and cultural campaign.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting Mao's policy as only success or only disaster"}
A good answer distinguishes areas (land, health, education, the economy, politics) and periods (1949-57 and later).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Party consolidated power by land reform, campaigns against opponents, the Marriage Law and the Korean War; the First Five-Year Plan followed the Soviet model.
- The Great Leap Forward (1958-62) aimed at rapid growth through communes and backyard steel but led to a famine in which an estimated 15 to 45 million or more died.
- The Sino-Soviet split (1956-60, with border clashes in 1969) separated China from the USSR and later led to contacts with the United States.
- The Cultural Revolution (1966-76) attacked the Party's officials, intellectuals and traditional culture, causing widespread persecution and disruption.
- At Mao's death China was independent and more educated and healthier than in 1949, but poor, divided and scarred; historians weigh gains against costs.
:::
