## Introduction

The Bolsheviks of 1917 wanted to create not only a new state but a new kind of person: equal women, educated workers, no religion and a culture that served the revolution. By the 1930s Stalin had built the industry and the secret police that no one could resist, and he also reshaped the everyday life of tens of millions of people. Cities grew, schools spread, women went to work and the Church was battered. Yet in other ways the regime turned back to older ideas of family, discipline and Russian national pride. Cambridge asks "How far did Stalin transform Soviet society?", and the answer depends on a careful choice of examples.

You should know the economic changes of the First Five Year Plan and the nature of the Terror from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="The Great Retreat"}
A name given by the historian Nicholas Timasheff to the shift in the 1930s away from the radical social experiments of the 1920s towards traditional ideas about the family, discipline in schools and national pride.
:::

:::definition{term="Socialist Realism"}
The official style for the arts from 1932-34: works had to show life in its revolutionary development, in a way that was accessible to ordinary people, optimistic, and in service to the party and the building of socialism.
:::

:::definition{term="Komsomol"}
The Communist Youth League, for young people from about 14 to 28, which trained future party members and organised young people for the plans. Younger children joined the Pioneers.
:::

:::definition{term="Korenizatsiya"}
The 1920s policy of "rooting" Soviet rule in the non-Russian republics by promoting local languages, schools and officials. It was reversed in the 1930s.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1920: abortion legalised. 1936: abortion banned (except for medical reasons) and divorce made harder. 1944: further family laws.
- 1928-31: the Cultural Revolution. 1932: independent literary groups abolished; 1934: Union of Soviet Writers and Socialist Realism. January 1936: Pravda attacks Shostakovich's opera.
- 1929: law against religious activity; League of the Militant Godless (1925). 1937: census results on religion suppressed.
- 1937-38: Koreans deported from the Far East. 1938: Russian made compulsory in non-Russian schools.
:::

## Women and the family

After 1917 the Bolsheviks gave women legal equality, easy divorce and, in 1920, the right to abortion, and the party's women's department (Zhenotdel, 1919-30) campaigned for women's rights. In the 1930s the regime turned to a more conservative policy. It needed a growing population and a stable family to produce workers and soldiers. The law of 1936 banned abortion except on medical grounds, made divorce more costly and offered payments to mothers of many children. The Constitution promised equality, and women entered the workforce on a large scale, so that by 1940 they were roughly two-fifths of the workforce (an estimate). Many trained as doctors, teachers and engineers. But they also did most of the housework and childcare, with few nurseries or public canteens, and they were rarely found in senior party posts. Historians describe this "double burden" and the growth of a conventional ideal of the Soviet mother.

## Young people and education

The regime saw the young as the builders of socialism. The Komsomol and Pioneers organised sports, camps and political education. Young people were encouraged to report on those who opposed the regime; the story of Pavlik Morozov, a boy said to have denounced his father in 1932 and to have been killed by relatives, was publicised as an example, though historians dispute the details.

In schools the experimental methods of the 1920s were abandoned in 1931-36. The state restored lessons, textbooks, examinations, marks, uniforms and the authority of the teacher, and history was taught as a story of Russian and Soviet progress. Mass education brought real results: the share of the population who could read rose from about one half in 1926 to about four-fifths by 1939 (census figures), and many working-class and peasant children gained places in technical colleges. Workers promoted to management and the party filled the places of those killed in the Terror, which gave a sense of opportunity. From 1940 fees were introduced in upper secondary and higher education.

:::example{title="How far did Stalin transform education?"}
A strong paragraph weighs evidence on both sides. Evidence for transformation: a huge rise in literacy, new technical schools and social mobility for the children of workers and peasants, who rose into the new elite. Evidence of limits: the system was strictly controlled, teaching served propaganda, the experimental ideas of the 1920s were scrapped, and fees from 1940 limited access. A balanced judgement is that education was expanded and made useful for the state, but it was made conservative and tightly controlled rather than liberating.
:::

## Culture and Socialist Realism

During the Cultural Revolution of 1928-31 the party attacked "bourgeois" specialists and encouraged proletarian writers and artists. In 1932 all independent literary groups were dissolved, and in 1934 the Union of Soviet Writers was founded with the doctrine of **Socialist Realism**. Novels, films, paintings and music showed heroic workers, happy collective farmers and wise leaders; literature, theatre and film had to celebrate the plans and Stalin. Artists who followed the line gained honours, housing and privileges; those who did not were silenced. The composer Dmitri Shostakovich was condemned in *Pravda* in January 1936 after Stalin attended his opera, and many writers and directors were arrested in the Terror or died in the camps (for example, the poet Osip Mandelstam in 1938). Some work of lasting quality was produced, including films such as those of Sergei Eisenstein and the music of Shostakovich and Prokofiev, but the arts were firmly under state control.

## Religion

The Bolsheviks regarded religion as a rival to the party's ideas. The League of the Militant Godless (founded 1925) ran anti-religious propaganda. The law of 1929 restricted religious activity to worship, many churches, mosques and synagogues were closed or turned into stores, and priests, mullahs and rabbis were arrested. During the Terror many thousands of clergy were shot; by 1939 only a few hundred Orthodox churches were open, a small fraction of those that had existed. In the 1937 census a question on belief showed that a large share of adults, probably a majority, still described themselves as believers; the results were suppressed and the officials were punished. Religious life survived in private and in the villages. The regime eased its policy only in the Second World War.

## National groups

In the 1920s the policy of *korenizatsiya* promoted local languages, schools and officials in the non-Russian republics, and the USSR (formed in 1922) was officially a union of equal republics. In the 1930s the policy changed. The leaders of national communist parties were purged as "bourgeois nationalists"; Russian was made a compulsory subject in all schools (1938); and the Russian people were called the "elder brother" of the others. Some groups were deported as supposedly disloyal: for instance, about 170,000 Koreans were moved from the Far East to Central Asia in 1937. The collectivisation of Kazakh herders led to a catastrophic famine in which estimates of the dead run to well over a million. Alongside these hardships there were gains for some, such as written languages for peoples that had none, schools and clinics.

| Culture | New mass audiences, great artists | Socialist Realism and censorship; arrests |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing all change as progress, or all as repression"}
Stalin's policies expanded literacy and women's work and also tightened control. Both must be assessed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming women were freer in the 1930s than in the 1920s"}
They gained jobs and training, but lost the right to abortion, and divorce became harder.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that religion disappeared"}
It was heavily attacked, but many people still believed, as the 1937 census suggests.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The 1930s "Great Retreat" brought a more conservative policy on the family: abortion was banned in 1936 and divorce made harder, while women worked in large numbers.
- Schools were made stricter and more traditional, literacy rose from about one half to about four-fifths, and the Komsomol organised the young.
- Culture was controlled through Socialist Realism and the Union of Soviet Writers; artists who did not conform were silenced.
- Religion was attacked by propaganda, closures and arrests, but belief survived.
- Policy on national groups moved from korenizatsiya to Russification, purges and deportations.
- The transformation was real in education, work and cities, but limited by control and by continuities such as the family and religion.
:::
