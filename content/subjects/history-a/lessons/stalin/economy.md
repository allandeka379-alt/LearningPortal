## Introduction

In 1928 the Soviet Union was a mainly peasant country that still farmed largely with simple tools, and its industrial output was only just back to the level of 1913. Twelve years later it was one of the world's leading industrial powers, able to produce tanks and aircraft in large numbers. Between those dates, millions of peasants were driven from their land into collective farms, millions died in a famine, and workers were mobilised in a vast building campaign. Historians still argue whether this "revolution from above" was necessary, whether it was effective and whether the costs can be justified.

You should know about the New Economic Policy and about the power struggle in which Bukharin was defeated (see the earlier lessons).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Collectivisation"}
The policy of replacing small peasant farms with large collective farms (kolkhozy) owned and worked in common, with the aim of controlling grain supplies and mechanising farming. State farms (sovkhozy) were run directly by the state.
:::

:::definition{term="Dekulakisation"}
The campaign from 1929-30 to remove the "kulaks", the supposedly rich peasants, as a class. In practice the label was applied to any peasants who resisted. Families were deported, or sent to labour camps, and their property was seized.
:::

:::definition{term="Five Year Plan and Gosplan"}
A Five Year Plan set targets for production in each sector of the economy. Gosplan, the State Planning Commission, drew up the plans.
:::

:::definition{term="Stakhanovite movement"}
A campaign launched in 1935 after Alexei Stakhanov, a miner in the Donbas, was reported to have cut 102 tons of coal in one shift, many times the norm. Workers were urged to follow his example, and those who did were rewarded with privileges.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1927-28: grain procurement crisis. December 1929: Stalin announces the liquidation of the kulaks as a class. March 1930: the article "Dizzy with Success" slows collectivisation.
- 1928-32: First Five Year Plan (declared complete in four years and three months); 1933-37: Second; 1938-41: Third (interrupted by war in June 1941).
- 1932-33: famine. August 1935: Stakhanov's record. Magnitogorsk (steel), Dnieprostroi (dam) and the Moscow metro (1935) were symbols of the plans.
:::

## Why the Great Turn?

Historians give several reasons for Stalin's decision in 1928-29 to end NEP.

- **The grain crisis.** In 1927-28 peasants withheld grain because state prices were low, and the towns were short of food. Stalin sent officials to seize grain. He concluded that the state could not rely on peasants selling voluntarily.
- **Industrial need and security.** Stalin argued that the USSR was far behind the West and would be crushed in a future war unless it industrialised quickly. The "war scare" of 1927 supported this.
- **Ideology.** Socialism required state ownership and planning. NEP seemed to favour richer peasants and traders.
- **Politics.** Taking up the programme of the defeated Left gave Stalin a policy of his own and allowed him to defeat Bukharin.
- **Export earnings.** Machinery had to be imported, and grain was the main export.

## Collectivisation and dekulakisation

In December 1929 Stalin announced the end of the kulaks as a class. Party activists, backed by the OGPU and by workers sent to the villages (the "25,000ers"), forced peasants into collective farms. Resistance was widespread: peasants killed their livestock rather than hand it over, and there were thousands of disturbances. The numbers of cattle, pigs and sheep fell sharply. In March 1930 Stalin blamed local officials for excess zeal in "Dizzy with Success", and the share of peasants in collectives briefly fell, but pressure resumed. By 1932 roughly three in five households were in collectives and by the late 1930s nearly all. Historians estimate that about 1.8 million people were deported as kulaks in 1930-31 and that many died in exile.

The state set high quotas for grain deliveries from the collectives, which left peasants too little to eat in some regions. Combined with a poor harvest and bad weather in some areas, this produced the **famine of 1932-33**. Historians estimate that between 5 and 8 million people died, in Ukraine, the Volga region, Kazakhstan and elsewhere; the figures are uncertain. In Ukraine the famine is called the Holodomor. Some historians and governments call it a deliberate genocide against Ukrainians; others argue that it was the result of policies against the peasantry as a whole, in which the leadership refused to cut grain exports or deliveries. The regime denied it was occurring. The collective farms gave the state control of grain, and the Machine Tractor Stations provided machinery to the farms. In the mid-1930s peasants were allowed small private plots, which produced a large share of vegetables, milk and meat. Grain output recovered only slowly, and livestock numbers did not reach the 1928 level until the late 1930s.

:::example{title="Was collectivisation a success?"}
A balanced answer sets a criterion. If the aim was state control of the grain supply and a surplus to feed the cities and to export, it largely succeeded after 1933, and labour was released for industry. If the aim was a more productive agriculture, it failed in the short term: output and livestock fell, and millions died. The long-term judgement is that collectivisation served the state's needs for control and industrialisation, at a terrible human cost, and left agriculture inefficient for decades.
:::

## Industry and the Five Year Plans

The **First Five Year Plan** (1928-32) concentrated on heavy industry: coal, iron, steel, electricity and machinery. Giant projects included the steel city of Magnitogorsk, the Dnieprostroi hydroelectric dam, the Stalingrad tractor works and the White Sea Canal built by prisoners. Targets were raised during the plan, and the plan was declared fulfilled in four years and three months, although many targets were missed and figures were exaggerated. The **Second Plan** (1933-37) was more realistic, stressed transport and machine-building, and built the Moscow metro. The **Third Plan** (1938-41) emphasised defence industries and was cut short by the German invasion.

Industrial growth was real and fast. Soviet figures showed that steel, coal and electricity output rose several times over by 1940; western estimates are lower, but accept that heavy industry grew very rapidly. New industrial cities were built in the Urals and Siberia, and millions moved from the countryside to the towns. The costs included poor-quality goods, waste, accidents, shortages of consumer goods and the use of prisoners' forced labour. Planning suffered from false reporting, since managers feared punishment for failure and falsified results.

## Managers and workers

**Managers** were given targets and held responsible. Successful ones gained privileges such as better housing, special shops and holidays, but they risked arrest as "wreckers" if targets were missed. The Shakhty trial (1928) and the later purges of managers showed this danger.

**Workers.** Many welcomed new jobs, free education, health care and chances of promotion; women entered industry in large numbers. But living standards were hard. Real wages fell sharply in the early 1930s, food was rationed until 1935, housing was overcrowded (families lived in barracks or shared apartments), and work discipline was severe: internal passports were introduced in 1932 and workers could be punished for lateness or for leaving a job without permission. The Stakhanovite movement raised output norms and caused resentment among fellow workers, although Stakhanovites received higher pay and honour. Many historians conclude that real wages and consumption stayed below the 1928 level for most of the 1930s, although some groups gained.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing collectivisation with dekulakisation"}
Collectivisation was the reorganisation of farming into collective farms. Dekulakisation was the attack on the "kulaks", used as a way of breaking resistance to it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using Soviet production figures as exact"}
Official figures were exaggerated and the plans' success was announced early. Give them as estimates and mention the reliability problem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that the Five Year Plans benefited everyone"}
Industrial growth was real, but living standards for most workers fell, and peasants paid the highest price.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stalin ended NEP in 1928-29 because of the grain crisis, the need for rapid industrialisation for security, ideology and politics.
- Collectivisation and dekulakisation gave the state control of grain but caused resistance, a collapse of livestock and the famine of 1932-33, in which an estimated 5 to 8 million died.
- The Five Year Plans built heavy industry and new cities quickly, though figures were exaggerated and quality was poor.
- Managers had privileges and risks; workers had jobs and promotion but low real wages, harsh discipline and poor housing.
- Success depends on criteria: the state's goals were largely met, but the human costs and inefficiency were severe.
:::
