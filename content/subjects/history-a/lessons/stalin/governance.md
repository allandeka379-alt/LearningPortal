## Introduction

By 1934 Stalin had no rival left in the Politburo, the first Five Year Plans had transformed the economy and the party had celebrated its victories. Yet in the next four years the Soviet state turned on its own people and on its own party. Hundreds of thousands of people were shot, millions were sent to camps, and almost the whole leadership of the revolution was destroyed. At the same time the regime issued a democratic-looking constitution and built a vast cult around its leader, and then made a pact with Hitler. For Cambridge Paper 4 and for ZIMSEC the central question is how effectively, and at what cost, Stalin governed. This lesson explains the Terror, the cult and the Constitution, and foreign policy up to the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 1939.

You should know from the previous lessons how Stalin won power and what the Five Year Plans were.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="The Great Terror (the Purges)"}
The campaign of arrests, show trials, executions and imprisonments of 1936-38, directed by Stalin and carried out by the NKVD (the People's Commissariat of Internal Affairs, the secret police), against supposed enemies in the party, army and society.
:::

:::definition{term="Show trial"}
A public trial staged to convince the people of the guilt of the accused, in which defendants confessed to impossible crimes in return for promises or after pressure and torture.
:::

:::definition{term="Gulag"}
The Soviet system of forced-labour camps and colonies run by the secret police. Prisoners built canals and railways and worked in mines and forests.
:::

:::definition{term="Cult of personality"}
The creation of an exaggerated public image of a leader through propaganda, so that he is presented as all-wise and beloved.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1 December 1934: Sergei Kirov, the Leningrad party chief, is shot by Leonid Nikolaev. August 1936: the trial of Zinoviev and Kamenev. January 1937: second show trial. June 1937: Marshal Tukhachevsky and other generals are shot. March 1938: trial of Bukharin.
- NKVD chiefs: Yagoda (to 1936), Yezhov (1936-38; "the Yezhovshchina"), Beria (from late 1938).
- 5 December 1936: the Constitution. 1935: Comintern adopts the Popular Front. 1936-39: Spanish Civil War. 23 August 1939: Nazi-Soviet Pact.
:::

## The Terror and the Purges

**Kirov's murder.** On 1 December 1934 Kirov was killed at his office. Historians still debate whether Stalin had ordered it; there is no proof, and the evidence is disputed. What is certain is that Stalin used it. A decree of the same day sped up trials for "terrorism" and removed the right of appeal. Thousands were arrested in Leningrad and a hunt began for a conspiracy.

**The show trials.** In August 1936 Zinoviev, Kamenev and others confessed to plotting with Trotsky and were shot. In January 1937 Pyatakov and Radek were tried; in March 1938 Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda, the former head of the NKVD, were convicted. Almost all were executed. These trials, held in public before foreign journalists, were meant to show that the leaders of the revolution were traitors.

**The army.** In June 1937 Marshal Tukhachevsky and seven other senior officers were tried in secret and shot on a charge of spying for Germany. Historians estimate that tens of thousands of officers (about 30,000 to 35,000 by some accounts) were removed, including three of the five marshals and most senior commanders, although some were later reinstated.

**The Yezhovshchina.** Under Nikolai Yezhov the NKVD set quotas for arrests in each region (Order 00447 of July 1937) and carried out "national operations" against groups such as Poles. Archive records suggest that in 1937-38 more than 1.5 million people were arrested and about 700,000 were shot, with many more sent to the gulag. Figures are estimates, and historians debate them. Ordinary people, from workers to peasants, scientists to priests, were among the victims, along with party officials. In late 1938 Yezhov was himself replaced by Beria and later shot, which reduced the scale of the Terror.

The **gulag** held between one and two and a half million prisoners in the late 1930s (estimates vary), many of whom died of cold, hunger and overwork.

:::example{title="Why did Stalin launch the Terror?"}
A strong answer presents the main views and weighs them. The traditional view (the historian Robert Conquest's The Great Terror, 1968, is a classic example) sees Stalin's ambition and suspicion: he wanted to destroy all possible rivals and to make everyone obey. Other historians, such as J. Arch Getty (Origins of the Great Purges, 1985), pointed to pressures from below: local officials, fear of "wreckers", economic failures and the machinery of quotas which carried the campaign beyond his plan. A third factor was the fear of war: with Germany and Japan hostile and the Spanish Civil War, Stalin was determined to remove any "fifth column". A balanced answer says that Stalin directed the Terror, but that several causes combined, and that the evidence does not show that any single motive explains it.
:::

## Cult of personality and the 1936 Constitution

The **cult of Stalin** grew rapidly from his fiftieth birthday in 1929. He was called "Vozhd" (the leader), "Great Teacher" and "Father of the Peoples". Statues, portraits, songs, films and poems praised him, and cities and factories were named after him; Tsaritsyn had become Stalingrad in 1925. Children were taught to thank him for a happy childhood. In 1938 the *Short Course* history of the Communist Party was published and became a compulsory text; it presented Stalin as Lenin's partner and removed his victims from the story. Photographs were altered to erase fallen leaders. The cult helped to win loyalty and to make opposition unthinkable, and it was also a sign of insecurity.

The **Constitution of 1936**, issued on 5 December, was called the most democratic in the world. It promised universal suffrage by secret ballot to all citizens over eighteen, a Supreme Soviet and rights to work, rest and education, and guaranteed freedoms of speech and assembly. But it also declared the Communist Party the leading force in society, there was only one candidate for each seat, and it was published at the height of the Terror. Its value was largely propaganda, to show the Soviet Union as a model abroad, and to present the end of class conflict after the collectivisation of agriculture.

## Foreign policy

From the early 1930s Soviet policy turned to **collective security**. The USSR joined the League of Nations in 1934, signed pacts with France and Czechoslovakia in 1935, and the Comintern urged communists to join "Popular Fronts" with other anti-fascists. In the **Spanish Civil War** (1936-39) the Soviet Union sent arms and advisers to the Republic, while Germany and Italy helped Franco. In **Asia** it signed a non-aggression pact with China in 1937 and supplied the Nationalist government against Japan, while border clashes with Japan culminated in 1939.

The policy was weakened by distrust. Britain and France did not trust Stalin, who was removing his own officers, and the Munich Agreement of September 1938 excluded the USSR from decisions about Czechoslovakia. In the summer of 1939 Soviet talks with Britain and France made little progress. On **23 August 1939** the USSR signed the **Nazi-Soviet Pact** with Germany, with a secret protocol that divided Poland and the Baltic states into spheres of interest. Historians offer several reasons: Stalin wanted time to rebuild the army, he distrusted the West and suspected that it wished to turn Hitler eastwards, and the pact offered territory. Its results were that Germany invaded Poland on 1 September, the USSR took eastern Poland on 17 September, then attacked Finland and annexed the Baltic states in 1940. But the pact also encouraged Hitler to go to war, confused communists abroad, and left the USSR exposed to the attack of 1941.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Stalin was proved to have ordered Kirov's murder"}
It is a question of dispute. What is certain is that he exploited it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the Terror as a purge of the party only"}
The Terror hit the army, the NKVD itself, scientists, minorities and ordinary people, not only party leaders.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing that the Constitution gave real freedom"}
It was democratic on paper, but one party controlled everything, and it appeared at the peak of the Terror.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After Kirov's murder (1 December 1934) Stalin launched the Terror: show trials (1936-38), the destruction of the army's leadership, and the Yezhovshchina.
- Archive records suggest that over 1.5 million were arrested and about 700,000 shot in 1937-38 (estimates); the gulag held millions.
- Historians debate the causes: Stalin's ambition and suspicion, pressures from below and fear of war.
- The cult of personality and the democratic-looking 1936 Constitution strengthened his authority and presented the USSR as a model.
- Foreign policy moved from collective security to the Nazi-Soviet Pact (23 August 1939), which bought time and territory but left the USSR exposed in 1941.
:::
