## Introduction

The Holocaust could not have been carried out by Hitler and a few leaders alone. It needed thousands of killers, tens of thousands of officials, soldiers, policemen, railway workers and clerks, and the cooperation or silence of many more across Europe. Historians therefore ask three connected questions. Who were the perpetrators and why did they take part? How did the victims respond, and what counts as resistance? And what did bystanders, including the governments of the Allied and neutral states, know and do? The Cambridge Paper 3 syllabus asks you to explain interpretations of these questions. This lesson presents the main views without choosing one for you,.

You should know the narrative of the Holocaust and the debate about its origins from the previous two lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Perpetrator"}
A person who took part in carrying out the persecution and murder, whether by killing, by giving orders, or by organising the deportations and the confiscation of property. Historians distinguish "desk" perpetrators from those who killed face to face.
:::

:::definition{term="Bystander"}
A person or government that knew, or could have known, what was happening and neither took part nor tried to stop it. The term is debated, because some bystanders benefited from the persecution while others helped victims.
:::

:::definition{term="Resistance"}
Opposition to the persecution. Historians disagree whether the term should cover only armed fighting or also actions such as hiding, keeping records, running schools and refusing to give up one's humanity.
:::

## Who carried out the Holocaust, and why?

**The scale of participation.** The SS, the police, the army, civil servants, doctors, lawyers, railway officials and businessmen all played a part. The Einsatzgruppen and the police battalions did the shooting in the east; the Wehrmacht (the regular army) supplied logistics, guarded prisoners and in many places helped in the killing of Jews and others, a role that Germans long denied in the myth of a "clean" army and that historians have documented since the 1990s. Railway officials ran the trains, and companies used forced labour. Adolf Eichmann organised deportations from his office.

**Why did ordinary people take part?** Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) studied Reserve Police Battalion 101, whose middle-aged members from Hamburg shot thousands of Jews in Poland in 1942. He argued that most were not fanatical Nazis; conformity to the group, obedience, careerism and the habit of racist propaganda explained why most went on killing, although a minority refused or excused themselves without serious punishment. Daniel Goldhagen, in a rival study that also used the evidence of the police battalions, argued that the men acted from an antisemitism common in German society. Critics said this ignored the evidence of varied motives. Other writers apply the psychology of obedience, such as the experiments of Stanley Milgram, but historians warn that obedience alone does not explain enthusiasm or initiative. Hannah Arendt, reporting on Eichmann's trial in 1961, wrote of the "banality of evil", the idea that terrible crimes can be carried out by unremarkable bureaucrats; later research suggests that Eichmann was a more committed antisemite than she thought.

**Collaboration beyond Germany.** The Holocaust was European. Local police and auxiliaries in the Baltic states, Ukraine and Poland took part in killings and in rounding up Jews. In France, the Vichy government and French police assisted in arrests, including the roundup in Paris on 16-17 July 1942. Romania carried out its own massacres. Croatia's Ustaše regime, Hungary's gendarmes in 1944 and Slovakia's government all assisted. Many people saw opportunities in taking over Jewish homes and businesses. At the same time, in some places, such as Denmark and parts of Italian-occupied Europe, governments or peoples refused to cooperate fully. The degree of local participation depended on the occupation regime, existing antisemitism and the pressure of German power.

## How should Jewish resistance be defined?

For many years, a view was widespread that Jews had gone to their deaths passively. Some early historians, such as Raul Hilberg in *The Destruction of the European Jews* (1961), emphasised the limited armed resistance and the role of the Judenräte. Later historians have challenged the idea of passivity and asked why the question was posed in this way. They note that victims were starved, deceived, unarmed, surrounded by hostile or indifferent populations, and that reprisals fell on the entire community.

A broader definition of resistance includes not only armed uprisings (Warsaw, Treblinka, Sobibor, Auschwitz and the partisan groups) but also hiding, escape, forging papers, smuggling food, keeping schools and diaries (Ringelblum's archive), and religious observance. The Israeli historian Yehuda Bauer used the Hebrew word *amidah*, "standing up", for a broad range of such acts. Critics of the broad definition warn that stretching the word "resistance" can blur the difference between fighting back and the struggle to survive. Historians generally agree that a fair judgement considers the conditions in which choices were made.

## Should other victims be included?

Some historians restrict the word "Holocaust" to the genocide of the Jews, which they see as unique because the Nazi aim was to murder every Jewish man, woman and child in Europe regardless of location, age or behaviour. Others use a broader term to cover the murder of Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war and others, and point to the shared racial thinking and sometimes the same personnel and methods. Roma and Sinti survivors and their representatives have long argued that their suffering has been neglected. Many historians take a middle position: the persecution of the Jews had a central place and a unique goal, but other groups were also victims of Nazi racial policy and should be remembered.

## Responses of the Allies and neutral states

**The United States.** Strict immigration quotas, public opinion and the attitude of some State Department officials limited the number of refugees. In 1939 the ship *St Louis*, carrying over 900 Jewish refugees, was turned away from Cuba and the United States; most of its passengers returned to Europe. The historian David Wyman, in *The Abandonment of the Jews* (1984), argued that the American government could have done much more to help; others, such as William Rubinstein, argued that the chances of rescue were limited and that the only real answer was military victory. The War Refugee Board, set up in January 1944, helped to save tens of thousands.

**Britain.** Britain admitted some refugees, including the Kindertransport children, but limited entry to Palestine by the White Paper of 1939 and shared the Allied priority of winning the war. In 1944 the Allies were asked to bomb the rail lines or the gas chambers at Auschwitz and did not. Historians dispute whether bombing was practicable and would have saved many lives.

**Neutral states and others.** Switzerland closed its border to many refugees in 1942, though it later admitted more. Sweden took in Danish Jews in 1943 and its diplomat Raoul Wallenberg helped many Jews in Budapest in 1944. The Vatican did not speak out publicly; historians still argue about the reasons. Individuals such as the Japanese diplomat Chiune Sugihara, who issued visas in Lithuania in 1940, and thousands of people who hid Jews, are honoured as "Righteous Among the Nations".

:::example{title="Judging the responsibility of ordinary people"}
A strong answer separates degrees of involvement: leaders who gave orders, those who organised, those who carried out killings, those who profited, and those who looked away. It compares explanations (conformity and peer pressure, ideology, careerism, fear) and tests them against the evidence, noting that Browning's men could in fact refuse and that most did not. It also avoids two errors: blaming all Germans equally, and ignoring the people who made a different choice.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing two explanations"}
To judge why ordinary men killed, set Browning's emphasis on conformity and obedience beside Goldhagen's emphasis on antisemitism. Ask which explains the minority who refused, which explains variety of behaviour, and which fits comparison with other countries. A judgement that uses these tests, and notes that both may be partly true, is stronger than a statement of preference.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating perpetrators as a single type"}
They included committed ideologues, careerists and conformists, and men who killed face to face and officials who signed papers. Explain which type and which evidence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming only Germans"}
Collaboration happened across Europe, though under German occupation and pressure. Mention it with examples, but do not suggest that responsibility was equal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Jewish victims as passive"}
Show the range of resistance and the conditions that limited it. Use precise examples and dates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 2"}
In extract questions, identify whether the historian stresses ideology, conformity or the state in explaining the perpetrators, or takes a narrow or broad view of resistance, and test the extract against examples (Battalion 101, Vichy, Warsaw, Evian, the War Refugee Board). In essays, give both sides, name historians only with their arguments, and reach a supported judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Thousands of people from the SS, police, army, civil service and business took part in the Holocaust, as did collaborators in many occupied countries.
- Browning stressed conformity and obedience in ordinary men; Goldhagen stressed antisemitism; critics find both incomplete.
- Historians disagree on how to define Jewish resistance, from armed revolt to a broader sense of standing up (amidah).
- Some historians limit the Holocaust to the murder of the Jews; others include Roma, disabled people and other victims.
- The US and Britain gave priority to winning the war and restricted refugee entry; historians disagree on how much more could have been done.
:::
