## Introduction

Historians agree on what happened in the Holocaust; they disagree about how and why it happened, and when the decision to murder the Jews of Europe was taken. This is one of the most important debates in modern history because it is about responsibility: was the Holocaust the carrying out of one man's aim, the result of ideas deep in European and German culture, or the product of a chaotic state that escalated step by step? The Cambridge Paper 3 question asks you to assess "who or what was to blame", and to judge extracts that argue different cases. This lesson explains the main interpretations in neutral terms. It paraphrases historians' positions and names works only where they are well known.

You should know the narrative of persecution and genocide from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Intentionalism"}
The interpretation that the Holocaust was the result of Hitler's long-held intention to destroy the Jews, so that his ideas and decisions were the main cause.
:::

:::definition{term="Structuralism (functionalism)"}
The interpretation that stresses the structure of the Nazi state, with its rival agencies and improvisation, and the pressures of war, so that the genocide came about through a "cumulative radicalisation" rather than a single plan. Historians use the labels "structuralist" and "functionalist" for slightly different emphases; for examination purposes they are often treated together.
:::

:::definition{term="Cumulative radicalisation"}
The idea, associated with Hans Mommsen, that Nazi policy grew more extreme step by step because officials competed to please the regime and solve problems by harsher means.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 2"}
This lesson supports the Paper 3 debate on the origins of the Holocaust (A Level only). Questions ask how far the Holocaust was the consequence of racist ideas that pre-dated the Nazis, and the extract question tests your knowledge of the historians' approaches. It is not a ZIMSEC topic.
:::

## Did the Holocaust grow from earlier ideas?

**The case for continuity.** Antisemitism was old in Europe, and racial antisemitism, eugenics and social Darwinism were widespread in the late nineteenth century. Some historians argue that German culture had been shaped by a particularly strong hostility to Jews, so that Nazi policy found a ready audience. The most controversial form of this argument was made in 1996 by Daniel Goldhagen in *Hitler's Willing Executioners*, which claimed that ordinary Germans were driven by an "eliminationist" antisemitism that was widespread in German society. Saul Friedländer, in *Nazi Germany and the Jews*, wrote about a "redemptive antisemitism" in the Nazi leadership that saw the removal of the Jews as the saving of the race. Others show a link between earlier ideas of racial hygiene, colonial violence and later racial policy.

**The case against a straight line.** Most historians reject the idea of an unbroken path. Antisemitism was at least as strong in some other countries, such as Poland, Russia and Romania, and in France during the Dreyfus Affair; in Germany, Jews were well integrated before 1933, and antisemitic parties performed poorly before 1914. The Nazis had toned down their antisemitism in election campaigns in 1930-32, which suggests that it was not the chief reason for their votes. Goldhagen's thesis was criticised for treating all Germans as the same, for ignoring evidence of variety and for explaining too much. A typical judgement is that old prejudice was a necessary condition, creating a climate in which persecution was accepted, but not a sufficient one: it did not make the Holocaust inevitable, and needed the Nazi regime and the war.

## The intentionalist case

Intentionalists place Hitler at the centre. They point to his open hatred of the Jews from his earliest political writing, such as a letter of September 1919, to *Mein Kampf* and to a speech in the Reichstag on 30 January 1939 in which he threatened that a new war would result in the destruction of the Jewish race in Europe. Historians such as Lucy Dawidowicz (*The War Against the Jews*, 1975) argued that Hitler had decided on genocide long before it began and that the Nazi state carried out his goal; Eberhard Jäckel made a similar argument about Hitler's world view. In this view the persecution of the 1930s was a first stage, and the war gave the opportunity to complete the plan.

**Strengths.** The case explains why antisemitism was so central to the regime and why Jews were singled out; it takes seriously what the leader wrote and said. **Weaknesses.** No written order from Hitler to kill the Jews survives, and there is little sign of a detailed plan before 1941. The regime considered other solutions, such as the Madagascar plan of 1940, a scheme to deport Jews to the island that was never realistic and was dropped. Policy changed direction several times between 1939 and 1941, which fits poorly with a fixed programme.

## The structuralist and functionalist case

In the 1970s and 1980s historians including Martin Broszat and Hans Mommsen argued that the Nazi regime was not a tightly directed system but a "polycracy" of rival agencies: party offices, the SS, ministries and local governors competed, and officials tried to anticipate what Hitler wanted. Policy radicalised because each agency "worked towards" the leader's goals. The war created problems, such as how to feed, house and control millions of Jews in occupied Poland, and local officials looked for violent ways of solving them. Ghettos, shootings and, in the end, gassing were adopted as the earlier solutions failed. On this view Hitler's role was less direct, and the genocide was a product of the system.

**Strengths.** The case fits the evidence of improvisation, of local initiative (for example the early gassing at Chełmno, and the shootings carried out by regional commanders) and of the pressure of the war. **Weaknesses.** It can underplay the ideological aim that guided all these officials, the evidence that Hitler approved key steps, and the fact that Jews were targeted in all the occupied countries. It also risks making the Holocaust seem an accident rather than a deliberate crime. Mommsen's idea of a "weak dictator" is contested by those who argue that Hitler kept the final say.

## Synthesis interpretations

Since the 1990s most historians have combined the two views. Christopher Browning, in works such as *The Path to Genocide* (1992) and *The Origins of the Final Solution* (2004), argued that the decision was reached in stages in 1941 as German victories in the Soviet Union seemed to make a "final" solution possible, and that Hitler gave the necessary approval, but that the process was shaped by officials' proposals and the changing course of the war. Ian Kershaw's phrase "working towards the Führer" describes how officials, aware of Hitler's goals, took initiatives to please him, which meant that the leader's will and the actions of the system fed one another. Peter Longerich and others have also traced a gradual extension of killing across 1941-42 with Hitler's repeated authorisation. Historians disagree on the date of the decisive step: some choose the summer of 1941 (the order of Göring to Heydrich of 31 July 1941 to prepare a "total solution" is a key document), others the autumn of 1941 or Hitler's talk to party leaders on 12 December 1941, and others the spring of 1942 after Wannsee.

:::example{title="Testing an interpretation against the evidence"}
Suppose an extract states that "the Holocaust resulted from a plan that Hitler formed in the 1920s and carried out when the war gave him the chance". To test it, set out what supports it (Mein Kampf, the speech of 1939, the centrality of antisemitism, Hitler's approval of key stages) and what challenges it (no written order, the Madagascar plan, changes of policy in 1939-41, local initiative, the role of war). Then judge: the extract captures the importance of Hitler's aims but is too simple about the process, since the scale and form of the killing developed in the circumstances of 1941.
:::

:::example{title="Describing an interpretation precisely"}
Compare two sentences. "The historian talks about Hitler" says little. "The historian argues that the Holocaust grew from Hitler's long-held aim, and gives little weight to the structure of the state" states the argument and the approach. In the Paper 3 extract question, aim for the second kind of sentence, then support it with a quotation-free reference to the extract and test it with knowledge.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the debate as a choice between Hitler and the system"}
Most historians now combine the two, and a strong answer explains how ideology, Hitler's role, institutions and war interacted.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using historians' names without their arguments"}
Naming Goldhagen or Browning earns little unless you state what each argued and how far the evidence supports it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that structuralists deny the Holocaust or excuse Hitler"}
They do not. Structuralists accept that the genocide happened and that the regime was responsible; their question is about how decisions were made.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 3, Topic 2"}
In the extract question, identify the extract's main argument (its interpretation) and approach (what the historian emphasises: ideas, leadership, institutions, the war) and test it with accurate knowledge. In the essay, "How far was the Holocaust the result of ideas that pre-dated the Nazis?" requires arguments for continuity (earlier antisemitism, race science) and against (the role of the Nazi regime and the war), with a clear judgement. Never quote historians; paraphrase.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Antisemitism and race theory pre-dated the Nazis, but most historians see them as a precondition and not a sufficient cause.
- Intentionalists such as Dawidowicz and Jäckel stress Hitler's aims and role, but no written order survives and policy changed course.
- Structuralists and functionalists such as Broszat and Mommsen stress rival agencies, improvisation and war.
- Synthesis interpretations (Browning, Kershaw) combine Hitler's goals with cumulative radicalisation in 1941.
- Historians disagree on when the decisive decision was taken, from the summer of 1941 to the spring of 1942.
:::
