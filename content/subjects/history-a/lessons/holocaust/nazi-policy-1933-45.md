## Introduction

The Holocaust was the systematic, state-organised persecution and murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children by Nazi Germany and its allies and collaborators between 1933 and 1945. It was not an accident of war: it was a policy, carried out by many thousands of people across Europe. Historians study it so that victims are remembered as individuals and societies understand how prejudice, state power and indifference can combine in genocide. This lesson sets out what happened and in what order, to inform and not to shock; the debates about causes and responsibility belong to the two lessons that follow.

You should know from the lesson on Nazi society how Jews were persecuted before 1939.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antisemitism"}
Hostility to or prejudice against Jews. Religious hostility goes back many centuries; the racial form, which treated Jews as a biological "race", developed in the nineteenth century.
:::

:::definition{term="Genocide"}
The deliberate destruction of a national, ethnic, racial or religious group. The word was created by the jurist Raphael Lemkin in 1944.
:::

:::definition{term="The Final Solution"}
The Nazi euphemism for the plan to murder all the Jews of Europe, developed in 1941-42. Historians quote the term without accepting its language.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1933: boycott and first laws. 1935: Nuremberg Laws. 9-10 November 1938: Kristallnacht.
- 1939-40: ghettos in occupied Poland. June 1941: the Einsatzgruppen follow the invasion of the USSR. 20 January 1942: Wannsee Conference.
- 1942: Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka begin killing; Auschwitz-Birkenau becomes the largest centre. 19 April 1943: the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising.
- Scholarly estimate: about six million Jewish victims.
:::

## Background: antisemitism and race theory

Jews had lived in Europe for centuries, and for much of that time they were targets of religious prejudice and periodic violence, including expulsions and massacres. In the nineteenth century, new ideas of race, pseudo-scientific writing about "Aryan" and "Semitic" peoples, and social Darwinism gave old hatred a biological language. Pogroms followed 1881 in Russia, the Dreyfus Affair of the 1890s divided France, and a forgery, *The Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, spread the false idea of a Jewish world conspiracy. In Germany, Jews were about 0.75 per cent of the population in 1933 and were well integrated, though after the defeat of 1918 right-wing groups spread the myth that Jews and socialists had "stabbed Germany in the back". Hitler made this hatred central to his world view.

## Persecution, 1933-41

The steps up to 1939 are summarised in the lesson on Nazi society: the boycott (April 1933), the exclusion from public service, the Nuremberg Laws (1935), "Aryanisation" of Jewish property, and Kristallnacht (November 1938). The aim before the war was to drive Jews out of Germany. About half of Germany's Jews emigrated by 1939, but they were allowed to take little with them, and other countries made it hard to enter. At the Evian Conference in July 1938, delegates from thirty-two states expressed sympathy but few agreed to take more refugees. Britain took about 10,000 unaccompanied Jewish children through the Kindertransport but restricted entry to Palestine in 1939.

## War and the radicalisation of policy

War changed everything. When Germany conquered Poland in 1939, millions more Jews came under its control. **Ghettos** were created in occupied Poland, at Łódź (sealed in 1940) and Warsaw (sealed in November 1940, with about 400,000 people crowded into a few streets). Starvation and disease killed many thousands. Jewish Councils (*Judenräte*) were forced to run the ghettos, a terrible dilemma for their members.

In June 1941, with the invasion of the Soviet Union, mobile killing squads called **Einsatzgruppen**, helped by police battalions, the army and local collaborators, shot Jewish men, women and children in occupied territory. At Babi Yar near Kyiv on 29-30 September 1941, more than 30,000 Jews were murdered in two days. Historians estimate that well over a million Jews were killed in these shootings.

In the autumn of 1941 the Nazis began to develop other killing methods. Gassing began at Chełmno in December 1941. On **20 January 1942** Reinhard Heydrich gathered senior officials at a villa at Wannsee, in Berlin, to coordinate the deportation of Europe's Jews to the east. The minutes list about eleven million Jews in Europe, and make clear that the plan was now for the whole continent. Historians argue about when the decision to murder all the Jews was taken (see the next lesson), but agree that by early 1942 it was being carried out.

The **extermination camps** were Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka (the Operation Reinhard camps, 1942), Chełmno, and Auschwitz-Birkenau, which combined a forced-labour camp with a killing centre and was the largest, as well as Majdanek. Trains carried Jews from across Europe. Most people arriving at the extermination camps were murdered within hours. Over 400,000 Hungarian Jews were deported mainly to Auschwitz in the spring and summer of 1944. Some were chosen for forced labour and died of exhaustion, hunger or disease. In 1944-45 the SS forced prisoners on "death marches", and the camps were liberated by Soviet, British and American forces.

## Jewish responses

Jews responded in many ways, in conditions in which survival depended on chance. Many tried to emigrate before 1939. In the ghettos, people ran schools, hospitals, and secret archives such as the Warsaw ghetto archive of the historian Emanuel Ringelblum; this is called spiritual or cultural resistance. Others hid, with help from non-Jewish friends or by false papers, or escaped to the forests, where some joined partisan groups. There was armed resistance too: the **Warsaw Ghetto Uprising**, begun on 19 April 1943 by young Jewish fighters with few weapons, held out for several weeks against German forces; there were revolts at Treblinka (August 1943) and Sobibor (October 1943) and by prisoners at Auschwitz in October 1944. Resistance was extremely hard: victims were starved, deceived, outnumbered and isolated, and reprisals fell on others.

## Other victims

The Nazis also murdered or caused the death of other groups, though the aim of killing every Jew was unique to the persecution of the Jews. About 70,000 disabled people were murdered in the T4 programme before it was officially halted in August 1941, and many more in later killings. Historians estimate that between 200,000 and 500,000 Roma and Sinti were killed. About three million Soviet prisoners of war died in German captivity, as did millions of Polish and Soviet civilians. Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexual men and political prisoners were also persecuted. Whether they belong within the term "Holocaust" is discussed in a later lesson.

## Reactions

Information reached the Allies in 1942, and on 17 December 1942 they issued a declaration condemning the extermination. They concentrated on winning the war, and critics argue they could have done more, for example by bombing the rail lines to Auschwitz or admitting more refugees. The Bermuda Conference (April 1943) achieved little; the American War Refugee Board (January 1944) helped to save some people. Many local people in occupied Europe helped the Germans, many did nothing, and a minority sheltered Jews; in Denmark about 7,000 Jews were taken to safety in Sweden in October 1943.

:::example{title="Explaining the change from persecution to genocide"}
Before 1939 the policy was to push Jews out of German life and force emigration. Conquest of Poland and the USSR placed millions more Jews under German control and made "emigration" impossible; the racial war in the east, local initiative and the example of the Einsatzgruppen radicalised policy in 1941; by Wannsee it covered the whole continent. Show the links without implying that the outcome was inevitable.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a euphemistic source"}
The Wannsee minutes speak of the "evacuation" of Jews to the east and of a "Final Solution". A student must read beyond the words. The language was chosen to hide the purpose, so historians interpret it with other evidence: the camps already operating, the shootings in the USSR and the deportation trains. The document is valuable because it shows coordination between departments, but it needs to be read with care and in context.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing persecution with genocide"}
Before the war the policy was exclusion and forced emigration. The mass murder began in 1941. Keep the stages and dates distinct.
:::

:::mistake{title="Attributing the whole Holocaust to the camps"}
Well over a million Jews were shot in the east by the Einsatzgruppen and their helpers, and many died in the ghettos, before and alongside the extermination camps.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing victims only as passive"}
There were many forms of resistance and survival. Avoid language that suggests that Jews went like sheep to their deaths; explain the conditions that limited resistance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Antisemitism was old in Europe; racial theories of the nineteenth century gave it a pseudo-scientific form that Nazism adopted.
- From 1933 to 1939 the Nazis excluded Jews from German life and forced about half to emigrate; Kristallnacht (1938) marked a violent escalation.
- War in 1939-41 created ghettos and, from June 1941, mass shootings by the Einsatzgruppen.
- Wannsee (20 January 1942) coordinated the deportation and murder of Europe's Jews in extermination camps such as Treblinka and Auschwitz-Birkenau; about six million Jews died (an estimate).
- Jews resisted in many ways; the Allies and neutral states did little; other groups were also persecuted and killed.
:::
