## Introduction

When archaeologists dug inside the Great Enclosure at Great Zimbabwe, they found blue-green Chinese porcelain, glazed pottery from Persia, glass beads made in India and a copper coin minted at Kilwa on the coast of present-day Tanzania. A thousand kilometres from the sea, these objects tell a story that long predates the Portuguese: the people of the Zimbabwe plateau were part of the great trading world of the Indian Ocean.

This lesson explains how that trade worked, who took part, what was exchanged, what evidence survives, and how the trade affected the societies of the plateau. The Portuguese, who arrived at the end of the fifteenth century and tried to take over the trade, are treated in the next lesson on Portuguese contacts. You should know the lesson on Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe, which gives the context.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Swahili coast"}
The coast of East Africa from Somalia to Mozambique, where African and Muslim traders created a string of port cities such as Kilwa, Mombasa and Sofala. "Swahili" refers to the language and culture of its people, who were Africans with long contact with Arab and Persian traders.
:::

:::definition{term="Monsoon"}
The seasonal winds of the Indian Ocean that blow from the north-east in the northern winter and from the south-west in summer. Sailors used them to cross the ocean and return.
:::

:::definition{term="Middleman"}
A trader who buys from producers and sells to other merchants. Coastal Swahili merchants and African traders in the interior acted as middlemen between the plateau and distant buyers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and facts to learn"}
- From about the eighth to tenth centuries: glass beads and other Indian Ocean goods reach southern Africa, for example at Chibuene on the Mozambique coast.
- 10th century: the Arab writer al-Masudi mentions Sofala and its gold.
- 1331: the traveller Ibn Battuta visits Kilwa.
- About 1220-1290 Mapungubwe; about 13th-15th centuries Great Zimbabwe: the main period of the plateau's trade with the coast.
- 1498: Vasco da Gama reaches the East African coast; 1505: the Portuguese take Sofala and Kilwa.
:::

## The Indian Ocean trade network

The Indian Ocean was joined by the monsoon winds. Dhows with triangular sails left Arabia, Persia and India in the north-east monsoon and sailed down the coast of East Africa, returning with the south-west wind. This allowed regular trade between East Africa, the Persian Gulf, India, South-East Asia and China. Muslim merchants from Arabia and Persia, and traders from Gujarat in India, were the main carriers; Chinese ships also reached East Africa in the early fifteenth century under Zheng He, though Chinese goods generally reached Africa through other traders.

On the African coast, port cities grew into wealthy **city-states**: **Kilwa** (which became rich in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and issued its own copper coins), **Mombasa**, **Mogadishu** and, farther south, **Sofala**, the main outlet for the gold of the Zimbabwe plateau. Many people in these towns were Muslim, built stone mosques and houses, and spoke Swahili. Tradition in Kilwa claims an origin in Shiraz in Persia, but historians stress that these were African towns whose culture mixed African and foreign elements.

## Goods exchanged

| From the plateau and southern Africa | From the Indian Ocean world |
|---|---|
| Gold, from alluvial and reef mining | Cotton cloth from India |
| Ivory from elephant hunting | Glass beads made in India and the Gulf |
| Copper | Chinese celadon porcelain |
| Animal skins | Persian and Islamic glazed pottery |
| Iron goods | Other luxury goods: brass, glass vessels, Kilwa coins |

The plateau's rulers desired imported goods as **prestige items**: cloth to wear, beads to ornament the elite, porcelain to show wealth. Gold was the item that the outside world wanted most. Some historians estimate that a large part of the gold that reached the Indian Ocean world came from the plateau.

## How the trade worked

Few foreign merchants went to the interior in the early period. African traders on the plateau and the Zambezi valley carried gold and ivory down to the coast, where Swahili merchants sold them on to Arab, Persian and Indian traders. Rulers of states such as Great Zimbabwe and, later, the Mutapa **controlled** the flow of goods: they taxed traders, received gifts and kept the best imports for themselves, which strengthened their authority. The trade was largely **peaceful and commercial**, between independent partners, though the balance of advantage favoured those who controlled the ports.

## Archaeological evidence

- **Mapungubwe and K2:** glass beads from India and the Gulf region found with burials and in the settlement; gold objects.
- **Great Zimbabwe:** Chinese celadon, Persian pottery, glass beads and copper coins from Kilwa found in the Great Enclosure and the Hill Complex, indicating contact with the coast in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries.
- **Khami and other sites:** imported ceramics and beads.
- **Coastal sites:** the ruins at Kilwa and Sofala, with large warehouses and coin finds, and written records by Arab writers and travellers such as al-Masudi and Ibn Battuta.
- **Gold-mining sites on the plateau**, with shafts and workings, support the evidence of large-scale production.

:::example{title="A source analysed: what do imported goods prove?"}
1. **The find:** a Chinese bowl in the Great Enclosure.
2. **What it proves:** the bowl reached Great Zimbabwe, probably through traders on the coast; therefore the plateau was linked to the Indian Ocean trade.
3. **What it does not prove:** that Chinese merchants came to Great Zimbabwe, or that the city was built by outsiders. The bowl shows exchange, not settlement.
4. **Cross-check:** the bowl is consistent with written reports of gold passing through Sofala and with the dating of the walls.
:::

## Impact on the societies of the plateau

**Positive effects.**
- **Wealth and power for rulers.** Control of the gold trade enriched the rulers of Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe and allowed them to reward followers and display their status.
- **State formation.** Trade strengthened centralised states and the division between the elite and the commoners, expressed in stone building and imported luxury goods.
- **Technical and cultural exchange.** Gold and copper working expanded, new goods were adopted, and some Muslim traders were welcomed at courts.

**Negative or mixed effects.**
- **Dependence on trade.** A state that relied on a single trade was vulnerable when routes shifted north to the Zambezi, a factor sometimes suggested for the decline of Great Zimbabwe.
- **Inequality.** The benefits went mostly to the elite; ordinary farmers and miners saw less gain.
- **Limits.** Islam and Swahili culture spread little into the interior, and the imports did not change the farming and cattle basis of life.

:::example{title="Weighing the importance of trade"}
Historians debate how much of Great Zimbabwe's greatness came from trade. One view says that the gold trade created the state; another says that the state rested on cattle and farming and that trade only added prestige goods to an existing system. A balanced answer notes that trade strengthened rulers but that the foundations were local.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that foreigners built Great Zimbabwe"}
Imported goods prove trade, not foreign builders. The stone city was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Indian Ocean trade with the Portuguese trade"}
The Indian Ocean trade began many centuries before the Portuguese arrived in 1498. The Portuguese tried to take over an existing network.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting Africa's role"}
Africans mined the gold and hunted the ivory, and African and Swahili traders carried and sold it. The plateau was an equal partner, not a passive recipient.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Indian Ocean trade, using the monsoon winds, linked East Africa with Arabia, Persia, India and China.
- Swahili city-states such as Kilwa and Sofala handled the plateau's gold and ivory, exchanged for cloth, beads and porcelain.
- Archaeology at Mapungubwe, Great Zimbabwe and Khami (beads, porcelain, Kilwa coins) provides evidence, supported by Arab written sources.
- The trade enriched rulers and helped state formation but made states dependent on trade routes and benefited mainly the elite.
- The plateau's societies were active partners, not passive recipients.
:::
