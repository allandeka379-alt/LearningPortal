## Introduction

By the 1880s the lands between the Limpopo and the Zambezi, ruled in the south-west by the Ndebele king Lobengula and in the north and east by Shona chiefdoms, had become the object of a rivalry among states that had their eyes on southern Africa. Britain held the Cape and Natal. Two Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, had been founded by trekkers who had left the Cape. Germany, a newly united state, had entered the scramble for colonies in 1884. Each of them had reasons to wish to extend its influence north, and each saw that if it did not, a rival would.

This lesson explains those interests, the diplomacy of the years 1885 to 1888, and the two treaties that were its outcome. It stops at the Rudd Concession, which is the subject of the next lesson. You will need the lesson on the activities of missionaries, hunters and traders, since many of the agents in this story came from those groups.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sphere of influence"}
An area in which one European state claimed the right to be the only one to make treaties or take territory, without actually ruling it. Such claims were made between European powers and did not depend on African consent.
:::

:::definition{term="Protectorate"}
A territory under the protection of a stronger state, which controls its foreign relations and often its defence. Britain declared a protectorate over Bechuanaland in 1885.
:::

:::definition{term="High Commissioner"}
The British official at the Cape who represented the Crown in dealings with African rulers and the Boer republics in southern Africa. In the 1880s the post was held by Sir Hercules Robinson.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1884: Germany establishes its first colony in south-west Africa (the Lüderitz claim at Angra Pequena).
- 1885: Britain declares a protectorate over Bechuanaland.
- 1886: gold is found on the Witwatersrand in the Transvaal.
- 1887: the Grobler Treaty between the Transvaal and Lobengula.
- 1888 (February): the Moffat Treaty between Britain and Lobengula.
:::

## Why each state was interested

**Britain.** British interests were of several kinds. The first was the strategy of the route to the north: the Cape Colony's leaders feared that German South West Africa and the Transvaal might join up and cut the British off from the interior. Bechuanaland, declared a protectorate in 1885, was seen as the road to the north. Secondly, there were minerals: rumours of gold in Matabeleland and Mashonaland grew after the discovery of gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Thirdly there was the wish of men such as Cecil Rhodes, who was prime minister of the Cape from 1890 and a leading diamond businessman, to extend the British Empire and make profit in the north. Finally, Britain wished to keep the Boer republics from extending their control over African rulers.

**Germany.** Germany had taken Angra Pequena in 1884. Its leaders and traders had a wider vision of a German presence in southern Africa, and the idea of joining German South West Africa with the Transvaal and the east coast alarmed British policy-makers. Germany never made a treaty with Lobengula, but the fear of one was a powerful influence on British decisions.

**The Transvaal (South African Republic).** The Transvaal wanted land for its farmers and mineral wealth. Its leaders also wished to prevent being encircled by British territory. After 1886 the gold of the Witwatersrand made it richer and stronger.

**The Orange Free State.** The Free State was less active than the Transvaal in the north, and its government did not seek a treaty with Lobengula. But Free State farmers and hunters were part of the wider Boer expansion, and the Boer republics shared the aim of keeping Britain from controlling the interior.

:::example{title="Explaining a cause: why was Britain alarmed by the Grobler Treaty?"}
1. **The treaty:** in 1887 the Transvaal made friendship terms with Lobengula.
2. **The fear:** a Transvaal link to the Ndebele king would put the Boers astride the road north.
3. **The connection with Germany:** a Boer–German link across the interior would cut the Cape off from the north.
4. **The reaction:** the High Commissioner acted to get Lobengula's agreement to a British arrangement.
Good answers show that the same fear of rivals explained the interest of each state.
:::

## Agents and diplomacy at Lobengula's court

Lobengula kept his power by balancing. He received traders, hunters, missionaries and agents of several governments, gave presents and rights where he judged it useful, and tried not to depend on any one visitor. His chief weapon was delay. He also tried to learn what was happening to the south through his advisers and his informants.

Several agents tried to influence him. **Pieter Grobler**, an agent of the Transvaal, came to Bulawayo in 1887. British officials used men they trusted: Sir Sidney Shippard, the administrator of Bechuanaland, and **John Smith Moffat**, a missionary and the son of Robert Moffat, were important. Rhodes and his associates worked through these officials and through Charles Rudd's party in 1888.

## The Grobler Treaty, 1887

In 1887 Grobler obtained from Lobengula a treaty of friendship and peace with the Transvaal. According to the usual account, its terms allowed the Transvaal to station an agent at Bulawayo and bound the two sides to friendship and to help each other. Lobengula's understanding of the treaty was later disputed: he told the British that he had agreed only to friendship and had not signed away control of his land. Grobler himself was killed on his journey back in 1887. Britain protested that the treaty was invalid and acted at once to prevent a Transvaal presence.

## The Moffat Treaty, 1888

In February 1888, at Bulawayo, John Smith Moffat obtained Lobengula's agreement to a treaty of friendship with Britain. Lobengula promised not to make a treaty with, or give land to, any other power without the knowledge and approval of the British High Commissioner. The treaty gave Britain a say in the king's foreign relations. In practice it shut out the Transvaal and Germany and made Matabeleland a British sphere. Lobengula believed that he had secured friendship and kept his independence; to the British, it meant that he had accepted a British claim.

## Impact on Ndebele independence

- **Foreign relations:** after February 1888 Lobengula could make no treaty with another power without British approval.
- **Dependence on advice:** he relied on missionaries and traders, some of whom served British interests.
- **Dividing his options:** he had hoped to keep several partners but ended with one claimant.
- **Preparing the way:** the Moffat Treaty made the Rudd Concession of October 1888 possible, since the British had a legal claim to protect.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Germany made a treaty with Lobengula"}
It did not. German interests mattered because Britain feared a German–Boer link, not because of a German agreement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing the Grobler and Moffat treaties"}
Grobler (1887) was the Transvaal's agent; Moffat (1888) acted for Britain. The Moffat Treaty was the British response to the Grobler Treaty.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Lobengula as passive"}
He played the visitors against each other, delayed and sought advice. His choices were limited by the pressure he faced, not by lack of intelligence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Britain, the Transvaal and (more distantly) Germany all had reasons to want influence north of the Limpopo: minerals, land, trade and strategy.
- The rivalry of states, and above all British fear of a Boer–German link, drove diplomacy.
- Grobler obtained a treaty for the Transvaal in 1887; Moffat obtained one for Britain in February 1888.
- The Moffat Treaty made Matabeleland a British sphere and limited Lobengula's right to deal with other powers.
- Lobengula tried to keep his independence by balancing, but his choices narrowed.
:::
