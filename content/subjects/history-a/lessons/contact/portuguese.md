## Introduction

For centuries the gold of the Zimbabwean plateau had travelled down the Zambezi and Save valleys to the Indian Ocean coast, where Swahili and Muslim merchants bought it and shipped it to Arabia, India and beyond. In 1498 a Portuguese fleet under Vasco da Gama sailed up the east African coast on its way to India, and Portuguese traders and officials soon decided that they wanted a share of the gold, and then the whole of it. This was the first sustained contact between Europeans and the societies of the plateau, and it began about four hundred years before the colonisation of Zimbabwe.

This lesson explains what the Portuguese wanted, how they set up bases and estates, how they tried to use missionaries and armies, and what effect they had. You should already know how the Mutapa state was organised; the lesson on the Mutapa deals with that state's own history, and here the focus is the Portuguese side of the story and its impact. The Portuguese were never the rulers of the plateau, and a good answer shows both their influence and its limits.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prazo"}
A large estate in the Zambezi valley, held by lease from the Portuguese Crown. The holder (a *prazeiro*) collected tribute from the African inhabitants and, in practice, ruled them with the help of armed followers. The system grew in the seventeenth century, and by the eighteenth century many prazeiros were as much African chiefs as Portuguese settlers.
:::

:::definition{term="Feira"}
A trading fair or market, such as Luanze or Dambarare, where gold and ivory were exchanged for cloth, beads and other imported goods. A feira operated with the permission of the Mutapa, who taxed it.
:::

:::definition{term="Captaincy"}
A Portuguese post under a captain, with a fort and a small garrison, used to control trade at a coastal or river site such as Sofala.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1498: Vasco da Gama's fleet reaches the east African coast.
- 1505: the Portuguese establish a fort at Sofala (and take Kilwa the same year).
- 1530s: Portuguese traders settle at Sena and Tete on the Zambezi.
- 1561: the Jesuit Gonçalo da Silveira is killed at the Mutapa's court.
- 1569-1572: Francisco Barreto's expedition up the Zambezi fails.
- 1607: treaty with the Mutapa Gatsi Rusere.
- 1629: Mavura Mhande accepts Portuguese backing and vassal status.
- 1690s: Changamire Dombo's Rozvi army drives the Portuguese from the plateau.
:::

## Why the Portuguese came

Four motives worked together. The first was **gold**. Portuguese writers had heard that great quantities of gold came from the interior behind Sofala, and a European gold shortage made it very attractive. The second was the **Indian Ocean trade**: Portugal wished to control the route to India and to take over the trade of the Swahili towns, and posts on the east coast supplied ships and food. Third was **religion**: Portuguese kings saw themselves as defenders of Christianity and wished to spread it and to weaken Muslim merchants. The fourth was the wish for **ivory** and other goods, together with the hope that great rulers in the interior, imagined as Christian kings, might become allies.

## Trading posts and the push inland

The Portuguese took the coastal towns by force. They built a fort at **Sofala** in 1505, which became the main outlet for plateau gold, and a base on Mozambique Island a few years later. From the 1530s traders and adventurers moved up the Zambezi and settled at **Sena** and **Tete**, which became the centres of Portuguese life in the valley. From there they travelled to the courts and fairs of the interior. Some of these men took African wives, and their families formed a mixed community that spoke the local languages.

On the plateau the Portuguese lived by the Mutapa's rules. They were allowed to trade at the feiras, where they paid for the right to do so. They were few: the Portuguese in the interior were always a small number, and their strength lay in the guns and in the links they made with African factions.

## Missionaries: Silveira

Catholic missions followed the traders. The Jesuit Gonçalo da Silveira reached the Mutapa's court in 1560 and was well received; he baptised the ruler and members of his family. Muslim merchants, who feared that a Portuguese alliance would end their trade, and advisers at court persuaded the ruler that the priest was a sorcerer and a spy. Silveira was killed in 1561. Portugal used the killing as a reason to send an army.

:::example{title="Analysing a cause: why was Silveira killed?"}
1. **The background:** the Mutapa had welcomed the priest, so the killing was not a simple rejection of Christianity.
2. **The fear:** Muslim merchants controlled much of the gold trade and expected to lose it if the Portuguese were favoured.
3. **The court:** advisers worried that baptism would weaken the ruler's link with the ancestors and the mediums who supported his authority.
4. **The result:** Silveira's death hardened Portuguese attitudes and justified the expedition of the next decade.
A good answer separates religious, economic and political causes and does not say that the Mutapa simply "hated Christians".
:::

## Military expeditions

The expedition of **Francisco Barreto** (1569-72) was a large force intended to avenge Silveira, break the power of the Muslim merchants and seize the mines, which the Portuguese believed to be near the Mutapa's land. It was ruined by disease, by the heat and the difficult country, and by the loss of horses and men, and it never reached the gold fields in strength. The failure taught the Portuguese that conquest was impractical, and they turned to treaties, to alliances with African rivals and to private settlers.

## Treaties and interference in succession

In the seventeenth century the Mutapa state was weakened by quarrels about the throne. Rival claimants asked the Portuguese for help, and the captains gave it in return for concessions. In **1607** the Mutapa Gatsi Rusere agreed a treaty that allowed the Portuguese certain rights, including the right to trade and to mine, in return for help against rebels. In **1629** a Portuguese force helped to place Mavura Mhande on the throne, and he accepted terms which the Portuguese described as vassalage and which opened the mines to them. The treaties are known mainly from Portuguese documents, and the Mutapa rulers may not have understood them in the way the Portuguese did. Portuguese traders established themselves at fairs on the plateau, and Dambarare grew into a small centre.

## The prazo system

In the Zambezi valley the Crown leased vast estates, the prazos, to settlers, usually for three lives. Prazeiros collected tribute, raised armies of slaves and followers (the *chikunda* of later years), traded in ivory and later slaves, and ruled as chiefs. The prazos were a way for the Portuguese to hold land without a Portuguese army, but they made the valley a place of violence, and by the eighteenth century the prazeiros often defied both the Crown and the Mutapa.

## Impact on Zimbabwean societies

- **Political:** the Mutapa state lost authority; its rulers became dependent on Portuguese help, and rival chiefs used the Portuguese against each other.
- **Economic:** the Muslim traders were pushed aside, the Portuguese took a share of the gold and ivory trade, and mining was disrupted by wars. The profits mostly went to the coast.
- **Social and religious:** Christianity won few converts on the plateau, though a mixed Afro-Portuguese community grew in the valley.
- **Military:** guns appeared but did not give the Portuguese control, and in the 1690s the Rozvi under Changamire Dombo defeated them and removed them from the plateau. The Portuguese remained in the lower Zambezi.

:::example{title="Weighing influence against control"}
Portuguese influence was greatest in the Zambezi valley, where prazeiros held land and where the Mutapa ruler depended on their help. On the plateau, however, the Portuguese did not rule: they traded by permission, their expedition failed, and the Rozvi expelled them. A balanced answer says that they gained influence by exploiting African divisions but never conquered the plateau.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Portuguese conquered the Mutapa"}
They gained influence through treaties, alliances and prazos. Barreto's army failed, and the Rozvi drove the Portuguese from the plateau in the 1690s.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Silveira's death with the end of the Mutapa state"}
Silveira was killed in 1561. The Mutapa state continued for another two centuries. His death was a trigger for the Portuguese expedition of 1569-72.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only a narrative"}
Questions ask you to explain motives, assess influence or describe impact. Give reasons and an overall judgement, not just a list of dates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Portuguese came for gold and ivory, to control the Indian Ocean trade and to spread Christianity.
- They built forts at Sofala and settled at Sena and Tete, then moved inland to fairs on the plateau.
- Silveira was killed in 1561; Barreto's expedition of 1569-72 failed.
- Through treaties (1607, 1629) and the prazo system they gained influence over a divided Mutapa state.
- They never ruled the plateau: Changamire Dombo's Rozvi expelled them in the 1690s.
:::
