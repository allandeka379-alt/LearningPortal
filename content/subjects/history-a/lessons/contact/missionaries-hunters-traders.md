## Introduction

Between the 1850s and the late 1880s a stream of Europeans came to the lands north of the Limpopo. Some wanted to preach, some to hunt elephants, some to trade, some to explore and some, from the 1880s, to obtain a paper from Lobengula that gave them rights to dig for gold. Most of them arrived as guests of the Ndebele king, and most were few in number. Yet each group left something behind: knowledge of the country, new trade goods, a handful of Christian converts and, above all, a growing conviction in southern Africa that the land beyond the Limpopo was worth having.

This lesson describes each group and its effects on the Ndebele and Shona. Be careful with the chronology. The occupation of the country and the Rudd Concession belong to the lesson on the occupation to 1890, and the rivalry of the states belongs to the lesson on Britain, Germany and the Boer republics; here the focus is on individuals and groups.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Concession"}
A written grant, in this period from an African ruler to a European, giving the right to do something in the ruler's land (to prospect, to mine, to trade or to build). Concession seekers often presented the document as more than the ruler had understood.
:::

:::definition{term="Missionary"}
A person sent by a church or mission society to spread Christianity, and usually also to teach reading and to give medical help.
:::

:::definition{term="Explorer"}
In this period a traveller from outside Africa who recorded the geography, people and resources of the interior. Explorers' reports were read by governments and businessmen.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1859: the London Missionary Society (LMS) opens a mission at Inyati with the permission of Mzilikazi.
- 1870: the LMS station at Hope Fountain is founded near Bulawayo.
- 1871: the German geologist Karl Mauch visits Great Zimbabwe.
- 1870s: Frederick Selous and other hunters work in Matabeleland and Mashonaland.
- 1879: the first Jesuit party reaches Bulawayo; the Jesuit mission station at Empandeni follows in 1887.
- 1880s: concession seekers crowd Lobengula's court.
:::

## Missionaries

**The London Missionary Society.** The Ndebele king Mzilikazi had known the missionary Robert Moffat of Kuruman since 1829, and he allowed an LMS party to settle at **Inyati** in 1859. Its members, including William Sykes, Thomas Morgan Thomas and John Smith Moffat, were few and lived on the king's sufferance. A second station, **Hope Fountain**, followed in 1870. The missionaries ran small schools, preached, wrote down Ndebele and later Shona and produced the first books in these languages. They won almost no Ndebele converts for decades. The king and the regiments saw no reason to give up their beliefs and the system on which the state rested, and many Ndebele regarded the missionaries as foreigners who were useful for gifts and news.

**The Jesuits.** In 1879 a Roman Catholic party of Jesuits led by Father Henri Depelchin arrived at Lobengula's court. They found the climate and the ill health of their members hard, and made their first lasting stations only in the 1880s. They taught and built farms, and their mission at Empandeni, west of Bulawayo, grew in the colonial period.

**What the missionaries did for Lobengula.** Missionaries wrote letters for him, translated and interpreted at the court, and told him what was happening to the south. At the same time some of them became political agents of the Cape and of British interests. When Charles Helm interpreted at the signing of the Rudd Concession in 1888, and John Smith Moffat advised the king to trust the British, they helped to bring about the loss of Ndebele independence, though they did not intend this.

:::example{title="Evaluating the missionaries' impact"}
Look for two sides. **Gains:** schools, literacy, written languages and the first knowledge of the outside world for some Africans. **Costs:** few converts, the undermining of traditional authority in the long run, and the use of missionary reports and influence by those who wanted the country. A balanced answer notes that they were few and often had little effect on the Ndebele in the nineteenth century, but their later importance was considerable.
:::

## Hunters

Elephants were plentiful north of the Limpopo, and ivory was valuable. From the 1860s hunters such as Henry Hartley and from 1871 Frederick Selous came into Matabeleland and Mashonaland. Lobengula allowed them in, but they had to ask for permission and pay for it, and he protected his own interests by limiting where they hunted. Hunters brought guns, cloth and other goods in exchange, and wrote books that told Europeans about the country. Over time the herds were reduced. Some hunters later guided the Pioneer Column in 1890, and Selous was one of them.

## Traders

Traders from the Cape, Natal and the Transvaal came to Bulawayo with cloth, beads, blankets, guns and brandy, and took ivory, cattle hides and other goods back. The king could buy guns and wagons and gave presents. Some traders and hunters also gained influence at the court as advisers. In the north-east and east, Portuguese-speaking traders still came up from Mozambique, and the Shona traded with them. Trade brought new goods to the Ndebele, but it also made the king dependent on supplies of guns and goods from Europeans and gave the traders a base from which to report on the country.

## Explorers

Karl Mauch, a German geologist, visited Great Zimbabwe in 1871. He reported the ruins to the world, but wrongly suggested that they were built by a Near Eastern people, perhaps the Queen of Sheba. Archaeology has since shown that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona. Other explorers and surveyors, such as Thomas Baines, mapped parts of the country. Reports of rivers, gold and good land were published in Britain and South Africa and encouraged those who wanted to invest. Mauch also reported on gold at the Tati field in the south-west, which led to a small gold rush there in the late 1860s.

## Concession seekers

Gold discoveries on the Witwatersrand in 1886 made the land north of the Limpopo seem still more valuable. In the 1880s men with ambitions came to Bulawayo to ask the king for rights: to prospect, to dig, to trade and to hunt. Lobengula gave a number of concessions, partly to keep the whites occupied and to gain guns and goods. The competition for his signature led to the treaties and the Rudd Concession of 1888, which are dealt with in later lessons. The point to remember here is that concession seekers did not come to trade on equal terms; they came to obtain a legal basis for rights that they would later claim in the ruler's name.

## Impact on the Ndebele and Shona

- **Ndebele state:** a king who had to deal with many white visitors, some of whom reported home and some of whom would later claim his land. He tried to control entry and played the visitors against each other.
- **Economy:** guns, cloth and wagons entered; elephant herds fell; the king depended on traders.
- **Society:** a few converts, schools and printed languages; new diseases and drink; little immediate change for most people.
- **Shona:** less affected before 1890 than the Ndebele, but they were raided from the Ndebele state and traded with the Portuguese; the missionaries who came later changed their lives more.
- **Preparing for colonisation:** every report, map and treaty added to the knowledge and the interest of those who would occupy the country.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that missionaries won many converts in Matabeleland"}
They won very few before 1890. Their importance was in language, education, interpreting and in the use of their influence, not in numbers of Christians.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing that Mauch discovered Great Zimbabwe"}
The ruins were well known to the Shona and to earlier traders and Portuguese writers. Mauch was the first European to report on them in this period, and his explanation of them was wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing groups"}
Concession seekers (1880s, rights) are not the same as hunters (ivory) or missionaries (religion). Keep their aims separate.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Missionaries (LMS from 1859 at Inyati, Jesuits from 1879) preached, taught and interpreted but won few converts.
- Hunters and traders brought guns and goods, reduced the elephant herds and made the king dependent on imports.
- Explorers such as Mauch reported on Great Zimbabwe, gold and land.
- Concession seekers from the 1880s sought legal rights to minerals, preparing the way for the Rudd Concession.
- Together these visitors brought knowledge, goods and new interests that led to colonisation.
:::
