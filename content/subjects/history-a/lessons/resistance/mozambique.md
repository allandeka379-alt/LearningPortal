## Introduction

Mozambique shares a long border with Zimbabwe, and its story is closely tied to ours. The armed struggle that began there in 1964 ended Portuguese colonial rule in 1975, five years before Zimbabwe's independence, and from 1972 ZANLA used Mozambique's liberated areas to open a new front against Rhodesia. In 1976 Mozambique closed its border with Rhodesia and supported the Zimbabwean nationalists at heavy cost.

This lesson covers the causes of the armed struggle in Mozambique, the formation of FRELIMO, the course of the war, Portuguese counter-measures, outside support and the final collapse of Portuguese rule. Unlike the pre-colonial lessons, this is a story of a modern liberation movement. You should know from the earlier lessons how the Scramble divided Africa and how European powers took control of different areas.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chibalo"}
The system of forced labour in Portuguese Mozambique, under which Africans could be compelled to work for the state, for companies or for settlers for little or no pay.
:::

:::definition{term="Assimilado"}
An African who, in Portuguese law, had passed a test of "civilised" status (speaking Portuguese, holding a job, living like a European) and gained limited rights. Only a tiny share of the population qualified.
:::

:::definition{term="Aldeamento"}
A "strategic village" in which the Portuguese gathered rural people to cut them off from the guerrillas and control them.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 25 June 1962: FRELIMO formed in Dar es Salaam under Eduardo Mondlane.
- 25 September 1964: the armed struggle begins in northern Mozambique.
- 3 February 1969: Mondlane killed by a parcel bomb; Samora Machel leads FRELIMO from 1970.
- 25 April 1974: coup in Lisbon (the Carnation Revolution).
- 25 June 1975: independence, with Machel as president.
:::

:::

## Portuguese rule

Portugal claimed Mozambique from the sixteenth century, but its effective occupation was completed only in the early twentieth century. Rule was harsh and centralised:

- **Forced labour (chibalo).** Africans were compelled to work on roads, plantations and farms, and many were sent under contract to the South African mines.
- **Cash crops.** Peasants were forced to grow cotton and rice for Portuguese companies, often at the expense of food crops and at low prices.
- **Concession companies.** Large areas were handed to chartered companies that taxed and ruled their regions.
- **Political repression.** After the Second World War Portugal made Mozambique an "overseas province" in the 1950s and encouraged white settlers to come. The secret police, PIDE, used spies and detention against critics. Political parties and trade unions for Africans were banned.
- **Little education.** The great majority of Africans were illiterate, and only a few could become assimilados.

## The formation of FRELIMO

Mozambican exiles formed small parties in neighbouring countries, among them UDENAMO, MANU and UNAMI. The independence of Tanganyika (1961) and other African states encouraged them. In June 1960 at Mueda in the north, Portuguese forces fired on a crowd of people who had gathered to petition; historians agree that there were deaths, but the numbers are disputed. The event became a symbol of Portuguese violence.

With the help of Tanzania's government, the exiles united in Dar es Salaam on 25 June 1962 to form the **Frente de Libertação de Moçambique (FRELIMO)**. Its president was **Eduardo Mondlane**, a university-trained sociologist who had worked at the United Nations. FRELIMO first tried to press Portugal to negotiate, but Portugal refused to grant independence and in 1961 had begun to fight a war in Angola. The movement concluded that only armed struggle could succeed.

## The course of the armed struggle

The war began on 25 September 1964 with attacks on Portuguese posts in Cabo Delgado in the north. FRELIMO trained its fighters in Algeria and Tanzania and received arms and training from the Soviet Union, China and others. Its bases were in Tanzania, and later Zambia. The fighters gradually took control of rural areas in Cabo Delgado and Niassa. In these "liberated zones" FRELIMO organised schools, clinics and farming cooperatives, and it won support from peasants who had suffered under forced labour.

FRELIMO suffered from internal disputes in the 1960s, over race, ideology and leadership, and Mondlane was killed in Dar es Salaam on 3 February 1969 by a parcel bomb; those responsible have not been agreed, but suspicion fell on Portuguese agents. After a period of shared leadership **Samora Machel**, who had commanded the army since 1966, became president in 1970. By the early 1970s the war had spread to Tete province, close to the future Zimbabwe border, where the Cahora Bassa dam was being built.

## Portuguese counter-measures

- **Military force.** Portugal sent over a hundred thousand troops to its African colonies, including many African soldiers, and bombed areas under FRELIMO control. Operation "Gordian Knot" in 1970 aimed to destroy FRELIMO bases in the north but failed.
- **Aldeamentos.** Hundreds of thousands of people were resettled in strategic villages.
- **Secret police and informers.** PIDE spies and informants hunted FRELIMO members.
- **Reforms.** Late reforms, such as the end of the Indigenato code in 1961 and more schools, came too late to win support.
- **Atrocities.** The killing of villagers at Wiriyamu in Tete in December 1972, reported abroad in 1973, damaged Portugal's reputation.

:::example{title="Explaining why the Portuguese could not defeat FRELIMO"}
1. FRELIMO had secure bases in Tanzania and later Zambia where Portugal could not strike easily.
2. It won peasant support by ending forced labour in its zones and by providing schools and clinics.
3. It was supplied with arms and training by Tanzania, the Soviet Union, China and others.
4. Portugal was fighting three wars (Angola, Guinea-Bissau and Mozambique) at once, and these consumed a large share of a poor country's budget.
5. Resettlement and bombing alienated the very people that Portugal needed to win. Therefore FRELIMO's guerrillas could not be dislodged.
:::

## Outside support

Tanzania under Julius Nyerere gave bases, training grounds and political backing; Zambia and, later, other Frontline States helped. The Organisation of African Unity set up its Liberation Committee in Dar es Salaam in 1963 to coordinate help. The Soviet Union and China sent weapons, and Sweden and some other countries gave humanitarian aid. Portugal in turn relied on its NATO membership and on support from Rhodesia and South Africa, who treated the Portuguese colonies as a barrier against black majority rule.

## The fall of the Portuguese regime and independence

The decisive event came in Lisbon. On 25 April 1974 junior officers who were tired of the wars overthrew the dictatorship in the "Carnation Revolution". The new government accepted the principle of self-determination. In September 1974 Portugal and FRELIMO signed the Lusaka Accord, and a transitional government took office. Mozambique became independent on **25 June 1975**, with Machel as president.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing the date of the start of the war with the formation of FRELIMO"}
FRELIMO was formed in June 1962; the armed struggle began in September 1964.
:::

:::mistake{title="Attributing the collapse of Portuguese rule only to FRELIMO"}
The coup in Lisbon in 1974 was decisive. FRELIMO's pressure and Portugal's other wars helped to bring it about, but the immediate cause was in Portugal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the links with Zimbabwe"}
From 1972 ZANLA used Mozambique, and FRELIMO's example inspired Zimbabwean nationalists. Mention this where a question asks about the region.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Portuguese rule rested on forced labour, forced cotton growing, concession companies and political repression.
- FRELIMO was formed on 25 June 1962 under Eduardo Mondlane; the armed struggle began on 25 September 1964.
- FRELIMO built liberated zones and was supported by Tanzania, the OAU, the Soviet Union, China and others; Samora Machel led from 1970.
- Portugal used troops, bombing and strategic villages but could not win, and it was fighting three colonial wars.
- The Lisbon coup of 25 April 1974 led to the Lusaka Accord and independence on 25 June 1975.
:::
