## Introduction

Between July 1905 and 1907 tens of thousands of people in southern German East Africa (mainland Tanzania today) fought against German rule. The movement is known as **Maji Maji**, from the Swahili word *maji*, "water", because the leaders gave fighters a medicine of water mixed with other substances that was believed to protect them from bullets. It was one of the largest African rebellions against colonial rule, and one of the few in which many different peoples joined together.

This lesson explains the causes, the leader and the religious ideas, the course of the war and its suppression, the heavy loss of life, and an assessment of the methods used. You should know from the earlier lessons that European powers had carved up Africa by treaty and conquest, and that Africans resisted in many different ways. Germany's colony in East Africa dates from the 1880s.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Maji"}
The Swahili word for water. In the Maji Maji movement, the medicine of water (with castor oil and millet seeds, according to oral accounts) believed to turn German bullets into water.
:::

:::definition{term="Akida"}
An official in German East Africa, usually a coastal African, Arab or Swahili man, who ran a district for the Germans and collected taxes and labour. Akidas were often resented for abuse.
:::

:::definition{term="Scorched earth"}
A military policy of destroying crops, villages and food stores so that the enemy and the civilian population cannot be fed. The Germans used it against the rebels.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1898: hut and head tax introduced in German East Africa.
- 1902 onwards: compulsory communal cotton plots under Governor von Götzen.
- July 1905: outbreak in the Matumbi hills; Kinjikitile Ngwale hanged in August 1905.
- 1905-06: German scorched-earth tactics and famine.
- 1907: the last fighting ends.
:::

## Causes

The Germans took control of the coast in the 1880s and put down early resistance such as the Abushiri revolt (1888-89) and the Hehe under Mkwawa (to 1898). By 1900 they were trying to make the colony pay.

1. **Forced cotton growing.** From about 1902 local headmen were ordered to organise communal cotton plots on which villagers had to work without pay for long periods. Cotton earned money for the German firms and the administration but little for the growers, and it took time away from food crops.
2. **Taxes.** The hut and head tax (1898) could be paid only in cash, which forced people to sell crops cheaply or work for wages.
3. **Forced labour.** People were compelled to build roads and work on plantations and for officials.
4. **Abuse by officials and agents.** Akidas and jumbes, who often came from outside the region, were accused of beatings, theft and the sexual abuse of women. People had few means of complaint.
5. **Loss of land and independence.** Chiefs lost their authority and the German state arrived to demand obedience from communities that had been self-governing.

A religious movement turned these grievances into a common cause.

## Leadership and the maji medicine

The movement began around **Kinjikitile Ngwale**, a spirit medium living at Ngarambe in the Rufiji area, who claimed to be possessed by a spirit called **Hongo**. He taught that the German rulers could be driven out and that the ancestors would help. He gave pilgrims the maji, believed to protect them from European bullets. People came from many districts, took the water home, and spread the message. The Germans arrested Kinjikitile and hanged him in August 1905. By then the rising was under way, and the belief did not die; it had been passed to local leaders.

Historians such as Gilbert Gwassa have argued that the maji movement allowed people of many different groups, who had been rivals or strangers, to see themselves as having a shared enemy. Others point out that the unity was limited and that local leaders and grievances differed from place to place.

:::example{title="Explaining why a religious idea could unite many peoples"}
1. Each community had its own grievance (cotton, tax, labour), but there was no shared political organisation.
2. The maji teaching was simple and could be carried by individuals, so it travelled along trade routes and pilgrim paths.
3. It offered protection from the weapon that Africans feared most, the rifle, which gave courage to fight.
4. It was linked to the ancestors and to the land, so it was a cause that different groups could share.
5. This explains why peoples who had few links before 1905 rose within weeks of one another.
:::

## The course of the uprising

The fighting began in July 1905 in the Matumbi hills in the south-east, where people uprooted the cotton plants they had been forced to grow, and it spread rapidly through the Rufiji valley and to the Kilwa hinterland, then westwards to peoples such as the Ngindo, Pogoro, Bena and Ngoni. Many peoples took part, though some groups, including the Hehe, stayed out or took the German side. The rebels attacked mission stations, trading posts and government stations, and in August 1905 besieged the post at Mahenge. Their attacks often failed against forts defended by machine guns.

The German response was led by Governor Gustav Adolf von Götzen. He asked for reinforcements, and troops and naval units arrived from Germany and from other German colonies. The army used machine guns and also a policy of scorched earth: soldiers burned villages and crops over large areas in 1905-06 to starve the rebels into surrender. A famine followed, and disease spread. By 1907 the last resistance had ended.

## Loss of life

No one knows how many people died. German figures were low, and later estimates by historians range widely, from about 75,000 to well over 200,000, with some going higher. Most deaths were from famine and disease after crops and villages were destroyed, not from battle. Whatever figure is used, the loss was a large share of the population of the affected areas and left deep scars on the region. In answers, say that the numbers are uncertain and give a range.

## Results and consequences

- Many villages and fields were left empty, and the south remained poor for decades.
- The Germans were shaken. In 1907 Germany created a separate colonial office under Bernhard Dernburg, who promoted reforms in the colonies, including lighter demands on labour and an effort to reduce the worst abuses. Historians debate how far the rising itself, rather than other scandals, caused these changes.
- Maji Maji became a memory of unity. It was later used by Tanzanian nationalists as an example of the people's resistance, though historians note that this memory was shaped by later political needs.

## How effective were the methods?

The rebels' strengths were their numbers, local knowledge and shared faith, and the unity that the maji gave. But their weaknesses were serious: the belief in protection against bullets proved false, which cost lives; they lacked central command and modern weapons; and the Germans had machine guns, supplies and reinforcements. Starvation, brought by German scorched-earth tactics, did more than battle to end the rising. A fair assessment is that the methods were effective for building unity and quick mobilisation, but ineffective against a modern army, and the cost was very high.

:::example{title="An essay paragraph with judgement"}
"The use of religion was both the greatest strength and a weakness of the Maji Maji. It united peoples who had few links and gave courage to fight, so the rising spread within weeks. But the belief that maji turned bullets into water was false, and the lack of firearms and of a single command meant that German machine guns won the open fights. The effectiveness of the method therefore lay in mobilisation, not in military success."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the rising was caused by one grievance"}
Forced cotton growing was important, but taxes, forced labour, abuse by akidas and loss of independence also mattered. Show the causes working together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a single death toll"}
Estimates vary widely and most deaths came from famine. Give a range and state that it is an estimate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting German policy after the rising"}
Germany reformed some colonial practice from 1907, though historians debate how far the rising caused this. Include it when asked about results.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Compulsory cotton growing, taxes, forced labour and abuse by akidas were the main causes; many peoples joined the rising in 1905.
- Kinjikitile Ngwale and the maji medicine gave religious unity; he was hanged in August 1905.
- The Germans under von Götzen crushed the rising by 1907 using machine guns and scorched earth.
- Estimates of deaths range from about 75,000 to well over 200,000, mostly from famine and disease.
- The methods built unity but could not defeat modern weapons; the rising led to some German reforms and became a symbol for later nationalists.
:::
