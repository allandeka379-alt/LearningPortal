## Introduction

In 1815 Italy was, as Metternich is remembered for dismissing it, little more than a geographical name rather than a nation: a peninsula divided into several states, with the north under the influence of Austria. Within fifty-five years, between 1815 and 1870, almost all of it was united under the king of Piedmont-Sardinia. The movement is called the **Risorgimento** ("rising again" or resurgence). The lesson explains how it happened, who contributed most, and why the result was less complete than the dream: many Italians in the south and the poor gained little, and the new state faced deep divisions. Italy is a ZIMSEC topic only; the Cambridge course covers German unification, to which this story is often compared.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Risorgimento"}
The Italian word for the movement towards national unity and independence, from about 1815 to 1870.
:::

:::definition{term="Carbonari"}
Secret societies ("charcoal burners") of the early nineteenth century that plotted for constitutions and national freedom in Italy. Their revolts of 1820-21 and 1831 were crushed.
:::

:::definition{term="Plebiscite"}
A vote of the people on a single question. Plebiscites were used in 1860 to approve the union of central and southern Italian lands with Piedmont, although the voting was not secret in many places and the results were lopsided.
:::

## The background to unification

**The Napoleonic legacy.** Napoleon's rule from 1796 had united much of northern and central Italy in French-controlled states and kingdoms, with the Code, a uniform administration, and careers open to talent. When the old rulers returned in 1815 these experiences were not forgotten, and the idea of a unified Italy gained supporters among the educated.

**The Vienna settlement.** In 1815 Italy was divided into Piedmont-Sardinia (the one independent Italian-ruled kingdom of any size, under the House of Savoy); the northern provinces of Lombardy and Venetia, ruled directly by Austria; the duchies of Parma, Modena and Tuscany, ruled by Habsburg princes or relatives; the Papal States in the centre; and the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies in the south. Austria dominated the peninsula.

**Secret societies and Mazzini.** The Carbonari plotted revolutions in Naples and Piedmont in 1820-21 and in central Italy in 1831, but Austrian troops crushed them. **Giuseppe Mazzini** founded **Young Italy** in 1831 and argued that Italians must rise by their own efforts to form a united republic, "God and the people". His uprisings failed, but he inspired a generation. Other thinkers offered moderate plans, such as Gioberti's idea of a federation under the Pope, and Pope Pius IX's early reforms in 1846 raised hopes.

**1848-49.** Revolutions broke out across Italy. In March 1848 Milan rose in the "Five Days" and King Charles Albert of Piedmont declared war on Austria, but the Austrians won at Custoza (1848) and Novara (1849), and Charles Albert abdicated in favour of his son, Victor Emmanuel II. In Rome a republic was proclaimed under Mazzini, with Garibaldi defending it; French troops sent by Louis Napoleon destroyed it in 1849 and restored the Pope. The lessons were clear: Italians alone could not defeat Austria, and Piedmont, which kept its constitution, was the only state able to lead.

## Piedmont, Cavour and the war of 1859

Count **Cavour**, prime minister of Piedmont from 1852, was a practical statesman rather than a revolutionary. He modernised Piedmont's economy (railways, trade, banks) and strengthened its army. He took Piedmont into the Crimean War on the side of Britain and France (1855) to win a place at the peace conference of 1856, where he raised the Italian question. In July 1858 he met Napoleon III in secret at **Plombières**: France would help Piedmont drive Austria from northern Italy in return for Savoy and Nice, and a kingdom of northern Italy would be formed under Victor Emmanuel.

Cavour provoked Austria into declaring war in April 1859. French and Piedmontese forces won at Magenta and Solferino (June 1859), but Napoleon III, alarmed by the losses and by Prussian movements, made the armistice of Villafranca with Austria in July. Piedmont gained Lombardy, but Venetia remained Austrian, and Cavour resigned in anger. In central Italy, however, Tuscany, Parma, Modena and part of the Papal States had expelled their rulers and voted by plebiscite to join Piedmont in 1860. Cavour, back in office, gave Savoy and Nice to France.

## Garibaldi and the south, 1860

**Giuseppe Garibaldi**, a former follower of Mazzini and a famous guerrilla leader, led about a thousand volunteers (the Redshirts, the *Mille*) from Genoa in May 1860 to help a revolt in Sicily. They landed at Marsala on 11 May, won at Calatafimi, took Palermo and crossed to the mainland, entering Naples on 7 September as the Bourbon army collapsed. Garibaldi's success alarmed Cavour, who feared he would march on Rome and bring in the French, and who also wanted to prevent a republic. Cavour sent a Piedmontese army through the Papal States, which defeated the papal troops at Castelfidardo (September 1860). At Teano in October, Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel II. Plebiscites in the south approved union.

On 17 March 1861 the first Italian parliament proclaimed Victor Emmanuel II King of Italy. Cavour died in June 1861.

## Venetia and Rome

In 1866 Italy allied with Prussia against Austria. Italy lost the battles of Custoza and Lissa, but Prussia won decisively and Venetia was transferred to Italy through Napoleon III. Rome remained under papal rule protected by French troops. Garibaldi's attempts to take it failed in 1862 and in 1867 (at Mentana). When France withdrew its garrison at the start of the Franco-Prussian War in 1870, Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870 and the city became the capital in 1871. The Pope refused to accept the loss of Rome and withdrew into the Vatican, a quarrel which lasted until the Lateran Treaties of 1929.

## Who contributed most?

:::example{title="Weighing contributions"}
**Mazzini** provided the idea and inspired nationalism, but his uprisings failed. **Cavour** provided diplomacy, the Piedmontese state and the French alliance, but he was not a nationalist in 1850 and did not plan the conquest of the south. **Garibaldi** provided popular action and the conquest of Sicily and Naples, but needed Piedmont's army to complete the work. **Victor Emmanuel II** gave the monarchy a role that conservatives could accept. **Foreign powers** mattered greatly: French arms in 1859, Prussia's victory in 1866 and the withdrawal of France in 1870 made unification possible. A strong answer ranks them. For example: "Cavour made the most important contribution, because without his diplomacy and Piedmont's leadership the other efforts would have failed, but he depended on Napoleon III, on Garibaldi's daring and on the unplanned success of Prussia."
:::

Historians note that unification was largely carried out by Piedmont's elite, with limited popular involvement: many peasants, particularly in the south, were indifferent, and the new state extended Piedmont's laws and taxes across the peninsula. The gap between north and south remained a problem for the next century.

:::example{title="Judging a turning point"}
Test the claim that Plombières was the turning point: it brought French arms against Austria and led to Lombardy and the central duchies in 1859-60, but Villafranca limited the gain and Garibaldi's expedition of 1860 took the unification beyond what Cavour had planned. A balanced answer says it was necessary but not sufficient.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Making Garibaldi the founder of Italy"}
Garibaldi conquered Sicily and Naples, but he handed his gains to the king, and the unification depended on Piedmont and foreign powers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing Plombières, Villafranca and Teano"}
Plombières (1858) was the secret agreement of Cavour and Napoleon III. Villafranca (1859) was Napoleon's armistice with Austria. Teano (1860) was Garibaldi's handing over of the south to Victor Emmanuel.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Napoleon's rule and the Vienna settlement created both the idea of Italian unity and the Austrian domination that blocked it.
- Secret societies and Mazzini kept the ideal alive, but the revolutions of 1820, 1831 and 1848-49 failed.
- Piedmont under Cavour won French help at Plombières and defeated Austria in 1859, gaining Lombardy and the central duchies.
- Garibaldi's Thousand conquered Sicily and Naples in 1860; Victor Emmanuel II was proclaimed King of Italy in March 1861.
- Venetia (1866) and Rome (1870) completed the unification, helped by Prussia's victories and France's withdrawal.
:::
