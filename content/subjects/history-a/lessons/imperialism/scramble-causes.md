## Introduction

In 1880 Europeans controlled only a fraction of Africa. Historians usually say that perhaps one tenth of the continent was under European rule, mostly in a few coastal areas: Algeria (French since the 1830s), the Cape and Natal, the Portuguese coastal posts in Angola and Mozambique, and trading stations in Senegal and the Gold Coast. By 1900 almost the whole continent had been divided up. Only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. This sudden change, in about twenty years, is called the **Scramble for Africa**.

Why did it happen then, and not fifty years earlier or later? This is a favourite essay topic: "Account for the Scramble", "Which was the more important cause, economic or political?". The conference and the partition itself are in the next lesson; here we look at causes. You should already know about the abolition of the slave trade and the legitimate trade that replaced it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Imperialism"}
The policy or process by which one state extends its control over other peoples and territories, by conquest, treaty or economic domination.
:::

:::definition{term="Scramble for Africa"}
The rapid European occupation and division of Africa between about 1880 and 1900, when the European powers claimed almost the whole continent.
:::

:::definition{term="Civilising mission"}
The European belief, used to justify colonial rule, that Europeans had a duty to bring Christianity, commerce and "civilisation" to Africans. It rested on assumptions of racial superiority which historians reject, though many people at the time sincerely held it.
:::

## Economic motives

By the 1870s Europe's factories needed raw materials: palm oil and groundnuts for soap and lubricants, rubber, cotton, copper, and later gold and diamonds. They also needed markets. From 1873 Europe suffered a long period of falling prices and weak growth, sometimes called the Long Depression, and governments raised tariffs on imports. Merchants and manufacturers feared that unprotected markets would close to them, so they urged their governments to claim territory where their goods would have a protected market.

Discoveries added to the attraction. Diamonds were found near Kimberley in the 1860s and 1870s, and gold on the Witwatersrand in 1886. Rumours of a "second Rand" fed the ambitions of men such as Cecil Rhodes, who sought mineral wealth north of the Limpopo.

Two famous writers made economics the centre of the explanation. The British economist J. A. Hobson (*Imperialism: A Study*, 1902) argued that wealthy investors needed new places to invest. Lenin (*Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism*, 1916) developed this into a Marxist theory. Later historians pointed out that in the 1880s most European investment went to the Americas and Russia, not Africa. Economic hope was strong; profit often came later.

## Political and strategic motives

Prestige and security mattered as much as money. After Germany's unification in 1871 Europe was a tense place, and colonies became signs of great-power status. Bismarck, who had been cool about colonies, allowed Germany to acquire Togoland, Cameroon, South West Africa and East African territories in 1884-85, partly for trading interests and partly for domestic politics and diplomacy. The French, humiliated in 1870-71, sought compensation overseas. Italy, newly united, wanted a "place in the sun". Portugal feared losing its old claims to rivals.

Strategy was central for Britain. The Suez Canal (1869) shortened the sea route to India, the "jewel" of the empire. When an Egyptian nationalist revolt threatened the Canal's security in 1882, Britain occupied Egypt. To protect Egypt, Britain then needed the upper Nile and East Africa, and to protect the Cape route it needed southern Africa. The historians Robinson and Gallagher (*Africa and the Victorians*, 1961) argued that this strategic concern for India, rather than a hunger for African wealth, drove British policy. Their view has been criticised for neglecting economic and African causes, but it showed that the Scramble was partly a defensive reaction.

:::example{title="Explaining a cause step by step: Egypt and the Scramble"}
1. Britain valued the Suez Canal as the short route to India (1869).
2. Egyptian finances collapsed and a nationalist movement threatened the Canal in 1881-82.
3. Britain occupied Egypt in 1882, an act that alarmed France, which had interests in Egypt.
4. To protect Egypt, Britain claimed the Sudan and the headwaters of the Nile, leading to the clash with France at Fashoda in 1898.
5. Other powers, seeing Britain act, hurried to claim their own areas. A single strategic decision therefore set off a chain of claims.
:::

## Humanitarian and religious motives

In 1857 David Livingstone told a Cambridge audience that Africa needed "Christianity, Commerce and Civilisation" to end the slave trade. Missionaries went to Africa in large numbers and wrote home about slave raiding in East and Central Africa. Anti-slavery societies and church groups campaigned, and the demand to end the trade by Arab and Swahili traders became a public argument for intervention. Leopold II described his Congo project as a humanitarian one. Social Darwinist ideas, which ranked peoples as "higher" and "lower" races, were widely used to defend conquest. Historians generally treat humanitarian arguments as sincere for many individuals but also as a cover used to win public support. The Congo Free State, where Leopold's agents forced people to collect rubber, shows how far reality departed from the claims.

## Technology and medicine

Steamships and river steamers carried men and goods up the Niger, Congo and Zambezi. Telegraph cables improved communication. Railways later moved armies and goods inland. Quinine, taken as a regular preventive, sharply cut deaths from malaria: the Niger expedition of 1854 led by W. B. Baikie is the famous case of a European party that did not suffer deaths from fever. Weapons mattered too. Breech-loading rifles gave Europeans a decisive advantage in firepower, and from the 1880s the Maxim machine gun made it possible for small forces to defeat much larger armies in open battle, as at the Shangani in 1893 and at Omdurman in 1898. Technology did not cause the Scramble, but it removed the obstacles that had kept Europeans on the coast.

## European rivalry and African conditions

Once one power claimed territory the others feared being shut out, and competition fed on itself. The scramble for the Congo basin between Leopold, France (under de Brazza) and Portugal led directly to the Berlin Conference.

African conditions also made conquest easier, though they did not make it inevitable. Many African societies were small and politically divided, and rulers who feared a neighbour sometimes sought European help or signed treaties without understanding that the Europeans meant them to surrender sovereignty. Some regions were weakened by drought, disease, the slave trade, civil wars and, in southern Africa, the wars of the Mfecane. Yet it is wrong to picture Africa as passive. Ethiopia under Menelik II defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896; Samori Touré resisted the French for sixteen years; Zulu and Ndebele armies fought bravely. Many conquests needed hard campaigns, and African soldiers and allies did much of the fighting for Europeans.

## Weighing the causes

Historians disagree about which cause mattered most, so compare them. Economic hopes and strategic fears created the *motive*, new weapons and medicine the *means*, and African divisions the *opportunity*. No single cause fits every region: Egypt was mostly strategic, the Congo partly commercial, South Africa about minerals.

:::example{title="An essay paragraph with judgement"}
"Economic motives were important but not the main cause of the Scramble. Merchants wanted markets and raw materials, and the Long Depression made protected markets attractive. However, most European trade and investment in the 1880s was not with Africa, and in Egypt the key decision was made to defend the route to India. The most convincing explanation therefore combines strategic fear and national rivalry, which turned economic hope into a race."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming one cause only"}
"The Scramble was caused by the need for raw materials" is incomplete. Give several causes, link them, and judge which mattered most.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing causes with results"}
Forced labour, taxation and the loss of African independence were results of colonial rule, not reasons why Europeans scrambled. Keep motives and consequences separate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Africans as passive"}
African divisions helped Europeans, but many societies resisted fiercely, and some ruled by treaty and bargaining. Include African agency.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Account for the Scramble for Africa", "Which was the more important motive, economic or strategic?", "To what extent did technology make the Scramble possible?" Group causes (economic, political, humanitarian, technological, African conditions), give a named example for each, link them, and finish with a judgement. Mention the historians' debate with Hobson, Lenin and Robinson and Gallagher but never copy their words.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Before about 1880 Europeans held only a tenth of Africa, mainly coastal areas; by 1900 only Ethiopia and Liberia were independent.
- Economic motives (raw materials, markets, minerals), political and strategic motives (prestige, Egypt and the route to India) and humanitarian and religious claims all played a part.
- Steamships, quinine, rifles and the Maxim gun gave Europeans the means.
- European rivalry made each claim set off others; African political divisions made conquest easier but Africans also resisted.
- Historians disagree on the weight of the causes; the best answers compare them and give a reasoned judgement.
:::
