## Introduction

Ask a classmate who "divided Africa" and many will say: "The Europeans at Berlin." The truth is more careful. A conference in Berlin in 1884-85 laid down rules for claiming African territory, but it did not draw the map. The map was drawn in the next fifteen years by treaties and agreements between European capitals, by agents on the ground who signed papers with African rulers, and by armies who enforced them. No African ruler took part in the decisions at Berlin.

This lesson explains why the conference met, what it decided, how partition proceeded, and what the effects were in Africa and in Europe. You should already know the causes of the Scramble (the previous lesson). The stories of resistance that follow in this topic show how Africans responded.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Effective occupation"}
The principle, agreed at Berlin, that a European power could claim African coastal territory only if it had real control there (an administration and some force to keep order) and not merely a flag or a treaty on paper. In practice powers used treaties and small posts to satisfy it.
:::

:::definition{term="Spheres of influence"}
Areas in which a European power was recognised by the other powers as having the exclusive right to make treaties, trade and later to rule, even before it had occupied them.
:::

:::definition{term="Partition"}
The division of Africa among the European powers, with borders drawn by Europeans, mostly without regard to the African peoples, states and trade routes they cut across.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885: the Berlin Conference, chaired by Otto von Bismarck of Germany. The General Act was signed on 26 February 1885.
- Fourteen states attended (Europe's powers, the United States and the Ottoman Empire); no African ruler was present.
- The conference recognised the claim of King Leopold II's International Association of the Congo, which became the Congo Free State.
- 1890: Britain's ultimatum to Portugal and the Anglo-German agreement. 1898: Fashoda.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The Berlin Conference and the partition are ZIMSEC only (Paper 2). Cambridge's covered options do not require them.
:::

## Why the conference met

By 1884 rival claims were colliding, above all in the Congo basin and the Niger. Leopold II was working through the International African Association; Stanley made treaties for him from 1879; the Frenchman de Brazza claimed land north of the Congo for France in 1880; Portugal insisted on its old rights at the river's mouth; and Britain was drawn in. In early 1884 Britain and Portugal agreed a treaty that would have given Portugal control of the mouth of the Congo, and France, Germany and others protested. Bismarck, who wanted to prevent a dangerous quarrel and to protect German trading interests, joined with France in inviting the powers to Berlin. Germany, newly a colonial power, had just claimed Togoland, Cameroon and South West Africa. Bismarck was a skilled chairman who wished to keep Britain and France from becoming dangerous rivals in Africa without war in Europe.

## What the conference decided

The General Act of 26 February 1885 contained several main points:

1. **Free trade** in the Congo basin, and free navigation on the Congo and the Niger.
2. **Effective occupation**: a power could claim *coastal* territory only if it actually controlled it.
3. **Notification**: any power taking new coastal territory had to tell the others, so that they could object.
4. **The Congo Free State**: the conference recognised the claim of Leopold's International Association over the Congo basin, as a personal possession of the king and not of Belgium.
5. A declaration against the slave trade, which in later years was used to justify intervention.

Notice what the Act did not do. It did not divide Africa or give any power a particular territory, apart from recognising Leopold's claim. It spoke of the coast, and it did not set rules for the interior. It did not ask what the African inhabitants wanted. Its principles were widely used in practice, though, and the demand for "effective occupation" encouraged a rush to place posts and sign treaties before rivals did.

:::example{title="Reading a source: what Berlin did and did not do"}
Suppose a student writes: "At Berlin the powers divided Africa among themselves." You can improve this by testing it against the evidence. The General Act dealt with trade, navigation, coastal claims and the Congo; the borders appeared later in agreements such as the Anglo-German agreement of 1890 and the Anglo-French agreement of 1898. So Berlin gave the *rules* and encouraged the race; it did not itself make the map. A better sentence is: "Berlin set the rules for claiming territory and speeded up the partition, which was then carried out by separate agreements."
:::

## How partition proceeded

After Berlin, claims were made by treaties with African rulers (often signed without full understanding), by treaties between European powers, and by force. Typical results include:

- **Anglo-German agreement of 1890**: Germany gave up claims in Zanzibar and Uganda; Britain gave Heligoland to Germany.
- **The Portuguese claim**: Portugal's "Rose-coloured Map" claimed a belt from Angola to Mozambique, blocking Rhodes's plans for the north. In January 1890 Britain sent an ultimatum, Portugal backed down, and an Anglo-Portuguese treaty of 1891 fixed the boundaries between Mozambique and what became Southern Rhodesia. This is the link to Zimbabwe's story.
- **The Fashoda incident** (1898): a French expedition met a British-Egyptian force at Fashoda on the Nile. France withdrew and agreed to a settlement; relations improved, and in 1904 the two signed the Entente Cordiale.
- By 1900 the whole continent except Ethiopia and Liberia was divided among Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy and Spain.

## Effects on Africa

- **Loss of sovereignty.** African states that had traded and made treaties as equals now found themselves under foreign rulers. Treaties with African rulers were treated as transfers of sovereignty even where the rulers meant only friendship or trade.
- **Artificial borders.** Boundaries were often drawn on maps by officials who knew little of the ground. They divided peoples such as the Somali, the Kongo and the Ewe among several colonies, and joined peoples with different histories into one. Many of these boundaries survived at independence.
- **Exploitation.** Colonies were run for the benefit of the rulers: cash crops, minerals, taxes and forced labour. The worst case was the Congo Free State, where Leopold's agents used forced labour and violence to collect rubber, until international protests led Belgium to take over in 1908.
- **Resistance.** Africans responded in many ways, from armed resistance to diplomacy and migration. The next lessons deal with Ethiopia, the Maji Maji rising and others.

## Effects on Europe

The partition reduced the risk of war over Africa at first, since the powers agreed rules and compromises. But rivalry continued, and it fed the tension that helped lead to 1914. Fashoda in 1898 and the two crises over Morocco in 1905 and 1911 brought Britain, France and Germany to the brink. The colonies also raised pride and a sense of greatness at home, and strengthened ideas of racial superiority.

:::example{title="Weighing a claim"}
Claim: "The Berlin Conference was the cause of the partition of Africa." A balanced answer: "It is partly true. The principle of effective occupation encouraged a race to occupy, and Berlin recognised Leopold's claim. But the causes of the Scramble (strategic fear, economic hopes and rivalry) were at work before 1884, and the actual borders came from later agreements. Berlin was therefore a stage in the Scramble rather than its origin."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Africa was divided at Berlin"}
The conference set rules; the borders were drawn afterwards by agreements between the powers and by conquest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the date range"}
The conference lasted from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885. "1885" alone is acceptable only if you know the General Act was signed in February that year.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing effects only in Africa"}
A question on "effects" may require both African and European consequences. Include rivalry and tension for Europe.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Why was the Berlin Conference held?", "Describe the decisions of the Berlin Conference", "To what extent was the Berlin Conference responsible for the partition of Africa?", "Assess the effects of the partition on Africa". Give the terms by name, point out that Africans were absent, and for judgement questions weigh the conference against the earlier causes and the later agreements.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Berlin Conference (15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885) met because rival claims in the Congo and Niger regions threatened conflict.
- Its General Act dealt with free trade and navigation, effective occupation, notification of coastal claims and recognition of the Congo Free State.
- No African ruler attended, and the conference did not itself draw the map.
- Partition followed in separate treaties and agreements, including the 1890 Anglo-German agreement, the 1890-91 Anglo-Portuguese settlement and the Fashoda settlement of 1898.
- Africa lost sovereignty and suffered arbitrary borders and exploitation; Europe gained empires but also deeper rivalry.
:::
