## Introduction

In 1789 France was the largest state in western Europe, ruled by an absolute monarch. Within months the Estates-General he had called to solve a money problem had become a National Assembly, a fortress in Paris had been stormed and privilege had been abolished in law. The ideas of liberty and equality set out then were later used by people in Africa and elsewhere to challenge colonial rule. This lesson explains the causes of the Revolution (long-term weaknesses, the financial crisis of 1787-89 and the political mistakes that followed) and then the events of 1789 up to October.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ancien Régime"}
"The old order": the system of government and society in France before 1789, with an absolute monarch, a society divided into three unequal estates and a mass of privileges that differed by group and region.
:::

:::definition{term="Estates-General"}
A representative assembly of the three estates (clergy, nobility and everyone else), summoned by the king for advice and money. It had not met since 1614, so no one in 1789 remembered how it worked.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1787: the Assembly of Notables meets (February); Calonne is dismissed (April).
- 5 May 1789: the Estates-General opens at Versailles.
- 17 June 1789: the Third Estate declares itself the National Assembly. 20 June: the Tennis Court Oath.
- 14 July 1789: the storming of the Bastille.
- 4-11 August 1789: the August Decrees. 26 August: the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.
- 5-6 October 1789: the March of the Women to Versailles.
:::

## The Ancien Régime: government and society

**Government.** Louis XVI (reigned 1774-92) claimed to rule by divine right and made law through his own council. Royal officials called *intendants* governed the provinces, and the *parlements*, law courts staffed by nobles, had to register royal edicts and used the right to protest against new taxes. The king could imprison people without trial by a *lettre de cachet*.

**Society.** Society was divided into three estates. The First Estate, the clergy, was small (well under 150,000 people) but owned much land, collected the tithe and paid no regular direct taxes. The Second Estate, the nobility, numbered roughly 350,000 to 400,000, held most high offices and was largely exempt from the main direct tax, the *taille*. The Third Estate was everyone else, over 95 per cent of about 28 million people, and it was not united: peasants paid the taille, the tithe, feudal dues and the salt tax (the *gabelle*); wealthy lawyers and merchants (the bourgeoisie) were excluded from top posts; and urban workers spent most of their wages on bread. Privileged groups kept their exemptions while the burden fell on those least able to pay.

## The influence of new ideas and the American war

Thinkers of the Enlightenment questioned authority. Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) argued for a division of powers, Voltaire attacked intolerance and Church privilege, and Rousseau (*The Social Contract*, 1762) argued that government rests on the will of the people. Few people read the philosophes, but their ideas gave the educated Third Estate a language for its grievances. In January 1789 the priest Sieyès published *What is the Third Estate?*, arguing that the Third Estate was the nation.

France's help for the American colonists, who declared independence in 1776, cost a great deal (France entered the war openly in 1778; historians estimate well over a billion livres, mostly borrowed) and showed a successful republic that had declared the rights of man.

## The financial crisis and failed reforms

The root problem was the state's debt. France was not poor, but its tax system was unfair and inefficient, and wars were paid for by loans. By the late 1780s servicing the debt took roughly half of royal spending, by the usual estimates. The obvious remedy, taxing the privileged, was blocked by the parlements and the nobility.

| Minister | Years | Policy | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turgot | 1774-76 | Free trade in grain, end of the guilds, tax in place of forced road labour | Opposed by the privileged and the parlements; dismissed in 1776 |
| Necker | 1777-81 | Borrowed to pay for the war; published an account of royal finances in 1781 that looked healthy | Fell from power; the account hid the true deficit |
| Calonne | 1783-87 | Spent and borrowed to restore confidence; in 1786 proposed a land tax on all owners and the end of internal customs | The Notables refused; dismissed April 1787 |
| Brienne | 1787-88 | Tried to carry Calonne's ideas; faced the parlements | Forced to call the Estates-General; resigned August 1788 |

:::example{title="Explaining why Calonne's plan failed"}
Calonne proposed a land tax on every owner and asked a hand-picked **Assembly of Notables** (February 1787) to approve it. The Notables would themselves pay, so they refused and said only an Estates-General could agree new taxes. The king dismissed Calonne, his successor met the same opposition, and the "aristocratic revolt" of 1787-88 forced the calling of the Estates-General.
:::

## The crisis of 1787-89 and the calling of the Estates-General

In 1788 a hailstorm and poor harvest sent bread prices up through the winter, so the poor were hungry just as political hopes rose, and there was disorder (the Réveillon riots in Paris in April 1789). Brienne agreed to call the Estates-General and Necker was recalled. In December 1788 the Third Estate was given as many deputies as the other two together, but the question of voting remained open: by order (one vote per estate, so the privileged could outvote the Third) or by head. Parishes and estates also drew up lists of grievances, the *cahiers de doléances*, which mostly asked for a constitution and fairer taxes while expressing loyalty to the king.

## From Estates-General to National Assembly, May-July 1789

The Estates-General opened at Versailles on **5 May 1789**, and weeks passed in a dispute about voting. On 17 June the Third Estate, joined by some clergy, declared itself the **National Assembly**. When the deputies found their hall closed on 20 June, they met in a nearby royal tennis court and swore the **Tennis Court Oath**, promising not to separate until France had a constitution. The king ordered the estates to meet separately on 23 June, but the deputies refused, and on 27 June he told the clergy and nobles to join them.

Meanwhile the king gathered troops near Paris and on 11 July dismissed Necker. Parisians feared a royal attack on the Assembly and needed bread. On **14 July** a crowd searched for arms, then attacked the **Bastille**, a royal fortress that held few prisoners but was a symbol of arbitrary power and held gunpowder. The importance was political: the king had lost control of Paris, and a new city government and National Guard under Lafayette appeared.

## Revolution in the countryside and the new order

In July and August rumours spread that bandits or aristocrats were coming to destroy the harvest. This panic, the **Great Fear**, led peasants to arm themselves and attack châteaux and burn the records of dues. To calm the countryside the Assembly passed the **August Decrees** (4-11 August), abolishing feudal dues, the tithe, the special rights of the nobles and the sale of offices, though some payments were to be compensated.

On 26 August the Assembly issued the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**: men are born free and equal in rights; liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression are natural rights; sovereignty rests in the nation; law is the expression of the general will; all citizens are equal before the law and may hold office by talent. The Declaration said nothing about women, and slavery in the colonies was not abolished.

The king delayed approving the decrees. In October, with bread short in Paris, thousands of Parisian women marched about 20 kilometres to Versailles on **5 October**, followed by the National Guard under Lafayette. On 6 October the royal family was escorted to Paris and lived in the Tuileries, and the Assembly soon followed. The king was now under the eyes of the capital.

A cahier from a village asks for fairer taxes but praises the king. ## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Revolution began on 14 July"}
The 1789 Revolution began as a political revolution in June, when the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly. The Bastille was a turning point that saved the Assembly, not the start.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-cause answers"}
Hunger, ideas and debt each matter, but none explains the Revolution alone. Separate long-term causes, the financial crisis and the triggers, and say how they combined.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Ancien Régime combined absolute monarchy with a society of unequal estates and unfair, uneven taxation.
- Enlightenment ideas and the American war strengthened criticism; the war also deepened the debt.
- Turgot, Necker and Calonne all failed to reform taxation; the Notables' refusal in 1787 led to the Estates-General.
- The dispute over voting turned the Estates-General into the National Assembly (June 1789); the Tennis Court Oath and the Bastille secured it.
- The Great Fear, the August Decrees, the Declaration of Rights and the March of the Women ended the old order and brought the king to Paris.
:::
