## Introduction

Historians still argue about the meaning of the French Revolution, but most agree that it was one of the great turning points in modern history. It ended the absolute monarchy and the legal privileges of the old estates in France; it made the idea that sovereignty belongs to the people a political force; and it began a series of wars that changed the map of Europe. This lesson asks what changed and what did not in France, how the Revolution's ideas spread, and how other states responded. ZIMSEC candidates need the whole lesson; Cambridge candidates meet some of these themes in other lessons but do not have a separate key question.

You should know the main events from the earlier lessons in this unit. Do not repeat the story: use it as evidence for judgements about change.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Popular sovereignty"}
The principle that political authority comes from the people and not from a king or a church. The Declaration of 1789 said that sovereignty rests in the nation.
:::

:::definition{term="Secularisation"}
The process by which the state, law and education become independent of the Church, so that civil matters (such as birth, marriage and death records, and divorce) are handled by the state.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
A political belief that people who share language, history and territory form a nation with the right to govern themselves. The Revolution and the wars encouraged it in France and in the lands that France invaded.
:::

:::

- 1 January 1804: Haiti declares independence.
- 1808-1825: independence movements in Spanish America.

## Changes in France

**Law and society.** The Revolution abolished the three estates and the privileges that went with them: feudal dues, the tithe, tax exemptions and the sale of offices. All male citizens were equal before the law, and careers were open to talent. A new, uniform system of law replaced local customs, ending with the Code of 1804. Jews and Protestants gained civil rights, and divorce and civil marriage were introduced in 1792 (divorce was restricted later).

**Church and state.** Church lands were nationalised, the clergy came under state control, and civil registration of births and marriages replaced Church records. A republican calendar and the campaign against the Church (1793-94) failed to last, but the Concordat of 1801 left the state, not the Church, in charge of civil life.

**Government and administration.** The departments, elected local officials and a more uniform tax system replaced the old provinces and exemptions; Napoleon's prefects strengthened a central state. Standard weights and measures, the metric system, were adopted in the 1790s. Some historians, following the French writer Tocqueville (*The Old Regime and the Revolution*, 1856), argue that the Revolution continued the centralising work of the monarchy rather than starting it.

**Land.** Peasants gained freedom from dues, and many families bought land that had belonged to the Church and the émigrés. This created a large class of landowners with an interest in preserving the Revolution's gains.

| Area | What changed | What did not change |
|---|---|---|
| Law | Equality before the law, a single legal code | Fathers and husbands kept strong authority |
| Society | Feudal privilege ended | A new elite of wealth emerged; poverty remained |
| Women | Equal inheritance and the right to divorce (for a time) | No vote, and women's political clubs banned in 1793 |
| Slavery | Abolished in the colonies in 1794 | Restored by Napoleon in 1802 (Haiti stayed free) |
| Politics | Popular sovereignty became a principle | Authoritarian rule under Napoleon, and monarchy returned in 1814 |

:::example{title="Judging how much changed"}
A strong paragraph can say: "The Revolution changed the legal and social order more than it changed daily life. Equality before the law and the end of feudal dues transformed the position of the peasants and the bourgeoisie. But women gained few lasting rights, since Olympe de Gouges was executed in 1793 and women's clubs were banned, and slavery was abolished in 1794 but restored in 1802. For the poor, hunger and inequality continued." This puts evidence on both sides before reaching a judgement.
:::

## The spread of ideas

The Declaration of Rights, the idea of the nation and the words *liberty, equality, fraternity* spread through pamphlets, newspapers and the armies. In the lands conquered by France (the Netherlands, Italy, the Rhineland and Switzerland), French troops set up sister republics and introduced new laws, which ended serfdom and feudal dues in many places. These changes were welcomed by some people, but the burdens of occupation, taxes and conscription made French rule resented, and the resentment encouraged national feeling, in Spain, in Germany and elsewhere. After 1815 the monarchs tried to restore the old order, but liberal and national movements kept pressing for constitutions and unity, which led to the revolutions of 1830 and 1848 and to the unification of Italy and Germany.

## The response of other powers

At first some observers in Britain and Germany welcomed the Revolution. By 1790 the British writer Edmund Burke, in *Reflections on the Revolution in France*, was warning against it. Monarchs feared the example, and the flight to Varennes and the Declaration of Pillnitz (1791) increased tension. After war began in 1792, Britain, Austria, Prussia, Spain and other states formed coalitions against France. In Britain the government under Pitt used repressive laws against radical societies. Russia's Catherine the Great also opposed the Revolution. The Revolution also inspired reformers abroad, and the Polish Constitution of 3 May 1791 was influenced by Enlightenment ideas of this kind; there were also sympathisers in Ireland and Germany.

## Haiti, the Americas and beyond

In 1791 enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose against the planters. Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man, became their leading commander; in 1794 the Convention abolished slavery in the colonies, and he then served the French Republic. When Napoleon sent an army in 1802 to restore French control and slavery, the army was defeated, and Haiti declared independence on 1 January 1804, the first state in the Americas founded by formerly enslaved people. Napoleon's invasion of Spain and Portugal weakened their control of their American colonies; leaders such as Bolívar and San Martín led independence movements from 1808, citing Enlightenment and revolutionary ideas.

Beyond the Americas, the ideas of equality and the right of peoples to govern themselves were quoted by later movements, including anti-colonial movements in Africa and Asia in the twentieth century, though historians debate how direct this influence was and point out that the Revolution's own record on slavery and empire was mixed. Napoleon's invasion of Egypt in 1798 also began a period of increased European involvement in the Middle East and North Africa.

:::example{title="Comparing two kinds of impact"}
On the question of equality: in law, all male citizens were equal and feudal dues ended (a lasting change); in practice, women had no vote, slavery was restored in 1802 and the poor stayed poor. Putting the two side by side lets you judge that the change was real but partial.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
A question on impact needs both change and continuity. Do not say the Revolution changed everything, or that it changed nothing; give evidence for each.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the limits of equality"}
Equality in 1789 meant legal equality of male citizens. Women, enslaved people and poor men without property were excluded from many rights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Haiti's independence with the French abolition of slavery"}
The Convention abolished slavery in 1794; Napoleon tried to restore it; the Haitians resisted and declared independence in 1804.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Assess the impact of the French Revolution on France", "To what extent did the Revolution change France?" and "Examine the influence of the Revolution on Europe and the world." Organise by theme (law, church, government, women, slavery, ideas and nationalism), use dates and names, and end with a supported judgement about change and continuity.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In France the Revolution ended feudal privilege, introduced legal equality and a uniform administration, and secularised law.
- Many changes were limited: women gained few lasting rights, slavery was restored in 1802, and political freedom was ended by Napoleon.
- The ideas of liberty, equality and national sovereignty spread through France's armies and writings and encouraged liberalism and nationalism in Europe.
- Other powers saw the Revolution as a threat and fought France in coalitions, while reformers abroad were inspired.
- The revolt in Saint-Domingue produced independent Haiti in 1804; the Revolution also influenced independence movements in Spanish America.
:::
