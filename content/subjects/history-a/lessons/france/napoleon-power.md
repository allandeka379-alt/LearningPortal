## Introduction

Napoleon Bonaparte is one of the most studied rulers in history. He was a Corsican artillery officer who rose through the Revolution's armies to rule France, first as First Consul and then as Emperor. He claimed to protect the gains of the Revolution, and he did keep many of them (equality before the law, the end of feudalism, a career open to talent), but he governed as an authoritarian ruler who ended free elections and a free press. The central question of this lesson is how he rose and held power, and what he did with it at home. His wars and fall are in the next lesson.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plebiscite"}
A vote by the whole people (here, all adult men) on a single proposal, such as a new constitution. Napoleon used plebiscites to claim popular approval, although the results were managed and exaggerated.
:::

:::definition{term="Prefect"}
An official appointed by the central government to run a department (introduced in 1800). Prefects replaced elected local officials and made local government an arm of the state.
:::

:::definition{term="Concordat"}
An agreement between the French government and the Pope (1801) that settled the relationship between the state and the Catholic Church after the quarrels over the Civil Constitution of the Clergy.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1796-97: Bonaparte's Italian campaigns. 1798-99: the expedition to Egypt.
- 9 November 1799 (18 Brumaire): the coup that makes him First Consul.
- July 1801: the Concordat. March 1802: Peace of Amiens (lasted to May 1803).
- 1802: Consul for life. 1804: the Napoleonic Code is published; 2 December 1804: Napoleon crowned Emperor.
:::

## The rise of Napoleon

Napoleon was born on Corsica in 1769 and trained as an artillery officer. He came to notice in 1793 at the siege of Toulon, which was in royalist and British hands, and in October 1795 he helped to save the Convention, and so the new regime, by firing on the royalist rising in Paris. In 1796 he took command of the army in Italy, and over the next year his victories in Italy forced Austria to make the Treaty of Campo Formio (1797). He wrote his own despatches and had newspapers printed for the army and for France, which built his reputation. When the Directory sent him to Egypt in 1798 to threaten British trade with India, the campaign was a military failure after Nelson destroyed the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile, but Napoleon's account presented it as a triumph. He left his army in Egypt in 1799 and arrived in France in October.

By then the Directory was weak, with defeats abroad, divisions in the councils and little popular support. The politician Sieyès, a Director, wanted a change to the constitution and looked for a general to support it. On 9 November 1799 (**18 Brumaire**) the Councils were moved out of Paris to Saint-Cloud on the pretext of a Jacobin plot. Napoleon's brother Lucien, president of the Council of Five Hundred, and soldiers led by Murat drove out the hostile deputies. A handful of remaining deputies then named three Consuls: Bonaparte, Sieyès and Roger Ducos. Bonaparte soon made himself the real ruler.

## Authoritarian control

**The constitution.** The Constitution of the Year VIII (December 1799) gave the First Consul wide powers: he appointed ministers, officials and judges and alone proposed laws. A Council of State, filled by his nominees, drafted laws; the Tribunate debated them, the Legislative Body voted without debate and a Senate protected the constitution. The Tribunate was purged in 1802 and abolished in 1807. A plebiscite approved the constitution, and later ones approved Consul for life (August 1802) and the Empire (1804), each with a huge official majority that historians treat with caution, since the voting was managed.

**Local control.** In 1800 prefects were appointed to every department, with mayors also chosen by the government. Power came from the top, a system that lasted long after him.

**Police and censorship.** Fouché, as Minister of Police, ran a network of spies and informers, and Napoleon had rivals watched. The number of political newspapers was cut, and the survivors were censored; books and plays were checked. In 1804, after a royalist plot, the Duke of Enghien, a Bourbon prince, was seized abroad and shot, which warned opponents.

**Propaganda.** The state used newspapers (the *Moniteur*), paintings by artists such as David, bulletins from the army, public festivals and the Imperial Catechism taught in schools. The image of a strong, wise ruler restoring order was central.

**Winning support.** Napoleon also won loyalty by reward. The Legion of Honour (1802) honoured service to the state, and after 1804 he created a new imperial nobility based on service. Émigrés who accepted the regime were allowed to return after an amnesty in 1802, and he employed former revolutionaries and royalists alike.

:::example{title="Assessing how far Napoleon preserved the Revolution"}
A balanced paragraph can say: Napoleon kept equality before the law, the sale of Church and noble lands to new owners and careers open to talent. Yet he removed political freedom, because there were no free elections or press, ended the Revolution's local democracy and restored hereditary monarchy in 1804. A candidate who concludes that he kept the social gains and ended the political ones, giving evidence for each, writes a developed judgement.
:::

## The domestic reforms

**The Napoleonic Code (1804).** A committee of lawyers wrote a single civil code for France to replace the many local laws. It confirmed equality before the law, the end of feudal dues and personal serfdom, the right of property and freedom of religion. But it also gave husbands and fathers strong authority, limited the rights of married women, and restricted workers (employers' word was trusted over workers' in disputes, and workers' associations remained illegal). Criminal and commercial codes followed. The Code was later spread across much of Europe.

**Education.** Lycées (state secondary schools) were founded in 1802 for the sons of the elite, with a military flavour, and the Imperial University (1808) controlled teaching. Primary education remained limited and was left largely to the Church. The purpose was to train loyal officials and officers.

**Finance and the economy.** The Bank of France was set up in 1800 to help the state and to manage credit; the franc was stabilised in 1803 with a fixed metal value; taxes were collected by salaried officials, which made the revenue more regular. Public works, such as roads and canals, were built. Inflation was ended and the state had a stable base, though the army and the later wars were costly.

**Religion.** The **Concordat** of July 1801 with Pope Pius VII recognised Catholicism as the religion of the great majority of French people (not as a state religion), and the state paid the clergy. Bishops were nominated by the First Consul and confirmed by the Pope. The Pope accepted the loss of Church lands. In 1802 the "Organic Articles" added state controls without the Pope's agreement. The Concordat ended the religious quarrel, though Napoleon himself saw religion mainly as a tool of order; Protestants and Jews were given legal recognition.

:::example{title="Analysing a plebiscite"}
A plebiscite approving Napoleon as Consul for life returns a vast majority. A good answer notes what it shows (he wanted to appear to have popular consent), why it is unreliable (the counting was managed and there was no free debate) and what other evidence is needed (the police reports and the lack of organised opposition).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Napoleon was elected"}
He seized power by a coup. The plebiscites gave an appearance of consent, but the voting was managed and the results are not reliable evidence of free choice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the Code simply liberal"}
The Code kept equality and property but strengthened the authority of fathers and husbands and restricted workers. A good answer notes both.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2"}
Typical questions: "Why was Napoleon able to seize power in 1799?", "How did he establish control over France?", "How far did Napoleon preserve the achievements of the Revolution?" and "Assess the importance of the Concordat." Name the institutions, give dates, and always say whether a reform helped the state, the people or both. On Cambridge Paper 1 you may be given a source from Napoleon's circle: it will be propaganda, and you should say so.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Napoleon's military successes in Italy and the Directory's weaknesses allowed the coup of Brumaire in November 1799.
- The Constitution of the Year VIII gave him power as First Consul; later he became Consul for life (1802) and Emperor (1804).
- Control rested on prefects, the police under Fouché, censorship, propaganda and managed plebiscites, as well as honours and rewards.
- The Napoleonic Code, lycées, the Bank of France and the Concordat gave France order and stability.
- Napoleon kept the social gains of the Revolution, such as equality before the law, while ending political freedom.
:::
