## Introduction

The execution of Louis XVI on 21 January 1793 made reconciliation impossible. The Republic now faced war against much of Europe, revolt in the west, hunger in the towns and a quarrel at the heart of its own government. In response, the Convention built a system of emergency government that is called the Terror. A year after it reached its height, Robespierre was arrested and executed, and France spent the next five years under a cautious Directory that could neither end the war nor win lasting loyalty. This lesson explains how the Republic was governed in 1793-99, why the Terror was used, and why both the Convention and the Directory proved unstable.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="The Terror"}
The system of emergency government in 1793-94 in which suspects were arrested and tried by special courts, and many were executed, in the name of defending the Republic against internal and foreign enemies.
:::

:::definition{term="Sans-culottes"}
Working Parisians (artisans, shopkeepers, wage earners) who took part in the sectional assemblies and street politics. The name "without breeches" referred to their trousers, not the knee-breeches of the well-to-do.
:::

:::definition{term="Levée en masse"}
The decree of August 1793 that called all the nation to the war effort: young unmarried men to fight and the rest to supply and support the army.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 21 January 1793: Louis XVI executed. February-March 1793: Britain, Spain and the Dutch Republic at war with France; revolt in the Vendée begins.
- 31 May-2 June 1793: the Paris sections force the expulsion of the Girondin deputies.
- July 1793: Robespierre joins the Committee of Public Safety; 17 September: the Law of Suspects.
- 27 July 1794 (9 Thermidor): Robespierre arrested; executed 28 July.
- October 1795: the Directory begins. 4 September 1797 (18 Fructidor): the coup against the royalists.
- 9 November 1799 (18 Brumaire): the Directory overthrown.
:::

## Problems facing the Republic in 1793

**War.** After the king's execution Britain, Spain and the Dutch Republic joined Austria and Prussia in the First Coalition. The French army suffered defeats, and in April General Dumouriez deserted to the Austrians.

**Revolt.** In March 1793 a call for 300,000 recruits provoked a rising in the Vendée in the west, where peasants were angry about the Church reforms, conscription and the demands of the towns. Several southern cities later rose in the "federalist" revolts.

**Hunger and inflation.** The assignats had lost much of their value, bread and other goods were dear, and the sans-culottes demanded price controls and action against hoarders.

**Divided government.** The Girondins and the Montagnards (led by Robespierre, Danton and Marat) quarrelled over the war, the king and the power of Paris. After a rising of the Paris sections between 31 May and 2 June 1793, the Convention expelled 29 Girondin deputies.

## The Terror, 1793-94

The Convention created a set of institutions. A **Revolutionary Tribunal** (March 1793) tried political suspects. The **Committee of Public Safety**, set up in April 1793 and reorganised in July when Robespierre joined it, directed war and government; its members included Carnot (who organised the army), Saint-Just and Couthon. The **Committee of General Security** controlled the police. "Representatives on mission" travelled to the provinces with great powers. The **Law of Suspects** (17 September 1793) allowed the arrest of anyone thought hostile to the Revolution.

The **Constitution of 1793**, which promised universal male suffrage, was approved by a plebiscite but suspended in October, on the argument that government must remain "revolutionary until the peace". The **General Maximum** (September 1793) fixed prices of essential goods and, in time, wages. The levée en masse of August 1793 created an army of hundreds of thousands and brought victories. In the autumn the Republic adopted a new calendar and began a "dechristianisation" campaign, which Robespierre came to disapprove of.

Prominent people were executed: Marie Antoinette (October 1793), leading Girondins, Hébert and his supporters (March 1794) and, on 5 April 1794, Danton and his followers, who had urged an end to the Terror. After the Law of 22 Prairial (June 1794) removed safeguards from trials, the "Great Terror" brought many more executions in Paris.

**Numbers.** Historians estimate that the revolutionary courts passed about 16,000 to 17,000 death sentences, and that tens of thousands more people died in prison, in summary executions or in the repression of revolts. The war in the Vendée killed far more, with estimates varying widely.

:::example{title="Why was the Terror used?"}
An answer can weigh two views. One is that the Terror was a response to emergency: with foreign invasion, civil war and hunger, the Committee used harsh measures to save the Republic, and the army's recovery shows that it worked. Another is that it was a result of revolutionary ideology and fear: it continued after the military danger had passed (the victory at Fleurus came in June 1794, yet the Great Terror followed), which suggests that Robespierre and his colleagues also used it to remove opponents and to impose virtue. A balanced answer says that the emergency explains its origin, while ideology and factional fear help to explain why it grew.
:::

## Thermidor and its aftermath

The victories of 1794 made the emergency seem less urgent, and many deputies feared that they might be next on Robespierre's list. On **9 Thermidor Year II (27 July 1794)** deputies shouted down Robespierre and ordered his arrest; he was executed on 28 July with Saint-Just, Couthon and others. His successors are called the **Thermidorians**.

They closed the Jacobin club (November 1794), reformed the Revolutionary Tribunal and ended the Maximum in December 1794. Prices rose steeply and the winter of 1794-95 was hard. There was a **White Terror** in 1795, when royalists and others killed Jacobins and their sympathisers, especially in the south-east. Hunger brought risings in Paris (Germinal in April and Prairial in May 1795), which the army suppressed. In October 1795 (13 Vendémiaire) a royalist rising in Paris was defeated by troops under Barras, with the young General Napoleon Bonaparte directing the guns.

## The Directory, 1795-99

The **Constitution of the Year III** (1795) aimed to avoid both royal and mob power. Executive power belonged to five **Directors**, chosen by the legislature; the legislature was in two councils (the Five Hundred, which proposed laws, and the Ancients, who accepted or rejected them); the vote was restricted by a property test; and one third of the councils retired each year. The Convention decreed that two thirds of the new deputies must come from its own members.

**Successes.** The Directory held the Republic together for four years. Armies won victories: Bonaparte's Italian campaign of 1796-97 ended in the Treaty of Campo Formio (October 1797), and conquered territories provided money. The currency was stabilised after the collapse of the assignat and the repudiation of two thirds of the public debt in 1797, a bankruptcy that cleared some of the burden. The Directory also revived local administration and education.

**Failures.** The regime had no firm support. Royalists and Jacobins conspired (the Conspiracy of the Equals under Babeuf was crushed in 1796). When elections returned deputies the Directors disliked, they used the army to cancel results: the coup of **18 Fructidor** (4 September 1797) annulled royalist gains, and in 1798 and 1799 other elections were set aside or Directors forced out. Corruption discredited the government, and renewed war in 1798-99 brought defeats while Bonaparte was away in Egypt. Many people wanted a strong government, and on 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799) Bonaparte seized power.

:::example{title="Using an exact date in an answer"}
Instead of writing "later Robespierre fell", write "on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794), a month after the victory at Fleurus, deputies who feared the Law of 22 Prairial turned on him". The date, the cause and the link to the Terror's later phase show knowledge and analysis together.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the Terror as the work of Robespierre alone"}
The Committee of Public Safety had twelve members, the Convention voted the laws, the representatives on mission and local committees made many arrests, and the sans-culottes pressed for it. Robespierre was important but not a dictator.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2"}
Typical questions: "Why was the Terror introduced?", "How far was Robespierre responsible for the Terror?", "Why did the Directory fail?" and "How well was France governed, 1793-99?" Use precise institutions and dates, separate the aims from the results, and give a judgement that weighs emergency and ideology. Cambridge Paper 1 may supply sources from Robespierre's circle or from opponents; remember that both are partisan.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After Louis XVI's execution the Republic faced a coalition, revolt in the Vendée and the south, hunger and divided leaders.
- The Committee of Public Safety, the Revolutionary Tribunal, the Law of Suspects and the Maximum made up the emergency system called the Terror.
- The army was reorganised and victories came by 1794; the Terror continued into the Great Terror of June-July 1794.
- Robespierre fell on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794); the Thermidorians ended the Maximum, and a White Terror and hunger risings followed.
- The Directory (1795-99) won victories and stabilised the currency, but depended on coups and the army, and fell to Bonaparte in November 1799.
:::
