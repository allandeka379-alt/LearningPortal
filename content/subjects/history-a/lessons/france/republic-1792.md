## Introduction

In October 1789 almost nobody in France called for a republic. Three years later the king was a prisoner, the monarchy had been abolished and the National Convention had proclaimed the French Republic. The question this lesson answers is how a revolution that began as an attempt to give France a constitutional king ended in the fall of the king himself. The answer lies in the reforms of the Constituent Assembly, which created new enemies; in the king's own conduct; and in the war that began in April 1792.

You should already know the events of 1789 from the previous lesson. The Terror and the war after 1792 are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Constitutional monarchy"}
A system in which a king keeps his throne but his powers are limited by a constitution and an elected assembly. The Constitution of 1791 set up such a system in France.
:::

:::definition{term="Active citizen"}
Under the Constitution of 1791, a man who paid taxes worth at least three days' local wages and so could vote in the first stage of elections. Men who paid less (and all women) were "passive" citizens with civil but not full political rights.
:::

:::definition{term="Refractory priest"}
A priest who refused to swear the oath of loyalty to the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790). Those who swore were "constitutional" priests.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1789-91: assignats, 83 departments, the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790).
- 20-21 June 1791: the flight to Varennes. 17 July 1791: the Champ de Mars shooting.
- September 1791: the Constitution is completed; the Legislative Assembly meets on 1 October.
- 20 April 1792: France declares war on Austria.
- 10 August 1792: the Tuileries is stormed; the king is suspended. 2-6 September: the September Massacres.
- 20 September 1792: Valmy and the first meeting of the National Convention; 21-22 September: monarchy abolished, republic declared.
:::

## The reforms of the Constituent Assembly

Between 1789 and 1791 the National Assembly (called the Constituent Assembly because it wrote a constitution) rebuilt France.

**Finance.** The state still needed money. In November 1789 the Assembly took over the lands of the Church; the **assignats**, bonds issued from December 1789 and secured on those lands, soon circulated as paper money. They helped the government at first, but as more were printed they lost value. The old indirect taxes such as the gabelle were abolished and new taxes on land and property were created, but collection was poor.

**Local government.** France was divided into 83 *départements*, themselves subdivided, with elected councils. This replaced the old provinces with their different customs and privileges and gave citizens a share in local decisions, although it also gave the new officials a heavy burden.

**Justice.** The parlements and lettres de cachet were abolished; judges were elected; trial by jury was introduced for serious crimes; torture was ended. The system was more equal, but inexperience caused confusion in some places.

**The Church.** Church land was nationalised and monastic vows were ended. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** (July 1790) made priests and bishops elected, paid by the state and independent of Rome in practice. When priests were ordered in November 1790 to swear an oath to it, about half refused, and in 1791 the Pope condemned the reform. This divided Catholic France, made enemies of many peasants in regions such as the west, and gave the counter-revolution a cause.

**Other changes.** Noble titles were abolished in 1790. Guilds were abolished in 1791, and the Le Chapelier law of June 1791 banned workers' associations and strikes, which angered workers who had hoped the Revolution would help them. Many historians stress that these reforms created a more rational and equal state, and that they also created new divisions.

## Revolutionary and counter-revolutionary groups

| Group | Who | Aims |
|---|---|---|
| Feuillants | Moderates such as Barnave and Lafayette, who left the Jacobins in July 1791 | Keep the king and the Constitution of 1791; end the Revolution |
| Jacobins | Members of the Society of the Friends of the Constitution, a political club with branches across France | Protect the gains of the Revolution; became more radical as the king lost trust |
| Girondins (Brissotins) | Deputies such as Brissot, many from the Gironde | Spread the Revolution by war; suspicious of the king |
| Cordeliers and sans-culottes | Paris clubs and workers' sections, with Danton and Marat among their allies | Direct action; democracy; cheaper bread; a republic |
| Counter-revolutionaries | Émigré nobles who left France (including the king's brother, the comte d'Artois), refractory priests, loyal peasants | Restore the king's power and the Church; help from foreign monarchs |

The counter-revolutionaries were not able to defeat the Revolution before 1792. The émigrés gathered abroad and sought help from Austria and Prussia (the Declaration of Pillnitz of August 1791 was a conditional threat), but they were divided, and their efforts made the Revolution more suspicious of the king and the nobility. In France the refractory priests had support in the west and south but no single leader or army.

## Instability, 1791

The king never accepted the Revolution. On the night of 20-21 June 1791 he, the queen and their children left Paris in disguise for the eastern frontier; they were recognised and stopped at **Varennes** and brought back. The flight destroyed trust. It left a declaration against the Revolution, and it suggested that the king could not be a loyal constitutional monarch. The Assembly claimed that he had been abducted so that the constitutional monarchy could survive, but many people now began to speak of a republic.

On 17 July 1791 a crowd gathered on the Champ de Mars in Paris to sign a petition for the king's removal. The National Guard under Lafayette fired on them and killed a number of people (historians say about fifty). The shooting split the Jacobins, produced the Feuillants and left the left in Paris bitter against Lafayette and the moderates.

The Constitution of 1791 made France a constitutional monarchy. A **Legislative Assembly** of 745 members was elected by the active citizens (the tax qualifications meant that millions of men could not take full part), and the king kept a suspensive veto. The Assembly met on 1 October 1791. It was made up of men with no experience of the Constituent Assembly, since no former member could be elected.

## War and the fall of the monarchy

War became the central issue. The Girondins argued that war would unite the people and spread liberty; the king and queen, for different reasons, also wanted war, since the king hoped defeat would bring back his power. France declared war on Austria on **20 April 1792**. The French army, weakened by the flight of many noble officers, suffered early defeats and Prussia joined Austria. In July the Brunswick Manifesto, issued by the Prussian commander of the invading army, threatened Paris with destruction if the royal family was harmed. When it was published it enraged Parisians, who suspected the king of treason.

On 10 August 1792 the Paris sections and volunteers (fédérés) from Marseille and other cities attacked the Tuileries; the Swiss Guard was overwhelmed and many were killed. The Legislative Assembly suspended the king and called a National Convention, to be elected by all adult men. In the first week of September, as the Prussian army advanced and fear of a prison plot spread, crowds in Paris killed more than a thousand prisoners in the **September Massacres**. On 20 September the French army stopped the invaders at **Valmy**, and the same day the Convention met. On 21 September it abolished the monarchy and on 22 September it declared the Republic.

:::example{title="Explaining why the Republic came about"}
A strong explanation joins three steps. First, the Church reform and the king's conduct made enemies of the Revolution (about half the priests refused the oath, and the flight to Varennes showed that the king did not accept the constitution). Second, the war turned this into a crisis: defeats and the Brunswick Manifesto made the king look like a traitor. Third, the people of Paris acted on 10 August, and the Assembly could no longer defend the monarchy. A candidate who adds that few people in 1789 wanted a republic, and that it came out of events rather than a plan, shows the contingent nature of the change.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing the Church reform"}
To assess the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, set the aim (to bring the Church under the state and pay for it) against the result (about half the priests refused the oath, the Pope condemned it, and a loyal Catholic population turned against the Revolution). A judgement could be that it was a reform that created an enemy the Revolution never overcame.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Constituent and Legislative Assemblies"}
The Constituent Assembly (1789-91) wrote the Constitution of 1791. The Legislative Assembly (October 1791 to September 1792) sat under it. The National Convention (from September 1792) followed and declared the republic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2"}
Typical questions are "Why did the monarchy fall in 1792?", "How far did the Church reforms weaken the Revolution?" and "Explain the aims of the Jacobins and the Girondins." On Cambridge Paper 1 you may be given sources on Varennes or the Champ de Mars to compare. Use dates, name the groups accurately, and link causes (the flight, war, 10 August) in a chain rather than a list.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Constituent Assembly reformed finance, local government, justice and the Church, creating a more equal state but also new enemies.
- The Civil Constitution of the Clergy divided Catholics; about half of priests refused the oath.
- The flight to Varennes in June 1791 destroyed trust in the king; the Champ de Mars shooting split the left.
- War against Austria (April 1792), defeats and the Brunswick Manifesto led to the attack on the Tuileries on 10 August 1792.
- The Convention abolished the monarchy on 21 September and proclaimed the Republic on 22 September 1792.
:::
