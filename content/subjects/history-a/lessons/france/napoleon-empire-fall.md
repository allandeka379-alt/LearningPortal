## Introduction

Napoleon ruled France for fifteen years and spent most of them at war. His victories created an empire that stretched from the Atlantic to the borders of Russia, and in 1814 he was forced to abdicate after the armies of four great powers entered Paris. The lesson has two aims. The first, needed by ZIMSEC candidates, is to describe his foreign policy and explain what his rule did to France and Europe. The second, needed by both boards, is to explain why he fell. Cambridge asks only for the reasons for his fall, with military campaigns in outline, so the order of campaigns is here as a framework rather than as a list to memorise.


## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Continental System"}
Napoleon's plan to defeat Britain by closing the ports of Europe to British trade (the Berlin Decree of November 1806 and the Milan Decrees of 1807), because he could not defeat the Royal Navy at sea.
:::

:::definition{term="Grand Empire"}
The name for the territories Napoleon ruled directly or through relatives and allies in Europe at its height (about 1810-12): France itself, annexed lands such as Belgium and the Rhineland, satellite kingdoms and allied states.
:::

:::definition{term="Guerrilla"}
A "little war": fighting by small bands of irregular fighters who attack supplies and isolated units. The word comes from the Spanish resistance to the French from 1808.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 21 October 1805: Trafalgar (Nelson destroys the French and Spanish fleet). 2 December 1805: Austerlitz.
- November 1806: the Berlin Decree begins the Continental System. July 1807: the Treaties of Tilsit.
- 1808-14: the Peninsular War (Spain and Portugal; Wellington).
- June 1812: the invasion of Russia; Borodino 7 September; Moscow entered 14 September.
- 16-19 October 1813: Leipzig. 31 March 1814: allies enter Paris. April 1814: abdication; exile on Elba.
- March-June 1815: the Hundred Days; 18 June 1815: Waterloo; exile on St Helena.
:::

## Napoleon's wars, 1800-1807

After Marengo (June 1800) Napoleon made peace with Austria and, in March 1802, with Britain at Amiens. The peace broke down in May 1803, and war with Britain continued until 1814. His plan to invade Britain ended at **Trafalgar** (21 October 1805), where Nelson destroyed the French and Spanish fleet. After that Britain commanded the seas, and Napoleon could attack it only by economic war.

On land he was almost always victorious. At **Austerlitz** (2 December 1805) he defeated Russia and Austria, and in 1806 Napoleon formed the Confederation of the Rhine, and the Holy Roman Empire, headed by the Austrian emperor, came to an end. He defeated Prussia at Jena and Auerstedt in October 1806, and fought Russia at Eylau and Friedland. By the Treaties of **Tilsit** (July 1807) Russia became his ally and Prussia lost half its territory. At this moment Napoleon dominated continental Europe.

## The Grand Empire

France was enlarged by annexations (Belgium, the left bank of the Rhine, parts of Italy). Beyond them Napoleon set up satellite states under relatives: his brother Joseph became king of Naples and then of Spain, Louis king of Holland, Jérôme king of Westphalia. He introduced the Code, ended feudal dues and reformed administration in many of these lands, but the empire also served France: the lands paid taxes, supplied soldiers, had to follow the Continental System and lost works of art.

## The causes of Napoleon's fall

Historians offer several causes, and a good answer weighs them.

**1. The Continental System.** Napoleon hoped that closing Europe to British goods would ruin Britain. It did not, since Britain traded with the wider world and with smugglers, but the System hurt France and its allies, raised prices and made enforcing it an excuse for more wars. Russia, which depended on British trade, left the System in 1810, which led to the invasion of 1812.

**2. The Peninsular War (1808-14).** In 1808 Napoleon removed the Spanish king and placed his brother Joseph on the throne. Spaniards rose in revolt (the Madrid rising of 2 May 1808), fought a guerrilla war and, with a British army under Wellington, tied down hundreds of thousands of French troops for years. Napoleon later called it the "Spanish ulcer".

**3. Nationalism.** French rule at first brought reform, but conscription, taxes and French troops made foreign rule hated. Spaniards, Germans and Italians began to see themselves as peoples with a right to freedom, and in Prussia reformers rebuilt the army and a patriotic movement grew.

**4. Russia, 1812.** In June 1812 the Grande Armée, with allied troops probably over half a million men, crossed into Russia. The Russians retreated, drawing it deep into the country and burning supplies. After the bloody battle of Borodino (7 September) Napoleon entered Moscow on 14 September, but the city burned and the Tsar would not make peace. In October the army began a long retreat in which hunger, disease, cold and attacks destroyed it. Historians' estimates of losses vary widely, but most agree that only a small part of the army returned. The disaster encouraged Prussia, Austria and others to rise.

**5. Leipzig and the invasion of France (1813-14).** At Leipzig (16-19 October 1813), the "Battle of the Nations", Napoleon was heavily outnumbered by Russian, Austrian, Prussian and Swedish troops and lost; his German allies deserted. The allies, having agreed at Chaumont in March 1814 to remain united, entered France, and on 31 March 1814 they occupied Paris. His marshals refused to fight on. Napoleon abdicated in April 1814 and was sent to the island of Elba.

**6. Loss of support at home.** From 1810 France suffered an economic crisis, conscription took ever more young men, and the middle classes and the army wanted peace.

**7. Napoleon's own mistakes.** Many historians stress that his decisions in Spain and Russia, and his refusal to accept peace terms in 1813, reflected overconfidence and overstretch.

:::example{title="Weighing causes in an essay"}
Rather than listing the seven causes, group them and rank them. For example: "The invasion of Russia was the turning point, because it destroyed the army and encouraged his enemies to unite; but it was itself the result of the Continental System, which Russia refused to obey. The Peninsular War drained his armies earlier, and nationalism turned subject peoples against him. The allies' unity after 1813 finally ended him." This links causes in a chain and reaches a judgement.
:::

## The Hundred Days and the final defeat

Louis XVIII was restored. Napoleon escaped from Elba in March 1815, and the army went over to him, but the powers declared him an outlaw. He was defeated at **Waterloo** on 18 June 1815 by the armies of Wellington and Blücher, abdicated again and was exiled to the island of St Helena in the South Atlantic, where he died in 1821.

## The impact on France and Europe

For France: the Code, the prefects, the Concordat and the Bank of France remained, and the monarchy was restored in 1814-15 without the old privileges. The wars cost many lives (many historians put the French dead at perhaps a million or more) and ended with France reduced to its borders of 1790. For Europe: the end of the Holy Roman Empire, the redrawing of borders, the spread of the Code, the end of feudalism in many places and the growth of nationalism and liberalism, which shaped the settlement at Vienna and the unification movements of Germany and Italy.

:::example{title="A chain of causes"}
Link the causes: the Continental System, which failed to ruin Britain, pushed Napoleon into Spain and then Russia; the Russian disaster of 1812 gave Prussia and Austria the chance to turn on him in 1813; and the allied unity of 1814 finished the work. Showing the chain is worth more than a list.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming the winter alone for Russia"}
The Russian retreat, scorched earth, distance, disease and the Russian army were all factors. Weather alone does not explain a defeat that began before the cold.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Trafalgar and Waterloo"}
Trafalgar (1805) was a naval defeat of the French and Spanish by Nelson. Waterloo (1815) was a land defeat by Wellington and Blücher.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2"}
Questions use "Why did Napoleon fall?" and "How far was the Russian campaign responsible for his defeat?" or "Assess the impact of Napoleon on Europe." For "how far", defend a ranking of causes and show links. Cambridge Paper 2 has a part (a) of 10 marks asking you to explain the reasons for the fall, and a part (b) of 20 marks asking you to judge, so prepare a ranked list with evidence. Do not narrate every battle.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Napoleon defeated his continental enemies until 1807 but could not defeat Britain after Trafalgar.
- The Continental System, the Peninsular War and the rise of nationalism wore down his empire.
- The Russian campaign of 1812 destroyed his army; at Leipzig in 1813 he was beaten, and the allies entered Paris on 31 March 1814.
- He abdicated in April 1814, returned in the Hundred Days and was defeated at Waterloo on 18 June 1815.
- His rule left the Code and the end of feudalism in Europe, and nationalism that would reshape the continent.
:::
