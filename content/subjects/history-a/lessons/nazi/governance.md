## Introduction

Once Hitler had destroyed the opposition in 1933-34, he had to keep control of eighty million people and to deliver on his promises to overturn the Treaty of Versailles. The Nazi state governed through a mixture of terror, persuasion and success. It frightened those who resisted, shaped the beliefs of those who did not, and won applause for foreign policy triumphs before it led Germany into war. This lesson explains how that mixture worked, how much opposition there was, and what Nazi foreign policy aimed to do.

You should know from the previous lesson how Hitler became Führer in August 1934.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Totalitarian state"}
A state that tries to control not only government but the whole of public life and private belief. The Nazi claim to be a totalitarian state was never fully realised, because the regime relied on the churches, the army and business, and ordinary people found private spaces.
:::

:::definition{term="Lebensraum"}
"Living space": Hitler's aim of winning land in eastern Europe, especially in the Soviet Union, for German settlement and food, a goal bound up with racial ideas.
:::

:::definition{term="Appeasement"}
The policy, followed by Britain and France in the 1930s, of making concessions to Germany to avoid war. The causes of the war are the subject of the lesson on the Second World War.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1933: Goebbels heads the Ministry of Propaganda; Dachau opens; Germany leaves the League of Nations (October). 1934: the People's Court.
- 1936: Himmler becomes head of the German police; the Berlin Olympics (August); Rhineland remilitarised (March).
- 1938: Anschluss (March) and Munich Agreement (September). 1939: Nazi-Soviet Pact (23 August); invasion of Poland (1 September).
- 22 June 1941: Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the USSR.
:::

## The terror state

**The SS and the police.** The *Schutzstaffel* (SS), begun as Hitler's bodyguard, grew under Heinrich Himmler (its head from 1929) into a state within the state. In 1936 Himmler was made chief of the German police, and he joined the SS to the police, with Reinhard Heydrich running the security police. The **Gestapo**, the secret state police first created by Göring in Prussia in 1933, could arrest without trial. The SD (*Sicherheitsdienst*) was the party's intelligence service.

**Camps.** Dachau opened in March 1933, at first for communists and socialists. Over the 1930s the camps held political opponents, and later Jews, Roma, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses and people the regime called "asocial". After 1936 they were run by the SS.

**Courts.** Judges were coordinated, many were already conservative, and in 1934 the People's Court was set up to try treason cases. The law was subordinated to the will of the Führer.

**How far was it terror?** The Gestapo was smaller than people imagined. Historians such as Robert Gellately have shown that it relied heavily on denunciations from ordinary Germans, who sometimes informed on neighbours for personal reasons. This suggests that the regime's power rested on cooperation as well as fear, though those who were targeted, such as the political opposition and the Jews, saw only terror.

## Propaganda and control of culture

In March 1933 Hitler created the Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda under **Joseph Goebbels**. In September the Reich Chamber of Culture required writers, artists, musicians and broadcasters to join, so that those who were not approved could not work. Newspapers were controlled by the party and the Editors' Law of 1933. On 10 May 1933 students and Nazi activists burned thousands of books by Jewish, socialist and other authors. Radio was made cheap with the "People's Receiver" so that speeches could reach every home, and film included works by Leni Riefenstahl such as *Triumph of the Will* (1935), a record of the Nuremberg rally. The Nuremberg rallies each September showed order, mass loyalty and Hitler's leadership. The Berlin Olympics of August 1936 were used to show a peaceful and efficient Germany to visitors, and the regime quietly softened its anti-Jewish displays for the duration.

The messages were few: loyalty to the Führer, national unity (*Volksgemeinschaft*, the "people's community"), hatred of Jews and Bolsheviks, and the restoration of German honour. Propaganda worked best when it reinforced beliefs people already held, such as hostility to Versailles, and when it was backed by success. It worked less well when it contradicted experience, for example when the war went badly. Historians such as Ian Kershaw have argued that the "Hitler myth", the image of a leader above the daily failures of the party, was among the regime's strongest assets.

## Opposition

Opposition was real but divided and mostly weak.

- **The left.** Communists and Social Democrats were destroyed in 1933-34, though small groups kept working underground and the Social Democrats had an exile leadership in Prague.
- **The churches.** Pastor Martin Niemöller and the Confessing Church resisted Nazi control of the Protestant Church. The Catholic Church condemned some Nazi ideas in the encyclical *Mit brennender Sorge* (1937), and Bishop von Galen protested publicly in 1941 against the killing of disabled people. Most church leaders did not oppose the regime's treatment of the Jews.
- **Young people.** The Edelweiss Pirates and the "Swing" youth rejected Hitler Youth discipline. The White Rose group at Munich University, led by Hans and Sophie Scholl, distributed leaflets in 1942-43 and its members were executed.
- **The army and the elite.** Some officers, among them Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg, planned to kill Hitler; the bomb plot of 20 July 1944 failed. An earlier attempt had been made by a lone carpenter, Georg Elser, in November 1939.
- **Passive resistance.** Many people avoided the Hitler salute, told jokes, listened to foreign radio or helped Jews to hide.

Why was it so weak? The terror made open protest deadly; many Germans approved of the regime's economic and foreign successes; the opposition groups were split and the army's oath bound its officers; and there was no organisation able to unite resistance.

## Nazi foreign policy

**Aims.** Hitler's aims were to destroy the Treaty of Versailles, to unite all Germans in one state (including Austria and the German-speaking parts of Czechoslovakia and Poland), to win *Lebensraum* in the east, and to destroy what he called Jewish Bolshevism. Historians disagree whether he followed a plan or was an opportunist. The "intentionalist" view is that he followed a programme set out in *Mein Kampf*; in 1961 A.J.P. Taylor argued that he was an opportunist who took advantage of other states' mistakes. Most historians today accept that the aims were consistent but the methods and timing responded to chances.

![Timeline of Nazi foreign policy: leaving the League in October 1933, conscription in 1935, the Rhineland in 1936, the Anschluss and Munich in 1938, the Nazi-Soviet Pact and the invasion of Poland in 1939 and Barbarossa on 22 June 1941.](media:diagrams/nazi-foreign-policy-timeline.png "Figure: timeline of Nazi foreign policy, 1933-1941")

**Methods.** Hitler combined demands for equality with threats, quick surprises and the divisions among Britain, France and Italy.

|---|---|

| January 1934 | Non-aggression pact with Poland |

| March 1935 | Conscription announced, breaking Versailles |

| March 1936 | German troops enter the Rhineland |

| September 1938 | Munich Agreement: the Sudetenland |

| 23 August 1939 | Nazi-Soviet Pact |

| 22 June 1941 | Invasion of the Soviet Union (Barbarossa) |

:::example{title="Was Nazi foreign policy consistent?"}
Evidence of consistency: Hitler wanted to undo Versailles, unite Germans and win land in the east from the 1920s, and each step in the 1930s moved towards this. Evidence of opportunism: the Austrian Anschluss was hurried forward in March 1938 rather than carried out to a timetable, the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 1939 contradicted his hatred of Bolshevism, and the war with Britain and France in 1939 came sooner than he expected. A balanced answer says that the aims were consistent while the means and the timing were flexible.
:::

:::example{title="Analysing a propaganda image"}
Take a poster that shows Hitler standing above a crowd of smiling workers. Ask four questions. What is the message? Loyalty to a strong leader and national unity. Who is the audience? Ordinary Germans, especially workers. What is the purpose? To build the Hitler myth and a sense of belonging. What is missing? Any sign of the people excluded from the community, or of the terror. This shows that propaganda must be judged for what it leaves out as well as what it says.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing about the terror as if everyone lived in constant fear"}
Many Germans experienced the regime as order, jobs and national pride. Explain who was most affected by the terror and why many others complied.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Anschluss with the Munich Agreement"}
The Anschluss (March 1938) was the union of Germany and Austria. The Munich Agreement (September 1938) gave Germany the Sudetenland of Czechoslovakia. Learn the sequence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the July Plot as the only resistance"}
The plot of 1944 was an act by a small group of officers late in the war. The resistance included workers, churchmen, young people and individuals acting from conscience across the whole period.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4"}
Typical questions: "How effective was propaganda in Nazi Germany?", "Why was there so little opposition to Hitler?", "To what extent was Nazi foreign policy a continuation of earlier German aims?" Use named institutions and dates, separate terror from persuasion, and for foreign policy weigh aims against opportunism. For "how effectively" questions, say what the regime wanted, what it achieved and what limited it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Nazi state combined terror (SS, Gestapo, courts, camps) with propaganda controlled by Goebbels and much popular cooperation.
- Propaganda used rallies, radio, film and the Berlin Olympics to build the Hitler myth and the idea of a people's community.
- Opposition came from the left, churches, young people and some officers, but it was divided, watched and rarely effective.
- Hitler's foreign policy aimed to destroy Versailles, unite Germans and win Lebensraum in the east.
- The sequence ran from leaving the League (1933) through the Rhineland, the Anschluss and Munich to the Pact and the invasion of Poland (1939).
:::
