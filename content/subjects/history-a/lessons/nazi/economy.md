## Introduction

When Hitler became Chancellor, about six million Germans were registered as unemployed. The Nazis had promised "work and bread", and a regime that could not deliver would have lost its support. By 1936 unemployment had fallen sharply and by 1939 Germany faced a shortage of workers. The recovery was one of the Nazis' strongest claims to credit, and historians ask how much of it was their own doing, how much came from the world economy, and what it cost. The story also has a lesson for students in Zimbabwe, where debates about economic recovery, import controls and living standards are part of the national history: the aims of economic policy cannot be separated from its price.

You should know from the first lesson of this topic how the Depression helped to bring the Nazis to power.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Autarky"}
Economic self-sufficiency: producing at home what a country needs so as not to depend on imports. For the Nazis it was linked to the plan for a war in which Germany could not be blockaded as in 1914-18.
:::

:::definition{term="Deficit financing"}
Government spending greater than its income, paid for by borrowing or creating credit, used to create jobs. The Nazi government used it for public works and rearmament, hiding part of it in the Mefo bills of 1934.
:::

:::definition{term="Guns or butter"}
The choice between spending resources on armaments and on goods for consumers. The phrase describes the tension in Nazi policy after 1936.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1933: Hjalmar Schacht becomes President of the Reichsbank; the Reich Labour Service and the autobahn programme begin; trade unions replaced by the German Labour Front (May); the Reich Food Estate and Reich Entailed Farm Law (September).
- 1934: Schacht Minister of Economics; the New Plan (September).
- 1935: conscription and compulsory Reich Labour Service for young men.
- 1936: the Four Year Plan under Göring (announced in October). 1937: Schacht resigns.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge key question 3.3 asks "How successful were Nazi economic policies?" and expects a judgement about aims, achievements and costs. ZIMSEC expects the main policies and their effects on workers, farmers and the preparation for war.
:::

## Dealing with the Depression: Schacht and public works

Hjalmar Schacht, a banker and a conservative who was not a Nazi in his ideas, became President of the Reichsbank in March 1933 and Minister of Economics in August 1934. He used **deficit financing** to pay for large government spending. The state created work through the following measures.

- **Public works.** Money was spent on roads, canals, housing, repairs and reforestation. The building of the *autobahns* (motorways) began in September 1933 under Fritz Todt; they were presented as a symbol of the new Germany, though the number of workers was smaller than the propaganda suggested and the roads served the military as well as the motorist.
- **The Reich Labour Service** (RAD). Young men did manual labour on farms and projects, at first voluntarily and, from 1935, compulsorily for six months, with low pay and army-style discipline.
- **Rearmament.** From 1934 the state spent heavily on the army, navy and air force, at first secretly. The Mefo bills, a device by which the government paid contractors with bills guaranteed by a dummy company, kept the cost out of the open budget.
- **Conscription**, introduced in March 1935, took young men from the labour market.

Recovery had begun in 1932, as the world economy slowly emerged from the slump, so the Nazis inherited some of the upturn. Yet their spending hastened it.

## The New Plan and the Four Year Plan

By 1934 Germany was running short of the foreign currency it needed to buy raw materials. Schacht's **New Plan** (September 1934) controlled imports by licence and made trade agreements with countries in south-eastern Europe and South America, which supplied food and raw materials in exchange for goods in a system of barter.

Hitler, however, wanted a quicker pace of rearmament and a self-sufficient economy. In 1936 he wrote a secret memorandum saying that the army must be ready for war within four years and the economy must be prepared for it. The **Four Year Plan**, announced in October 1936 and put under Hermann Göring, aimed to increase home production of synthetic oil and rubber, iron ore and substitutes ("ersatz" goods), and to control prices and wages. Schacht objected that it risked breaking the economy and resigned as Minister of Economics in November 1937. The plan did not achieve autarky: Germany still depended on imported oil, iron ore and some foodstuffs in 1939. The result was a constant shortage and a contest between military spending and consumer goods: "guns or butter".

## Agriculture

The Nazis idealised the peasant as the keeper of German "blood and soil". The **Reich Food Estate**, led by Walther Darré, organised all farmers and set prices and quotas. The **Reich Entailed Farm Law** (September 1933) protected medium-sized hereditary farms from being sold or divided or seized for debt, so owners were secured but could not borrow against their land. Farm debts were eased and prices were raised. But the drift of workers from the land to the cities and factories continued, and farmers resented the controls.

## Employment, wages and living standards

| Year | Registered unemployed (approximate) |
|---|---|
| 1932 | about 5.6 million |
| 1933 | about 4.8 million |
| 1936 | about 1.6 million |
| 1938 | about 0.4 million |

Official figures overstate the recovery, because they leave out some groups. Jews and political opponents who lost their jobs were not counted, many women were pushed out of employment, men in the RAD and the army were not counted as unemployed, and those in the camps do not appear.

**Workers.** In May 1933 the trade unions were abolished and replaced by the **German Labour Front** (DAF) under Robert Ley, which could not bargain for wages or strike. Wage rates were frozen at about the level of the Depression; a longer working week and overtime raised take-home pay, but real hourly wages stayed close to or below the level of 1929. Workers could not change jobs freely. The DAF's **Strength through Joy** (KdF) organised cheap holidays, cruises, theatre and sport, and its **Beauty of Labour** programme improved canteens and factory conditions. Many workers enjoyed such benefits, though the poorest could seldom afford the holidays. The **Volkswagen** ("people's car") scheme invited people to save for a car, but the factory made military vehicles when war came and none of the savers received a car.

**Business.** Large firms did well from rearmament contracts, while small shopkeepers and craftsmen, to whom the party had made promises, found that large department stores survived and taxes and controls rose. Industrialists had to accept state direction under the Four Year Plan, and companies such as IG Farben benefited.

:::example{title="Assessing the success of Nazi economic policy"}
Evidence of success: unemployment fell from about six million to under half a million, industrial output rose, and the regime showed it was able to act. Evidence of weakness: the recovery rested on rearmament and borrowing; autarky was not achieved; consumer goods were scarce; wages were low and workers had lost their unions; and by 1939 the economy was set on a path to war which needed conquest to pay for it. A balanced answer says that the policies were highly successful in the narrow aim of reducing unemployment and building arms, but unbalanced and dependent on war.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a statistic with care"}
The fall in registered unemployment from about 5.6 million (1932) to about 0.4 million (1938) looks like a complete recovery. A careful candidate asks what the figure counts. Jews and political opponents dismissed from work, women encouraged to leave jobs, men in the Labour Service and army, and prisoners were all excluded. The figure still shows a real fall, but a smaller one than the regime claimed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Crediting the Nazis with the whole of the recovery"}
The world economy began to recover in 1932. Nazi policies sped the recovery up, and tilted it towards armaments, but they did not start it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unemployment figures without question"}
The official figures leave out Jews, women pushed out of work, men in the labour service and army, and prisoners. Always say that they overstate the fall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the New Plan and the Four Year Plan"}
The New Plan (1934, Schacht) controlled trade; the Four Year Plan (1936, Göring) aimed at autarky and war readiness. Schacht fell because he opposed the second.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4"}
Typical questions: "How far did Nazi policies reduce unemployment?", "Assess the aims and results of the Four Year Plan", "Did workers benefit from Nazi rule?" Give figures with the word "about", set aims against results, and always note what the figures hide. For "how successful", judge by the regime's own aims: full employment, rearmament and autarky.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Schacht used public works, the Reich Labour Service, deficit financing and hidden rearmament to cut unemployment sharply.
- The New Plan (1934) controlled imports; the Four Year Plan (1936) pursued autarky and war readiness under Göring, and Schacht resigned in 1937.
- Farmers were protected by the Reich Entailed Farm Law and controlled by the Reich Food Estate, but the drift from the land went on.
- Workers lost their unions and were enrolled in the Labour Front; wages rose little, but Strength through Joy offered leisure benefits.
- Official unemployment figures overstate the recovery, and the economy depended on armaments and, in the end, war.
:::
