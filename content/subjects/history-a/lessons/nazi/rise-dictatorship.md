## Introduction

On 30 January 1933 Adolf Hitler became Chancellor of Germany. Eighteen months later he was head of government and head of state, with no party, trade union, free press or independent court able to check him. The path from a small Munich party to a dictatorship over one of Europe's most advanced states raises a question that matters well beyond Germany: how can a democracy be destroyed from within, often by legal-looking steps? Students ask what makes a constitution safe and what makes it fragile.

You should know from the earlier lessons how the Weimar Republic was founded in 1919 and how it suffered from the Treaty of Versailles, hyperinflation in 1923 and the divisions of its own politicians.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Article 48"}
The article of the Weimar Constitution that allowed the President to rule by emergency decree when public order was threatened. After 1930 it was used to govern without a Reichstag majority, which weakened the habit of parliamentary government.
:::

:::definition{term="Gleichschaltung"}
"Coordination" or "bringing into line": the Nazi process of 1933-34 by which states, parties, trade unions, clubs and professions were placed under Nazi control.
:::

:::definition{term="Enabling Act"}
The law of 23 March 1933 that allowed Hitler's government to make laws without the Reichstag, even laws that departed from the Constitution, for four years. It was renewed in 1937 and 1939.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 8-9 November 1923: Munich Putsch. Nazi vote: 2.6 per cent (1928), 18.3 (1930), 37.3 (July 1932), 33.1 (November 1932).
- 30 January 1933: Hitler Chancellor. 27 February: Reichstag Fire. 23 March: Enabling Act.
- 30 June-2 July 1934: Night of the Long Knives. 2 August 1934: Hindenburg dies; Hitler becomes Führer.
:::

## The early party, 1919-29

The German Workers' Party was founded in Munich in January 1919. Hitler, a former army corporal angered by Germany's defeat, joined in September 1919. In 1920 it became the National Socialist German Workers' Party (the Nazi party) and issued a Twenty-Five Point Programme that attacked Versailles and excluded Jews from German citizenship. Hitler became leader in 1921, and the party formed a uniformed force, the *Sturmabteilung* (SA, the Brownshirts), to protect its meetings and break up those of its enemies.

In the crisis of 1923 Hitler tried to seize power in Munich. On 8-9 November the Nazis and General Ludendorff moved in the Beer Hall Putsch and were stopped by the police. Hitler served about nine months in Landsberg prison, where he dictated the first volume of *Mein Kampf*, setting out his racism, his hatred of communism and his plan for "living space" in the east. He concluded that the Nazis must win power through elections as well as force. After refounding the party in 1925 he built a national organisation, yet in May 1928 it won only 2.6 per cent of the vote.

## The Depression and the collapse of Weimar democracy

The Wall Street Crash of October 1929 ended the American loans on which German recovery depended. Banks failed, factories closed and registered unemployment rose to about six million by early 1932. The coalition of the Social Democrat Hermann Müller broke up in March 1930 in a quarrel about unemployment insurance, and President Hindenburg appointed Heinrich Brüning of the Centre Party, who had no Reichstag majority and governed by emergency decree under Article 48. His cuts and tax rises deepened the misery. Democracy was losing the respect of many Germans before the Nazis took over.

## Why Nazi support grew, 1929-32

Historians point to several linked reasons and disagree about their weight.

- **Economic crisis.** The Nazis promised work and bread to the unemployed, to indebted farmers and to frightened shopkeepers.
- **Hitler's appeal.** He was a powerful speaker who offered simple answers, blamed Jews, communists and the "November criminals" who signed the armistice, and promised renewal.
- **Propaganda and organisation.** Goebbels used mass meetings, posters, films and Hitler's aeroplane tour of 1932, and local branches tailored the message to farmers or workers.
- **The SA.** By 1932 it numbered several hundred thousand men; its street violence against communists frightened many into seeking a strong government.
- **Fear of communism and elite help.** Property owners saw the Nazis as a barrier to the Communist Party, and nationalists such as Alfred Hugenberg gave publicity, though most business funding went to other parties.

The results were dramatic: 18.3 per cent in September 1930 (107 seats); 37.3 per cent and 230 seats in July 1932, making the Nazis the largest party. In the presidential election of 1932 Hitler won 36.8 per cent in the second round, and Hindenburg was re-elected. In November 1932 the vote fell to 33.1 per cent. Their voters were mainly Protestants, farmers, the self-employed and the young, rather than unemployed workers, who tended to vote communist.

## January 1933: how Hitler became Chancellor

The Nazis did not win a majority. Hitler came to power through a deal among conservatives. Franz von Papen, Chancellor from June to November 1932, resented General Kurt von Schleicher, who had forced him out, and approached Hitler in January 1933. The President's advisers, among them his son Oskar and the state secretary Otto Meissner, were persuaded that a government led by Hitler but dominated by conservatives could command a majority and keep him under control. Hindenburg gave way. On 30 January 1933 Hitler became Chancellor with Papen as Vice-Chancellor and only two other Nazis, Frick and Göring, in a cabinet of twelve.

:::example{title="Reading the election figures"}
The Nazi vote ran 2.6 per cent (1928), 18.3 (1930), 37.3 (July 1932) and 33.1 (November 1932). Asked whether Hitler's rise was inevitable, use the last figure: the vote had fallen before he was appointed, so appointment cannot be explained by popular support alone. State the figure, say what it shows, then link it to the question.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing the causes of Hitler's appointment"}
Separate the conditions from the trigger. The Depression, Weimar's weakness and fear of communism made the Nazis the largest party. The trigger was a decision by a small group around Hindenburg to bring Hitler in, after the Nazi vote had fallen in November 1932. Some historians therefore give more weight to political miscalculation than to popular support.
:::

## From Chancellor to dictator, 1933-34

Within weeks the Nazis used the powers of the state. On 27 February 1933 the Reichstag building burned. A Dutch council communist, Marinus van der Lubbe, was arrested at the scene; historians still disagree about whether he acted alone, though the Nazis used the fire in either case. The next day Hindenburg signed the Decree for the Protection of People and State, which suspended civil liberties and allowed mass arrests of communists. In the election of 5 March, held amid violence, the Nazis won 43.9 per cent, still short of a majority.

On 23 March the Reichstag, meeting under SA guard in the Kroll Opera House, passed the Enabling Act by 444 votes to 94. The Social Democrats voted against, the Communist deputies had been arrested or driven out, and the Centre Party, persuaded by promises about the Church, voted in favour. **Gleichschaltung** followed: state governments lost their independence, trade unions were abolished on 2 May and replaced by the German Labour Front, other parties dissolved or were banned, and the Law against the Formation of New Parties (14 July 1933) made Germany a one-party state. Early camps such as Dachau held the first prisoners.

One threat remained inside the movement. The SA under Ernst Röhm, with well over two million members, wanted a "second revolution" and a place in the army, and the generals were alarmed. In the Night of the Long Knives (30 June to 2 July 1934) the SS and Gestapo murdered Röhm and other SA leaders, and also old enemies such as Schleicher and Gregor Strasser; at least 85 people are known to have died, and some historians suggest more. The army was pleased. When Hindenburg died on 2 August 1934, the offices of President and Chancellor were merged under a law passed the day before, and army and officials swore a personal oath to Hitler as Führer. A plebiscite on 19 August approved the change, with about 90 per cent voting yes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Hitler was elected with a majority"}
The Nazis never won more than 37.3 per cent in a free national election. Hindenburg appointed Hitler after negotiations, and the dictatorship was built afterwards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming the Depression alone"}
Other countries suffered it without a dictatorship. Explain the part played by Weimar's political weakness, Nazi methods and the decisions of the elite.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Reichstag Fire with the Enabling Act"}
The Fire (27 February) led to the decree that removed civil rights. The Enabling Act (23 March) allowed Hitler to legislate without the Reichstag.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After the failed Putsch of 1923 Hitler decided to seek power through elections.
- The Depression, decree government and Weimar's divisions created a crisis; Nazi support grew through distress, Hitler's appeal, propaganda, the SA and fear of communism.
- Conservatives who believed they could control him brought Hitler into office in January 1933.
- The Reichstag Fire decree, the Enabling Act, Gleichschaltung and the Night of the Long Knives gave him total power by August 1934.
:::
