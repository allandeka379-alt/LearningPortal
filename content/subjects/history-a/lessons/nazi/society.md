## Introduction

The Nazis did not only want to rule Germany; they wanted to remake it. They aimed at a "people's community" (*Volksgemeinschaft*) of racially "pure" Germans, united in loyalty to the Führer, with women as mothers, boys as soldiers, and Jews and other groups excluded. This lesson examines how they tried to do this in the 1930s and how far they succeeded. It also deals with the persecution of Jews and other minorities before the war. The mass murder of the Holocaust is treated, with the care it requires, in the separate lessons on that subject.

Students in Africa will recognise parts of this story. The racial ideas of the Nazis grew from nineteenth-century European race science, which was also used to justify colonial rule, and African and Black German people were among those who suffered.

You should know from the earlier lessons how Hitler consolidated power by 1934.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Volksgemeinschaft"}
The "people's community": the Nazi ideal of a nation united by race and loyalty, in which class divisions were to disappear and those considered outsiders were excluded.
:::

:::definition{term="Eugenics"}
The belief that a population can be "improved" by controlling who may have children. The Nazis used the idea to justify sterilising people they labelled hereditarily ill, and later to murder disabled people.
:::

:::definition{term="Aryan"}
A Nazi term for the supposedly superior "race" of northern Europeans. It has no scientific basis; the Nazis defined who counted as Jewish by ancestry rather than by belief.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1 April 1933: the boycott of Jewish businesses. 1933: Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring (sterilisation); Concordat with the Vatican (July).
- 1935: Nuremberg Laws (15 September). 1936: the Hitler Youth becomes the state youth organisation. 1937: Mit brennender Sorge.
- 9-10 November 1938: Kristallnacht. 1939: Aktion T4, the killing of disabled people, begins.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge key question 3.4 asks "How far did the Nazis transform German society?" and expects a judgement on women, youth, religion and minorities. ZIMSEC covers the same policies. The Holocaust itself is a Cambridge Paper 3 topic (lessons holocaust.*), so here you need the persecution to 1939.
:::

## Women and the family

Nazi ideology said that a woman's place was in the home, summed up as "children, kitchen, church" (*Kinder, Küche, Kirche*). Women were removed from posts in the civil service, medicine and law, and marriage loans of 1,000 marks (cut by a quarter for each child) were offered under the law of June 1933. The Honour Cross of the German Mother (1938) rewarded women with four or more children. Organisations such as the Nazi Women's League, led by Gertrud Scholtz-Klink, trained women for motherhood. Abortion and contraception were restricted for "Aryan" women, but sterilisation and forced abortion were used on women the regime considered "unfit".

Yet policy was not consistent. As rearmament created a labour shortage after 1936, the regime needed women in factories, offices and farms, and the number of women in work rose, though many were in low-paid jobs. The birth rate rose after 1933 but did not reach the level the Nazis wanted. Most historians conclude that the Nazis changed the way women were treated more than they changed how women lived.

## Young people and education

The aim was to train a generation loyal to Hitler. The **Hitler Youth** (for boys: the Jungvolk at 10-14 and the Hitler Youth proper at 14-18) taught camping, sport, marching and obedience, and the **League of German Girls** (BDM) trained girls for motherhood and homemaking. All other youth organisations were banned or absorbed, the Hitler Youth became the state organisation in 1936, and membership was made compulsory in 1939. Many young people enjoyed the activities, the sense of belonging and the chance to escape parental control, though some resented the discipline.

In schools, teachers had to join the Nazi Teachers' League, Jewish and political opponents were dismissed, and textbooks and syllabuses were rewritten. Biology taught race science, history glorified the German past and the Nazi movement, and physical education received more time. Elite schools, such as the *Napolas* (national political training schools) and the Adolf Hitler Schools, prepared future leaders. The effect was real: young people came to hear the regime's slogans everywhere. But the evidence of the Edelweiss Pirates, and of university students after 1942, shows that indoctrination was not total.

## The churches

About two thirds of Germans were Protestant and a third Catholic. In July 1933 Hitler signed a **Concordat** with the Vatican that guaranteed the Catholic Church's schools and worship in return for its withdrawal from politics; the Nazis soon broke it by harassing Catholic organisations and priests. Many Protestant pastors supported a "Reich Church" under Reich Bishop Ludwig Müller that would be Nazi-friendly. A minority, led by Pastor Martin Niemöller, formed the **Confessing Church** and opposed state control. In 1937 the Pope's letter *Mit brennender Sorge* was read from Catholic pulpits and condemned Nazi ideas. The regime arrested some clergy but did not try to destroy the churches, since it wanted to avoid a clash with millions of believers. Few church leaders spoke up for the Jews.

## Race, Jews and other minorities

Nazi racial thinking ranked peoples and held that the "Aryan" race should be kept "pure". **Jews** were the principal target. The steps of persecution can be set out in order.

| Date | Measure |
|---|---|
| 1 April 1933 | Boycott of Jewish shops and professionals |
| 7 April 1933 | Civil Service Law removes Jews and political opponents from public posts |
| 15 September 1935 | Nuremberg Laws: Jews lose citizenship and are forbidden to marry or have relations with "Germans" |
| 1936-38 | More professions closed to Jews and Jewish businesses forced into non-Jewish hands ("Aryanisation"); Jewish passports are marked with a "J" in October 1938 |
| 9-10 November 1938 | Kristallnacht: attacks across Germany and Austria |

The **Nuremberg Laws** defined who was a Jew by their grandparents and turned a minority into second-class subjects. On the night of 9-10 November 1938, after a young Jewish man, Herschel Grynszpan, shot a German diplomat in Paris, the regime encouraged a wave of attacks on Jewish shops, homes and synagogues; about 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to camps, many dozens of people were killed, and the Jewish community was fined heavily. Pressure forced about half of Germany's roughly 500,000 Jews to emigrate before 1939, and many of those who left lost their possessions.

**Other victims.** The regime also persecuted:
- **disabled and "hereditarily ill" people**, around 400,000 of whom were sterilised under the law of July 1933, and thousands of whom were killed from 1939 in Aktion T4;
- **Roma and Sinti**, who were classed as an inferior race and put under special laws;
- **Jehovah's Witnesses**, who refused the Hitler salute and military service;
- **homosexual men**, who were prosecuted under a harsher version of the criminal law (Paragraph 175) from 1935;
- **"asocials"**, such as beggars and vagrants, and Black Germans and Africans living in Germany, among whom several hundred children of mixed parentage were secretly sterilised.

:::example{title="How far was society transformed?"}
A balanced answer separates ideas from outcomes. The Nazis succeeded in bringing young people into their own organisations, in persecuting Jews and minorities, and in destroying independent groups such as unions. They failed to push women out of work, to break the churches or to remove class differences: the old elites kept much of their power. Some historians, such as David Schoenbaum, argued that Nazi rule did in effect modernise society, while others reject that as a misreading of a regime whose aims were racial and reactionary. A judgement must weigh what changed in the structure of society against what changed in the beliefs and treatment of groups.
:::

:::example{title="Analysing one policy"}
Consider the marriage loans of 1933. The aim was to raise the birth rate and take women out of the labour market. The method was a loan of 1,000 marks, cut by a quarter with each child. The result was a rising marriage and birth rate, but the labour shortage after 1936 pulled women back into work. Setting aim, method and result side by side shows both an effect and a limit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the Nuremberg Laws and Kristallnacht as the start of anti-Jewish policy"}
Persecution began in 1933 with the boycott and the Civil Service Law. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) made it law, and Kristallnacht (1938) was a violent escalation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Kristallnacht a spontaneous outburst"}
Anti-Jewish violence was organised and encouraged by the party leadership, including Goebbels, though it was presented as the anger of ordinary Germans.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that all young people were Nazis"}
The Hitler Youth was compulsory only from 1939, many young people were bored or resentful, and there were groups that resisted.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing persecution before 1939 with the Holocaust"}
The persecution of 1933-39 was aimed at forcing Jews to leave and removing their rights. The systematic mass murder came after the war began. Keep the stages apart.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Nazis wanted a racial "people's community" in which women were mothers, young people were trained for loyalty and outsiders were excluded.
- Women were encouraged to marry and bear children, but rearmament after 1936 brought them back into work.
- The Hitler Youth, the BDM and rewritten schooling shaped many young people, though not all.
- The Concordat and the Reich Church failed to bring the churches under full control.
- Jews were persecuted step by step (the boycott, the Nuremberg Laws, Kristallnacht), as were Roma, disabled people, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexual men and others.
:::
