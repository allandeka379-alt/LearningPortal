## Introduction

When the powers restored the Bourbons in 1814, the new king, Louis XVIII, faced a country that had lived through a revolution and an empire. Millions of people had bought Church and émigré land, the Code governed their lives, and the army remembered Napoleon with pride. At the other end of the political spectrum were nobles and clergy who wished to undo the Revolution. This lesson asks how Louis XVIII and his brother Charles X tried to govern between these forces, and why the monarchy was overthrown in July 1830 after only fifteen years. It also shows how the July Revolution was part of a wider wave of liberal revolts in Europe in 1830.

You should know the Vienna settlement from the previous lesson and the main changes of the Revolution and Napoleon from earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ultra-royalist (ultra)"}
A supporter of the Bourbons who wished to restore as much as possible of the Ancien Régime: the power of the king, the privileges and land of the nobles and the influence of the Church. The ultras were "more royalist than the king".
:::

:::definition{term="The Charter of 1814"}
The constitution granted by Louis XVIII, which kept many revolutionary gains (equality before the law, the Code, religious toleration, the sale of national lands) while keeping real power with the king and a tiny electorate.
:::

:::definition{term="Ordinance"}
An order issued by the king alone, without the consent of parliament. Charles X's ordinances of July 1830 were issued under Article 14 of the Charter, which allowed the king to make "ordinances for the security of the State".
:::

## Louis XVIII and the Charter, 1814-1824

Louis XVIII, brother of the executed king, was a cautious man who understood that a return to 1789 was impossible. The **Charter** (1814) recognised equality before the law, the Napoleonic Code and administration, religious toleration (though Catholicism was the state religion), the freedom of the press with limits, and the new owners' rights to the "national lands". The king alone proposed laws; the Chamber of Peers was chosen by him; and the Chamber of Deputies was elected by only about 90,000 men (by common estimates) who paid at least 300 francs in tax. The white flag replaced the tricolour. Many liberals and old Bonapartists felt cheated, while ultras thought the Charter gave away too much.

After Napoleon's return and defeat in 1815, a **White Terror** broke out in the south: royalists attacked Bonapartists, republicans and Protestants, for example in Nîmes, and some officials and soldiers were killed. The courts executed Marshal Ney, one of Napoleon's marshals who had gone over to him, in December 1815. The elections of August 1815 produced an ultra majority, the *Chambre introuvable*, which pressed for stern measures, but Louis XVIII dissolved it in September 1816 because he feared a counter-revolution would endanger the throne. Moderate ministers such as Richelieu and Decazes then held office until 1820.

In February 1820 the Duke of Berry, a nephew of the king and the hope of continuing the dynasty, was murdered by a man with a grudge against the Bourbons. The ultras blamed liberalism. Censorship was strengthened, and a new electoral law gave the richest voters a double vote. From 1821 the ultra Villèle led the government. It paid compensation to nobles who had lost land in the Revolution (the "billion for the émigrés", 1825), and in 1823 French troops restored the Spanish king Ferdinand VII, which brought some military prestige.

## Charles X and the crisis, 1824-1830

Charles X came to the throne in 1824. He was crowned at Reims in 1825 with the ceremony of the old monarchy, and made clear that he wanted the alliance of throne and altar. The Sacrilege law (1825) imposed severe penalties for profaning sacred objects in churches, including death in the gravest cases, and the Church gained greater influence over education. These measures angered liberals, anticlerical townspeople and many middle-class property owners, who feared a return to the old order.

By 1827 opposition was strong. A bad harvest and an economic downturn raised the price of bread and put workers out of jobs. Elections in 1827 weakened the ultras, and the moderate Martignac governed from 1828 until the king, who distrusted compromise, dismissed him in August 1829 and appointed the ultra **Polignac**. In March 1830 the Chamber told the king, in an address supported by 221 deputies, that his ministers did not have the nation's confidence. Charles dissolved the Chamber, but the election of July 1830 gave the opposition an even larger majority. The conquest of Algiers in July brought no popular gratitude.

On 25 July 1830 the king, relying on Article 14, issued the **July Ordinances**. They suspended freedom of the press, dissolved the newly elected Chamber, changed the electoral law to cut the electorate sharply and called new elections.

## The July Revolution

Liberal journalists, including Thiers and Mignet, published protests. On 27 July the people of Paris, workers, students and former soldiers, began to build barricades. In three days, the "Three Glorious Days" (27-29 July), the royal troops under Marmont, short of food and unwilling to fire, lost control of the capital. Charles X offered to withdraw the ordinances, but it was too late. He abdicated on 2 August and went to Britain. The liberal politicians, fearing a republic, offered the throne to Louis-Philippe, Duke of Orléans, who accepted a revised Charter as "King of the French". The tricolour returned, the electorate was widened slightly, and Catholicism was no longer the religion of the state.

:::example{title="Why did Charles X fall?"}
Group the causes. **Long-term**: the Bourbons had come back with the help of foreign armies, and the middle classes and the army distrusted a regime that seemed to favour the nobles and Church. **Medium-term**: the ultra policies of the 1820s (the Sacrilege law, compensation for émigrés, censorship) united the opposition. **Immediate**: economic distress, Polignac's appointment, the election result and the ordinances, which broke the Charter in spirit and provoked the barricades. **Mistakes**: the king's refusal to compromise and a plan with too few troops. A strong answer says which mattered most, for instance that the ordinances were the trigger but only because the monarchy had already lost the support of the educated and the property-owning classes.
:::

:::example{title="Reading the Charter"}
The Charter let the king alone propose laws, yet it kept the Code and equality before the law. A candidate can use this to show that Louis XVIII tried to reconcile old and new France, and that Charles X's use of Article 14 to issue the July Ordinances broke that compromise.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Louis XVIII and Charles X"}
Louis XVIII was a moderate who accepted the Charter; Charles X was an ultra who tried to roll back the changes. Both were brothers of Louis XVI.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Charter restored the Ancien Régime"}
The Charter kept equality before the law, the Code, the sale of national lands and a constitution. The ultras disliked it for that reason.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the revolution of 1830 as a republican victory"}
The liberals chose another king, Louis-Philippe, rather than a republic, and the new regime favoured the wealthy.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Louis XVIII accepted the Charter of 1814, which kept many revolutionary gains but limited the vote to a few thousand wealthy men.
- The White Terror of 1815 and the ultra majority showed the strength of royalist reaction; the king dissolved the Chamber in 1816.
- After 1820 the ultras (Villèle) gained influence; Charles X (from 1824) favoured the Church and nobles and provoked opposition.
- Polignac's appointment in 1829 and the July Ordinances of 1830 led to the Three Glorious Days.
- Charles X abdicated; Louis-Philippe became "King of the French" under a revised Charter.
:::
