## Introduction

After more than twenty years of revolution and war, the victorious powers met at Vienna in the autumn of 1814 to decide the shape of Europe. Napoleon's fall left a vacuum: France had been defeated but the lands that he had conquered or reorganised had to be given to someone, and monarchs who had seen thrones overturned wanted security above all. This lesson explains what the powers wanted, what they agreed, how successful the settlement was, and how the great powers tried to enforce it through regular conferences, the Congress System, which broke down in the early 1820s.

You should know the story of Napoleon's fall and the impact of the Revolution from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Legitimacy"}
The principle that rulers whose families had held thrones before the Revolution and Napoleon were the rightful rulers and should be restored. Talleyrand, the French representative, used it to argue for the Bourbons and for a fair place for France.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of power"}
The idea that peace is best kept when no single state is strong enough to dominate the others. The powers wanted to make sure that France could not threaten Europe again, but also that no ally became too strong.
:::

:::definition{term="Congress System"}
The attempt by the great powers to meet in conferences ("congresses") to settle disputes and to maintain the 1815 settlement, sometimes called the Concert of Europe.
:::

## The statesmen and their aims

**Metternich**, Austria's foreign minister, was the leading figure. He wanted to preserve the Habsburg Empire, which contained many nationalities, and to prevent revolution and French or Russian dominance. **Castlereagh** of Britain wanted a balance of power on the Continent that left Britain free to keep its sea power and trade; he preferred a moderate peace with France. **Tsar Alexander I** wanted to expand Russian influence, especially over Poland, and also spoke of a Christian union of monarchs. **Hardenberg** and Humboldt represented Prussia, which wanted Saxony and lands on the Rhine. **Talleyrand**, who had served both Napoleon and the Bourbons, skilfully used legitimacy to win France a place at the talks.

The Congress was interrupted by Napoleon's return in March 1815 (the Hundred Days). After Waterloo, the second Peace of Paris (November 1815) was harsher than the first: France was confined to its borders of 1790, had to pay an indemnity and accept an occupying army for up to five years.

## The territorial settlement

- **France**: the Bourbon Louis XVIII was restored. Around France the powers placed strong neighbours as barriers: the Netherlands and Belgium were united under William I of Orange, Prussia was given lands on the Rhine, and Piedmont-Sardinia was strengthened with Genoa.
- **Austria**: gained Lombardy and Venetia in northern Italy and lands on the Adriatic, as well as Salzburg, while giving up Belgium. It chaired the **German Confederation** of 39 states, with a Diet at Frankfurt.
- **Prussia**: gained about two fifths of Saxony, Westphalia and the Rhineland, which made it a power on the Rhine and a future leader of Germany.
- **Russia**: gained most of Poland as a kingdom with the Tsar as king, and kept Finland.
- **Italy**: remained divided, with Austrian influence in the north, the Pope restored in the Papal States and a Bourbon king in Naples and Sicily.
- **Britain**: kept colonies taken in the war, including the Cape and Ceylon, Malta and Mauritius, as well as naval bases.
- **Switzerland**: its neutrality was guaranteed. Norway was handed from Denmark to Sweden.
- **The slave trade**: the powers in February 1815 issued a declaration condemning the Atlantic slave trade as contrary to humanity, though they set no date or means for ending it.

:::example{title="Assessing the settlement"}
A balanced judgement can say: "The Vienna settlement was moderate towards France, since it did not destroy the country, and it created a balance of power that helped to prevent a general European war between the great powers until the Crimean War of 1854. But it ignored nationalism and liberalism: Belgians, Poles, Italians and Germans were handed to rulers they had not chosen, so it stored up trouble. It lasted in the sense that the great powers stayed at peace, and failed in the sense that revolutions in 1830 and 1848 broke its principles." This puts evidence on both sides.
:::

## The Congress System

The settlement was supported by treaties. The **Holy Alliance** (September 1815) was a declaration by Russia, Austria and Prussia to rule by Christian principles, which Metternich regarded as vague but useful. The more important **Quadruple Alliance** (November 1815) of Britain, Austria, Prussia and Russia promised to meet to deal with threats to the peace.

- **Aix-la-Chapelle (1818)**: the occupation of France ended and France joined the powers as an equal, making the system a Concert of five.
- **Troppau (1820)**: after revolts in Spain and Naples, Austria, Russia and Prussia issued the Troppau Protocol, claiming a right to intervene against revolutions. Britain refused to agree.
- **Laibach (1821)**: the powers authorised Austria to put down the revolt in Naples, and Austrian troops restored the king; a revolt in Piedmont was also crushed.
- **Verona (1822)**: the powers authorised France to restore the Spanish king by force. Britain, now under Canning, withdrew from the system, and French troops restored Ferdinand VII in 1823.

## Why the system collapsed

1. **Britain's withdrawal.** Castlereagh already doubted that Britain should intervene in other states' internal affairs, and Canning rejected it outright, preferring British interests and non-intervention. He recognised the independence of former Spanish colonies in America (1824-25).
2. **Conflicting interests.** The powers had different aims: Russia and Austria quarrelled over the Balkans, and the Greek revolt that began in 1821 split them.
3. **Nationalism and liberalism.** Revolts could be put down in Naples and Spain, but the ideas continued. Greece won its independence with outside help and Belgium broke away from the Netherlands in 1830.
4. **No institutions.** The system had no secretariat or armed force and depended on the agreement of governments.

The Congress System thus ended in the 1820s, although meetings of the great powers continued to settle crises on occasion, such as the London conference on Belgium in 1830-31.

:::example{title="Applying a principle"}
Apply legitimacy to a case: restoring the Bourbons to France, Spain and Naples followed it; giving Belgium to the Dutch king, Poland to the Tsar and Norway to Sweden did not, because the powers also wanted a balance of power. Using the principles against actual decisions shows that they were applied selectively.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling Vienna a harsh peace for France"}
The first Peace of Paris (1814) and Vienna were mild; even after the Hundred Days France lost only some frontier lands and had to pay an indemnity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Holy Alliance with the Quadruple Alliance"}
The Holy Alliance was a vague religious declaration. The Quadruple Alliance was the real agreement to hold congresses and enforce the settlement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Britain led the Congress System"}
Britain took part in 1818 but refused the Troppau Protocol and withdrew by 1822.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Assess the aims and achievements of the Congress of Vienna", "Why did the Congress System collapse?" and "To what extent did the Vienna settlement keep the peace of Europe?" Give dates, names and the principles by their names, and weigh success (peace among the great powers) against failure (ignoring nationalism). For the collapse, rank causes and link them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The powers at Vienna aimed at legitimacy, a balance of power, compensation for the victors and the containment of France.
- Metternich, Castlereagh, Alexander I, Hardenberg and Talleyrand shaped the settlement; Prussia, Austria and Russia gained territory and France was ringed by strong neighbours.
- The settlement kept the great powers from general war for decades but ignored nationalism and liberalism.
- The Congress System met at Aix-la-Chapelle, Troppau, Laibach and Verona to enforce the settlement and put down revolutions.
- It collapsed because Britain withdrew, the powers disagreed (for example over Greece), and nationalism and liberalism proved too strong.
:::
