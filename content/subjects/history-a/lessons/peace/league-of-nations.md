## Introduction

The League of Nations was the first world organisation created to keep peace. It was born from the horror of the First World War, which killed millions, and from the belief of Woodrow Wilson and others that war could be prevented if nations agreed to settle disputes by discussion, to protect each other from aggression and to reduce their arms. The League's Covenant was part of the Treaty of Versailles, and the League began work on 10 January 1920, with its headquarters in Geneva. By the late 1930s it had lost most of its authority, and it was dissolved in April 1946, when the United Nations took over its assets and many of its functions.

For a student in Zimbabwe the League is relevant for two reasons. It supervised the mandates that placed Germany's African colonies under Britain, France, Belgium and South Africa. And its failure in 1935-36 to protect Abyssinia (Ethiopia), the African state that had defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, was one of the great betrayals felt by African and Pan-African thinkers. You should know the terms of the Paris settlement, which the previous lesson describes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Covenant"}
The founding document of the League of Nations, containing its rules. Article 10 pledged members to protect each other's territory, and Article 16 provided for sanctions against a member that went to war in breach of the rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective security"}
The idea that all members of the League would act together against any aggressor, so that no state could attack another without facing the combined strength of the rest.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions"}
Measures such as ending trade or financial links, used to put pressure on a state that has broken international rules. The Covenant allowed economic sanctions and, in theory, military action.
:::

:::definition{term="Unanimity"}
The rule that decisions in the League's Council and Assembly required the agreement of all members present. It made action slow and easy to block.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 10 January 1920: the League begins work; first Assembly 15 November 1920 in Geneva.
- 1921: Aaland Islands settled; 1923: the Corfu incident; 1925: the Greek-Bulgarian incident; 1926: Germany joins.
- 18 September 1931: the Mukden incident and the Japanese invasion of Manchuria; 1933: Japan leaves the League.
- 3 October 1935: Italy invades Abyssinia; the Hoare-Laval plan in December 1935; 30 June 1936: Haile Selassie addresses the League.
- 1939: the USSR is expelled after attacking Finland; 1946: the League is dissolved.
:::

## Aims and organisation

The League's aims, set out in the Covenant, were to prevent war through **collective security**; to encourage **disarmament**; to settle disputes by **negotiation and arbitration**; and to improve **living and working conditions**. Its organisation had several parts:

| Part | Role |
|---|---|
| **Assembly** | All members, one vote each; met once a year; debated and recommended; decisions needed unanimity |
| **Council** | Smaller body: permanent members (Britain, France, Italy and Japan, later Germany and the USSR) and several elected members; met several times a year to handle disputes |
| **Secretariat** | The permanent staff, led by the Secretary-General |
| **Permanent Court of International Justice** | At The Hague (from 1922); gave rulings on legal disputes |
| **Mandates Commission** | Supervised the mandated territories |
| **Agencies** | The International Labour Organization, the Health Organisation, the Refugee Organisation and others |

Weaknesses were built in. The **United States**, whose president had inspired the League, never joined, because the Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles. Germany and the USSR were at first excluded. The League had **no army** and depended on its members to supply force, and decisions required **unanimity**.

## Successes in the 1920s

- **Disputes between small and medium states.** In 1921 the League settled a dispute over the Aaland Islands between Sweden and Finland, giving them to Finland with protection for the islanders. In 1925, when Greek troops entered Bulgaria after an incident on the border, the League ordered a withdrawal, which Greece obeyed, and it fined Greece.
- **Humanitarian and social work.** The League returned about 400,000 prisoners of war to their homes, helped refugees under Fridtjof Nansen (the Nansen passport), led campaigns against disease and drugs, fought forced labour and slavery (the Slavery Convention of 1926), and supported the International Labour Organization, which set standards for working conditions.
- **Diplomacy.** The Locarno treaties of 1925, negotiated outside the League, produced the "spirit of Locarno" and Germany joined in 1926. In 1928 the Kellogg-Briand Pact, also outside the League, renounced war as a means of national policy.

## Failures in the 1920s

The League was less successful when great powers were involved. In 1920 Poland seized Vilna from Lithuania and the League could not make it withdraw. In 1923, after the murder of an Italian general on Greek soil, Mussolini bombarded and occupied the Greek island of Corfu; the matter was taken from the League to the Conference of Ambassadors, and Greece had to pay compensation. The lesson learned was that a great power could defy the League.

## The 1930s: Manchuria and Abyssinia

The Depression of the 1930s caused nationalism and militarism, and the League faced its greatest tests.

**Manchuria.** On 18 September 1931 a bomb exploded on the railway near Mukden, and Japan, claiming it was a Chinese attack, invaded Manchuria and set up a puppet state, Manchukuo, in 1932. China appealed to the League. The League appointed the Lytton Commission, which took months to investigate and reported in October 1932 that Japan's action was not justified as self-defence. The Assembly voted to accept the report in February 1933, and Japan announced its withdrawal from the League in March 1933. No sanctions or force were used, partly because Britain and France were concerned about the economic depression and were unwilling to risk war, and the United States, which was not a member, did not act.

**Abyssinia.** On 3 October 1935 Mussolini's army invaded Abyssinia, a member of the League since 1923. The League condemned Italy as the aggressor and imposed economic sanctions, but they did not include oil, coal or steel, and the Suez Canal remained open to Italian ships. Britain and France were reluctant to alienate Mussolini, whom they hoped to keep as an ally against Hitler. In December 1935 the Hoare-Laval plan, a secret proposal to give Italy much of Abyssinia, leaked and was condemned. Italy took Addis Ababa in May 1936, and sanctions were abandoned in July. Emperor Haile Selassie spoke in person to the League in Geneva on 30 June 1936 and warned that the failure to defend Abyssinia would be followed by other aggression. The League lost the confidence of small states.

:::example{title="Explaining why the League failed to stop Italy in Abyssinia"}
1. Italy was a great power and a permanent member of the Council, and Britain and France did not want to fight it.
2. They hoped to keep Mussolini as an ally against Germany, so they chose weak sanctions.
3. The sanctions omitted oil, coal and steel, and the Suez Canal stayed open, so they did not stop the invasion.
4. The Hoare-Laval plan showed that the two powers were ready to reward the aggressor.
5. Therefore Italy conquered Abyssinia by May 1936, and the League's reputation for collective security was destroyed.
:::

## The collapse of the League

By the later 1930s Germany (which left in 1933), Japan and Italy (1937) had left the League, and Hitler's actions, such as the remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the Anschluss and the occupation of Czechoslovakia, were not dealt with by the League but by direct diplomacy. The Soviet Union was expelled in December 1939 after attacking Finland. The war that began in September 1939 showed that the League had failed in its main task. The main reasons are usually given as:

- **the absence of the United States** and the exclusion of Germany and the USSR in the early years;
- **no army and the unanimity rule**, which made enforcement slow and uncertain;
- **self-interest of Britain and France**, the leading members, who put their own interests and fear of war ahead of collective security;
- **the Depression**, which made states put economic recovery first and encouraged aggressive nationalism;
- **the growth of dictatorships** in Germany, Italy and Japan, which ignored the League;
- **the link with the Treaty of Versailles**, which many countries saw as unjust.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the League failed in everything"}
The League settled several disputes in the 1920s and did valuable work for refugees, health and labour. A balanced answer gives successes as well as failures.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming one cause for the collapse"}
Combine the absence of the USA, lack of force, great-power self-interest, the Depression and aggressive dictatorships.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Manchuria with Abyssinia"}
Manchuria (1931, Japan against China) and Abyssinia (1935, Italy against Ethiopia) are different cases with different details.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The League of Nations began work in 1920 to prevent war through collective security, arbitration and disarmament, and to improve living conditions.
- It was weakened by the absence of the USA, the lack of an army and the unanimity rule.
- It had successes in the 1920s (Aaland, Greece and Bulgaria, refugees, health, labour) and failures against stronger states (Vilna, Corfu).
- In the 1930s it failed to stop Japan in Manchuria (1931) and Italy in Abyssinia (1935-36); the dictators left it.
- It was dissolved in 1946; its failure and its structure influenced the creation of the UN.
:::
