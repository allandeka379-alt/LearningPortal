## Introduction

The First World War ended with an armistice on 11 November 1918. It remained for the victors to decide what kind of peace to make. From 18 January 1919, delegates from more than thirty states met in Paris, and over the following months they redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, took Germany's colonies, and set the terms on which the defeated powers could live. The most famous result was the Treaty of Versailles with Germany, signed on 28 June 1919, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo.

The settlement matters for a Zimbabwean student for several reasons. It shaped the world of the 1920s and 1930s that led to the Second World War. It created the League of Nations and the mandate system, under which Germany's African colonies were placed under Britain, France, Belgium and South Africa. It gave South West Africa (Namibia) to South Africa to administer, with consequences that lasted until 1990. And it is a standard test of whether a peace can be both just and lasting. You should know the causes and course of the First World War, which are covered in other lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Armistice"}
An agreement to stop fighting, which is not itself a peace treaty. The armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting on the Western Front.
:::

:::definition{term="Reparations"}
Payments demanded from a defeated state to compensate the victors for the cost of the war. Germany was required to pay reparations after 1919.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-determination"}
The idea that peoples should be able to decide by what government they are ruled, often by forming their own nation-state. It was a principle of Wilson's Fourteen Points.
:::

:::definition{term="Mandate"}
Territory taken from Germany and the Ottoman Empire and placed under the administration of a victorious power on behalf of the League of Nations, with the duty to prepare the people for self-government.
:::

:::definition{term="Diktat"}
German word for a "dictated peace". Germans used it to describe the Treaty of Versailles because they were not allowed to negotiate its terms.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 8 January 1918: Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points. 11 November 1918: the armistice.
- 18 January 1919: the Paris Peace Conference opens. The "Big Three" (with Orlando of Italy, the "Big Four"): Wilson (USA), Clemenceau (France), Lloyd George (Britain).
- 28 June 1919: the Treaty of Versailles is signed in the Hall of Mirrors; it comes into force on 10 January 1920.
- Other treaties: Saint-Germain (Austria, September 1919), Neuilly (Bulgaria, November 1919), Trianon (Hungary, 1920), Sèvres (Ottoman Empire, 1920, replaced by Lausanne in 1923).
- 1921: the reparations bill is fixed at 132 billion gold marks (about £6.6 billion).
:::

## The aims of the leaders

**Woodrow Wilson**, President of the United States, wished for a just and lasting peace based on his Fourteen Points: open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, free trade, reduction of armaments, an impartial adjustment of colonial claims, **self-determination** for peoples of Europe, an independent Poland with access to the sea, and a **League of Nations** to keep peace. He opposed a harsh peace and did not wish Germany to be crippled.

**Georges Clemenceau**, Prime Minister of France, wanted security above all. France had been invaded twice in fifty years, and the war was fought largely on its soil, with huge loss of life and destruction in the north-east. He wanted Germany weakened permanently, with its army limited, the Rhineland detached or controlled, Alsace-Lorraine returned, and reparations to pay for the damage.

**David Lloyd George**, Prime Minister of Britain, held a middle position. He had campaigned in the general election of December 1918 on promises to make Germany pay, but he wished to restore trade (Germany had been a major trading partner), to protect the British Empire and to avoid a peace so harsh that it would provoke revenge or push Germany towards communism. Britain also wished to keep control of the seas and to take German colonies.

Vittorio Orlando of Italy wanted the territory that Italy had been promised in 1915; he left the conference for a time in April 1919 when Italy's claims were refused.

## The Treaty of Versailles

Germany was not allowed to take part in the negotiations. The treaty was presented to the German delegates on 7 May 1919, and they were given a short time to comment and then threatened with the renewal of the war. Its main terms were:

| Area | Terms |
|---|---|
| **War guilt** | Article 231, the "war guilt clause": Germany and its allies accepted responsibility for the loss and damage of the war |
| **Reparations** | To be paid by Germany; the sum was fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks |
| **Territory** | Alsace-Lorraine to France; Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium; North Schleswig to Denmark after a vote; West Prussia and Posen to Poland, giving it a "corridor" to the sea; Danzig a free city under the League; the Saar under League control for 15 years, with its coal going to France |
| **Colonies** | All German colonies were taken and became mandates of the League |
| **Armed forces** | An army of 100,000 men with no conscription, no tanks, no military aircraft and no submarines; a small navy; the Rhineland demilitarised |
| **Union with Austria** | Forbidden (Anschluss) |
| **League of Nations** | Created; Germany was not allowed to join at first |

Separate treaties dealt with Germany's allies. The Treaty of Saint-Germain broke up Austria-Hungary and made Austria a small state; the Treaty of Trianon reduced Hungary; the Treaty of Neuilly took territory from Bulgaria; and the Treaty of Sèvres was to dismember the Ottoman Empire, but was replaced after Turkish resistance by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923. New or enlarged states, such as Poland, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, were created in central and eastern Europe on the principle of national self-determination, although many included minorities.

## The mandate system

Germany's colonies and the Arab territories of the Ottoman Empire were not given independence. They became **mandates** supervised by the League, in three classes: Class A (the Middle East) to be prepared for independence soon; Class B (central Africa, such as Tanganyika, Togoland, the Cameroons and Ruanda-Urundi) under administration by Britain, France or Belgium; and Class C (South West Africa and Pacific islands) governed as part of the mandatory's own territory, in practice by South Africa. Africans were not consulted. Critics have called the mandates colonialism under a new name, and defenders say that they imposed obligations and international supervision. South Africa governed South West Africa and extended apartheid to it, and refused to hand it over to the UN until 1990.

## German reactions

Germans of all political views condemned the treaty as a Diktat. They resented the war guilt clause, the loss of territory (roughly 13 per cent of its land and about 10 per cent of its population) and the colonies, the size of the reparations, and the limit on the army. The government that signed it, led by the Social Democrats, was accused by nationalists of the "stab in the back". The German fleet was scuttled at Scapa Flow on 21 June 1919 so that it would not be handed over. The grievance was exploited by extremists, including the Nazis, during the 1920s and 1930s.

:::example{title="Explaining why the treaty was a compromise that satisfied no one"}
1. Wilson wanted a fair peace and the League; Clemenceau wanted security and revenge; Lloyd George wanted a compromise.
2. The treaty took territory, colonies, arms and money from Germany, which pleased Clemenceau in part.
3. But it did not break up Germany, which remained the largest economy in Europe, so France felt insecure and the British thought it too harsh.
4. Germany felt it was punished unjustly, and Wilson's ideal of self-determination was applied only partly.
5. Therefore the treaty was too harsh to be accepted by Germany and too weak to keep it down permanently.
:::

## Was the settlement fair and workable?

Some historians and contemporaries, beginning with the economist John Maynard Keynes in *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), argued that the treaty was too harsh and the reparations impossible to pay, and that it created the conditions for another war. Others, including some modern historians, argue that the treaty was less severe than it seemed: Germany remained united and potentially strong, the reparations were smaller than the sum first demanded and were largely not paid in full, and the treaty was milder than the one Germany had imposed on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918. They point also to weaknesses of enforcement: the United States Senate refused to ratify the treaty, so the USA did not join the League or guarantee France, and Britain and France disagreed on enforcement. On this view the problem was not that the treaty was too harsh, but that it was not enforced. A reasonable judgement is that the settlement was a compromise, harsh enough to cause resentment in Germany but not strong enough to prevent Germany from recovering, and that its fate depended on later decisions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the treaty ended the war"}
The armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting. The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the other treaties and the mandates"}
Versailles was only one treaty. Mention Saint-Germain, Trianon, Neuilly and Sèvres, and the mandate system, especially when the question concerns Africa.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the aims of the three leaders as the same"}
Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George wanted different things; the treaty was a compromise between them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 2"}
Typical questions: "Describe the main terms of the Treaty of Versailles", "Why did the leaders disagree at Paris?", "To what extent was the Treaty of Versailles fair?" Give dates, aims, terms and German reactions, use the mandate system for Africa, and balance the view that the treaty was harsh with the view that it was too weakly enforced before reaching a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Paris Peace Conference opened on 18 January 1919; the Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 and Germany was not allowed to negotiate.
- Wilson wanted a fair peace and a League, Clemenceau wanted security, and Lloyd George held a middle position; the treaty was a compromise.
- Germany lost territory and colonies, had its armed forces limited, accepted war guilt and was required to pay reparations (fixed in 1921).
- Other treaties dealt with Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria and the Ottoman Empire; German colonies became League mandates.
- Germans called it a Diktat; historians debate whether it was too harsh or too weakly enforced.
:::
