## Introduction

An A-Level history essay is not a story and not a list of facts. It is an argument: an answer to a question, supported by evidence and tested against other possible answers. A student who writes everything she knows about the Mfecane may earn a few marks. A student who answers "To what extent was Shaka responsible for the Mfecane?" with a clear line, three or four well-chosen factors weighed against one another, and a conclusion that says how far, earns many more.

This lesson shows how to turn knowledge into argument. It applies to ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2, where you write four 25-mark essays in three hours, and to Cambridge Paper 2 (part (a), 10 marks, and part (b), 20 marks) and Paper 4 (one 30-mark essay). It uses examples from several periods, so that you can practise the method on any topic. Source and extract questions are taught in the lessons on source evaluation and on interpretations. You should know the factual background of at least one topic well.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Command word"}
The word in a question that tells you what kind of answer is wanted: *describe*, *explain*, *to what extent*, *how far*, *assess*, *evaluate*, *how successful*.
:::

:::definition{term="Line of argument"}
The main answer to the question, stated at the start and developed in every paragraph, so that the essay reads as a connected case and not as a list of points.
:::

:::definition{term="Criteria for judgement"}
The standards by which you decide which factor, view or outcome matters most, for example how widespread, how long-lasting, how essential or how far it made other factors possible.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Concepts of historical thinking"}
- **Cause and consequence**: why events happened and what followed; long-term causes, triggers, and short-term and long-term consequences.
- **Change and continuity**: what altered and what stayed the same over a period, and how fast.
- **Significance**: how important an event, person or development was, judged by its effects, its lasting impact and what it reveals.
:::

## What the command words ask

| Command word | What you must do |
|---|---|
| Describe | Give the main features, with accurate detail. No explanation needed. |
| Explain (why) | Give reasons and show how each led to the result. Develop each reason. |
| To what extent / How far | Weigh the given view against others and reach a judgement that says how much. |
| Assess / Evaluate | Judge the importance or value of a factor or view, weighing evidence for and against, and reach a reasoned verdict. |
| How successful | Define success (aims, outcomes), measure the results against it and judge. |

In both boards the highest marks go to answers that argue and judge. A "describe" part may need only description, but a 25-mark ZIMSEC essay and a Cambridge 20-mark or 30-mark question reward explanation and judgement.

## Planning the answer

Spend four or five minutes planning, because a plan saves time and keeps the essay on the question.

1. **Underline the command word and the key terms** of the question and the dates it covers.
2. **State your line of argument in one sentence.** For "To what extent was the Rudd Concession the main reason for the occupation of Mashonaland?" you might write: "It was an important legal step but not the main reason, because the Company's need for gold and the British Government's support were decisive."
3. **List three or four factors**, with the key evidence for each.
4. **Decide your criteria** for ranking them.
5. **Outline the paragraphs**, with the most important factor, or the one you will argue is the main one, in a clear place.

:::example{title="A plan in six lines"}
Question: "To what extent was the Terror the result of war?" Line: war was the main cause, but ideas and rivalry shaped its form. Factors: (1) war and invasion of 1793, evidence: levée en masse, Law of Suspects; (2) fear of counter-revolution, evidence: Vendée, Lyon; (3) the ideas of Robespierre and the Committee, evidence: virtue and terror; (4) factional rivalry, evidence: Hébertists and Dantonists. Criteria: which cause explains the timing, the scale and the form of the Terror? Conclusion: war explains timing and scale; ideas explain form.
:::

## Structuring paragraphs

Each paragraph deals with one factor or one part of the argument. A useful pattern is **point, evidence, explanation, link**.

- **Point**: say what the factor is and link it to the question.
- **Evidence**: give precise facts: dates, names, places, figures with care.
- **Explanation**: show how the evidence supports the point, which is where most students lose marks.
- **Link**: say how important the factor is, compared with others or with the question's claim.

Compare a descriptive and an analytical paragraph on the 1896 risings. *Descriptive:* "The Ndebele lost land and cattle, and they had to work for the settlers. Taxes were also imposed." *Analytical:* "Loss of land and cattle was the underlying cause of the rising, because both were the basis of Ndebele wealth and social order; when the Company seized land and cattle after 1893-94 it removed that basis, and this explains why the grievance was felt across the whole society. The rinderpest epidemic of 1896 made things worse, but it came on top of an existing grievance."

## Using evidence

Use specific evidence: names, dates, laws, battles. Avoid vague claims such as "many people suffered". Give figures only when you are confident of them and describe them as estimates where historians differ. Choose evidence that supports your point and explain it. If you are unsure of a date, write the general period ("in the early 1890s") and not a wrong date.

## Weighing and judging

The weakest essays list factors. The strongest weigh them. Methods of weighing include:

- **Necessary and sufficient**: could the event have happened without this factor? (The Frankfurt Parliament's lack of an army was necessary to its failure; was it enough on its own?)
- **Links between factors**: show how one factor made another possible, and which is deeper. (Railways and the Zollverein created a middle class that took up liberal ideas.)
- **Long-term and short-term**: separate underlying conditions from triggers.
- **Scale and duration**: which factor affected more people and lasted longest?
- **Alternative views**: where historians disagree, give the main views fairly and judge them with evidence.

A judgement is a claim, not a summary: "Economic strength was the most important factor because it made the others possible" is a judgement; "Many factors were important" is not.

## Introductions and conclusions

A good **introduction** is two to four sentences: it defines key terms if needed, states the line of argument and names the factors to be discussed. It does not tell the story. A good **conclusion** answers the question directly, says how far and why, and reflects the weighing in the essay. It does not introduce new evidence.

| Part | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Retells the background | States the argument and the factors |
| Paragraph | Describes events | Makes a point, supports it, explains it, links it |
| Conclusion | Repeats the paragraphs | Gives a direct, reasoned judgement |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Narrative instead of argument"}
Telling the story of events in order, without stating how they answer the question, gains little. Begin each paragraph with a claim about the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Answering a different question"}
Check the dates and the key term. An essay on "the causes of the Second Chimurenga" is not an answer to "the success of the armed struggle", although it may use some of the same facts.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers to 'how far'"}
You must consider the view you are asked about and at least one alternative. An answer that agrees without testing is incomplete.
:::

:::mistake{title="A conclusion that does not conclude"}
"Therefore there were many causes" does not answer the question. Say which cause mattered most, or how far the view is correct, and why.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2: choose four essay questions in three hours (about 45 minutes each), each marked out of 25 using levels of response. Cambridge Paper 2: part (a) "Explain why…" (10 marks) and part (b) "To what extent…" (20 marks) per question, 105 minutes for two questions. Cambridge Paper 4: one 30-mark "Assess" or "Evaluate" essay. Both reward argument, accurate knowledge and judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Using your time"}
In ZIMSEC, about 45 minutes per essay: five for the plan, thirty-five for writing, five for checking. In Cambridge Paper 2, about 50 minutes per 30-mark question, with roughly 15 minutes on part (a) and 35 on part (b). Begin with your strongest question. Write the line of argument in your first paragraph and return to it at the end. If you run out of time, write a short conclusion; an essay without a judgement cannot reach the top level.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read the command word and key terms, and plan a line of argument before writing.
- Explain and judge: use causes and consequences, change and continuity, and significance as ways of thinking.
- Build paragraphs with a point, precise evidence, explanation and a link to the question.
- Weigh factors using criteria such as necessity, links, scale and duration, and consider other views.
- Introduce with the argument and conclude with a direct, reasoned judgement that says how far.
:::
