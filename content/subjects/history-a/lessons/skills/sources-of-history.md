## Introduction

For most of the past 1,500 years, the people of the Zimbabwe plateau did not write books about themselves. Yet we know a great deal about their farming, their kings, their trade and their beliefs. How? Historians do not simply read the past; they reconstruct it from **evidence**, and different kinds of evidence answer different questions. A stone wall can tell us how a city was built but not what its king was called. A song can give us the king's name but not the date of his death. A Portuguese letter can tell us what a visitor saw but not what he failed to understand.

This lesson sets out the main kinds of source, what each can and cannot do, and how they are combined. It is the foundation for every pre-colonial lesson that follows, from Mapungubwe to the Mandinka. Judging one particular source in an examination question is a separate skill, taught in the source-evaluation lesson.

You should already know the difference between a primary and a secondary source. A **primary** source comes from the time of the events or from a participant or witness. A **secondary** source is written later, usually by someone who was not there, using other sources.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oral tradition"}
Knowledge about the past passed from generation to generation by word of mouth: genealogies, praise poems, songs, proverbs, myths and accounts of events. Oral *history* usually refers to memories recorded from people who lived through events.
:::

:::definition{term="Archaeology"}
The study of the material remains of past people (buildings, tools, pottery, bones, rubbish pits, graves and rock paintings) recovered by survey and excavation and dated by scientific methods.
:::

:::definition{term="Stratigraphy"}
The layering of deposits in the ground. Lower layers are normally older than the layers above them, so the order of layers gives a relative sequence of events.
:::

:::definition{term="Radiocarbon dating"}
A scientific method that measures the decay of carbon-14 in organic material such as charcoal, wood or bone to estimate its age. It was developed in the late 1940s and is used at many southern African sites.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1506: Diogo de Alcacova, a Portuguese official, writes a letter from Sofala describing the inland kingdom of the Mutapa.
- 1871: the German geologist Karl Mauch visits Great Zimbabwe and suggests it was built by outsiders.
- 1905: the archaeologist David Randall-MacIver finds evidence at Great Zimbabwe that it is medieval and African.
- 1929: Gertrude Caton-Thompson excavates and reaches the same conclusion.
- 1933: the burials at Mapungubwe, including gold objects, are found.
- 1986: Great Zimbabwe and Khami are inscribed as World Heritage Sites.
:::

## The main kinds of source

### Written sources

Written sources for the plateau come almost entirely from outsiders before the nineteenth century: Arab and Swahili geographers who described Sofala and its gold, Portuguese traders, soldiers, officials and priests from the early sixteenth century, and later missionaries, hunters and travellers. They are valuable because they are **contemporary**, contain dates, names and descriptions, and can be cross-checked against each other. Portuguese documents are the main evidence for the Mutapa state's trade and politics in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Their weaknesses are serious. Writers saw only the places they visited, usually the coast, the Zambezi valley and royal courts. They did not understand local languages and customs well, and often wrote to impress a king, win a payment or justify a conquest. Many were prejudiced against Africans. Their silence proves nothing: a writer who never mentions the Rozvi does not show that the Rozvi did not exist.

### Oral traditions

Oral tradition is the main source on how African societies saw themselves. Shona clans recite praise names and totems; the Ndebele keep royal praise poems (*izibongo*) of Mzilikazi and Lobengula; spirit mediums (*mhondoro*) preserve the histories of dynasties. These sources give the African viewpoint, names of rulers, migrations, kinship and the ideas behind a state's religion.

The difficulties are well known. Memory changes in the telling. Stories may be **telescoped** (events of many generations compressed into one), shaped to flatter the ruling family or to support a claim to land or chieftainship, and rarely carry exact dates. Different groups remember the same events differently. For these reasons historians collect traditions from many people, compare versions, and check them against written and archaeological evidence. A tradition that agrees with independent evidence is strong; one that stands alone is treated with caution.

### Archaeology

Archaeology is often the *only* source for farmers and hunters who left no writing. Excavation at Great Zimbabwe, Khami and Mapungubwe has revealed building techniques, house floors, cattle bones, gold working, imported glass beads, Chinese porcelain and Persian pottery. Radiocarbon dates and stratigraphy establish when places were occupied. Soil and bone studies show what people ate.

Archaeology has limits. Evidence survives unevenly: stone, pottery and iron last, but wood, cloth and grass do not. It is slow and expensive, and an excavation destroys part of the site. Objects do not explain themselves, so beliefs, names and politics must be inferred, and inferences can be wrong. Early excavators sometimes dug carelessly and removed layers that can never be re-read.

### Other sources

- **Rock art.** The paintings of San artists in the Matopo Hills and elsewhere show animals, human figures and ritual scenes. They are evidence of beliefs and way of life, but they are hard to date and their meanings are debated.
- **Linguistics.** The relationships between the Bantu languages, and words shared for farming, iron and cattle, help to trace migrations and contacts. Language cannot give precise dates.
- **Visual and other sources.** Drawings, maps and photographs made by travellers, and museum collections, add information, though the artist's purpose and viewpoint matter.
- **Anthropology and ethnography.** Studies of living customs help interpret the past but may assume that old societies were just like modern ones.

| Source | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Written | Dated, detailed, contemporary | Outsiders' views; limited coverage |
| Oral tradition | African viewpoint; names and ideas | Telescoping; bias; few dates |
| Archaeology | Evidence where nothing was written; scientific dating | Uneven survival; needs interpretation |
| Rock art | Beliefs and daily life of artists | Hard to date and explain |
| Linguistics | Long-term movements and contacts | No exact dates |

## Combining sources

No source stands alone. Historians **triangulate**: they look for agreement among independent kinds of evidence, and for disagreement that needs explaining.

:::example{title="Combining evidence: where did Great Zimbabwe's wealth come from?"}
1. **Archaeology** finds Chinese porcelain, Persian pottery and glass beads inside the Great Enclosure, so the site's rulers had links to the Indian Ocean. Radiocarbon dating places most of the stone building between about the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries.
2. **Written sources** show that gold and ivory from the plateau were sold at Sofala and Kilwa to Muslim merchants. Arab writers mention Sofala's gold before the Portuguese arrived.
3. **Oral tradition** among Shona groups links the site to the ancestors of the Shona and to the founding of the later Mutapa state.
4. **Conclusion:** each source alone is incomplete, but together they support the claim that Great Zimbabwe was an African city whose rulers profited from the gold trade.
:::

The history of Great Zimbabwe also shows why interpretation matters. In 1871 Karl Mauch argued that the ruins were too grand for Africans, and suggested the Queen of Sheba or Phoenician builders. The idea suited the settlers' claims and was repeated for decades. It was refuted by archaeology: Randall-MacIver (1905) and Caton-Thompson (1929) found African objects, building techniques and layers in the walls, and concluded that the site was medieval and the work of African people. Later radiocarbon dating and Shona oral tradition confirmed this, and archaeologists today agree that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona.

:::example{title="Weighing an oral tradition"}
A tradition says that the first Mutapa, Mutota, left Great Zimbabwe to find salt. A historian asks: *Who preserved this story, and why? Does it agree with other evidence?* Archaeology shows the decline of Great Zimbabwe around the fifteenth century, and Portuguese writers record the Mutapa kingdom in the north. The tradition therefore fits independent evidence on the broad movement, even though the single reason ("salt") may be a simplification.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying oral tradition is unreliable and archaeology is always right"}
Both have strengths and weaknesses. Archaeology can be misinterpreted, as the myth of foreign builders showed, and oral tradition can be confirmed by independent evidence. A good answer assesses each source for the question being asked.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing primary sources with reliable sources"}
A primary source is close to the events, not automatically true. A Portuguese official's letter is primary but may be biased; a modern archaeologist's report is secondary in some uses but based on careful scientific method.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing sources without saying what they are used for"}
Do not simply list advantages and disadvantages. Say what each source contributes to a particular topic, such as the origins of Great Zimbabwe, the Mutapa's trade or the Ndebele state.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1 essays on the sources of history"}
Questions such as "Assess the contribution of oral tradition and archaeology to the history of pre-colonial Zimbabwe" need a balanced line of argument: what each source tells us, its weakness, examples (Great Zimbabwe, praise poems, Portuguese letters) and a judgement about how they work together. Use named examples, not general statements.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge"}
Cambridge History 9489 does not set a question on this topic as such, but the idea that evidence differs in value and must be tested underlies Paper 1 and Paper 3. Practise the same habit of asking who produced the evidence and why.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Historians use written sources, oral tradition, archaeology, rock art, linguistics and visual evidence, each with different strengths and weaknesses.
- Written sources for the plateau are mostly by outsiders; oral traditions give the African viewpoint but are open to telescoping and bias.
- Archaeology fills the gaps where nothing was written, but survival is uneven and objects need interpretation.
- Combining independent sources (triangulation) gives the most reliable account, as in the case of Great Zimbabwe's African origin.
- The myth of foreign builders shows how interpretation can be shaped by prejudice and corrected by evidence.
:::
