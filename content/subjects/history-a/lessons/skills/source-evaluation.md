## Introduction

A source is not an answer; it is a piece of evidence that someone created for a reason. A historian who simply copies a source repeats its author's claims. A historian who evaluates it asks who made it, when, for whom and why, and then checks what it says against what else is known. This is the skill examiners test whenever they set a source question: in ZIMSEC it is the objective of interpreting historical evidence, and in Cambridge Paper 1 it earns all 40 marks of a question.

This lesson teaches the method with invented sources from different periods, so that you can practise it on any topic. No new period knowledge is needed here. The types of source (written, oral, archaeological, visual) are covered in the lesson on sources of history, and historians' interpretations in the lesson on interpretations and approach. You should know the difference between a primary and a secondary source.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inference"}
A conclusion that can reasonably be drawn from a source although it is not stated directly, for example that the writer was afraid, or that a certain practice was common.
:::

:::definition{term="Provenance"}
The origin of a source: who made it (author), when (date), for whom (audience), why (purpose) and in what form (letter, report, speech, cartoon, oral account).
:::

:::definition{term="Reliability and usefulness"}
A source is reliable if it can be trusted to give an accurate account. It is useful if it helps to answer a particular question. An unreliable source can be very useful: a propaganda poster is a poor guide to what happened, but a good guide to what a government wanted people to believe.
:::

:::definition{term="Bias and point of view"}
Bias is a one-sided presentation that distorts the facts. A point of view is the view from which a person sees events. Every source has a point of view; this does not make it worthless. A value judgement is a statement of opinion ("a cruel regime") and not a fact.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The method in five steps"}
1. Message: what does the source say and mean? 2. Inference: what can I work out from it? 3. Provenance: who, when, for whom, why? 4. Test: check it against my own knowledge and other sources. 5. Judge: how reliable and how useful is it for this question?
:::

## Message, inference and tone

Start with what the source says, in your own words. Then ask what it implies. Read this invented source.

> **Source A** (invented for this question): a report to the Company by a fictional magistrate in Mashonaland, 1895. "The people are quiet, and the hut tax has been collected without trouble, though the chiefs complain of the demand for labour."

The *message* is that the district was orderly and the tax was collected. An *inference* is that the demand for labour was a source of tension, since the chiefs complained. The *tone* is calm and rather dismissive: the magistrate calls the people "quiet" and treats the complaint as minor. Examiners reward inferences supported by words from the source. "The magistrate wishes to appear in control" is an inference; "the magistrate is lying" is a claim that needs evidence.

## Provenance: author, date, audience, purpose

Every question about reliability begins with provenance. Ask four things.

**Author.** Was the writer present, well informed, an expert, an outsider, someone with a stake in the outcome? An official wrote Source A, so he knew the district but had a reason to show that his administration was successful.

**Date.** Is the source contemporary, or written years later? A contemporary account is not automatically better, since a participant may know only a small part of events, but a recollection written decades later may be shaped by memory and by later events.

**Audience.** Who was it written for? A private letter to a brother may be franker than a report to superiors, and a speech to a crowd is written to persuade.

**Purpose.** Why was it made? To inform, persuade, justify, entertain or conceal? Propaganda, official reports and memoirs all have purposes that shape what they include and omit.

:::example{title="Applying provenance"}
> **Source B** (invented for this question): a recollection told in the 1950s by a fictional Shona elder. "In those days the chiefs were no longer chiefs. Our young men were taken to work, and when the rains failed we were told that the ancestors were angry."

Provenance: the elder was a participant, so he knew what ordinary people felt, a perspective missing from Source A. But he spoke some 60 years after the events and may have been influenced by later events and by what interviewers wanted to hear. Comparing the two, both mention labour, and the difference is the people's attitude: calm in A, resentment in B. That difference is explained by provenance: an official reporting to his superiors has a reason to say that all is well, while an elder telling his own history has reasons to remember grievance.
:::

## Reliability and usefulness

Reliability and usefulness are different questions. A source can be used in four ways.

1. **For what it says**: the content may be accurate; check it against your own knowledge (does it fit what you know about the hut tax and labour demands?).
2. **For what it shows without meaning to**: for example, the language of Source A reveals the official's attitude to the people.
3. **For its purpose**: why was it made, and what does that tell us?
4. **Together with other sources**: corroboration (agreement with independent sources) increases confidence; contradiction requires an explanation.

When you judge reliability, give a reason from provenance and a check from content and knowledge. "It is biased because it is a government report" is weak. "It is likely to play down unrest because the magistrate would be blamed for it, and this is supported by evidence that unrest was widespread in 1896" is strong. When you judge usefulness, say for what question: Source A is useful for showing how officials described the situation, but less useful for showing African attitudes.

## Comparing two sources (Cambridge Paper 1, part (a))

In "Compare Sources A and B as evidence about…", do not summarise each source in turn. Organise by point: for each point say what A and B say, whether they agree or differ, and why. Then add an overall judgement, a reasoned conclusion on which source is more useful or how the differences should be explained. A strong comparison has three stages:

1. **Similarities and differences in content**, with short references to each source.
2. **Explanations** of the differences from provenance and context (author, date, audience, purpose).
3. **Evaluation**: which is more convincing for this question, and why?

## How far do the sources support a statement? (Cambridge Paper 1, part (b))

The question gives a statement, such as "The risings were caused mainly by religious leaders", or "Bismarck's policy was mainly responsible for unification". You use all the sources and your own knowledge. Plan the answer in this way.

1. Sort the sources into those that **support** the statement, those that **challenge** it, and any that do both.
2. Evaluate each one: how much weight can it bear, given its provenance, and what does your own knowledge say?
3. Bring in **own knowledge** to confirm, challenge or fill gaps (what the sources do not mention).
4. Reach a **judgement** about the extent of support and justify it. A good conclusion says how far and why, for example "the sources support the statement in part: A and C agree, but B and D show other causes, and my knowledge supports the more cautious view".

:::example{title="A short judgement paragraph"}
Statement: "The Terror was mainly a response to war." Two invented sources: a speech by a fictional deputy in 1793 saying the republic is in danger from foreign armies, and a diary by a fictional Paris shopkeeper in 1794 saying that neighbours were arrested for private quarrels. A paragraph: "Source A supports the statement, since the deputy links emergency measures to the armies on the frontier; as a speech to the Convention it was meant to persuade and exaggerates the danger, but the war is confirmed by my knowledge of the invasion of 1793. Source B challenges it by suggesting that the arrests depended on personal quarrels; the shopkeeper was a witness but wrote privately and his view is limited to one neighbourhood. Taken together, the sources show that war was an important cause but that fear and local rivalries also mattered, so they support the statement only in part."
:::

| Question | What to do |
|---|---|
| What does the source say or suggest? | Message, inference, tone, with words from the source |
| How reliable is it? | Provenance, then check against knowledge |
| How useful is it? | Useful for which question? |
| Compare A and B | By point; similarities, differences, explanation, judgement |
| How far do the sources support...? | Support, challenge, evaluation, own knowledge, judgement |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Rejecting a source because it is biased"}
All sources have a point of view. Ask what the bias reveals and what the source is still good for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using stock phrases"}
"It is a primary source so it is reliable" or "it was written later so it is unreliable" are not arguments. Give a reason linked to this source and this question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing sources one after another"}
In part (a) compare by theme; in part (b) group by their relation to the statement. Do not write a paragraph on each source in turn.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out own knowledge"}
In part (b) the sources are not enough. Use your knowledge to test and extend them, and to say what they omit.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test this skill. ZIMSEC's assessment objective on interpreting historical evidence appears in source-based questions and in the way essays use evidence. Cambridge Paper 1 (AS) sets four sources and two questions: part (a), compare two sources (15 marks), and part (b), how far the sources support a statement (25 marks), using all sources and own knowledge.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Source questions"}
Spend a minute on provenance before you write. Use short quotations or precise references. In part (a) give two or three points of comparison, each with an explanation from context. In part (b) aim to use every source, state the extent of support early and return to it in the conclusion. Divide your time in Cambridge Paper 1 roughly in proportion to the marks: 15 for part (a) and 25 for part (b).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read a source for message, inference and tone, using words from the source to support what you say.
- Use provenance (author, date, audience, purpose) to judge how far the source can be trusted and for what.
- Reliability and usefulness are different; even a biased source is useful for the attitudes and purposes it reveals.
- To compare sources, organise by point, explain similarities and differences from context and judge.
- To test a statement, sort sources into support and challenge, evaluate each, add own knowledge and reach a supported judgement about the extent.
:::
