## Introduction

Two historians can read the same documents and tell different stories. One says that British abolitionists ended the slave trade out of moral conviction; another says that Britain's economic interests had already turned against it. Neither is simply lying, and neither has a monopoly on truth. They are offering different **interpretations**, shaped by the questions they ask and the way they work. In Cambridge Paper 3 (40 marks) you meet an unattributed extract of up to 600 words and must say what you can learn from it about the historian's interpretation and approach, using your own knowledge of the topic. Half of the marks are for knowledge and half for the analysis of the interpretation.

This lesson teaches the skill using invented extracts on topics that are not set for Paper 3 (the ending of the slave trade and the partition of Africa), so that you can practise it first without the pressure of the exam topics. The Paper 3 debates are treated in their own lessons. For ZIMSEC students the skill is also useful: it helps with questions that ask you to weigh "historians' views" on the decline of Great Zimbabwe, the Mfecane or the origins of the Cold War.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Interpretation"}
A historian's overall explanation or judgement about a past event, person or period: what happened, why, and how important it was. An interpretation is a reasoned argument based on evidence, not a statement of fact.
:::

:::definition{term="Approach"}
How a historian goes about studying the past: the questions asked, the kinds of evidence and methods used, the assumptions and values behind the work, and the school of thought it belongs to (for example political, economic, social, "history from below", Marxist or nationalist).
:::

:::definition{term="Historiography"}
The study of how history has been written, including how interpretations have changed over time and why.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Why historians differ"}
1. **Evidence is incomplete.** Sources survive by chance and some voices are missing. 2. **Selection.** Historians choose which evidence to stress. 3. **New evidence and the passing of time.** New archives and methods change the picture. 4. **The time and place in which they write.** A historian's own society affects the questions asked. 5. **Ideology and beliefs.** Political, religious or national views influence emphasis. 6. **The questions asked.** An economic question gives a different answer from a moral or political one.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge only"}
This skill belongs to Cambridge Paper 3 (A Level only). ZIMSEC students benefit from it in essays that discuss different views, but the unattributed extract question is a Cambridge format.
:::

## Interpretation and approach

An **interpretation** is the answer a historian gives to a big question. The **approach** explains how the historian reached it. They are linked: a historian who asks economic questions and looks at trade statistics will probably reach an economic interpretation. In an exam you must be able to state both, in separate sentences, with evidence from the extract.

To find the **interpretation**, ask: What is the main claim of the whole passage? What does the author think was the cause, result or significance? Look for the topic sentence, the conclusion and the words that show judgement ("principally", "merely", "decisive", "a myth").

To find the **approach**, ask: What questions is the writer asking? What kinds of evidence are used (statistics, official papers, private letters, the voices of ordinary people)? What does the writer treat as most important (rulers, ideas, money, ordinary people)? What assumptions and values show in the language? Is the passage written with confidence or caution, and to whom?

## Extract A (invented): ending the slave trade

:::example{title="Extract A and its analysis"}
**Extract** — a passage written in the style of a historian's work (invented for this question; not the words of any real historian)

> The abolition of the British slave trade in 1807 is often presented as the triumph of conscience. Yet by 1800 the West Indian sugar islands were crowded with enslaved people and the sugar they produced was in surplus; planters in the older islands needed no more labour and were beginning to fear falling prices. Meanwhile, Britain's new industrial towns were finding other markets and other sources of wealth. A trade that had been the foundation of Atlantic profit was no longer indispensable. The Quakers and evangelical campaigners who petitioned Parliament deserve credit, but they succeeded because their cause no longer threatened the main interests of the country. Moral zeal required economic opportunity before it could win.

**Analysis.** *Interpretation:* abolition came about mainly because the slave trade had ceased to be essential to Britain's economy, so humanitarian campaigners could succeed. *Approach:* economic; the historian asks about profits, markets and labour supply, plays down moral motives ("deserve credit, but") and writes with confidence ("no longer indispensable"). *Own knowledge:* supports the view that industrialisation was changing Britain's economy and that planters worried about sugar prices, although historians dispute how far the slave economy was in decline in 1807. It challenges the view by pointing to the campaign of Wilberforce, Clarkson and Equiano, the mass petitions, and the evidence that the trade remained profitable to many merchants; some historians argue that abolition cost Britain money. The balance: the extract is one reasoned view among several and gives less weight to popular mobilisation.
:::

## Extract B (invented): the partition of Africa

:::example{title="Extract B and its analysis"}
**Extract** — a passage written in the style of a historian's work (invented for this question; not the words of any real historian)

> Historians who explain the partition of Africa by the needs of European investors forget that few investors were interested in Africa in 1880. Most of the continent was costly to enter, and its trade was small. The scramble began in the offices of ministers and diplomats who feared that a rival would seize a key position on the route to India or on the Nile. African rulers appear in this account as obstacles to be removed or friends to be won, and their own plans are barely mentioned. The partition was a by-product of European power politics, carried out by men who thought of strategy and prestige and who knew little of the societies they were dividing.

**Analysis.** *Interpretation:* the partition arose mainly from European strategic rivalry and diplomacy, not from economic needs. *Approach:* a political and diplomatic history, based on official papers; it challenges an economic argument directly ("forget that"), and itself admits that African agency is missing. *Own knowledge:* supports the importance of Egypt and the route to India for Britain, and the role of Berlin diplomacy; challenges the view by Leopold's commercial interests in the Congo, the role of companies and the economic motives of traders in West Africa, and the African resistance of the period (for example Menelik at Adwa in 1896). The extract's silence on Africans is a feature of its approach.
:::

## Using your knowledge

Knowledge is used in three ways: to **support** a point in the extract, to **challenge** it, and to **add** what the extract leaves out. Each use must be specific (dates, people, events) and linked to a claim in the extract. A paragraph of knowledge that is not attached to the extract earns little credit.

## A method for the question

1. **Read the whole extract once** and state its overall interpretation in one sentence.
2. **Break it down**: identify key phrases that reveal the argument (the claim and the reasons) and the approach (the question asked, the evidence, the values).
3. **Plan paragraphs** that each take a feature (for example, the claim about causes; the treatment of Africans; the tone), explain it with a quotation or reference, and test it with your knowledge.
4. **Judge the extract as a whole**: its strengths, its limits, and what it leaves out.
5. **Do not** summarise line by line, write a full account of the period, or treat "biased" as meaning "wrong".

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Summarising instead of analysing"}
Repeating what the extract says shows that you have read it. The question asks what it tells you about the historian's interpretation and approach.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a historian"}
The extracts are unattributed. Do not guess an author. Describe the approach (for example "an economic approach") without naming a person unless the question does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using knowledge without linking it"}
A long account of the topic that does not support or test a point in the extract earns little. Always say how your knowledge relates to the extract.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 3 (40 marks): about half of the marks reward your knowledge of the topic and half the analysis of the interpretation and approach. Spend a few minutes on reading and planning, aim for three or four developed paragraphs with references to the extract, and finish with a short judgement on how far the extract is a convincing whole. Practise on unfamiliar topics so that you can transfer the skill to the three set topics. ZIMSEC essays that ask about views of historians can be handled in the same way, with named schools of thought and your own evidence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An interpretation is a historian's reasoned explanation or judgement; the approach is how the historian studied the topic (questions, evidence, assumptions, values).
- Historians differ because of incomplete and selected evidence, new evidence, the time and place of writing, ideology and the questions they ask.
- To read an extract, find its overall claim, the way it argues and the features that show its approach, such as tone, evidence and silences.
- Use your own knowledge to support, challenge and add to the extract, linking each point to the passage.
- Treat the extract as a whole, do not name an author, and do not mistake bias for error.
:::
