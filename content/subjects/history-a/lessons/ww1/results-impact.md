## Introduction

When the guns stopped on 11 November 1918, millions of families in Europe, Africa, Asia and America counted their dead. The war had destroyed four empires, redrawn the map of Europe and the Middle East, shifted economic power across the Atlantic and changed the lives of women, workers and colonial peoples. It also left unsolved problems that helped to cause a second world war only twenty years later.

This lesson examines the results of the war under four headings: human, political, economic and social, and territorial, and then considers its impact on Africa and on the world. The peace settlement (the Treaty of Versailles) is covered in the lessons on the peace settlements and the League of Nations; here we concentrate on consequences. You should know the course of the war from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mandate"}
A territory formerly ruled by Germany or the Ottoman Empire that the League of Nations placed under the administration of a victorious power, which was supposed to prepare it for eventual self-government.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-determination"}
The principle that peoples should be allowed to choose their own government. It was promoted by the American president Woodrow Wilson in 1918 and used to justify new states in Europe, but it was not applied to colonies.
:::

:::definition{term="Reparations"}
Payments demanded from a defeated state to compensate the victors for the damage of war.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1917-1923: the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires end.
- 28 June 1919: Treaty of Versailles. 1920: League of Nations begins. 1918-19: influenza pandemic.
- 1918: women over 30 gain the vote in Britain; 1918-19: women in Germany; 1920: the 19th Amendment in the United States.
- Woodrow Wilson (American president); Georges Clemenceau (France); David Lloyd George (Britain).
:::

## Human losses

Historians' estimates differ, and exact figures do not exist. About nine to ten million soldiers are usually thought to have died, and some twenty million were wounded, many of them permanently. Several million civilians also died, from famine, disease, massacre and the effects of blockade and occupation. If civilians are included, most estimates for total deaths lie between about fifteen and twenty million. France and Germany each lost well over a million men, and Russia and Austria-Hungary also had very heavy losses. Entire communities lost a generation of young men, and societies had to care for widows, orphans and disabled veterans.

## Political results

The war ended four great empires. Russia's Tsar fell in 1917, the German Kaiser abdicated on 9 November 1918 and Germany became a republic, the Austro-Hungarian Emperor lost his throne and his empire broke into new states, and the Ottoman sultanate was abolished in 1922 as Turkey became a republic in 1923. New or restored states appeared in Europe: Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary and the Kingdom of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia), together with Finland and the Baltic states. Wilson's principle of self-determination was used to justify the new borders, but many states contained large minorities, which later caused conflict.

The Bolshevik revolution made Russia the first communist state, which alarmed other governments and brought revolutionary attempts in Germany and Hungary in 1918-19. The League of Nations was founded in 1920 to prevent future wars, but without the United States, whose Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, and without Germany and Russia at first, it was weak. In Germany many people believed the "stab in the back" myth, that the army had not been defeated but had been betrayed at home, which damaged the new republic.

## Economic and social results

Europe's economy was damaged. Governments had borrowed heavily, mostly from the United States, and Britain, once the world's main creditor, now owed large debts to the United States. Farmland and factories in northern France and Belgium were destroyed. Inflation hit many countries, and in Germany in 1923 it became hyperinflation, when savings were wiped out. The United States emerged as the strongest economy and the world's leading creditor; Europe depended on American loans, a weakness exposed later by the depression. Reparations and the repayment of war debts created disputes between the Allies.

The war also changed society. Women took jobs in factories, offices, transport and agriculture while men were away, and they worked as nurses and drivers. This contributed to the extension of the vote to women in several countries. But many women lost their jobs when men returned, and views about women's roles changed more slowly. Trade unions grew stronger, governments took more control of the economy, and wartime propaganda, rationing and censorship changed people's attitudes to the state. The influenza pandemic of 1918-19, spread by the movement of troops and ships, killed far more people than any other epidemic of the period. Estimates of the world total are very uncertain, from about twenty million to as many as fifty million or more, and millions died in Africa and Asia.

## Territorial changes

Germany lost about 13 per cent of its territory: Alsace and Lorraine to France, land to Poland (giving it access to the sea and cutting off East Prussia), small areas to Belgium and Denmark, and the Saar was placed under the League for fifteen years. It lost all its colonies, and the Rhineland was demilitarised. The Austro-Hungarian Empire was divided, and the Ottoman Empire lost its Arab provinces, which became mandates: Syria and Lebanon went to France, and Iraq and Palestine to Britain. Italy and Japan gained territory but Italians felt they had not received enough.

## The impact on Africa

Germany's African colonies became mandates: Tanganyika to Britain, Togoland and Cameroon divided between Britain and France, South-West Africa to South Africa, and Rwanda and Burundi to Belgium. The people were not consulted. Africans had served as soldiers and carriers, and many had died; they had seen Europeans fighting each other, which weakened the idea of European superiority. Veterans and educated Africans began to ask for rights, and the Pan-African Congress of 1919 met in Paris. Colonial governments often responded by raising taxes and recruiting labour to pay for the war's costs. Africans in the settler colonies received little reward, and settlers increased their demands for political control. In Southern Rhodesia, whose volunteers had fought in the war, white settlers voted in 1922 for self-government, which they gained in 1923. Historians link the war to the later growth of African nationalism, but they stress that other causes were also important.

:::example{title="Weighing short-term and long-term results"}
Question: what was the most important result of the war? A strong answer chooses and tests. "The most important result was political, because the collapse of the empires created new states with disputed borders and left Germany humiliated and resentful, which helped to cause the Second World War. The economic effects mattered, but they worked through politics: Germany's reparations and inflation damaged the republic. The social effects, such as the vote for women, were important but did not shape international relations."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving exact casualty figures"}
Death tolls are estimates and vary. Write "historians estimate that about nine to ten million soldiers died".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing results with the peace treaties"}
The treaties are part of the results, but the war also had economic, social and colonial effects that do not come from the treaties.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Africa as unaffected"}
Africa supplied soldiers and carriers, changed rulers in the former German colonies and suffered from influenza; mention these.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Estimates of deaths range widely: about nine to ten million soldiers, and fifteen to twenty million including civilians.
- Four empires fell (Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman) and new states appeared; the League of Nations was founded.
- Debts, inflation and the rise of the United States as creditor changed the world economy.
- Women gained jobs and, in several countries, the vote; the influenza pandemic of 1918-19 killed millions.
- German colonies became mandates; Africans served as soldiers and carriers, and the war weakened European prestige.
:::
