## Introduction

The First World War was fought on land, at sea and in the air, in Europe, the Middle East, Asia and Africa. It lasted from 28 July 1914 until the armistice of 11 November 1918. Many expected a short war: soldiers were told they would be home by Christmas. Instead, the Western Front settled into lines of trenches that hardly moved for more than three years, and the war became a contest of resources, endurance and industry.

This lesson follows the main events and turning points: the failure of the German plan in 1914, trench warfare and the great battles of 1916 and 1917, the war on the Eastern Front, the war at sea, the colonial campaigns in Africa, the entry of the United States and the withdrawal of Russia, and the final German offensive and collapse in 1918. It does not discuss strategy debates. You should know the causes of the war from the previous two lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Western Front"}
The line of trenches, running about 700 kilometres from the North Sea to the Swiss frontier, on which the main German armies faced the armies of France, Britain and Belgium.
:::

:::definition{term="Total war"}
A war in which a state uses all its economic and human resources, including factories, workers, civilians and propaganda, to defeat the enemy.
:::

:::definition{term="Unrestricted submarine warfare"}
The German policy of sinking merchant ships without warning in a war zone around Britain, in order to cut the supply of food and goods. It was begun in 1915, restricted after protests, and resumed on 1 February 1917.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- August-September 1914: Belgium invaded; the Battle of the Marne halts the Germans. 26-30 August 1914: Tannenberg.
- 1916: Verdun (February-December) and the Somme (from 1 July). 31 May 1916: Jutland.
- 1917: unrestricted submarine warfare (1 February); the United States declares war (6 April); Russia leaves the war (Brest-Litovsk, 3 March 1918).
- 21 March 1918: German spring offensive. 8 August 1918: Amiens. 11 November 1918: armistice.
:::

## 1914: the failure of the quick victory

Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August 1914. The advance through Belgium was slowed by Belgian resistance at Liège and by the British Expeditionary Force, which fought at Mons on 23 August. In the Battle of the Marne (5-12 September) French and British forces halted the German advance near Paris, and the Schlieffen Plan failed. Both sides tried to outflank each other in the "race to the sea" and dug trenches. By the end of 1914 the front ran from the Channel coast to Switzerland.

In the east, Russia mobilised faster than Germany had expected, but its armies were poorly supplied. At Tannenberg (26-30 August 1914) the Germans under Hindenburg and Ludendorff destroyed a Russian army. Against Austria-Hungary the Russians had success in Galicia. The Ottoman Empire entered the war on the German side in October-November 1914.

## Trench warfare and new weapons

Soldiers lived in trenches protected by barbed wire and machine guns, which made attack extremely costly. Artillery bombardments, sometimes lasting days, were followed by infantry attacks that often gained little ground. Germany first used poisonous gas on a large scale at Ypres in April 1915, and both sides used it. Tanks were first used by Britain in September 1916 on the Somme. Aircraft were used for observation and later for combat and bombing; submarines threatened shipping. Conditions in the trenches brought mud, rats, disease and "shell shock", the name then given to severe psychological trauma.

## 1915-1917: battles of attrition

The generals tried to break the deadlock with great offensives. At **Verdun** (February-December 1916) Germany attacked a French fortress in the hope of "bleeding France white"; the French under Pétain held on, and both sides suffered enormous losses. At the **Somme** (from 1 July 1916) the British and French attacked to relieve Verdun; the British suffered about 57,000 casualties on the first day, one of the heaviest in their history, and the battle ended in November with little ground gained. In 1917 a French offensive under Nivelle failed and was followed by mutinies in the French army; the British fought the long battle of Passchendaele (Third Ypres) from July to November. Historians estimate that casualties for these battles were in the hundreds of thousands on each side, and figures vary.

On the Eastern Front the Germans and Austrians drove the Russians back in 1915. The Russian Brusilov Offensive of June 1916 had success against Austria but lost heavily. Italy joined the Allies in May 1915 and fought Austria along the Isonzo; Bulgaria joined the Central Powers in 1915 and Romania the Allies in 1916. In 1915-16 the Allies tried and failed to force the Dardanelles at Gallipoli.

## The war at sea

Britain used its navy to blockade Germany, which led to food shortages. The only major fleet action was at **Jutland** (31 May - 1 June 1916), which was indecisive, but the German fleet stayed largely in port after it. Germany used U-boats against merchant ships. The sinking of the liner *Lusitania* in May 1915, in which about 1,200 people died, including more than 100 Americans, led the United States to protest, and Germany restricted its campaign. On 1 February 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, believing that it could starve Britain before American help arrived. Britain countered with the convoy system from 1917, which reduced shipping losses.

## The colonial war in Africa

The war reached Africa within days. Togoland was captured by British and French forces in August 1914. In Cameroon the Germans were defeated by 1916. German South-West Africa was conquered in 1915 by forces from the Union of South Africa under Louis Botha. In German East Africa (Tanganyika) the German commander Paul von Lettow-Vorbeck, with African askari soldiers, led a long guerrilla campaign against British, Belgian, Portuguese and South African forces; he surrendered only after the armistice, on 25 November 1918, in Northern Rhodesia. Hundreds of thousands of Africans served as soldiers or, much more often, as carriers; many died of disease and hardship, and the numbers are uncertain. Settler volunteers from Southern Rhodesia fought in East Africa and in Europe, and Africans served in the Rhodesia Native Regiment and as carriers. Africans could not choose whether to be involved in a war between European powers.

## 1917-1918: the turning points and the end

In 1917 two events changed the war. After the sinking of American ships and the publication of the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany proposed an alliance with Mexico against the United States, the United States declared war on 6 April 1917. It brought money, supplies and, by 1918, large numbers of fresh troops. In Russia, revolution led to the fall of the Tsar in March 1917 and to Bolshevik rule in November. The Bolsheviks made peace by the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** (3 March 1918), in which Russia lost large territories.

Germany now moved troops west and launched a series of offensives from 21 March 1918, which advanced far but did not break through, and exhausted its army. On 8 August the Allies attacked at Amiens with tanks and aircraft, in what Ludendorff called the black day of the German army, and pushed the Germans back in the "Hundred Days". Germany's allies collapsed: Bulgaria signed an armistice on 29 September, the Ottoman Empire on 30 October and Austria-Hungary on 3 November. Revolution broke out in Germany at the start of November, with a naval mutiny at Kiel. The Kaiser abdicated on 9 November, and the armistice was signed at Compiègne and took effect at 11 am on 11 November 1918.

:::example{title="Explaining a turning point"}
Question: why did Germany lose the war in 1918? **Step 1**, the entry of the United States gave the Allies resources that Germany could not match. **Step 2**, the British blockade left Germany short of food and materials. **Step 3**, the spring offensive failed and used up the best German troops, while the Allies, with tanks, aircraft and better coordination, advanced from August. **Step 4**, Germany's allies collapsed and revolution broke out at home. A good answer then ranks the reasons: the combination of exhaustion at home and military defeat in the field.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing every battle"}
Examination questions ask for turning points and reasons. Choose a few battles and explain what they changed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting Africa"}
The war was global. Name Togoland, Cameroon, South-West Africa and East Africa, and the use of African soldiers and carriers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking Russia left the war when the Tsar fell"}
The Tsar abdicated in March 1917, but the Provisional Government continued the war. Russia left only with the Bolsheviks' Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March 1918.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Schlieffen Plan failed at the Marne (September 1914); trench lines then stretched from the North Sea to Switzerland.
- Machine guns, barbed wire and artillery made defence stronger than attack; gas, tanks and aircraft were new weapons.
- Verdun and the Somme (1916) were battles of attrition with enormous losses; the sea war included Jutland and the U-boat campaign.
- In Africa the German colonies were conquered, apart from the guerrilla campaign in East Africa; Africans served as soldiers and carriers.
- The United States entered in April 1917 and Russia left in March 1918; the Allied advance from August 1918 and the collapse of the Central Powers brought the armistice of 11 November 1918.
:::
