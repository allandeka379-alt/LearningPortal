## Introduction

When war broke out in August 1914, Europe was at the height of its power. It controlled most of Africa and Asia, produced most of the world's industrial goods and had been free from a war between all the Great Powers since 1815. Yet within five years roughly nine to ten million soldiers would be dead (historians' estimates differ), four empires would be gone, and the colonies of Africa would have been dragged into a quarrel not of their making. Why did the Great Powers of Europe fight?

This lesson explains the **long-term causes**, the developments between about 1890 and 1914 that made war more likely: rivalry among the Great Powers, the system of alliances, militarism and arms races, imperial and economic competition, the Balkans, and the war plans. The decisions of July 1914 are treated in the next lesson. For ZIMSEC this is the "causes of the First World War"; for Cambridge it is the context for Paper 3 Topic 1, where historians' interpretations are examined (see the debate lessons). You should know the settlement of 1871 and the Berlin Conference of 1884-85.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alliance system"}
The network of treaties by which Great Powers promised support to each other. By 1907 Europe was divided between the Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and the Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain).
:::

:::definition{term="Militarism"}
The belief that a country should maintain a large military force and be ready to use it, and the influence of the army and navy on government policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Weltpolitik"}
"World policy": the German aim, under Kaiser Wilhelm II from the late 1890s, to win a larger share of colonies, trade and influence worldwide, including by building a battle fleet.
:::

:::definition{term="Schlieffen Plan"}
The German military plan, developed by Alfred von Schlieffen (chief of the General Staff 1891-1905) and later modified, to avoid a long war on two fronts by defeating France quickly through Belgium and then turning against Russia.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1879: Dual Alliance (Germany and Austria-Hungary); 1882: Triple Alliance with Italy. 1890: Bismarck dismissed; the treaty with Russia lapses. 1894: Franco-Russian alliance confirmed.
- 1898 and 1900: German Navy Laws (Tirpitz). 1904: Entente Cordiale. 1906: HMS *Dreadnought*. 1907: Anglo-Russian Convention.
- 1905-06 and 1911: Moroccan crises (Tangier; Algeciras; Agadir). 1908: Austria annexes Bosnia-Herzegovina. 1912-13: Balkan Wars.
:::

## The alliance system and the diplomatic revolution

Bismarck's aim after 1871 was to keep France isolated. He bound Austria-Hungary to Germany in the Dual Alliance (1879), added Italy in 1882, and kept Russia friendly by treaties, the last the Reinsurance Treaty of 1887. After the young Kaiser Wilhelm II dismissed him in 1890 the Reinsurance Treaty was allowed to lapse, partly because Germany's new leaders thought it incompatible with the Austrian alliance. Russia, which wanted an ally against Germany, turned to France, and the two signed a military alliance confirmed in 1894.

Britain had remained outside permanent alliances. But it felt isolated during the Boer War (1899-1902) and troubled by German naval building and colonial ambitions. It made an alliance with Japan (1902), settled its colonial disputes with France in the Entente Cordiale (1904), and, after Russia's defeat in the war with Japan, reached an agreement with Russia in 1907. The **diplomatic revolution** was the shift from the Bismarckian system, in which France was isolated, to two opposing blocs, in which Germany felt encircled. The Entente was not a formal alliance, but staff talks between Britain and France from 1906 made a British commitment more likely. Historians note that the system was not rigid: Italy's commitment was weak, Britain was not bound to fight, and the blocs were not certain to act together. But they increased mistrust and meant that a local conflict could draw in several powers.

## Militarism, arms races and the naval race

Every Great Power except Britain had conscription, and army sizes grew. In 1912-13 Germany passed large Army Laws, France extended military service to three years, and Russia began a programme of expansion. The most dramatic competition was naval. Admiral Tirpitz persuaded the Kaiser to pass Navy Laws in 1898 and 1900, and later added others, to build a fleet strong enough to challenge Britain. To Britain, dependent on the sea for food and trade, this was a threat. In 1906 it launched the *Dreadnought*, a battleship so powerful that it made older ships obsolete, and a race in dreadnoughts followed, with a "scare" in Britain in 1909 about German building. British attempts to agree limits on naval building (such as the Haldane mission of 1912) failed. The race increased cost, made Britain and Germany hostile, and drove Britain towards France and Russia.

## Imperial and economic rivalry

German leaders believed that the country's industry and population entitled it to a "place in the sun". Germany's steel output passed Britain's before 1914. Colonial and economic rivalry produced two Moroccan crises. In March 1905 the Kaiser visited Tangier to challenge France's plans for Morocco; at the Algeciras Conference (1906) Germany found that only Austria supported it, and Britain stood with France. In July 1911 Germany sent the gunboat *Panther* to Agadir, and Britain warned it; the crisis ended with Germany receiving territory in the French Congo in return for recognising a French protectorate in Morocco. Each crisis strengthened the Entente and left German public opinion resentful. Not all historians give economic rivalry great weight: Germany and Britain were also major trading partners, and many businessmen wanted peace.

## The Balkans

The decline of the Ottoman Empire left a gap in south-eastern Europe. Austria-Hungary, a multinational empire, feared the nationalism of the Slavs within its borders, and saw Serbia, which wanted to unite Serbs and other South Slavs, as a danger. Russia saw itself as protector of Slavs and wanted influence in the Balkans. In October 1908 Austria annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina, which it had occupied since 1878; Serbia was furious and Russia, unprepared for war, had to accept the annexation, though it resented this. In the Balkan Wars of 1912-13 Serbia and its allies drove the Ottomans out of most of Europe, and then fought among themselves; Serbia grew larger, and Austria blocked its access to the sea by supporting the creation of Albania. By 1914 Austria's leaders felt that Serbia must be dealt with, and Serbian nationalist groups, among them the secret society known as the Black Hand, wished to challenge Austrian rule in Bosnia.

## War plans and nationalism

The war plans shortened the time for diplomacy. The Schlieffen Plan assumed that Russia would be slow to mobilise, so France must be defeated in about six weeks through Belgium; once Russia mobilised, Germany would have to attack France at once, whatever the cause of the crisis. France's Plan XVII proposed an offensive into Alsace-Lorraine, lost in 1871. Russia's mobilisation could not easily be limited to a single frontier. Nationalism worked in different ways: French desire for revenge for Alsace-Lorraine, pan-Germanism, pan-Slavism, and the nationalism of peoples within Austria-Hungary.

:::example{title="Weighing the long-term causes"}
Question: which long-term cause was most important? A strong paragraph chooses and justifies. "The Balkans were the most important long-term cause, because it was there that the other tensions came together. Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia, Russia's role as protector and the alliances that bound Germany to Austria and France to Russia meant that a Balkan quarrel was likely to become a European war. The naval race and Moroccan crises increased mistrust, but they had not produced a war, whereas the Balkans did." The paragraph ends by testing the claim: the Balkan crisis only led to a world war because of the alliances and war plans.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the alliances caused the war"}
They increased the risk of a wider conflict, but they did not force anyone to fight; the decisions of July 1914 mattered (see the next lesson). Britain, for example, was not bound by treaty to support France.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a list of causes without weighing them"}
Name each cause with a specific fact, link it to the outbreak, then compare. Which were permissive conditions and which were direct pressures?
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming Germany alone"}
Historians disagree about responsibility. Treat French revanchism, Russian mobilisation, Austrian aims in the Balkans and British policy as well as German ones.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bismarck's alliances isolated France; after 1890 Russia and France allied, and Britain agreed ententes with France (1904) and Russia (1907), producing two blocs.
- Militarism and arms races, above all the Anglo-German naval race after the Navy Laws and the *Dreadnought*, increased mistrust.
- The Moroccan crises of 1905-06 and 1911 strengthened the Entente; economic rivalry mattered but historians debate its weight.
- Austro-Serbian hostility, the annexation of Bosnia (1908) and the Balkan Wars (1912-13) made the Balkans the most dangerous region.
- War plans, especially the Schlieffen Plan, reduced the time for diplomacy; historians disagree about how far long-term causes made war inevitable.
:::
