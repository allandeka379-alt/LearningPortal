## Introduction

On the morning of 28 June 1914 an Archduke and his wife drove through Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia, in an open car. Five weeks later, five Great Powers were at war. The speed is striking: the Austrian government took nearly four weeks to deliver an ultimatum, but once it was sent it took only twelve days for the war to spread from a quarrel with Serbia to a continental war that Britain also joined. Historians still ask whether the war came because of long-term forces, which the previous lesson described, or because of decisions taken by a small number of men in July 1914.

This lesson follows the crisis day by day, identifies the decisions that mattered, and considers the role of chance, which Cambridge calls contingency. It is the second half of the ZIMSEC topic on the causes of the war and the main subject matter for Cambridge Paper 3 Topic 1. You should know the tensions and alliances described in the lesson on the long-term causes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blank cheque"}
The name given to the assurance that Germany gave Austria-Hungary on 5-6 July 1914, promising German support whatever action Austria decided to take against Serbia.
:::

:::definition{term="Ultimatum"}
A final demand with a time limit, which threatens serious consequences, such as war, if it is not met.
:::

:::definition{term="Mobilisation"}
Calling up reserves and moving troops and supplies to the frontiers. In 1914 it was regarded as a step to war, because the great armies took days to assemble and the war plans required speed.
:::

:::definition{term="Contingency"}
The role of chance, accident and unforeseen circumstances in events, as opposed to long-term causes or deliberate aims.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 28 June 1914: Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie are shot at Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip, a young Bosnian Serb nationalist.
- 5-6 July: Germany's blank cheque. 23 July: the Austrian ultimatum. 25 July: Serbia's reply. 28 July: Austria declares war on Serbia.
- 30 July: Russia orders general mobilisation. 1 August: Germany declares war on Russia. 3 August: Germany declares war on France. 4 August: Germany invades Belgium; Britain declares war on Germany.
- Individuals: Berchtold, Conrad, Tisza (Austria-Hungary); Wilhelm II, Bethmann Hollweg, Moltke (Germany); Nicholas II, Sazonov (Russia); Poincaré (France); Grey, Asquith (Britain).
:::

## The assassination

Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, visited Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 to inspect army manoeuvres. A group of young Bosnian Serb nationalists was waiting along the route. One of them threw a bomb that missed. Later the Archduke's driver took a wrong turn, and the car stopped almost in front of Gavrilo Princip, who shot the Archduke and his wife Sophie. Historians usually point to the part of chance: with better security, or a driver who knew the route, the attack would have failed.

The assassins had been helped by Serbian weapons and contacts. Austrian investigators concluded that the plot had been prepared in Belgrade. Historians agree that officers connected with the secret Black Hand organisation helped, but they disagree about whether the Serbian government knew of the plot and what it did to prevent it.

## Austria and the blank cheque

Austria-Hungary's leaders, including the foreign minister Berchtold and the chief of the General Staff, Conrad, saw an opportunity to deal with Serbia by force. The Hungarian prime minister Tisza was cautious, fearing a wider war, but was persuaded. Before acting, Vienna needed to know whether Germany would support it against Russia. On 5-6 July the Kaiser Wilhelm II and the Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg told the Austrian ambassador and Count Hoyos, who had brought a letter from Vienna, that Germany would back whatever Austria decided. This is the **blank cheque**. The German leaders hoped that the war would be a localised one between Austria and Serbia, and that Russia would not intervene; they were also prepared to take the risk of a larger war. Historians disagree about whether this was a calculated gamble on a limited war or a decision to risk a European war.

## The ultimatum and the failure of mediation

Austria waited until 23 July to deliver its ultimatum, partly to finish its preparations and partly so that the French president and prime minister, who were visiting Russia, would have left St Petersburg. The note made ten demands and gave Serbia 48 hours. It required Serbia to suppress anti-Austrian propaganda and organisations and to allow Austrian officials to take part in an inquiry within Serbia. Serbia replied on 25 July, accepting most of the demands but rejecting the participation of Austrian officials on Serbian soil. Austria broke off relations and, on 28 July, declared war on Serbia. Most historians think that Austria had drafted the ultimatum so that it would be rejected.

Attempts to prevent a wider war failed. The British foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, proposed a conference of the powers on 26 July, which Germany declined. On 28 July the Kaiser, who had read Serbia's reply, suggested that Austria should halt in Belgrade, but Austria did not accept, and Germany did not press it hard. Exchanges of telegrams between the Kaiser and the Tsar ("Willy-Nicky") also failed.

## Mobilisation and declarations of war

Russia, which saw itself as the protector of Serbia and had been humiliated in 1908-09, ordered partial mobilisation against Austria on 29 July and, after its generals said that this was impractical, general mobilisation on 30 July. Tsar Nicholas II hesitated and changed his mind. Germany regarded general mobilisation as a threat, and, since the Schlieffen Plan depended on speed, demanded that Russia stop. When it did not, Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August. It declared war on France on 3 August, claiming that French aircraft had attacked, a statement now regarded as false, and in practice because the war plan required an attack on France. France had promised Russia support, and President Poincaré had reaffirmed this in St Petersburg.

On 2 August Germany presented Belgium with an ultimatum demanding free passage; when Belgium refused, German troops invaded on 4 August. Britain had a treaty of 1839 guaranteeing Belgian neutrality, and many British leaders saw a German attack on France through Belgium as a threat to British interests, since it would put a hostile power on the Channel coast. The Cabinet was divided until the invasion. Britain sent an ultimatum, which expired at 11 pm on 4 August, and then declared war. Austria declared war on Russia on 6 August. Italy declared neutrality, claiming that the Triple Alliance was defensive, and entered the war against Austria in 1915.

## Individuals and contingency

Historians weigh the decisions of key individuals very differently. Berchtold and Conrad pressed for war against Serbia. Wilhelm II and Bethmann Hollweg took the risk of the blank cheque; Moltke wanted war sooner rather than later, believing that Russia's military expansion would soon make Germany's task harder. Sazonov and the Russian generals pressed for mobilisation, and Poincaré supported Russia. Nicholas II hesitated. Grey did not make clear to Germany before 29 July what Britain would do, which some argue weakened deterrence. Contingency also played a part: the wrong turn in Sarajevo, the Kaiser's absence on a cruise in the early days, delays in telegrams and the speed of the mobilisation timetables. Cambridge candidates must therefore weigh motive, the aims of governments, against contingency, accident and misjudgment.

:::example{title="Analysing a decision"}
Question: how important was the blank cheque in the outbreak of war? **Step 1**, it gave Austria the confidence to send a harsh ultimatum because it believed Germany would deter Russia. **Step 2**, it meant that Germany lost control of events, since Austria decided the timing and terms. **Step 3**, but Austria had its own reasons for acting, and Russia's mobilisation and France's support turned a local war into a European one. A balanced answer would say that the blank cheque was necessary for the crisis to escalate but not sufficient: other decisions in St Petersburg, Paris and Berlin were also needed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the assassination caused the war"}
It was the trigger. Without the alliances, tensions and war plans, a murder in Bosnia need not have led to a world war.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing mobilisation with a declaration of war"}
Mobilisation was a step towards war, and under the German plan it made war almost immediate, but it was not itself a declaration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the events out of order"}
Learn the sequence: ultimatum, Serbia's reply, Austria's declaration, Russian mobilisation, German declarations, Belgium, Britain.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip shot Franz Ferdinand and Sophie at Sarajevo; chance helped the assassins.
- Germany's blank cheque (5-6 July) encouraged Austria to send a harsh ultimatum to Serbia on 23 July; Serbia's reply (25 July) was not accepted and Austria declared war on 28 July.
- Attempts at mediation failed; Russia mobilised (29-30 July), and Germany declared war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August).
- The invasion of Belgium on 4 August brought Britain into the war.
- Historians weigh the aims of key individuals against alliances, war plans and chance (contingency).
:::
