## Introduction

After the revolutions of 1848-49 the two great German powers stood in very different positions. Austria had been rescued by its army and by Russian help, and in 1850 it seemed to have won the contest for leadership in Germany when Prussia gave way at Olmütz. Twelve years later the balance had moved: Prussia was richer, Austria was in financial difficulty and without friends, and Prussian liberals looked to Berlin for leadership. This lesson explains why the balance moved, and what part Otto von Bismarck played before he became Minister-President in 1862.

It links the failure of 1848-49 with the events of the 1860s. It is background to the next lesson, which deals with the constitutional crisis and the wars of 1864 and 1866. You should know what the Frankfurt Parliament tried to do and how it failed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Olmütz (the Punctation of Olmütz)"}
The agreement of 29 November 1850 in which Prussia, under pressure from Austria and Russia, agreed to demobilise, to give up its Erfurt Union plan and to accept the restored German Confederation. Prussians called it the "humiliation of Olmütz".
:::

:::definition{term="Erfurt Union"}
A plan of 1849-50, associated with the Prussian adviser Radowitz, to unite the German states except Austria in a federal union led by the King of Prussia.
:::

:::definition{term="Realpolitik"}
Politics based on power and practical interests, not on principles or ideals. The word is associated with Bismarck's methods.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 29 November 1850: Olmütz. Schwarzenberg (Austrian minister-president) and Manteuffel (Prussian minister-president).
- 1851: Bismarck becomes Prussia's delegate to the Federal Diet at Frankfurt (until 1859). 1859-62: ambassador in St Petersburg, then briefly in Paris.
- 1853: the Zollverein is renewed without Austria. 1853-56: Crimean War.
- 1858: Wilhelm becomes regent for his brother, Friedrich Wilhelm IV, whose mind has failed. 1859: war between Austria and France with Piedmont; the Nationalverein is founded.
:::

## Olmütz, 1850

In 1849-50 Prussia tried to turn the failure of Frankfurt to its advantage by proposing the Erfurt Union, in which the king would lead a smaller Germany without Austria. Many princes joined reluctantly; Austria under Schwarzenberg refused to accept it and insisted that the old Confederation, with the Diet at Frankfurt and Austria presiding, be restored. The crisis came in autumn 1850 in Hesse-Kassel, where the Elector quarrelled with his parliament and appealed to the Diet. Austrian and Bavarian troops prepared to enter the state and Prussia mobilised, because a Prussian military road crossed it. The Tsar supported Austria.

At Olmütz on 29 November the Prussian minister Manteuffel yielded: Prussia would demobilise, abandon the Erfurt Union and attend conferences to revive the Confederation. The Confederation was restored in 1851. Prussians were humiliated, but Prussia was not ready to fight: its army was not prepared, it had no allies, and Russia supported Austria.

## Economic rivalry: the Zollverein

Austria's next move was to try to break Prussian leadership of the Zollverein by proposing a much larger central European customs union that would include the whole Habsburg Empire. The plan failed, because Austria's industry and finances could not bear free trade and because the southern states valued the benefits of the existing Zollverein. After tense negotiations in 1851-53, the Zollverein treaties were renewed without Austria (Hanover joined in 1854), and Austria had to accept a trade agreement with only modest concessions. Prussia emerged as the clear economic leader.

**Prussian growth.** Coal mining and iron production in the Ruhr and Silesia expanded, the railway network grew, and firms such as Krupp in Essen made steel and arms. The Zollverein gave large firms a market. Prussia's revenue grew with its economy, which later allowed the army to be modernised. In 1862 a trade treaty with France opened Prussian markets further and placed the southern states in a position where leaving the Zollverein, and with it the Prussian market, would have been very costly.

**Austrian difficulties.** Austria's economy was largely agricultural and its finances were weak. Its government spent heavily on the army and on the costly mobilisation during the Crimean War, borrowed repeatedly and relied on paper money. The war of 1859 in Italy cost more.

## Diplomatic and political change

In the Crimean War (1853-56) Austria refused to help Russia, which had rescued it in 1849, and also did not fight against Russia. Russia felt betrayed and Britain and France did not trust Austria either, so Austria was left isolated. Prussia was neutral, was admitted late to the peace congress in Paris and stayed on good terms with Russia, which later mattered.

In 1859 France and Piedmont fought Austria in northern Italy. Austria lost the battles of Magenta and Solferino and ceded Lombardy. Prussia mobilised but declined to help unless it was given command of the federal forces in Germany, which Austria would not grant. The war made German nationalists look at Prussia as the only possible leader. In 1859 liberals and nationalists in the German states founded the **Nationalverein** (National Association), which supported a united Germany under Prussian leadership.

Inside Prussia, politics was changing. The constitution of 1850 gave an assembly elected on a three-class franchise, which favoured the wealthy, but liberals won the elections of 1858 after Wilhelm became regent, a period of liberal hopes called the "New Era". This growth of liberalism, and the pressure for an army that could defend Prussia, created the conflict over the army reform that Bismarck inherited in 1862.

## Bismarck's role up to 1859

Bismarck was a Pomeranian landowner who entered politics as a staunch conservative and royalist. In 1850 he spoke in defence of the decision at Olmütz, partly because he wanted to avoid a war Prussia could not win. In 1851 he went to Frankfurt as Prussian envoy to the Diet. There he saw how Austria treated Prussia as a junior partner, and his view changed. He came to believe that Austria would never give Prussia an equal place, and that Germany was too small for both powers to lead. His letters from Frankfurt suggested that Prussia should look to its own interests, even to cooperation with France, and not to old loyalty to Austria. During the war of 1859 he argued that Prussia should use Austria's difficulty to strengthen its own position. His hostility to Austria was thus based on power politics and not on nationalist ideas, and he did not yet have a plan for unification. In 1859, after the Regent's new government took a different line, he was moved from Frankfurt to St Petersburg as ambassador, and in 1862 he was briefly in Paris.

Question: why did Prussia become stronger than Austria between 1850 and 1862? A strong paragraph chooses a main cause and justifies it: "Economic growth was the most important reason, because it allowed Prussia to build railways, produce iron and steel and raise the revenue to modernise its army, while Austria's weak finances limited what it could spend. Diplomacy mattered, since Austria's isolation after the Crimean War left it without allies, but this isolation could be overcome, whereas an economic gap cannot be closed quickly."

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Bismarck planned unification from 1851"}
Bismarck was in the 1850s a diplomat who wished to improve Prussia's position against Austria. Historians disagree about how far he had a long-term plan; the evidence suggests that he adapted to events.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing Olmütz settled the question"}
It only postponed the contest. Prussia gave way because it was weak in 1850, but the humiliation was remembered.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining only Prussian strength"}
The change was also due to Austria's weaknesses: its finances, its isolation after the Crimean War and its defeat in 1859.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Austria forced Prussia to abandon the Erfurt Union at Olmütz (29 November 1850), and the Confederation was restored.
- Austria's attempt to join or take over the Zollverein failed; the treaties were renewed in 1853 without Austria.
- Prussia's coal, iron, railways and revenue grew, while Austria's finances were weak and its mobilisations in 1853-56 and 1859 were costly.
- Austria was isolated after the Crimean War and lost Lombardy in 1859; Prussian liberals and the Nationalverein looked to Prussia.
- Bismarck began as a defender of Olmütz but, after his years at Frankfurt, concluded that Austria and Prussia could not share leadership.
:::
