## Introduction

On 13 March 1848 Prince Metternich, who had directed Austrian and German politics for nearly forty years, resigned and fled Vienna. Ten weeks later hundreds of deputies were meeting at Frankfurt to write a constitution for the whole of Germany. Yet by the summer of 1849 the princes were back in control, the parliament had been scattered by soldiers, and the revolutionaries were in prison or in exile. How could a movement that seemed to have swept everything away end in failure so quickly?

This lesson follows the spread of revolution, the work and collapse of the Frankfurt Parliament, the reasons why the old order recovered, and the results. It builds on the previous lesson, which explained why the revolutions broke out, and leads into the rivalry of Austria and Prussia after 1849. You should know the aims of liberals and nationalists and the strength of the Confederation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Frankfurt Parliament"}
The National Assembly that met in St Paul's Church, Frankfurt, from 18 May 1848, elected to draw up a constitution for a united Germany. Most of its members were professionals: lawyers, professors, civil servants and landowners.
:::

:::definition{term="Grossdeutsch and Kleindeutsch"}
"Greater German" and "Smaller German" solutions. The first would include the German lands of Austria in the new state; the second would exclude Austria, so that Prussia would lead.
:::

:::definition{term="Reassertion of control"}
The recovery of power by rulers and armies after the first weeks of revolution, when concessions were withdrawn or overridden.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 24 February 1848: Louis Philippe falls in Paris. 13 March: Metternich resigns in Vienna. 18-19 March: street fighting in Berlin.
- 18 May 1848: the Frankfurt Parliament opens. Heinrich von Gagern is its first president; Archduke John of Austria is chosen as regent.
- 28 March 1849: constitution passed and Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia elected emperor. 3 April 1849: he refuses.
- June-July 1849: the remnant of the Parliament is dispersed; the last revolutionary risings (Baden) are crushed.
:::

## The spread of revolution

News of the revolution in Paris reached the German states in the last days of February 1848. In the south-west, Baden and other states faced mass meetings that demanded a free press, juries, a citizen militia and a German parliament. Rulers quickly appointed liberal ministers, the so-called "March ministries", because their armies were small or unreliable and they feared the crowds. The Confederation's Diet itself gave way and in March accepted the black-red-gold flag.

**Vienna.** On 13 March students and workers marched on the Diet of Lower Austria and fighting began. The imperial court was frightened, and Metternich, who had become the symbol of repression, resigned and escaped to England. The government promised a constitution and abolished censorship; in the following months the Habsburgs also faced revolts in Hungary, Bohemia and northern Italy.

**Prussia.** In Berlin, crowds gathered at the palace on 18 March. Two shots were fired as troops cleared the square, barricades went up, and fighting went on through the night. On 19 March Friedrich Wilhelm IV ordered the army to leave the city, and on 21 March he rode through Berlin wearing the black-red-gold and said that Prussia would merge into Germany. He appointed liberal ministers and called a Prussian assembly. The king was not a revolutionary: he had given way to prevent worse, and he kept the loyalty of the army, which had left Berlin without being defeated.

## The Frankfurt Parliament

A self-appointed "Pre-Parliament" met at Frankfurt at the end of March and arranged elections to a National Assembly. It was elected by a wide male vote in most states and opened in St Paul's Church on 18 May 1848. Its members were overwhelmingly educated men, with very few artisans, workers or peasants, which is why it was sometimes called a "Professors' Parliament". It had no army, no civil service, no revenue and no power to compel the princes to obey. In June it created a provisional central power with Archduke John of Austria as imperial regent, and in December it published a list of basic rights, including equality before the law and freedom of speech.

It made slow progress for several reasons. The first was disagreement over frontiers. The Austrian empire could not join a united Germany without breaking itself up, and in 1849 Schwarzenberg's government in effect refused to divide the empire, so the *Grossdeutsch* plan failed. The deputies then turned to a *Kleindeutsch* solution led by Prussia, a plan that Austria opposed. The second problem was Schleswig-Holstein. When Denmark claimed the duchies, Prussian troops fought for German interests; in the Malmö armistice of August 1848 Prussia, under pressure from Britain and Russia, accepted terms the Parliament had not been consulted about. After angry debates the deputies accepted the armistice in September, which led to riots in Frankfurt in which two deputies were murdered, and revealed that the Parliament depended on the armies of the very princes it hoped to supplant.

On 28 March 1849 it adopted a constitution for a *Kleindeutsch* empire with a hereditary emperor, a two-chamber parliament and a lower house elected by the men of the country. It elected Friedrich Wilhelm IV emperor. On 3 April 1849 he refused. He would not accept a crown offered by an elected assembly rather than by the other princes; he may also have feared war with Austria and Russia. The Parliament then lost its purpose. Most moderates left, and a rump moved to Stuttgart, where soldiers of Württemberg dispersed it on 18 June 1849.

## Why the conservatives won

**The strength of the conservatives.** The armies stayed loyal to their rulers, and the civil service and the landed nobility did too. In Austria, General Windischgrätz crushed the Prague rising in June 1848 and retook Vienna in October, when the radical deputy Robert Blum, who had come from Frankfurt, was shot. A new emperor, Franz Joseph, took the throne in December 1848. Radetzky defeated Piedmont in Italy in 1848-49, and in 1849 Russian troops helped to crush the Hungarian revolution. In Prussia, the king dismissed the liberal ministers in the autumn, brought troops back into Berlin, dissolved the Prussian assembly in December 1848 and issued his own constitution.

**Divisions among the revolutionaries.** The liberals, who wanted a constitutional monarchy with a limited vote, were afraid of the democrats and workers, who wanted a republic and social reform. Nationalists quarrelled about frontiers. Artisans demanded guilds and protection, which conflicted with the free trade the liberal middle class wanted. Peasants were largely satisfied when feudal dues were abolished in the spring of 1848, and then took little interest in politics. The last risings in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden in May-July 1849 were crushed by Prussian troops.

:::example{title="Explaining a cause step by step"}
Question: why did the Frankfurt Parliament fail? **Step 1**, it had no army or tax revenue, so it relied on the princes' forces. **Step 2**, it lost time debating rights and frontiers while the Austrian and Prussian armies regained confidence. **Step 3**, it split over *Grossdeutsch* and *Kleindeutsch* plans. **Step 4**, when it offered the crown to Friedrich Wilhelm IV he refused, so no ruler would enforce the constitution. Conclusion: its lack of power was the underlying reason; the king's refusal was the immediate cause of its end.
:::

## The impact of the revolutions

In the short term the revolutions failed to unify Germany or to establish liberal governments, and many "Forty-Eighters" were imprisoned or emigrated, some to the United States. Press censorship and political controls returned. In the longer term, however, the revolutions left marks. Prussia kept a written constitution (revised in 1850), with a parliament elected on an unequal three-class franchise, and liberals learnt that unity needed power as well as principles, which prepared many for the *Realpolitik* of the 1860s. The Frankfurt constitution became a model for later ones, and peasants kept their freedom from feudal dues. Historians debate the meaning. Some have called 1848 a turning point at which German history failed to turn, because liberalism lost its chance; many scholars today stress instead the lasting political education the revolutions provided.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the revolutions failed because of Russia"}
Russia helped to crush Hungary, not the German revolutions. The main reasons were the lack of power of the Parliament, the loyalty of the armies and the divisions among the revolutionaries.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Friedrich Wilhelm IV as a liberal"}
He gave way to the Berlin crowds to protect his throne and refused the imperial crown when it was offered. Say why: it came from an assembly, not from the princes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Grossdeutsch with Kleindeutsch"}
Grossdeutsch included Austria; Kleindeutsch excluded it. Give the Parliament's final choice (Kleindeutsch under the Prussian king) and Austria's reaction.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Revolution spread from Paris in February 1848 and forced rulers in Vienna, Berlin and the smaller states to concede; Metternich resigned on 13 March.
- Friedrich Wilhelm IV gave way in Berlin on 18-21 March but kept the loyalty of his army.
- The Frankfurt Parliament, elected in spring 1848 and made up mainly of educated men, wrote a constitution but had no army or revenue.
- It failed because of its lack of power, divisions over Grossdeutsch or Kleindeutsch and Schleswig-Holstein, and the king's refusal of the crown on 3 April 1849.
- Conservatives won because the armies stayed loyal, Austria recovered, the revolutionaries were divided, and the peasants were satisfied; the revolutions nevertheless left Prussia with a constitution and a lesson about power.
:::
