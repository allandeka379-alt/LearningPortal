## Introduction

In 1815 there was no country called Germany. There was a patchwork of kingdoms, duchies, free cities and one great empire, Austria, whose rulers governed many peoples besides Germans. Thirty-three years later, in March 1848, crowds in Vienna, Berlin and dozens of smaller capitals demanded constitutions, a free press and a united nation. Why did a settlement designed to keep Europe quiet produce such pressure within a generation?

This lesson explains the structure of the German Confederation, the conservative system built by Prince Metternich, the rise of liberal and nationalist ideas, and the economic changes of the 1830s and 1840s that made the old order harder to defend. It is the background to the 1848 revolutions (the next lesson) and, in the end, to unification in 1871. Many Africans know the same pattern from their own history: borders drawn by outside powers, and a generation later a movement that asked who has the right to rule and in whose name. You should already know that Napoleon had swept away the old Holy Roman Empire and reorganised the German lands (see the lesson on the French Revolution's impact).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="German Confederation"}
The loose association of 39 German states (originally 38 in 1815, 39 from 1817) created by the Congress of Vienna in 1815, with a Federal Diet (Bundestag) at Frankfurt made up of delegates of the princes, not elected representatives.
:::

:::definition{term="Liberalism"}
A set of ideas which in Germany in this period meant a written constitution, rule of law, an elected parliament with a property-based franchise, freedom of the press and of association, and usually a constitutional monarchy, not a republic.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
The belief that people who share a language, culture and history should live in one state. German nationalists argued over what the state should include: a "Greater Germany" with Austria, or a "Smaller Germany" without it.
:::

:::definition{term="Zollverein"}
The German customs union, begun by Prussia and in force from 1 January 1834, which removed tariffs between its member states and set a common external tariff.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1815: Congress of Vienna ends (June); the German Confederation is created.
- 1817: students gather at the Wartburg. 1819: Carlsbad Decrees, after the murder of Kotzebue by a student, Karl Sand.
- 1832: Hambach Festival (May); the Six Articles (June) and Ten Articles (July).

- 1846-47: harvest failure and economic crisis. Klemens von Metternich: Austrian foreign minister from 1809 and chancellor from 1821.
:::

## The German Confederation, 1815

Napoleon had reduced about 300 German states to a few dozen. The Congress of Vienna kept this simplification but did not create a nation. Austria and Prussia were members only for their German-speaking lands, and some members were ruled by foreign kings: the King of Denmark for Holstein, the King of the Netherlands for Luxembourg and, until 1837, the British king for Hanover. Austria presided over the Diet at Frankfurt.

**Strengths.** The Confederation protected the middle-sized and small states from French attack and from absorption by their larger neighbours. It kept Austria and Prussia cooperating, avoided war between German states for half a century, and was acceptable to the other Great Powers, who wanted a stable centre of Europe.

**Weaknesses.** The Diet was a gathering of envoys who took instructions from their princes. Important decisions needed unanimity, so one state could block action. There was no common executive, no federal court of any real power, no common currency or tariff, and the federal army was a promise rather than a force. Article 13 of the Federal Act said that "estates" would be set up in each state, but did not say what that meant, so Austria and Prussia did not act on it while Bavaria, Baden, Württemberg and Hesse-Darmstadt granted constitutions in 1818-20. The Confederation therefore had no way of answering the hopes that the wars against Napoleon had raised.

## Metternich's system

Metternich feared nationalism and liberalism because either could break up the multinational Austrian Empire. His method was to unite the German princes in defence of the existing order. After a student, Karl Sand, murdered the writer August von Kotzebue in March 1819, he persuaded the Confederation to pass the **Carlsbad Decrees** (September 1819): newspapers and pamphlets were censored; the Burschenschaften (student fraternities, founded in 1815 and associated with the black-red-gold colours of national hopes) were banned; each university received a government curator to supervise teaching; and a commission at Mainz investigated "revolutionary" activity. Some professors lost their posts and some students were imprisoned.

The system worked for a time but could not remove the ideas it feared. It was strongest in Austria, Prussia and the small states, and weakest where southern rulers had granted constitutions.

## Liberal and nationalist ideas

German **liberals** came from the educated and commercial middle class: lawyers, civil servants, professors, merchants and manufacturers. They wanted constitutional government and a free press, and they usually favoured a restricted vote that protected property, so that they were suspicious of the working poor as well as of the princes. Support in Prussia was real but uneven. The Rhineland, with its Napoleonic legal codes and business class, and the eastern city of Königsberg were liberal centres, while the landed Junker nobility, the army and the civil service were conservative. In 1847 Friedrich Wilhelm IV summoned a United Diet in Berlin to approve a loan for railway building; the liberal majority refused unless he granted regular parliaments, and the king dissolved it, which showed both the strength of liberal feeling and the king's reluctance.

**Nationalism** grew through universities, songs and festivals. The 1830 revolutions in France and Belgium prompted disturbances in Brunswick, Saxony, Hesse-Kassel and Hanover, where rulers granted or revised constitutions. In May 1832 tens of thousands of people attended the Hambach Festival in the Bavarian Palatinate, singing patriotic songs and demanding freedom and unity. The Confederation responded with the **Six Articles** (June 1832), which strengthened princes against their own assemblies, and the **Ten Articles** (July 1832), which banned political associations, public meetings and national symbols. In 1837 seven professors at Göttingen, including the brothers Grimm, protested when the new King of Hanover cancelled the constitution, and were dismissed. In the 1840s a diplomatic scare with France (the Rhine crisis of 1840) and a dispute with Denmark over Schleswig-Holstein in 1846 made nationalism popular among people who had never read a book on politics.

## The economic and social background

The **Zollverein** started with Prussia's customs law of 1818, and by 1834 it joined 18 states into a single market. Most German states except Austria joined in the next decade. It encouraged trade, made Prussia the economic leader of north Germany and, by excluding Austria, hinted at a "Smaller Germany". It did not itself create political unity, and historians disagree about how far it caused unification: it prepared the ground but did not make it inevitable.

**Railways** helped even more. The first German line opened between Nuremberg and Fürth in 1835, and from a few hundred kilometres in 1840 the network grew to nearly 6,000 kilometres by 1850. Railways created demand for iron, coal and machinery, moved goods and people, and built a sense of a common space. Their cost also forced governments to ask for loans, and therefore to face demands for parliaments, as the United Diet showed.

Growth had a human cost. Population rose faster than jobs, and towns such as Berlin and Cologne filled with migrants. Factory goods undercut skilled artisans, and in June 1844 Silesian weavers, ruined by machine competition and low wages, rose in protest. In 1845-47 the potato blight and poor grain harvests raised food prices, and in 1846-47 an industrial and financial slump added unemployment. By the winter of 1847-48 workers, artisans and peasants had grievances that liberals and nationalists could channel into political demands.

:::example{title="Explaining a cause step by step"}
Question: how did railways help to cause the 1848 revolutions? **Step 1**, they made trade, travel and the exchange of newspapers faster, so people in different states shared ideas and a sense of belonging to one nation. **Step 2**, they stimulated industry, which increased the size of the middle class and the number of workers in towns. **Step 3**, they were costly, and in Prussia the king needed a loan, which gave liberals a chance to demand a parliament. **Step 4**, industrial change harmed artisans, so more people had reasons to join a protest. The paragraph ends with a judgement: railways were not a direct cause but they changed society so that old controls became harder to apply.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Confederation was a German state"}
It was an association of sovereign princes with no elected body and no real executive. Calling it "Germany" hides the very weakness that nationalists complained about.
:::

:::mistake{title="Making the Zollverein the cause of unification"}
It created economic links and made Prussia stronger, but Austria's exclusion and the political divisions remained. Say it prepared the ground, then give a reason it was not enough.
:::

:::mistake{title="Narrating events instead of explaining"}
Listing the Carlsbad Decrees, the Hambach Festival and the Six Articles is description. Explain how repression pushed liberals and nationalists together, and why it did not remove their ideas.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers and how to answer"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 2 (part (a), "Explain why…", 10 marks) test this. Cambridge Paper 1 may use it in a source set on Germany. For "explain why" questions, give three or four causes, develop each with a fact and a link ("this mattered because…"), and say which was most important. For "to what extent was Metternich's system successful" give successes (it stopped open revolt until 1830 and 1848) and failures (it could not stop ideas or economic change). Use dates, names and decrees; a vague answer about "nationalism" earns little.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The German Confederation of 1815 joined 39 states under Austrian leadership, but it had no elected body, no strong executive and required near-unanimity.
- Metternich used the Carlsbad Decrees (1819) and the Six and Ten Articles (1832) to control universities, the press and associations.
- Liberals, mostly middle-class, wanted constitutions and a free press; nationalists wanted unity, and the two movements overlapped.
- The Zollverein (1834) and railways drew the states together economically and made Prussia the leader of the north.
- Poor harvests, unemployment and the crisis of 1846-47 gave artisans, workers and peasants reasons to protest, which helped to turn liberal hopes into revolution in 1848.
:::
