## Introduction

The word *democracy* comes from the Greek for "rule by the people". In a democracy the people have the final say in how they are governed, through elections, through representatives, and through the freedom to speak, organise and criticise. Zimbabwe's history helps to explain why the idea matters so much: under colonial rule and the Rhodesian Front the majority had no vote that counted, and the liberation struggle was fought for "one person, one vote". On 18 April 1980 that principle became law, and the first general election under it was held in February 1980.

This lesson explains what democracy is, the main types, the way in which the 2013 Constitution distributes power among the executive, the legislature and the judiciary, how elections and devolution work, and the efforts made to promote democracy. You should know the earlier lessons on the Constitution and on human rights. As with all recent history, the lesson presents the framework and then different views of how it works in practice.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Democracy"}
A system of government in which power comes from the people, who choose their leaders in free and fair elections and are protected by rights and the rule of law.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct democracy"}
A system in which citizens vote on laws and decisions themselves, for example in a referendum or an assembly. It is practical only in small communities or for specific questions.
:::

:::definition{term="Representative democracy"}
A system in which citizens elect representatives to make laws and decisions on their behalf. Most modern states, including Zimbabwe, use this form.
:::

:::definition{term="Separation of powers"}
The division of government into three branches, the executive (which carries out the law), the legislature (which makes it) and the judiciary (which interprets it and settles disputes), so that no single person or body holds all the power.
:::

:::definition{term="Devolution"}
The transfer of some powers and resources from the national government to provincial and local levels of government.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- February 1980: first elections with universal adult suffrage; 18 April 1980: independence.
- 2009: the Global Political Agreement and the Government of National Unity (to 2013); constitution-making outreach by COPAC.
- 16 March 2013: referendum approves the new Constitution; harmonised elections of 2013 and 2018.
- Chapter 14 of the Constitution: devolution; the Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC) manages elections.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Democracy in Zimbabwe is ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine it.
:::

## Characteristics and types of democracy

A democratic system usually has: regular, free and fair elections; universal adult suffrage; competing political parties; the rule of law and an independent judiciary; protection of rights; freedom of the media and assembly; accountability of leaders; and majority rule with protection of minorities. Types of democracy include:

| Type | Features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Citizens vote on decisions themselves | A referendum on a constitution |
| Representative | Citizens elect representatives | Parliament and local councils |
| Liberal (constitutional) | Representative government limited by a constitution and rights | Zimbabwe's 2013 Constitution |
| Participatory | Citizens take part between elections, in hearings, committees and petitions | Public consultation on bills |

A one-party state is different: the people may vote, but there is no choice between parties. Zimbabwe has had a multiparty system since independence; the idea of a one-party state was discussed in the 1980s, and the ruling party decided against it in 1990.

## The organs of government under the 2013 Constitution

**The executive.** The President is Head of State and Government, and Commander-in-Chief of the Defence Forces. The President is elected directly by voters for a five-year term and may serve two terms. A candidate needs more than half of the valid votes; if no one wins, a run-off is held. The President appoints Vice-Presidents and ministers, who together form the Cabinet and are accountable to Parliament.

**The legislature.** Parliament has two houses. The **National Assembly** has 210 members elected from constituencies and, under the original text, 60 additional seats reserved for women elected by proportional representation. The **Senate** has 80 members: six from each of the ten provinces, 16 chiefs, and two members for persons with disabilities, plus the President and Deputy President of the National Council of Chiefs. Parliament makes laws, approves the budget, scrutinises the executive through committees and questions, and can amend the Constitution by the procedure it sets out.

**The judiciary.** Judicial authority belongs to the courts: the Constitutional Court (the highest court on constitutional matters), the Supreme Court, the High Court, Labour Court, Administrative Court, magistrates' courts and others. The Judicial Service Commission recommends judges for appointment. The Constitution requires judges to be independent and impartial and subject only to the Constitution and the law.

**Separation of powers.** Section 3 of the Constitution lists the separation of powers among the founding values, and the three branches have distinct functions, with checks such as the power of courts to rule laws and actions unconstitutional, and the power of Parliament to question ministers. In practice, critics argue that the executive remains dominant and that the independence of the judiciary and the legislature has been questioned, while the government points to the Constitution and to cases in which the courts ruled against it.

:::example{title="Explaining how the three organs check one another"}
1. Parliament passes a law; the President can sign it or refer it back, so the executive checks the legislature.
2. A citizen challenges the law as unconstitutional; the Constitutional Court can declare it invalid, so the judiciary checks both.
3. Ministers answer questions in Parliament and appear before committees, so the legislature checks the executive.
4. Judges are appointed on the recommendation of the Judicial Service Commission, which is meant to protect their independence.
5. Judgement: the design spreads power, but how well it works depends on the independence of institutions and on political practice.
:::

## Elections and participation

The Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC), an independent commission, registers voters, conducts elections every five years, and announces results. The Constitution sets the voting age at 18 and provides for a voters' roll, observers and the right of every citizen to vote in secret. Elections held since 1980 have included presidential, parliamentary and local elections, now held together as **harmonised elections**. Observers and parties have judged them differently: international and regional observer missions have reported different levels of satisfaction, and parties have raised concerns about the voters' roll, access to the media and violence, while the government has said that elections have been run in line with the law. Citizens also take part through public hearings on bills, petitions, political parties, trade unions, churches and civil society groups, and through traditional leaders in local matters.

## Devolution

Chapter 14 provides for devolution of power and responsibilities to the ten provincial and metropolitan councils (eight provinces and the metropolitan provinces of Harare and Bulawayo) and to local authorities. The Constitution says that at least 5 per cent of national revenue should go to provinces and local authorities. Devolution is meant to bring decisions closer to people, to share resources more fairly, and to recognise the regions' diversity. Implementation has been gradual, with a devolution policy approved in 2020, and observers disagree about how fast it has progressed.

## Efforts to promote democracy

These include the 2013 Constitution, produced after outreach by the parliamentary committee COPAC and approved by referendum; the creation of independent commissions (ZEC, ZHRC, Gender, Media, Peace and Reconciliation); the quota of seats for women; public participation in lawmaking; devolution; and voter education. Weaknesses cited by critics include the dominance of the executive, delays in aligning laws, limits on the media, and unequal access to resources between parties.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the organs"}
The legislature makes laws, the executive carries them out and the judiciary interprets them. Do not describe the President as the maker of laws.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the written system"}
Examiners reward answers that explain how the system works in practice and give strengths, weaknesses and different views.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using party language"}
Write about institutions and arguments, not about parties or leaders as good or bad.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Define democracy and outline its characteristics", "Describe the roles of the three organs of government", "Explain the importance of the separation of powers", "To what extent has Zimbabwe promoted democracy since 1980?" Define terms, name each organ with its functions, give examples of checks and balances, explain devolution, and finish with a judgement that weighs efforts against criticisms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Democracy means rule by the people through free and fair elections, rights and the rule of law; types include direct, representative, liberal and participatory.
- The 2013 Constitution divides power between the executive (President and Cabinet), Parliament (National Assembly and Senate) and the courts, with checks on each.
- ZEC runs elections every five years; citizens also take part through hearings, petitions, parties and civil society.
- Devolution under Chapter 14 shares power with provinces and local authorities and sets aside at least 5 per cent of national revenue.
- Views differ on how well the system works in practice; a good answer weighs efforts against criticisms.
:::
