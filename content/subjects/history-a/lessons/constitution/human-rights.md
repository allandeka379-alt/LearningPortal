## Introduction

Human rights are the basic freedoms and protections to which every person is entitled simply because they are human. In Zimbabwe they matter in everyday life: whether a child can go to school, whether a citizen can speak, assemble and vote freely, whether a person who is arrested is treated fairly, and whether a woman or a person with a disability is protected from discrimination. For most of the colonial period the law treated Africans differently from whites, so the idea of equal rights was central to the liberation struggle.

This lesson explains what rights and responsibilities are, the Declaration of Rights in the Constitution adopted in 2013, the international instruments Zimbabwe has accepted, the rights of children, the bodies that promote rights and the criticisms that have been made. You should know about the colonial laws that discriminated by race and how the constitution of 1980 and its amendments developed. Rights are a topic on which different groups have strong views, so this lesson presents the legal framework, then the different judgements.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Human rights"}
Rights that belong to every person by virtue of being human, such as life, liberty, equality before the law and freedom of expression. They are universal, meaning that they apply to everyone.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsibility (duty)"}
An obligation that goes with a right, such as respecting the rights of other people, obeying the law, paying taxes and taking part in the community. Rights and duties are linked: my right to speak has to be balanced with your right to dignity.
:::

:::definition{term="Declaration of Rights"}
The chapter of a constitution that lists the rights and freedoms of individuals and groups. In the Constitution of Zimbabwe of 2013 it is Chapter 4.
:::

:::definition{term="Limitation of rights"}
A restriction on a right that is allowed by law only where it is fair, reasonable, necessary and justifiable in a democratic society, for example to protect public safety or the rights of others.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 10 December 1948: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) is adopted by the UN General Assembly.
- 1981: the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights is adopted by the OAU; it enters into force in 1986.
- 1989-90: the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; Zimbabwe ratifies it in 1990.
- 16 March 2013: the referendum approves the new Constitution; it is signed into law in May 2013.
- The Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission (ZHRC) is one of the independent commissions in Chapter 12 of the Constitution.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Human rights and responsibilities are ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine this topic.
:::

## The Declaration of Rights

The 2013 Constitution places a long Declaration of Rights in Chapter 4. It says that the state and all its institutions must respect, protect, promote and fulfil these rights, and that they apply to all people and to the courts. Among the rights listed are:

- **life** (the death penalty is retained only for murder in aggravating circumstances and may not be imposed on women, people under 21 or over 70);
- **personal liberty and dignity**, and freedom from torture and cruel or degrading treatment;
- **equality and non-discrimination**, including between women and men;
- **freedom of conscience, expression, the media, assembly, association and movement**, and **access to information**;
- **political rights**, such as the right to vote and to form or join a political party;
- **fair trial and rights of arrested persons**;
- **property**, including a right not to be arbitrarily evicted from one's home;
- **labour rights** and fair labour practices;
- **economic, social and cultural rights**, including to education, health care, food and water, and a clean environment;
- **rights of particular groups**: women, children, older people, persons with disabilities, and war veterans.

Rights may be limited by a law that is fair, reasonable, necessary and justifiable in a democratic society, and the Constitution says that some rights, such as the right to life and the freedom from torture, may not be limited. Anyone whose rights are infringed can approach a court, and the Constitutional Court is the final guardian of the constitution. This is a major development from the Lancaster House constitution, which had a shorter Declaration that was focused on civil and political rights and did not guarantee social rights such as education and health in the same way.

:::example{title="Applying a right to a case"}
**Case:** a girl, Chipo, aged 15, is told to leave school because she is pregnant.

1. The Constitution says that every child has the right to education and that the best interests of the child are paramount.
2. Equality and non-discrimination protect her from unfair treatment.
3. A Constitutional Court judgment (Mudzuru v Minister of Justice, 2016) also fixed 18 as the minimum age of marriage, showing the courts' role in protecting children.
4. In 2020 the Education Act was amended to prohibit the exclusion of pupils because of pregnancy.
5. Judgement: the rights are clearly stated, though schools and communities need to apply them in practice.
:::

## International instruments

Zimbabwe belongs to the United Nations and the African Union, and the Constitution says that courts must take international law into account when interpreting the Declaration of Rights. Important instruments are:

| Instrument | Date | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Universal Declaration of Human Rights | 1948 | A common standard of rights; not a treaty, but very influential |
| African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights | 1981 (in force 1986) | Includes individual, collective and "peoples'" rights, and duties of individuals |
| Convention on the Rights of the Child | 1989 | Best interests of the child, education, protection from abuse and exploitation |
| African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child | 1990 | Adds African contexts such as harmful traditional practices |
| Convention on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW) | 1979 | Equality for women |

## Promotion and protection

The **Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission** (ZHRC) is an independent body set up under Chapter 12 of the Constitution to promote awareness of rights, to monitor and investigate violations, and to recommend improvements. Other bodies include the Zimbabwe Gender Commission, the Zimbabwe Media Commission, the National Peace and Reconciliation Commission, the courts, the police, Parliament and civil society groups. The government has also aligned some laws with the 2013 Constitution, for instance on the minimum age of marriage, and has run public education.

## Responsibilities

Rights come with duties. These include respecting the rights and dignity of others, obeying the law, paying taxes, taking part in elections, protecting the environment and public property, and defending the country. The African Charter, unlike many other human rights documents, lists duties of the individual towards the family, society and the state. Teachers often say that rights without responsibility lead to conflict, and rights activists add that duties cannot be used as an excuse to deny rights.

## Criticisms and different views

Views of how well rights are protected in Zimbabwe differ. Human rights organisations, opposition parties and some foreign governments have criticised the application of laws such as the Public Order and Security Act (replaced in 2019 by the Maintenance of Peace and Order Act) and restrictions on assembly, expression and the media, and the delay in aligning laws with the 2013 Constitution. They also point to cases of violence and arrests around elections. The government has responded that it protects rights through the Constitution and the courts, that restrictions are lawful limits necessary for peace and order, that the Constitution is among the most progressive in Africa, and that criticism from some states ignores their own record and the effect of sanctions on social rights. Others add that implementing economic and social rights depends on resources, and that awareness of rights is still uneven, especially in rural areas.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying rights are absolute"}
Most rights may be limited by law under the Constitution if the limit is fair and necessary; only a few, such as freedom from torture, cannot be limited.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting responsibilities"}
Questions often ask about rights and responsibilities together. Give at least two duties and link them to rights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting one view"}
Give the legal framework, the efforts of the government and institutions, and the criticisms. Avoid taking sides on individuals or parties.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Define human rights and responsibilities", "Describe the rights in the Declaration of Rights", "Describe the work of the Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission", "To what extent are human rights protected in Zimbabwe?" Give definitions, name Chapter 4 and some rights, use examples, include international instruments, and balance efforts against criticisms before concluding.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Human rights belong to every person; responsibilities such as respecting others and obeying the law go with them.
- Chapter 4 of the 2013 Constitution lists civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights and the rights of particular groups, and limits rights only where this is fair, reasonable and necessary.
- Zimbabwe is influenced by the UDHR (1948), the African Charter (1981) and the Convention on the Rights of the Child (ratified 1990).
- The ZHRC, other commissions, the courts and civil society promote and protect rights.
- Views differ on how well rights are protected in practice; a good answer presents efforts, limits and criticisms fairly.
:::
