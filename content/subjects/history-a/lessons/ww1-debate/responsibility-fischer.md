## Introduction

Few questions in modern history have been argued over as long as "Who or what was to blame for the First World War?". The question was not only academic. In 1919 it was written into a peace treaty, it shaped German politics for a generation, and in the 1960s it divided German historians so bitterly that the dispute became a national argument about the country's past. Different answers have been given in different decades, and the answers often tell us as much about the historian's time as about 1914.

This lesson follows the debate through four stages: Article 231 and the argument about "war guilt"; the revisionist view of shared responsibility in the 1920s and 1930s; the effect of the Second World War; and the thesis of Fritz Fischer with the reactions to it. It is part of Paper 3 Topic 1. The events of 1914 are in the lessons on the long-term causes and the July Crisis, and the modern interpretations are in the next lesson. You should know the sequence of events in July 1914.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Article 231"}
The article of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) in which the Allies stated that Germany and its allies were responsible for causing all the loss and damage suffered by the Allies as a result of a war imposed on them by their aggression. It formed the legal basis for reparations.
:::

:::definition{term="Historiography"}
The study of how historians have written about a subject, and how and why their interpretations have changed.
:::

:::definition{term="Revisionism"}
In this debate, the view of the 1920s that the verdict of Versailles that Germany alone was responsible was unfair, and that responsibility should be shared.
:::

:::definition{term="Fischer thesis"}
Fritz Fischer's argument, in *Griff nach der Weltmacht* (1961; in English, *Germany's Aims in the First World War*, 1967), that Germany's leaders sought European dominance and world-power status, and deliberately risked war in 1914.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1919: Treaty of Versailles, Article 231. 1920s: German government publications and campaign against the "war guilt lie".
- 1928: Sidney Fay, *The Origins of the World War* (shared responsibility). 1950s: Gerhard Ritter, defender of German policy.
- 1961: Fischer, *Griff nach der Weltmacht*. 1969: *Krieg der Illusionen* ("War of Illusions"). 9 September 1914: the "September Programme" of Bethmann Hollweg.
:::

## Article 231 and the war guilt debate

The victorious powers insisted in 1919 that Germany must pay for the damage the war had caused. Article 231 provided the basis. Its drafters, notably American lawyers, saw it as a legal statement of liability that would justify reparations, since Germany could not pay the entire cost. It did not use the word "guilt". But most Germans read it as a charge of guilt, a moral judgement they bitterly rejected. The major German political parties, from the right to the left, condemned the article, and the 1920s saw a campaign to disprove what was called the "war guilt lie".

The German Foreign Ministry set up an office to collect evidence and published a large collection of documents (*Die Grosse Politik*, 1922-27). Its editors were not neutral: they selected papers to show German policy in a favourable light. Other governments also published documents, and the Bolsheviks published tsarist papers that showed Russia's role in the Balkans. The debate thus began as a political quarrel over responsibility for reparations, and its early history shows how interpretation is shaped by a country's interests.

## Shared responsibility in the 1920s and 1930s

As scholars studied the documents, many rejected the idea that Germany alone was to blame. The American historian Sidney Fay, in *The Origins of the World War* (1928), argued that no single power wanted a European war and that all the Great Powers shared some responsibility through the alliance system, militarism, secret diplomacy and nationalism. Serbia's encouragement of nationalists, Austria's harsh ultimatum, Russia's mobilisation, French support for Russia and German recklessness each played a part. This view found wide support in Britain and the United States, where there was disillusionment with the war and a feeling that the treaty had been too harsh. David Lloyd George, who had been prime minister, later wrote that the nations had drifted into the war and had not wanted it. Other historians, such as Bernadotte Schmitt, still placed the main responsibility on Germany and Austria-Hungary.

Why did the idea of shared responsibility arise? There were several reasons: the documents revealed a more complex picture than wartime propaganda; the harshness of Versailles encouraged sympathy for Germany; the experience of war led many to believe that mistakes were common to all; and in Britain and France some wanted to justify a policy of conciliating Germany. Historians today note that the argument was influenced by the politics of the time as well as by the evidence.

## The effect of the Second World War

The coming of the Second World War and the crimes of the Nazi regime changed the debate. For many historians, Hitler's aggression and the Holocaust made it harder to treat Germany as an ordinary participant in 1914. Historians began to ask whether there was a continuity in German aims from Wilhelm II to Hitler. Others, mostly German conservatives, responded that Hitler was an aberration and that Imperial Germany's leaders had not been criminals. Luigi Albertini's work on the origins of the war, written in Italy before and during the early 1940s and published in English in the 1950s, laid heavy responsibility on Germany and Austria. In West Germany after 1945, historians such as Gerhard Ritter defended the view that Germany's leaders had acted defensively, that Bethmann Hollweg had been a moderate man in difficult circumstances, and that Germany had been caught in a European system of rivalry. They wished to avoid a verdict that the whole of German history led to Hitler.

## The Fischer thesis

In 1961 Fritz Fischer, a Hamburg historian, published *Griff nach der Weltmacht*, based on a large body of German government papers. His central arguments were:

- German leaders pursued a "bid for world power" and aimed at hegemony in Europe, as shown by Bethmann Hollweg's September Programme of 1914, which proposed German control of Belgium, parts of France, a Central European economic union and a colonial empire in Africa.
- The war aims did not arise merely from the circumstances of the war; they continued the aims of the pre-war years, so Germany bore the main responsibility for the war.
- Germany's leaders took a deliberate risk of a European war in July 1914, encouraging Austria with the blank cheque, and some wanted the war. Fischer pointed to the meeting known as the "war council" of December 1912 as evidence of preparation.
- In his second major book (1969) he argued that domestic pressures, including fear of the Social Democrats and a wish to preserve the old order, drove German foreign policy, and that there was a continuity of aims from Wilhelm II to later German regimes.

## The German reaction and the challenges to Fischer

The reaction in Germany was strongly hostile. Ritter and others accused Fischer of damaging the national reputation, and of confusing war aims of 1914-18 with pre-war intentions. Fischer was attacked in the press, and there were attempts to cut official support for his lecture tour of the United States before it was restored. Yet his critics were not all defending German policy. Other scholars made specific objections:

- **Evidence.** Critics said that war aims written after the war began did not prove what leaders intended before it, and that Fischer relied on a few documents, such as the September Programme, which he treated as plans, when they could also be seen as responses to military success.
- **Risk.** Some historians, such as Egmont Zechlin and, later, Konrad Jarausch, argued that Bethmann Hollweg took a calculated risk, hoping to keep the war localised and to split the Entente, and was defensive, afraid of Russia's growing strength, not seeking war.
- **Other powers.** Others stressed the responsibilities of Austria-Hungary, Russia and France, and structures such as the alliances, so that Germany was not unique.

Over time, much of Fischer's central claim, that Germany bore a heavy share of responsibility, found wide acceptance, especially among younger German historians, while his claims about continuity and planned war have been disputed. Some later historians, including John Röhl, supported his emphasis on German policy and the role of the Kaiser and military leaders.

:::example{title="Reading an interpretation in its context"}
Question: why did Sidney Fay's view of shared responsibility become popular after 1928? **Step 1**, new documents had been published and showed a complex pattern of decisions, not a single plot. **Step 2**, many in Britain and the United States felt that Versailles had been too harsh and that the war had been a mistake. **Step 3**, the view suited German politicians who wished to revise the treaty. A strong answer then adds the other side: Fay's view depended on evidence which had been selected by the governments that published it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing as if Fischer said Germany alone caused the war"}
Fischer stressed German responsibility but did not deny that others contributed. State his claim accurately: that Germany's leaders bore the main responsibility and pursued expansion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting or inventing historians' words"}
Paraphrase the interpretations. Do not attribute exact sentences to a historian unless you are certain of them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring context"}
An interpretation reflects its time. Link Article 231, the 1920s, the Second World War and the 1960s to the interpretations they produced.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) became the basis for reparations and was read by Germans as a charge of guilt, which they rejected in the 1920s.
- Revisionists such as Sidney Fay argued that responsibility was shared among the Great Powers; their view was shaped by new documents and by the politics of the time.
- The Second World War and the Nazi regime led historians to ask about continuity in German aims and to view Germany's responsibility more seriously.
- Fischer (1961) argued that Germany pursued world power and deliberately risked war in 1914, supported by the September Programme and the war council of 1912.
- German critics such as Ritter attacked him; others questioned his evidence and argued for calculated risk and the responsibility of other powers; his claim of heavy German responsibility has been widely accepted.
:::
