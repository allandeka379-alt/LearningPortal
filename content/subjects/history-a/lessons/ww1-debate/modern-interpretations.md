## Introduction

By the 1980s many historians believed that the main question about 1914 had been answered: Germany bore the heaviest responsibility, as Fritz Fischer had argued. Yet in the decades that followed, new archives were opened, new questions were asked, and some of the most successful books of the twenty-first century reopened the issue. The centenary of 1914 brought the subject back into public life in Britain, Germany, France, Serbia and elsewhere. This lesson explains how modern historians have approached the origins of the war, and why they disagree.

It covers four themes from the Cambridge syllabus: interpretations exploring the responsibility of nations other than Germany; the relative importance of long-term and short-term factors; the importance of contingency over motive; and how the centenary affected the debate. It builds on the debate over Article 231 and Fischer (previous lesson) and uses the events described in the lessons on the long-term causes and the July Crisis. Historians' positions are paraphrased, and works are named only where the details are well established.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Multipolar system"}
An international system with several powers of comparable importance, none able to dominate, which makes alliances and calculations more complex than in a system of two blocs.
:::

:::definition{term="Contingency"}
The role of chance and unforeseen events in history; to stress contingency is to argue that outcomes could have been different if small events had gone differently.
:::

:::definition{term="Structure and agency"}
Structures are lasting conditions, such as alliances, economies and ideas, that shape what people can do; agency is the power of individuals to make choices. Historians differ over which explains 1914 better.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and works to learn"}
- Christopher Clark, *The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914* (2012). Margaret MacMillan, *The War That Ended Peace* (2013).
- Sean McMeekin, *The Russian Origins of the First World War* (2011). Niall Ferguson, *The Pity of War* (1998).
- John Röhl and Annika Mombauer, who stress German responsibility. 2014: the centenary of the outbreak of war.
:::

## Responsibility beyond Germany

From the 1980s historians used archives in Vienna, Belgrade, Moscow and Paris, many of them opened or more widely used after the end of the Cold War, to study the decisions of the other powers.

**Austria-Hungary.** Studies of the Habsburg government show a leadership that felt itself to be under threat from Slav nationalism and convinced that Serbia must be punished. Berchtold, Conrad and others wished to settle accounts, and the ultimatum was drafted to be rejected. Many historians conclude that Vienna was more than a passive partner in the blank cheque: it had its own reasons for war, and the initiative was Austria's.

**Serbia.** Clark's book pays more attention to Serbia than earlier accounts, describing a government whose policy was shaped by nationalism, by officers connected with the Black Hand and by an expansionist programme for a South Slav state. The Serbian government knew of some of the dangers and did not do enough to stop the plot. Critics reply that Serbia was a small state surrounded by larger ones and that Austria's response was out of proportion.

**Russia.** McMeekin argues that Russian leaders had ambitions of their own, especially concerning the Straits and Constantinople, and that the Russian mobilisation was a decisive step towards a general war. Others reply that Russia was responding to Austrian aggression and to the German blank cheque, and that its aims were more cautious.

**France and Britain.** Historians have asked whether French leaders, particularly President Poincaré, encouraged Russia too far, and whether Britain, by not making clear its position before 4 August, weakened deterrence. Ferguson argued that Britain made a mistake in joining the war, since a German victory on the continent would not have been a danger to Britain in the way its leaders believed, and that British intervention turned a European war into a world war. This is a minority view, and opponents argue that a German victory would have given Germany dominance in Europe and threatened British security and trade.

## Long-term and short-term factors

Fischer and many structural historians stressed long-term factors: German ambitions, the alliance blocs, the arms race and nationalism. More recent writers have asked how far these made war likely. Clark describes a multipolar Europe in which the powers were flexible and competing, not locked into two fixed blocs, and the Balkan crises changed the balance. Others argue that the pre-war crises (Morocco in 1905 and 1911, Bosnia in 1908, the Balkan Wars in 1912-13) had all been settled without a general war, so the system could handle tension. That makes the decisions of July 1914 especially important. Yet critics respond that the earlier crises had also left lasting mistrust and increased the stakes, and that the war plans and arms race made the July decisions more dangerous.

## Key individuals and contingency

Clark's argument is, in his own account, a shift from asking "why" to asking "how". He describes leaders who were uncertain and who took decisions on partial information, in a system of mistrust. Hence the word "sleepwalkers": he suggests that they were not villains plotting a war but men who failed to see the consequences of their actions. MacMillan also stresses the part of individuals, such as the Kaiser, Tsar Nicholas II, Poincaré and Grey, and the sense that the war could have been avoided if different choices had been made. These interpretations place more weight on contingency (chance and misjudgment) than on motive (aims).

Critics, among them historians who stress German responsibility, such as Mombauer and Röhl, argue that this approach downplays the German government's decisions: the blank cheque, the refusal of mediation and the readiness to risk a European war. They say that in July 1914 the initiative lay in Berlin and Vienna, and that Clark's emphasis on shared confusion makes it harder to see this. Defenders reply that the argument does not deny German and Austrian responsibility for the escalation, but sets it among the decisions of other powers.

## The centenary of 1914

In 2014 governments, museums, schools and broadcasters commemorated the outbreak of war, and many new books appeared. Public discussion showed how the interpretation of 1914 is connected with national memory. In Britain, politicians and historians argued about whether the war was a necessary defence against German aggression or a futile slaughter. In Germany, Clark's book was widely read, and some commentators welcomed its stress on shared responsibility while others feared that it would excuse German leaders. In Serbia and Bosnia, debates about Gavrilo Princip, whom some saw as a hero and others as a terrorist, showed the strength of national feeling. Historians noted that the centenary also encouraged attention to the global effects of the war, including Africa and Asia. Many scholars see the centenary as having moved the debate away from the blame question towards the study of how decisions were made, but the question of responsibility has not disappeared.

| Theme | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Beyond Germany | Austria-Hungary, Serbia, Russia, France and Britain also made choices |
| Long-term or short-term | Structures versus the decisions of July 1914 |
| Contingency and individuals | Chance, misjudgment and key leaders |
| Centenary (2014) | Public debate; national memories; focus on "how" as well as "who" |

:::example{title="Comparing two interpretations"}
Question: how does a contingency-based interpretation differ from Fischer's? Fischer argued that the war grew from Germany's aims, so that German leaders deliberately risked it. A contingency-based historian argues that no power planned the war, that leaders acted on partial information and that chance events and misjudgment led to escalation. The two differ in their view of motive (intention against misjudgment), responsibility (German against shared) and method (aims and documents of one government against decisions of several in a multipolar system). A good answer then tests both against the July Crisis: the blank cheque supports Fischer, and the confusion of the crisis supports the other view.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the Sleepwalkers thesis as 'nobody was to blame'"}
Clark does not say that no one is responsible; he argues that responsibility was shared and that the question "how" matters as well as "who". Critics dispute the balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing books instead of analysing interpretations"}
Name the author only briefly. Concentrate on the argument (who or what is blamed), the approach (evidence and emphasis) and how convincing it is when tested against events.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting context"}
Interpretations are shaped by the time: the Cold War, German reunification, the opening of archives and the centenary all influenced what historians asked.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Modern historians have used archives in several countries to study the responsibility of Austria-Hungary, Serbia, Russia, France and Britain as well as Germany.
- Clark (2012) describes a multipolar Europe whose leaders "sleepwalked" into war; MacMillan (2013) stresses the choices of individuals; critics such as Mombauer and Röhl say these views understate German decisions.
- Historians disagree about the weight of long-term causes (alliances, arms race) against short-term decisions in July 1914.
- Contingency and misjudgment are given more weight than motive in some modern interpretations.
- The centenary of 1914 renewed public debate and moved attention towards how decisions were made, while the question of responsibility remained alive.
:::
