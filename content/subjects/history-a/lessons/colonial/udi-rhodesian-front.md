## Introduction

On 11 November 1965 the government of Southern Rhodesia, led by Ian Smith, declared that the colony was independent without the agreement of Britain. Britain called the act illegal, the United Nations called for sanctions, and for fourteen years the country, which its government called Rhodesia, lived under isolation, war and sanctions. UDI was meant to secure white rule for a long time. Instead it hardened African opposition, helped to turn nationalist politics into armed struggle, and ended in the independence of Zimbabwe in 1980.

This lesson explains why the Rhodesian Front came to power, why UDI took place, what sanctions did and how the economy coped, why talks failed and what the settlements of 1971-72 and 1978-79 tried to do. The liberation war itself is covered in other lessons. You need the earlier lesson on settler rule, since the Rhodesian Front was a reaction to the end of the Federation and to African nationalism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="UDI"}
The Unilateral Declaration of Independence of 11 November 1965, by which the Rhodesian government declared independence from Britain without British consent.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions"}
Measures taken by one or more states to punish or pressure another by cutting trade or financial links. Britain imposed sanctions after UDI and the United Nations followed.
:::

:::definition{term="NIBMAR"}
"No independence before majority rule", the British policy after 1965 that independence would be granted only on terms that led to African majority rule.
:::

## The Rhodesian Front and the road to UDI

The Rhodesian Front (RF) was formed in 1962 by white voters who thought the Southern Rhodesian government was moving too fast towards African participation. It rejected majority rule, defended the interests of white farmers and workers and demanded independence on its own terms. It won the election of December 1962, under Winston Field, and from April 1964 was led by Ian Smith.

The break-up of the Federation in 1963 and the independence of Zambia and Malawi in 1964 showed Rhodesian whites that Britain would give independence to African majorities elsewhere. Smith's government demanded that Britain give independence to Southern Rhodesia under the constitution of 1961, which limited African representation. Britain, under Harold Wilson's Labour government from October 1964, insisted on conditions: unimpeded progress to majority rule, guarantees against retrogressive amendment, immediate improvement of African political status, an end to racial discrimination and a settlement acceptable to the whole people. After talks failed, Smith declared UDI on 11 November 1965, claiming to defend the existing order and the constitution of 1961; Britain called it an act of rebellion.

**Domestic pressure.** The RF suppressed opposition. The nationalist parties ZAPU (banned 1962) and ZANU (banned 1964) were restricted; their leaders, including Joshua Nkomo, Ndabaningi Sithole and Robert Mugabe, were detained or exiled. Laws such as the Law and Order (Maintenance) Act allowed detention without trial.

## Sanctions and how Rhodesia coped

Britain imposed economic measures straight after UDI, including a ban on oil and on Rhodesian sugar and tobacco. The United Nations Security Council imposed selective mandatory sanctions in December 1966 and comprehensive sanctions in May 1968.

**How Rhodesia coped:**
- **Allies and loopholes.** South Africa and Portuguese-ruled Mozambique and Angola traded with Rhodesia and supplied oil; some companies and countries evaded the ban.
- **Import substitution.** Rhodesia built factories to make goods it had previously imported, and manufacturing grew.
- **Farming and mining.** Farmers switched from tobacco to maize, cotton and cattle, and mining continued.
- **Secrecy and rerouting.** Export figures were hidden and goods were sold through third countries.

The economy grew in the late 1960s and early 1970s, though at a cost: foreign exchange was short, investment was limited and many Africans gained little. After 1972 the cost of the war, the closing of the Mozambique border in 1976 and the drop in world demand strained the economy badly.

:::example{title="Weighing the effect of sanctions"}
**Evidence they worked partly:** isolation, loss of markets, shortage of foreign exchange and the cost of the war weakened Rhodesia. **Evidence they failed at first:** South African and Portuguese help, oil supplies and import substitution allowed growth until the early 1970s. A balanced answer says that sanctions did not bring the regime down on their own, but combined with the war and the loss of Mozambique as an ally they helped to force negotiation.
:::

## Attempts at settlement

- **HMS Tiger, December 1966.** Wilson and Smith met aboard a British warship off Gibraltar. Smith's cabinet rejected the terms.
- **HMS Fearless, October 1968.** A second meeting, with similar terms, also failed.
- **The Smith-Home proposals, November 1971.** Britain's foreign secretary Sir Alec Douglas-Home and Smith agreed terms that would have lengthened the route to majority rule. A commission under Lord Pearce tested African opinion in early 1972. Africans, organised in the African National Council under Bishop Abel Muzorewa, rejected the proposals, and the Commission reported that they were not acceptable to the majority. The result showed Africans' refusal to accept a settlement without majority rule.

## Constitution and republic

In 1969 the RF passed the **Land Tenure Act**, which replaced the Land Apportionment Act and divided the land into roughly equal parts for Europeans and Africans, although Europeans were a small minority. A new constitution in 1969 gave the whites control of parliament and set a racial franchise. In March 1970 Rhodesia was proclaimed a republic, cutting the last link with the Crown. Neither move was recognised by Britain or by almost any other state.

## The internal settlement, 1978-79

As the liberation war intensified, Smith negotiated with moderate African leaders. On 3 March 1978 he signed an agreement with Bishop Muzorewa, the Rev. Ndabaningi Sithole and Chief Jeremiah Chirau for a transitional government and a vote for all adults. Elections were held in April 1979, and Muzorewa became prime minister of "Zimbabwe-Rhodesia" in June 1979. The settlement kept white control of the army, police, civil service and judiciary, and the leaders of ZANU and ZAPU (the Patriotic Front) rejected it. Britain and the UN did not recognise it, sanctions continued and the war went on. It paved the way for the Lancaster House talks, which are covered in a later lesson.

## Impact on Africans and the economy

For Africans, UDI meant tighter control, detention of leaders, the Land Tenure Act, limited schools and jobs, and, after 1972, war in the rural areas, with curfews, "protected villages" and many deaths. For the economy, it meant isolation but also growth through import substitution in the first decade, then strain.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying UDI was recognised by other states"}
No state formally recognised Rhodesia's independence, although South Africa and Portugal traded with it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sanctions had no effect or ended UDI by themselves"}
Both are too simple. Sanctions strained the economy and isolated the regime, but they worked together with the war, the loss of Mozambique and the withdrawal of South Africa's support.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Pearce Commission with the Lancaster House talks"}
The Pearce Commission (1972) tested African opinion on the Smith-Home terms. Lancaster House (1979) was the conference that led to independence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Rhodesian Front won in 1962 and, under Smith from 1964, demanded independence without majority rule.
- UDI was declared on 11 November 1965; Britain called it illegal and the UN imposed sanctions in 1966 and 1968.
- Rhodesia coped through South African and Portuguese help and import substitution, but the war and the loss of Mozambique strained the economy.
- Settlement talks (Tiger, Fearless, the Pearce Commission) failed; the Land Tenure Act (1969) and republic (1970) entrenched white power.
- The internal settlement of 1978-79 failed to win recognition, and the war continued to Lancaster House.
:::
