## Introduction

From 1 October 1923 Southern Rhodesia governed itself. The voters were almost all white, the parliament was elected by them, and the laws they passed reflected their interests. Britain kept some reserved powers, especially over laws that affected Africans differently from others, but in practice it seldom used them. Over the next forty years the settler government passed a series of Acts that divided the land, protected white workers, controlled the movement of Africans and later tried to reorganise African farming.

This lesson explains the main Acts and their effects on land, work, education and political rights, how Africans reacted, and why the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland was formed and ended. You should know Company rule and the grievances it created. The Rhodesian Front and UDI are in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Segregation"}
The policy of keeping racial groups apart by law in land, housing, work and public life, with the best resources reserved for the dominant group.
:::

:::definition{term="Federation"}
A union of several territories with a central government for shared matters such as defence, economic policy and transport, and territorial governments for others. The Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland joined Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
The slogan of the Federation, which claimed that the races would work together, though in practice white leaders kept control.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}

## The Land Apportionment Act, 1930

The Act divided the land of Southern Rhodesia into areas where each race could own or occupy land. Roughly half of the land went to European ownership, though Europeans made up only a small percentage of the population. The Native Reserves covered a large area but were mainly in poorer, drier regions and were already crowded. A third category, the Native Purchase Areas, allowed a limited number of better-off Africans to buy farms. Africans living on land in European areas had to move out or live there only as workers. The Act also forbade Africans from buying land in the European area and so became the legal base of racial segregation in land. It created a land shortage in the reserves, caused overcrowding and soil erosion, and pushed people to seek work in towns and on farms.

## Laws on work and movement

**The Industrial Conciliation Act, 1934.** It set up a system of negotiation between employers and registered trade unions, but its definition of "employee" left out Africans in most jobs. This meant that African workers could not belong to the registered unions, could not use the machinery of negotiation, and were in effect shut out of skilled jobs, which were reserved for whites. The Act protected white workers from competition.

**Native Registration and pass laws.** Registration and pass systems, strengthened by the Native Registration Act of 1936, required Africans to carry documents and gave officials the power to control their movement. Urban areas were designed for African workers rather than as homes, and later laws required separate townships. Pass raids and arrests for failing to carry documents were a constant grievance.

**Protection of white farmers.** The Maize Control Act of 1931 and other marketing rules gave white farmers better prices and made it hard for African growers to compete.

:::example{title="Explaining the effect of an Act step by step: the Land Apportionment Act"}
1. **The law:** land was divided by race, with the best land for Europeans.
2. **The reserves:** Africans were confined to poorer, crowded areas.
3. **The result in the reserves:** soil erosion, falling yields and rural poverty.
4. **The result for labour:** men went to the towns and farms for wages, supplying cheap labour.
5. **The political effect:** the Act became a symbol of injustice and a target of nationalists later.
Answers should link each effect to the Act and distinguish short-term from long-term results.
:::

## Education and political rights

African education was left mainly to missions, with some government grants; there were far fewer secondary places than for whites, and what was taught was often limited. Africans had no part in making the laws. The franchise had no racial bar in law, but income and property qualifications meant that almost no Africans could vote. Governments in the 1950s began to widen the franchise slightly, but most Africans were still shut out.

## The Native Land Husbandry Act, 1951

The Act aimed to improve farming and conservation in the reserves. It gave individual Africans fixed plots and limited the number of cattle they could keep, with the aim of preventing overstocking and soil erosion. In practice, it was enforced through destocking and the reorganisation of holdings. Many people lost land or cattle, a class of landless Africans grew, and officials were seen as interfering with the way of life. The Act became one of the main grievances of the 1950s and was suspended in the early 1960s.

## African reactions and urbanisation

Africans protested in many ways. Early organisations included the Rhodesia Bantu Voters' Association (1923) and the Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union branch in Southern Rhodesia in the 1920s. Welfare societies and churches gave some education and a platform. After the Second World War towns grew, and workers organised. Railway workers struck in 1945, and in 1948 there was a general strike in Bulawayo and other towns, which the government ended by force and by minor concessions. In the 1950s, new political bodies such as the City Youth League (1955) appeared, which prepared the way for the nationalist parties of the next decade.

## The Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland, 1953-63

**Why it was formed.** Settler leaders such as Huggins and Roy Welensky argued that a larger state would be wealthier, since the copper of Northern Rhodesia, the labour of Nyasaland and the industry of Southern Rhodesia would complement each other, and that it could resist African nationalism and the influence of South Africa. Britain agreed, hoping for economic strength and a state in which "partnership" would benefit all. Africans in Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland opposed it, fearing the spread of Southern Rhodesian segregation.

**How it worked.** The Federation had its capital in Salisbury. It built the Kariba hydro-electric scheme, expanded the university and made the economy grow; however, the benefits went mainly to whites and to Southern Rhodesia. Partnership did not alter the laws in Southern Rhodesia.

**Why it ended.** African nationalism grew strongly, especially in Nyasaland and Northern Rhodesia under Hastings Banda and Kenneth Kaunda. The emergency in Nyasaland in 1959 and the Monckton Commission of 1960 showed that most Africans opposed it. Britain's policy moved towards African majority rule in the north. The Federation was dissolved on 31 December 1963, and Zambia and Malawi became independent in 1964. Southern Rhodesia, with its settler government, remained.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Land Apportionment Act gave Africans equal land"}
It reserved about half of the land for whites, a small minority. The reserves were poorer and crowded.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the Federation and UDI"}
The Federation (1953-63) was a union of three territories under Britain. UDI (1965) was the declaration of independence by the Southern Rhodesian government alone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Acts without their effects"}
For each Act, give the content, the effect on Africans and the African reaction.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- From 1923 an almost all-white electorate governed Southern Rhodesia and passed segregationist laws.
- The Land Apportionment Act (1930) divided land by race; the Industrial Conciliation Act (1934) shut Africans out of skilled work and unions; pass laws controlled movement; the Land Husbandry Act (1951) provoked resentment.
- Africans protested through associations, strikes and, later, nationalist organisations.
- The Federation (1953-63) was formed for economic and political reasons and collapsed as African nationalism grew in the north.
- These laws created the grievances which fed nationalism.
:::
