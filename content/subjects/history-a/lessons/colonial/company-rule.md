## Introduction

For thirty-three years, from 1890 to 1923, Zimbabwe was governed by a business company. The British South Africa Company (BSAC) had received a Royal Charter in 1889 and acted as government, landowner and employer. It hoped for profit from gold, and when the great goldfield did not appear, it turned to land sales, farming, mining of other minerals, railways and taxes. For Africans, Company rule meant the loss of land, new taxes, the demand for labour and a government in which they had no voice. For settlers, it meant a government that gradually gave them more power and led to self-government.

This lesson covers how the Company ruled, what it did to land, work and people, how the economy developed, what Africans complained of, and how Company rule ended. You should know the occupation, the war of 1893-94 and the risings of 1896-97, because the risings forced the first big reforms. Settler rule after 1923 is the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Administrator"}
The Company's chief official in the territory. Leander Starr Jameson held the post from 1891 to 1896, followed by Earl Grey (1896-98), William Milton (1898-1914) and Francis Chaplin (1914-23).
:::

:::definition{term="Native Commissioner"}
A Company official in charge of a district's African population, with powers to collect tax, settle disputes, supervise chiefs and recruit labour.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsible government"}
Self-government by a colony's own settlers, with an elected legislature and ministers, while the British Crown kept some reserved powers. Southern Rhodesia received it in 1923.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1894: Matabeleland Order in Council.
- 1898: Southern Rhodesia Order in Council; Legislative Council from 1899.
- 1897-99: railways reach Bulawayo and Salisbury.
- 1914-15: Native Reserves Commission examines the reserves.
- 1918: the Privy Council finds that unalienated land belongs to the Crown and not the Company.
- 27 October 1922: the settlers' referendum chooses responsible government.
- 1 October 1923: responsible government begins.
:::

## How the Company governed

**The Charter and the Administrator.** The Charter allowed the Company to administer the territory under the supervision of the British High Commissioner in Cape Town. The Administrator ran the territory day to day with a small staff and the British South Africa Company Police. The Company had to pay for government out of its own funds, which is why it sought revenue from every source.

**The Legislative Council.** The Order in Council of 1898 set up a Legislative Council (first meeting in 1899) with nominated Company members and a few elected settler members. A Resident Commissioner represented the imperial government. Settlers gained an elected majority in 1903. Africans had no part, although the franchise, in law, had no race bar; high property and income qualifications meant that almost no Africans qualified.

**The Native Department.** After the risings of 1896-97 the Native Department was reorganised. Native Commissioners in each district collected taxes, judged cases under African law, supervised chiefs and headmen, who were given small salaries, and helped to recruit labour. They became the face of the government for most Africans.

:::example{title="Analysing the system: why Native Commissioners mattered"}
1. **Power:** one official was administrator, judge, tax collector and labour agent.
2. **Chiefs:** a chief who obeyed was paid and supported; one who resisted could be removed.
3. **Effect for Africans:** there was little means of appeal, and laws were made without their consent.
4. **Effect for the Company:** with few officials it could govern a large territory cheaply.
A good answer links the system to the aims of cheap government and a supply of labour.
:::

## Land

After 1894 and especially after 1898, the Company marked out reserves for Africans and sold or granted the rest to settlers, mining companies and the Company itself. Reserves were often in drier, poorer areas. Many Africans on land that was designated for whites lived as tenants, paying rent or labour, or were moved. The Company claimed that it owned all unalienated land. In 1918 the Privy Council ruled that this land belonged to the Crown, not the Company, though the Company was entitled to be repaid for its costs. In 1919 Ndebele leaders petitioned London for land; their petition was not granted.

## Taxes and labour

The Company needed money and labour. A **hut tax** and later other taxes had to be paid in cash, so men had to earn wages on mines and farms. Pass laws and Native Registration rules controlled movement. Labour agents recruited men, including from Nyasaland and Mozambique, for the mines. Work in mines was hard and dangerous, and wages were low. Many Africans called this labour *chibaro*, a word associated with forced or contract labour. The Company also encouraged cash crops by Africans in some areas, but the best land and markets went to settlers.

## The settler economy

Mining developed: gold at many small mines, and later coal at Wankie from 1903, copper, chrome and asbestos. The railways reached Bulawayo in 1897 and Salisbury in 1899 from the south and east, and the Victoria Falls in 1904, which brought machinery and settlers and carried minerals and farm produce out. White farming grew, with maize and tobacco, helped by markets, cheap labour and the Company's land policy. The Company's own profits were limited, and its shareholders were disappointed for many years.

## African grievances and responses

Africans had grievances about land, tax, labour, the pass laws, the lack of political voice, poor schools and the power of Native Commissioners. They responded in several ways: by migrating to work and returning, by refusing or evading tax and labour, by appealing to the Company and to London (the Ndebele petition of 1919), by forming welfare societies and by joining churches that gave some education. The scale of open resistance was far smaller than in 1896, because the Company had shown it would use force.

## The end of Company rule

The Company's Charter was renewed in 1914 for ten years, but settlers wanted self-government, and the Company wished to hand over its administrative costs. In 1922 the white voters chose between joining the Union of South Africa and "responsible government"; on 27 October 1922 about 8,800 voted for responsible government and about 6,000 for union. Africans did not vote. In 1923 Britain annexed the territory as the colony of Southern Rhodesia, and responsible government began on 1 October 1923 under its first premier, Charles Coghlan. The Company kept its mineral rights and railways.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Britain ruled Southern Rhodesia directly before 1923"}
The Company governed, subject to imperial supervision through the High Commissioner and a Resident Commissioner. Britain held the Crown's reserve powers, but day-to-day rule was the Company's.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Africans voted in the 1922 referendum"}
Only the white voters on the roll took part.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Company as a benefit only"}
Railways and mines developed under the Company, but the costs fell mostly on Africans: land, taxes, labour and lack of rights.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The BSA Company governed under its Charter, with an Administrator, a Legislative Council from 1899 and Native Commissioners.
- Reserves were created, the best land went to settlers and the Company, and taxes and pass laws forced Africans into wage labour.
- Railways, mines and white farms developed, but Africans bore much of the cost.
- Africans protested by petitions, evasion and organisation, but without political rights.
- The 1922 referendum of white voters chose responsible government, which began on 1 October 1923.
:::
