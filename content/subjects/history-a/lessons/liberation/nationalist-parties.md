## Introduction

Between 1959 and 1964 African nationalism in Southern Rhodesia passed through a rapid series of parties: the National Democratic Party (NDP), the Zimbabwe African People's Union (ZAPU) and the Zimbabwe African National Union (ZANU). Each was banned by the government in turn, and in 1963 the movement divided into two rival parties. By August 1964, when both were banned and their leaders detained, peaceful politics seemed to be closed, and many nationalists turned to armed struggle.

This lesson sets out how each party arose, what it wanted, who led and supported it, and how repression and the split shaped what followed. The military events belong to the next lessons. You should know about the Congress of 1957 and the Federation from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Majority rule"}
Government by the majority of the people. In Southern Rhodesia, nationalists demanded "one man, one vote", which would give power to the African majority.
:::

:::definition{term="Detention"}
Imprisonment without trial, used by the Rhodesian government under emergency powers and security laws to remove nationalist leaders from political life.
:::

:::definition{term="Banning"}
A government order that makes an organisation illegal, so that it cannot hold meetings or collect members.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- February 1959: Congress banned; state of emergency; hundreds detained.
- 1 January 1960: the NDP formed (early leaders included Michael Mawema; Nkomo became president in October 1960).
- December 1961: NDP banned; ZAPU formed under Nkomo. September 1962: ZAPU banned (the People's Caretaker Council followed).
- August 1963: ZANU formed under the Rev. Ndabaningi Sithole (with Robert Mugabe, Leopold Takawira, Enos Nkala and others).
- August 1964: ZANU and the PCC (ZAPU) banned; leaders detained.
:::

:::

## From Congress to the NDP

The Southern Rhodesia African National Congress, led by Joshua Nkomo, campaigned against the Federation and for the vote, land and an end to discrimination. In February 1959 the government declared a state of emergency, banned Congress and detained about 500 people. The government also passed new laws against subversion and unlawful organisations. Leaders who had not been arrested, and the movement's supporters, formed the **National Democratic Party** on 1 January 1960. Under Nkomo, who became president in October 1960, it organised rallies, demonstrations and a petition campaign, and won the support of many workers, students and rural people. It demanded majority rule, an end to racial discrimination and a share of the land. Its first leaders included Michael Mawema, who was the first president, and the party quickly became larger than Congress had been.

## The constitutional crisis of 1961

In 1961 a constitutional conference was held in Salisbury, with the British government and the Prime Minister, Sir Edgar Whitehead. The new constitution proposed a franchise with two rolls: an "A" roll for those with higher qualifications, which most whites would meet, and a "B" roll for those with lower qualifications, which would contain mostly Africans. Africans would have 15 of 65 seats in parliament, with the prospect of more in the future but no guaranteed majority. Nkomo at first appeared to accept the proposals, but his colleagues and the NDP rejected them, and he changed his position; the Constitution was approved in a referendum in July 1961 in which only voters already on the roll, nearly all of them white, could vote. The episode showed both the party's eagerness for compromise and the difficulties it faced in holding a united line.

## ZAPU

The government banned the NDP in December 1961. Within days its leaders formed the **Zimbabwe African People's Union** (ZAPU) on 17 December 1961, with Nkomo as president. ZAPU continued the demand for majority rule, organised protest and in places violence, such as petrol bombs and sabotage of property. It also looked abroad for diplomatic and military help. The government banned ZAPU in September 1962 and restricted Nkomo. Its supporters formed the **People's Caretaker Council** (PCC) in 1962 to carry on the work.

## The split of 1963 and the formation of ZANU

By 1963 many nationalists were dissatisfied with Nkomo's leadership, and disagreements had grown. Some felt that he was too cautious or that he spent too much time abroad, and others criticised the way decisions were made. Others argued that the movement needed to prepare for a longer struggle. In 1963 the Rev. Ndabaningi Sithole, Robert Mugabe, Leopold Takawira, Enos Nkala and others broke away to form the **Zimbabwe African National Union** (ZANU), announced in August 1963 with Sithole as president. The nationalists were thus divided into two parties, ZAPU (and the PCC) and ZANU. Historians stress several causes of the split: arguments over leadership and strategy, regional and personal rivalries, and external influences, and they disagree about their relative weight.

:::example{title="Explaining the split of 1963"}
1. Under the strain of bans and detention, some leaders felt that Nkomo's strategy of diplomacy abroad was not producing results.
2. Others objected to his style of leadership and his plan to set up a government in exile.
3. Personal and regional rivalries sharpened the quarrel, although both parties drew support across the country.
4. The split was formalised by the creation of ZANU in August 1963.
5. The result was bitter rivalry, with violence between supporters in the townships in 1963-64, which weakened the movement and provided the government with an excuse for stronger action.
Always say that historians differ about the weight of these causes.
:::

## Leaders, support and policies

Both parties wanted majority rule and an end to discrimination, and they demanded land. In practice they differed in organisation and support. ZAPU drew its main support in Matabeleland and parts of the towns and was later supplied mainly by the Soviet Union and its allies. ZANU had its main strength in the Shona-speaking areas and in the urban townships of Salisbury and later looked to China for training and weapons. These regional patterns are generalisations; each party had members in all regions. ZANU's leaders were more inclined to argue that armed struggle would be necessary, and both parties formed military wings: ZAPU's was the Zimbabwe People's Revolutionary Army (ZIPRA) and ZANU's the Zimbabwe African National Liberation Army (ZANLA).

## The bans of 1964 and the turn to armed struggle

The Rhodesian Front, in office from December 1962 under Winston Field and from April 1964 under Ian Smith, took a tougher line. The government refused to discuss majority rule. In August 1964 it banned ZANU and the PCC and detained their leaders, among them Nkomo, Sithole and Mugabe. Many were held for a decade. After this, open political activity inside the country was impossible and the nationalist movements moved their leadership abroad, mainly to Zambia and Tanzania, and began to train guerrillas. The government's refusal to negotiate, the bans and the detention of the leaders were therefore important causes of the turn to armed struggle, together with the split that left the movement weakened.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the dates of the bans"}
Congress was banned in 1959, the NDP in 1961, ZAPU in 1962, and ZANU and the PCC in 1964. Learn the sequence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting the split as a simple ethnic quarrel"}
Ethnicity and regional ties played some part, but historians stress leadership, strategy and personal rivalries as well. Give several causes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming only one party for the violence"}
Violence between supporters of both parties occurred in 1963-64. Write about it factually and without taking sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Congress was banned in 1959; the NDP (1960) and then ZAPU (1961) followed and were banned in turn.
- Nationalists demanded majority rule, the end of discrimination and land.
- In 1963 a group led by Sithole broke away to form ZANU, so the movement was divided.
- ZAPU drew support mainly in Matabeleland and from the Soviet bloc; ZANU in Mashonaland and from China, with armed wings ZIPRA and ZANLA.
- The bans and detentions of 1964, with the refusal to negotiate, pushed the nationalists towards armed struggle.
