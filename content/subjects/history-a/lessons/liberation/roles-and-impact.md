## Introduction

The liberation war was not fought only by soldiers. Villagers fed and hid guerrillas, spirit mediums gave the struggle meaning, women worked as fighters and organisers, churches spoke out, and others served the Rhodesian state. The war also fell heavily on ordinary people, many of whom suffered at the hands of both sides. A good historian must record all of these roles and experiences and avoid simple stories of heroes and villains.

This lesson looks at the main groups and their roles, and then at the impact of the war on African communities. It uses the facts of the previous two lessons and keeps to the established evidence. You should be aware that people remember the war differently, and that some questions, such as numbers of deaths and the responsibility for particular incidents, are still disputed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Spirit medium"}
A person believed to be possessed by the spirit of an ancestor or a guardian spirit of the land, who speaks with its authority. Mediums were respected in Shona society, and in the 1970s some gave guidance to guerrillas.
:::

:::definition{term="Collaborator"}
A person who worked with the other side. In this war the word was used by the guerrillas and nationalists for Africans who served the Rhodesian state as soldiers, police, informers or auxiliaries. The word is loaded, and people had varied reasons for their choices.
:::

:::definition{term="Refugee"}
A person who flees from danger across a border or to safety elsewhere. By the late 1970s many thousands of Zimbabweans were refugees in Mozambique, Zambia and Botswana.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Roles to learn"}
- Spirit mediums: legitimacy and guidance; links to 1896-97.
- Peasants: food, shelter, information; mujibas and chimbwidos.
- Women: fighters, organisers, carers.
- Chiefs: divided; some assisted guerrillas, some were government agents.
- Churches: criticism, relief and sometimes suffering; some church leaders and missions were targeted.
- Nationalist organisations: ZAPU, ZANU, the African National Council.

:::

## Spirit mediums

In Shona society mediums spoke for the ancestors and the guardian spirits of the land. In 1896-97 mediums such as those of Nehanda and Kaguvi helped to lead the First Chimurenga, and in the 1970s ZANLA drew on this memory. Mediums advised fighters on customs that would protect them, such as respect for sacred places and rules about food and behaviour, they helped to settle disputes between guerrillas and villagers, and they linked the war to the ancestors and the land. Their authority convinced many elders to cooperate. Historians such as David Lan have studied the relationship in detail and stress that mediums and guerrillas used each other: the fighters gained legitimacy and the mediums gained a role in a new political movement. Not all mediums took the guerrillas' side.

## Peasants

Rural families were the base of the war. They gave food, shelter and information, attended pungwes and carried supplies. The young men (mujibas) and young women (chimbwidos) watched for soldiers and carried messages and food. Peasants joined because they had real grievances about land, cattle destocking, taxes and discrimination, and because guerrillas used persuasion and, sometimes, pressure. They were also in danger from both sides: the army punished villages thought to have helped the guerrillas, while guerrillas could punish those thought to inform. Some villagers simply tried to survive.

:::example{title="Weighing the experience of villagers"}
Question: "Were villagers supporters or victims of the war?" A balanced answer notes that many villagers actively supported the struggle because of real grievances, giving food, shelter and information. But they were also victims: the army punished them, the protected villages removed them from their land, and guerrillas sometimes used pressure or violence against suspected informers. The answer to the question is therefore "both", with experiences that varied by place and time.
:::

## Women

Women took part in many ways. Some trained as fighters in the ZANLA and ZIPRA camps, and women commanders and political leaders emerged. Others served as nurses, teachers and cooks, or worked in the villages as chimbwidos and food providers. Women also lived with the burden of running households while men were at war or in detention, and many suffered sexual violence and the loss of family members. Historians have shown that women's contribution was larger than early accounts suggested, and they also point out that their role in government after independence did not always match.

## Chiefs

The Rhodesian government paid chiefs and expected them to collect taxes, keep order and report on strangers. Some chiefs served the state, and some were accused by the guerrillas of being government agents. Others supported or secretly helped the guerrillas, and a number were killed by one side or the other. The chiefs' position was therefore difficult. After independence the status of chiefs remained a subject of political argument.

## Churches

Christian churches had many members in the war zones. Some churches helped refugees and the wounded; some church leaders spoke out against injustice and violence. In 1976 the Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace published reports about the conduct of the war, and Bishop Donal Lamont of Umtali, a sharp critic of the government, was convicted in 1976 of failing to report the presence of guerrillas and was later deported. The Methodist bishop Abel Muzorewa became a political leader. Mission schools and hospitals in rural areas were closed in many districts; some missionaries and African staff were killed. Church leaders were divided in their views on how to end the war, and the war affected their own members.

## Nationalist organisations

ZAPU and ZANU provided leadership, recruited fighters, ran camps and schools and handled diplomacy abroad. The African National Council, led by Bishop Muzorewa, opposed the Smith-Home proposals in 1972, and later became a party within the internal settlement. The Patriotic Front (1976) represented the armed movements. These organisations were rivals as well as partners, and this affected the conduct of the war.

## War collaborators and Africans in the Rhodesian forces

Many Africans served in the Rhodesian security forces, notably in the Rhodesian African Rifles, the police and the intelligence services, and some served as informers. From 1978 the government armed African auxiliaries linked to the political leaders of the internal settlement. Their reasons varied: a wage, loyalty to a regiment, fear, or opposition to one nationalist group. The nationalists called them collaborators, and the experience of these men after the war was sensitive. A balanced answer notes that the label hides different motives and that these soldiers were also part of the war's story.

## Impact on African communities

- **Displacement.** Hundreds of thousands of people, according to common estimates, were moved into protected villages or fled from the war zones to towns.
- **Loss of life.** Estimates of total deaths range from about 20,000 to 30,000 or more, most of them civilians. These are estimates only.
- **Disruption of schools and clinics.** Many rural schools and clinics, including mission institutions, closed or were destroyed, interrupting education and health care.
- **Refugees.** Tens of thousands of people fled to Mozambique, Zambia and Botswana, and many lived in camps.
- **Economic loss.** Cattle, crops and homes were lost, and fields went unfarmed.
- **Social and psychological effects.** The war split families and communities and left long memories of fear and loss.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Presenting a single African experience"}
Different groups and places had different experiences. Show variety and avoid saying "all Africans supported the guerrillas" or "all villagers were forced".
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'collaborator' without comment"}
The word was used by one side. Explain it and note the varied reasons for serving the state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving death figures as facts"}
Say "estimates range from...", since no exact total is agreed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="ZIMSEC Paper 1"}
Typical questions: "Assess the contribution of peasants and spirit mediums to the liberation struggle", "Examine the role of women", "Describe the effects of the war on African communities", "To what extent did the war benefit the African people?" Name the group, give two or three specific roles, show the other side of the experience and make a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Spirit mediums gave legitimacy and linked the war to 1896-97; peasants fed, hid and informed; women served as fighters and organisers.
- Chiefs and churches were divided and often caught between sides; nationalist organisations gave leadership.
- Many Africans served in the Rhodesian forces and were called collaborators, for varied reasons.
- The war caused displacement, protected villages, loss of life, closed schools and clinics and refugees.
- Experiences varied, and numbers of casualties are estimates only.
:::
