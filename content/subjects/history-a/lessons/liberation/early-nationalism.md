## Introduction

When Africans lost their independence in 1890-97 and later after the risings, they did not stop resisting. They changed the methods. From the 1920s, in the towns and on the mines and railways, Africans formed associations, unions and eventually political parties to demand better wages, fair treatment and a share in government. By 1957 these efforts had produced a mass political movement, the Southern Rhodesia African National Congress, which began the nationalist campaign that led to independence in 1980.

This lesson traces the early organisations, explains why trade unionism gradually gave way to mass nationalism, and shows how the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland (1953) fed African anger. The next lesson looks at the parties that followed Congress. You should know about the colonial laws, such as the Land Apportionment Act (1930), that shaped the grievances.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
A movement that demands self-government for a people who believe that they form a nation. African nationalism in Southern Rhodesia demanded majority rule and the end of white minority control.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade union"}
An organisation of workers that bargains with employers for better pay and conditions. African workers' unions had little legal protection in Southern Rhodesia because the Industrial Conciliation Act (1934) did not count Africans as "employees".
:::

:::definition{term="Federation"}
Here, the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland (1953-63), in which Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland were joined in one state dominated by the white settlers of Southern Rhodesia.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1923: Rhodesia Bantu Voters' Association (Abraham Twala) to defend African voters.
- Late 1920s: the ICU branch; leaders such as Masotsha Ndlovu and Charles Mzingeli.
- 1945: railway workers' strike; 1948: general strike (Benjamin Burombo's African Voice Association).
- 1953: Federation formed. 1955: City Youth League. 1956: Salisbury bus boycott.
- September 1957: the Southern Rhodesia African National Congress, with Joshua Nkomo as president.
:::

## Welfare and voters' associations

In the 1920s the first African organisations were not political parties but associations of the small educated class, such as teachers, clerks and church leaders. Welfare societies and "native associations" asked for better schools, land rights and fairer treatment. The Rhodesia Bantu Voters' Association, founded in 1923 by Abraham Twala, tried to use the few African votes that qualification rules allowed, to press for African interests. These associations were cautious and respectful, but their appeals were largely ignored by the government.

## The ICU and early unions

In the late 1920s a branch of the Industrial and Commercial Workers' Union (ICU), a South African movement of African workers, spread to Southern Rhodesia. Its Bulawayo leaders included Masotsha Ndlovu, and in Salisbury Charles Mzingeli became a long-time champion of urban workers and tenants. The ICU spoke for domestic servants, railway and mine workers and for the town dwellers who suffered from low wages, pass-like controls and poor housing. It was harassed by employers and police, and it split, but it showed that African workers could organise across ethnic lines.

After the Second World War, towns grew and wages lagged behind prices. In October 1945 African railway workers went on strike, and the strike spread. In 1948 a general strike in Bulawayo and other towns, supported by Benjamin Burombo's African Voice Association, forced employers and government to take notice. The strikes won some concessions but also showed the limits of unionism: strikes were put down by police and the law, and unions had no legal standing.

:::example{title="Explaining why trade unions were limited"}
1. The Industrial Conciliation Act of 1934 defined "employee" so as to exclude Africans, so African unions had no legal right to bargain.
2. Employers dismissed strikers and the police broke up gatherings.
3. Most Africans lived in the reserves or on farms, where unions could not reach them.
4. The main grievances (the loss of land, the franchise, the pass-like controls) were political and could not be won by workplace bargaining.
5. Therefore many leaders concluded that Africans needed a political party to demand a change in government itself.
:::

## The Federation of 1953

In 1953 Britain joined Southern Rhodesia, Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland into a federation, with a capital at Salisbury and a parliament dominated by white settlers. The settlers wanted bigger markets and the copper of the north, and the British government promised "partnership" between the races. African leaders in all three territories opposed it, for they feared that it would strengthen white control and spread Southern Rhodesian racial policies. In practice, the partnership brought little benefit: the Federation continued discrimination in jobs, land and the vote. The Federation therefore convinced many Africans that peaceful pleas were not enough and that they needed organised political power.

## The City Youth League and the bus boycott

In 1955 young, educated townsmen in Salisbury formed the **City Youth League**. Its leaders included James Chikerema and George Nyandoro, and its supporters were mostly urban workers and students. The League campaigned against the lack of rights and for the African vote. In 1956 it led a boycott of Salisbury's buses after fares were raised, in which thousands of Africans walked to work, and the fares were reduced. The success showed that mass action could work, and it brought a younger generation into politics.

## The formation of the Southern Rhodesia African National Congress

In September 1957 the Youth League joined the Bulawayo-based African National Congress, and the movement became the **Southern Rhodesia African National Congress**. **Joshua Nkomo**, a former railway union official, became its president. Congress was a mass party, open to all Africans, and its demands included an end to racial discrimination, a wider franchise and a fairer share of land. It was the first nationwide African political organisation, and its leaders adopted the methods of peaceful protest and negotiation. Its further history is in the next lesson.

## Why did unionism give way to mass nationalism?

Four reasons are often given. First, unions were legally weak and could not win political change. Second, the African urban population grew rapidly after 1945 and developed shared grievances. Third, the Federation threatened to entrench white rule. Fourth, independence movements elsewhere in Africa, such as the Gold Coast, which became independent as Ghana in 1957, gave an example that Africans could win self-rule. Many early nationalists were former trade unionists, so the two movements overlapped rather than ending at one date.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that African politics began in 1957"}
Congress was the first mass party, but welfare associations, the ICU, the strikes and the Youth League came earlier.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the ANC of 1957 with the South African ANC"}
They shared a name and ideas, but the Southern Rhodesia African National Congress was a separate organisation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the Federation as a cause only of the later war"}
The Federation also shaped the early nationalist movement. Link it to the rise of Congress.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Early African organisation began with welfare and voters' associations and the ICU in the 1920s.
- Strikes in 1945 and 1948 showed the power of workers but also the legal limits of unionism.
- The Federation of 1953 strengthened white control and angered Africans.
- The City Youth League (1955) and the bus boycott (1956) brought in a younger urban leadership.
- In September 1957 the Southern Rhodesia African National Congress was formed, with Joshua Nkomo as president.
