## Introduction

The armed struggle of the 1960s and 1970s is called the **Second Chimurenga** (or Second Umvukela) because many Zimbabweans saw it as a continuation of the risings of 1896-97 against colonial rule. Why did Africans take up arms after years of peaceful politics? And why, in a war that lasted fifteen years, did the many efforts to end it by negotiation fail until 1979?

This lesson answers both questions. It has two halves: the causes, grouped as political, economic and social, and international; and the attempts at a peaceful settlement, in order. The war itself is in the next two lessons, and the successful negotiation at Lancaster House is in a separate lesson. You should know about the nationalist parties and their banning in 1959-64.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="UDI"}
The Unilateral Declaration of Independence, announced by Ian Smith's Rhodesian Front government on 11 November 1965, by which the white minority government claimed independence from Britain without agreeing to majority rule. Britain called it illegal and the United Nations imposed sanctions.
:::

:::definition{term="NIBMAR"}
"No independence before majority African rule", the British principle announced after 1965 that Southern Rhodesia would not receive independence unless the constitution provided for rule by the African majority.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions"}
Penalties, such as bans on trade, imposed by states to force a government to change its policy. Sanctions on Rhodesia began in 1965-66 and became mandatory under the United Nations from December 1966 and were widened in 1968.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 11 November 1965: UDI. December 1966: HMS Tiger talks. October 1968: HMS Fearless talks.
- 1972: the Pearce Commission finds that Africans reject the Smith-Home proposals.
- August 1975: Victoria Falls Conference. October-December 1976: Geneva Conference.
- 1977: Anglo-American proposals. 3 March 1978: the internal settlement.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The causes of the Second Chimurenga and the peaceful attempts are ZIMSEC only (Paper 1). Cambridge's covered options do not examine them. The Lancaster House talks are in a separate lesson.
:::

## Causes of the armed struggle

**Land.** The Land Apportionment Act (1930) divided land between races, giving the best land to whites. The Land Husbandry Act (1951) and the Land Tenure Act (1969) added to the pressure. African reserves, later called Tribal Trust Lands, were crowded and poor, and families were moved from areas marked for white farms. Land remained the central grievance.

**Discrimination.** Africans faced job reservation for whites, lower wages, segregated schools, hospitals and public places, and a shortage of secondary and university places.

**Loss of political rights.** The franchise was limited by income and education qualifications, so almost all voters were white. The 1961 constitution offered Africans 15 of 65 seats, and the 1969 republican constitution (adopted after a referendum of white voters in 1969) ended any path to majority rule.

**Repression.** The Law and Order (Maintenance) Act (1960), states of emergency, detention without trial and the banning of the NDP, ZAPU and ZANU closed legal politics.

**UDI.** The declaration of 1965 showed that the white government would not give up power peacefully, and the ending of British constitutional authority left Africans with no one to appeal to.

**Failure of constitutional methods.** Strikes, boycotts, petitions and negotiations had not won the vote, and leaders were in detention.

**Decolonisation.** The independence of Ghana (1957), Tanganyika (1961), Zambia and Malawi (1964), and the armed struggles in Mozambique and Angola showed that colonial rule could be ended, and independent states offered bases.

:::example{title="Grouping causes in an essay"}
Group the causes: **underlying** (land, discrimination, lack of the vote); **triggers** (bans and detention of the leaders in 1964 and UDI in 1965); and **enabling** (independence of neighbours and foreign help). A strong paragraph then argues which mattered most. For example: "Land was the deepest cause because it affected most Africans and linked the struggle to the risings of 1896, but the nationalists turned to arms only when the bans and the refusal to negotiate closed the political road."
:::

## Attempts to end the war peacefully

After UDI Britain refused to recognise the Smith government. Efforts to reach a settlement followed.

- **HMS Tiger (December 1966).** British Prime Minister Harold Wilson met Ian Smith aboard HMS Tiger near Gibraltar. Wilson proposed a return to legality under a constitution with a path to majority rule; Smith returned to Salisbury and his cabinet rejected the terms.
- **HMS Fearless (October 1968).** A second meeting on board HMS Fearless failed on similar grounds; Smith would not accept a return to legality.
- **The Pearce Commission (1972).** In November 1971 Smith and the British foreign secretary, Sir Alec Douglas-Home, agreed proposals that would have allowed African majority rule only in the distant future. Britain sent a commission under Lord Pearce to test opinion. Led by Bishop Abel Muzorewa's new African National Council, Africans rejected the terms in large numbers, and the commission reported in May 1972 that the proposals were unacceptable to the people as a whole.
- **Détente and the Victoria Falls Conference (August 1975).** In 1974-75 South Africa's Prime Minister John Vorster and Zambia's president Kenneth Kaunda urged talks. Smith and the nationalists met on a railway carriage on the bridge at Victoria Falls on 25-26 August 1975, but they could not agree on the procedure, and the conference ended in failure.
- **Kissinger and Geneva (1976).** The American Secretary of State, Henry Kissinger, persuaded Smith in September 1976 to accept majority rule within two years. A conference met in Geneva in October-December 1976 under the British chairman Ivor Richard, with the Patriotic Front (an alliance of ZAPU and ZANU), Muzorewa, Sithole and Smith's delegation. It failed on the question of who would hold power during the transition.
- **The Anglo-American proposals (1977).** Britain and the United States put forward a plan for a transition under a British resident commissioner, a new constitution, an independence army and a development fund. Smith rejected it, and the Patriotic Front objected to parts.
- **The internal settlement (3 March 1978).** Smith, Muzorewa, Sithole and Chief Jeremiah Chirau agreed a constitution with a black prime minister but with white control over the police, army, civil service and judiciary and 28 of 100 seats reserved for whites. Elections in April 1979 produced the state of "Zimbabwe-Rhodesia" under Muzorewa. The Patriotic Front did not take part, the war continued, and no foreign government recognised it.

## Why did the peaceful attempts fail?

Several reasons recur. The Smith government would not give up white control, and its proposals always kept power with whites for a long time. The nationalists, in turn, would not accept anything short of majority rule, and the two African parties were divided. Britain could not use force and relied on sanctions and talks. The war and the economy mattered: until 1976-78 the Rhodesian government could believe that it could hold out. The internal settlement then failed because it excluded the Patriotic Front and kept white privileges, so it did not end the war.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing HMS Tiger and HMS Fearless"}
Tiger was 1966 and Fearless 1968. Both were meetings between Wilson and Smith that failed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that the Pearce Commission rejected the proposals"}
The commission tested opinion. It reported that the Africans of Rhodesia as a whole rejected them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating UDI as a cause of the nationalist movement"}
The nationalist movement began earlier. UDI strengthened the case for armed struggle but did not start the movement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land, discrimination, loss of the vote, repression, UDI, failed constitutional methods and decolonisation caused the armed struggle.
- Wilson-Smith talks on HMS Tiger (1966) and HMS Fearless (1968) failed.
- The Pearce Commission (1972) found that Africans rejected the Smith-Home proposals.
- The Victoria Falls Conference (1975), Geneva (1976) and the Anglo-American proposals (1977) also failed.
- The internal settlement of 1978 left power with whites and did not end the war, which led to Lancaster House in 1979.
:::
