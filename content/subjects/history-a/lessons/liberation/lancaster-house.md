## Introduction

On 21 December 1979, in a room at Lancaster House in London, delegates signed an agreement that ended the war, brought in a British governor and set the way to elections and independence. Four months later, on 18 April 1980, Zimbabwe became independent. The settlement was a compromise reached under pressure. It ended fifteen years of fighting but also contained terms, on land, seats for whites and the protection of property, that shaped the country's politics for decades.

This lesson explains why the conference met, who attended, what was agreed, how the transition and elections worked, and how the results can be judged. Earlier peaceful attempts to end the war are in the lesson on causes and peaceful attempts. You should also know the course of the war in 1972-79.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Independence constitution"}
The constitution agreed at Lancaster House and adopted at independence in 1980. It set out the structure of government, a declaration of rights and protections for property, with some provisions that could not be changed for ten years.
:::

:::definition{term="Common roll"}
A single voters' roll on which all adult citizens could vote. At independence 80 seats were elected from the common roll and 20 were reserved for whites, elected from a separate roll.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceasefire"}
An agreement to stop fighting. At Lancaster House ZANLA and ZIPRA fighters agreed to assemble at designated points under Commonwealth monitoring.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1-7 August 1979: the Commonwealth Heads of Government meeting at Lusaka.
- 10 September - 21 December 1979: Lancaster House Conference, chaired by the British foreign secretary Lord Carrington.
- December 1979: Lord Soames arrives in Salisbury as governor. 28 February 1980: elections.
- 18 April 1980: independence; Canaan Banana president, Robert Mugabe prime minister.
:::

## Why was the conference convened?

1. **The war.** By 1979 Rhodesia was under heavy pressure. The war covered most of the country, thousands had died, the economy was strained and white morale was falling. The Rhodesian forces could not defeat the guerrillas.
2. **Sanctions.** Economic sanctions had hurt the economy for fourteen years, although South Africa had helped Rhodesia to survive.
3. **Failure of the internal settlement.** The elections of April 1979, in which Bishop Abel Muzorewa became prime minister of "Zimbabwe-Rhodesia", did not end the war because the Patriotic Front did not take part, whites kept power in the army, police and courts, and no other government recognised the new state.
4. **Pressure from Africa and the Commonwealth.** At the Commonwealth meeting at Lusaka in August 1979, led by Zambia's president Kenneth Kaunda, the leaders persuaded the new British prime minister, Margaret Thatcher, to call a conference of all parties to produce an independence constitution that was acceptable to the world. Many historians note that Thatcher had been expected to recognise the Muzorewa government, and the Lusaka meeting changed her position, a point on which historians debate how much weight to give to Commonwealth, American and African pressure.
5. **The Frontline States.** Mozambique, Zambia, Tanzania, Botswana and Angola were suffering from Rhodesian attacks and the war's effects, and they pressed the Patriotic Front to negotiate. Mozambique's president Samora Machel was especially important in persuading Robert Mugabe's party to attend and to compromise.

:::example{title="Explaining why the Patriotic Front agreed to negotiate"}
1. The war was costly for neighbouring states, which hosted the camps and suffered raids.
2. Machel and the other Frontline leaders told the nationalists that they could not expect unlimited support.
3. The nationalists were not certain of a military victory, but believed they would win an election.
4. The conference offered a route to a fair election and international recognition.
5. They therefore attended, though not without objections on land and on the reserved seats.
:::

## The delegations

The conference met in London from 10 September 1979 under Lord Carrington, with Sir Ian Gilmour and the diplomat Sir Robin Renwick among the British team. Three groups took part: the British government; the delegation of the Zimbabwe-Rhodesia government led by Bishop Muzorewa, which included Ian Smith; and the Patriotic Front, led jointly by Joshua Nkomo (ZAPU) and Robert Mugabe (ZANU). The Frontline States did not take part but were consulted and pressed their allies.

## The agreement

Negotiations took place in three stages: the constitution, the transition, and the ceasefire.

**The independence constitution.** Its main provisions were:

- a parliament with a House of Assembly of 100 members, of whom 80 would be elected by Africans on a common roll and 20 by whites on a separate roll, and a Senate; the 20 white seats were to last at least seven years;
- a declaration of rights and protection for property, including the right to compensation in a transferable currency if land was taken, with these provisions unamendable for ten years, except by unanimous vote;
- guarantees for pensions and for the civil service, judiciary and police.

**Land.** The nationalists wanted compulsory acquisition, but the constitution allowed purchases on a "willing seller, willing buyer" basis. Britain and the United States indicated they would support a land programme, but no firm commitment was written into the agreement. The issue nearly broke the conference.

**The transition and ceasefire.** For about four months Rhodesia returned to British rule under Governor Lord Soames, who arrived in Salisbury on 12 December 1979. The sanctions were lifted. The guerrilla armies and the Rhodesian forces were to withdraw to assembly points monitored by a Commonwealth force, and elections would follow under British supervision. The agreement was signed on 21 December and the ceasefire began on 28 December.

## The elections and independence

Elections were held on 27-29 February 1980. ZANU (PF), led by Robert Mugabe, won 57 of the 80 common-roll seats; Nkomo's PF-ZAPU won 20, and Muzorewa's UANC 3. Ian Smith's party won all 20 white seats. Mugabe became prime minister and formed a government that included members of PF-ZAPU and two white ministers. On 18 April 1980 Zimbabwe became independent, with Rev. Canaan Banana as ceremonial president. Prince Charles represented the British Crown at the ceremony.

## Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages.** It ended the war and a fifteen-year conflict; brought international recognition and the end of sanctions; allowed all parties to compete in a fair election supervised by Britain; led to majority rule; protected civil rights; and avoided a collapse into continued war.

**Disadvantages.** It kept 20 seats for the white minority for seven years; the property clause protected land owners, and left unresolved the land question that was a main cause of the war; the nationalists accepted terms under pressure that many felt did not reflect their victories; the civil service, army and courts continued to contain many officials of the old regime; and the arrangements for integrating three armies created later tensions.

Historians differ about the settlement. Some stress that it was a compromise that avoided further bloodshed and gave a peaceful transition. Others argue that the provisions on land and property limited what the first government could do and left a lasting grievance. The balanced view is that it was a necessary compromise whose terms had long-term costs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the internal settlement with Lancaster House"}
The internal settlement (1978) excluded the Patriotic Front. Lancaster House (1979) included all the parties and led to independence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that the guerrillas won a military victory"}
The war put heavy pressure on Rhodesia, but the end came by negotiation, helped by the Frontline States and Britain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the land provisions"}
Land was one of the most contested parts of the conference. Mention the property clause and willing seller, willing buyer.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- War, sanctions, the failure of the internal settlement and pressure from the Commonwealth and the Frontline States led to the conference.
- Lancaster House (10 September - 21 December 1979) brought Britain, the Patriotic Front and the Zimbabwe-Rhodesia delegation together.
- The constitution reserved 20 of 100 seats for whites for seven years and protected property; land was left to willing seller, willing buyer.
- Lord Soames governed during the transition; elections in February 1980 gave ZANU (PF) 57 of 80 common-roll seats.
- Independence on 18 April 1980 ended minority rule, but the land issue and the settlement's terms remained controversial.
:::
