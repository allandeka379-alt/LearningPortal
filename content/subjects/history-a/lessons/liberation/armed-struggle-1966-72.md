## Introduction

On 28 April 1966, near the town of Chinhoyi (then Sinoia), a small group of guerrillas fought the Rhodesian security forces. The date is remembered in Zimbabwe as the start of the Second Chimurenga, the armed struggle that ended in 1979-80. Yet the war in its first years did not threaten the Rhodesian state. Between 1966 and 1972 the guerrillas lost most of the battles, and by 1972 the Rhodesian government believed that it was winning.

This lesson describes the early sabotage and the two main phases of fighting, the roles of ZANLA and ZIPRA, the Rhodesian response, and the reasons why this first phase did not succeed. The reasons are important: they explain why a quite different strategy was used from 1972. You should know about the nationalist parties, the bans of 1964 and the causes of the war from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Guerrilla warfare"}
A style of fighting in which small, mobile forces use surprise, local knowledge and the support of the people to wear down a stronger enemy, rather than meeting it in open battle.
:::

:::definition{term="ZANLA"}
The Zimbabwe African National Liberation Army, the armed wing of ZANU, trained mainly in China and Tanzania.
:::

:::definition{term="ZIPRA"}
The Zimbabwe People's Revolutionary Army, the armed wing of ZAPU, trained mainly in the Soviet Union and its allies.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 28 April 1966: Battle of Chinhoyi; seven ZANLA fighters killed.
- August-September 1967: the Wankie campaign (ZIPRA with fighters of the South African ANC).
- March-April 1968: the Sipolilo campaign.
- 1967: South African police units arrive to help Rhodesia.
- 1969: ZANU forms the Dare reChimurenga (war council) under Herbert Chitepo; ZANLA's commander was Josiah Tongogara.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## Preparing for war

After the bans of 1964 the nationalist leaders in detention or exile organised military training. ZAPU sent recruits to the Soviet Union, Egypt, Algeria and other countries. ZANU sent them to China, and later to Tanzania. Both parties used bases in Zambia and Tanzania, whose governments supported them, and received money and weapons from African states and from the communist powers. Between 1963 and 1966 there were scattered acts of sabotage inside the country, such as petrol bombs, and a few attacks on farms, but these were small and did not threaten the state. The Rhodesian police and army arrested the groups quickly.

## The Battle of Chinhoyi, 28 April 1966

A group of seven ZANLA fighters, trained abroad and equipped with modern weapons, entered the country from Zambia. Rhodesian police and soldiers tracked them to the Chinhoyi area and, after a fight on 28 April, killed all seven. The fighters had hoped to carry out attacks and to begin a wider rising. The military result favoured Rhodesia, but the battle became a symbol. It showed that the nationalists were willing to fight and its date is commemorated as the beginning of the Second Chimurenga. The Rhodesian government treated it as a criminal and terrorist incident, which reflected its view of the struggle.

## The Wankie and Sipolilo campaigns, 1967-68

In 1967 and 1968 ZIPRA joined with the military wing of the South African African National Congress, Umkhonto we Sizwe, in a plan to cross the Zambezi and open a way to South Africa. They were organised in large groups that included some experienced fighters.

- **Wankie (August-September 1967).** About eighty fighters crossed the Zambezi near Victoria Falls and moved into the Wankie game-reserve area. Rhodesian security forces, helped by South African police, hunted them down for several weeks. Many guerrillas were killed or captured, and some escaped across the border into Botswana, where they were arrested.
- **Sipolilo (March-April 1968).** Another group entered the north, in the Sipolilo (Guruve) area, and fought the security forces. Again most were killed or captured.

The fighters showed courage, but they were operating in large columns, in unfamiliar country, with little contact with the local people, and they could not hold ground. The Rhodesian forces used helicopters and trackers.

## Rhodesian counter-measures

- **Strong security forces.** The army, police, air force and Special Branch were well trained and equipped, and Rhodesia could call up reservists.
- **South African help.** South African police and equipment supported Rhodesia from 1967.
- **Laws.** The Law and Order (Maintenance) Act and emergency regulations allowed detention without trial and severe penalties, including the death penalty, for acts of sabotage and possession of arms.
- **Control of the countryside.** The government used chiefs, headmen and informers, and restricted movement in border areas.
- **Support from neighbours.** Portuguese Mozambique and South Africa formed a buffer to the east and south.

:::example{title="Explaining why Wankie failed"}
1. The guerrillas travelled in large groups, which were easy to detect from the air.
2. They had little contact with local people, who could have hidden and fed them.
3. The terrain was unfamiliar and water was scarce.
4. Rhodesian forces had helicopters, trackers and South African police to help them.
5. The joint ZIPRA-ANC force had not been prepared for sustained guerrilla warfare, so it was pushed towards Botswana and defeated.
:::

## Why did the first phase not succeed?

1. **Lack of political preparation.** The fighters expected the people to rise, but little time had been spent explaining the aims and winning support in the countryside.
2. **Military weaknesses.** Large groups with conventional tactics were no match for modern forces with air power; communications and supplies were poor.
3. **Rhodesian strength.** Well-trained forces, South African police and good intelligence from informers and chiefs.
4. **Geography.** The Zambezi had to be crossed, and the northern border gave only a long, exposed route.
5. **Division.** ZAPU and ZANU fought separately and, at times, against each other, so they could not coordinate.
6. **Colonial neighbours.** Portugal and South Africa allowed Rhodesia to move troops and to keep lines open.

## Lessons for a new strategy

ZANLA, in particular, drew conclusions. Under Herbert Chitepo and Josiah Tongogara it decided that the war could only succeed if the fighters first won the support of the rural people, so that they could hide among them. Cooperation with FRELIMO in Tete province, Mozambique, from about 1970 opened a route into the north-east of Rhodesia. This led to the new phase of the war from December 1972, described in the next lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Chinhoyi was a victory"}
The battle was a military defeat for the seven fighters. It is remembered because it marked the start of the armed struggle.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up ZIPRA and ZANLA"}
ZIPRA was ZAPU's army and ZANLA was ZANU's. Wankie and Sipolilo were mainly ZIPRA operations with the ANC of South Africa.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only military reasons for failure"}
Political preparation, support from the people and divisions between the parties were also important.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After the bans of 1964, ZAPU and ZANU sent recruits for training abroad; ZANLA went mainly to China, ZIPRA mainly to the Soviet Union.
- Chinhoyi (28 April 1966) was a defeat for seven ZANLA fighters but is remembered as the start of the Second Chimurenga.
- The Wankie (1967) and Sipolilo (1968) campaigns by ZIPRA and the ANC were defeated.
- Rhodesian strength, South African help, lack of political preparation and the divisions between the parties explain the failure.
- ZANLA learned the need to win the rural people, leading to the new strategy of 1972.
:::
