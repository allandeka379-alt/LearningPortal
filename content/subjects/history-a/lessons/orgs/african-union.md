## Introduction

On 25 May 1963, in Addis Ababa, the leaders of thirty-two independent African states signed the Charter of the Organisation of African Unity (OAU). The date is still celebrated as Africa Day. The founders agreed on the need for unity against colonialism and for the defence of the new states, but disagreed on how far unity should go: some, led by Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, wanted a union government for the whole continent, while others, such as Nigeria and Ethiopia, preferred a loose association of sovereign states. The compromise was the OAU. In 2002 it was replaced by the African Union (AU), which aimed to be more active and more closely integrated.

This lesson explains the origins and work of the OAU, its weaknesses, the reasons for the change to the AU, the AU's structure and aims, and an assessment of both organisations. It follows the lessons on the United Nations and the partition of Africa, and links with the liberation struggle in Zimbabwe, which the OAU supported.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pan-Africanism"}
The belief that people of African descent share a common history and interest and should work together for liberation and unity. It inspired the OAU and the AU.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-interference"}
The principle that states do not intervene in each other's internal affairs. It was a central principle of the OAU, and the AU Constitutive Act qualifies it by allowing intervention in grave circumstances.
:::

:::definition{term="Regional integration"}
The process by which neighbouring states reduce barriers to trade, movement and co-operation, moving from free trade areas to customs unions, common markets and possibly economic union.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 25 May 1963: the OAU Charter is signed in Addis Ababa by 32 states; the Secretariat is based in Addis Ababa.
- 1963: the OAU Liberation Committee, based in Dar es Salaam.
- 1964: the Cairo Declaration, accepting colonial borders as they stood at independence.
- 1981: the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights; 1999: the Sirte Declaration, calling for a union.
- 2000: the Constitutive Act of the African Union is adopted at Lomé; 9 July 2002: the AU is launched in Durban.
- 2015: Agenda 2063 adopted; 2018: the African Continental Free Trade Area agreement signed, in force 2019.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## The OAU, 1963-2002

**Aims.** The Charter's aims were to promote the unity and solidarity of African states; to co-ordinate and intensify co-operation for development; to defend their sovereignty and territorial integrity; to eradicate all forms of colonialism from Africa; and to promote international co-operation, with due regard to the UN Charter and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Its principles included the sovereign equality of members, non-interference in internal affairs, respect for existing borders, peaceful settlement of disputes, and non-alignment.

**Organs.** The supreme organ was the Assembly of Heads of State and Government, which met once a year; the Council of Ministers prepared its work; the General Secretariat in Addis Ababa was led by a Secretary-General (the first was Diallo Telli of Guinea); and a Commission of Mediation, Conciliation and Arbitration was provided for but was hardly used.

**The Liberation Committee.** The Committee for the Liberation of Africa, based in Dar es Salaam, collected contributions from members and distributed money, training and arms to liberation movements in Angola, Mozambique, Rhodesia, Namibia and South Africa. It recognised and supported movements including ZAPU and ZANU, and OAU diplomacy kept the issue before the UN and helped to isolate the white-minority regimes. Its work was helped by Frontline States such as Tanzania and Zambia, though disputes between movements, including in Zimbabwe, made co-operation difficult. In 1977 the OAU summit recognised the Patriotic Front, the alliance of ZAPU and ZANU, as the legitimate Rhodesian nationalist movement.

**Other achievements.** The OAU helped to settle the Algeria-Morocco border dispute of 1963; adopted the 1969 Convention on refugee problems in Africa and the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights (1981); and gave Africa a united voice in the UN. Its principle that colonial borders should be respected, confirmed in the Cairo Declaration of 1964, limited border wars between states.

**Weaknesses.** The OAU had no armed force and little money, since many members did not pay their dues. Its principle of non-interference meant that it could not act when governments mistreated their own people, which critics said made it a "club of presidents", and its decisions were not binding. It failed to prevent or end conflicts such as the Nigerian civil war (1967-70), the Rwandan genocide of 1994 and the civil wars in Liberia and Somalia. Some members were themselves dictatorships.

:::example{title="Explaining the replacement of the OAU by the AU"}
1. By the 1990s decolonisation was complete, with the end of apartheid in 1994, so the OAU's main purpose had been achieved.
2. The OAU's rule of non-interference had left it powerless in the face of civil wars and genocide.
3. Globalisation and poverty meant that African states needed to work together for trade and development.
4. African leaders, including those of Libya and South Africa, proposed a stronger union; the Sirte Declaration (1999) and the Constitutive Act (2000) set out new aims and the right to intervene in grave circumstances.
5. Therefore the AU was launched in 2002 with a wider mission and more organs.
:::

## The African Union

**Aims.** The Constitutive Act sets out aims such as achieving greater unity and solidarity; defending sovereignty; accelerating political and economic integration; promoting peace, security and stability; promoting democratic principles, good governance and human rights; and establishing the conditions for Africa to play its role in the global economy. Article 4(h) gives the Union the right to intervene in a member state in grave circumstances, namely war crimes, genocide and crimes against humanity, and Article 30 provides for the suspension of governments that come to power by unconstitutional means.

**Organs.** The main organs are the **Assembly** of Heads of State and Government; the **Executive Council** of foreign ministers; the **Commission** (the secretariat) in Addis Ababa; the **Peace and Security Council** (operational from 2004); the **Pan-African Parliament** (Midrand, South Africa); the **African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights** (Arusha); the Court of Justice; and the Economic, Social and Cultural Council. It works with regional economic communities such as SADC, ECOWAS and the East African Community.

**Agenda 2063.** Adopted in 2015, it is a 50-year plan for "the Africa we want", with goals such as inclusive growth, integration, good governance, peace, culture and a strong role in world affairs. The **African Continental Free Trade Area** (AfCFTA) agreement, signed in 2018 and in force from 2019, aims to create a single market.

**Achievements.** The AU has sent peace operations to Burundi, Darfur and Somalia, suspended governments after coups, mediated in elections and disputes, and promoted continental health, trade and development programmes.

**Challenges.** The AU still depends heavily on outside funding for its operations; members are reluctant to criticise one another and to give up sovereignty; implementation of decisions is slow; and there are wide differences in the size and strength of national economies. Regional bodies overlap, and conflicts in places such as the Sahel and the Horn of Africa continue.

| Feature | OAU (1963-2002) | AU (from 2002) |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim | End colonialism and defend independence | Unity, integration, development, peace and good governance |
| Intervention | Non-interference | Right to intervene in grave circumstances |
| Organs | Assembly, Council of Ministers, Secretariat | Assembly, Executive Council, Commission, Peace and Security Council, Parliament, Court |
| Funding | Member dues, often unpaid | Member dues, with heavy external support |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the AU began in 1963"}
The OAU began in 1963; the AU was launched in 2002. Make clear which organisation a point refers to.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the OAU as a failure only"}
The OAU achieved much in decolonisation and in giving Africa a voice. A balanced answer also lists its weaknesses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overlooking the change in principle"}
The key difference is that the AU allows intervention in grave circumstances, where the OAU insisted on non-interference.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The OAU was founded on 25 May 1963 by 32 states to promote unity, defend sovereignty and end colonialism; it was based on non-interference and respect for borders.
- Its Liberation Committee supported movements in southern Africa; the OAU also adopted the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights.
- Weaknesses were lack of money, force and binding decisions, and a reluctance to criticise members; it failed to prevent conflicts such as Rwanda.
- The AU was launched in 2002 with a Constitutive Act that allows intervention in grave circumstances and with new organs; Agenda 2063 and the AfCFTA aim at integration.
- The AU has achievements in peace operations and diplomacy, but still faces problems of funding and implementation.
:::
