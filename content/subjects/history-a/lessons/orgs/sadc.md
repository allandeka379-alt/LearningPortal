## Introduction

When Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980, it joined a region in which the economies of most states were tied to South Africa. Roads, railways, ports, power lines, labour and trade routes all led towards the apartheid state, which used that dependence as a weapon. Less than three weeks earlier, on 1 April 1980, leaders of nine southern African states had met at Lusaka, in Zambia, and founded the **Southern African Development Coordination Conference (SADCC)** to reduce this dependence. It was a regional answer to a political problem, and Zimbabwe, then about to become independent, took part in the founding and is counted among its nine founders. In 1992 the SADCC became the **Southern African Development Community (SADC)**, a body with the more ambitious goal of integration.

This lesson explains the origins, aims and structure of SADCC and SADC, the idea of regional integration, the progress made, the problems that remain and Zimbabwe's part in the organisation. It links to the lessons on the Frontline States and on the OAU and the AU, and it should be read with the Zimbabwe foreign policy lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regional integration"}
The process by which neighbouring countries link their economies and policies, often in stages: a free trade area, a customs union, a common market, an economic and monetary union and, finally, political union.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove tariffs on trade among themselves, while each keeps its own tariffs on imports from outside the group.
:::

:::definition{term="Customs union"}
A free trade area in which members also adopt a common external tariff on goods from outside the group.
:::

:::definition{term="Common market"}
A customs union in which labour, capital and services as well as goods can move freely between members.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 1 April 1980: the Lusaka Declaration, "Southern Africa: Towards Economic Liberation", founds SADCC with nine states: Angola, Botswana, Lesotho, Malawi, Mozambique, Swaziland (Eswatini), Tanzania, Zambia and Zimbabwe.
- 1982: the Secretariat is set up in Gaborone, Botswana.
- 1990: Namibia joins at its independence.
- 17 August 1992: the Windhoek Declaration and Treaty create SADC; 1994: South Africa joins.
- 2008: the SADC Free Trade Area begins.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
SADCC and SADC are ZIMSEC only (Paper 2). Cambridge's covered options do not examine them.
:::

## Origins of SADCC

The founders were mostly the Frontline States, joined by Zimbabwe, Malawi, Lesotho and Swaziland, which were small and heavily dependent on South Africa. Their aims, set out in the Lusaka Declaration, were to **reduce economic dependence**, in particular on South Africa; to build links to create genuine and equitable regional integration; to **mobilise resources** for national and regional policies; and to take concerted action to secure international co-operation within a framework of strategic economic liberation. SADCC did not aim at once at a common market. It focused on **projects** in sectors such as transport, energy and food, and it was decentralised: each member was given a sector to co-ordinate, for example Mozambique for transport and communications, Angola for energy and Zimbabwe for food security.

The SADCC attracted aid from donors who wanted to support development without helping South Africa. Its main achievements were improvements in transport routes (for instance the rehabilitation of the Beira and other corridors that gave land-locked states access to the sea without using South African ports), regional energy links, and research on food and crops. But apartheid South Africa's destabilisation of Mozambique, Angola and others destroyed many facilities and undermined results.

## From SADCC to SADC

Several developments in the late 1980s and early 1990s led to change. Namibia became independent in March 1990, Nelson Mandela was freed in February 1990, and the end of apartheid was in sight. A regional body based mainly on opposition to South Africa needed a new purpose. In addition, trading blocs were forming in Europe and elsewhere, and southern African states argued that closer integration would make them stronger. At Windhoek on 17 August 1992 members signed a Declaration and Treaty that created SADC, with the aim of deeper integration, and the Treaty came into force in 1993. South Africa joined in 1994 after its first democratic elections; later members included Mauritius, the Seychelles, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Madagascar, and the Comoros. SADC now has 16 member states.

:::example{title="Explaining why SADCC became SADC"}
1. SADCC was created to reduce dependence on apartheid South Africa and to coordinate projects.
2. In 1990 Namibia became independent and South Africa began to change, so the original political purpose faded.
3. Members wanted to deepen co-operation into integration, covering trade, politics and security, not only projects.
4. Globalisation and the formation of other trading blocs made a bigger regional market seem necessary.
5. Therefore in 1992 the Windhoek Treaty turned a co-ordination conference into a community with legal obligations, and South Africa could join in 1994.
:::

## Aims and structure of SADC

The Treaty sets objectives that include promoting sustainable and equitable economic growth and socio-economic development; reducing poverty; promoting common political values and democratic institutions; consolidating peace and security; and achieving complementarity between national and regional strategies. The main institutions are:

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| **Summit** of Heads of State or Government | The supreme policy-making body; meets once a year |
| **Council of Ministers** | Oversees implementation of policy; prepares for the Summit |
| **Organ on Politics, Defence and Security Co-operation** | Handles peace, security and defence matters |
| **SADC Tribunal** | A regional court at Windhoek; suspended in 2010 and restructured later |
| **Secretariat** | Executive body in Gaborone, led by the Executive Secretary |
| **National Committees** and the SADC Parliamentary Forum | Link member states and parliaments to the community |

## Integration: progress and problems

SADC set stages of integration in the **Regional Indicative Strategic Development Plan** (RISDP, 2003): a free trade area by 2008, a customs union by 2010, a common market by 2015, a monetary union and a single currency later. A **Free Trade Area** began in 2008, and most tariffs on trade among the participating members were removed by 2012. The customs union has not yet been completed, and later targets have been put back. Other achievements include protocols on trade, gender and development, shared water courses and energy; the Southern African Power Pool; the Kazungula Bridge over the Zambezi (opened in 2021); regional mediation in places such as Madagascar and Lesotho; and common programmes on health and disease.

Problems include:

- the **dominance of South Africa's economy** and wide differences in size and wealth between members;
- **overlapping membership** of other groupings such as COMESA and the East African Community, which complicates rules of origin and tariffs;
- **poor transport and energy links** and shortages of finance;
- **political differences** and reluctance to give up sovereignty, with disputes such as those over the SADC Tribunal and the intervention in the Democratic Republic of Congo in 1998;
- dependence on donor funding, and slow implementation of decisions.

## Zimbabwe's role

Zimbabwe was a founding member of SADCC and hosted the SADCC food security sector in Harare. It supported the Frontline States, guarded the Beira Corridor in Mozambique, and hosted SADC summits and chaired the SADC Organ on Politics, Defence and Security in the 1990s. As the region's second most industrialised economy for many years, it traded with its neighbours, although regional trade rules and the economic difficulties of Zimbabwe after 1997 affected its role. Within SADC Zimbabwe has also been the subject of mediation efforts, for example in 2007-2008, over which views differ.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing SADCC and SADC"}
SADCC (1980) was a conference to co-ordinate development projects and reduce dependence on South Africa. SADC (1992) is a community with a treaty, aiming at integration, and South Africa joined it in 1994.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the stages of integration"}
A free trade area removes internal tariffs; a customs union adds a common external tariff; a common market adds free movement of labour and capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that SADC achieved all its targets"}
A free trade area was launched in 2008, but the customs union and later stages have been delayed. Give both progress and problems.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SADCC was founded at Lusaka on 1 April 1980 by nine states to reduce dependence on apartheid South Africa and to co-ordinate projects in transport, energy and food; Zimbabwe was a founding member.
- SADC replaced it by the Windhoek Treaty of 17 August 1992, with the aim of deeper integration; South Africa joined in 1994.
- Its main institutions are the Summit, Council of Ministers, Organ on Politics, Defence and Security, the Tribunal and the Secretariat in Gaborone.
- A Free Trade Area began in 2008; the customs union and later stages are delayed by differences in size, overlapping memberships, poor infrastructure and political disagreements.
- Zimbabwe has been active in the organisation, especially in food security and regional security.
:::
