## Introduction

On 24 October 1945, when enough states had approved its Charter, the United Nations came into existence. Its founders had lived through two world wars and the failure of the League of Nations to prevent the second, and they wanted an organisation with more power to keep peace and with a wider purpose of improving lives. Today the UN has 193 member states, and its agencies deal with health, food, children, refugees, education and development, while the Security Council deals with peace and security.

For a Zimbabwean student the UN matters in several ways. It imposed sanctions on Rhodesia after UDI, it helped to bring about the independence of Namibia, which borders Zimbabwe's region, and Zimbabwe has been a member since 1980, sitting on the Security Council and taking part in peacekeeping. This lesson explains how the UN began, how it is organised, what it does and how it is criticised. You should know why the League failed, which the next lesson explains.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Collective security"}
The principle that peace is best kept when states act together against an aggressor. The UN Charter makes the Security Council responsible for this.
:::

:::definition{term="Veto"}
The power of each of the five permanent members of the Security Council (the United States, Russia as successor to the Soviet Union, China, France and the United Kingdom) to block any substantive decision by voting against it.
:::

:::definition{term="Peacekeeping"}
The deployment of international military, police and civilian personnel, usually with the consent of the parties, to monitor a ceasefire, protect civilians and help a peace process.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialised agency"}
An international organisation linked to the UN that deals with a particular field, such as the World Health Organization (WHO), UNESCO or the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates and names to learn"}
- 26 June 1945: the Charter is signed at San Francisco; 24 October 1945: it comes into force (United Nations Day).
- 1946: the first General Assembly and the first Secretary-General, Trygve Lie of Norway.
- 1960-64: the UN operation in the Congo (ONUC); Dag Hammarskjöld dies in an air crash near Ndola in September 1961.
- 1966 and 1968: sanctions on Rhodesia (Resolutions 232 and 253); lifted in December 1979.
- 1980: Zimbabwe joins the UN; 1989-90: UN supervision of Namibia's independence (UNTAG).
:::

## Origins

During the Second World War the Allies planned a new organisation. The Atlantic Charter (1941), the declarations of the United Nations in 1942, conferences at Dumbarton Oaks (1944) and Yalta (February 1945) shaped it, and the San Francisco conference of April to June 1945 drafted the Charter, which was signed by 50 states on 26 June (Poland signed later, making 51 original members). The founders learned from the League: the United States and the Soviet Union were included, the Security Council was given power to act, and the organisation was given an economic and social mission, on the belief that poverty and injustice cause war.

## Aims and principles

Article 1 of the Charter sets four purposes: to maintain **international peace and security**; to develop **friendly relations** among nations based on equal rights and self-determination of peoples; to achieve **international co-operation** in solving economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and in promoting **human rights**; and to be a centre for harmonising the actions of nations. Its principles include the **sovereign equality** of members, settlement of disputes by peaceful means, refraining from the threat or use of force, and non-interference in matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction, with an exception for enforcement action under Chapter VII.

## The organs

| Organ | Membership and role |
|---|---|
| **General Assembly** | All members, one vote each; debates any matter; recommends; approves the budget; elects non-permanent members of the Council |
| **Security Council** | 15 members: five permanent with a veto and ten elected for two years; primary responsibility for peace and security; its decisions bind members; nine votes are needed, including no veto by a permanent member |
| **International Court of Justice** | At The Hague; 15 judges; settles disputes between states that accept its jurisdiction and gives advisory opinions |
| **Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)** | 54 members; co-ordinates economic and social work and the specialised agencies |
| **Secretariat** | Led by the Secretary-General; the administration of the UN |
| **Trusteeship Council** | Supervised trust territories; its work was suspended in 1994 after the last trust territory became independent |

Specialised agencies and programmes include WHO, UNESCO, FAO, the International Labour Organization, UNICEF, the UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), the World Food Programme and the UN Development Programme.

## The work of the UN

**Keeping peace.** The UN has mediated disputes, imposed sanctions, authorised the use of force and deployed peacekeepers. Its first large African operation was in the Congo (1960-64), where it helped to end the secession of Katanga but lost its Secretary-General, Dag Hammarskjöld, who was killed in a plane crash in September 1961 near Ndola in what was then Northern Rhodesia, on his way to meet a Katangese leader. Later operations included Namibia (1989-90), Mozambique (1992-94) and Somalia (1992-95), and the weak mission in Rwanda in 1994, which failed to stop a genocide.

**Decolonisation and apartheid.** The UN General Assembly adopted the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples (1960) and set up a Special Committee on decolonisation. It condemned apartheid and imposed an arms embargo on South Africa (1977).

**Development and humanitarian work.** UNICEF, WHO, FAO and the World Food Programme provide vaccines, food and emergency relief, and the UNHCR protects refugees. The UN set the Millennium Development Goals in 2000 and the Sustainable Development Goals in 2015.

## The UN and Zimbabwe

After UDI in November 1965 the Security Council declared the situation a threat to peace. Resolution 232 (December 1966) imposed the first mandatory selective sanctions in UN history, and Resolution 253 (May 1968) extended them to almost all trade. Rhodesia was supported by South Africa and Portugal, and sanctions were widely evaded, but they raised the cost of UDI. They were lifted on 21 December 1979, as part of the Lancaster House settlement. Zimbabwe joined the UN in 1980. Since independence it has served on the Security Council, contributed peacekeepers to UN missions, and received help from UN agencies for refugees, children's health, food relief and development.

:::example{title="Assessing the effect of the UN sanctions on Rhodesia"}
1. Sanctions made trade, finance and arms harder, forcing Rhodesia to rely on South Africa and Portugal and to find ways around the rules.
2. The economy adapted by import substitution and by sanction-busting trade, so it did not collapse.
3. But sanctions cut export earnings, raised costs and, with the war, left the country short of foreign currency.
4. They also gave international legitimacy to the nationalists' cause and kept the issue before the world.
5. Judgement: sanctions did not bring about majority rule alone, but they were one of several pressures that led to the settlement of 1979.
:::

## Criticisms

- **The veto** can paralyse the Security Council when permanent members disagree, as in the Cold War, and the permanent membership reflects the power of 1945. The African Union has called for two permanent seats for Africa (the Ezulwini Consensus, 2005).
- **Limited power to enforce**: the UN has no army of its own and depends on members to supply troops and money.
- **Failures** such as Rwanda in 1994.
- **Unequal treatment**: critics say powerful states can ignore the UN, while weaker states are held to account.
- **Funding and bureaucracy**.

Supporters answer that the UN has prevented a third world war, helped decolonisation, saved lives through its agencies and provided a place for all states to talk.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Assembly and the Council"}
The General Assembly includes all members and makes recommendations; the Security Council has 15 members and its decisions are binding. Only the Council has a veto.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the UN has its own army"}
Peacekeepers are provided by member states; the UN has no standing army.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the link with the League"}
Explain what the UN did differently from the League when asked about origins.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The UN was founded in 1945 to keep peace and promote co-operation, learning from the failure of the League.
- The Charter's aims include peace and security, friendly relations, co-operation and human rights; the main organs are the General Assembly, Security Council (with the veto), ICJ, ECOSOC and the Secretariat.
- Specialised agencies work on health, food, children, refugees and development; peacekeeping has included the Congo, Namibia and Mozambique.
- The UN imposed sanctions on Rhodesia from 1966; Zimbabwe joined in 1980.
- Criticisms include the veto, lack of enforcement power and failures such as Rwanda; supporters point to decolonisation, humanitarian work and the avoidance of world war.
:::
