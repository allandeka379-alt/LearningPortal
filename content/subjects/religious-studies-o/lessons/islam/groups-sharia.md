## Introduction

At a school fair in Gweru, Tafadzwa buys a snack and asks whether his Muslim friend Yusuf can eat it. Yusuf checks the label and says he will have the vegetable samosa, but not the one with pork. That small choice shows that for Muslims, faith shapes everyday decisions. The framework behind these choices is **Sharia**.

In this lesson you learn what Sharia is and where it comes from, how Muslims decide what is halal and haram, the moral teachings of Islam, the true meaning of jihad, and the main groups within Islam. You should already know the Qur'an and Hadith, which are the sources of Sharia, and the beginning of the Sunni and Shi'a division.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sharia"}
Literally "the path": the body of guidance, drawn from the Qur'an and the Sunnah, that tells Muslims how to live. It covers worship, family life, business and morality.
:::

:::definition{term="Halal and haram"}
*Halal* means permitted and *haram* means forbidden under Islamic teaching.
:::

:::definition{term="Jihad"}
Literally "striving" or "struggle". Muslims use it for the effort to do good, to resist wrong in oneself and, under strict conditions, to defend the community.
:::

:::keyfact
**Sources of Sharia:** (1) Qur'an, (2) Sunnah, (3) ijma (agreement of scholars), (4) qiyas (reasoning by analogy). **Sunni schools of law:** Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali. **Riba:** interest, forbidden (Qur'an 2:275).
:::

## Sharia and its sources

Sharia is not one fixed book of laws but a way of life that scholars have studied for centuries. Its sources, in order of authority for most Muslims, are:

| Source | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Qur'an | The word of God | Commands to pray, give Zakat and be honest |
| Sunnah | The example of the Prophet, in the Hadith | The detail of how to pray |
| Ijma | Agreement among qualified scholars | A ruling that scholars accepted for a new situation |
| Qiyas | Reasoning by analogy from an earlier case | Applying a ruling on one drink to a similar new substance |

Because the sources must be interpreted, scholars have formed **schools of law** (*madhahib*). The four main Sunni schools are the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali, and the Shi'a have their own, the Ja'fari. They agree on the main teachings and differ on details. Sharia is applied by individuals and families in daily life; in a few countries parts of it are state law. In Zimbabwe, Muslims follow the law of the country, and certain family matters may be dealt with according to religious or customary principles where the law allows.

## Halal and haram

The Qur'an (5:3) lists the foods forbidden to Muslims: pork, meat from animals that died of themselves, blood, and meat from animals not slaughtered in the name of God. Muslims also avoid alcohol and other intoxicants, and gambling. Halal meat comes from an animal slaughtered quickly, with God's name pronounced, and the blood drained.

Islam also forbids **riba**, usually translated as interest (Qur'an 2:275 says that God has permitted trade and forbidden riba). Muslim banks therefore use methods such as profit-sharing and leasing. Islamic teaching on **dress** asks for modesty, as explained in the previous lesson. In all these rules the reason Muslims give is obedience to God and care for health, justice and self-respect.

## Moral teachings

Islam teaches **justice**, **honesty**, **charity**, **family loyalty** and **respect for parents**. Qur'an 17:23-24 asks believers to treat parents with kindness and never to speak harshly to them, especially in old age. Muslims are expected to keep promises, to be fair in trade, to care for orphans and neighbours, and to speak the truth even when it is hard. The rules on family, such as consent in marriage and care for children, are part of this moral vision. Muslims also teach that cruelty to animals is wrong.

## The meaning of jihad

In the Qur'an and in Muslim usage, **jihad** means striving in the way of God. Many Muslim scholars describe two kinds:

- The **greater jihad** is the inner struggle against selfishness, pride and temptation, and the daily effort to live a good life.
- The **lesser jihad** is the physical defence of the community against attack. Classical Muslim teaching sets strict conditions: it must be defensive or to remove oppression, must be declared by a legitimate authority, must not harm civilians, women, children or the elderly, and must not destroy crops or places of worship.

Mainstream Muslim scholars and organisations around the world reject **terrorism** as contrary to Islam. The Qur'an (5:32) teaches that killing an innocent person is like killing all of humankind, and Qur'an 2:256 says that there is no compulsion in religion. Those who commit violence against civilians in the name of Islam are condemned by most Muslims. A student must not describe jihad as "holy war" without explaining these meanings.

## Groups within Islam

### Sunni Islam

The great majority of Muslims are **Sunni**. They accept the leadership of the first four caliphs, follow one of the four schools of law, and regard the Qur'an and the Sunnah as the main sources of guidance. For Sunnis the imam of a mosque is a prayer leader and teacher.

### Shi'a Islam

The **Shi'a** believe that leadership of the community rightly belonged to Ali and his descendants, called **imams**, who are honoured as holy leaders of the family of the Prophet. The largest Shi'a group, the "Twelvers", believes in twelve imams, the last of whom is hidden and will return. Shi'a Muslims share the Five Pillars and the Qur'an, but add special devotion to the family of the Prophet, mourn Husayn on Ashura, and have their own collections of Hadith and their own school of law.

### Sufism

**Sufism** is the mystical tradition in Islam. Sufis seek to draw close to God through remembrance (*dhikr*), prayer and a simple life, often following a spiritual guide in a brotherhood called a *tariqa*. Sufis may be Sunni or Shi'a. Some Muslims value Sufi practices greatly; others criticise some of them. Scholars and communities should be described fairly, without ranking one against the other.

| | Sunni | Shi'a | Sufi |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leadership after the Prophet | The elected caliphs | Ali and the imams | A mystical path found among Sunni and Shi'a |
| Shared beliefs | Qur'an, Five Pillars, one God | Qur'an, Five Pillars, one God | Same, with an emphasis on inner devotion |
| Special feature | Four schools of law | Twelve imams (in the main group) | Dhikr and spiritual guides |

:::example{title="Applying halal and haram"}
Thandeka is organising a school picnic with Muslim and non-Muslim classmates. She chooses food without pork or alcohol and asks the shop about the source of the meat. An examination answer on halal food should give a rule (no pork, no alcohol), a reason (the Qur'an's command) and a practical effect (food choices, shopping).
:::

:::example{title="Greater and lesser jihad"}
Nyasha, a Muslim, says that her greater jihad this year is to control her temper at school. A news report mentions the word jihad in connection with a violent group, and her classmate Rudo is confused. Nyasha explains that most Muslims use the word for personal struggle and that violence against civilians is condemned by Muslim scholars. This shows the importance of explaining both meanings.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Translating jihad only as holy war"}
Jihad means striving. The greater jihad is an inner struggle; the lesser jihad is defence under strict rules; terrorism is rejected.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Sunni, Shi'a and Sufi as three religions"}
They are branches and traditions within one religion, sharing the Qur'an and the Five Pillars.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Sharia is a single book of laws"}
Sharia is the way of life drawn from the Qur'an, the Sunnah and scholarly reasoning. Schools of law differ on details.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe or outline"}
For *outline the sources of Sharia*, name the four sources and give one detail about each. For *describe the differences between Sunni and Shi'a Islam*, state the origin (leadership), the Shi'a imams, and what they share.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sharia is the way of life drawn from the Qur'an, the Sunnah, ijma and qiyas, interpreted in schools of law.
- Halal and haram guide food (no pork or alcohol), finance (no riba) and conduct.
- Islam teaches justice, honesty, charity and respect for parents.
- Jihad means striving; the greater jihad is the inner struggle, and the lesser jihad is defence under strict conditions.
- Mainstream scholars reject terrorism and affirm that there is no compulsion in religion.
- Sunni, Shi'a and Sufi are traditions within one religion, which share the Qur'an and the Five Pillars.
:::
