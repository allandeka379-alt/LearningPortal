## Introduction

During Ramadan, a Muslim family in Bulawayo wakes before dawn for a light meal, and the family goes through the day without food or drink. At sunset they break the fast together with dates and water, and often invite a neighbour or a poor family to join them. At the end of the month the family gives food or money to the poor before the festival of Eid. A relative has saved for years to travel to Makkah for the Hajj.

In this lesson you learn the last three Pillars: **Zakat** (giving), **Sawm** (fasting) and **Hajj** (pilgrimage). They are acts of worship that shape how Muslims use their wealth, their bodies and their time. You should already know the Shahadah and Salah, and that the Eid festivals are taught in a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Zakat"}
Obligatory giving by Muslims whose wealth is above a minimum level. It is usually 2.5 per cent of qualifying savings held for a lunar year. The word means purification and growth.
:::

:::definition{term="Sadaqah"}
Voluntary charity given at any time, in money, kindness or help.
:::

:::definition{term="Sawm"}
Fasting from dawn to sunset in the month of Ramadan.
:::

:::definition{term="Hajj"}
The pilgrimage to Makkah in the month of Dhul-Hijjah, to be made once in a lifetime by those who are physically and financially able.
:::

:::keyfact
**Zakat:** usually 2.5% of qualifying savings held for a lunar year; recipients listed in Qur'an 9:60. **Sawm:** Ramadan, the ninth month; Qur'an 2:183. **Hajj:** Dhul-Hijjah, the twelfth month; Qur'an 3:97. **Laylat al-Qadr:** the Night of Power, in the last ten nights of Ramadan.
:::

## Zakat: obligatory giving

Muslims believe that all wealth belongs to God and that those who have more than they need must share. Zakat is therefore a duty and a way of purifying one's wealth, not a favour. It is normally 2.5 per cent of savings and similar assets held above a minimum amount for one lunar year. Different Muslim scholars give different rules for farm produce, livestock and business goods.

Qur'an 9:60 lists those who may receive Zakat: the poor, the needy, those who collect it, those whose hearts are to be won, those freeing people from bondage, those in debt, those serving God's cause and travellers in need. Zakat is not given to those who are wealthy or, in most scholars' view, to one's own parents or children, who the giver must already support.

**Sadaqah** is voluntary and may be given at any time and in any form, including a smile, help with a task or money. At the end of Ramadan Muslims also give **zakat al-fitr**, a small gift of food or its value, so that the poor can celebrate Eid.

The purposes of Zakat are to help the poor, to reduce greed, to build community and to teach that wealth is a trust from God.

## Sawm: fasting in Ramadan

**Ramadan** is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. The Qur'an (2:183) says that fasting was prescribed for Muslims as it was for earlier communities, so that they may grow in God-consciousness. During Ramadan adult Muslims who are able abstain from eating, drinking and marital relations from dawn to sunset. They get up before dawn for a meal called **suhoor**, and they break the fast at sunset with **iftar**, often beginning with dates and water. Many gather at the mosque for extra night prayers called *taraweeh*, and many read the Qur'an through during the month.

**Who is exempt?** Children before puberty, the sick, travellers, women who are menstruating, pregnant or nursing, and the elderly who cannot fast. Many of them make up the missed days later, and those who cannot fast at all may feed a poor person instead.

**Laylat al-Qadr** is the Night of Power, when Muslims believe the first revelation came to the Prophet. It falls in the last ten nights of Ramadan, and many Muslims spend those nights in prayer. Ramadan ends with the festival of **Eid al-Fitr**.

The purposes of fasting are to build self-control and God-consciousness, to feel sympathy for the hungry, to strengthen family and community life and to give thanks to God.

## Hajj: the pilgrimage to Makkah

Every adult Muslim who is physically and financially able must make the Hajj once in a lifetime (Qur'an 3:97). It takes place on fixed days of the month of **Dhul-Hijjah**. The main rites follow the story of Ibrahim, his wife Hajar and their son Ismail.

| Stage | What pilgrims do | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ihram | Enter a state of purity; men wear two white unsewn cloths and women dress simply; avoid quarrels and hunting | Equality: rich and poor look the same |
| Tawaf | Walk seven times anticlockwise around the Ka'bah | Worship centred on the one God |
| Sa'i | Walk or hurry seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa | Remembers Hajar's search for water for her son |
| Arafat | Stand in prayer on the plain of Arafat from noon to sunset | The high point of Hajj; reminds of the Day of Judgement |
| Muzdalifah and Mina | Gather pebbles; throw them at three pillars at Mina | Rejecting evil, in memory of Ibrahim |
| Sacrifice | An animal is sacrificed and its meat shared with the poor | Ibrahim's willingness to obey God; celebrated as Eid al-Adha |
| Final rites | Cut or shave the hair, farewell tawaf | The end of the state of ihram |

Pilgrims often drink water from the well of Zamzam. The meaning of the Hajj is obedience to God, equality, unity (Muslims from every nation stand together), and repentance, since a pilgrim aims to return forgiven. Someone who has completed it is called a *hajji*.

**Umrah** is a lesser pilgrimage that may be made at any time of the year. It includes ihram, tawaf and sa'i, but it does not replace the Hajj.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that every Muslim must go on Hajj every year"}
Hajj is required once in a lifetime for those who are able. Ill, poor or unable people are not required to go.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Zakat and sadaqah"}
Zakat is obligatory at a set rate; sadaqah is voluntary and can be given at any time and in any form.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Muslims fast from food only"}
Fasting also includes drinks and marital relations, and Muslims are encouraged to avoid anger, gossip and lies during Ramadan.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zakat is obligatory giving, usually 2.5 per cent of qualifying savings held for a lunar year; sadaqah is voluntary.
- Zakat recipients include the poor, the needy, those in debt and travellers (Qur'an 9:60).
- Sawm is fasting from dawn to sunset in Ramadan; some people are exempt; Laylat al-Qadr falls in the last ten nights.
- Hajj is the pilgrimage to Makkah in Dhul-Hijjah, once in a lifetime for those who are able.
- The Hajj rites include ihram, tawaf, sa'i, standing at Arafat, the stoning at Mina and the sacrifice.
- Each Pillar teaches obedience to God, discipline, generosity and equality.
:::
