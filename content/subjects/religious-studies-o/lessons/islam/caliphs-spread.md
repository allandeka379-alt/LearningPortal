## Introduction

Stand on a busy street in Harare or Bulawayo on a Friday around midday and you will see Muslim men and women heading for the mosque. Their community is part of a worldwide faith that began in Arabia and reached southern Africa along trade routes. In this lesson you learn what happened when the Prophet Muhammad died in 632 CE, who led the Muslim community afterwards, how a division between Sunni and Shi'a Muslims began, and how Islam came to Africa and to Zimbabwe.

You should already know the life of the Prophet and that he is the last prophet in Islam. The beliefs and groups within Islam are taught in later lessons; here the focus is on history, told fairly and without ranking any group.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Caliph"}
From the Arabic *khalifah*, "successor": the leader of the Muslim community after the Prophet's death.
:::

:::definition{term="Rightly guided caliphs (Rashidun)"}
The first four caliphs: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali. Sunni Muslims especially honour them as models of leadership.
:::

:::definition{term="Sunni and Shi'a"}
The two main branches of Islam. They began as a disagreement about who should lead the community after the Prophet.
:::

:::keyfact
**632 CE:** the Prophet dies; Abu Bakr becomes the first caliph. **The four caliphs:** Abu Bakr (632-634), Umar (634-644), Uthman (644-656), Ali (656-661).
:::

## The four rightly guided caliphs

When the Prophet died, the community had to decide who would lead it. The senior Companions chose his close friend **Abu Bakr**, who is often remembered for holding the community together after the Prophet's death. During his time as caliph the Qur'an was first gathered into a single written collection, so that nothing would be lost.

**Umar** (634-644 CE) led during a period of rapid growth. The Muslim community took on large territories in Syria, Egypt and Persia, and he organised the government of the new lands. Tradition also credits him with fixing the start of the Islamic calendar at the Hijrah. He was killed by a man who held a personal grievance.

**Uthman** (644-656 CE) ordered a standard written copy of the Qur'an to be made, with copies sent to the main cities, so that Muslims everywhere recited from the same text. He was killed during a rebellion against his rule.

**Ali** (656-661 CE) was the Prophet's cousin and the husband of the Prophet's daughter, Fatimah. His time as caliph was marked by civil conflict between Muslim groups, and he was killed in 661 CE. After him power passed to the Umayyad family, who ruled from Damascus.

|---|---|---|

| Umar | 634-644 | Expansion; organised government; the Islamic calendar |
| Uthman | 644-656 | A standard written text of the Qur'an sent to the main cities |
| Ali | 656-661 | Cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet; a time of civil conflict |

## How the Sunni and Shi'a division began

Most Muslims accepted that the community should choose its leader from among the senior Companions; they are called **Sunni**, from *ahl al-sunnah*, "people of the Prophet's way". Others held that the Prophet had pointed to his cousin Ali and that leadership belonged to Ali and to his descendants through Fatimah; these are the **Shi'a**, from *shi'at Ali*, "the party of Ali". The disagreement was political at first and developed into differences in religious practice. A painful event for Shi'a Muslims is the death of Ali's son Husayn at Karbala in 680 CE, remembered on Ashura. Both groups share the Qur'an, the Five Pillars and the belief in one God; they are described in a later lesson.

## The spread of Islam

Islam spread in different ways in different places. In the century after the Prophet, Muslim armies and governors extended Muslim rule over the Middle East and North Africa. Over the following centuries many people accepted Islam freely, through trade, marriage, learning and the example of Muslim merchants and teachers, while others kept their religion and lived within Muslim states.

**North and West Africa.** Muslim rule reached Egypt in the 640s CE and later spread along the north coast. Traders crossing the Sahara carried Islam south, and rulers and merchants in West African states accepted it. The pilgrimage of the ruler of Mali, Mansa Musa, to Makkah in 1324 CE is famous in the history of the region.

**The East African coast.** Muslim traders from Arabia and Persia sailed on the monsoon winds to the Swahili coast, from Somalia to Mozambique. Coastal towns such as Kilwa grew rich, and Swahili culture blended African and Arab influences. The ports connected the interior to the Indian Ocean.

**Sofala and the plateau trade.** Sofala, on the coast of what is now Mozambique, was a port from which gold and ivory from the Zimbabwe plateau were shipped. Arab-Swahili traders took part in this trade, and a few reached the interior. Islam therefore reached the edge of the plateau early, but this link did not turn into large Muslim communities inland at that time.

## Islam in Zimbabwe today

Muslims in Zimbabwe form a minority community, with mosques in Harare, Bulawayo, Mutare and other towns. Many belong to families that came in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries from Malawi, Mozambique, South Africa and South Asia; others are Zimbabweans who have embraced Islam in recent generations. Muslim organisations run schools (madrasahs), charities and clinics, and the community celebrates the Eid festivals openly. The Constitution of Zimbabwe, section 60, protects freedom of conscience, including the right to practise one's religion.

:::example{title="Reading a map of spread"}
Tafadzwa is given a map showing Muslim traders sailing from the Swahili coast to Sofala. A good examination answer does not just say "Islam came by trade". It names a route (the monsoon sea route to Sofala), says what was traded (gold and ivory from the plateau) and explains the effect (contact with Muslim merchants, though large inland communities came later).
:::

:::example{title="A shared past, two paths"}

Aisha's family is Sunni and honours all four caliphs; her friend's cousin is Shi'a and honours Ali and his descendants with special devotion. ## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Islam spread only by war"}
Armies extended Muslim rule in some regions, but in much of Africa and Asia Islam spread mainly through trade, teaching and example. Do not write that everyone was forced to convert.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the caliphs"}
Abu Bakr was first, Umar second, Uthman third and Ali fourth. Remember the Qur'an gathered (Abu Bakr), the standard text (Uthman).
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Sunni and Shi'a as separate religions"}
They are two branches of one religion who share the Qur'an and Five Pillars. Explain the origin of the difference: leadership after the Prophet.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- After the Prophet died in 632 CE, the community was led by the four rightly guided caliphs: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali.
- The Qur'an was gathered under Abu Bakr and a standard text was sent to the cities under Uthman.
- Sunni and Shi'a began as a disagreement about leadership after the Prophet; both share the Qur'an and the Five Pillars.
- Islam spread to Africa through trade routes across the Sahara and the Indian Ocean as well as by Muslim rule in North Africa.
- Arab-Swahili traders took part in the plateau trade through Sofala.
- Today Muslims in Zimbabwe are a minority community with mosques in many towns and legal protection for their religion.
:::
