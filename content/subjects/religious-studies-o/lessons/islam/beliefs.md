## Introduction

Sipho's classmate Ibrahim leaves the Form 2 classroom for midday prayer, and the next day he explains something simple: "Islam means giving yourself to God." Many other things that Muslims do, from praying five times a day to giving to the poor, follow from a short list of beliefs. In this lesson you learn that list and what each belief means, as Muslims themselves explain it.

You should already know that Muslims follow the Prophet Muhammad, who received the Qur'an, and that Islam is practised by Sunni and Shi'a Muslims. This lesson presents the beliefs held by Muslims in general; the extra Shi'a beliefs belong to a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Islam"}
An Arabic word meaning submission or surrender to the will of God, and also peace.
:::

:::definition{term="Muslim"}
A person who follows Islam: one who submits to God.
:::

:::definition{term="Tawhid"}
The oneness of God: the central belief of Islam.
:::

:::definition{term="Shirk"}
Associating partners with God. Muslims consider it the greatest wrong, because it denies the oneness of God.
:::

:::keyfact
**Six articles of faith:** belief in (1) Allah, (2) the angels, (3) the revealed books, (4) the prophets, (5) the Day of Judgement and the afterlife, (6) divine decree (al-Qadr). **Allah** is the Arabic word for God; Arab Christians also use it.
:::

## One God: Tawhid

Muslims believe that there is only one God, **Allah**, the creator and sustainer of everything. God is all-knowing, all-powerful, merciful and just, and has no partners, no family and no image. Surah 112 (Al-Ikhlas), a very short chapter that Muslims recite often, says in summary that God is one, that all depend on God, and that God neither has children nor was born. Because of this, Muslims never make images of God, and the greatest wrong is **shirk**, which means treating anything, whether a person, an object or an idea, as equal to God. The Shahadah, the declaration of faith, begins with this belief.

Muslims use ninety-nine beautiful names to describe God, such as *ar-Rahman* (the Merciful), *al-Adl* (the Just) and *al-Khaliq* (the Creator). Many Muslims count prayers on beads using these names.

## The angels

Muslims believe that angels are beings created by God from light, who obey God completely. They cannot disobey. **Jibril** (Gabriel) brought the revelations to the prophets, including the Qur'an to Muhammad; **Mika'il** (Michael) is associated with rain and sustenance; **Israfil** will blow the trumpet at the end of time. Muslims also believe in angels who record each person's deeds. The belief reminds Muslims that their actions are known to God.

## The revealed books

Muslims believe that God sent guidance to humanity in books. They name four: the **Tawrat** (the revelation to Musa, Moses), the **Zabur** (to Dawud, David), the **Injil** (to Isa, Jesus), and the **Qur'an** (to Muhammad). Muslims hold that the Qur'an is the final and complete revelation, preserved in its original Arabic word for word, and that earlier books were altered over time or are no longer in their original form. This is a point on which Jews and Christians hold their own, different, beliefs about their scriptures, and it should be described respectfully.

## The prophets and messengers

Muslims believe that God has sent prophets to every people, beginning with Adam. Qur'an 2:285 lists belief in God, the angels, the books and the messengers together. The prophets named in the Qur'an include Nuh (Noah), **Ibrahim**, Musa, Dawud and **Isa**. Muslims honour Isa as a prophet, born to Maryam (Mary), but do not believe he is the son of God, a point of difference with Christians. Muslims believe that **Muhammad** is the "seal of the prophets" (Qur'an 33:40), the last of them, so no prophet comes after him. All prophets are respected, and a Muslim does not make a distinction between them in belief.

## The Day of Judgement and the afterlife

Muslims believe that this life is a test and that every person will be raised and judged by God on the Day of Judgement (*Yawm al-Qiyamah*). Their deeds will be weighed. Those who have believed and done good will enter **Jannah** (paradise); those who have rejected God and done great wrong will face **Jahannam** (hell). Muslims also stress God's mercy, and believe that sincere repentance can be accepted. This belief affects daily life by encouraging honesty, charity and care for others, since they will be answerable to God.

## Divine decree: al-Qadr

Muslims believe in **al-Qadr**: God knows and has power over everything that happens. This does not take away human responsibility. Muslims understand that people have free will to choose between right and wrong and will be judged for their choices. When something hard happens, a Muslim is encouraged to be patient and to trust in God.

| Article of faith | What Muslims believe | Effect in life |
|---|---|---|
| God (Tawhid) | One God, merciful and just | Worship of God alone; no images |
| Angels | Beings of light who obey God | Awareness that deeds are recorded |
| Books | Tawrat, Zabur, Injil and the final Qur'an | Reading and respecting the Qur'an |
| Prophets | Adam to Muhammad, the seal | Following the example (Sunnah) of the Prophet |
| Last Day | Judgement; Jannah and Jahannam | Honest, charitable living |
| Al-Qadr | God's knowledge and decree; human free will | Patience, trust and responsibility |

:::example{title="Belief in practice: a hard week"}
Thandeka's neighbour, Aisha, fails an examination she studied hard for. Her mother reminds her that Muslims believe God knows all things and that people are asked to do their best and then trust God; Aisha decides to study again. This shows al-Qadr understood as patience together with effort, not as a reason to give up.
:::

:::example{title="Sharing prophets"}
In a school discussion in Gweru, Rudo notes that Ibrahim, Musa and Isa are all prophets in Islam, and that Muslims, Jews and Christians all honour Ibrahim. A good examination answer explains the similarity (shared prophets, one God) and the difference (for Muslims, Muhammad is the final prophet, and Isa is a prophet, not the son of God), stated without calling any belief wrong.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Muslims worship Muhammad"}
Muslims worship only God. The Prophet is deeply respected as God's final messenger, but he is not worshipped.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing Allah as a different God"}
Allah is the Arabic word for God. Muslims, and Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews, use it for the one God.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the articles of faith and the Five Pillars"}
The articles of faith are what Muslims **believe** (six); the Five Pillars are what they **do** (five). Keep the two lists apart.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe or outline"}
For *outline the main beliefs of Islam*, give the six articles as separate points and add a detail to each: for example "angels, such as Jibril, who brought the Qur'an to the Prophet".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}
For *explain why Tawhid is important*, say what it means, show how it affects practice (no images, worship of God alone, the Shahadah) and state what shirk is.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Islam means submission to God; a Muslim is one who submits.
- Muslims hold six articles of faith: God, angels, books, prophets, the Last Day and al-Qadr.
- Tawhid, the oneness of God, is the central belief, and shirk is the greatest wrong.
- God sent prophets to every people, ending with Muhammad, the seal of the prophets.
- The Qur'an is the final revelation; the Tawrat, Zabur and Injil are honoured as earlier books.
- Muslims believe in judgement, paradise and hell, and in divine decree alongside human responsibility.
:::
