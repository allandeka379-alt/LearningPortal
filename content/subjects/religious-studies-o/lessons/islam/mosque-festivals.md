## Introduction

On the morning of Eid, the streets near a mosque in Harare fill with families in their best clothes. Children carry gifts, neighbours greet one another with "Eid Mubarak" and many people of different faiths stop to watch. Yusuf, who is Muslim, invites his classmates Tendai and Rudo to visit his home for the meal that follows. The mosque and the festivals are where many people meet Islam in public.

In this lesson you learn about the mosque and the people who serve it, about Muslim dress and modesty, about the Islamic calendar and about the main festivals and sacred days. You should already know the Five Pillars, because most Muslim festivals arise from them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Masjid (mosque)"}
A place of prostration: the building where Muslims gather for prayer and for many community activities.
:::

:::definition{term="Imam"}
The person who leads the prayer in the mosque and usually gives the Friday sermon. In Sunni Islam an imam is a trained leader, not a priest. For Shi'a Muslims the word also has a special meaning, discussed in a later lesson.
:::

:::definition{term="Eid"}
A festival in Arabic. Muslims have two main Eids: Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha.
:::

:::keyfact
**Eid al-Fitr:** 1 Shawwal, ends Ramadan. **Eid al-Adha:** 10 Dhul-Hijjah, at the end of the Hajj, remembering Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son. **Mawlid an-Nabi:** 12 Rabi al-Awwal. **Islamic New Year:** 1 Muharram. **Ashura:** 10 Muharram. The Islamic calendar is lunar, about 354 days.
:::

## The mosque

The mosque is not a church or a temple; it is a place for the community to pray together, and Muslims may also pray anywhere that is clean. A mosque normally has these features:

| Feature | What it is | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Prayer hall | A large carpeted room without furniture | Rows of worshippers stand, bow and prostrate |
| Mihrab | A niche in the wall | Shows the direction (qiblah) of the Ka'bah |
| Minbar | A raised stand with steps | Where the imam gives the Friday sermon |
| Minaret | A tower | Traditionally where the muezzin gave the adhan; now often a loudspeaker is used |
| Ablution area | Taps and seats | Where worshippers perform wudu |
| Dome | A rounded roof, in many mosques | Decoration and acoustics; not required |

Islam forbids images of God, and most mosques do not display pictures or statues of living beings. Instead they are decorated with calligraphy of Qur'anic verses and geometric patterns. Worshippers remove their shoes at the entrance, so the carpet stays clean.

The mosque has many roles. It is the centre for the five daily prayers, the Jumu'ah prayer and the Eid prayers. It runs the **madrasah**, classes where children learn to read the Qur'an and the basics of their faith. It hosts weddings and funeral prayers, gives advice on family and religious questions, organises the collection and distribution of Zakat, and holds iftar meals in Ramadan. In Zimbabwe many mosques also run schools, clinics and charitable projects open to people of any faith.

## Leaders and practitioners

| Leader | Role |
|---|---|
| Imam | Leads the prayers, gives the Friday sermon and advises the community |
| Muezzin | Gives the adhan; the first was Bilal |
| Ulama (scholars) | People trained in the Qur'an, Hadith and law who teach and answer religious questions |
| Qadi | A judge who applies Islamic law, for example in family matters, where it is recognised |

Any knowledgeable Muslim may lead prayer in an emergency, and there is no priesthood in Sunni Islam. Authority rests on learning and the trust of the community.

## Dress and modesty

The Qur'an (24:30-31) asks believing men and women to be modest in behaviour and dress. Muslims apply this in different ways. Many Muslim women wear a headscarf (**hijab**) in public and loose clothing; many Muslim men wear a cap (**kufi**) at prayer and dress modestly, and some wear a long shirt. In some communities women also wear a face veil, in others they do not wear a headscarf at all. Practice varies according to local customs, family tradition and each person's understanding. Muslims say that modesty expresses respect for oneself and for God.

## The Islamic calendar

The Islamic calendar has twelve months of 29 or 30 days, based on the cycles of the moon, so a year is about 354 days, roughly eleven days shorter than the solar year. Because of this, Muslim festivals move about eleven days earlier each year. A month begins when the new moon is sighted. Year 1 is the year of the Hijrah, 622 CE. The months are Muharram, Safar, Rabi al-Awwal, Rabi al-Thani, Jumada al-Awwal, Jumada al-Thani, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhul-Qa'dah and Dhul-Hijjah.

## Festivals and sacred days

**Eid al-Fitr** ("the festival of breaking the fast") is held on the first day of Shawwal after Ramadan. Muslims give *zakat al-fitr* before the Eid prayer so that the poor can celebrate. In the morning they attend a special congregational prayer, usually in a large hall or open space, put on their best or new clothes, visit family and friends, exchange gifts and eat sweet foods.

**Eid al-Adha** ("the festival of sacrifice") begins on 10 Dhul-Hijjah, as the pilgrims on the Hajj complete their rites. It remembers the story of Ibrahim, who was willing to sacrifice his son in obedience to God, and was given an animal to sacrifice instead. Families who can afford it sacrifice a sheep, goat or other animal, and share the meat in three parts: for the family, for relatives and friends, and for the poor. There is also a special Eid prayer. The festival teaches obedience, sacrifice and generosity.

**Mawlid an-Nabi** is the birthday of the Prophet, on 12 Rabi al-Awwal. Many Muslims mark it with sermons, recitations and charity. Some Muslims do not observe it, because they believe it was not practised in the early community. Both views are held in good faith.

**The Islamic New Year** (1 Muharram) remembers the Hijrah. It is usually marked quietly with prayers and reflection.

**Ashura** (10 Muharram) is observed in different ways. Many Sunni Muslims fast on this day, remembering, according to tradition, that Musa and his people were saved from Pharaoh. For Shi'a Muslims it is also a day of mourning for Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet, who died at Karbala in 680 CE.

:::example{title="Why Eid moves"}
Chipo asks why Eid al-Fitr was in March one year and in a different month later. Her teacher explains that the Islamic year is lunar, about eleven days shorter than the solar year, so a festival that falls in March in one year falls about eleven days earlier the next. After roughly 33 years it returns to the same season. A good answer to a calendar question names the cause (lunar months) and gives the effect (festivals move through the seasons).
:::

:::example{title="Sharing the sacrifice"}
Mthulisi's neighbour, Ibrahim, sacrifices a goat at Eid al-Adha. He keeps a third of the meat for his family, gives a third to relatives and friends, and gives a third to families who cannot afford meat. This practice links the festival to Ibrahim's obedience and to the duty of giving to the poor.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the two Eids"}
Eid al-Fitr ends Ramadan and is marked by breaking the fast; Eid al-Adha comes at the Hajj and remembers Ibrahim's sacrifice. Do not mix them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the imam a priest"}
An imam leads prayer and teaches, but in Sunni Islam there is no priesthood that stands between a person and God.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that all Muslims dress the same way"}
Muslim dress and modesty vary by country, family and personal conviction. Use words such as "many" and "some".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe"}
For *describe the main features of a mosque*, name each feature (mihrab, minbar, minaret, ablution area, prayer hall) and say what it is for. Mention that there are no images.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}
For *explain the importance of Eid al-Fitr or Eid al-Adha*, give the event it remembers, how Muslims celebrate and what it teaches (thankfulness, sharing, obedience).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The mosque is a place of prayer and community life, with a prayer hall, mihrab, minbar, minaret and ablution area, and no images.
- The imam leads prayer; the muezzin gives the adhan; the ulama teach; a qadi judges.
- Muslims are asked to dress modestly, and practice varies widely.
- The Islamic calendar is lunar, about 354 days, so festivals move about eleven days earlier each year.
- Eid al-Fitr ends Ramadan; Eid al-Adha remembers Ibrahim's obedience and the sacrifice shared with the poor.
- Other days include Mawlid an-Nabi, the Islamic New Year and Ashura.
:::
