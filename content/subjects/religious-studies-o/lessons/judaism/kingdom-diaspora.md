## Introduction

If you visit the Jewish community in Harare or Bulawayo, you will find a small congregation that is part of a worldwide people. Jews live on every continent, yet their prayers, their festivals and their sense of history point back to one small land, and especially to the city of Jerusalem. How did a people that began with a few families become scattered across the world, and why do its members still feel one history?

This lesson follows the story from the kings of ancient Israel to the present. It is a history lesson in a Religious Studies course, so it explains how events shaped the **beliefs and practices** of Judaism. You should already know the covenant with Abraham and the Exodus. The lesson takes no position on current Middle East politics; it states only the well-established dates and facts.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monarchy"}
Rule by a king. Israel's first kings were Saul, David and Solomon.
:::

:::definition{term="Exile"}
Forced removal from one's homeland. The Babylonian exile began when Jerusalem was destroyed in 586 BCE.
:::

:::definition{term="Diaspora"}
The "scattering" of Jews outside the land of Israel, and the Jewish communities that live there.
:::

:::definition{term="Rabbinic Judaism"}
The form of Judaism that developed after the Second Temple was destroyed, centred on the synagogue, prayer, the study of Torah and the teaching of rabbis.
:::

## The judges and the first kings

After the entry into Canaan, the tribes were led for a time by judges, leaders who guided them in times of danger (the Book of Judges). The people then asked for a king. **Saul** became the first king. **David** united the tribes, made Jerusalem his capital and is remembered as Israel's greatest king and the traditional author of many Psalms. His son **Solomon** built the **First Temple** in Jerusalem, which became the centre of worship, sacrifice and pilgrimage. These events happened roughly a thousand years before the Common Era.

## The divided kingdom and the prophets

After Solomon, the kingdom divided into the northern kingdom of **Israel** and the southern kingdom of **Judah**, whose capital remained Jerusalem. During these years **prophets** such as Amos, Hosea, Isaiah, Micah and Jeremiah spoke in God's name. They called the people and their rulers to worship one God and to practise justice, and they warned that injustice would bring disaster. In the eighth century BCE the northern kingdom was conquered by Assyria.

## The Babylonian exile

In **586 BCE** the Babylonian army destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple, and many people of Judah were taken to Babylon. This was the **Babylonian exile**. Far from the Temple, the exiles learned to keep their faith through prayer, study and the Sabbath, ideas that later shaped the synagogue. After the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon, many exiles were allowed to return, and the **Second Temple** was built in Jerusalem. Others stayed in Babylon, which became a major centre of Jewish learning.

## The Second Temple and the Roman destruction

The Second Temple stood for several centuries under foreign rulers, among them the Greeks and then the Romans. In **70 CE** the Romans destroyed Jerusalem and the Temple after a Jewish revolt. Only the western supporting wall of the Temple platform survives, and it is a place of prayer today. After this loss, sacrifice could no longer be offered, and Judaism had to continue without the Temple. Teachers called **rabbis** led the way by developing a Judaism based on the synagogue, prayer, study and the keeping of the commandments. Their teaching was gathered in the Mishnah and the Talmud.

## The diaspora and persecution

Jewish communities had existed outside the land for centuries, and now they spread widely across the Mediterranean world, Europe, North Africa and Asia. In the **diaspora** Jews kept their identity through the Sabbath, the festivals, the Hebrew scriptures and the hope of return. Many communities lived in peace and contributed to trade, medicine and learning. Others suffered persecution: Jews were forced to live in separate quarters, expelled from some countries (such as Spain in 1492), and attacked in riots called pogroms.

:::keyfact
Learn these dates: **586 BCE** destruction of Jerusalem and the First Temple; **70 CE** destruction of the Second Temple by the Romans; **1941-45** the mass murder of the Holocaust; **14 May 1948** proclamation of the State of Israel.
:::

## The Holocaust and the State of Israel

In the twentieth century the Nazi regime in Germany, which came to power in 1933, persecuted Jews and, between 1941 and 1945 during the Second World War, carried out the systematic murder of about **six million Jewish men, women and children**. Millions of other people were also persecuted, but Jews were singled out for total destruction. This is called the **Holocaust** or **Shoah**. It is remembered each year on Holocaust Remembrance Day. After the war, many survivors rebuilt their lives in different countries, and on **14 May 1948** the **State of Israel** was proclaimed. Many Jews see the state as the fulfilment of an ancient hope; others hold different views about it. This course does not discuss current politics.

## Jews in Zimbabwe

Jews came to Bulawayo and Harare in the early colonial period, and the synagogues in both cities served families who gathered for prayer and festivals, ran schools and took part in the life of the country. The community is small today, but its congregations continue to hold services, including on Shabbat and festivals, and take part in charity and education.

:::example{title="The Lemba (Remba) tradition"}
The **Lemba** (also called Remba) are a people living in parts of Zimbabwe and South Africa. Their oral tradition says that their ancestors came from the north, crossed the sea and travelled south, and that they descended from Jewish ancestors. Lemba customs include male circumcision, the avoidance of pork, and the care taken in slaughtering animals. Scholars, including researchers who studied genetic markers, have investigated the claim and have reached varied conclusions, and not all scholars agree about it. In an examination, write this as a **claim studied by scholars**, not as a proved fact and not as a rejected one.
:::

:::example{title="How exile changed worship"}
Imagine the exiles in Babylon. There is no Temple, no sacrifice and no priests to lead them. They meet on the Sabbath to pray, read and remember. When Tendai's Jewish friend Miriam explains the synagogue, she says it carries on from that experience: a community can worship wherever it gathers, because prayer, study and the keeping of commandments do not depend on a building.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the two Temples"}
The **First Temple** (Solomon) was destroyed in 586 BCE by Babylon. The **Second Temple** was destroyed in 70 CE by Rome.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about politics"}
Describe the State of Israel as an event in Jewish history and give the date; leave current political disputes out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the Lemba claim as proved"}
Present it as a tradition studied by scholars, with varied conclusions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe or outline"}
For *outline the history of the Jews from the kings to the diaspora*, give a chain of events with dates (586 BCE, 70 CE) and one result for each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain significance"}
For *explain why the destruction of the Temple was important*, say that sacrifice ended, rabbis and the synagogue became central, and Jews dispersed in the diaspora.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Saul, David and Solomon were Israel's first kings; Solomon built the First Temple in Jerusalem.
- The kingdom divided into Israel and Judah; prophets called both to worship one God and to practise justice.
- In 586 BCE the Babylonians destroyed the First Temple and exiled many; a Second Temple was built after the return.
- In 70 CE the Romans destroyed the Second Temple; rabbinic Judaism and the synagogue became central, and the diaspora spread.
- About six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust (1941-45); the State of Israel was proclaimed on 14 May 1948.
- Jewish communities exist in Harare and Bulawayo; the Lemba tradition of Jewish ancestry is a claim studied by scholars.
:::
