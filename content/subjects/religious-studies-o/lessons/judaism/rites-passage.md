## Introduction

Think of the big occasions in a family: a baby is born, a child grows up, two people marry, and eventually a loved one dies. In every community, these moments are marked by ceremonies. In Judaism they are called **life-cycle events**, and each one links the person to God, the family and the Jewish people. If your neighbours in Harare are Jewish, you may be invited to a naming, a coming-of-age celebration or a wedding, and you should know what is happening and why.

In this lesson you learn the main Jewish rites of passage: birth, coming of age, marriage and divorce, and death and mourning. You should already know the covenant with Abraham, where circumcision first appears. Remember that practice differs among Jewish groups: this lesson says "Orthodox" and "Reform" where it matters, without ranking them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rite of passage"}
A ceremony that marks a person's move from one stage of life to another, such as birth, adulthood, marriage and death.
:::

:::definition{term="Brit milah"}
"Covenant of circumcision": the removal of the foreskin of a baby boy on the eighth day, carried out by a trained person called a **mohel**.
:::

:::definition{term="Bar mitzvah and bat mitzvah"}
"Son of the commandment" and "daughter of the commandment": the ceremonies when a Jewish boy (at 13) or girl (at 12 or 13) becomes responsible for keeping the commandments.
:::

:::definition{term="Shiva"}
The seven days of intense mourning after a burial, kept at home by the close family.
:::

## Birth: brit milah and naming

A Jewish baby boy is circumcised on the **eighth day** after his birth, as God commanded Abraham (Genesis 17). The ceremony, the **brit milah**, is performed by a mohel, usually at home or in the synagogue, in the presence of family and friends, with the baby held by a person who has been given the honour. The boy is given his Hebrew **name** during the ceremony, and the guests wish him a life of Torah, marriage and good deeds. The ceremony joins him to the covenant between God and the Jewish people.

For a baby girl there is no circumcision, but a **naming ceremony** is held, often in the synagogue on a Shabbat in the weeks after the birth, or in the home. Her name is announced, and prayers are said for the child and her mother. In many families the new baby is named after a relative who has died.

## Coming of age: bar mitzvah and bat mitzvah

At **13** a Jewish boy becomes a **bar mitzvah**. From then on he is responsible for keeping the commandments, he counts toward a minyan, and he may be called up to read from the Torah. He prepares for the occasion for months, learning Hebrew and the part of the Torah to be read. On the first Shabbat after his birthday, he is called up to the Torah, may read the weekly portion or the haftarah and often gives a short talk, and the family holds a celebration.

For girls, the **bat mitzvah** is held at **12** in Orthodox tradition and at **12 or 13** in other groups. In Reform and Conservative synagogues a girl usually reads from the Torah in the same way as a boy. In Orthodox communities, a girl's celebration is often a party or a talk, and not a Torah reading in the main service. Coming of age is not a miracle but a recognition: a person becomes accountable for their own actions.

:::example{title="A bar mitzvah in Bulawayo"}
Thirteen-year-old David has studied for a year. On Saturday morning, he is called up to the Torah scroll and chants a passage in Hebrew, then speaks about its message. His grandfather gives him a prayer shawl. After the service the congregation sings (in some communities sweets are thrown), and the family hosts a meal. David's friend Tendai, who is not Jewish, learns that the day marks a change: now David counts as an adult member of the community and is responsible for his own observance of the commandments.
:::

## Marriage

Judaism values marriage and the family. A wedding has several parts:

| Part | What happens | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Betrothal | The couple agree to marry; in some communities, an engagement ceremony | A serious promise |
| Ketubah | A marriage contract signed by witnesses and read at the wedding | Protects the bride and sets out the husband's duties |
| Chuppah | A canopy under which the couple stand | Represents the home they will build together |
| Rings and blessings | The groom gives a ring to the bride; the seven blessings are said over wine | Joy, commitment and God's blessing on the couple |
| Breaking the glass | The groom (in many weddings, both) breaks a glass underfoot | Remembers the destruction of the Temple even at a moment of joy |

The guests then shout "mazal tov" (good luck), and a celebration follows.

### Divorce

Judaism accepts divorce as a last resort when a marriage has broken down. The husband gives his wife a document called a **get**, which ends the marriage. In Orthodox practice the get must be given willingly by the husband, which can cause serious difficulty for a woman whose husband refuses; rabbis and organisations work to address this. Reform Judaism also recognises civil divorce.

## Death, burial and mourning

Jewish funerals follow the principle of respect for the dead. The rites are as follows:

| Stage | What happens | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Burial | Takes place as soon as possible, usually within a day or two; the body is washed and wrapped in a simple shroud by a burial society (*chevra kadisha*); the coffin is plain | The rich and poor are treated alike; respect for the body |
| Tearing a garment | Close relatives tear a garment or ribbon (*kriah*) | Expresses grief |
| Funeral and kaddish | A short service; mourners say the **kaddish**, a prayer that praises God and does not mention death | Faith in God even in sorrow |
| Shiva | Seven days of mourning at home; mourners sit on low seats, mirrors are covered, and friends visit and bring food | The community supports the family |
| Sheloshim | Thirty days of lighter mourning after the burial | A gradual return to ordinary life |
| Yahrzeit | The anniversary of the death, marked each year by a memorial candle and kaddish | Remembers the dead |

Orthodox Jews do not permit cremation; Reform Jews are more accepting of it, although burial is the norm. When Jews visit a grave, they often place a small stone on it as a sign that someone has visited.

:::example{title="Supporting a mourning family"}
When Rachel's grandfather dies, her family sits shiva for seven days. Friends bring meals, because the mourners should not need to cook. A minyan gathers each evening to pray, and Rachel's father says the kaddish. A non-Jewish neighbour, Nyasha, visits to greet the family quietly. The example shows how the community shares the grief and how the rites move the family gradually from deep sorrow back to everyday life.
:::

## Differences among Jewish groups

| Rite | Many Orthodox Jews | Many Reform Jews |
|---|---|---|
| Bat mitzvah | Often a celebration or talk | Usually a Torah reading like a bar mitzvah |
| Divorce | A religious get is required | Civil divorce is recognised |
| Cremation | Not permitted | Accepted by some, though burial is usual |
| Women as rabbis | Not ordained | May be ordained and may conduct the wedding |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that the bar mitzvah makes a boy Jewish"}
A person is Jewish by birth to a Jewish mother (or by conversion). The bar mitzvah marks responsibility; it does not make someone Jewish.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing shiva, sheloshim and yahrzeit"}
Shiva is seven days; sheloshim is thirty days; yahrzeit is the yearly anniversary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that all Jews do the same"}
Practice differs on the bat mitzvah, divorce and cremation. Use "many Orthodox" and "many Reform".
:::

For *describe the Jewish marriage ceremony (or funeral)*, give the stages in order and add the meaning of each in a phrase: the chuppah as home, the glass as remembering the Temple.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A baby boy is circumcised on the eighth day (brit milah) and named; a girl is named at a ceremony in the synagogue or home.
- Bar mitzvah (13) and bat mitzvah (12 or 13) mark responsibility for the commandments.
- A wedding includes the ketubah, the chuppah, the seven blessings and the breaking of the glass.
- Divorce uses the get; practice differs among Jewish groups.
- Burial is soon, simple and respectful; mourning follows shiva (7 days), sheloshim (30 days) and yahrzeit (yearly).
- Mourners say the kaddish, a prayer of praise to God.
:::
