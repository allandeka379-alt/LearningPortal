## Introduction

A farmer in Gweru finishes harvesting his field, but he leaves the grain at the edges and the stalks that fell, so that poor people can collect them. A doctor in Harare is called out on a Saturday to save a patient's life. A shopkeeper gives honest weight to every customer. In Judaism these are not unusual acts of kindness but part of a religious duty. Jewish ethics begin from the belief that God is just and merciful, and that those who belong to the covenant are asked to act likewise.

In this lesson you learn the main sources and principles of Jewish moral teaching: the commandments, justice, charity, kindness, care for the stranger, the value of life, the duty to repair the world, and family ethics. You should know the Exodus, the Torah and the Sabbath already. Remember to write about Jewish teaching as teaching ("Jews believe that…", "the Torah says…") and to note that Jews apply these principles in different ways.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mitzvah (plural mitzvot)"}
A commandment; also used more widely for any good deed done in obedience to God's will.
:::

:::definition{term="Tzedakah"}
Charity, from a word meaning "righteousness" or "justice": giving to those in need is seen as a duty, not just a kind gesture.
:::

:::definition{term="Pikuach nefesh"}
The saving of a human life, which Jewish law says takes priority over almost every other commandment, including the Sabbath laws.
:::

:::definition{term="Tikkun olam"}
"Repairing the world": the idea that human beings, working with God, are responsible for making the world more just and caring.
:::

## Sources of Jewish ethics

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5) are the best-known moral law. They include duties to God (worship only God; keep the Sabbath) and duties to other people: honour your father and mother, and do not murder, commit adultery, steal, give false testimony or covet what belongs to another. Jewish tradition teaches that the Torah contains **613 mitzvot** in all, traditionally 248 positive commands ("do") and 365 negative commands ("do not"). Many of them concern ethics: honest weights, fair wages, care for the poor and fair treatment of foreigners. Later teachers expanded these principles in the Talmud and in the writing of rabbis.

:::keyfact
Learn: **613** mitzvot; **tzedek** = justice; **tzedakah** = charity; **gemilut hasadim** = acts of loving-kindness; **pikuach nefesh** = saving life; **tikkun olam** = repairing the world. Love your neighbour: **Leviticus 19:18**. Love the stranger: **Leviticus 19:34**.
:::

## Justice, charity and loving-kindness

### Justice (tzedek)

The Torah demands justice in courts and in daily life: judges must not take bribes or favour the rich or the poor, and merchants must use honest weights and measures (Leviticus 19:35-36). Deuteronomy 16:20 tells the people to pursue justice. Employers must pay workers on time (Deuteronomy 24:14-15).

### Charity (tzedakah)

Judaism treats helping the poor as a duty. The Torah commands farmers to leave the edges of the field and any dropped grain for the poor (Leviticus 19:9-10) and to open their hands to a needy neighbour (Deuteronomy 15:7-11). Many Jewish homes keep a collection box for charity. The medieval scholar Maimonides described eight levels of giving; the highest is to help a person become self-supporting, for example with a loan, a job or a partnership, so that they no longer depend on others.

### Loving-kindness (gemilut hasadim)

Acts of loving-kindness include visiting the sick, welcoming guests, comforting mourners, helping a bride and attending a funeral. Unlike charity, they can be done for rich or poor, and they involve personal presence and not only money.

:::example{title="Maimonides' highest level of giving"}
A Jewish business owner in Bulawayo meets a young man, Tendai, who sells vegetables from the roadside. Instead of giving him a gift of money, she lends him money to rent a stall and teaches him to keep accounts. Within a year Tendai no longer needs help. According to Maimonides, this is the highest form of tzedakah, because it restores a person's independence and dignity. The example shows that Jewish ethics care about the way help is given as well as the giving.
:::

## Love of neighbour and the stranger

**Leviticus 19:18** commands, in paraphrase, that a person must love their neighbour as themselves. **Leviticus 19:34** extends this to the foreigner living among the people: they are to be treated like a native, since the Israelites were strangers in Egypt. The memory of slavery therefore becomes a basis for justice and compassion. The Torah repeatedly protects the weak: the widow, the orphan, the stranger and the poor.

## The sanctity of life: pikuach nefesh

Jewish teaching holds that every human life is of infinite value, since all people are made in the image of God (Genesis 1). The law of **pikuach nefesh** says that preserving life overrides almost all other laws. A Jewish doctor may treat a patient on the Sabbath, and a person who is seriously ill may break the Yom Kippur fast on medical advice. The only commandments that may not be broken even to save a life are the prohibitions on murder, certain sexual offences and idolatry.

:::example{title="The doctor and the Sabbath"}
David's uncle is an Orthodox Jewish doctor in Harare. One Saturday morning, he is called to the hospital when a child is brought in with a severe injury. He drives to the hospital, although he normally would not drive on Shabbat. He explains that the Torah's rule of saving life takes priority, so helping the child is not a breach of the Sabbath but an act that the law requires. The example shows how Jewish law ranks its values: life comes first.
:::

## Repairing the world: tikkun olam

The idea of **tikkun olam** developed in rabbinic writings and later Jewish thought. Today many Jews, especially in the Reform and Conservative movements, use it to describe social action: working for the poor, the environment, education and human rights, as a way of sharing God's work. Orthodox Jews also stress care for others through tzedakah and gemilut hasadim, although they may not use the phrase as much.

## The prophets' call for justice

The prophets told the people that worship without justice is empty. **Amos 5** warns that God rejects festivals and offerings when the poor are treated unfairly, and says justice should flow constantly, like a river that does not dry up. **Micah 6:8** sums up what God requires: to act justly, to love kindness and to walk humbly with God. These passages show that Jewish ethics are inseparable from faith.

## Family and sexual ethics

Judaism values marriage, children and the duty to honour parents (Exodus 20:12). The Torah and later tradition teach faithfulness within marriage and prohibit adultery. Sexual ethics are discussed in different ways among Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews, who hold varied views on matters such as the place of same-sex relationships and contraception. A Religious Studies answer reports these views without taking sides and notes that the groups apply the traditions differently.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing the Ten Commandments without explaining them"}
In an examination, give the commandment and say what moral teaching it expresses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Jews must always keep the Sabbath, even when life is in danger"}
Pikuach nefesh overrides the Sabbath laws. Use it as an example of how Jewish law ranks values.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that charity is optional in Judaism"}
The word tzedakah comes from justice: giving to the poor is a duty.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe"}
For *describe the moral teachings of Judaism*, give four or five teachings (justice, charity, kindness, love of neighbour and stranger, sanctity of life) and support at least two with a reference (Leviticus 19; Micah 6:8).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain or how relevant today"}
For *how relevant is Jewish moral teaching today*, link a teaching to a modern problem (poverty, refugees, fair wages) and reach a short judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Ten Commandments and the 613 mitzvot are the foundation of Jewish moral law.
- Tzedek (justice), tzedakah (charity, a duty) and gemilut hasadim (loving-kindness) are central.
- Leviticus 19:18 and 19:34 command love of neighbour and of the stranger, based on the memory of slavery in Egypt.
- Pikuach nefesh: saving life overrides almost all laws, including the Sabbath.
- Tikkun olam (repairing the world) encourages social action; the prophets (Amos 5; Micah 6:8) link faith to justice.
- Jewish groups apply family and sexual ethics in different ways.
:::
