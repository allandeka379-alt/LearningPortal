## Introduction

Imagine two lunch boxes at a school in Mutare. One belongs to a Jewish boy named David, and it contains cheese sandwiches and fruit; his friend's box contains a meat pie. When they sit together, David politely explains that he does not mix meat and milk. For a Jewish family the kitchen is not only a place to cook. It is part of the way the family lives its faith, and so is the doorway of the house, which carries a small case that tells visitors something about the home.

This lesson looks at the Jewish home and the dietary laws called **kashrut**. You should already know the Shema, since the mezuzah comes from it, and the Sabbath, since many home customs belong to it. Ethical teaching is covered in the last lesson of this topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Kashrut"}
The Jewish dietary laws, which say which foods may be eaten and how they must be prepared.
:::

:::definition{term="Kosher and treif"}
**Kosher** means "fit" or permitted food. **Treif** (*tereifah*) is food that is not kosher. The word kosher is often used for food prepared according to the laws.
:::

:::definition{term="Shechitah"}
The Jewish method of slaughtering animals for food, performed by a trained person (a *shochet*) with a very sharp knife.
:::

:::definition{term="Mezuzah"}
A small case on the doorposts of a Jewish home containing a scroll with the verses of the Shema.
:::

## The home as a centre of religious life

Many Jews say that the home is a "small sanctuary". Judaism does not depend only on the synagogue: the home is where children learn their faith, where Shabbat and festivals are kept and where the family lives out the commandments. Features of the Jewish home include:

- **The mezuzah.** Fixed on the right side of the doorpost of most rooms, as commanded in Deuteronomy 6:9. People touch it and kiss their fingers when they pass.
- **Shabbat and festival meals.** The family gathers around the table with candles, wine and bread. Meals are times for blessings, songs and conversation about the Torah.
- **Teaching the children.** The Shema commands parents to teach the commandments to their children at home and when they walk. Parents tell the stories of the festivals, teach prayers and send children to learn Hebrew and the Torah.
- **Blessings.** Jews say a blessing before eating and before many daily actions, as a way of recognising God in ordinary life.
- **Charity.** Many homes keep a small collection box (*pushke*) for tzedakah, charity to the poor.

:::example{title="Teaching the Passover story at home"}
In a Bulawayo home, Rachel's mother tells her younger brother the Exodus story each year. She asks him to find the hidden piece of matzah, then to ask the four questions at the seder table. Rachel's father reminds the children to fulfil the commandment to tell the story to the next generation. This is the Shema's command to teach children carried out in a family.
:::

### Family roles

Jewish teaching values the family and the duty to honour parents (Exodus 20:12). In traditional Orthodox homes the mother lights the Shabbat candles and manages the home, and the father says kiddush and leads the meal; both parents teach the children. In Reform and many Conservative homes, roles are shared more equally and boys and girls receive the same Jewish education. Families differ, and a good answer does not suggest that every Jewish family follows one pattern.

## The dietary laws: kashrut

The basis of the dietary laws is found in the Torah in **Leviticus 11** and **Deuteronomy 14**. The main rules are as follows.

| Rule | What it says | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Land animals | Only animals that chew the cud and have split hooves may be eaten | Cattle, sheep and goats are permitted; pigs, camels and rabbits are not |
| Fish | Only fish that have fins and scales | Tilapia is permitted; shellfish and catfish are not |
| Birds | Birds of prey and scavengers are not permitted | Chicken, duck and turkey are permitted |
| Blood | Blood may not be eaten; meat is soaked and salted to remove it | Leviticus 17 |
| Meat and milk | Meat and dairy products are not mixed or eaten together, based on a command not to boil a kid in its mother's milk (Exodus 23:19) | Separate dishes and utensils; no cheeseburgers |
| Parve | Foods that are neither meat nor dairy can be eaten with either | Fruit, vegetables, eggs and grains |

Kosher animals must also be slaughtered by **shechitah**: a trained shochet uses a very sharp knife to make one swift cut, which Jewish teaching says causes the animal minimum suffering. The animal must be healthy, and the blood is drained. A kosher kitchen keeps two sets of dishes, one for meat and one for dairy. In many countries food packets carry a kosher symbol to show that a rabbinic authority has approved them.

:::keyfact
Kosher animals chew the cud **and** have split hooves; fish need **fins and scales**; meat and milk are kept **apart**; animals are killed by **shechitah**; blood is not eaten. Basis: **Leviticus 11** and **Deuteronomy 14**.
:::

## Why do Jews keep the dietary laws?

The Torah does not give a single explanation, and Jews offer several reasons:

- **Obedience to God.** For Orthodox Jews the main reason is that God commanded it, and it needs no further reason.
- **Holiness and self-discipline.** The laws make every meal a reminder of God and train people to control their appetite.
- **Compassion for animals.** Shechitah and the ban on eating blood teach respect for life.
- **Identity and community.** Eating differently marks Jews as a people with their own tradition, and sharing kosher meals strengthens the community.
- **Health.** Some Jews also point to health benefits, although this is not the main reason.

## Differences among Jews

Many Orthodox Jews keep the laws strictly. Many Conservative Jews keep them, with some differences in detail. Many Reform Jews treat them as a personal choice, and some keep only a few, such as avoiding pork. Secular Jews may not keep them at all. In places where few Jews live, keeping kosher can take planning, and families may prepare more food at home.

:::example{title="Eating with a Jewish friend"}
Tafadzwa is invited to lunch at the home of his Jewish friend Miriam. He brings fruit and biscuits, because he has been told that the family keeps kosher and that food bought outside the home may not be acceptable. At the table Miriam's mother explains that the family keeps separate dishes for meat and milk. Tafadzwa learns that a rule that seems strict to an outsider is, for the family, a daily way of showing respect for God.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing that kosher means blessed by a rabbi"}
Kosher means that the food follows the laws. A rabbi (or authority) may certify it, but that is not a blessing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that all Jews follow the same food rules"}
Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews keep kashrut in different ways. Use "many Orthodox Jews".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only health reasons"}
The main reason Jews give is obedience to God's commandments. Health may be added as a secondary view.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Jewish home is a small sanctuary: mezuzah, Shabbat and festival meals, blessings and teaching of children.
- Family roles differ between Orthodox and Reform homes; children are taught the Torah and prayers.
- Kashrut is based on Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14: permitted animals chew the cud and have split hooves; fish need fins and scales.
- Meat and milk are kept apart and blood is not eaten; animals are slaughtered by shechitah.
- Jews give reasons of obedience, holiness, compassion, identity and (sometimes) health.
- Many Orthodox Jews keep the laws strictly; others keep them to a lesser degree.
:::
