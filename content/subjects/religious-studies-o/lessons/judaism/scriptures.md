## Introduction

When the Torah scroll is taken out of the ark in a synagogue in Bulawayo, the congregation stands. A reader carries it through the room, people touch it with the fringe of a prayer shawl or with a prayer book and kiss it, and then it is placed on a reading desk and read aloud. The way a community treats a book tells you what it believes about it, and Jews treat the Torah with the greatest respect.

In this lesson you learn what the Jewish scriptures are, how they are organised, why Jews say there is an oral Torah as well as a written one, and how the scriptures are used in prayer, study and everyday life. You should already know the story of the patriarchs and of Moses, because the Torah tells them. The scriptures' teaching on belief is covered in the lesson on Jewish beliefs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tanakh"}
The Hebrew Bible, the whole of the Jewish scriptures. The word is made from the first letters of its three parts: **T**orah, **N**evi'im and **K**etuvim.
:::

:::definition{term="Sefer Torah"}
A Torah scroll: the five books of Moses handwritten in Hebrew on parchment by a trained scribe (sofer) and read in the synagogue.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral Torah"}
Teaching that Jews believe was given to Moses at Sinai alongside the written Torah and passed on by word of mouth before being written down in the Mishnah and Talmud.
:::

:::definition{term="Talmud"}
The great collection of rabbinic discussion about the Torah, made up of the Mishnah (the earlier law code) and the Gemara (the later discussion).
:::

:::keyfact
Tanakh = **Torah** (5 books) + **Nevi'im** (Prophets) + **Ketuvim** (Writings). The Torah is the first part of the Tanakh, not the whole of it.
:::

## The Tanakh

| Part | Meaning | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Torah | Teaching | Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy |
| Nevi'im | Prophets | Historical books (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) and the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve, such as Amos and Micah) |
| Ketuvim | Writings | Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ruth, Esther, Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, Chronicles and others |

The **Torah** tells the story from creation to the death of Moses and contains the laws, including the Ten Commandments. The **Nevi'im** tell the history of Israel and record the messages of the prophets. The **Ketuvim** include poetry, wisdom, stories and prayers; the book of Psalms provides many prayers used in worship, and Esther is read at the festival of Purim.

## The Torah scroll and how it is treated

A Sefer Torah is made with great care. A scribe writes it by hand with a quill on parchment, and a single mistake means the text must be corrected. Some other features of how it is treated:

- It is kept in the **ark** of the synagogue and often wears a decorated cover.
- It is carried in procession and everyone stands as it passes.
- The reader does not touch the writing with the hand but follows the words with a pointer, the **yad**.
- A worn-out scroll is not thrown away but buried with respect.

The Torah is divided into weekly portions (**parashot**), so that the whole scroll is read over a year. The cycle ends and begins again at the festival of **Simchat Torah** (Rejoicing in the Torah), when people dance with the scrolls. The Torah is read on Shabbat morning, on Mondays and Thursdays in many synagogues, and on festivals.

:::example{title="A Torah reading, step by step"}
On Shabbat morning in Harare the ark is opened and the Torah is carried to the reading desk. A person called to the Torah says a blessing before the reading, and the reader chants the weekly portion from the scroll, following the text with a yad. The one called up says a blessing after the reading. Afterwards, the scroll is lifted so the congregation can see the text, and it is returned to the ark. Each part of the ceremony shows honour for God's teaching.
:::

## The oral Torah, Mishnah and Talmud

The written Torah is short, and it does not explain every law. For example, the commandment to rest on the Sabbath does not list in full what counts as work. Jews therefore believe that Moses also received an **oral Torah**, a tradition of explanation handed down by teachers. When Jews were scattered after the destruction of the Second Temple, rabbis feared that this tradition would be forgotten, and it was written down.

| Work | What it is | Approximate date |
|---|---|---|
| Written Torah | Five books of Moses | Given at Sinai in Jewish belief |
| Mishnah | The collected laws of the oral Torah, compiled by Rabbi Judah the Prince | About 200 CE |
| Gemara | Discussion of the Mishnah by later rabbis | Written down over the following centuries |
| Talmud | Mishnah and Gemara together; there is a Jerusalem Talmud and a larger Babylonian Talmud | Babylonian Talmud completed about 500 CE |

The Talmud preserves arguments between rabbis, with different opinions recorded, and it shows that Judaism values questioning and debate. Later scholars wrote commentaries on the Torah and the Talmud, such as the medieval teacher known as **Rashi**. This chain of commentary is why Jews say that the Torah is "interpreted by the rabbis" and not read alone.

## How Jews use scripture

Scripture is used in many ways:

- **In worship:** the Torah is read in the synagogue, passages from the prophets (the *haftarah*) follow it, and the prayer book is full of verses from the Psalms and other books.
- **In study:** studying Torah is a duty and a joy. Many Jews study every day, often with a partner, and children learn Hebrew to read it.
- **In daily life:** the Shema is recited, blessings are said over food, and the commandments in the Torah are the basis of Jewish law about the Sabbath, food and ethics.
- **At rites of passage:** a boy or girl reads from the Torah at a bar or bat mitzvah.

:::example{title="Two students compare their scriptures"}
Chipo, who is Christian, asks David, who is Jewish, whether his Bible is the same as hers. David explains that the Tanakh contains the same books as the Christian Old Testament, though arranged in a different order and with different names. Jews do not accept the New Testament as scripture and so do not call the Tanakh the "Old Testament", because "old" suggests that it has been replaced. Chipo adds that for Christians the Old Testament leads on to the New Testament. Each is describing what their own community believes.
:::

## The Tanakh and the Christian Old Testament

Most of the content of the Tanakh is shared with the Christian Old Testament, but the order differs, and Christians add the New Testament as a second part of their Bible. For Jews, the Tanakh together with the oral Torah is a complete body of teaching. A Religious Studies answer reports both views without judging between them.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using Torah and Tanakh as the same word"}
The Torah is the first five books. The Tanakh is the whole Hebrew Bible. In a wider sense "Torah" may mean all Jewish teaching, so state which sense you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the Tanakh the Old Testament in Jewish contexts"}
Jews use the name Tanakh. Use "Old Testament" only when writing about the Christian Bible.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that the Talmud replaced the Torah"}
The Talmud explains and applies the Torah; it does not replace it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Tanakh has three parts: Torah, Nevi'im and Ketuvim.
- The Sefer Torah is handwritten by a scribe, kept in the ark and read with a yad; it is read weekly and the cycle ends at Simchat Torah.
- The oral Torah, written down in the Mishnah (about 200 CE) and the Talmud, explains and applies the written Torah.
- Rabbis' commentary, such as Rashi's, helps Jews to interpret scripture.
- Jews use scripture in worship, study, blessings, law and rites of passage.
- The Tanakh shares most books with the Christian Old Testament, but Jews do not accept the New Testament.
:::
