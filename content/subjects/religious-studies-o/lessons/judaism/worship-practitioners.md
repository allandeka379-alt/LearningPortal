## Introduction

Think of a school assembly hall: it has a stage, chairs, a notice board and a person who leads. A synagogue is also a hall for gathering, but its design tells a story. Everything in it faces one direction, there is a special light that never goes out, and the most important object is not an altar but a cupboard of scrolls. If you visited a synagogue in Harare or Bulawayo as a guest, knowing what the features mean would help you follow the service and answer questions about it.

This lesson covers the synagogue, daily prayer, the people who lead worship, the clothing and symbols that Jews use, and worship in the home. You should already know that the Torah is kept in the ark and read aloud. The Sabbath and the festivals are covered in the next lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Synagogue"}
A Jewish place of assembly, prayer and study. In Hebrew it is called *beit knesset* ("house of assembly"); many Jews also say *shul*.
:::

:::definition{term="Minyan"}
The group of ten adult Jews required for certain prayers. In Orthodox practice it means ten men; many Conservative and Reform synagogues count women as well.
:::

:::definition{term="Rabbi"}
A teacher and leader of a Jewish community, trained in the Torah and Jewish law. The word means "my teacher".
:::

:::definition{term="Cantor (hazzan)"}
A person trained in singing who leads the congregation in prayer.
:::

## The synagogue

Synagogues began during the exile and became the centre of Jewish life when the Second Temple was destroyed. They serve three purposes: **prayer**, **study** and **community** (a hall for meetings and teaching). The Jewish people do not need a synagogue in order to pray, but they must gather for communal prayer when they can.

| Feature | What it is | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ark (*aron hakodesh*) | A cabinet on the wall that faces Jerusalem, where the Torah scrolls are kept | Honours the Torah; the direction of prayer is toward Jerusalem |
| Torah scrolls | Handwritten scrolls, usually with covers and a crown | God's teaching, central to worship |
| Bimah | A raised platform or desk where the Torah is read | Gives honour to the reading |
| Ner tamid | A lamp that is always lit above the ark | Recalls the lamp that burned in the Temple and the constant presence of God |
| Menorah | A lamp-stand with seven branches, often seen in the building | Recalls the Temple lamp-stand |
| Separate seating | In Orthodox synagogues, men and women sit apart, usually separated by a screen or in a gallery | Follows tradition; Conservative and Reform synagogues have mixed seating |

There are no statues or pictures of God in a synagogue, because Jews teach that God has no body and cannot be represented.

## Daily prayer and the minyan

Traditional Jews pray three times a day: **shacharit** (morning), **minchah** (afternoon) and **maariv** (evening). The prayers are in the prayer book, the **siddur**, and include the Shema and the Amidah, a central prayer said standing. Some prayers, such as the reading of the Torah and the Kaddish, require a **minyan**. On Shabbat there are longer services. A person may pray alone, but praying with others is encouraged.

The Shabbat morning service is the longest of the week. It includes the opening prayers and psalms, the Shema, the Amidah, the reading from the Torah with a passage from the prophets (the haftarah), a sermon or talk by the rabbi, and closing prayers and songs. Many congregations then share a kiddush, a light meal after the service, where people talk and welcome visitors. Because the service follows a fixed order, a visitor who knows the pattern can follow it even without understanding Hebrew.

:::example{title="A weekday morning prayer"}
At 6 a.m. in a Bulawayo synagogue, nine men and a boy of thirteen who has just had his bar mitzvah are waiting. With his coming of age the boy counts as the tenth member of the minyan, and so the prayers that need ten adults can begin. The group wear their prayer shawls and tefillin, say the Shema and then the Amidah. The example shows why communal prayer matters: each person is needed.
:::

## Religious leaders

| Leader | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rabbi | Teacher, interpreter of Jewish law, counsellor; leads services and sermons, takes part in weddings and funerals | Has no priestly powers; a rabbi is a teacher, not a priest. Reform and Conservative Judaism ordain women; Orthodox Judaism does not |
| Cantor (hazzan) | Leads the singing and chanting of prayers | Trained in the traditional melodies |
| Kohanim (priests) | Descendants of Aaron who served in the Temple | Today they have a few honours, such as being called first to the Torah |
| Levites | Members of the tribe of Levi who assisted the priests | Today they are also called to the Torah in a set order |
| Lay members | Ordinary Jews who read from the Torah and lead parts of the service | Judaism has no rule that only clergy may lead prayer |

## Attire and symbols

| Item | What it is | Why Jews use it |
|---|---|---|
| Kippah (yarmulke) | A small cap | A sign of respect for God above; worn by men and, in some groups, by women |
| Tallit | A prayer shawl with fringes (*tzitzit*) at the corners | Recalls the command in Numbers 15:37-41 to remember the commandments |
| Tefillin | Two small leather boxes with Shema verses, bound with straps to the arm and head | Obey the command in Deuteronomy 6 to bind the words as a sign; worn in weekday morning prayer |
| Star of David (*magen David*) | A six-pointed star | A well-known symbol of the Jewish people |
| Menorah | A seven-branched lamp-stand | Recalls the lamp-stand in the Temple |

:::example{title="Putting on tefillin, step by step"}
Rudo's Jewish friend David, who is thirteen, wraps a strap around his left arm, so that the small box rests near his heart. He places the second box on his forehead and says a blessing. He explains that the boxes contain the Shema verses, and the position of the straps shows that he wants to love God with his mind and his heart. The example shows how a practice follows from a verse in scripture and expresses a belief.
:::

## Worship in the home

Worship is not limited to the synagogue. The home has a **mezuzah** at its doors, and on Friday evening the family lights candles, blesses the wine and bread and eats a Shabbat meal. Blessings are said before eating and at other moments. Parents teach their children prayers and the Shema. For this reason, Jews call the home a "small sanctuary". Home worship is covered more fully in the lesson on the Jewish home and food laws.

## Differences among Jewish groups

| Feature | Many Orthodox synagogues | Many Reform synagogues |
|---|---|---|
| Seating | Men and women separate | Mixed |
| Rabbi | A man | A man or a woman |
| Language | Mostly Hebrew | Hebrew and local language |
| Attire | Men wear kippah and tallit | Varies; some women also wear them |
| Music | Singing without musical instruments on the Sabbath | Some use an organ or other instruments |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a rabbi a priest"}
A rabbi is a teacher and not a priest. The Jewish priests (kohanim) served in the Temple and have only a few honours today.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Jews worship the Torah"}
Jews honour the Torah as God's teaching; they worship God alone. Say "they treat the Torah with respect".
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the tefillin or tallit without the reason"}
Explain what each item is and which verse or belief it expresses.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The synagogue is a place of prayer, study and community; its main features are the ark, bimah, ner tamid and Torah scrolls.
- Jews pray morning, afternoon and evening; certain prayers need a minyan of ten adults.
- A rabbi is a teacher; the cantor leads singing; kohanim and Levites have a few honours from Temple times.
- The kippah, tallit and tefillin follow commands in scripture; the Star of David and menorah are symbols.
- Worship in the home (mezuzah, Shabbat meal, blessings) is as important as the synagogue.
- Orthodox and Reform practice differ on seating, women as rabbis and music.
:::
