## Introduction

Every spring, Jewish families in Harare and Bulawayo sit down to a special meal, the seder, and tell a story that is thousands of years old. It is the story of how the Israelites were slaves in Egypt and were led to freedom. Children ask questions, the story is told again, and everyone at the table is encouraged to feel as if they themselves had left Egypt. No other event is remembered as often in Judaism as the **Exodus** (the going out).

Before reading this lesson you should know who Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph were, because the Exodus begins with the descendants of Jacob living in Egypt. You will learn the story as the Book of Exodus tells it, the meaning of the covenant at Sinai, and why Jews say that these events made them a people. The festival of Passover itself is studied in the lesson on the Sabbath and festivals, and the commandments as moral teaching in the lesson on Jewish ethics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Exodus"}
The departure of the Israelites from slavery in Egypt under the leadership of Moses, described in the Book of Exodus. The word means "going out".
:::

:::definition{term="Passover (Pesach)"}
The festival that remembers how God "passed over" the Israelite houses in the last plague and freed the people from Egypt (Exodus 12).
:::

:::definition{term="Ten Commandments"}
The ten statements of God given at Sinai (Exodus 20; Deuteronomy 5), the heart of the Sinai covenant. Jews call them the Decalogue, or the ten sayings.
:::

:::definition{term="Tabernacle and ark"}
The **tabernacle** was the portable sanctuary built in the wilderness. The **ark** was the chest kept inside it that held the tablets of the law.
:::

## Slavery in Egypt and the call of Moses

Exodus 1 tells that after Joseph's time a new Pharaoh feared the growing number of Israelites and made them slaves, forcing them to work on building projects. He then ordered that baby boys be killed. According to **Exodus 2**, a baby, **Moses**, was hidden by his mother in a basket on the river and found by Pharaoh's daughter, who raised him in the palace. As an adult he killed an Egyptian who was beating an Israelite, and he fled to Midian.

In **Exodus 3** Moses is looking after sheep when he sees a bush that burns without being burnt up. God speaks to him from the bush, says that the suffering of the Israelites has been heard, and sends Moses to bring them out of Egypt. Moses protests that he is not a good speaker, and his brother **Aaron** is appointed to speak for him (Exodus 4). Jews see this as the moment when God is revealed as a God who hears the cry of the oppressed.

## The plagues and the first Passover

Moses and Aaron asked Pharaoh to let the people go. When he refused, a series of ten plagues struck Egypt (Exodus 7 to 12), including water turned to blood, frogs, darkness and finally the death of the firstborn. Before the last plague, God told the Israelites to prepare a meal in a hurry, to kill a lamb, to mark their doorposts with its blood and to eat bread without yeast. The plague would then pass over the marked houses. This is the first **Passover** (Exodus 12). After this Pharaoh let the people go.

:::keyfact
Two things to remember from Exodus 12: the Israelites ate **unleavened bread (matzah)** because they left in haste, and the festival is named **Pesach** because God "passed over" their houses.
:::

## Crossing the sea and the wilderness

Pharaoh changed his mind and pursued the Israelites to the edge of the sea. In **Exodus 14** the waters opened, the Israelites crossed on dry ground, and the pursuing army was caught by the returning waters. Miriam, the sister of Moses, led the women in a song of thanks (Exodus 15). The Hebrew name of the sea is often translated Red Sea or Sea of Reeds.

In the wilderness the people complained of hunger and thirst. The Book of Exodus says that God provided bread called manna (Exodus 16) and water from a rock (Exodus 17). The journey lasted forty years.

## The covenant at Sinai

In the third month the Israelites came to **Mount Sinai** (Exodus 19). There God spoke, and the people agreed to keep the covenant. The **Ten Commandments** (**Exodus 20**; repeated in **Deuteronomy 5**) include the call to worship only one God, not to make or worship idols, to keep the Sabbath, to honour father and mother, and not to murder, steal, commit adultery, give false testimony or covet. Jewish and Christian traditions number them in slightly different ways, so in an answer list the commandments by their content rather than by number.

Jews believe that at Sinai the whole people received the Torah. Many other laws were given there too, and Judaism counts them as 613 commandments (mitzvot). The people broke the covenant by making a golden calf while Moses was on the mountain (Exodus 32), but the covenant was renewed. Jews therefore see Sinai as the second great covenant, after the covenant with Abraham.

## The tabernacle and the ark

God also gave instructions for a portable sanctuary, the **tabernacle** (Exodus 25 to 31, 35 to 40), a tent where the people could worship as they travelled. Its most important object was the **ark of the covenant**, a chest that held the stone tablets. Priests from the tribe of Levi, descended from Aaron, served in it. It is the forerunner of the Temple in Jerusalem and of the ark in a modern synagogue where Torah scrolls are kept.

## The wilderness years and entry into Canaan

Because the generation that left Egypt lacked faith (Numbers 13 to 14), they spent forty years in the wilderness. Moses died within sight of the promised land (Deuteronomy 34). **Joshua** became leader, and the Book of Joshua describes how the Israelites crossed the river Jordan and entered Canaan, including the fall of Jericho (Joshua 1 to 6). The land was divided among the tribes.

## Why the Exodus is central to Jewish identity

:::example{title="Telling the story at the seder"}
At the seder in Bulawayo, Miriam's youngest cousin asks why this night is different from all other nights. The family answers by reading from the Haggadah, eating matzah and bitter herbs, and tasting salt water. A saying in the Mishnah teaches that each person in every generation should see themselves as if they had personally come out of Egypt. The Exodus is therefore not only remembered but re-lived each year.
:::

Jews give four reasons why the Exodus is central:

- **It created the people.** Slaves became a nation with a covenant and a law.
- **It shows the character of God.** God is the redeemer who frees the oppressed.
- **It shapes ethics.** The Torah repeatedly reminds the people "you were strangers in Egypt", which is a reason to treat foreigners, the poor and workers fairly.
- **It is remembered in worship.** Passover, the Sabbath prayers and daily prayer refer to it.

:::example{title="From memory to conduct"}
Thandeka and David compare their lessons. Thandeka says that Moses led people to freedom and that this is why the story matters. David adds that for Jews the story also gives a rule for daily life: because the Israelites knew what slavery felt like, the Torah tells them to treat a foreign worker and a poor neighbour with justice. Together they explain the Exodus as both a memory and a command.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Exodus covenant with the covenant of Abraham"}
Abraham's covenant promised land and descendants and was marked by circumcision. The Sinai covenant gave the people the Torah and commandments. Name both and show how they connect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Numbering the commandments"}
Different traditions number the Ten Commandments differently. Give each by its content.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only the plagues"}
A question on the Exodus needs the whole sequence: slavery, call of Moses, Passover, crossing, Sinai. The plagues are one part.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): narrate"}
For *describe / narrate the story of the Exodus*, tell it in order: slavery (Exodus 1), Moses' call (Exodus 3), plagues and Passover (Exodus 7-12), crossing (Exodus 14), Sinai (Exodus 19-20). One developed sentence per stage earns marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): significance"}
For *explain the significance of the Exodus for Jews*, link the story to Passover today, to Jewish identity as a people freed by God, and to the duty to protect the stranger.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Israelites were enslaved in Egypt; God called Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3) to lead them out.
- The ten plagues, the first Passover and the crossing of the sea freed the people (Exodus 7-14).
- At Sinai the people received the Ten Commandments and the Torah in a covenant (Exodus 19-20; Deuteronomy 5).
- The tabernacle and the ark were the portable place of worship in the wilderness.
- After forty years, Joshua led the Israelites into Canaan.
- The Exodus is central to Jewish identity: it made the people, shows God as a redeemer, shapes ethics and is retold each Passover.
:::
