## Introduction

Imagine that your neighbour in Harare is Jewish. On Friday evening he hurries home before sunset, and on his front door you notice a small case fixed to the doorpost. If you ask him what Jews believe, he may begin with one sentence that he has said since childhood: the Lord is one. Almost everything else in Judaism grows from that belief.

In this lesson you learn the main beliefs of Judaism as Jews themselves explain them. Remember that Judaism is practised in different ways: Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews agree on many beliefs but understand some of them differently, and the lesson says so where it matters. You should already know the covenant with Abraham and the giving of the Torah at Sinai. The Jewish scriptures themselves are studied in a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monotheism"}
Belief in only one God. Judaism was one of the first religions to teach this clearly.
:::

:::definition{term="Shema"}
The central Jewish declaration of faith, from Deuteronomy 6:4-9, beginning with the word "Hear" (Shema in Hebrew).
:::

:::definition{term="Torah"}
Literally "teaching" or "instruction". In its narrow sense it is the five books of Moses; in its wider sense it means all Jewish teaching and law.
:::

:::definition{term="Messiah (mashiach)"}
Meaning "anointed one": the figure Jews expect to bring a time of peace and justice.
:::

:::keyfact
**Shema:** Deuteronomy 6:4-9. **Thirteen Principles of Faith:** written by Maimonides (Rambam), a great Jewish scholar of the twelfth century CE. **Olam ha-ba:** "the world to come".
:::

## One God and the Shema

The first and most important Jewish belief is that there is **one God**, who has no body, is eternal and is the only one to be worshipped. This is expressed in the **Shema** (Deuteronomy 6:4-9). Paraphrased, it says: listen, Israel, the Lord is our God, the Lord is one; love the Lord with all your heart, soul and strength; keep these words in your heart; teach them to your children; talk about them at home and on the road, in the morning and at night; fix them as a sign on your hand and between your eyes; and write them on your doorposts.

Jews obey these verses in practice. They recite the Shema morning and evening, wear **tefillin** in weekday morning prayer, and fix a **mezuzah**, a small case with the verses inside, on the doorposts of the home. The Shema is the last prayer for many Jews at the end of life. A belief in one God therefore appears in the daily routines of Jewish life.

## God as creator, just and merciful

Genesis 1 describes God as the creator of the universe, who made the world and called it good. Jews believe that God is **just**: God cares for justice and expects human beings to do right. They also believe that God is **merciful**: in Exodus 34:6-7 God is described as compassionate and gracious, slow to anger and full of kindness. These two qualities are held together. On Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, Jews ask God for forgiveness, trusting that God is merciful as well as just.

## The covenant and the chosen people

From Abraham to Sinai, Judaism teaches that God made a covenant with the Jewish people. Jews speak of themselves as the **chosen people** (see Deuteronomy 7). Most Jews explain this not as being better than others, but as being given a **responsibility**: to keep the Torah, to live by God's ways and to be, as the prophets say, a light to others. The Jewish scriptures also teach that God cares for all nations, and that righteous people of any nation have a share in the world to come.

:::example{title="Choosing or being chosen?"}
Nokuthula asks her Jewish classmate Rachel whether Jews think they are better than everyone else. Rachel answers that the word "chosen" is about duty, not rank: the Torah gives Jews many commandments to keep, such as justice, charity and the Sabbath, so being chosen means being asked to do more, not to be proud. A good examination answer states the belief and then explains its meaning in this way.
:::

## The Torah as God's teaching

Jews believe that the Torah comes from God, given to Moses and the people at Sinai, and that it contains the laws and teaching by which God wishes people to live. It is studied throughout life. Orthodox Jews believe that the whole Torah was given by God and that its commandments (mitzvot) remain binding. Many Reform Jews respect it as inspired by God but think that people today must decide how to interpret and apply it. Both agree that the Torah is the centre of Jewish life.

## The Messiah

Jews believe that history is moving toward a time of peace, justice and knowledge of God. The **Messiah** is the person who is expected, in traditional teaching a descendant of King David, to bring this time. Orthodox Jews expect a personal Messiah. Many Reform Jews speak instead of a **messianic age** that human beings and God will bring about together. Jews do not believe that the Messiah has already come; this is one of the main differences between Judaism and Christianity, and it should be stated plainly and respectfully.

## Life after death

Judaism teaches less about the afterlife than about this life, and Jews hold different views. Many traditional Jews believe in **olam ha-ba**, the world to come, and in the **resurrection of the dead** at the end of time. Others believe that the soul lives on after death, and some stress that the main task is to do good in this world. Jewish teaching says that what matters most is how one lives, because God is just and merciful.

## Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of Faith

In the twelfth century CE the scholar **Moses Maimonides** (Rambam) set out thirteen principles of faith, which many Jews know in a hymn called *Yigdal*. In outline:

| Group | Principles |
|---|---|
| About God | God exists and is the creator; God is one; God has no body; God is eternal; only God should be worshipped |
| About revelation | Prophecy is true; Moses is the greatest of the prophets; the Torah is from God and will not be changed |
| About justice and the future | God knows human actions; God rewards and punishes justly; the Messiah will come; the dead will be raised |

Not every Jew accepts all thirteen in the same way. The principles are an authoritative summary rather than a creed that every Jew must recite.

## Differences within Judaism

| Belief | Many Orthodox Jews | Many Reform Jews |
|---|---|---|
| The Torah | The whole Torah is from God and binding | Inspired by God; each generation interprets it |
| The Messiah | A personal Messiah of the line of David | A messianic age of peace |
| The afterlife | World to come; resurrection of the dead | Varied; emphasis on this life |

:::example{title="Belief in practice"}
David, a Jewish boy in Harare, says the Shema before sleep and his father touches the mezuzah when entering the house. Each action reminds them that God is one, that the family is part of a covenant, and that the commandments are to be remembered "when you lie down and when you rise". Beliefs in Judaism are expressed in what people do as well as in what they say.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Jews believe they are better than others"}
The chosen people means chosen for responsibility under the covenant. Explain that meaning.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that all Jews believe the same"}
Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews differ on the Torah, the Messiah and the afterlife. Use "many Orthodox Jews" and "many Reform Jews".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Torah and Tanakh"}
The Torah is the five books of Moses (and, in a wider sense, all Jewish teaching). The Tanakh is the whole Hebrew Bible.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe or outline"}
For *outline the main beliefs of Judaism*, give separate beliefs (one God, creator, covenant, Torah, Messiah, life after death) and add one detail to each, such as the Shema or the mezuzah.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}
For *explain why the Shema is important*, say what it states (one God), how Jews use it (daily recitation, mezuzah) and what that shows about the centrality of the belief.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Jews believe in one God, expressed in the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4-9), recited daily and written in the mezuzah.
- God is the creator, just and merciful.
- The covenant and the idea of a chosen people mean responsibility to keep the Torah, not superiority.
- The Torah is God's teaching; Orthodox and Reform Jews understand its authority differently.
- Jews await the Messiah; views of the afterlife (olam ha-ba, resurrection) vary.
- Maimonides summarised Jewish belief in thirteen principles.
:::
