## Introduction

Think of the Christian churches in your town: Catholic, Methodist, Pentecostal and apostolic churches share a faith in Jesus but worship in different ways. Judaism is like this too. Two Jewish families on the same street may both light Shabbat candles, and yet one family may drive to the synagogue on Saturday and the other may walk, one may keep strict kosher rules and the other may not, one may have a woman rabbi and the other may not.

In this lesson you learn the main groups within Judaism and how they differ. The lesson **does not rank the groups**: each is described as its members understand it. Words such as "many" and "most" are used because there are differences within each group as well. You should already know the Torah, the synagogue and the Sabbath. The history of the diaspora is in an earlier lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Movement (denomination)"}
A group within Judaism with its own approach to the Torah, worship and daily life. The main movements are Orthodox, Conservative and Reform.
:::

:::definition{term="Halakhah"}
Jewish law: the body of rules based on the Torah and the Talmud which guide daily life.
:::

:::definition{term="Ashkenazi and Sephardi"}
Two main traditions of Jewish culture. **Ashkenazi** Jews come from the Jewish communities of central and eastern Europe; **Sephardi** Jews come from Spain, Portugal and the Mediterranean world. Each has its own customs, music and prayer melodies.
:::

:::definition{term="Secular Jew"}
A person who regards themselves as Jewish by family, culture or history, but who does not follow the religious practices of Judaism.
:::

## Orthodox Judaism

Orthodox Jews believe that the Torah, written and oral, was given by God at Sinai and that halakhah remains binding. They keep the Sabbath and the dietary laws strictly, and they hold that the Torah's commandments apply today as they did in the past. Within Orthodoxy there are several streams:

- **Modern Orthodox** Jews keep halakhah while taking part in modern society, studying at universities and working in professions.
- **Haredi** ("ultra-Orthodox") Jews live more apart from modern society, with distinctive dress and a strong emphasis on Torah study.
- **Hasidic** Jews form a movement within Haredi Judaism that began in eastern Europe in the eighteenth century and stresses joy in prayer, devotion and loyalty to a spiritual leader. They are often recognised by their dress.

In Orthodox synagogues, men and women sit separately, the service is mostly in Hebrew, and the rabbi is a man.

## Conservative (Masorti) Judaism

Conservative Judaism, called **Masorti** ("traditional") in some countries, developed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It holds that halakhah is binding but may develop over time through study by rabbis. Conservative Jews keep the Sabbath and many keep kosher, though with some changes of detail compared with Orthodoxy. Many Conservative synagogues have mixed seating, count women in the minyan and ordain women as rabbis.

## Reform, Progressive and Liberal Judaism

Reform Judaism began in Germany in the early nineteenth century and is also called **Progressive** or **Liberal** Judaism in some countries. Reform Jews believe that Judaism can change with the times. Many Reform Jews regard the Torah as inspired by God but written by human beings, and they give each person responsibility for deciding which laws to keep. Reform services use both Hebrew and the local language, seating is mixed, women and men have equal roles, and women may be rabbis. Many Reform Jews keep the Sabbath and festivals and give weight to the ethical commandments of the prophets, but treat ritual laws such as the food laws as a matter of personal choice.

## Secular Jews

Many Jews describe their Jewishness mainly as identity, family and culture. They may light Hanukkah candles or attend a seder, and they may support Jewish charities, but they do not follow the religious law. A secular Jew is still a Jew. Their existence reminds students that "Jewish" is a word for both a religion and a people.

## Ashkenazi and Sephardi traditions

Alongside the movements, Jews belong to cultural traditions. The **Ashkenazi** tradition developed among Jews of Germany and central and eastern Europe, who spoke Yiddish, a language with Hebrew and German elements. The **Sephardi** tradition developed among Jews of Spain and Portugal, who spoke Ladino, a language of Spanish origin, and who after the expulsion from Spain in 1492 settled around the Mediterranean and beyond. Some groups from the Middle East and North Africa are called **Mizrahi**. The differences concern pronunciation of Hebrew, melodies, foods and some customs, for example the foods eaten at Pesach. Both traditions can be found in Orthodox, Conservative and Reform synagogues.

:::keyfact
Orthodox, Conservative and Reform are **movements**. They are listed here from the most to the least strict in applying halakhah, which is a description of practice and not a ranking of value. Ashkenazi and Sephardi are **cultural traditions**, not movements.
:::

## Comparing the groups

| Question | Orthodox | Conservative (Masorti) | Reform / Progressive |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Torah | Given by God at Sinai; binding | Binding, but may develop through study | Inspired by God; each generation interprets |
| Sabbath | Strict rest, no driving or electricity use in many communities | Rest observed, with some differences | Observed, with personal choice about details |
| Food laws | Kept strictly | Kept by many, with some flexibility | Personal choice |
| Synagogue | Separate seating; mostly Hebrew | Often mixed seating | Mixed seating; Hebrew and local language |
| Women | Men are rabbis; women do not count in the minyan | Women count in the minyan; may be rabbis | Equal roles; women are rabbis |

:::example{title="Two synagogues, one city"}
Nokuthula is writing a project on Judaism and visits two synagogues. At the first she sits in a gallery with the women and hears the service in Hebrew led by a male rabbi. At the second, families sit together, a woman is the rabbi and some prayers are in English. She asks Rachel, a Jewish friend, which is the "real" one. Rachel explains that both are real: they follow the same Torah but understand how to apply it differently, and that Jews hold different views about which approach is best. Nokuthula's project describes both fairly, without taking a side.
:::

:::example{title="Who counts as a Jew?"}
David tells Chipo that the traditional rule is that a person is Jewish if their mother is Jewish or if they have converted to Judaism. Orthodox and Conservative movements follow this rule. Some Reform communities also accept a person with a Jewish father, if the person is raised as a Jew. Chipo learns that questions of identity are also discussed within the Jewish community, and that a Religious Studies answer reports the different views without judging them.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ranking the movements"}
Do not write that one movement is "the real Judaism" or "more correct". Describe how each understands the Torah and practice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing movements and traditions"}
Orthodox, Conservative and Reform are movements. Ashkenazi and Sephardi are cultural traditions that exist within the movements.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that secular Jews are not Jews"}
A secular Jew belongs to the Jewish people by birth or family, even if they do not practise the religion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): outline or describe"}
For *outline the differences between Orthodox and Reform Jews*, choose three areas (the Torah, worship, the role of women, food laws) and give one point for each group in the same area.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}
For *explain why Jews have different practices*, give two or three reasons (different views of the authority of the Torah, response to modern life, history in different countries) and avoid judging.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Orthodox Judaism holds that the Torah is from God and halakhah is binding; it includes Modern Orthodox, Haredi and Hasidic streams.
- Conservative (Masorti) Judaism holds halakhah binding but developing; many congregations have mixed seating and women rabbis.
- Reform, Progressive and Liberal Judaism let each person decide which laws to keep and have equal roles for women.
- Secular Jews identify as Jewish by family and culture but do not follow the religious law.
- Ashkenazi and Sephardi are cultural traditions with their own languages (Yiddish, Ladino), melodies and customs.
- The groups are described without ranking.
:::
