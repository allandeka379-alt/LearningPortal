## Introduction

A Jewish family in Harare spends Friday afternoon cleaning the house, cooking the meal and getting dressed. As the sun sets, a mother lights two candles and the home becomes quiet. The telephone is put away, the table is laid with a white cloth, and for the next day the family will not go to work, school or the shops. This is **Shabbat**, the weekly day of rest, and it is the heartbeat of Jewish life.

Jews also keep a yearly cycle of festivals that remember events in their history and the seasons of the land. In this lesson you learn how Shabbat is kept and why, and what each major festival means and how it is observed. You should already know the Exodus and the covenant at Sinai, because many festivals remember them. Rites of passage are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Shabbat (Sabbath)"}
The seventh day of the week, a day of rest and holiness, kept from sunset on Friday to nightfall on Saturday.
:::

:::definition{term="Kiddush"}
The blessing said over a cup of wine that sanctifies Shabbat and festivals.
:::

:::definition{term="Havdalah"}
"Separation": the ceremony at the end of Shabbat that separates the holy day from the ordinary week, with wine, spices and a braided candle.
:::

:::definition{term="Seder"}
The ritual meal on the first night(s) of Pesach, at which the story of the Exodus is told from the Haggadah.
:::

:::keyfact
Shabbat: **Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall**. Basis: **Genesis 2:1-3** (God rested on the seventh day) and **Exodus 20:8-11** (keep the Sabbath day holy). Jewish days begin at sunset.
:::

## Shabbat

Genesis 2:1-3 says that God finished creation and rested on the seventh day, blessing and making it holy. In **Exodus 20:8-11** the fourth commandment tells the people to remember the Sabbath day and keep it holy, and to rest from work, with their household and workers. Jews therefore rest to imitate God and to remember creation. In Deuteronomy 5 the Sabbath is also linked to the freedom from slavery in Egypt, so rest is a sign of freedom.

### How Shabbat is kept

| Stage | What happens | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation (Friday) | House cleaned and meals cooked before sunset | Respect for the day |
| Candles | The family lights two candles shortly before sunset | Welcomes Shabbat and brings peace to the home |
| Synagogue (Friday evening) | Prayers welcoming Shabbat | Community begins the day together |
| Kiddush and the meal | Blessing over wine; two loaves of bread (*challah*); the family meal | Sanctifies the day and brings the family together |
| Saturday morning | Synagogue service with Torah reading; a rest, study and time with family | Worship and rest |
| Havdalah | At nightfall: wine, spices and a braided candle | Ends Shabbat and prepares for the week |

On Shabbat, observant Jews refrain from ordinary work, such as cooking, writing, lighting a fire or travelling by vehicle (in Orthodox practice). Reform and many Conservative Jews apply the rules less strictly. For all, the focus is rest, prayer, study and family.

:::example{title="Friday evening in a Jewish home"}
In Bulawayo, Miriam's mother lights two candles and covers her eyes while she says a blessing. Her father says kiddush over the wine, and the family washes their hands and shares two loaves of challah. They sing, discuss the Torah reading for the week, and bless the children. Miriam explains that the candles bring peace to the home, the wine marks the day as holy, and the two loaves recall the double portion of manna in the wilderness. Everything has a reason in scripture or history.
:::

## The pilgrim festivals

Three festivals are called pilgrim festivals because in Temple times Jews travelled to Jerusalem to celebrate them.

- **Pesach (Passover)** falls in spring and lasts seven or eight days. It remembers the Exodus from Egypt. The family holds a **seder** on the first night(s), reading the Haggadah, eating matzah (unleavened bread), bitter herbs and other symbolic foods, and drinking four cups of wine. Leavened bread (*chametz*) is removed from the house for the festival.
- **Shavuot** falls seven weeks after Pesach. It celebrates the giving of the Torah at Sinai and, in Temple times, the first harvest. Jews study Torah, read the Ten Commandments, and many eat dairy foods.
- **Sukkot** falls in autumn, five days after Yom Kippur. It remembers the shelters in which the Israelites lived in the wilderness and is also a harvest festival. Families build a **sukkah**, a hut with a roof of branches, eat their meals in it, and wave the **four species** (palm, myrtle, willow and citron) in the synagogue.

## The High Holy Days

- **Rosh Hashanah** ("head of the year") is the Jewish new year, in autumn. The **shofar** (ram's horn) is blown in the synagogue to call people to repent. Families eat apples dipped in honey for a sweet year. The binding of Isaac (Genesis 22) is read.
- **Yom Kippur**, the **Day of Atonement**, comes ten days later. It is the holiest day of the year. Jews **fast** (no food or drink) for about 25 hours, spend the day in prayer in the synagogue, and ask forgiveness from God and from people they have wronged. The period between the two is called the Ten Days of Repentance.

:::example{title="Yom Kippur in a synagogue"}
David's grandfather goes to the synagogue dressed in white, which stands for purity. He does not eat or drink all day, and he stays for the long services. He says that before Yom Kippur he telephones people he has hurt to apologise, since Jewish teaching says that God forgives wrongs against God, but a person must seek pardon from those he has wronged. The fast ends at nightfall with the sounding of the shofar.
:::

## Hanukkah and Purim

- **Hanukkah** falls in winter and lasts eight nights. It remembers the rededication of the Temple in Jerusalem in the second century BCE, after the Maccabees led a revolt against a foreign ruler who had banned Jewish worship. According to tradition, a small amount of oil lasted eight days. Families light a nine-branched lamp, the **hanukkiah**, adding one light each night, and give gifts and eat foods fried in oil. Hanukkah is not a pilgrim festival.
- **Purim** falls in early spring and remembers how Queen Esther and her cousin Mordecai saved the Jews of Persia from a plot by Haman (the Book of Esther). The scroll of Esther is read aloud in the synagogue, people give gifts of food and to the poor, and children dress up.

| Festival | When | What it remembers | Customs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pesach | Spring | The Exodus | Seder, matzah, no chametz |
| Shavuot | Seven weeks later | The giving of the Torah | Study, dairy foods |
| Sukkot | Autumn | Shelters in the wilderness | Sukkah, four species |
| Rosh Hashanah | Autumn | New year, judgement | Shofar, apples and honey |
| Yom Kippur | Ten days later | Repentance, forgiveness | Fast, prayer |
| Hanukkah | Winter | Rededication of the Temple | Hanukkiah, eight nights |
| Purim | Early spring | Esther saves the Jews | Reading the scroll, gifts |

The Jewish calendar follows the moon and is adjusted to the sun, so festivals fall on the same Hebrew dates but move in the civil calendar.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing Shabbat and Sunday"}
Shabbat is Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall and is kept by Jews. Sunday is the Christian day of worship. Seventh-day Adventists also keep Saturday.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur and Hanukkah"}
Rosh Hashanah is the new year, Yom Kippur is the fast of atonement, Hanukkah is the winter festival of lights with eight nights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing customs without meaning"}
For each festival state what it remembers and how a custom expresses it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Shabbat runs from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall; it is based on Genesis 2 and Exodus 20.
- Shabbat customs include candles, kiddush, challah, the synagogue and havdalah.
- Pesach, Shavuot and Sukkot are pilgrim festivals remembering the Exodus, Sinai and the wilderness.
- Rosh Hashanah (new year, shofar) and Yom Kippur (fast of atonement) are the High Holy Days.
- Hanukkah remembers the rededication of the Temple; Purim remembers Esther.
- The Jewish calendar follows the moon, so festival dates move in the civil calendar.
:::
