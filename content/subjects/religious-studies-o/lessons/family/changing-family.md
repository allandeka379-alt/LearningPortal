## Introduction

Chipo's grandmother grew up in a village in Buhera where almost every relative lived within walking distance. Chipo lives in Harare with her mother. Her father works in another country and sends money and messages every week. Her grandmother follows the family WhatsApp group on a borrowed phone. Within three generations the shape of the family, the way relatives communicate and the pressures on them have changed greatly.

This lesson looks at how culture and the family are changing, how religions respond, how family life links to national values and what can strengthen families. It builds on the earlier lessons on family types, values, inter-dependency and identity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Culture"}
The way of life of a group of people: language, beliefs, customs, values, food, dress and arts passed from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Migration"}
The movement of people from one place to another, within a country (for example from a village to a town) or between countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Diaspora"}
People who have left their country of origin and live abroad but keep links with it.
:::

:::definition{term="Cohabitation"}
Two people living together as a couple without being married.
:::

## Causes of change

| Cause | How it changes culture and the family |
| --- | --- |
| Urbanisation and migration | Families live in small flats and townhouses; relatives live far apart; the extended family is stretched; remittances (money sent home) support relatives |
| The diaspora | Parents or children live abroad; grandparents may raise grandchildren; families keep in touch by phone and video |
| Education | Young people gain skills and independence; they may delay marriage, and learn ideas that differ from those of their elders |
| Technology and social media | Faster communication and new ways to keep contact; also exposure to different values, and concerns about time spent online and about safety |
| Economic pressure | Long working hours, unemployment and the cost of living reduce time for family and make it hard to support relatives |
| HIV and AIDS and other illnesses | Where adults have died, grandparents or older children raise children; child-headed and single-parent families have increased |
| Changing relationships | More single-parent families, blended families and cohabiting couples than in the past |

:::example{title="A family spread over three places"}
Farai works in Johannesburg, his wife Nyasha and their two children live in Mutare, and his mother lives in Buhera.

1. Farai sends money each month for school fees and groceries.
2. Nyasha organises the children's schooling and takes them to see their grandmother in the school holidays.
3. The family uses a WhatsApp group to share news and photos.

The family has stayed close, but its daily life is very different from that of a family that lives together in one homestead.
:::

## Changing family forms

Several family forms have become more common: single-parent families, blended families, child-headed families and, in some places, cohabiting couples. These families often face difficulties, such as a lack of income, or lack of an adult role model, but many also show strength and resilience. The community, relatives and religious groups can help.

## Religious responses

Religions respond in several ways.

| Religion | Response (in outline) |
| --- | --- |
| Indigenous Religion | Elders and traditional leaders remind families of their duties to relatives and the ancestors; many families keep ceremonies even when they live far away, sometimes adapting them |
| Judaism | Communities encourage family life and Shabbat meals, and support families in need through community organisations |
| Christianity | Churches run marriage and parenting programmes, support single parents and orphans, and counsel couples. Churches differ in what they teach about divorce and remarriage |
| Islam | Mosques and communities teach the duties of spouses, parents and children, and zakat helps families in need. Views on matters such as divorce are set out in Islamic law, applied differently in different communities |

Within each religion there are different views about change, and believers discuss how to apply their teachings to new circumstances. A good answer says "some Christians" or "many Muslims" rather than writing one view for the whole religion.

## The family and national values

National values such as **unhu/ubuntu** (humanity towards others), **patriotism** (love of one's country), **hard work** and **respect for human rights** are first learnt in the family. When families teach children honesty, respect and service, they prepare citizens who contribute to the nation. When the family is under strain, schools, churches, mosques and community groups often help to pass on these values.

Respect for human rights also applies within the family. The Constitution protects the equal rights of women and men (section 56 and section 80) and the rights of children (section 81). The Domestic Violence Act (2007) protects family members from abuse. Marriage law, in outline, recognises both civil and customary marriage and sets the minimum age of marriage at 18. If someone is in danger or being abused, a trusted adult, the police Victim Friendly Unit or Childline Zimbabwe (116) can help.

:::example{title="Supporting a child-headed family"}
After both parents die, seventeen-year-old Tafadzwa takes charge of his two younger sisters in Bindura. A paternal aunt (vatete) visits each month with groceries, and the village head asks the community to watch over the household. The school helps with fees, and the local church and the mosque each give school stationery. The family stays together and the children stay in school. The example shows how relatives, schools, religious groups and the wider community can share responsibility, so that a family that has changed shape can still protect its children and teach them values.
:::

## Strengthening families

Families, communities and religious groups can strengthen family life by:

- **Communicating:** spending time to talk and listen, including by phone and video when apart.
- **Sharing duties fairly:** parents and children share household tasks, and decisions respect the rights of all members.
- **Counselling and mediation:** couples and relatives use trained counsellors, elders or religious leaders to settle disputes.
- **Economic support:** savings clubs, burial societies and small projects help families to cope with costs.
- **Education and health:** keeping children in school and seeking trained health workers.
- **Religious and community support:** attending worship, ceremonies and community events that give a sense of belonging.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Judging family types as good or bad"}
Describe the changes and the difficulties they bring without saying that one type is wrong. Write about the families' needs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming a single cause"}
Change comes from several causes at once (urbanisation, education, economy, illness). Give more than one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only problems"}
Change brings benefits too: education, communication, new opportunities. Balanced answers include both.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe and explain change"}
In Paper 2 (a) give at least four causes of change, each with an example. In (b) *discuss how far religion can help families cope with change* give religious responses from two religions and a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link to values"}
Link family life to national values: for instance, "Families teach honesty and respect, which are part of unhu/ubuntu and so support the nation".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Urbanisation, migration, the diaspora, education, technology, economic pressure and illness are changing culture and families.
- Single-parent, blended and child-headed families have become more common.
- Religions respond through teaching, counselling, community support and care for the vulnerable, with differing views inside each religion.
- Families pass on national values such as unhu/ubuntu, hard work and respect for rights; the law protects family members.
- Families are strengthened by communication, fair sharing of duties, counselling, economic and community support.
:::
