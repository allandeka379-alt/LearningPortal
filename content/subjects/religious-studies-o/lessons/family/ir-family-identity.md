## Introduction

A teenager from Gweru visits her grandparents' homestead in the rural area for the school holidays. Before she leaves, her grandmother takes her aside and tells her: "Remember whose child you are." She means the family's name, the family's history and the ancestors who stand behind them. In Indigenous Religion (IR), family identity is not only about the people alive today. It is about a line that stretches back to the ancestors and forward to the children not yet born.

This lesson looks at how IR shapes family identity. Names and totems are covered in their own lesson, and detailed IR rites in the earlier lessons; here the focus is on how the family itself is understood. You should know the basic ideas of ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi) and of extended family.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Identity"}
The sense of who a person or a group is: name, family, history, beliefs and place in the community.
:::

:::definition{term="Lineage"}
A line of descent through which a family traces its ancestors, usually through the father's or the mother's side.
:::

:::definition{term="Homestead"}
The family home and its surroundings, including houses, kitchen, granary, fields and the places connected with the ancestors. In Shona it is called **musha** and in Ndebele **umuzi**.
:::

## Ancestors as members of the family

In IR the ancestors remain part of the family after death. They are believed to watch over the family, protect it from harm, guide it in decisions and share in its joys and sorrows. For this reason the family tells them about births, marriages and journeys, and asks for their help in sickness and difficulty. Someone who forgets the ancestors forgets part of their own family.

The ancestors also help to define the family: a person says "we are the family of..." and names the line of grandfathers and great-grandfathers. This long chain gives the family a sense of continuity that is stronger than the lives of individuals.

## Family rituals

Rituals make identity visible. Families gather for:

| Ritual | Meaning for family identity |
| --- | --- |
| Naming and welcoming a baby | The child receives a name and is introduced to the family and ancestors |
| Roora/lobola and wedding customs | Two families are joined, and the bride is introduced to her new family and its ancestors |
| Funeral and kurova guva/umbuyiso | The family cares for its dead and brings the spirit home as an ancestor |
| Bira / family ceremony | The family gives thanks to the ancestors or asks for help, led by elders |

Each ritual shows who belongs, who leads and who has duties. A person who misses family ceremonies without good reason may be asked to explain, because belonging is shown by taking part.

## Roles of elders, aunts and uncles

Elders hold the family's memory. Grandparents and older relatives tell stories, recite the family's history and settle disputes. The paternal aunt (vatete/ubabakazi) advises girls and takes part in marriage negotiations. The maternal uncle (sekuru/umalume) is often a friend and protector of his sister's children. The father's brothers share in the upbringing and discipline of the children. Through these relatives a young person learns what the family expects.

:::example{title="A grandmother's evening lesson"}
Every evening during the holidays, Tendai's grandmother sits with the grandchildren by the fire.

1. She tells a story about how the family came to settle in their area.
2. She names the grandfathers who lived there before.
3. She reminds the children of the family's rules: greet elders, share food, honour the ancestors.

The children learn who they are, where they come from and how they are expected to behave. The story is a teaching tool as well as entertainment.
:::

## The homestead

The homestead is a place of belonging. Even when family members live in towns or abroad, many still speak of the rural homestead as "home" (*kumusha* in Shona, *ekhaya* in Ndebele). It is the place where the family's graves are, where ceremonies are held and where the ancestors are believed to be near. In many families, certain places in the homestead, such as a particular tree or a spot in the kitchen hut, are used for speaking to the ancestors. A person's links to the homestead stay strong, which is why families travel long distances for funerals and ceremonies.

## Inheritance of names and roles

Family identity is passed on through names, roles and duties. Children take or are given family names, a grandparent's name may be given to a grandchild, and a family may keep roles from generation to generation: an elder who leads the family ceremonies, a person chosen to be a spirit medium, or a family member who keeps the family's stories. Totems (mutupo/isibongo) are studied in their own lesson. The heir, usually chosen by the family, takes on responsibility for the family's property and the care of its members. The law today protects the rights of all children and of spouses, including daughters and widows.

## Respect and obligations

Belonging brings duties:

- Respect for parents and elders, in speech and action.
- Obedience to family rules and attendance at family ceremonies.
- Sharing food, money and work with relatives who are in need.
- Honouring the ancestors by taking part in rituals and keeping taboos.
- Caring for children, the sick and the old.

These duties go both ways: elders must lead fairly, protect the young and listen. A family that neglects its duties is believed to lose the ancestors' blessing, and also loses trust.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the family 'worships' its ancestors"}
In IR the ancestors are honoured and asked for help; God is the supreme being. Use words such as "honour", "consult" and "inform".
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing identity as only a name"}
Identity includes ancestors, rituals, roles, the homestead and obligations. A good answer covers several of these.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the modern setting"}
Many families now live in towns. Mention how they keep links with the rural homestead through visits and ceremonies.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the role of ancestors in family identity"}
Link each point to a reason: "The ancestors are told of births and marriages (informing) because they are believed to be family members who protect it."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss"}
For *how far does IR shape family identity today?* give examples of continuity (ceremonies, kumusha) and of change (urban life, other religions), then a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In IR the ancestors are members of the family and help to define its identity and continuity.
- Rituals such as naming, marriage, funerals and bira show who belongs and who has duties.
- Elders, aunts and uncles pass on the family's history, values and expectations.
- The homestead (musha/umuzi) is a place of belonging, graves and ceremonies.
- Names and roles are inherited, and belonging brings obligations of respect, sharing and care.
:::
