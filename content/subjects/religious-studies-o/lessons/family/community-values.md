## Introduction

It is the first week of the rainy season in a village in Zaka. On Saturday, a neighbour's field has to be ploughed and planted before the rains stop. Forty people arrive with hoes, and the host family brews beer and cooks sadza for everyone. By evening the field is planted. Next month the same neighbours will plant the fields of the other households in turn.

This is a **nhimbe** (Shona) or **ilima** (Ndebele) work party, and it shows values that hold families and communities together. This lesson explains these values, shows how Indigenous Religion (IR) supports them and how Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach similar values. The general idea of unhu/ubuntu has its own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Value"}
A principle or standard of behaviour that a family or community regards as important.
:::

:::definition{term="Nhimbe / ilima"}
A work party in which neighbours help one another with big tasks, such as ploughing, weeding or harvesting, and are rewarded with food and drink.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective responsibility"}
The idea that the whole community shares responsibility for the well-being and behaviour of its members, especially children and the vulnerable.
:::

## Family values

Family values are the qualities that parents and elders teach in the home.

| Value | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Respect | Greeting elders, listening to them, using polite language |
| Love | Caring for one another, forgiving, putting the family first |
| Obedience | Following the guidance of parents and elders |
| Sharing | Sharing food, clothes, money and tasks |
| Hard work | Helping in the home, the field or the shop, and studying |
| Honesty | Telling the truth and keeping promises |
| Chastity | Respect for one's body and for marriage; sexual relations within marriage |

These values are taught by example, by proverbs and stories, and through family rituals. A child who is greeted and taught from an early age learns that respect is part of belonging.

## Community values

Community values extend family values to the village and town.

- **Cooperation:** work parties such as nhimbe/ilima, and joint projects such as digging a well or building a school.
- **Hospitality:** visitors are welcomed, offered water and food, and given a place to sleep.
- **Collective responsibility for children:** any adult may correct a child who misbehaves, and neighbours help a child whose parents have died.
- **Care for the vulnerable:** orphans, widows, the sick, older people and people with disabilities are supported by relatives and neighbours.
- **Peaceful settlement of disputes:** elders and the chief hear disputes and try to restore peace rather than punish.

:::example{title="A work party and what it teaches"}
Chipo's family in Zaka holds a nhimbe to weed a large field.

1. Neighbours arrive early, bringing their own hoes.
2. They work, singing, until the afternoon; Chipo's mother serves food and beer to everyone.
3. The next week Chipo's father works on a neighbour's field.

The work party shows cooperation (many hands finish the work), reciprocity (each helps the others in turn), hospitality (the host feeds the workers) and community feeling.
:::

## Indigenous Religion and the community

IR links values to belief. The ancestors are believed to watch over the community and to be pleased when its members live in harmony and displeased when they quarrel or neglect the needy. The following people and practices support values:

| Institution or practice | Contribution to community values |
| --- | --- |
| The chief (and headmen and village heads) | Settles disputes, leads ceremonies, allocates land in the traditional system and represents the community; works within the Constitution and the law |
| The village | A group of homesteads that share water, grazing and ceremonies |
| Shared rituals | Rain ceremonies, harvest ceremonies and funerals bring people together and remind them that they depend on one another |
| Taboos and sanctions | Protect the community's peace, health and environment |
| Elders and the dare / inkundla | The meeting place where problems are discussed, advice is given and decisions are made |

When the community prays for rain, for example, everyone in the village contributes and everyone benefits.

## Other religions and the same values

Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach values that are close to those of IR, in their own ways.

| Religion | Value | Teaching or practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Judaism | Love of neighbour; charity; hospitality | Leviticus 19:18 teaches love for one's neighbour; tzedakah (giving to those in need) is a duty; welcoming guests is a valued practice |
| Christianity | Love and service | Jesus summarised the law as love of God and neighbour (Mark 12:28-31), and the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) teaches care for anyone in need |
| Islam | Kindness and charity | The Qur'an teaches good treatment of parents, relatives, orphans, the poor and neighbours (Qur'an 4:36), and zakat is a pillar of Islam |

Each religion supports honesty, respect for parents, care for orphans and widows, and fair dealing. People of different faiths in Zimbabwe often work together in burial societies, school committees and clean-up campaigns.

:::example{title="Values tested at a school"}
At a school in Gwanda, Sipho finds a wallet with money in the playground. He could keep it. Instead he gives it to a teacher, who asks the school assembly whether anyone has lost it. The owner, a Grade 7 pupil, collects it with thanks. Afterwards the headmistress says that honesty, like respect and sharing, is a value learnt at home and strengthened by the community. Sipho later says that his grandmother always told him that "what is not yours is not yours". The example shows how a family value (honesty) becomes a community value when a school, a family and a neighbourhood all support it.
:::

## Value in daily life

These values are put to the test in a modern setting: a student who finds a lost phone and returns it shows honesty; a family that takes in an orphaned cousin shows care for the vulnerable; a group of neighbours who share a borehole show cooperation. At the same time, communities debate whether some customs, such as obedience without question, should allow children to be heard, and how to care for the vulnerable when families are under economic strain.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing values without explaining"}
"Respect, love and sharing" earns few marks. For each value say what it looks like and why it matters.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing family and community values"}
Family values are taught in the home; community values apply to the village or town. They overlap, but a clear answer separates them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying only IR teaches these values"}
Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach similar values. Give at least one source or example for each religion when asked to compare.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or explain values"}
In Paper 2 (a), *describe the values taught in the family* needs four or five values with an example each. In (b) *discuss how far religion supports these values* use IR plus one other religion, and give a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use correct terms"}
Use nhimbe/ilima, hospitality, collective responsibility, cooperation and care for the vulnerable.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Family values include respect, love, obedience, sharing, hard work, honesty and chastity.
- Community values include cooperation (nhimbe/ilima), hospitality, collective responsibility for children and care for the vulnerable.
- IR supports values through the chief, the village, shared rituals, taboos and the belief that the ancestors watch over the community.
- Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach similar values, with their own sources.
- Values are tested in modern life and communities discuss how to apply them fairly.
:::
