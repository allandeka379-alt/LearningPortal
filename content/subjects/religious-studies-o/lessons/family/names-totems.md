## Introduction

At a new school in Bindura, a teacher calls the register: Tendai Moyo, Nokuthula Ndlovu, Yusuf Patel, Miriam Cohen, Farai Shumba. A student asks, "What do our names mean?" The class discovers that Tendai means "give thanks", that Moyo is a family totem meaning heart, that Ndlovu means elephant, and that each name comes from a religion or tradition that the family belongs to.

Names and totems tell us who a person is and where he or she belongs. This lesson explains how names are chosen and what they mean in the four religions of the syllabus, how totems work in Shona and Ndebele society, and how change affects them. Families and the idea of lineage from earlier lessons will help you.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Totem"}
An animal, plant or other natural object that is the emblem of a clan or family group and that members respect and may not harm or eat. It is called **mutupo** in Shona (plural **mitupo**) and **isibongo** in Ndebele (plural **izibongo**).
:::

:::definition{term="Praise poetry"}
Poetry that praises a clan or person, reciting names, totems and history. It is called **detembo** in Shona and **izibongo** in Ndebele.
:::

:::definition{term="Clan"}
A large group of families who claim descent from a common ancestor and share a totem.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Why names matter"}
A name gives identity, belonging and often a message about the family's hopes, history or beliefs.
:::

## Names in Indigenous Religion

Shona and Ndebele names are rarely chosen only because they sound good. They may express thanks (Tendai, Tatenda), joy (Farai), love (Rudo), grace (Nyasha), gift (Chipo, Sipho), hope (Themba) or peace (Nokuthula). Some names record events or circumstances at birth, such as a death in the family or a time of hardship. Others honour an ancestor, in the belief that the ancestor's qualities will help the child. Naming is usually done by elders at a family gathering, and is a moment of welcome as well as identity.

A praise name or nickname may be added later, and some people receive names in adulthood that express their character or role.

## Names in other religions

| Religion | Naming custom (in outline) |
| --- | --- |
| Judaism | A boy is named at his circumcision, brit milah, on the eighth day, and a girl is named in the synagogue. Many children are named after a relative, and Hebrew names such as Miriam, David and Rachel recall figures in the Tanakh. |
| Christianity | In many churches a child receives a Christian name at baptism, often from the Bible or from a saint, such as John or Mary. Practice varies between denominations. |
| Islam | Parents choose a name with a good meaning, often from the Qur'an or the family of the Prophet Muhammad, such as Aisha, Yusuf, Fatima or Ibrahim. The name is announced in the first week, often with the aqiqah, in which an animal is sacrificed and shared with the poor and with relatives. |

:::example{title="Three naming ceremonies"}
1. **Indigenous Religion:** in Lupane, elders gather, speak to the ancestors and announce the baby's name, Themba ("hope"), asking them to protect him.
2. **Judaism:** in Bulawayo, a baby boy is circumcised on his eighth day and named David at the same ceremony in the presence of family and friends.
3. **Islam:** in Harare, a family whispers the call to prayer into the baby's ear, names her Aisha in the first week and shares food with relatives and the poor.

In all three cases, naming welcomes the child and gives the child identity within a faith community.
:::

## Totems (mutupo / isibongo)

A totem links an individual with a clan and its ancestors. Examples of Shona totems include *Shumba* (lion), *Moyo* (heart), *Nzou* (elephant) and *Soko* (monkey). In Ndebele society, a common clan name such as *Ndlovu* means elephant and *Dube* means zebra. Children usually take their father's totem. People who share a totem regard one another as relatives, even if they have never met.

### Functions of totems

| Function | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Identity and belonging | A totem tells a person which clan they belong to and links them to its history |
| Preventing incest | People of the same totem do not marry, because they are treated as relatives |
| Conservation | Members do not hunt, kill or eat their totem animal, so species and places are protected |
| Unity and hospitality | A stranger who shares your totem is treated as family and welcomed |
| Respect | Totems are sacred symbols; showing respect for one's own and others' totems builds mutual respect |

### Praise poetry

Praise poetry names a person's totem and recounts the clan's history and qualities. In Shona, *detembo* is recited at ceremonies and gatherings and when greeting a person; in Ndebele, *izibongo* are praise names recited for chiefs, heroes and family members. Reciting the praises affirms identity and honours the ancestors.

## Change and identity

Names and totems have been affected by change.

- **Christianity and Islam:** many people have Christian or Muslim names alongside or instead of African names, and families may have names from several traditions.
- **Urban life:** children who grow up in towns sometimes know their totems less well, and totem rules about marriage are not always observed.
- **Education and politics:** after independence, some people returned to African names; others keep English names for convenience.
- **Revival:** some families teach their children their totem and praise poetry deliberately, as a source of pride.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a totem with a surname"}
A totem is a symbol with sacred meaning and rules (such as not eating the animal). A surname can be linked to a totem, but the two are not the same.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying people 'worship' totems"}
Totems are respected as clan symbols. Members honour them; they do not treat the animals as gods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the religions' naming customs"}
Brit milah and the eighth day belong to Judaism, baptism to Christianity, and aqiqah and the first week to Islam.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Names give identity and often carry meaning; naming welcomes a child into a family and faith community.
- Naming customs differ: elders and ancestors in IR, brit milah in Judaism, baptism in Christianity, aqiqah in Islam.
- A totem (mutupo/isibongo) is a clan emblem that gives identity, prevents incest, protects nature and builds unity.
- Praise poetry (detembo/izibongo) recites names and history to honour the clan.
- Christian and Muslim names, urban life and revival have changed how names and totems are used.
:::
