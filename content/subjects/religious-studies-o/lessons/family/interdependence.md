## Introduction

A man dies in Chitungwiza on a Tuesday. By Wednesday morning his burial society has confirmed that it will cover the costs of the coffin and transport. Neighbours bring maize meal and firewood to the family. A church group sends women to cook. His brother-in-law, who lives in Bulawayo, is already travelling. No family can handle a funeral alone. Families depend on one another, and this dependence is a strength of the way people live in Zimbabwe.

This lesson explains how families depend on one another, how religion builds and strengthens those links, and what threatens them. It builds on the earlier lessons about family structure, values and community. You should be able to recall the roles of aunts and uncles and the idea of collective responsibility.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inter-dependency"}
A relationship in which people or groups depend on one another and each gives and receives help.
:::

:::definition{term="Kinship network"}
The web of relatives, by birth and marriage, who recognise duties to one another.
:::

:::definition{term="Burial society"}
A group whose members pay small regular contributions so that the society pays towards funeral costs and supports a bereaved family.
:::

:::definition{term="Mukando"}
A savings or lending club (Shona) in which members contribute regularly and take turns to receive the pooled money or goods.
:::

## How families depend on one another

| Way | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Marriage alliances | A marriage joins two families. Each gains new relatives and obligations, such as supporting the couple and welcoming each other at ceremonies. |
| Kinship networks | A person may call on uncles, aunts and cousins for help with school fees, accommodation in town, or advice. |
| Mutual help at life events | Relatives and neighbours help at weddings, funerals, births and during illness. |
| Farming and work | Work parties (nhimbe/ilima) help with ploughing, weeding and harvest. Families may exchange seed, tools and draught animals. |
| Community child care | Neighbours and relatives look after children when parents are away, ill or have died. |
| Burial societies and savings clubs | Members pool money for funerals, school fees or business, so that no household carries a big cost alone. |

:::example{title="A marriage alliance at work"}
Tafadzwa marries Nyasha. The families agree on roora.

1. Nyasha's family now has a relationship with Tafadzwa's relatives and will visit on festive days.
2. When Tafadzwa's father dies, Nyasha's uncles attend the funeral and help.
3. When Nyasha's grandmother needs a place to stay in Harare, Tafadzwa's family hosts her.

Each side has gained a new network of support, which would not exist without the marriage.
:::

## Mutual help in time of need

Illness, death and bad harvests are times when families depend most on others. Relatives take turns to nurse the sick, neighbours bring food, and members of the kinship network contribute money. After a death, the kinship network helps to organise the funeral, comforts the family and may look after the children. This help is not charity from strangers: it is a duty within the network that people expect to receive in turn.

### Burial societies and savings clubs

Many communities have burial societies, savings clubs such as *mukando*, and similar rotating schemes. Members make small regular contributions. When a member needs money for a funeral or school fees, the group provides it or pays it out in turn. These groups combine inter-dependency with modern organisation and often include people of different religions and from different places.

## The role of religion

Religion strengthens inter-dependency by giving it a sacred meaning and by providing meeting places.

| Religion | How it builds links between families |
| --- | --- |
| Indigenous Religion | Shared rituals bring kin together: naming, marriage, funerals and kurova guva/umbuyiso. The ancestors are believed to watch over all the families of the clan. |
| Judaism | The synagogue is a place where families meet for prayer and festivals, and community members support the sick, the poor and mourners. |
| Christianity | The church is described as a family of believers. The New Testament compares the church to a body with many parts that depend on one another (1 Corinthians 12:12-27), and the first Christians are described as sharing what they had (Acts 2:44-45). Church groups visit the sick and support funerals. |
| Islam | The mosque is a centre of community life. The Qur'an says that believers are brothers and sisters (Qur'an 49:10), and zakat provides for the needy. |

In practice, families of different faiths in Zimbabwe often work side by side in funerals, school committees and community projects.

:::example{title="Help across religions"}
When the Ibrahim family in Mutare loses a grandmother, the burial arrangements follow Muslim practice and the community of the mosque supports them. Their neighbours, the Dube family, who attend a church, bring food and sit with the family. A few months later, when the Dube family's roof is damaged in a storm, the Ibrahim men are among the first to help repair it. Faith communities differ in their rituals, but the help each gives is a form of inter-dependency, and each family finds that its neighbours' faith does not prevent them from supporting one another.
:::

## Threats to inter-dependency

- **Individualism:** a stress on personal success and private wealth can weaken the sense of duty to relatives.
- **Migration:** relatives move to towns or abroad, so everyday contact and help become harder; money sent home (remittances) can partly make up for this.
- **Conflict:** disputes over inheritance, land, money or religious differences can divide families.
- **Poverty and the cost of living:** when families struggle to feed their own, they have less to share.
- **HIV and AIDS and other illness:** where many adults have died, the extended family has been stretched to care for orphans.

Communities and religious groups respond by mediating disputes, supporting orphans, and organising support groups. Teachers, pastors, imams, rabbis and chiefs sometimes help families to resolve conflicts through dialogue.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing only one-way help"}
Inter-dependency means help both ways. Show how each family gives and receives support.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the role of religion"}
When asked about religion, name the religion, the meeting place or practice (church, mosque, ceremony) and how it links families.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating migration as only harmful"}
Migration can weaken daily contact, but remittances, phones and visits can also keep families connected. Give both sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Families depend on one another through marriage alliances, kinship networks and mutual help at life events and in farming.
- Burial societies and savings clubs (such as mukando) are modern forms of inter-dependency.
- Religion strengthens the links through shared rituals, places of worship and teaching about care for others.
- Individualism, migration, conflict and poverty threaten inter-dependency.
- Communities and religious leaders respond by mediating disputes and supporting those in need.
:::
