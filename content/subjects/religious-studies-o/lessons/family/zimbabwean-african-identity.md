## Introduction

At a Heroes' Day gathering in a town in Zimbabwe, a school choir sings in Shona and Ndebele, a pastor offers a prayer, a traditional leader in formal dress sits in the front row and an imam and a rabbi are among the guests. A young girl asks her teacher, "What does it mean to be Zimbabwean?" The teacher answers that it means many things, including a shared history, shared values, shared languages, and a shared respect for one another's beliefs.

This lesson looks at the part that religion plays in two kinds of identity: being Zimbabwean and being African. You should know about Indigenous Religion (IR), the ancestors and the idea of unhu/ubuntu. Questions about national symbols are in Heritage Studies, not here.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Identity"}
Who a person or a nation understands itself to be: shared history, languages, values, beliefs and symbols.
:::

:::definition{term="Africanisation"}
The process by which a religion or idea that came from outside is adapted to African languages, music, leadership and ways of life.
:::

:::definition{term="Multi-faith nation"}
A country whose people follow many religions, and whose laws and customs allow them to live together with respect.
:::

## Indigenous Religion and Zimbabwean identity

IR contributes to Zimbabwean identity in several ways.

| Contribution | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Heritage sites | Sacred and historic places linked to IR beliefs, such as Great Zimbabwe, Khami and the Matobo Hills (a cultural landscape linked to the Mwari shrines), are on the UNESCO World Heritage List |
| Language | Shona and Ndebele words for God, ancestors and rites (Mwari, uMlimo, midzimu, amadlozi) carry beliefs and are part of national languages |
| Values | Unhu/ubuntu, respect for elders, hospitality and care for the vulnerable are widely regarded as Zimbabwean values |
| National ceremonies | Many national and community events begin with traditional music, dance and praise poetry |
| National heroes | Historical figures such as Nehanda (Charwe Nyakasikana) and Kaguvi, who were spirit mediums and leaders in the 1896-97 uprisings and were executed in 1898, are honoured in the country's history; the roles of IR in the liberation struggle are covered in separate lessons |
| Rites of passage | Naming, roora/lobola, funerals and kurova guva/umbuyiso are practised by many families, whatever religion they belong to |

:::keyfact{title="The Matobo Hills"}
In 2003 the Matobo Hills were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as a cultural landscape. They are associated with Mwari shrines and with the long history of the Ndebele and Shona peoples.
:::

## Indigenous Religion and African identity

Zimbabwe's IR shares features with beliefs found in many parts of Africa: belief in a supreme being who made the world, respect for ancestors as members of the family, a strong sense of community, rites of passage and the idea that human life is bound up with the lives of others. Scholars such as John Mbiti have described these shared features. The saying "a person is a person through other people" (*umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*) expresses the value of ubuntu that appears in many African languages.

African identity has also been shaped by movements that aim to value African cultures, languages and history, sometimes called a cultural revival. The African Union, formed in 2002, encourages cooperation among African states, including the protection of African heritage. People in Zimbabwe also take part in this identity by wearing African dress, using African names and holding festivals of music, dance and food.

:::example{title="Naming and identity"}
Nokuthula chooses to use her Ndebele name at work after being addressed for years by an English nickname. She says that her name tells her family's story and links her to many other people who bear the same name across southern Africa. For her, an African name is a statement of identity as well as a label.
:::

## Other religions in Zimbabwe: Africanisation

Christianity and Islam came to Zimbabwe from outside, yet both have been Africanised.

- **Christianity:** African Independent / Initiated Churches (AICs) were founded by Africans, use African languages and forms of worship, such as drumming, dance and communal singing, and address concerns such as healing and community. Many mainline churches also use Shona and Ndebele hymns, African leaders and local music.
- **Islam:** Islam came to Africa from the seventh century and spread along trade routes across the Sahara and the East African coast. In Zimbabwe it came mainly through trade and migration, and its communities use local languages in addition to Arabic for worship.
- **Judaism:** Zimbabwe has had small Jewish communities in towns such as Harare and Bulawayo that practise their religion as part of Zimbabwean society.

Each of these shows that religions can be both universal and local: the beliefs are shared across the world, yet the music, language and community life are shaped by where people live.

## A multi-faith nation

Zimbabwe has people of many faiths. The Constitution (2013) does not name an official state religion. Section 56 protects equality and non-discrimination, and section 60 protects freedom of conscience, including the right to practise and change one's religion. Debates exist, for example about how school assemblies, public holidays and national ceremonies should include different faiths, about the place of traditional leaders and healers, and about how to respect those who follow no religion. A respectful approach is to listen to one another, to learn about each other's practices, and to protect the rights of everyone.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying IR is the only authentic African religion"}
Christianity and Islam have lived in Africa for centuries and are part of many Africans' identities. Present each religion on its own terms and do not rank them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Heritage Studies and Religious Studies"}
Describing national symbols such as the flag or the bird is Heritage Studies. In this subject, keep to the religious part of identity (beliefs, sacred places, values).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'African' as if it meant one culture"}
Africa has many peoples and religions. Say "many African communities" or name the people.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or explain contributions to identity"}
In Paper 2 (a), *describe how IR contributes to Zimbabwean identity* use four distinct points (heritage sites, language, values, national figures), each with an example. In (b) *assess* the view that religion unites or divides a nation using more than one religion.
:::

For multi-faith questions, mention section 56 (equality) and section 60 (freedom of conscience) of the Constitution to support your point.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- IR contributes to Zimbabwean identity through heritage sites, languages, values, ceremonies and national figures such as Nehanda and Kaguvi.
- IR shares beliefs with many African communities: a supreme being, ancestors, community and ubuntu.
- Christianity and Islam have been Africanised in language, music and leadership; AICs are an example.
- Zimbabwe is a multi-faith nation with no official religion; sections 56 and 60 protect equality and freedom of conscience.
- Respect for different beliefs is part of national identity.
:::
