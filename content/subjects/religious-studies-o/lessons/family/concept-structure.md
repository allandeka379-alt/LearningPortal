## Introduction

On a Saturday in Marondera, Tafadzwa wakes up in a house with his parents and two sisters. At lunch his grandmother arrives from the rural home, and in the afternoon his father's elder brother, his wife and their children visit. By evening there are twelve people at the table. Who counts as "family" in this story? The people sleeping in the house, the relatives visiting, or all those connected by blood, marriage and the ancestors?

This lesson begins Family and Religious Studies by asking what a family is, how families are organised, what members do for one another and how the four religions in the syllabus describe family life. You do not need earlier knowledge, only your own experience of family.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Family"}
A group of people related by birth, marriage or adoption, or who live together as relatives, who care for one another and share responsibilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Nuclear family"}
A family made up of two parents and their children.
:::

:::definition{term="Extended family"}
A family that includes other relatives, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who have close ties and share responsibilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Kinship"}
The system of relationships by birth and marriage through which people know who is related to whom and what they owe one another.
:::

## Types of family

| Type | Who belongs | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuclear | Two parents and their children | Rudo, her husband and their two children in a flat in Mutare |
| Extended | Parents, children and other relatives | A homestead in Gwanda with grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins |
| Single-parent | One parent and children | A mother in Chinhoyi raising three children after a divorce or the death of her husband |
| Child-headed | Children living without an adult, led by an older sibling | An orphaned sixteen-year-old caring for younger brothers and sisters |
| Blended | Two families joined by remarriage, with children from earlier relationships | A man and woman each bringing children into their new marriage |
| Polygamous | One husband with more than one wife and their children | Found in customary law and in some religious traditions |

![Six types of family and who belongs to each: nuclear (two parents and children), extended (parents, children and other relatives), single-parent, child-headed, blended (two families joined by remarriage) and polygamous (one husband with more than one wife).](media:diagrams/family-types.png "Figure: the six types of family and who belongs to each")

No type is complete in itself, and a family can change type as people marry, move, are born and die. Many Zimbabwean children grow up in different types of family at different times.

## Roles and relationships

Families work through roles. Roles differ between families, but the aim is the same: members care for one another.

- **Parents:** provide food, shelter, love, guidance and discipline, and teach values.
- **Grandparents:** share wisdom, tell stories, care for grandchildren and are respected as links to the past.
- **Aunts and uncles:** advise and guide; in many families they have particular roles in marriages and discipline.
- **Children:** respect parents and elders, help in the home and study.
- **Older siblings:** care for younger siblings and set an example.

Relationships are based on respect, love and obedience, but also on responsibility: when a family member is in need, the others are expected to help.

### Kinship terms

Shona and Ndebele have many words for relatives that English does not distinguish.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| vatete / ubabakazi | Father's sister (paternal aunt) |
| sekuru / umalume | Mother's brother (maternal uncle); sekuru is also used for a grandfather |
| babamukuru | Father's elder brother |

These terms show that relatives on the father's side and the mother's side have different duties. For example, the paternal aunt often advises young women about marriage, and the maternal uncle is often a close friend and supporter of his sister's children.

:::example{title="Whose responsibility?"}
Thandeka's father dies and her mother cannot pay school fees.

1. Her maternal uncle (umalume) offers to pay part of the fees.
2. Her paternal aunt (ubabakazi) talks with her about her future.
3. Her grandmother cares for her little brother after school.

No single person carries the whole burden. This is the strength of the extended family.
:::

## Why belonging matters

Belonging to a family gives a person identity (name, totem, history), security (food, shelter, support in sickness and old age), education (values and skills) and religious life (ceremonies for birth, marriage and death). People who grow up without family support, such as children in child-headed households, often need extra help from the community, schools and religious groups.

### What families do

Whatever their type, families carry out functions that no other group does as well:

- **Reproduction and care:** families bring up children and look after the sick and the old.
- **Socialisation:** children learn language, manners and values at home before they go to school.
- **Economic support:** members share food, housing and income, and many families work together in fields or small businesses.
- **Religious education:** children learn prayers, festivals, rites and the beliefs of the family from the people closest to them.
- **Protection and belonging:** a person who is rejected by outsiders can still find acceptance at home.

When these functions are weak, as when poverty or conflict divides a family, schools, churches, mosques and community groups often step in to help.

## The family in four religions

| Religion | Family teaching (in outline) |
| --- | --- |
| Indigenous Religion | The family includes the living and the ancestors. Elders, aunts and uncles guide the young, and family rituals keep relations between the living and the dead. |
| Judaism | The family is the centre of religious life. Children are taught the Torah at home, and Shabbat and festivals are celebrated around the family table. Honouring one's parents is a commandment (Exodus 20:12). |
| Christianity | The family is a gift of God. Children are told to honour their parents (Ephesians 6:1-3), and Christians are taught to love and care for the family. |
| Islam | The family is the basis of society. The Qur'an teaches kindness to parents (Qur'an 17:23) and describes love and mercy between spouses (Qur'an 30:21). |

Each religion also accepts that families vary, and each encourages care for orphans, widows and the vulnerable.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the 'real' family is the nuclear family"}
No single type is the only family. Describe each type and its strengths and difficulties without ranking them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing family types without examples"}
Examiners reward a definition and a short example for each type (for instance, "child-headed: an older sister caring for her brothers after both parents have died").
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'religion says' about the family"}
Name the religion and, where possible, the source: "In Islam, the Qur'an teaches kindness to parents (Qur'an 17:23)".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify, describe, explain"}
*Identify* two types of family needs two names. *Describe* needs a short description of each. *Explain the importance of the family* needs reasons developed with "because": for identity, security, education and religion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 questions"}
Family topics appear in Paper 2. In (a) describe the structure or roles; in (b) *discuss* or *assess* usually asks for two views or two religions, then a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A family is a group related by birth, marriage or adoption who care for one another.
- The main types are nuclear, extended, single-parent, child-headed, blended and polygamous.
- Members have roles and responsibilities; kinship terms such as vatete/ubabakazi, sekuru/umalume and babamukuru show the duties of relatives.
- Belonging to a family gives identity, security, education and religious life.
- Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam all value the family and care for the vulnerable.
:::
