## Introduction

Long before there were school bells, Zimbabwean children were already being educated. A girl in a village near Masvingo learnt to grind grain, care for a younger sibling and greet elders properly by watching her mother, aunts and grandmother. A boy learnt to herd cattle, make a hoe handle and listen respectfully at the dare, the place where men met to talk. Nobody gave them a certificate, yet they were being prepared for adult life in a society that valued them.

This lesson describes **indigenous education**: the way traditional Shona and Ndebele communities passed on knowledge, skills, values and religion from one generation to the next. It is the first lesson in Topic 7, so it gives you a standard against which to measure the later lessons on mission schools and modern schooling. You should already know the main beliefs of Indigenous Religion (God, ancestral spirits, taboos) from Topic 2, because religion runs through the whole of indigenous education.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indigenous education"}
The informal, community-based way in which traditional societies prepare children for adult life, through living, watching and taking part rather than through a school building and a written syllabus.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal education"}
Learning that happens in everyday life, within the family and community, without fixed classrooms, timetables or examinations.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral tradition"}
Knowledge such as history, law, stories and proverbs that is passed on by speaking and remembering, not by writing.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn: Shona and Ndebele traditional **teachers** were parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles and the wider community. **Methods** included observation, participation, folk tales (ngano in Shona; izinganekwane in Ndebele), proverbs, songs, riddles and initiation. **Content** included practical skills, values, history and religion.
:::

## Aims of indigenous education

Indigenous education had practical and moral aims. It aimed to:

- prepare children to survive and earn a living (farming, herding, building, cooking, crafts);
- teach respect for elders, the ancestors and the community, and good manners;
- pass on the history, customs and family or clan identity of the people;
- prepare young people for the roles of husband, wife, parent and member of the community;
- teach the religious beliefs and duties that kept the community in good relationship with God and the ancestral spirits.

## Content: what was taught

| Area | Examples for boys | Examples for girls |
|---|---|---|
| Practical skills | herding, hunting and trapping in season, ploughing, building, making tools, carving | cooking, fetching water and firewood, grinding grain, pottery, basketry, childcare |
| Values | respect, courage, hard work, honesty, sharing | respect, modesty, hard work, patience, caring for others |
| History and identity | clan history, totem (mutupo / isibongo), praise poems, the deeds of past leaders | the same family history, songs and praises, family relationships |
| Religion | respect for God and the ancestors, taboos, how to take part in family rituals | the same, together with the roles of women in rituals such as brewing beer for a ceremony |

Boys and girls learnt many of the same values but, as in most societies at the time, the practical training was divided by gender. Later lessons on gender (Topic 8) discuss that division.

### The religious content

Religion was not a separate subject. Children learnt that **God** (Mwari, uMlimo or uNkulunkulu) is the creator, that **ancestral spirits** (midzimu / amadlozi) care for the family, and that **taboos** (zviera) protect the community and the environment. They learnt to speak respectfully to elders because elders were close to the ancestors, and how to take part in family ceremonies such as the *bira*. Moral teaching and religious teaching were one and the same.

## Methods and teachers

| Method | How it worked | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Observation and imitation | children watched adults at work and copied them | a boy watching his father repair a hut |
| Participation (learning by doing) | children took a small share of real tasks, then larger ones | a girl helping to sieve grain and later preparing a whole meal |
| Folk tales | stories told by the fire, often with animal characters and a moral | tales in which Tsuro the hare is clever but pays for being selfish |
| Proverbs and riddles | short sayings that carry community wisdom | a Shona proverb meaning that one thumb cannot crush a louse teaches cooperation |
| Songs, dances and praise poems | memorised verses on history, work and values | clan praises recited at gatherings |
| Initiation and rites of passage | formal teaching at key stages of life | teaching given at puberty and before marriage (see the rites lessons in Topic 2) |

**Teachers.** Parents were the first teachers. Grandparents, especially grandmothers (ambuya, gogo) told stories and gave advice. A girl's paternal aunt (*vatete*) often had special responsibility for her instruction about marriage and family life; uncles guided boys. In the evening the boys gathered in the dare or *ixhiba* (the men's gathering place) while the girls and young women gathered in the kitchen hut with the women. Elders, chiefs and spirit mediums taught the community's history and religious duties. The whole community was a classroom.

:::example{title="Learning a proverb at the fire"}
One evening in a village near Gweru, Ambuya Chipo tells her grandchildren that "one thumb cannot crush a louse". Nyasha, aged eight, asks what it means. Ambuya explains that a single person cannot finish a hard task alone and gives the example of the whole village helping to build a granary. Nyasha learns a value (cooperation), a method of work (shared labour) and a way of speaking that the village understands. No book was used; the lesson was spoken, remembered and then lived the next time the village worked together.
:::

:::example{title="From observation to responsibility"}
Tendai, aged ten, watches his uncle mend a cattle pen in Lupane every day after school holidays. First he only carries poles. At twelve he ties the poles with strips of bark, and at fifteen he leads the repairs while his uncle watches. The example shows the stages of indigenous learning: observe, assist, practise, then take responsibility. The reward was the trust of the family, not marks on a report.
:::

## Strengths and limitations

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Practical and relevant to daily life; learners were never idle | Limited to what the community already knew; little new science or world knowledge |
| Taught values, character and respect together with skills | No written records, so knowledge could be lost when elders died |
| Everyone took part, so no child was left out of community life | Roles were fixed by gender and age; talent outside the expected role was not always developed |
| Free of cost and based in the home | No qualifications for employment in a modern economy |
| Strengthened identity, family and unity | Rarely questioned custom; change was slow |
| Taught religion and morality as one way of life | Some harmful beliefs and practices could be passed on without challenge |

Because of these limitations, many Zimbabweans welcomed formal schooling when it came. Most people today value a combination: the practical, moral and cultural strengths of indigenous education together with the reading, writing, science and skills taught in school.

## Indigenous education today

Many families still teach these values at home: respect for elders, stories from grandparents, proverbs, family histories. Schools in Zimbabwe include Heritage Studies and cultural activities, and many parents see the moral and religious teaching given at home as complementary to school. Some rural communities still hold traditional ceremonies at which young people learn. Indigenous education has therefore changed but not disappeared.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling indigenous education 'no education'"}
It was a complete system with aims, content, methods and teachers. It was informal, not absent. Describe it on its own terms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing methods without explaining them"}
"Proverbs and folk tales" earns a mark; "folk tales taught moral lessons through stories of animals, so children remembered the values" earns the development mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about religion or only about skills"}
Indigenous education included skills, values, history and religion. A strong answer shows how they were joined together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing indigenous education with mission education"}
Mission schools (Topic 7.2) used buildings, books and teachers from outside the community. Indigenous education did not.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe"}
For *describe indigenous education*, cover aims, content, methods and teachers, and give an example for each. Aim for five to eight developed points.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess strengths and limitations"}
For *assess the strengths and limitations of indigenous education*, give at least two strengths and two limitations, each explained, and end with a judgement such as "its strength was relevance and values; its limit was the lack of written and scientific knowledge".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous education was informal and community-based; it prepared children for adult life.
- Teachers were parents, grandparents, aunts, uncles and the whole community.
- Content: practical skills, values, history and identity, and religion.
- Methods: observation, participation, folk tales, proverbs, songs, riddles and initiation.
- Strengths: relevance, values, community unity, no cost. Limitations: no writing, fixed roles, limited new knowledge.
- Many of its values are still taught in homes today.
:::
