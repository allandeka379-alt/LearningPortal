## Introduction

Many Zimbabweans can name the school where their grandparents, parents or they themselves first learnt to read. Very often it was a church or mission school, with a chapel on the grounds and the name of a saint or a founding church in its history. Even today a large number of primary and secondary schools in Zimbabwe, as well as colleges and universities, were started by religious organisations. Religions have therefore shaped who got educated, what they were taught and how the nation's skilled workforce grew.

This lesson explains how and why religious bodies founded educational institutions in Zimbabwe, names some well-known examples where the founding church is certain, and assesses their contribution. You should already know what indigenous education was (the previous lesson) and the main Christian denominations (Topic 4). Later lessons look at how religion supports education today and where it has had negative effects.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mission school"}
A school started and run by a Christian church or missionary society, usually next to a mission station that also had a church and often a clinic or hospital.
:::

:::definition{term="Human resource capacity"}
The skills, knowledge and training of the people of a country, which it needs for its economy and public services (for example teachers, nurses, engineers and managers).
:::

:::definition{term="Madrasah"}
A school where Muslim children learn to read the Qur'an and study Islamic teaching; some madrasah also teach the national school subjects.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn: Inyati (London Missionary Society, 1859) and Hope Fountain (also LMS) are in the Ndebele-speaking west. Chishawasha and Kutama are Catholic (Jesuit) missions. Old Mutare and Waddilove are Methodist. Dadaya is a Church of Christ mission. Universities: Africa University (United Methodist, Mutare), Catholic University of Zimbabwe (Catholic bishops, Harare), Solusi University (Seventh-day Adventist, near Bulawayo).
:::

## Why did religious organisations found schools?

Religious bodies had several reasons, which a good answer separates.

- **To teach people to read the scriptures.** Protestant missionaries wanted converts to be able to read the Bible, so literacy was part of the mission.
- **To train local helpers.** Churches needed evangelists, catechists, pastors and teachers, so they trained them.
- **To spread the faith and share Christian values.** Schools reached children and families that would otherwise be hard to contact.
- **To serve the community.** Schools, clinics and hospitals were seen by many missionaries as acts of Christian service and love.
- **To prepare people for work.** Many missions taught carpentry, building, farming and nursing as well as reading and arithmetic.

Muslim and Jewish communities also teach religion to their children, to pass on their faith, language and values, and they have their own reasons for doing so.

| Hope Fountain | London Missionary Society | Near Bulawayo; school and training |

| Kutama | Catholic (Jesuit) | Zvimba area; later a well-known secondary school |

| Waddilove | Methodist | Near Marondera; school and teacher training |

Many other schools were started by Anglican, Brethren, Dutch Reformed, Salvation Army, Seventh-day Adventist, Lutheran, Presbyterian, Catholic and other church bodies. Only institutions whose founding church is certain are named here.

### Colleges and universities

| Institution | Religious body | Place |
|---|---|---|
| Africa University | United Methodist Church | Mutare |
| Catholic University of Zimbabwe | Catholic bishops of Zimbabwe | Harare |
| Solusi University | Seventh-day Adventist Church | near Bulawayo |

Religious bodies also run teachers' colleges, nursing schools attached to mission hospitals, and seminaries and Bible colleges that train their clergy. These institutions are open to students of different religions, although each has its own ethos.

### Islamic and Jewish education in outline

Muslim communities in Zimbabwe run madrasah, often attached to a mosque, where children learn to recite the Qur'an and study Islam, usually after ordinary school or at weekends; some Muslim organisations also run schools that teach the national curriculum together with Islamic Studies. Jewish communities elsewhere in the world run day schools and classes in Hebrew and the Torah; the Jewish community in Zimbabwe is small, and religious classes for children are organised within the community.

:::example{title="A mission station in action"}
Imagine a fictional mission station near Gweru. The missionaries build a chapel, a small clinic and a school with two classrooms. Rudo's grandfather learns to read in that school and later trains as a teacher. He teaches at a rural school for thirty years, and several of his pupils become nurses and clerks. The example shows how one institution produced educated people who in turn educated others, which is how mission schools contributed to the growth of human resource capacity.
:::

## Contribution to human resource capacity

Religious institutions added to the skills of the nation in several ways:

- they trained **teachers, nurses, clergy, clerks, builders and farmers**, and later professionals and graduates;
- they offered education in rural areas where few other schools existed;
- they opened schooling to girls as well as boys in many places, and trained teachers;
- many later leaders in business, government, medicine and the churches were educated at mission schools;
- they preserved and wrote down **local languages**, producing early grammars, hymn books and Bible translations in Shona and Ndebele;
- they provided a model of **boarding and discipline**, and of linking schooling with community service.

## The link to colonial policy

In the colonial period the government left much of the education of Africans to mission bodies, giving them grants for approved schools while spending far more on schools for the settler community. Mission education therefore grew where the state did little. Critics point out that the curriculum often stressed religion, discipline and practical or "industrial" training suited to the colonial economy, and that mission schools sometimes looked down on African customs. Defenders note that mission schools also gave Africans literacy, qualifications and the confidence to demand more, and that many nationalist leaders were educated in them. A balanced answer reports both views. After independence in 1980 the government expanded education greatly and works with churches as **responsible authorities** for many schools.

:::example{title="Two views on a mission school"}
Mthulisi's grandmother attended a mission school where she learnt to read, write and sew. She is proud of what she gained. His history teacher says that the same school once forbade children to speak their home language in class and that its aims included changing local customs. Both views are part of the story: a fair account recognises the real educational benefits and the real criticisms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming a founding church you are not sure about"}
Only name the institutions and churches you have learnt. A wrong church is a factual error; a general statement ("many mission schools were founded by Methodist, Catholic and Church of Christ missionaries") is safe.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about Christianity"}
Muslim madrasah and Jewish community education also exist. Mention them briefly when a question asks about religions in general.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the contribution without explaining"}
"They trained teachers" earns a mark; "they trained teachers, who then staffed rural schools and so raised literacy" earns the development mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Praising or criticising mission education without balance"}
Report the benefits and the criticisms, and let the evidence lead to a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): explain the role of religions in establishing institutions"}
Give four to six developed points: reasons for founding schools, named examples, what was taught, and who was reached. Name two or three examples correctly.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess the contribution to human resource capacity"}
Write one paragraph on contributions (teachers, nurses, leaders, literacy), one on criticisms (colonial control, bias, cost, loss of custom), and a conclusion such as "the contribution was large, although it came with limits".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Religious bodies founded schools to spread literacy, train helpers, serve communities and share their faith.
- Examples: Inyati and Hope Fountain (London Missionary Society), Chishawasha and Kutama (Catholic), Old Mutare and Waddilove (Methodist), Dadaya (Church of Christ).
- Church universities include Africa University, the Catholic University of Zimbabwe and Solusi University.
- Muslim communities run madrasah; Jewish education is organised within a small community.
- Mission schools trained teachers, nurses and leaders and recorded local languages.
- Colonial policy left much African education to missions; the contribution and its criticisms should both be reported.
:::
