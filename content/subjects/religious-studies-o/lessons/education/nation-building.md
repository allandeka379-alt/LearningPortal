## Introduction

On a Monday morning in many Zimbabwean towns, pupils in a church-run school line up for assembly, sing a hymn or hear a short reading, and then go to lessons in mathematics, science and languages. In another school, a Muslim pupil and a Christian pupil sit side by side in a class on Family and Religious Studies. Behind these ordinary scenes is a partnership: religious bodies and the government working together so that children can learn.

This lesson looks at the role of religion in education and in building the nation **today**, going beyond the history of mission schools (the previous lesson). You should know what a responsible authority is, the idea of human resource capacity, and the right to education in section 75 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe. A good answer to this topic is balanced: it shows both what religions contribute and the concerns that people raise.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nation building"}
The work of strengthening a country's unity, skills, values, economy and institutions so that its people can live together in peace and progress.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsible authority"}
The body (for example a church, a trust or a council) that owns or manages a school, appoints its governing body and works with the Ministry of Education. In Zimbabwe many schools have a church as their responsible authority.
:::

:::definition{term="Chaplaincy"}
A school or college service in which a chaplain (a minister, priest, imam or other religious leader) gives pastoral care, leads worship for those who wish to take part, and counsels students.
:::

:::definition{term="Bursary"}
Money given to pay school or college fees for a student who could not otherwise afford them.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn the six ways: **running schools**, **training teachers**, **bursaries and scholarships**, **special-needs education**, **values and moral education**, **chaplaincy and counselling**. Add partnership with government and national development. Section 75 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe protects the right to education.
:::

## How religious bodies support education today

| Contribution | What it involves | Effect on the nation |
|---|---|---|
| Running schools | Churches and other religious bodies own and manage primary and secondary schools, sometimes with boarding, and work with the Ministry on staffing and standards | more school places, especially in rural areas |
| Teacher training | Church-run teacher training colleges and university education programmes | more qualified teachers |
| Bursaries and scholarships | Church funds, congregations and charities pay fees for orphans and children from poor families | more children stay in school |
| Special-needs education | Some religious bodies run schools, units or hostels for children with hearing, sight or learning needs, or support them with materials | children with disabilities enjoy their right to education |
| Values and moral education | Chapel, assemblies, school rules and teaching on honesty, respect and responsibility; Religious Studies as a subject | young people with a sense of right and wrong |
| Chaplaincy and counselling | Pastoral care for students and staff in times of grief, exams or personal difficulty | healthier, more supported students |
| Adult and community education | Literacy classes, skills training, health education | more skilled citizens |

Muslim organisations and Jewish communities also support education within their own communities, and members of many faiths give time and money to school development projects.

:::example{title="A bursary from the congregation"}
At a church in Bindura the members hold a harvest thanksgiving every year. Part of the gifts is sold, and the money goes into a bursary fund. Last term the fund paid school fees for Rudo, an orphan, who is now sitting her O-Level examinations. Without the fund she may have had to leave school. The example shows how a religious community, acting from its teaching on care for the needy, supports education and so helps to prepare a skilled adult for the nation.
:::

## Partnership with government

The government sets the national curriculum, examinations, teacher registration and standards. Religious bodies, as responsible authorities, manage the premises, help to raise funds, set the ethos of the school and take part in governance. This partnership shares the cost and effort of education. The Constitution protects the right to education (section 75), and it also protects freedom of conscience and religion (section 60), so all schools must respect the beliefs of pupils. A church-run school may hold worship, yet no pupil should be forced to abandon their own faith.

## Contribution to national development

Religious involvement in education supports nation building in these ways:

- **human resources**: graduates, nurses, teachers and technicians are produced, which supports the economy;
- **values**: honesty, respect, unhu/ubuntu, peace and responsibility are taught, which strengthen the community and reduce crime and corruption;
- **unity**: pupils of different backgrounds learn side by side, and Religious Studies teaches respect for other religions;
- **inclusion**: bursaries and special-needs provision help children who might otherwise be left out;
- **community development**: schools linked to religious bodies often start health, agriculture and water projects.

:::example{title="A school that teaches respect for other faiths"}
In a school in Mutare, a Religious Studies class has pupils from Christian, Muslim, Indigenous Religion and Jewish homes. For a project each group presents its religion's teaching on honesty. Yusuf, a Muslim pupil, explains the Qur'anic emphasis on truthfulness; Tafadzwa describes the Shona teaching on unhu. Pupils conclude that each religion values honesty. The example shows how education in religion can build mutual respect, which is a foundation of national unity.
:::

## Criticisms, presented fairly

Not everyone agrees about religion in schools, and a good answer reports the concerns as well as the benefits.

| Concern | Explanation | Reply or response |
|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | Some church or mission schools charge high fees or levies, which poor families may struggle to pay | Many also offer bursaries; school fees in public schools are set with the approval of the Ministry |
| **Denominational bias** | A school may favour members of its own church in admissions, staffing or worship | The Constitution guarantees non-discrimination and freedom of conscience; schools are expected to admit pupils of all faiths |
| **Concerns about proselytising** | Some people worry that pupils may be pressed to join a particular faith | Schools should invite, never compel; pupils may opt out of worship |
| **Unequal spread** | Church schools may be concentrated in certain areas | Government provides schools elsewhere |
| **Mixing faith and science** | Disagreements can arise where religious and scientific views differ | Good teaching explains both and respects the pupil's right to ask questions |

These concerns do not cancel the contribution, but they explain why schools, parents and the government keep discussing how religion and education should work together.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing only about the past"}
This lesson is about the present role of religion in education. Mention mission schools briefly, then describe today's contributions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting criticism as proof that religion harms education"}
Cost, bias and proselytising concerns are real but are balanced by the benefits. Weigh them, and avoid slogans.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting non-Christian examples"}
Muslim madrasah, Jewish community education and the Religious Studies class itself are part of the picture.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing without explaining"}
"Bursaries" is a point; "bursaries keep orphans in school, so more children complete their education" is a developed point.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain the contribution"}
Give four to six developed points from the table: schools, teacher training, bursaries, special needs, values, chaplaincy. Add one concrete example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess the role of religion"}
Write two paragraphs of contributions and one of criticisms, and finish with a judgement such as "religion has made a major contribution to education and nation building, provided that schools respect every pupil's freedom of conscience".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Religious bodies run schools, train teachers, give bursaries, support special-needs education, teach values and provide chaplaincy.
- They work with government as responsible authorities; the Constitution protects both education (section 75) and freedom of conscience (section 60).
- Their work builds human resources, values, unity and inclusion.
- Criticisms include cost, denominational bias and concerns about proselytising.
- A balanced answer reports both the contribution and the concerns, and reaches a judgement.
:::
