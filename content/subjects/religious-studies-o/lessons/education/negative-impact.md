## Introduction

Most religious communities in Zimbabwe encourage their children to go to school, and many of the earlier lessons showed how much religion has contributed to education. Yet a fair study also asks a harder question: are there cases where a religious belief or practice, or the way it is applied, stops a child from attending school or learning? Honest answers help communities, religious leaders and government to solve real problems, and they respect children's rights.

This lesson examines the negative impacts of **some** beliefs and practices on education and ways of addressing them. No faith is the problem as a whole: in each case a particular belief or custom, or a particular interpretation of one, is the issue, and within every religion there are leaders who speak strongly in favour of education. You should already know the right to education (section 75 of the Constitution) and the rights of children (section 81), which are the standards against which these practices are measured.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Right to education"}
The right of every child to basic education, protected by section 75 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe and by international agreements such as the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989). Policy makes basic education free and compulsory for all children.
:::

:::definition{term="Child marriage"}
A marriage in which one or both partners is under 18. The Constitution (section 78) sets 18 as the minimum age for marriage, and child marriage is harmful and unlawful.
:::

:::definition{term="Pledging (kuzvarira)"}
The harmful custom of promising or giving a girl in marriage, often to settle a debt or to gain protection or favour. It violates the child's rights and is unlawful.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender bias"}
Treating boys and girls unequally, for example by sending sons to school while keeping daughters at home.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn five groups of problems: **refusal of schooling or subjects**, **child marriage and pledging**, **gender bias**, **conflict between religious and scientific views**, and **clashes of religious activity with school time**. Law: Constitution section 75 (education), section 78 (marriage at 18 or over), section 81 (children's rights); the Education Amendment Act 2020 (free and compulsory basic education as policy, in outline).
:::

## Beliefs and practices that can harm education

| Problem | How it affects education | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Refusal of schooling, or of some subjects or services | A child may miss school entirely, or a part of the curriculum such as health education or some examinations, because parents or leaders believe modern education is unnecessary or harmful | A small number of cases; no group should be stigmatised, and most religious leaders support schooling |
| Child marriage and pledging | A girl is withdrawn from school to marry or to be given to another family; she loses education, health and opportunity | Illegal; harms the girl's rights; sometimes linked to poverty and to the idea that a girl's role is only in the home |
| Gender bias | Parents with limited money send sons first because they believe daughters will marry into another family; girls do more household work | Found in many societies and justified sometimes by religious or customary teaching; religions also contain teachings of equal worth |
| Conflict between scientific and religious views | A pupil may find it hard to accept a scientific idea that seems to contradict their family's religious teaching; a teacher may dismiss the pupil's faith | Good teaching explains the science and respects the pupil's belief; many believers see no conflict |
| Religious activities that clash with school time | Long ceremonies, all-night services, funerals, initiation seasons, pilgrimages or fasting can keep children out of class or make them too tired to learn | A matter of planning; many schools make allowances |
| Expectations about early adult roles | Some young people are expected to become religious workers, caretakers or earners early, ending their studies | Less common but real |

:::example{title="A girl withdrawn from school"}
Nyasha, aged fourteen, is one of the best pupils in her class at a school near Mutare. After her father's death, a relative arranges for her to be promised in marriage to an older man in return for money for the family. Her teachers visit her aunt, and the headmaster reports the case to the Victim Friendly Unit of the police and to the local child-protection committee, in line with section 78 of the Constitution. With help from a church bursary, Nyasha returns to school. The example shows how child marriage and pledging end education, how poverty and customary pressure can lie behind them, and how the law and the community can respond.
:::

:::example{title="A pupil between school and faith"}
Farai's family belongs to a congregation that holds a long ceremony every year, during term time. Farai misses a week of school. His teacher speaks with his parents and with the church elders, and together they agree that the main ceremony will be held in the school holidays, with a shorter service during the term. The example shows that conflicts between religious activities and school time can often be solved by dialogue and flexibility, and that most religious leaders want children to succeed in school.
:::

## The law and the right to education

The Constitution protects every child's right to basic state-funded education (section 75), the rights of children to protection from exploitation and abuse (section 81), and the minimum age of marriage, 18 (section 78). The Education Amendment Act 2020 aligns the law with these rights and sets the policy of free and compulsory basic education. Freedom of religion (section 60) is protected, but it does not allow parents or leaders to deny a child's rights to education and safety. Where children's rights and religious practice appear to clash, the best interests of the child must come first.

## How can the problems be addressed?

- **Dialogue and awareness.** Teachers, headmasters, chiefs, church and mosque leaders talk with parents about the value of education for boys and girls.
- **Religious leaders teaching in favour of education.** Many pastors, imams and elders preach that learning is a duty and that all children have equal worth.
- **Enforcing the law.** Cases of child marriage and pledging can be reported to the police Victim Friendly Unit, to a trusted adult or to Childline Zimbabwe (116).
- **Support for poor families.** Bursaries, school feeding and fee relief reduce the pressure that leads to pledging and child marriage.
- **Flexible school arrangements.** Schools and communities agree on dates of ceremonies, rest days and worship so that learning is not interrupted.
- **Respectful teaching of science and religion.** Teachers explain scientific ideas without ridiculing belief, and religious leaders accept that faith and science can both serve the pupil.
- **Keeping girls in school.** Policies that allow girls who leave school to return, and community campaigns on girls' education.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming a whole religion"}
Describe a specific belief or practice and say that many members of the same religion reject it. Avoid language that stigmatises any community.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about girls"}
Gender bias and child marriage are important, but you should also mention boys' problems, such as being withdrawn early to herd cattle or earn money, and other negative impacts such as ceremonies and clashes of views.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating problems without solutions"}
Syllabus objective 7.4.2 asks you to suggest ways of addressing them. Always add solutions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the law"}
Mention section 75 and, where relevant, section 78 and the Education Amendment Act 2020 to show that the right to education is protected.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain negative impacts"}
Give four to six developed points, each naming a belief or practice and its effect on education, with one concrete example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): how can the problems be solved?"}
Suggest three or four solutions (dialogue, law, bursaries, religious leaders' support), say who would act and why it would work, then judge which is most effective.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Some beliefs and practices can harm education: refusing schooling or subjects, child marriage and pledging, gender bias, clashes of views and activities.
- Most religious communities support education; the problems come from particular practices, not from whole religions.
- Section 75 protects the right to education; section 78 sets the minimum marriage age at 18; the Education Amendment Act 2020 supports free and compulsory basic education.
- Solutions: dialogue, religious leaders' support, enforcing the law, help for poor families and flexible school arrangements.
- Report harmful practices to a trusted adult, the police Victim Friendly Unit or Childline (116).
:::
