## Introduction

When a grandmother dies in a village near Masvingo, her family in Harare, Bulawayo and Mutare drops what it is doing and travels home. Neighbours arrive with firewood, maize meal and pots, and a funeral that may last several days begins. Death in Zimbabwe is a family and community event, and in Indigenous Religion (IR) it is not the end of a person's relationship with the family.

This lesson explains how IR understands death, the main funeral and burial customs, the ceremony that brings the spirit home, and how these customs are changing. You should already know about the ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi) and rites of passage.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Living dead"}
A phrase used by the scholar John Mbiti for ancestors, who are believed to remain part of the family and to keep an interest in its life, even though they have died.
:::

:::definition{term="Kurova guva / umbuyiso"}
The ceremony of "bringing home" the spirit of a dead person, in Shona kurova guva ("striking the grave") and in Ndebele umbuyiso ("bringing back"), held about a year or more after death so that the spirit can join the family's ancestors.
:::

:::definition{term="Purification"}
Ritual cleansing, often by washing with water, after contact with death, marking the return of mourners to normal life.
:::

## Beliefs about death

In IR, death is understood as a **transition**, not an ending. The person's body is buried, but the spirit is believed to continue in the spirit world, close to the living, and to take a place among the ancestors when the correct rites are done. Death is therefore a rite of passage: the person is separated from the living (burial), passes through a period of mourning and uncertainty, and is later incorporated among the ancestors (kurova guva/umbuyiso).

Families often ask not only *how* a person died but *why*. Old age and illness are accepted as ordinary causes. In some families people also consult a n'anga/inyanga (a traditional healer) to understand a death, or look for a spiritual cause such as an unhappy ancestor. Accusations of witchcraft have caused great harm to innocent people, and it is an offence in the law of Zimbabwe to accuse another person of witchcraft.

## Funeral and burial customs

Practices vary between peoples and families, but the following are widely described.

| Stage | What happens | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Announcement | Relatives and the village headman are informed; the family gathers | The community is told that one of its members has died |
| Preparing the body | Close relatives, usually of the same sex as the deceased, wash and dress the body | A last act of respect |
| Wake | Mourners gather at the homestead for singing, prayers and speeches, often through the night | The family is supported and the dead are honoured |
| Burial | Men dig the grave; relatives bring the body to the graveside, and the family and elders speak | The body returns to the earth of the ancestors |
| Purification | After burial mourners wash their hands in water, sometimes with herbs, before eating | Marks the return to ordinary life |
| Mourning | The close family, especially a widow or widower, observes mourning customs for a period | Time for grief and respect |

The role of relatives is central. Brothers, uncles and in-laws take on duties: the paternal aunt (vatete) and maternal uncle (sekuru/malume) have particular roles in many families. Neighbours bring food, firewood and money, and women prepare meals for those who come, so that grief is shared.

:::example{title="A funeral at a rural home"}
Mr Moyo dies in Gweru. His body is taken to his rural home in Gwanda, where relatives wash and dress the body.

1. The village headman and neighbours are informed and arrive to help.
2. Mourners gather for a night vigil with songs and speeches.
3. The grave is dug by the men of the family and neighbours.
4. After the burial the family and mourners wash their hands in water, then share a meal prepared by the women.
5. The family tells the ancestors of the death and begins the mourning period.

The family shows respect for the dead, mutual support, and the belief that the spirit has left the body and is on its way to the world of the ancestors.
:::

## Kurova guva / umbuyiso

Many families believe that the spirit of a person who has recently died is not yet fully at rest and may wander near the family. After a period, often around a year, the family holds the **kurova guva** or **umbuyiso**.

- **Preparation:** relatives gather, beer is brewed and an animal such as a beast or goat is slaughtered.
- **The ceremony:** the family goes to the grave, speaks to the spirit, and invites it to come home. In many families the spirit is said to be received at the homestead and may speak through a family member or spirit medium.
- **Meaning:** the spirit becomes an ancestor who guides and protects the family. It can be called on in future ceremonies.

Without this ceremony, families fear that the spirit may be restless and could bring misfortune. This is why elders insist that the ceremony is not skipped, even when the cost is high.

## Inheritance rituals (outline)

In many families, after the funeral or the bringing-home ceremony, relatives meet to decide who will take the deceased's role in the family and how property will be shared. In the past the heir was usually a brother or the eldest son. Today the law and the Constitution (section 80, women's rights) protect the rights of widows and children, and a surviving spouse and children are entitled to a share of the estate. Property grabbing, in which relatives take everything and leave a widow and children without a home, is harmful and unlawful.

## The living dead and the family

Ancestors are believed to take an active interest in the family. They are asked for protection, help in difficulty and good fortune, and are thought to show displeasure if the family neglects them or breaks taboos. This is why families speak to them at births, marriages, journeys and in sickness.

## Change in funeral practice

Urbanisation, Christianity and economic pressure have changed funerals. Many people die in towns but are buried at the rural home. Families use funeral policies and burial societies, and Christian and Muslim families add their own prayers and services. Some families combine a church service with IR customs such as the ancestors' ceremony. High funeral costs and the length of mourning are discussed in many communities.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing that the dead are worshipped"}
In IR the ancestors are honoured and consulted as members of the family; God (Mwari/uMlimo/uNkulunkulu) is the supreme being. Write "honour" or "communicate with", not "worship" as if they were gods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the funeral with kurova guva"}
The funeral buries the body and begins mourning. Kurova guva/umbuyiso, about a year later, brings the spirit home to be an ancestor.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing customs as the same everywhere"}
Write "in many families" or "among some Shona communities". Practices differ across peoples and families.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In IR death is a transition to the spirit world, not an end of relationship with the family.
- Funeral customs include preparing the body, a wake, burial and purification, with strong support from relatives and neighbours.
- Kurova guva/umbuyiso, usually about a year later, brings the spirit home to join the ancestors.
- Inheritance rituals decide roles and property; the law protects the rights of widows and children.
- Urban life, Christianity and costs have changed funeral customs, and many families combine traditions.
:::
