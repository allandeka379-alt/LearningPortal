## Introduction

In a time of drought, elders in a village in Matabeleland South say, "We must speak to Mwari." In a Shona home, a mother may say, "Musikavanhu ndiye anoziva" (the Creator of people knows). A Ndebele grandfather may speak of uMlimo or uNkulunkulu. All of these speak of God in Indigenous Religion (IR).

The idea of God is at the heart of IR, though outsiders once wrongly claimed that African peoples knew nothing of a supreme God. This lesson describes the names, attributes and activity of God as believers understand them, God's relationship with the ancestors and with people, narratives about creation and death, and the Mwari shrines in the Matopo Hills. You should already know that IR is oral, community-based and part of daily life; the spirit world and the ancestors are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Supreme Being"}
The highest God, creator and ruler of the world, believed in by most followers of Indigenous Religion in Zimbabwe under various names.
:::

:::definition{term="Transcendent and immanent"}
**Transcendent** means beyond the world and above human reach; **immanent** means present and active within the world. Believers describe God as both.
:::

:::definition{term="Creation myth"}
A traditional sacred narrative about how the world and people came into being.
:::

:::definition{term="Intermediary"}
A being who carries requests between people and God. In Indigenous Religion the ancestors act as intermediaries.
:::

:::definition{term="Mwari shrines"}
Places in the Matopo Hills, such as Njelele and Matonjeni, where Mwari is believed to speak and where communities send petitions, especially for rain.
:::

## Names of God

Different peoples use different names, and each name says something about God.

| Name | Language | What it expresses |
|---|---|---|
| Mwari | Shona (and used by Kalanga and others) | The most widespread name for the Supreme Being; linked with the Mwari shrines |
| Musikavanhu | Shona | "Creator of people": God as maker of human beings |
| Nyadenga | Shona | "Owner of the sky": God as ruler of the heavens |
| Dzivaguru | Shona (in some northern communities) | "The great pool": God associated with water and rain |
| Chikara | Shona (recorded in some communities) | Another name for God, used in some places |
| uMlimo | Ndebele | A name for God, also connected with the voice at the Matopo shrines |
| uNkulunkulu | Ndebele | "The great one": God as the supreme being |
| uSimakade | Ndebele | "The eternal one": God who lasts for ever |

Other peoples, such as the Tonga, Venda, Kalanga and Nambya, have their own names. Use only the names you know to be correct, and remember that the meanings given are the commonly explained ones.

## Attributes of God

Believers describe God in several ways.

- **Creator:** God made the world, people and all living things (Musikavanhu).
- **Provider and rain-giver:** God sends rain, makes crops grow and sustains life.
- **Supreme and powerful:** God is above all, including the ancestors and other spirits.
- **Transcendent yet active:** God is high above the world, often thought of as far away, yet cares for people and responds to petitions, especially in times of drought and calamity.
- **Source of moral order:** Many believers hold that serious wrongdoing, such as killing a relative or breaking sacred taboos, can bring God's displeasure, shown for example in drought.
- **Eternal:** God has no beginning and no end (uSimakade).

:::keyfact
In Indigenous Religion, the Supreme Being is generally approached **through intermediaries**, the ancestral spirits, although in times of need people may also pray directly. The ancestors are honoured, but God is supreme.
:::

## God, the ancestors and people

Many believers picture a chain of communication:

| Level | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mwari / uMlimo (God) | Creator and supreme ruler |
| 2 | Territorial or tribal guardian spirits (mhondoro) | Guard the land and the people of the chiefdom, and bring community requests |
| 3 | Family ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi) | Guard the family, receive its offerings and pass on its requests |
| 4 | The living | Make offerings, perform rituals and live according to the moral code |

A family with a problem approaches its own ancestors, who pass the request upward. A whole community with a larger problem, such as drought, may turn to the territorial spirits and the Mwari shrines.

## Traditional narratives of creation and death

Peoples tell different stories, and these are sacred narratives (myths) and not scientific accounts. Common themes include:

- God created the earth and then human beings, often described as being made by God's own power, and gave people rules for life.
- In some Nguni accounts, the first people came out of a reed bed (uthlanga) by God's will.
- God also gave people knowledge: how to farm, to make fire and to live with others.

### The origin of death
A widely told story from Southern Africa says that God sent a messenger with a message of life and another with a message of death. In a common version the chameleon is sent with the message of life, but it moves slowly, and the lizard or another animal arrives first with the message of death. People therefore die. The stories differ from place to place. They help people to speak about a hard fact, and they raise questions about fairness, hope and the ancestors.

:::example{title="Drought and the Mwari shrines"}
In a fictional district near Gwanda, no rain has fallen by the usual time.

1. **Local action:** the community holds mukwerera, led by elders and a spirit medium, and offers beer and prayers to the ancestors.
2. **Larger concern:** when the drought continues, the elders decide to send a delegation, with gifts, to the Mwari shrine in the Matopo Hills.
3. **At the shrine:** the delegation presents the community's petition, and believers hold that the answer is given through the officials of the shrine.
4. **Meaning:** the story shows God as the giver of rain, who is approached with respect and honest conduct, and who is believed to be concerned with the whole community.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a creation account fairly"}
Imagine a grandmother in one community telling how God made the first people, and a Ndebele grandfather telling the reed-bed account.

1. **Different stories:** each community has its own way of telling how people began.
2. **Common themes:** God is the creator, people have a place in the world, and life carries duties to others.
3. **Fair description:** write "In this account, God created…" and not "Africans believed in a silly story".
:::

## The Mwari shrines in outline

The Mwari shrines, including **Njelele** and **Matonjeni** in the Matopo (Matobo) Hills near Bulawayo, are among the best-known centres of Indigenous Religion in Zimbabwe. Communities, including Shona, Kalanga and Ndebele, sent messengers with offerings and requests, especially about rain, harvest and national danger. Believers hold that Mwari answers through the shrine's officials. The shrines are sacred places, protected by rules and taboos, and visitors are expected to behave respectfully.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that Africans had no idea of a supreme God before missionaries"}
Indigenous Religion has had its own names and beliefs about a supreme God (Mwari, uMlimo, uNkulunkulu) for generations.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the ancestors as gods equal to Mwari"}
The ancestors are honoured intermediaries; Mwari is the supreme being.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the creation narratives 'silly stories'"}
They are sacred narratives. Write "According to the creation myth of this community…" and explain what they teach.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the names of God across languages"}
Musikavanhu is Shona ("creator of people"); uSimakade is Ndebele ("the eternal one"). Learn the language with the name.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- God in Indigenous Religion has many names: Mwari, Musikavanhu, Nyadenga, Dzivaguru (Shona); uMlimo, uNkulunkulu, uSimakade (Ndebele); other peoples have their own.
- God is creator, provider, rain-giver, supreme, eternal, transcendent yet active, and source of moral order.
- God is usually approached through the ancestors and territorial spirits, the intermediaries.
- Creation and death are explained in sacred narratives, such as the story of the chameleon and the lizard.
- The Mwari shrines in the Matopo Hills (Njelele, Matonjeni) are centres where communities send petitions, especially for rain.
:::
