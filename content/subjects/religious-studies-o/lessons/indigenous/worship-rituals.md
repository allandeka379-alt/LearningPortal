## Introduction

It is late in the dry season, and the maize is thin. In a village in Mashonaland, women brew beer for days. On the morning of the ceremony, relatives and neighbours gather at the chief's homestead. There is singing, clapping and the sound of drums. An elder speaks to the ancestors, and beer is poured on the ground. A short time later, in a town, a young man asks his grandmother why the family is holding a bira next month, and she answers, "To thank our grandfather and to ask for his guidance."

This lesson describes how Indigenous Religion (IR) is practised: prayer, offerings, ceremonies, music and sacred days. You should know the beliefs about God and the spirits and the practitioners and sacred places from the earlier lessons. Rites of passage such as birth, initiation, marriage and death are separate lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Worship (in Indigenous Religion)"}
Acts of respect and devotion directed to God (Mwari/uMlimo) and the ancestors, usually through prayer, offerings, song and ceremony.
:::

:::definition{term="Libation"}
The pouring out of a drink, such as traditional beer, as an offering. It is often done on the ground, with words to the ancestors.
:::

:::definition{term="Bira (ukuthetha in Ndebele)"}
A ceremony, often at night, with mbira music, singing and dancing, at which the family communicates with the ancestors, thanks them and asks for guidance.
:::

:::definition{term="Mukwerera (ukucela izulu in Ndebele)"}
The rain-making ceremony in which a community, led by elders and a medium, asks God and the ancestors for rain.
:::

:::definition{term="Chisi"}
A day of rest, observed in many Shona chiefdoms, on which fields are not worked, in respect of the land and the ancestors.
:::

## Forms of worship and ritual

| Form | What happens | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Prayer through the ancestors | An elder speaks to the ancestors, naming them, to carry the request to God | Respect for God's greatness and for the family line |
| Offerings and libations | Traditional beer (doro in Shona, umqombothi in Ndebele), snuff and sometimes food are offered, often by pouring on the ground or placing them at a shrine | Thanks, honour and a sign of the relationship with the ancestors |
| Bira / ukuthetha | A night of mbira, singing, clapping and dancing; a medium may be possessed; beer is shared | Communication with the ancestors, thanksgiving, guidance and unity |
| Rain-making: mukwerera / ukucela izulu | Elders, medium and community gather at a chosen place, brew beer, pray, sing and make offerings | Asking God and the ancestors for rain and harvests |
| Thanksgiving and first fruits | New grain is presented, with thanks, before it is eaten widely; in the Ndebele state the *inxwala* was the national first-fruits ceremony | Gratitude for the harvest and renewal of the community |
| Music, dance and clapping | Mbira, drums, rattles and songs accompany most rituals | Express feeling, set the ceremony apart and help to call the ancestors |

:::keyfact
Worship in Indigenous Religion is **communal** and **practical**: it is done by families and communities, usually to give thanks, to ask for rain, health or guidance, and to keep the relationship with the ancestors strong.
:::

### A bira step by step
1. **Reason:** the family decides on a bira, for example to thank an ancestor or after a time of difficulty.
2. **Preparation:** relatives are told, and the women brew beer over several days.
3. **Gathering:** on the day the family and neighbours gather.
4. **Ceremony:** the mbira is played, songs are sung and the elders speak to the ancestors.
5. **Possession:** the medium may be believed to be possessed by a spirit, who gives guidance.
6. **Closing:** the beer and food are shared, and the gathering ends in the morning.

The bira is not a form of entertainment; it is understood as a religious meeting between the living and the ancestors.

### Mukwerera, step by step
1. **Decision:** the chief and elders decide when the rains are late.
2. **Preparation:** households contribute grain, and beer is brewed.
3. **Gathering:** the community assembles at the chief's place or a sacred place, with the medium.
4. **Prayer and offering:** elders pray to the ancestors and to Mwari, and beer is poured out.
5. **Singing and dancing:** the community sings, claps and dances.
6. **After:** people hope for rain, behave carefully and may return with thanks. If the rain does not come, they may consult elders again.

## Sacred days of rest

In many Shona chiefdoms a day is set aside as **chisi**, when no ploughing or fieldwork is done. Which day it is differs from area to area. People who break chisi may be reminded by elders, fined or asked to hold a ceremony. The significance of chisi:

- **Religious:** respect for the land, the ancestors and God, believed to bring good rains and harvests;
- **Social:** the whole community rests on the same day, which gives time for gatherings and visiting;
- **Practical and conservation:** the land and people have rest, and the rule is followed by everyone, so it is fair.

Not all communities observe chisi in the same way, and many families today combine it with another day of rest.

:::example{title="A rain ceremony in a fictional ward near Chivi"}
In a fictional ward near Chivi, the rains are late, and the chief calls the elders.

1. **Contributions:** each household brings a small amount of grain for beer.
2. **Ceremony:** at the chosen place, the medium and elders greet the ancestors, pour beer on the ground and ask for rain, and the community sings and claps.
3. **Unity:** families who had quarrelled are encouraged to make peace before the ceremony.
4. **Meaning:** the ceremony expresses dependence on God and the ancestors, and strengthens unity.
:::

:::example{title="A family bira in a Bulawayo suburb"}
A fictional family in a Bulawayo suburb, whose grandfather has been honoured at his homecoming ceremony, holds a bira.

1. **Reason:** to thank the grandfather's spirit for the family's health during a difficult year.
2. **Preparations:** relatives from other towns travel to Bulawayo, and beer is brewed.
3. **Ceremony:** through the night, elders speak to the ancestors, songs are sung and a medium is believed to give guidance.
4. **Result:** the family feels closer to its ancestors and each other, and the youth learn the songs and praises.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing worship as 'worshipping the dead'"}
Believers honour and seek guidance from the ancestors but worship God. Say "venerate" or "honour" for the ancestors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the bira a party"}
The bira includes music, food and dance, but its purpose is religious: communication with the ancestors, thanksgiving and guidance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mukwerera and bira"}
Mukwerera is a communal ceremony to ask for rain; a bira is usually a family ceremony to honour the ancestors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that every community keeps chisi on the same day"}
The day differs between chiefdoms. Say "in many chiefdoms a day of rest called chisi is observed".
:::

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain the importance of rituals or of chisi*, give religious, social and practical reasons, and use examples. Where the question allows, add the view that ceremonies renew unity and pass on tradition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Worship in Indigenous Religion includes prayer through the ancestors, offerings and libations, the bira (ukuthetha), rain-making (mukwerera/ukucela izulu), thanksgiving and first-fruits ceremonies.
- Music, dance and clapping with the mbira, drums and rattles are part of most rituals.
- Offerings include traditional beer (doro, umqombothi), snuff and sometimes food.
- In many chiefdoms a day of rest, chisi, is observed to respect the land and the ancestors.
- Ceremonies express thanks and dependence, renew unity, pass on tradition and keep the link between the living and the ancestors.
:::
