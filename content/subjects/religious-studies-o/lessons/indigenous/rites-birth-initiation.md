## Introduction

A baby is born in a family in Gweru. Within days, aunts and grandmothers are busy, a name is being discussed and relatives are phoning from Harare and Bulawayo to ask when they may visit. The arrival of a child is never only a private event. In Indigenous Religion (IR) it is a moment when the family, the living and the ancestors, welcomes a new member and asks for the child's protection.

This lesson looks at the early stages of life: pregnancy, birth, naming and, in some communities, initiation into adulthood. You should already know what Indigenous Religion is and who the ancestors are (midzimu in Shona, amadlozi in Ndebele). Marriage and death rites have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rite of passage"}
A ceremony that marks a person's move from one stage of life or social status to another, such as birth, adulthood, marriage and death.
:::

:::definition{term="Initiation"}
A rite, often with a period of teaching, through which a young person is accepted as a responsible adult member of the community.
:::

:::definition{term="Puberty rite"}
A ceremony or period of instruction that marks the body's change from child to young adult and prepares the young person for adult duties.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Three stages of a rite of passage"}
The scholar Arnold van Gennep (1909) described rites of passage in three stages: **separation** (the person leaves the old status), **transition** (a period in between, often with teaching or tests) and **incorporation** (the person is welcomed back with a new status). This is a tool for study; communities use their own words for what they do.
:::

## Why rites of passage matter

In IR, life is seen as a journey that the whole family travels together. Each rite does three things: it announces a change to the community, it asks the ancestors to protect the person, and it teaches the new duties that come with the change.

| Stage of life | Main idea of the rite | Who takes part |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pregnancy and birth | Safe delivery; welcome of a new life | Mother, older women, husband's and wife's relatives |
| Naming | Identity and belonging | Parents, grandparents, elders |
| Initiation (where practised) | Entry into adulthood | Young people, instructors, elders |

## Pregnancy and birth

Pregnancy is welcomed because children continue the lineage and, in IR, the ancestors may return to the family through a newborn. Older women, such as the grandmother and aunts, advise the mother about food, work and rest. In many families, a woman expecting her first child spends time with, or gives birth near, her own mother or other close female relatives, who care for her afterwards.

Customs differ between peoples and families, and many are now combined with clinic care. In outline, some communities treat the dried umbilical cord stump with special care, for example by burying or keeping it, because it is seen as a link between the child and the family. The soft spot on a baby's head (the fontanelle) is handled gently in many communities. Mother and baby may be kept at home for some days or weeks before being formally introduced to relatives, so that both can recover.

Today the Ministry of Health and Child Care encourages women to give birth at a clinic or hospital with a trained midwife and to attend antenatal care. Many families see no conflict: they follow health advice and still hold family ceremonies to welcome the child.

## Naming

A name is more than a label. Shona and Ndebele names usually have meanings that reflect the family's situation, hopes or beliefs.

| Name | Language | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tendai | Shona | "Give thanks" |
| Chipo | Shona | "Gift" |
| Farai | Shona | "Be happy" |
| Sipho | Ndebele | "Gift" |
| Themba | Ndebele | "Hope" or "trust" |
| Thandeka | Ndebele | "Lovable" |

Names may record events (a name chosen after a death in the family), express thanks to God or ancestors, or honour a grandparent. Giving the name is often the work of the paternal grandparents or elders. A child may later receive a praise name or take a name from a different religion, as you will study in the lesson on names and totems.

:::example{title="Introducing a baby to the family"}
Tatenda's family in Masvingo has a new baby girl. After a few weeks the elders gather at the homestead.

1. **Separation:** the mother and baby have stayed close to home since the birth.
2. **Transition:** the grandmother carries the baby out, the elders speak to the ancestors, and the family tells them the baby's name and asks for protection.
3. **Incorporation:** relatives greet the child by name, bring gifts and share a meal, so that she is known as a full member of the family.

The meaning: the child belongs to the living family and is placed in the care of the ancestors.
:::

## Initiation and puberty rites

Not every community in Zimbabwe practises initiation, and where it exists it takes different forms. The following are examples, described factually and respectfully.

- **Venda:** girls' schools of instruction called **vhusha** and, at a later stage, **domba**, in which young women learn songs, dances and the responsibilities of adulthood, including marriage and family life.
- **Tsonga/Shangani and some other communities:** initiation schools for boys and girls where elders teach discipline, respect, community history and sexual and family values.
- **Remba (Lemba):** male circumcision is part of the community's religious and social identity.

The purposes are similar everywhere. Initiation teaches **respect for elders**, **responsibility**, **keeping community secrets**, **sex and family education**, **courage and endurance**, and the idea that adults belong to the whole community. Because the teaching is shared, the group of young people who go through initiation together often keep a strong bond for life.

### Contemporary debates

People inside and outside these communities discuss how initiation should work today.

- **Health:** circumcision should be done safely by trained people in clean conditions. The Ministry of Health and Child Care offers voluntary medical male circumcision, which reduces the risk of HIV infection for men, and families are advised to seek a trained health worker.
- **Consent and children's rights:** the Constitution of Zimbabwe protects children's rights (section 81). Any rite must not harm a child or force a young person against their will.
- **Schooling:** instruction should not stop children from attending school. Some communities hold rites during school holidays.
- **Change:** some families keep the full rite, some keep only the teaching, and some have replaced it with other ceremonies.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling rites 'primitive'"}
Describe rites as a community's way of marking life stages, not as outdated or backward. Examiners reward accurate, respectful description.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all Zimbabweans practise initiation"}
Initiation is practised in some communities only. Write "among the Venda, for example" rather than "Indigenous Religion requires initiation".
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing instead of explaining"}
Writing "naming, birth, initiation" earns little. For each rite say what happens and what it means (for example, naming gives identity and belonging).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or explain the purpose of a rite"}
In Paper 1 (a) *describe* means give the stages of the rite in order, in about two-mark points. In (b) *explain the significance* means link each practice to a meaning: "The child is named before the elders so that the ancestors know the child and protect her."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss the debates"}
For *how relevant are initiation rites today?*, give reasons on both sides (teaching of values against health and consent concerns) and a short judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A rite of passage marks a change of status; van Gennep described three stages: separation, transition, incorporation.
- In IR a birth is welcomed by the family and the ancestors, and older women support the mother.
- Names carry meaning and give identity and belonging.
- Initiation is practised by some communities, such as the Venda (vhusha and domba), to teach responsibility and values.
- Today health, consent and schooling shape how rites are carried out.
:::
