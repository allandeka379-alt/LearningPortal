## Introduction

Travellers in the Matopo Hills pass hills where visitors are asked to behave quietly. In some places a forest is left untouched, though firewood is scarce nearby. In others, a pool is said to be the home of a spirit, and nobody washes clothes in it. At a ceremony the medium wears particular cloths, the beer is served in clay pots, and the mbira player sits by the fire.

For believers, places, objects, clothing and symbols are not ordinary. They are set apart as **sacred**, and they carry meaning, protection and respect. This lesson describes the sacred places, objects, attire and symbols of Indigenous Religion (IR), and the taboos that keep them safe. You should know the practitioners and spirits from the previous lessons. The rituals performed at these places are in the next lesson, and the identity role of totems is covered in the family topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sacred place"}
A place set apart as holy because it is linked with God, the ancestors or the spirits, and treated with special respect.
:::

:::definition{term="Sacred object"}
An item used in worship or believed to have spiritual meaning, such as a beer pot, snuff, a staff or a musical instrument.
:::

:::definition{term="Taboo (zviera)"}
A rule that forbids certain actions or contact because they are believed to offend God or the spirits or to cause harm.
:::

:::definition{term="Totem (mutupo in Shona, isibongo in Ndebele)"}
An animal or other emblem linked with a clan, respected as a symbol of the clan's identity and often protected by rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Shrine"}
A place where worship is offered and where the sacred is believed to be especially present.
:::

## Sacred places

| Place | Why it is sacred | Example and care |
|---|---|---|
| Mountains and hills | Believed to be places where God or the spirits are close; sometimes sites of shrines | The Matopo Hills, with the Mwari shrines Njelele and Matonjeni |
| Caves | Linked with ancestors, burials or spirits; used for ceremonies | Caves used for ceremonies, often with rules for visitors |
| Pools, springs and rivers | Associated with water, rain and spirits | A pool where people do not wash or fish |
| Forests and groves | Protected as the home of spirits and as burial grounds | A grove where trees are not cut |
| Shrines | Places for offerings and prayer | The Mwari shrines; a family's own shrine place |
| Great Zimbabwe | A large stone city built from about the eleventh century, a centre of power and, for many, of religion; a national symbol | The Zimbabwe Bird stone carvings; people visit for history, and some for ceremonies |
| Graves | Places of the ancestors | Respect at the family burial place and at the homestead |

:::keyfact
Great Zimbabwe is a **World Heritage Site** (inscribed by UNESCO in 1986), a national symbol and, for many people, a sacred place. The country takes its name from the Shona *dzimba dzemabwe*, commonly explained as "houses of stone".
:::

### How sacred places are protected
Sacred places are protected by **taboos and customs** that are taught by the elders:

- not cutting trees or taking plants and animals from groves and shrines;
- not polluting or fishing in sacred pools;
- speaking and behaving quietly and respectfully, and seeking permission from the local custodian before visiting;
- in some places, observing rules on dress or on who may enter.

These customs have a side effect: they conserve forests, water and wildlife. Environmental effects are covered more fully in the environment topic.

## Sacred objects

| Object | Use |
|---|---|
| Clay beer pots and gourds | Hold traditional beer or water that is offered to the ancestors and shared |
| Snuff | Offered to the ancestors and shared as a sign of respect |
| Mbira | A musical instrument played at the bira, believed by many to help call the ancestors |
| Drums (ngoma/isigubhu) and rattles (hosho) | Give the rhythm for singing and dancing at ceremonies |
| Staffs and ritual axes | Signs of authority and role carried by elders, chiefs or mediums |
| Cloths, beads and animal skins | Worn or used to show a role in a ceremony |

## Attire and colours

Mediums, elders and other participants often wear special clothing. Mediums are commonly associated with cloths in black, white and red. The meaning of the colours varies between communities and families, so do not assume one meaning for all. Beads and animal skins may be worn as signs of a role. The attire marks that the wearer is acting in a sacred role, and sets the occasion apart from ordinary life.

:::example{title="Visiting a sacred grove near Marondera"}
A group of learners from a fictional school in Marondera visit a protected grove with their teacher.

1. **Permission:** the teacher first asks the local headman, who sends a guide.
2. **Instructions:** the guide asks the group to speak quietly, to take nothing from the grove and to leave no litter.
3. **Explanation:** the guide explains that, for the local community, the trees shelter the graves and spirits of their ancestors, so no one cuts them.
4. **Result:** the learners understand why the grove is sacred and see that the taboos also protect the forest.
:::

:::example{title="Sacred objects at a family ceremony"}
Before a ceremony in a fictional family in Gweru, the elders prepare the setting.

1. **Beer pots:** the women brew the beer and bring the clay pots to the place chosen for the ceremony.
2. **Snuff:** the elder opens the snuff and shares it with respect.
3. **Music:** the mbira and hosho players take their places.
4. **Attire:** the medium arrives wearing the cloths of the role.
5. **Meaning:** each object marks the occasion as sacred and tells the family what to do.
:::

## Totems as sacred symbols (outline)

A totem is a sacred symbol of a clan. In Shona communities, examples include animals such as the lion (shumba), the zebra (mbizi) and the elephant (nzou), and in Ndebele communities the *isibongo* serves a similar function. A person may not harm or eat their totem animal, and the totem teaches respect for living creatures and gives identity. The role of totems in family and identity is studied in the topic on family, and the theory of totemism in the lesson on the origins of religion.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling sacred places 'just old trees and rocks'"}
For believers these places are linked with God, the ancestors and the spirits. Describe them as sacred and say why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving one meaning for the colours of a medium's cloths"}
Meanings vary between communities. Say what the cloths show (a sacred role) and avoid fixed meanings unless you are sure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sacred places with rituals"}
This lesson covers places, objects, attire and symbols. The rituals performed there are in the next lesson.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the conservation effect of taboos"}
Taboos protect groves, pools and wildlife. Mention this when you explain why taboos matter.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (a)"}
When asked to *describe sacred places*, give the place, why it is sacred and one rule that protects it. For objects and attire, say what the item is and what it does. About seven well-explained points earn full marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain why taboos protect sacred places*, give reasons such as respect for the spirits, protection of forests and water, and shared responsibility, and say what may follow if a taboo is broken (a fine, a ceremony to restore balance).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sacred places in IR include mountains, caves, pools, forests and groves, shrines such as Njelele, Great Zimbabwe and graves.
- Sacred objects include beer pots, snuff, the mbira, drums, rattles, staffs, axes, cloths, beads and skins.
- Mediums and elders wear attire, including black, white and red cloths, to mark a sacred role; the meaning of colours varies.
- Totems are sacred symbols of clans, protected by rules.
- Taboos protect sacred places and also conserve forests, water and wildlife.
:::
