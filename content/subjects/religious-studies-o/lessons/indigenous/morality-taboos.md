## Introduction

A boy in a village near Hwange is told not to cut a certain tree beside the pool, and not to fish there on the rest day. Nobody has given him a long explanation. The rule comes from his grandmother, and behind her, from the elders and the ancestors. Later, at school, he learns that the pool and the trees are an important water source for the whole village.

This lesson explains how Indigenous Religion (IR) teaches right and wrong: where its morality comes from, what it teaches, how taboos work and what happens to people who break the rules. The general link between religion and morality (including unhu/ubuntu) is a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Morality"}
A community's ideas of right and wrong behaviour.
:::

:::definition{term="Taboo"}
A strong prohibition, based on a community's beliefs, against certain actions, places or things. In Shona taboos are **zviera** and in Ndebele **imilayo**.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanction"}
A penalty or consequence for breaking a rule; the opposite of a reward.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Sources of IR morality"}
God (Mwari/uMlimo/uNkulunkulu), the ancestors, the elders and the chief, and the community's long experience passed on in stories, proverbs and rites.
:::

## Sources of morality

IR morality is not found in a single written book. It is passed on orally by parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles, by elders at the *dare* or *inkundla* (the meeting place), by initiation instruction where it is practised, and through proverbs, riddles, songs and folk tales. Its authority comes from the belief that **Mwari/uMlimo** upholds right behaviour, that the **ancestors** watch over the family and care that the rules are kept, and that **the community** has tested the rules over many generations.

## What IR teaches

| Value | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Respect for elders | Greeting politely, listening, not interrupting, caring for older people |
| Hospitality | Welcoming visitors and offering water or food |
| Honesty | Telling the truth; not stealing; keeping promises |
| Sharing | Giving to neighbours in need; sharing the harvest and meat from a hunt |
| Chastity and faithfulness | Respecting marriage and the body; sexual relations in marriage |
| Respect for nature | Protecting water sources, forests and wildlife |
| Cooperation | Working together at *nhimbe/ilima* work parties |

## Taboos (zviera/imilayo)

Taboos are rules that people observe without needing a written law. Examples include:

- **Totem taboos:** not eating the animal of one's own totem (mutupo/isibongo), and not marrying a person of the same totem, which is treated as a form of incest.
- **Chisi:** the rest day on which farmers are told not to work in the fields, so that the land and the spirits are respected.
- **Sacred places:** not cutting trees or hunting in sacred groves, not polluting certain pools or hills.
- **Respect for elders and the dead:** not insulting an elder, and not disturbing graves.
- **Incest:** a strong prohibition on sexual relations between close relatives.

| Purpose | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Social order | Not marrying within the same totem prevents close relatives from marrying |
| Health | Rules about cleanliness after contact with death protect others from illness |
| Conservation | A ban on cutting trees or fishing at a sacred pool helps protect water and wildlife |
| Respect | Not insulting elders keeps the family strong and gives children a model of behaviour |

:::example{title="A taboo at a sacred pool"}
In the fictional village of Mukumbura near Kariba, the elders forbid fishing at the sacred pool on the rest day and forbid cutting trees on its banks.

1. **Belief:** the pool belongs to the ancestors and the spirit of the place.
2. **Practice:** villagers fish elsewhere, and cut wood from other areas.
3. **Result:** the water stays clean and the trees hold the soil, so the pool does not dry up.

For villagers, the reason is respect for the ancestors; for a scientist, the effect is conservation. Both explain why the taboo has lasted.
:::

## Proverbs and folk tales as moral teaching

Proverbs are short sayings that carry moral advice. **"Chara chimwe hachitswanyi inda"** (Shona: one thumb cannot crush a louse) teaches cooperation. **"Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu"** (Nguni languages including Ndebele: a person is a person through other people) teaches that we belong to one another. Folk tales (*ngano*, *inganekwane*) told by grandmothers at the fire teach that greed, laziness and disrespect lead to trouble, while kindness and honesty are rewarded.

## Rewards and sanctions

| Type | Reward | Sanction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social | Respect, trust and a good name | Shame, gossip, loss of trust, being avoided |
| Ancestral | Blessing, good health, good harvests (believed to come from the ancestors) | Misfortune or illness believed to be sent by an offended ancestor; for a serious wrong such as murder, a family may fear an avenging spirit (*ngozi*) |
| By the chief's court | Peace in the village | Fines, usually in livestock or money, in matters the court may lawfully decide |

Today chiefs' and headmen's courts handle some customary disputes within the limits set by the Constitution and the law. Accusations of witchcraft and punishments that harm a person's rights are unlawful and harmful.

:::example{title="A folk tale and its lesson"}
A grandmother in Gwanda tells the children of a hare who tricked his neighbours to take their food and ended up with nothing when the rains failed and no one would help him. The children laugh, then ask why no one helped. She replies that a person who has not shared cannot expect to be shared with. The tale teaches sharing, honesty and the value of good relationships, without a lecture.
:::

## Relevance today

Many IR values are widely shared in Zimbabwean schools, churches and mosques: respect, honesty and hospitality remain central. Taboos protecting water and forests link to modern conservation. But some people criticise taboos that restrict girls' or women's choices or encourage fear. Students should be able to explain both the value and the limits.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling taboos superstitions"}
Taboos are rules with purposes in a belief system: social, health, conservation and respect. Examiners expect the purpose, not a judgement that they are foolish.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing taboos without explaining"}
"No fishing on chisi" earns little unless you add *why* (respect for the spirits and conservation of the pool).
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that sanctions are only fear"}
Social sanctions (shame, loss of trust) and the chief's fines also exist, and many people observe taboos because they respect their elders.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or explain taboos"}
In Paper 1 (a), *describe the taboos* needs examples, each with its purpose. In (b) *explain why taboos are important* link the taboo to order, health, conservation or respect.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Assess relevance today"}
For *how relevant is IR morality today?* give reasons for (shared values, conservation) and against (restriction of rights, fear), then conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- IR morality comes from God, the ancestors, the elders and the community, passed on orally.
- It teaches respect, hospitality, honesty, sharing, chastity and respect for nature.
- Taboos (zviera/imilayo) protect social order, health, the environment and respect.
- Proverbs and folk tales teach morals; sanctions are social, ancestral and, in lawful cases, fines by the chief's court.
- Many values and conservation taboos remain relevant, while harmful or rights-restricting practices are criticised.
:::
