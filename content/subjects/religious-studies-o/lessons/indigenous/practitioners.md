## Introduction

At a family bira in Mutare, an elderly man in dark cloth sits near the mbira players. When the music rises, relatives wait quietly. At another time, the same family visits a woman known for her knowledge of herbs, and before a wedding, the groom's party listens carefully to the bride's paternal aunt. In a dispute over a field, the village head and the chief hear both sides.

Each of these people holds a respected role in Indigenous Religion (IR) and in community life. This lesson describes the main **practitioners and leaders**: who they are, how they are chosen, what they do and what limits and responsibilities they have. You should already know the spirit world (midzimu, mhondoro) from the previous lesson; the places and objects they use are covered in the next one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro, plural masvikiro)"}
A person believed to be chosen by a spirit, such as an ancestral or territorial spirit, to speak and act for it during possession.
:::

:::definition{term="Traditional healer"}
A practitioner trusted by the community to treat people, using knowledge of plants, counselling and rituals. The Shona word *n'anga* covers both herbalist and diviner. In Ndebele usage the **inyanga** is a herbalist and the **isangoma** a diviner.
:::

:::definition{term="Divination"}
Seeking to learn the cause of a problem or the wishes of the spirits by traditional means, for example by throwing the hakata, a set of carved divination pieces.
:::

:::definition{term="Custodian"}
A person who guards and looks after something on behalf of a community, such as land, customs and sacred places.
:::

:::definition{term="ZINATHA"}
The Zimbabwe National Traditional Healers Association, a body that represents traditional healers.
:::

## The main practitioners and leaders

| Practitioner | Role | How chosen or trained |
|---|---|---|
| Spirit medium (svikiro) | Hosts a spirit and speaks for it; gives guidance on rain, land, family and community matters; may lead ceremonies | Believed to be called by the spirit, often through dreams or illness, then guided by senior mediums and elders |
| Traditional healer: herbalist (n'anga, inyanga) | Offers treatments from plants and gives counsel | Learns from a senior healer, and may be called in a dream |
| Diviner (n'anga, isangoma) | Seeks the cause of illness or misfortune and the will of the spirits | Called by the spirits and trained over a period by an experienced diviner |
| Rain-makers and ritual leaders | Lead mukwerera/ukucela izulu with elders and the medium | Hold a recognised role by lineage or appointment of the community |
| Chiefs and headmen | Custodians of land, custom and sacred places; settle disputes; lead the community in ceremonies | By succession and the customs of their line; recognised by the Constitution of Zimbabwe (Chapter 15) |
| Family elders | Lead family rituals, speak to the ancestors and teach the young | By seniority within the family |
| Vatete (paternal aunt) and sekuru (maternal uncle or grandfather) | Special roles in family ceremonies, marriage arrangements and mourning | By their place in the family |

:::keyfact
The practitioners are a team. A medium speaks for a spirit, a healer treats and advises, a diviner looks for causes, and chiefs and elders keep order and custom. In many cases they work together with the family.
:::

### Spirit mediums
Believers hold that a spirit chooses the medium, not the other way round. The medium may live under rules, such as in dress, food or behaviour, and is respected as a bridge between the spirit and the people. Territorial mediums have been consulted about rain, land and leadership. Many people regard Nehanda and Kaguvi as great mediums of the past, whose roles in the 1896-97 risings are studied in a later topic.

### Healers and diviners
Traditional healers are valued for counsel, knowledge of plants and support to families. A diviner may use the hakata or other means to look for causes, and then recommend a ceremony or action. Their work is closely linked to belief about the spirit world.

Healers have responsibilities. They should not claim to cure diseases that need modern medicine. For serious illness, including HIV, tuberculosis, diabetes and injuries, a person should go to a trained health worker, as the Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization advise. No herbal remedy has been shown to cure HIV. Many healers work with clinics and refer patients.

### Chiefs and elders
Chiefs and headmen look after the land and its sacred places, hear disputes in customary courts, and lead ceremonies such as mukwerera. Within the family, elders speak to the ancestors, and the vatete and sekuru have recognised duties; for example, the paternal aunt has an important role in teaching girls and in marriage arrangements.

## Qualities and responsibilities

Practitioners are expected to be truthful, respectful of taboos, careful with confidential information and fair. They are not to harm others. Abuse of the role, for example by exploiting patients, demanding large payments, making false claims or accusing a person of witchcraft, is condemned by the community and may be a crime.

:::example{title="A medium and a dispute in a fictional district near Masvingo"}
Two families dispute the boundary of a field, and the village head cannot resolve it.

1. **Consultation:** the elders and the chief ask the territorial medium for guidance, after the proper greetings and offerings.
2. **Guidance:** the medium, believed to speak for the territorial spirit, reminds both families of the shared history of the land and urges them to settle the matter peacefully.
3. **Outcome:** the elders agree on the boundary, the families share a meal, and the village head records the decision.
4. **Meaning:** the medium serves as a spiritual authority who encourages peace, together with the chief and elders.
:::

:::example{title="A healer works with a clinic"}
A traditional healer in a fictional village near Lupane advises a woman who has persistent coughing and weight loss.

1. **Advice:** the healer listens, offers comfort and urges her to go to the clinic, because these signs need tests.
2. **Referral:** the woman is tested and treated at the clinic, and the healer later supports her with encouragement and a family ceremony.
3. **Responsibility:** the healer does not claim to cure the disease and does not blame any person for it.
4. **Result:** both the clinic and the healer have a role, each within its limits.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the mediums, healers and diviners"}
The medium speaks for a spirit; the healer treats and advises; the diviner looks for causes. In Ndebele, inyanga is the herbalist and isangoma the diviner.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing healers as witches"}
Traditional healers and mediums are respected practitioners. Witchcraft is the harmful act that people fear, and healers often oppose it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming that traditional medicine cures diseases"}
An answer should not say that herbs cure serious disease. Say that healers offer care and advice, and that serious illness needs a trained health worker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out chiefs and family elders"}
Practitioners include leaders who are not mediums or healers: chiefs, headmen, elders, vatete and sekuru.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (a)"}
When asked to *describe the roles of practitioners*, name each practitioner, state the role and add an example or a Shona/Ndebele term. Cover at least four kinds, including mediums, healers, chiefs and family elders.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain the importance of practitioners in the community*, develop guidance, healing and counsel, settlement of disputes, care of land and customs, and links with the ancestors. A balanced answer notes the need for responsible practice and for trained health workers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The main practitioners are spirit mediums (masvikiro), healers (n'anga, inyanga), diviners (n'anga, isangoma), rain-makers, chiefs and headmen, and family elders including vatete and sekuru.
- Mediums are believed to be chosen by spirits; healers and diviners are trained and called; chiefs and elders keep custom and land.
- Practitioners guide, counsel, heal, settle disputes and lead ceremonies.
- ZINATHA represents traditional healers; healers should refer serious illness to trained health workers and never claim cures.
- Practitioners are expected to be truthful and fair, and abuse of the role is condemned.
:::
