## Introduction

A family in Harare is preparing for a funeral. The service begins in a church in the suburbs. A few days later the family travels to the rural home, where an uncle speaks to the ancestors before the body is lowered into the grave. The same people take part in both. A grandmother who is a church member also asks a spirit medium for advice about a family quarrel, and her granddaughter uses WhatsApp to tell relatives when to arrive.

This lesson looks at how Indigenous Religion (IR) lives in the Zimbabwe of today. It has met Christianity, colonial rule, towns, schools and new technology. People have responded in different ways, and the picture is mixed. You should already know the main beliefs and rites of IR.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Continuity"}
The way beliefs and practices carry on from the past.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
Changing a belief or practice so that it fits new circumstances, while keeping its meaning.
:::

:::definition{term="Syncretism"}
The blending of beliefs and practices from two or more religions.
:::

:::definition{term="African Independent / Initiated Churches (AICs)"}
Christian churches started by Africans rather than by foreign missions. Many keep Christian teaching while using African forms of worship, healing and community life.
:::

## Meeting new influences

| Influence | What happened | Effect on IR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Christianity | Missions arrived from the sixteenth century (the Jesuit Gonçalo da Silveira, killed at the Mutapa court in 1561), and more widely from the nineteenth century (London Missionary Society at Inyati, 1859) | Many people became Christians; some mission teaching criticised IR practices; others combined faiths |
| Colonialism | Colonial rule from the 1890s brought new laws, land alienation and mission schools | Pressure on traditional leaders and rituals; the Witchcraft Suppression Ordinance of 1899 made accusing people of witchcraft and witch-finding offences; but IR also supported resistance (see the liberation lessons) |
| Urbanisation | Migration to towns and mines | Families live far from sacred places; funerals and ceremonies are adapted to urban life |
| Education | Mission and then state schools | Young people learn other worldviews; some questioned IR, others value it as heritage |
| Technology | Mobile phones, radio, social media | Families coordinate ceremonies; IR is discussed, defended and sometimes criticised online |

## Responses to change

People have not responded in one way.

- **Decline:** in some areas and among some families, fewer people take part in IR rituals, often because they have joined another religion or moved away.
- **Continuity:** many families still hold the bira, kurova guva/umbuyiso, naming, roora/lobola and rain ceremonies, and respect taboos.
- **Adaptation:** families hold ceremonies on weekends so that urban relatives can attend; they combine a church service with ancestral rites; they use clinics for births while keeping naming ceremonies.
- **Revival:** some people, including young people, are showing renewed interest in traditional languages, names, ceremonies and heritage sites.

:::keyfact{title="A balanced statement"}
Say that IR has changed and continues in many forms. Do not claim that it is dying out or that it is superior to other religions.
:::

## Syncretism and the African Independent Churches

Many Zimbabweans belong to AICs, often called apostolic or Zionist churches. These churches teach the Bible and the life of Jesus, and are led by prophets and church elders. They use features familiar from African tradition: drumming, dancing, communal singing, healing by prayer and laying on of hands, and attention to dreams and visions. They are also, in many cases, critical of ancestor rituals, and members differ in how they relate to IR. The AICs are one meeting point between Christianity and African culture; more detail is in the lesson on Christianity in Zimbabwe.

Individuals also practise **syncretism** in their own way, for example by taking part in a Christian wedding and then in roora negotiations.

:::example{title="A family combining traditions"}
Nokuthula's father dies in Bulawayo.

1. The family holds a church service at which the pastor preaches and the choir sings.
2. At the rural home in Matabeleland South, an uncle informs the ancestors of the death.
3. A year later the family holds an umbuyiso, and a minister is invited to pray at the grave.

Some family members see no conflict; others prefer to keep to only one tradition. Both views are respected in the family, and families often discuss these questions respectfully.
:::

## Law and recognition

Zimbabwean law recognises traditional institutions and cultural rights in outline.

- The Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013), section 60, protects freedom of conscience, including the right to practise and change one's religion; section 63 protects the right to take part in the cultural life of one's choice.
- Chapter 15 of the Constitution recognises traditional leaders (chiefs, headmen and village heads) and their role in customary law and local matters.
- The Traditional Medical Practitioners Council, set up by an Act of Parliament in 2000, registers traditional healers and sets standards.

Within the law, the rights of others must also be protected. Harmful practices such as child marriage and accusations of witchcraft are unlawful.

## IR and national life

IR contributes to identity in many ways: names with meanings, praise poetry, the use of Shona and Ndebele languages, rain ceremonies, respect for elders and the idea of unhu/ubuntu. Ceremonies and heritage sites, such as the Matopo Hills, remain important to many people. Some national events begin with traditional music and dance. Citizens who follow other religions also take part in many of these shared cultural customs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying IR is dying out"}
There is no single trend. Some people have left IR; many have kept it, adapted it or are reviving it. Present the range.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling AICs 'pagan' or 'not real Christians'"}
AICs are Christian churches. Describe their blending of Christian and African forms neutrally.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating IR as the same as culture only"}
Culture and religion overlap, but IR is a religion with beliefs about God, ancestors and moral order, not only customs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe or discuss change"}
For Paper 1 (a), *describe how IR has changed* use influences (Christianity, colonialism, urbanisation, education, technology) with an effect for each. For (b) *how far has IR survived?* give evidence for continuity, adaptation and decline, then a conclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Be neutral"}
Use words such as "many", "some" and "adapted". Avoid judging any religion as better or worse.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- IR has met Christianity, colonialism, urban life, education and technology.
- Responses include decline, continuity, adaptation and revival; the picture is mixed.
- Syncretism and the AICs are meeting points of IR and Christianity.
- The Constitution protects freedom of conscience and cultural life, and recognises traditional leaders.
- IR continues to shape identity, ceremonies and national life, within the limits of human rights.
:::
