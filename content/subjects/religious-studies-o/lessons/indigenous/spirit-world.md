## Introduction

Chipo is ill for several weeks, and the clinic finds nothing serious. Her grandmother asks whether the family has forgotten a duty to an ancestor. A neighbour suggests that something else is going on. Her father takes her to the clinic again and also agrees to a family gathering of the elders. In many Zimbabwean homes, a health problem, a dream, a drought or a family quarrel may be discussed in two ways at once: through the clinic or the school, and through the spirit world.

This lesson describes what believers in Indigenous Religion (IR) say about the **spirit world**. We present beliefs as beliefs, without endorsing or ridiculing them. Witchcraft accusations, which have caused real harm, are treated carefully. You should know that IR believes in a Supreme Being (Mwari, uMlimo, uNkulunkulu) and that the ancestors are intermediaries. Practitioners such as spirit mediums and healers are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ancestral spirit (mudzimu, plural midzimu; idlozi, plural amadlozi)"}
The spirit of a deceased family member who is believed to continue caring for the family. It is honoured with offerings and rituals.
:::

:::definition{term="Territorial or tribal guardian spirit (mhondoro)"}
A spirit believed to guard a chiefdom or region and its people, often the spirit of a respected ancestor of the ruling line. *Mhondoro* means "lion".
:::

:::definition{term="Alien spirit (shavi, plural mashavi)"}
A spirit that comes from outside the family or the area, often linked with the spirit of a stranger, and believed to give a person particular skills.
:::

:::definition{term="Avenging spirit (ngozi)"}
The spirit of a person who was wronged, for example by murder or by a debt that was never settled, and who is believed to seek justice from the wrongdoer's family.
:::

:::definition{term="Witchcraft (uroyi in Shona, ubuthakathi in Ndebele)"}
The belief that a person can harm others by secret spiritual means. It is a belief that affects how people explain misfortune.
:::

## Categories of spirits

| Category | Who they are believed to be | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi) | Deceased members of the family who have been properly honoured | Guard the family; bring blessing; may send illness or misfortune if neglected | A grandfather's spirit is thanked at a bira |
| Territorial spirits (mhondoro) | Spirits of great ancestors of a chiefdom or of the land | Guard the land, rain and the people; speak through mediums | Nehanda, Chaminuka and Kaguvi are well-known names in Shona belief and history |
| Alien spirits (mashavi) | Spirits from outside the family, such as strangers who died far from home | Give a person skills, for example in hunting, healing, music or dance | A person with a skill believed to come from a shavi |
| Avenging spirits (ngozi) | Spirits of people who were wronged or killed | Seek compensation or justice from the wrongdoer's family | A family troubled after a death that was never settled |

:::keyfact
Beliefs about the spirit world differ among Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Kalanga and other peoples, and among families. Use words such as "many believers" and "is believed to", and do not describe a belief as true or false.
:::

### How the ancestors become ancestors
Believers explain that a person becomes an ancestral spirit after the proper rites of death are completed. The spirit of the deceased is brought home to the family in the ceremony called **kurova guva** (Shona) or **umbuyiso** (Ndebele), covered in a later lesson. Until then, the spirit is thought to be unsettled. Once welcomed home, it joins the ancestors who protect the family.

### The responsibilities of the living
The relationship is reciprocal. The living honour the ancestors with offerings, prayers and ceremonies, and obey the family's moral rules. In return, the ancestors are believed to protect health, fertility and harvests. When the living neglect their duties, or break taboos, the ancestors may allow misfortune, and the family may consult elders or a practitioner to find out what is needed.

## How spirits are believed to communicate

- **Dreams:** a family member may see an ancestor in a dream with a message.
- **Illness or misfortune:** a long illness or repeated trouble may be understood as a sign that something is wrong in the family's relationship with the spirits.
- **Possession of a medium:** a spirit is believed to speak through a spirit medium (svikiro).
- **Signs in nature:** an unusual animal, such as a lion in the case of a mhondoro, or a particular bird or event may be read as a sign.
- **Divination:** a diviner may be consulted to find out what a spirit wants.

## Witchcraft and its social consequences

Many people hold that witchcraft (uroyi/ubuthakathi) exists and that it can cause illness, death or other misfortune. The belief gives an explanation for why, for example, misfortune falls on one person and not another.

The social consequences can be serious. Accusations of witchcraft have been made against elderly people, widows and others, sometimes without any evidence, leading to quarrels, banishment, beating and killing. Some people have been harmed because they were blamed for a death that a clinic could explain. For this reason:

- Zimbabwean law makes it an offence to accuse someone of witchcraft and treats violence against an accused person as a crime;
- religious leaders of several faiths, including traditional leaders, speak against accusations that harm people;
- a sick person should always be taken to a trained health worker, whatever other steps the family takes.

This lesson describes the belief and its consequences. It does not give any instruction about harming anyone.

:::example{title="A family consults the elders in Chinhoyi"}
In a fictional family in Chinhoyi, three children fall ill in one season, and a crop fails.

1. **Practical steps:** the parents take the children to the clinic and follow the nurse's advice.
2. **Spiritual enquiry:** the family elders meet and ask whether a duty to an ancestor was neglected. A diviner is consulted, and the family decides to hold a ceremony and to repair a quarrel between two brothers.
3. **Result:** the children recover with medical care, and the family believes that the ceremony restored harmony with the ancestors.
4. **Learning:** the example shows how the same event can be understood in medical and religious ways, and why accusations against neighbours are avoided.
:::

:::example{title="An avenging spirit and a settlement"}
In a fictional village, a man owes a debt to another man who dies before it is paid. Later the debtor's family suffers repeated misfortune, and the elders decide that the dead man's spirit may be seeking justice.

1. **Belief:** the spirit of a person wronged in life, a ngozi, is believed to trouble the wrongdoer's family until the matter is put right.
2. **Settlement:** elders mediate, the debt is settled with the dead man's family, and a ceremony is held.
3. **Harmful practice:** in the past, some families settled such matters by giving a girl to the wronged family. Today this is recognised as harmful, it is contrary to the Constitution of Zimbabwe, and it is illegal.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the four kinds of spirit"}
Midzimu/amadlozi are family ancestors, mhondoro guard a region, mashavi come from outside and give skills, and ngozi seek justice for a wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting beliefs as facts or as foolishness"}
Write "believers hold that…" and "is believed to…". Avoid "witches really exist" and "this is just superstition".
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all spirits as evil"}
Most spirits in IR are not evil. Ancestors protect the family; only some spirits, such as an avenging ngozi, are feared.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing witchcraft with the work of healers"}
Traditional healers and spirit mediums are respected practitioners; witchcraft is the harmful act people fear, and healers often oppose it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (a)"}
When asked to *describe the spirit world*, set out the categories (midzimu, mhondoro, mashavi, ngozi), say what each does and give an example. Two marks for each developed point, with Shona or Ndebele terms.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain why the ancestors matter in the life of a family*, develop points on protection, guidance, moral order and the link between the living and the dead, and note the duties of the living.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Believers in Indigenous Religion describe a spirit world with family ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi), territorial spirits (mhondoro), alien spirits (mashavi) and avenging spirits (ngozi).
- Ancestors are honoured with offerings and rituals; in return they are believed to guard the family.
- Spirits are believed to communicate through dreams, illness, mediums, signs and divination.
- Witchcraft (uroyi/ubuthakathi) is a belief about harm through secret means; accusations have caused real harm and are illegal.
- Sick people should see a trained health worker, and beliefs are described respectfully without endorsement or ridicule.
:::
