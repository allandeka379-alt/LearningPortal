## Introduction

On a Sunday in Mutare, a baby named Tendai is carried to the front of a church and water is poured on his head while the priest speaks the names of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. His parents and godparents promise to bring him up in the faith. Two weeks later, his cousin in a Baptist church is lowered into a pool as a teenager, having decided for herself to follow Jesus. Both are baptisms, yet the practice differs. These are examples of the rites through which Christians mark important moments in life and express their faith.

In this lesson you will study the meaning of **sacraments**, the practice of baptism and the Eucharist, the other sacraments in Catholic and Orthodox churches, and Christian rites of passage at birth, marriage and death. You should know the beliefs of Christians and the main denominations before you start, since the differences between Catholics, Orthodox Christians and Protestants matter here.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sacrament"}
A rite that Christians believe is an outward sign of God's grace. In Catholic and Orthodox teaching, a sacrament is a sign through which God gives grace; many Protestants use the term "ordinance" for rites that Christ commanded.
:::

:::definition{term="Ordinance"}
A rite that Jesus commanded, used by many Protestant churches for baptism and Holy Communion, which stress the symbolic meaning and obedience.
:::

:::definition{term="Eucharist"}
The sharing of bread and wine in memory of the Last Supper. Different churches call it the **Mass**, the **Eucharist**, **Holy Communion** or the **Lord's Supper**.
:::

:::definition{term="Rite of passage"}
A ceremony that marks a stage of life, such as birth, marriage or death.
:::

:::keyfact
The seven sacraments of Catholic and Orthodox churches: **baptism, confirmation (chrismation), Eucharist, reconciliation (confession), anointing of the sick, marriage and ordination (holy orders)**. Most Protestant churches keep two: **baptism and Holy Communion**.
:::

## Baptism

Baptism is the rite of entry into the Church, based on the baptism of Jesus by John (Mark 1:9-11) and the command of Jesus to baptise in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19). Water is the sign: it stands for washing away sin and for new life.

| | Infant baptism | Believers' baptism |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Babies and young children, brought by parents and godparents | Older children or adults who have decided to follow Jesus |
| Churches | Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and many others | Baptist, Pentecostal and many AICs |
| How | Water is poured or sprinkled; in the Orthodox Church, the child is immersed | Usually full immersion in water |
| Meaning | Entry into the Church; the parents promise to raise the child in the faith | A public declaration of personal faith |

Those who practise infant baptism usually link it with confirmation in later years, when the young person confirms the promises made on their behalf.

## The Eucharist, Holy Communion or Lord's Supper

The Eucharist goes back to the Last Supper, when Jesus shared bread and wine with his disciples (Mark 14:22-25; 1 Corinthians 11:23-26). Christians break bread and share wine or juice in his memory. Churches understand the rite differently.

- **Catholic:** the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ (called transubstantiation). The Mass is the central act of worship.
- **Orthodox:** the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ, though the Orthodox usually do not use the philosophical term transubstantiation.
- **Anglican and Lutheran:** Christ is present, in ways explained differently by different members.
- **Many other Protestants:** the bread and wine are a symbol and memorial of the death of Christ.

Most Christians receive the bread and wine in a service each Sunday or on special Sundays. Churches also differ on who may receive: some invite all baptised Christians, while others limit it to their own members.

## Other sacraments

| Sacrament | What happens | Churches |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation | A young person confirms the baptismal promises; a bishop lays on hands and anoints with oil in the Catholic Church | Catholic, Anglican, Methodist and others |
| Reconciliation (confession) | A person confesses sins to a priest and receives forgiveness | Catholic, Orthodox |
| Anointing of the sick | A priest anoints a seriously ill person with oil and prays (see James 5:14) | Catholic, Orthodox |
| Marriage | A couple make vows before God and the Church | Sacrament in Catholic and Orthodox churches; a blessed covenant in Protestant churches |
| Ordination | A man (or in some churches a woman) is set apart as deacon, priest or bishop | Catholic and Orthodox sacrament; recognised in other forms in Protestant churches |

## Rites of passage

Christians mark three great moments of life with rites.

**Birth.** Christians welcome a child with baptism or a service of thanksgiving and dedication, in which parents promise to teach the faith. A name is publicly given, often a Christian name, and godparents may be chosen.

**Marriage.** A church wedding usually includes a procession, readings from the Bible, an address, vows, the exchange of rings and a blessing. The couple promise lifelong faithfulness before God and the congregation. Many couples in Zimbabwe hold both a customary marriage (with roora or lobola) and a church wedding; the law recognises civil and registered customary marriages.

**Death.** A Christian funeral is a service of worship that gives thanks for the life of the person, offers comfort and expresses hope in the resurrection. The body is buried or cremated, with prayers at the grave. In many Zimbabwean churches friends and relatives sing hymns throughout the night, and a **memorial service** or **tombstone unveiling** is held some months or years later. Catholics also offer a Mass for the dead.

:::example{title="A church wedding in Gweru"}
Rudo and Farai are married in a Methodist church. The minister reads from the Bible, the couple make their vows, Farai places a ring on Rudo's finger and the minister blesses the marriage. The families sing, and later hold a feast at home. The wedding shows the Christian view that marriage is a lifelong promise made before God and the community, and it has a different form from the family negotiations of the roora stage.
:::

:::example{title="Why a Baptist and a Catholic baptise differently"}
Nokuthula, a Catholic, was baptised as a baby; her godmother held her while the priest poured water. Her friend Themba, a Baptist, was baptised at fifteen after saying publicly that he follows Jesus. Both believe baptism is the beginning of Christian life, yet the Catholic emphasises the Church's promise on behalf of the child and Themba emphasises personal decision. In an exam, describe both and name the churches.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all churches have seven sacraments"}
Catholic and Orthodox churches have seven. Most Protestant churches keep two (baptism and Holy Communion).
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sacrament and ordinance"}
They are different words for similar rites. Catholics and Orthodox use sacrament; many Protestants use ordinance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Eucharist as the same in every church"}
Catholics speak of transubstantiation; many Protestants see the bread and wine as a memorial. Name the group.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the ceremony and not its meaning"}
Always say what each rite means: baptism is entry into the Church and new life; the Eucharist remembers the Last Supper.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (a): describe"}
For *describe the baptism ceremony* or *a Christian wedding* give the steps in order (words, actions, symbols such as water, rings, oil) and name the church whose practice you describe.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 (b): explain"}
For *explain the significance of baptism or the Eucharist* give meaning (new life, remembering the Last Supper), community (belonging to the Church) and effect on daily life. Compare infant and believers' baptism if the question allows.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A sacrament is an outward sign of God's grace; Catholic and Orthodox churches have seven and most Protestant churches keep two (baptism and Holy Communion).
- Baptism is entry into the Church, practised for infants or for believers; water symbolises cleansing and new life.
- The Eucharist remembers the Last Supper; churches differ on how Christ is present.
- Other sacraments include confirmation, reconciliation, anointing of the sick, marriage and ordination.
- Christian rites of passage mark birth, marriage and death with thanksgiving, vows and hope in the resurrection.
:::
