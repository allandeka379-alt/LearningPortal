## Introduction

Many Zimbabwean families can trace their education, a baptism certificate or a clinic visit to a mission station. Schools such as those run by Catholic, Methodist, Anglican and other churches are still among the best known in the country. At the same time, on a Saturday or Sunday you may see white-robed worshippers praying on a hillside, or hear a Pentecostal choir from a tent. How did Christianity reach this land, and why are there so many different churches?

In this lesson you study the arrival of Christianity in Zimbabwe, the work of the missions, their link with colonisation, and the rise of African Independent and Pentecostal churches. This is a topic where historians and believers hold different views, so you must give both the positive and the critical sides with evidence. The role of the churches in the liberation struggle has its own lesson (**liberation.churches**), and the general differences between denominations are in the lesson on denominations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mission station"}
A settlement set up by a church to spread the Christian message, usually with a church, a school and often a clinic or hospital.
:::

:::definition{term="African Independent (Initiated) Church (AIC)"}
A church founded by Africans, not by a foreign mission, which combines Christian faith with African forms of worship, prophecy and healing. Many in Zimbabwe are called Apostolic churches.
:::

:::definition{term="Colonisation"}
The process by which one country takes control of another, here the British colonisation of the area that became Rhodesia from the 1890s.
:::

:::keyfact
**1561**: Gonçalo da Silveira, a Portuguese Jesuit, at the court of the Mutapa; he was killed the same year. **1859**: the London Missionary Society opens a station at **Inyati**. Missions in the 1890s and later: Jesuits, Methodists, Anglicans, Dutch Reformed, Salvation Army, Seventh-day Adventists and Brethren in Christ.
:::

## Early contact: the Portuguese and the Mutapa

The first known Christian missionary in the interior was **Gonçalo da Silveira**, a Portuguese Jesuit priest. In **1561** he travelled to the court of the Mutapa ruler, was received with honour and, according to the accounts, baptised the ruler and his household. Within months Silveira was killed on the order of the ruler after advisers, some of whom were said to be Muslim traders, convinced him that the priest was a danger. The mission did not last, though it is the first recorded Christian contact with the interior.

## The London Missionary Society at Inyati

In **1859** missionaries of the **London Missionary Society (LMS)** set up a station at **Inyati** in the Ndebele kingdom, after obtaining permission from King **Mzilikazi**. The mission was hard: there were few converts, illness was common, and the missionaries struggled to learn the language and gain trust. They did, however, begin the work of teaching reading, writing the language and translating parts of the Bible. Other LMS stations followed, such as Hope Fountain near Bulawayo.

## Missions after the 1890s

After the arrival of the Pioneer Column in 1890 and the establishment of colonial rule, many more missions came. They included Jesuits (Catholics, with their station at Chishawasha), Methodists, Anglicans, the Dutch Reformed Church, the Salvation Army, Seventh-day Adventists and the Brethren in Christ. Each tended to establish itself in a district, and some of these districts are still associated with a mission church today.

| Mission field | Work |
|---|---|
| Education | Mission schools taught reading, writing, arithmetic and trades, and trained teachers and clergy |
| Health | Clinics and hospitals offered care, especially in rural areas |
| Agriculture and trades | Many missions taught farming methods, carpentry and building |
| Language | Missionaries wrote down Shona and Ndebele, produced grammars and translated the Bible |
| Church life | Churches, Sunday schools and evangelists spread the Christian message |

## Missions and colonisation

Historians disagree about the link between the missions and colonisation. The following points are often made.

| Positive points | Critical points |
|---|---|
| Mission schools and hospitals offered education and care that the state did little to provide for Africans | Missionaries often came with the colonisers and some accepted or supported colonial rule |
| Mission education produced teachers, nurses, clergy and many future leaders, including nationalist leaders | Some missions criticised African customs such as ancestral rituals and bride wealth, and tried to replace them |
| The Bible was translated into African languages, helping to write and standardise them | Land was taken or mission land was held under colonial law, and education was sometimes limited to basic skills |
| Some missionaries spoke against injustice, for example in later years against racial discrimination | Churches were often led by Europeans, with Africans in lower positions |

A balanced answer includes both columns. Many Africans accepted Christianity because of the education and health care that came with it, and also because they found its message meaningful.

:::example{title="A mission school and its effects"}
Tafadzwa's grandfather attended a mission school in Masvingo. He learned to read in Shona, studied the Bible and became a teacher. The school gave him skills that opened a career. At the same time he was expected to leave behind some traditional customs, and he remembers a teacher who criticised ancestral ceremonies. His story shows the positive and critical effects of mission education in one life.
:::

## African Independent (Apostolic) churches

From the early twentieth century, Africans began to found their own churches. The reasons included wanting leadership roles, wanting worship that used African music and dance, and wanting prayer for healing. In Zimbabwe the best known are the Apostolic churches, whose members often wear white robes and worship in open-air sites.

- **Johane Masowe** (Shonhiwa Masedza) began his movement in the 1930s. His followers worship in the open air, often on hilltops; the name comes from the Shona word *masowe*, "wilderness".
- **Johane Marange** founded the African Apostolic Church from the 1930s, in the Marange area near Mutare. It teaches Sabbath-keeping on Saturday and has large gatherings.
- **Zion Christian Church (ZCC)**, which began in South Africa under the Lekganyane family, also has many members in Zimbabwe.

These churches combine the Bible with African forms of worship: singing, drumming, dance, prophecy, healing through prayer and holy water, and respect for community life. Critics note that some practices raise health concerns, for example refusal of medical treatment in some cases, and that leadership structures are not always transparent. Many members, for their part, value the sense of belonging, spiritual care and discipline. Statements about these churches must therefore be careful and respectful.

## Pentecostal churches

The **Apostolic Faith Mission (AFM)**, which came from South Africa in the early twentieth century, was one of the first Pentecostal churches. **ZAOGA**, the Zimbabwe Assemblies of God Africa, was founded by Ezekiel Guti in 1960. Since then many newer Pentecostal and charismatic churches have grown, especially in towns, offering lively worship and prayer for healing and prosperity. The teaching on prosperity is debated and is discussed in the lesson on Christian ethics.

## Christianity in Zimbabwe today

A large majority of Zimbabweans say that they are Christian, according to national surveys and the census conducted by ZIMSTAT; the exact figure depends on the survey and is not given here. Christian churches run many schools and health services, take part in public debates and meet national leaders. The Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013), in section 60, protects freedom of conscience, including the freedom to practise religion, so Christians live alongside followers of Indigenous Religion, Islam, Judaism and other faiths.

:::example{title="Mission and AIC compared"}
Sipho's cousin attends a Methodist school and church in Bulawayo, with a choir and printed hymn books. Sipho's neighbour worships with an apostolic church in a white robe on a hillside near Gwanda, with singing, dance and a prophet's guidance. Both are Christian communities in Zimbabwe, but the first came through a European mission and the second grew from African leadership.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying missions were wholly good or wholly bad"}
Mission education and health care were valued, and the missions were also linked to colonisation and often criticised African customs. Give both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Silveira (1561) with the LMS (1859)"}
Silveira was a Portuguese Catholic priest at the Mutapa court in 1561. The LMS was a British Protestant mission at Inyati in 1859.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating AICs as not really Christian"}
African Independent Churches are Christian churches. Describe their practices as they are, without ranking them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the two ZCCs"}
Zion Christian Church and Zimbabwe Council of Churches are different bodies.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Jesuit Gonçalo da Silveira reached the Mutapa court in 1561, the first recorded Christian contact with the interior.
- The London Missionary Society opened the Inyati station in 1859 with Mzilikazi's permission.
- Many missions arrived after the 1890s, running schools, clinics, hospitals and translation work, and they were linked to colonisation.
- Historians and believers note both the benefits of missions (education, health, Bible translation) and the criticisms (links with colonial rule, criticism of African customs).
- African Independent (Apostolic) churches such as Johane Masowe, Johane Marange and the Zion Christian Church combine Christianity with African forms of worship; Pentecostal churches such as AFM and ZAOGA emphasise the Holy Spirit.
- A large majority of Zimbabweans identify as Christian, and the Constitution protects freedom of religion.
:::
