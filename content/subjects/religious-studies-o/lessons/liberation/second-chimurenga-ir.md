## Introduction

At many family gatherings in rural Zimbabwe an older relative will tell a story from the war of the 1970s: a night meeting under the stars, singing that lasted until the cock crowed, a village that welcomed young men and women with food, and an old woman who was said to speak with the voice of an ancestor. These are memories of the **Second Chimurenga** (Shona) or **Umvukela** (Ndebele), the armed struggle that led to independence on 18 April 1980. This lesson does not cover the military or political history. It looks at the religious side: how Indigenous Religion and spirit mediums supported the war, what fighters and communities believed, and how people judge that role today.

Build on the previous lesson. In 1896-97 the mediums Nehanda and Kaguvi were leaders of the resistance, and in the 1970s their names were remembered as ancestors of the struggle.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ancestral legitimacy"}
The belief that a cause is right and will succeed because the ancestors and the territorial spirits approve of it. A cause with ancestral legitimacy is believed to have the protection and the blessing of the spirits of the land.
:::

:::definition{term="Pungwe"}
An all-night meeting held in a village during the war, at which villagers and fighters sang, danced and shared news and political teaching.
:::

:::definition{term="Chimurenga music"}
Songs of the struggle, often in Shona and using traditional rhythms such as those of the mbira and drums, that carried messages of resistance and honoured the ancestors.
:::

:::keyfact
The war was fought mainly in the 1970s and ended in the Lancaster House agreement of December 1979; independence came on **18 April 1980**. Mediums in the **north-east** were especially important, and an elderly Nehanda medium went with ZANLA into **Mozambique** in the early 1970s.
:::

## How Indigenous Religion supported the war

**1. Mediums gave ancestral approval.** In many rural areas the guerrillas arrived as young strangers. The mediums, who were respected as speakers of the territorial spirits, were able to introduce them to the community and to say that the ancestors supported the cause. This gave the fighters a standing in the eyes of the villagers that they could not have gained by weapons alone.

**2. Fighters observed taboos.** Fighters in many areas were told by the mediums to respect the land, the sacred places and the community, to behave properly towards villagers, and to follow certain rules about food and conduct. Details differ from area to area and from one account to another. The belief was that keeping the taboos protected the fighters and kept the favour of the spirits, while breaking them would bring misfortune.

**3. Mediums advised and blessed.** Mediums were consulted about where to move, when to act and how to avoid danger, and they blessed fighters before operations. In the memory of many communities this guidance strengthened confidence.

**4. The medium who crossed into Mozambique.** The elderly woman remembered as the Nehanda medium went with ZANLA into Mozambique in the early 1970s, where she was received with great respect. For many fighters this was a sign that the spirit of Nehanda, who had resisted in 1896-97, was with them again.

**5. Songs and night meetings.** Chimurenga songs honoured Nehanda, Kaguvi and the ancestors and encouraged unity, and the pungwe brought villagers and fighters together to sing, dance and discuss. Religion, music and politics were woven together.

| Feature | What happened | Religious meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Spirit mediums | Introduced and advised fighters in the north-east and elsewhere | The ancestors approved the struggle |
| Taboos | Fighters followed rules of conduct set out by mediums | Respect for land and spirits brought protection |
| Chimurenga songs | Songs honoured Nehanda, Kaguvi and the ancestors | Links the war to the First Chimurenga |
| Pungwe | All-night gatherings of villagers and fighters | Unity of the living with each other and with the spirits |

:::example{title="A village welcome (a fictional illustration)"}
In a fictional village in the north-east in 1976, the headman and the village medium meet a small group of young fighters. The medium, in a trance, tells the villagers that the ancestors have agreed that the fighters may stay, provided they respect the sacred pool and do not harm the villagers. The headman organises food and sleeping places, and that night the village holds a pungwe with songs and dances. The example shows how the mediums could open the door between fighters and a community that was wary of armed strangers.
:::

## The relationship between fighters and communities

The war depended on the support of rural families, who gave food, shelter and information, and young helpers known as *mujibhas* and *chimbwidos* often carried messages. Religion shaped these relationships, because mediums and elders could explain the cause in terms people trusted. However, village life was also hard. Families were caught between the guerrillas, who asked for food and support, and the Rhodesian forces, who punished villages for helping them. Many civilians suffered loss, fear and displacement. In the Matabeleland areas, where ZIPRA was active, the Mwari shrines and Ndebele and Kalanga beliefs were also part of the memory of the first rising, though the surviving accounts of how far they shaped events are less detailed.

## Different views of the role of Indigenous Religion

| View | Main points |
|---|---|
| Supportive | The mediums gave the struggle legitimacy, linked it to Nehanda and Kaguvi and gave people courage; without their approval many villages would have been closed to the fighters |
| Cautious | Some historians say that fighters were skilled organisers who used religion to win support, and that local leaders acted for local reasons |
| Critical or different | Some people, including some Christians and some communities, did not accept the authority of the mediums; some families were pressed to support the war against their wishes; there were disagreements over which medium was genuine |

A good answer does not decide for one view automatically. It reports what each group believed, uses the evidence, and reaches a supported judgement.

## Lessons for today

The memory of the struggle shapes how Zimbabweans see their ancestors, their land and their identity. Many families still honour fallen relatives with ceremonies, and the heroes of the struggle are remembered in national days. Students should respect both the sacrifices and the suffering of the war, and should write about the role of Indigenous Religion factually, without praise or blame of living politicians.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that religion won the war"}
The war was a complex political and military conflict. Indigenous Religion supported it in important ways, but do not claim that the spirits alone brought independence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the two Chimurengas"}
The First Chimurenga was in 1896-97 and the Second in the 1970s. The mediums Nehanda and Kaguvi of 1896-98 are not the same people as the elderly Nehanda medium of the 1970s; the spirit is said to have returned in another medium.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the mediums as superstition"}
Write "the people believed that ...". Do not call the beliefs primitive or false.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the suffering of villagers"}
A balanced answer notes that communities also suffered, and that not everyone shared the same view of the role of the mediums.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In the 1970s spirit mediums, especially in the north-east, gave ancestral approval to the struggle and advised and blessed fighters.
- An elderly Nehanda medium went with ZANLA into Mozambique in the early 1970s.
- Fighters observed taboos set by the mediums, and communities shared chimurenga songs and pungwe night gatherings.
- The links with 1896-97 gave the war ancestral legitimacy in the eyes of many people.
- People hold different views of the role of the mediums, so a balanced and respectful answer is expected.
