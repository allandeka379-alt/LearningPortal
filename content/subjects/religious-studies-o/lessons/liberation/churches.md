## Introduction

Many Zimbabweans were baptised, confirmed or educated in a mission school, yet many also remember that during the years of settler rule the churches were sometimes on the side of the government and sometimes on the side of the people. A grandfather in Bulawayo may say, "The mission taught me to read and to pray, and the mission taught me that my vote did not count." Another elder will remember the priest who hid people from soldiers and the church that wrote a report about what was being done to villagers. This lesson looks at that mixed record fairly. It tells you what is well established and asks you to weigh the evidence, not to praise or condemn a whole church.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mission station"}
A settlement set up by a church or mission society, usually with a church, a school and often a clinic or hospital, from which the Christian message and education were spread.
:::

:::definition{term="Prophetic witness"}
The Christian idea that the Church has a duty to speak out for justice, as the prophets of the Hebrew Bible spoke to rulers, even when this is dangerous.
:::

:::definition{term="Programme to Combat Racism"}
A programme begun by the World Council of Churches in 1970 that gave grants to organisations fighting racism, including some liberation movements in southern Africa. The grants were meant for humanitarian work such as education and health, and they caused controversy.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn these names: **Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace (CCJP)**, founded in 1972, which documented abuses; **Bishop Donal Lamont**, Catholic Bishop of Umtali, convicted in 1976 and later deported; **Bishop Abel Muzorewa** (United Methodist) and **Rev. Ndabaningi Sithole** and **Rev. Canaan Banana**, clergy who entered politics.
:::

## The mixed record of the churches

**Links with colonial rule.** Missionaries came to Zimbabwe from the 1850s, and some travelled with or received land from the settler administration after 1890. Mission farms and stations occupied land that African families had used, and some missionaries accepted the settlers' view that Africans were not ready for political power. The churches were also criticised for undervaluing African customs and for teaching that Indigenous Religion should be given up.

**Education that produced nationalists.** Mission schools and colleges, such as those at Kutama, Waddilove, Dadaya and Old Mutare, educated many of the people who became leaders in business, the professions and politics. Ideas of equality and justice taught in church and school were used by educated Africans in their case against discrimination. In this way the mission gave people the tools to oppose the system that the churches had partly supported.

**Church leaders who opposed settler rule.** From the 1960s a number of church leaders spoke out against unjust laws and, later, against abuses in the war.

- The **Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace (CCJP)**, formed in 1972, collected evidence of abuses by all sides. In 1976 it joined with a Catholic institute in London to publish a report on violations of human rights during the war.
- **Bishop Donal Lamont**, Catholic Bishop of Umtali, criticised racial laws and the treatment of villagers. In 1976 he was convicted for failing to report guerrillas and was deported in 1977. Many Christians and others saw him as a witness for justice.
- Other church leaders in several denominations also protested about racial laws, land and the treatment of civilians.

**Church members and leaders who supported the government.** Not all Christians opposed settler rule. Some church members and leaders believed that law and order should be kept, feared the war, or supported the government's argument that it was protecting Christian civilisation. This is part of the record that a balanced student includes.

**Clergy in politics.**

| Person | Church and role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bishop Abel Muzorewa | United Methodist Church bishop; led the African National Council from 1971 | Became prime minister of Zimbabwe Rhodesia in 1979 under an internal agreement that did not end the war |
| Rev. Ndabaningi Sithole | Congregational minister; first leader of ZANU in 1963 | Later also took part in the internal agreement |
| Rev. Canaan Banana | Methodist minister; first President of Zimbabwe from 1980 | The presidency was ceremonial at that time |

These examples show that some church ministers moved from the pulpit to politics, and that people in the churches took different political paths.

:::example{title="A mission station caught between two sides"}
Imagine a fictional mission station near Masvingo in 1977 (an illustration, not an event from the sources). The headteacher is a missionary, and most pupils come from villages in the area. Guerrillas visit the school at night and ask for food and medicine, and the next morning soldiers question the staff about who has visited. If the staff say nothing they risk being punished by the soldiers, and if they help the soldiers they risk their pupils' safety. Many mission stations in the war were in this position. Some were closed, and some staff and students suffered. The example shows why historians say the churches were "caught in the middle" as well as taking sides.
:::

## International church support

The World Council of Churches (WCC) began its Programme to Combat Racism in 1970. In 1978 it made grants to organisations that included the liberation movements of southern Africa, for humanitarian work such as education and health. The grants caused controversy: supporters said that it was right to help the victims of racism, and critics said that the money might be used for the war. Some member churches objected strongly. The example shows that churches in other countries were also divided.

## Muslims and the struggle

Muslim communities in Zimbabwe were small. Documentation of their role in the struggle is limited, so this lesson does not describe it in detail. Students should say only what is well established: Muslims, like other Zimbabweans, lived through the war and held a range of views.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that the churches all supported the struggle, or all supported settler rule"}
The record is mixed. Name examples on both sides and say that individuals in the same church could take different positions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the clergy in politics"}
Muzorewa (United Methodist bishop) led the African National Council; Sithole (Congregational minister) led ZANU in 1963; Banana (Methodist minister) became the first President in 1980. Learn the three names with their churches.
:::

:::mistake{title="Praising or blaming living politicians"}
Write about the actions and the evidence, not about whether a person was good or bad.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the education point"}
Many nationalists were educated in mission schools. Mention this when you explain how the churches helped the struggle indirectly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe the roles of the churches"}
Give about seven developed points from both sides: mission education, the CCJP and Bishop Lamont, clergy in politics, the WCC grants, the churches that supported the government, and the position of mission stations in the war.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess the view that the churches helped the struggle more than they hindered it"}
Weigh the helping evidence (education, witness, relief) against the hindering evidence (links with colonial rule, support for the government, criticism of African customs). Conclude with a judgement that recognises the mixed record.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The churches had a mixed role: links with colonial rule, but also education of nationalists and prophetic witness.
- The CCJP (1972) documented abuses, and Bishop Donal Lamont was convicted in 1976 and deported in 1977.
- Muzorewa, Sithole and Banana were clergy who entered politics, taking different paths.
- Mission stations were often caught between guerrillas and soldiers; some Christians supported the government.
- The WCC's Programme to Combat Racism gave controversial humanitarian grants; the role of Muslims is documented only in outline.
:::
