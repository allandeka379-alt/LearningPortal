## Introduction

Walk through Harare and you will find a street, a school or a hospital named after Nehanda. Ask a Zimbabwean grandmother in Mutare or Gwanda who Nehanda was and she may begin, "She was a spirit medium who refused to give up." The risings of 1896-97 are remembered in Zimbabwe as the **First Chimurenga** (Shona) or **Umvukela** (Ndebele), the first large resistance to the settler company that took control of the country after 1890. This lesson does not retell the whole history of the war, which belongs to History. It asks a Family and Religious Studies question: *what part did Indigenous Religion play?*

You should already know from Topic 2 that Mwari is the High God, that ancestral spirits (midzimu/amadlozi) guard families, that territorial spirits (mhondoro) guard the land, and that a spirit medium (svikiro) is a person through whom a spirit is believed to speak.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chimurenga / Umvukela"}
*Chimurenga* is a Shona word for a struggle of resistance; *Umvukela* is the Ndebele word for a rising. The First Chimurenga/Umvukela refers to the risings of 1896-97 against the rule of the British South Africa Company.
:::

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro)"}
A person believed to be chosen by a spirit, such as a mhondoro, so that the spirit can speak through them to guide the community on matters such as rain, war and justice.
:::

:::definition{term="Mwari shrine"}
A sacred centre of the Mwari cult, especially in the Matopo Hills (Njelele and Matonjeni), where messages from Mwari were believed to be given and where people from many areas sent requests about rain, harvests and national crises.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn the names and dates: the Ndebele rising began in March 1896 and the Shona rising in June 1896. **Mkwati** was a Mwari priest linked with the Matopo shrines. **Nehanda** (Charwe Nyakasikana) and **Kaguvi** (Gumboreshumba) were mediums in Mashonaland, executed in **April 1898**.
:::

## Religious beliefs behind the resistance

In Indigenous Religion, land is not only property. It belongs to the ancestors and to the territorial spirits, and the living are its guardians. When settlers took land and cattle, many people experienced this as an attack on the whole spiritual order: graves, shrines and rain-making places lay in the land that was being lost. Several events increased the distress.

| Grievance or event | Why it mattered religiously |
|---|---|
| Loss of land and cattle after 1890 | Land was linked to the ancestors and the mhondoro; cattle were used in roora/lobola and in ritual |
| Forced labour and taxation | Families were pulled away from their fields and homes and could not easily carry out their ceremonies |
| Drought, locusts and the cattle disease rinderpest (1896) | Rainfall and the health of herds were believed to depend on good relations with Mwari and the ancestors |
| Interference with shrines, mediums and chiefs | The authority of spirit mediums and chiefs was challenged |

Many communities concluded that the troubles were a sign of disorder in the spiritual world, and that Mwari and the ancestors expected the people to defend the land. This is how adherents understood events; historians also stress political, economic and military causes.

## The people and shrines

**The Mwari shrines.** Messengers from the Matopo shrines were consulted over rain and national crises. In 1896 people turned to them in the hope of guidance. Mkwati, who is associated with the shrines, is remembered as an influential religious figure who travelled into Mashonaland during the rising. The sources do not agree on the details of his movements, so students should state only what is well known: he was linked to the Mwari cult and he is remembered as a leader of the resistance.

**Nehanda.** Charwe Nyakasikana was the medium of the spirit Nehanda, a respected spirit of the Mazowe area of northern Mashonaland. She is remembered as a leading figure in the Shona rising.

**Kaguvi.** Gumboreshumba was the medium of the spirit Kaguvi, also in Mashonaland. Both mediums were captured in late 1897 and executed in April 1898.

:::example{title="How a spirit medium could influence an uprising"}
Imagine a village in Mashonaland in 1896 (a fictional account written to show the pattern, not an event from the sources). Drought has damaged the fields, cattle are dying and the village has lost land. The elders consult the medium, who speaks in the voice of the territorial spirit. The message is that the land has been dishonoured and that the people must stand together. Because the people trust the spirit, they accept the message as binding, and neighbouring villages hear of it through kin and messengers. The example shows why spirit mediums were respected: they linked religion, law and leadership, and a call from them carried the authority of the ancestors.
:::

## Capture, execution and memory

By the end of 1897 the settler forces had captured the main leaders of the Shona rising. Nehanda and Kaguvi were tried and executed in April 1898. Tradition says that Nehanda refused the missionaries' appeal to be baptised and foretold that her bones would rise, a saying that later generations took as a promise that resistance would return. It is remembered as tradition, and historians treat it with care. In the 1960s and 1970s nationalists looked back on the mediums as the first heroes of the struggle, and today Nehanda and Kaguvi are honoured at national level.

## Historians' debate

Historians do not agree on how far the risings were organised by religious leaders.

- **One view:** the risings were widely coordinated by the Mwari cult and the spirit mediums, who gave people a common religious message across Matabeleland and Mashonaland. The historian Terence Ranger argued this strongly in the 1960s.
- **Another view:** local grievances, chiefs and local leaders were the main driving force, and communication between the two risings was limited. Later historians stressed that each area acted for its own reasons.
- **A balanced judgement:** religion gave language, legitimacy and courage to the resistance in many places, but it was one cause among several, and we should not assume that every community acted on the same message.

:::example{title="Two views of one event"}
A colonial official in 1896 might have described the mediums as "agitators", while a Shona elder describing the same events in later years would say that the mediums were fulfilling a duty given by the ancestors. The two accounts describe the same facts and place them in different frameworks. A student answering an examination question should name the framework: "According to the beliefs of the Shona, ..." and "According to colonial records, ...".
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing a history essay instead of a religious one"}
The question asks about the part played by religion. Spend your time on Mwari, the mediums, the shrines and the beliefs about land and ancestors, not on every battle.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the people"}
Nehanda and Kaguvi were mediums in Mashonaland; Mkwati was linked to the Mwari shrines in the Matopo Hills. Do not call Mkwati a chief or a medium of Nehanda.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming that religion was the only cause"}
Say that religion was one cause alongside land, cattle, labour, tax, drought and rinderpest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the belief as a fact or as superstition"}
Write "the people believed that Mwari and the ancestors supported them". Do not say the spirits did or did not help, and do not describe the beliefs as primitive.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain the role of Indigenous Religion"}
Give about seven developed points: the shrines and Mkwati, the mediums Nehanda and Kaguvi, the link between land and ancestors, the grievances, the message the mediums gave, the capture and execution in April 1898, and the memory of the heroes.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess or how far"}
For "how far was religion the main driving force?" use both sides: religious leadership and belief (for), local and economic grievances (against), then a judgement such as "religion supplied the unity and courage, while land, cattle and tax were the underlying causes".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The First Chimurenga/Umvukela of 1896-97 was a rising against the rule of the British South Africa Company.
- Indigenous Religion gave the rising language and authority: the Mwari shrines (with Mkwati) and the mediums Nehanda and Kaguvi.
- Land, cattle, labour, tax, drought and rinderpest were grievances that people also understood in spiritual terms.
- Nehanda and Kaguvi were captured and executed in April 1898; they are remembered as heroes of the resistance.
- Historians debate how far religious leaders coordinated the risings, so a good answer is balanced.
:::
