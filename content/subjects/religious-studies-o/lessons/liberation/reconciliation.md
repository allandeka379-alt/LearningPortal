## Introduction

In many families there is an empty grave or a missing relative from the years of the war. Perhaps an uncle did not come home from the camps in Zambia or Mozambique, or a family cannot say where a loved one was buried. For such families, independence on 18 April 1980 brought joy, but also grief, and a question that every society after war must answer: *how do people who were enemies, or who suffered terribly, live together again?* That question is about **reconciliation** and **healing**. Religions have much to say about it, because forgiveness, cleansing, remembrance and burial of the dead are religious matters in every faith. This lesson looks at how religion contributed to reconciliation and healing after the struggle, and where the work is still unfinished.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reconciliation"}
Restoring relationships between people or groups that have been in conflict, so that they can live and work together again. It usually involves acknowledging what has happened, forgiveness and a commitment to a peaceful future.
:::

:::definition{term="Healing"}
Recovery from the emotional, spiritual, social and physical wounds of conflict, for individuals, families and communities.
:::

:::definition{term="Cleansing ritual"}
A ceremony in Indigenous Religion, performed by elders and religious specialists, to remove the spiritual impurity or disorder believed to follow a violent death or war, so that the living and the dead can be at peace.
:::

:::keyfact
Dates and sources to learn: policy of reconciliation announced at independence (**1980**); **Unity Accord, 22 December 1987**; **Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013, section 251**, which created the **National Peace and Reconciliation Commission** (NPRC).
:::

## The policy of reconciliation in 1980

At independence the new government announced a policy of reconciliation: people of all races and political groups were invited to work together for the future of the country. Churches and other religious bodies welcomed this call. For believers, forgiveness lies at the heart of their faith: Christians are taught to forgive (Matthew 18:21-22), Muslims are taught that forgiveness is a virtue and that making peace between people is rewarded (Qur'an 42:40, 49:10), Jews practise repentance and making amends especially at Yom Kippur, and Indigenous Religion teaches that harmony between the living and the dead and between relatives must be restored.

## How religious organisations contributed

| Contribution | Examples in outline |
|---|---|
| Preaching and teaching forgiveness | Churches and mosques taught that enemies could become neighbours again |
| Relief and resettlement | Church relief bodies, such as the Zimbabwe Council of Churches' Christian Care, helped refugees and returning families; mission hospitals and schools treated and taught those affected by the war |
| Support for ex-combatants | Churches ran counselling, training and reintegration for some former fighters and gave them a welcome in congregations |
| Prayer and memorials | Special services remembered the war dead and prayed for the nation |
| Documentation and truth-telling | The Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace published reports on abuses; in 1997 it published a report on the disturbances in Matabeleland and the Midlands in the 1980s |

## Indigenous Religion: cleansing and reburial

In Indigenous Religion a person who dies violently, or whose body is not properly buried, is believed to be restless and may become an avenging spirit (ngozi). Many families therefore held ceremonies after the war to bring home the spirits of those who died and to bury or rebury their remains with respect. They used rites of cleansing for fighters who had killed or who had seen death, and ceremonies such as *kurova guva* (Shona) and *umbuyiso* (Ndebele), which bring the spirit home so that it can join the family ancestors. These rituals helped families to grieve and to restore the link between the living and the dead.

:::example{title="A family brings home the spirit of a fighter"}
Rudo's uncle left to join the struggle in 1975 and never returned. After independence the family learned the place where he died and obtained his remains. Elders gathered at the family home, brewed beer, spoke to the ancestors and performed the ceremony of bringing him home. A Christian relative led a prayer at the grave, and the church choir sang. The family was able to grieve and to accept his place among the ancestors. The example shows how traditional and Christian rites can both help families to heal.
:::

## Unity Accord and the NPRC

In the 1980s there were serious disturbances in Matabeleland and parts of the Midlands in which many civilians died. The **Unity Accord** of 22 December 1987 ended the political conflict between the two main parties, which merged. Religious leaders had called for peace and dialogue. Many families nonetheless felt that questions had not been fully answered. The **Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013), section 251**, created the **National Peace and Reconciliation Commission**, whose task is to promote national healing, unity and conflict resolution. The work of the NPRC is continuing, and it draws on traditional leaders, churches and civil society. This lesson gives only an outline and takes no side in current political debates.

## Churches and national dialogue

Churches have also encouraged national dialogue at later times. For example, in 2006 three church groups (the Zimbabwe Catholic Bishops' Conference, the Evangelical Fellowship of Zimbabwe and the Zimbabwe Council of Churches) published a discussion document, *The Zimbabwe We Want*, inviting national debate. Some people welcomed such initiatives, while others thought churches should avoid politics. Students should present both views and the principle that religious bodies may call for peace and justice.

:::example{title="Two perspectives on healing"}
Sipho is a Christian who believes healing comes through confession, forgiveness and prayer. Thandeka is a member of a family that follows Indigenous Religion and believes healing also needs a ceremony to bring home the spirits of the dead and to cleanse those who have been touched by violence. Their teacher asks them to plan a community healing day. They agree to hold a joint service at which the churches pray and elders conduct the ceremonies, and they invite people of all backgrounds. The example shows that different religious traditions can contribute side by side.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the dates and bodies"}
The Unity Accord was signed on 22 December 1987; section 251 of the 2013 Constitution created the NPRC. Do not write that the NPRC was created in 1980.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about only one religion"}
Include both Christian and Indigenous Religion contributions, and mention other faiths where you can.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking political sides"}
Describe what religious bodies did and what the law provides. Do not praise or blame living politicians or parties.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying reconciliation was complete"}
Many families are still seeking answers. A balanced answer says what was achieved and what remains to be done.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): explain the role of religion in reconciliation"}
Give about seven developed points: teaching forgiveness, relief and resettlement, support for ex-combatants, prayer and memorials, cleansing and reburial rites, documentation of abuses, and calls for dialogue.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For "how far has religion helped national healing?" give successes (families supported, rites performed, forgiveness taught), limits (unresolved questions, divisions in the churches, political sensitivities), and a reasoned conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- At independence in 1980 a policy of reconciliation invited all to work together; religious bodies supported it.
- Religious teaching on forgiveness and peace (Matthew 18:21-22; Qur'an 49:10; Yom Kippur; harmony with the ancestors) underpins reconciliation.
- Churches helped with relief, resettlement, counselling, prayer and documentation of abuses.
- Indigenous Religion offers cleansing and rites to bring home and rebury the war dead (kurova guva/umbuyiso).
- The Unity Accord (22 December 1987) and the NPRC (section 251, Constitution 2013) continue the work of healing.
:::
