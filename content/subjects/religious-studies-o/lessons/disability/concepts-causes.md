## Introduction

In a classroom in Masvingo, Tatenda uses a wheelchair, and Chipo sits at the front because she has low vision and needs large print. Their teacher, Mr Sibanda, makes sure that the door has a ramp and that notes are printed in a bigger font. In another school, a pupil who is deaf has no interpreter, so she copies from the board without understanding the lesson. In the first school the pupils take part fully; in the second, a barrier keeps a pupil out.

This lesson begins the topic of Religion and Disability. You learn what disability means, how it is understood, the main types and the real causes, and how to recognise and correct the false beliefs that cause harm. Later lessons look at how religions teach about disability and at rights and inclusion. Throughout, use respectful language: speak of "a person with a disability", not of a person as their condition.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Impairment"}
A loss or difference in the way a part of the body or mind works, for example loss of sight or hearing, or a difficulty in moving a limb.
:::

:::definition{term="Disability"}
The disadvantage a person experiences when an impairment meets barriers in society, such as steps, lack of sign language or negative attitudes, that stop the person from taking part on an equal basis with others.
:::

:::definition{term="Medical model"}
The view that disability is a problem in the individual's body that medicine should treat or cure.
:::

:::definition{term="Social model"}
The view that much of what disables a person is created by barriers in society, which can be removed.
:::

:::keyfact
**Person-first language:** "a person with a disability", "a child who is deaf". **Types:** physical, visual, hearing, intellectual, mental health conditions, albinism and multiple disabilities. **Causes:** genetic, disease, accidents, malnutrition and conflict. Beliefs that disability comes from witchcraft, curses or punishment are myths.
:::

## Impairment and disability

A person may have an impairment, such as being unable to walk. Whether this becomes a disadvantage depends on the world around them. A student who cannot walk can take full part in school if the buildings have ramps, but is shut out if there are only steps. This is why modern definitions, including those of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), speak of disability as the result of the interaction between an impairment and barriers.

| | Medical model | Social model |
|---|---|---|
| Where is the problem? | In the person's body | In barriers in society |
| Main response | Treatment and cure | Removing barriers and changing attitudes |
| Example | Surgery for a limb; hearing aids | Ramps, sign language, large print, welcoming attitudes |
| Strength | Gives treatment and medical care | Gives equality and inclusion |
| Limitation | May ignore the person's wishes and rights | Does not remove the need for medical care |

Most experts today say that both matter: medical care when it is wanted and needed, and social change so that all can take part.

## Types of disability

| Type | Description | Examples of barriers |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Difficulty with movement, strength or use of limbs | Steps, narrow doorways, rough paths |
| Visual | Blindness or low vision | Printed notes, boards without large print or Braille |
| Hearing | Deafness or being hard of hearing | No sign language, lessons only spoken |
| Intellectual | Differences in learning and understanding | Lessons that move too fast, unkind teasing |
| Mental health conditions | Conditions such as depression or anxiety that affect thinking and mood | Stigma, fear of being judged |
| Albinism | An inherited condition in which the body makes little of the pigment melanin; people often have low vision and sensitive skin | Bright light, sun, and discrimination |
| Multiple | More than one, such as deafblindness | Lack of specialised support |

Albinism is a genetic condition and not an illness. It is often discussed together with disability because people with albinism may have low vision and face discrimination.

## Causes of disability

Disability has real, ordinary causes:

- **Genetic causes.** Some conditions are inherited, such as albinism and some forms of deafness.
- **Disease.** Illnesses such as polio, measles, meningitis and severe malaria can damage the body; immunisation protects children against several of them.
- **Accidents.** Road accidents, falls, burns and injuries at work can cause lasting impairments.
- **Complications at birth and before birth.** Problems during pregnancy or delivery can affect a baby; regular antenatal care and delivery with a trained health worker reduce the risk.
- **Malnutrition.** Lack of proper food in pregnancy or childhood can affect growth and development.
- **Conflict.** War causes injuries, and landmines left from conflict still harm people in some areas of Zimbabwe's borders.
- **Age.** Many people develop impairments in older age.

Many causes can be prevented or reduced by immunisation, good nutrition, safe roads, antenatal care and clearing landmines. If you are concerned about a child's health or development, speak to a clinic or trained health worker.

## Myths and misconceptions

In many societies, including Zimbabwe, some people have explained disability by false beliefs. They need to be explained and corrected with respect for the families who hold them.

| Myth | The facts |
|---|---|
| "Disability is caused by witchcraft or a curse." | Disabilities have medical and social causes. Blaming witchcraft leads to accusations that harm innocent people. |
| "It is a punishment for the sins of the parents or the child." | There is no evidence for this; major religions teach that disability is not a punishment (see the next lesson). |
| "It is contagious." | Disability cannot be caught from another person. |
| "A child with a disability cannot learn or contribute." | With support, children with disabilities can learn and work, and many do. |
| "People with albinism have special powers, or their body parts bring luck." | This is false and dangerous. Attacks on people with albinism are crimes. |

Such beliefs have led some families to hide children, neglect them or even abuse them. Hiding a child, denying a child schooling or harming a person because of such beliefs is wrong and, in many cases, against the law.

## Respectful language

Words matter. In many countries it is preferred to use **person-first language**: "a person with a disability" or "a learner who is blind", rather than "the disabled" or insulting words. Some people prefer to say "a disabled person" or "a Deaf person", because they see disability as part of their identity. The best practice is to use the words people choose for themselves, and never to use words that insult, such as those that suggest a person is worthless or incomplete.

:::example{title="Barrier or impairment?"}
Nokuthula uses a wheelchair and cannot enter the school library because it is up a flight of steps. A classmate says that her legs are the problem. Another classmate says that the steps are the problem. A strong answer explains both views: the impairment is in the body, but the disability is made by the steps. A ramp would remove the barrier and let her take part. This shows the social model in practice.
:::

:::example{title="Correcting a myth"}
Mthulisi's grandmother is worried because a neighbour's baby was born with a disability and some villagers say the family has been cursed. Mthulisi has learned that disabilities have medical causes, and he suggests the family take the baby to the clinic and that neighbours offer support. This correct, kind response shows how to challenge a myth without insulting people who believe it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing impairment and disability"}
An impairment is a difference in the body or mind; a disability is the disadvantage that arises when barriers exist. Define both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using insulting language"}
Avoid insulting words and avoid describing a person only by their condition. Use "a person with a disability".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving witchcraft or punishment as a cause"}
These are myths. State the real causes: genetic, disease, accidents, malnutrition, birth complications and conflict.
:::

For *explain the causes of disability*, give separate causes (genetic, disease, accidents, malnutrition, conflict) and add a detail to each, such as polio or landmines.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An impairment is a difference in the body or mind; disability arises when barriers in society keep people from taking part.
- The medical model sees the problem in the body; the social model sees it in barriers; both are useful.
- Types include physical, visual, hearing, intellectual, mental health conditions, albinism and multiple disabilities.
- Causes include genetic conditions, disease, accidents, malnutrition, birth complications and conflict.
- Beliefs in witchcraft, curses or punishment as causes are myths that cause harm.
- Use respectful, person-first language.
:::
