## Introduction

Tafadzwa is a Form 3 pupil who is blind. In his school in Gweru, his teachers give him notes in Braille, a classmate reads aloud the diagrams on the board, and the school choir includes him. On Sundays, his church has set aside seating near the door and prints hymn books in large print for people with low vision. Tafadzwa's experience is good because the people around him have removed barriers. Another pupil, in a school without such support, is left out.

In this lesson you learn the main rights of persons with disabilities, what inclusion means in schools and places of worship, what religious organisations have done and can do, and what each person can do. You should already know the meaning of disability and the religious teachings about it from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inclusion"}
Making sure that people with disabilities can take part fully in school, worship and community life, on an equal basis with others.
:::

:::definition{term="Reasonable accommodation"}
A sensible adjustment, such as a ramp, a sign-language interpreter or large print, that removes a barrier so that a person can take part.
:::

:::definition{term="Accessibility"}
The design of buildings, services and information so that everyone can use them.
:::

:::keyfact
**Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013):** section 22 (national objective: persons with disabilities), section 83 (rights of persons with disabilities), section 56 (equality and non-discrimination), section 75 (education). **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities:** adopted 2006. Sign language is among the officially recognised languages of Zimbabwe.
:::

## Rights of persons with disabilities

The Constitution of Zimbabwe protects the rights of persons with disabilities in several places. Section 56 guarantees equality and forbids unfair discrimination, including on grounds of disability. **Section 22** sets a national objective: the State must recognise the rights of persons with disabilities, promote their dignity, and help them to participate in the life of the nation. **Section 83** says that the State must take appropriate measures, within the resources available, to help persons with physical or mental disabilities to realise their full potential, to promote their self-reliance, to protect them from exploitation and abuse, and to provide access to education and medical and other services. Section 75 gives every citizen a right to basic education.

The country also has a **Disabled Persons Act** (1992), which deals with the welfare and rights of persons with disabilities; a reviewer should check the details of the Act and any recent changes. Internationally, the **UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities** (2006) says that persons with disabilities have the same human rights as everyone else and that societies must remove barriers. Its main principles include respect for dignity, non-discrimination, full participation, accessibility and equal opportunity.

| Source | Main message |
|---|---|
| Constitution, section 22 | The State must promote dignity and participation of persons with disabilities |
| Constitution, section 83 | The State must take measures for self-reliance, protection from abuse and access to education and care |
| Constitution, section 56 | Everyone is equal; unfair discrimination is forbidden |
| UN Convention (2006) | Equal rights; accessibility; removing barriers |

Rights on paper are not always rights in practice. Many children with disabilities still lack schools nearby, assistive devices or transport, and families may lack the information or means to claim support. Laws are effective only when communities, schools and religious groups help to put them into practice.

## Inclusive education

**Inclusive education** means that learners with disabilities learn in ordinary schools alongside others, with the support they need, rather than being left at home or always separated. Support may include ramps and accessible toilets, Braille and large-print materials, sign-language teaching, extra time in examinations, trained teachers and kind classmates. Some learners also attend **special schools**, which have trained staff and equipment, for example for learners who are blind or deaf. Both approaches have a place, and the aim is for every child to learn.

## Inclusive worship

Places of worship are part of the community, so they too can include or exclude. Inclusive worship can mean:

- ramps, wide doors and accessible toilets at the church, mosque, synagogue or meeting place;
- sign-language interpretation or written texts for people who are deaf;
- hymn books, prayers or Qur'an or Torah texts in large print or Braille;
- places for wheelchairs, and seats near the front for those with low vision;
- a welcoming attitude, and giving people with disabilities real roles, such as reading, singing or teaching, where the religion permits;
- not treating disability as a punishment, and not pressing people to be "healed" in public.

Religions differ on who may lead certain parts of worship, but all can welcome and support.

## The role of religious organisations

Religious bodies have played an important part in Zimbabwe's provision for people with disabilities. Many special schools and centres for the blind, the deaf and children with physical disabilities began as mission or church projects, and some clinics and rehabilitation centres are run by churches. Muslim, Jewish and other organisations also give practical help through charity. Religious organisations help in several ways: building and running schools and centres, collecting funds, training carers, running awareness campaigns, and speaking out for rights. A reviewer may add named institutions whose founding body is certain; none is named here.

Religious organisations also have challenges: some buildings are not accessible, some leaders still repeat myths, and charity can become pity instead of respect for rights. A good approach treats people with disabilities as equals and partners, not only as people to be helped.

## What individuals can do

Every person can help by:

- using respectful language and speaking to the person directly rather than about them;
- including classmates with disabilities in games, group work and clubs;
- asking before offering help, and listening to the answer;
- challenging myths, for example by explaining the real causes of disability;
- reporting abuse or neglect to a trusted adult, the police Victim Friendly Unit or Childline Zimbabwe on 116;
- supporting the removal of barriers in school and in the place of worship.

:::example{title="Making a church accessible"}
Nyasha's church committee in Chinhoyi wants to include a member who uses a wheelchair. They decide to build a ramp, set aside a space near the front, print the order of service in large print, and invite the member to join the music group. An examination answer on inclusion should give such actions and link each to a right (access, participation, non-discrimination) or to a religious teaching (every person has dignity).
:::

:::example{title="A school case"}
Rudo's class includes Thandeka, who is deaf. The school has no interpreter. Rudo suggests that the class learn basic sign language, that notes be written on the board, and that the head teacher apply to the Ministry for support. This links the right to education (section 75) to a practical solution.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating inclusion as charity"}
Inclusion is about rights and equal participation. Charity may help, but people with disabilities have rights, not only needs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting the wrong section"}
Section 22 is a national objective; section 83 sets out the rights and the State's duties; section 56 is equality; section 75 is education.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that special schools are always better or always worse"}
Both inclusive education and special schools have a place. Give reasons for each and avoid sweeping claims.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *describe ways in which a place of worship can include people with disabilities*, give separate ways (access, communication, roles, attitudes) and explain how each removes a barrier.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *how far have religious organisations helped people with disabilities?*, give achievements (schools, care, advocacy) and limits (inaccessible buildings, myths), then judge.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Constitution protects persons with disabilities: section 22 (national objective), section 83 (rights), section 56 (equality) and section 75 (education).
- The Disabled Persons Act and the UN Convention (2006) support equal rights and the removal of barriers.
- Inclusive education and inclusive worship use reasonable accommodations such as ramps, sign language, Braille and large print.
- Religious organisations have started many schools and centres, and can also challenge myths and make buildings accessible.
- Individuals can use respectful language, include others, challenge myths and report abuse.
:::
