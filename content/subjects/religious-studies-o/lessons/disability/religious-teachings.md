## Introduction

In a village near Lupane, a family prepares for a gathering for a child's naming. A neighbour's son, who uses crutches, is welcomed by the elders and given a seat at the front. In another home across the country, a family keeps a child with a disability out of sight because they fear what neighbours will say. Both families might describe themselves as religious. This shows that religious teaching and the way people apply it do not always match.

In this lesson you learn what Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach about people with disabilities, and how attitudes can both support and stigmatise. You should already know the real causes of disability from the previous lesson. As always, the religions are described as their followers understand them, and no religion is ranked above another.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stigma"}
A mark of shame or disgrace attached to a person by others, leading to unfair treatment.
:::

:::definition{term="Dignity"}
The worth of every person, which does not depend on ability, wealth or status.
:::

:::definition{term="Kinship care"}
Care for family members by the extended family, a strong value in Indigenous Religion and Zimbabwean society.
:::

:::keyfact
**Judaism:** Leviticus 19:14. **Christianity:** John 9:1-3; healing accounts such as Mark 10:46-52; 1 Corinthians 12:12-26 (the body and its parts). **Islam:** Surah 80 ('Abasa, 80:1-10). **Indigenous Religion:** care within the extended family; unhu/ubuntu.
:::

## Indigenous Religion

In Indigenous Religion in Zimbabwe, the family and the community, including the living and the ancestors, are central. The extended family is expected to care for all of its members, and this duty includes children and adults with disabilities. The values of **unhu** (Shona) and **ubuntu** (Ndebele) say that a person is a person through other people, so a family member in need must be supported.

Beliefs about the cause of disability in some families have been linked with the spirit world. Some people have believed that an impairment may show the displeasure of an ancestor, a neglected ritual or the work of a witch, and they have consulted a n'anga or inyanga to learn the reason or to seek healing. These beliefs have sometimes led to blame, secrecy or neglect. In the past some families hid children with disabilities, or treated them as a source of shame, and in cases of albinism there have been accusations and violence that are crimes today.

At the same time, many families and elders show great care. Many elders teach that a child with a disability is a gift from the ancestors who must be cared for, and children with disabilities have a place in family ceremonies. Modern leaders in Indigenous Religion, together with health workers, teach that disability has medical causes and that families should seek help at the clinic. A fair account says both things: there is a strong tradition of care and, in some cases, harmful beliefs.

## Judaism

The Torah gives a clear command in **Leviticus 19:14**: do not insult a person who is deaf, and do not put an obstacle in the way of a person who is blind, but fear God. Jewish teachers read this widely, as forbidding anyone to take advantage of a person's weakness, or to give misleading advice. Deuteronomy 27:18 also curses anyone who leads a blind person astray on the road.

Judaism teaches that every person is made in the image of God, so every person has worth. Jewish communities have a long tradition of charity (*tzedakah*) and of visiting the sick and caring for those in need. Jewish law also makes some adjustments: for example, a person who cannot do a commandment because of an impairment may be exempt from it. Today many synagogues have ramps, hearing loops and large-print prayer books, and Jewish organisations support people with disabilities. Views about participation in leading worship differ between Orthodox, Conservative and Reform communities.

## Christianity

In the Gospels, Jesus is reported to have healed blind, deaf and paralysed people, such as blind Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46-52), a man who could not hear (Mark 7:31-37) and a man who was paralysed and lowered through a roof (Luke 5:17-26). Christians understand these as signs of God's compassion and of the Kingdom of God. They also see them as examples to follow: Christians are called to care for people in need.

In **John 9:1-3** the disciples see a man blind from birth and ask whether he or his parents sinned. Jesus answers that neither did, and that the man's life shows God's work. Many Christians read this as rejecting the idea that disability is a punishment for sin. In 1 Corinthians 12:12-26, Paul compares the Church to a body, in which every part is needed and the weaker parts deserve special honour. Churches today run schools, hospitals and homes for people with disabilities, and many provide ramps, sign-language interpreters and Braille or large-print books. Christians differ on how to understand healing today, and some believe that prayer for healing is central, while others stress medicine, care and inclusion. A caution: people with disabilities have sometimes been hurt when they were told that they lacked faith or must be cured, and many Christians now stress acceptance.

## Islam

Islam teaches that all people are equal before God and are judged on faith and deeds, not on appearance or ability. In **Surah 80 ('Abasa, verses 1-10)**, according to Muslim tradition, the Prophet frowned and turned away from a blind man named Abdullah ibn Umm Maktum who interrupted him while he was speaking to the leaders of Makkah. The revelation gently corrected him: the blind man was sincerely seeking guidance, and the Prophet should not have turned away from him. Muslims learn from this that no person should be neglected or treated with less respect because of a disability or poverty. Ibn Umm Maktum later held a position of trust in Madinah, according to tradition.

Muslims have a duty of care for family and for those in need, including through Zakat and sadaqah, and Islamic teaching allows adjustments for people with impairments: for example, someone who is unable to fast or to go on the Hajj because of illness or disability is not required to do so. Muslim communities support people with disabilities in different ways, and Muslim teachers say that stigma and neglect go against the teaching of the faith.

| Religion | Key teaching or source | Supportive message | Possible stigma |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion | Unhu/ubuntu; care in the extended family | Family care; a place in ceremonies | Blame on spirits or witchcraft; hiding children |
| Judaism | Leviticus 19:14 | Do not insult or mislead; image of God | Barriers to participation in some communities |
| Christianity | John 9:1-3; Gospel healings | Disability is not punishment; the body needs every part | Telling people they lack faith |
| Islam | Surah 80 ('Abasa) | Equal before God; do not turn away anyone | Neglect where teaching is not applied |

## Support and stigma

Religious teaching can **support** people with disabilities by teaching dignity, giving community and care, and providing schools and services. It can also **stigmatise** when believers use teachings to blame a person or a family, when they say that a disability is a punishment, or when places of worship are not accessible. The teachings described above give religious believers strong reasons to reject stigma. Religious leaders can help by teaching the true causes, welcoming people with disabilities and speaking against myths.

:::example{title="Two views of John 9"}
Rachel, who is Jewish, and Farai, a Christian, read John 9:1-3 and Leviticus 19:14 together. Farai says that Jesus rejected the idea that the blind man was punished. Rachel says that the Torah already forbids insulting a blind person. They agree that both texts teach respect, although each belongs to a different tradition. In an examination, quote the reference, paraphrase it, and say what lesson believers draw.
:::

:::example{title="From teaching to action"}
After studying Surah 80, Aisha asks her mosque committee whether a ramp can be built, since an elderly member who uses a wheelchair cannot enter the prayer hall. Her action turns the lesson, that no one should be turned away, into practical help. This shows how teachings affect behaviour.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that one religion treats disability better than another"}
Describe what each religion teaches, and avoid ranking them. Mention that practice varies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting harmful traditional practices as Indigenous Religion in general"}
Some beliefs have caused harm in some families, but Indigenous Religion also teaches care, unhu and kinship. Give both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting a reference without explaining it"}
After John 9:1-3, Leviticus 19:14 or Surah 80, say what the passage teaches about disability.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *describe the attitudes of two religions to disability*, choose two, name a text or teaching for each, paraphrase it and say what it means for believers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *how far do religions help people with disabilities?*, give ways religion helps (care, schools, teaching of dignity) and ways it may harm (stigma, blame), then reach a supported judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In Indigenous Religion the extended family cares for all members (unhu/ubuntu), but beliefs about spirits or witchcraft have sometimes led to blame or hiding.
- Judaism forbids insulting a deaf person or putting an obstacle before a blind person (Leviticus 19:14).
- Christianity points to Jesus' healing ministry and to John 9:1-3, which many read as rejecting the idea that disability is punishment.
- Islam teaches equality before God; Surah 80 teaches that no one should be turned away.
- Religious attitudes can support people with disabilities or, when misapplied, stigmatise them.
:::
