## Introduction

At a funeral in Masvingo, a neighbour who barely knew the family arrives early, carries water, helps to cook and stays until the grave is filled. Nobody asked her. When someone thanks her, she says, "Munhu munhu navanhu." A person is a person because of other people.

That saying summarises **Unhu** (Shona), **Ubuntu** (Nguni) or **Vumunhu**, the Zimbabwean philosophy of being fully human through our relationships with others. This lesson explains what morality is, where moral ideas come from, whether morality needs religion, and how the four religions in this course teach the values that Unhu expresses. Applied ethics, such as health and sexuality, are covered in Topic 9. You should already know the functions of religion, including moral guidance.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Morality"}
The beliefs and practices about what is right and wrong, and good and bad, in human behaviour.
:::

:::definition{term="Ethics"}
The study of morality: asking what makes an action right or wrong and how people ought to live. In everyday speech, "ethics" and "morals" are often used as near synonyms.
:::

:::definition{term="Values"}
The principles that a person or community regards as important, such as honesty, respect, compassion and justice.
:::

:::definition{term="Norms"}
The accepted rules or expected patterns of behaviour in a group, for example greeting elders respectfully.
:::

:::definition{term="Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu"}
The philosophy that a person becomes fully human through relationships with others. It stresses respect, sharing, hospitality, responsibility, humility and care for the community. The Nguni saying *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* and the Shona saying *munhu munhu navanhu* both express it.
:::

## Sources of morality

| Source | How it guides behaviour | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Religion | Teaching from scripture, tradition and religious leaders | The Ten Commandments; the Qur'an; teachings of elders about the ancestors |
| Culture and family | Norms and values taught at home | Greeting elders; sharing food with visitors |
| Conscience | An inner sense of right and wrong | Feeling guilty after telling a lie |
| Law | Rules enforced by the state | The law against theft; the Constitution |
| Reason | Thinking about the effects of actions on others | Deciding not to cheat because it is unfair to others |

These sources overlap. A community's laws often reflect its religious values, and conscience is shaped by upbringing.

## Religion and morality

Is morality only possible with religion? People answer differently.

- **Many believers** say that religion gives morality its authority, since moral rules come from God or the ancestors, and it gives motives such as love of God and hope for reward.
- **Others, including people with no religion,** say that morality can also come from conscience, reason, human experience and law. They point to the many non-religious people who live honestly and kindly.
- **Most scholars** note that religion and morality are closely linked in practice, but are not identical. Religions teach about much more than morality, and morality exists in more than religions. Religions can also disagree with one another on particular moral questions.

:::keyfact
Religion is a major **source** of morality for many people, but not the only one. Religions share many moral values (honesty, respect for life, care for the needy), while differing on some detailed rules.
:::

## Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu

The values associated with Unhu include:

| Value | What it looks like in daily life |
|---|---|
| Respect | Greeting elders properly; listening to parents and teachers |
| Sharing | Giving food to neighbours; helping those in need |
| Hospitality | Welcoming visitors and strangers |
| Responsibility | Caring for children, the sick and the elderly; doing your share |
| Humility | Not boasting; accepting correction |
| Honesty and integrity | Telling the truth; keeping promises |
| Peace and forgiveness | Settling disputes through dialogue, as elders do |

A person with Unhu is described as *munhu chaiye* ("a real person"): someone whose conduct shows these values. A person who treats others badly is said to lack Unhu, even though they are physically a human being. Unhu is therefore a moral idea, not only a cultural one.

### How each religion supports these values

| Religion | Teaching that supports Unhu |
|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion | Elders and ancestors are believed to watch over family conduct; taboos (zviera) protect relationships and the land; the community shares in work, food and mourning |
| Judaism | The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); love of one's neighbour (Leviticus 19:18); care for the stranger (Deuteronomy 10:19); tzedakah (charity and justice) |
| Christianity | Love of God and neighbour (Matthew 22:37-40); the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12); the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37) |
| Islam | Kindness to parents (Qur'an 17:23); giving to relatives, orphans and the needy (Qur'an 2:177); zakat; hospitality and honesty |

Notice how each tradition teaches the same idea in its own words: that people are bound together and owe each other care.

:::example{title="Unhu at a school: three responses to a lost wallet"}
Farai finds a wallet with 20 US dollars in the corridor at his school in Mutare. He knows that no one saw him find it.

1. **Indigenous Religion and Unhu:** an elder would remind him that taking what belongs to another breaks the trust of the community and that the ancestors look for honesty in the family.
2. **Christian view:** many Christians would point to the commandment not to steal (Exodus 20) and to loving one's neighbour as oneself.
3. **Muslim view:** Muslims are taught to be honest and to return trusts to their owners.
4. **Action:** Farai hands the wallet to the teacher. His decision reflects honesty, respect and responsibility, whatever religion his family follows.
:::

:::example{title="Sharing and hospitality in a Hwange village"}
A stranger arrives at a homestead near Hwange at dusk, tired from a long walk.

1. **Welcome:** the family greets him, gives him water and invites him to eat.
2. **Meaning in Unhu:** hospitality shows that the stranger is a person who belongs to the human community.
3. **Other traditions:** Deuteronomy 10:19 calls Jews to love the stranger, many Christians recall the Good Samaritan, and Muslims are taught to honour the guest.
4. **Result:** the same value is expressed in several religious languages.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that only religious people can be moral"}
Morality also comes from conscience, reason, culture and law. Say that religion is a major source for many people, not the only one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating Unhu as only a Shona idea"}
Unhu (Shona), Ubuntu (Nguni) and Vumunhu are related expressions of a widely shared African philosophy, and each community has its own proverbs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing values and norms"}
Values are principles (honesty); norms are expected behaviours (telling the truth in court).
:::

:::mistake{title="Ranking religions by their morality"}
Never say that one religion is more moral than another. Show how each teaches care and honesty in its own words.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1"}

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Morality is beliefs about right and wrong; ethics studies morality; values are principles; norms are expected behaviours.
- Sources of morality include religion, culture and family, conscience, law and reason, and they overlap.
- Religion is a major but not the only source of morality, and religions share many values.
- Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu: a person is a person through other people, expressed in respect, sharing, hospitality, responsibility and humility.
- Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam each teach care for others, honesty and respect in their own words.
:::
