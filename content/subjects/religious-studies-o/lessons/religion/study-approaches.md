## Introduction

Rudo, Yusuf, Miriam and Tafadzwa are in the same class. Rudo's family holds ancestral ceremonies, Yusuf prays five times a day, Miriam keeps Shabbat, and Tafadzwa's family has no religion. When the teacher asks, "What do you believe about God?", they give four different answers. Fair study requires words that can describe all four without judging any, and a method that lets us listen to each of them.

This lesson gives you that vocabulary and method. It follows from the lesson on the meaning and features of religion and from the lesson on theories of origin. It also explains why this subject asks you to **describe and understand** beliefs, and not to judge or convert.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monotheism"}
Belief in one God. Judaism, Christianity and Islam are monotheistic religions.
:::

:::definition{term="Polytheism"}
Belief in many gods or goddesses, each often with its own role. Ancient Greek religion is an example, and Hinduism is sometimes described this way, although many Hindus explain their tradition differently.
:::

:::definition{term="Henotheism"}
Devotion to one god while accepting that other gods exist, as in some ancient traditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Pantheism"}
The belief that God is identical with the universe and nature, or that God and the world are one.
:::

:::definition{term="Atheism and agnosticism"}
**Atheism** is the belief that there is no God or gods. **Agnosticism** is the view that we cannot know whether God exists.
:::

:::definition{term="Secularism"}
A way of organising public life, such as government and law, so that it does not depend on or favour any one religion. A secular state can still protect religious freedom.
:::

:::definition{term="Revelation"}
God making himself or his will known. **General** revelation is knowledge of God through nature, conscience or reason; **special** revelation is knowledge given through a prophet, a vision or a scripture.
:::

## Types of belief about the divine

| Term | Number of gods | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monotheism | One | Judaism, Christianity, Islam | Christians describe one God in three persons (the Trinity) and still call this monotheism |
| Polytheism | Many | Ancient Greek and Roman religions | Many Hindus emphasise one ultimate reality shown in many forms |
| Henotheism | Many exist, one is worshipped | Some ancient traditions | A term scholars use; believers may not use it |
| Pantheism | God is everything | Some philosophers and some strands of religious thought | God is not separate from nature |
| Atheism | None | Some individuals and groups | A position on God, not a religion |
| Agnosticism | Unknown | Some individuals | Neither affirms nor denies |

**Indigenous Religion** needs special care. Many scholars describe belief in Mwari (uMlimo, uNkulunkulu) as belief in one supreme God who created the world, with the ancestors acting as intermediaries and not as gods. Others stress the importance of the spirit world. The ancestors are *venerated* (honoured and asked for help), and many believers say that they are not worshipped as God is. Avoid saying that Indigenous Religion is "polytheistic" or "ancestor worship" without explanation, and describe it in the way its followers do.

:::keyfact
**Revelation:** the Torah at Sinai (Jews), the Qur'an to the Prophet Muhammad (Muslims) and Jesus as the revelation of God (Christians) are examples of *special* revelation. Seeing God's work in creation is *general* revelation.
:::

## Insider and outsider perspectives

| Perspective | Also called | Who | Question asked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Insider | Emic | A believer describing their own religion | What does this mean to me, and how do we practise it? |
| Outsider | Etic | A scholar or student looking from outside | What does this religion teach, and how does it compare with others? |

Both are useful. An insider knows what a ritual means to those who perform it; an outsider can compare religions and notice patterns the insider takes for granted. A good student listens to insiders and uses the outsider's care and accuracy.

## Approaches used in this subject

- **Phenomenological approach:** describe a religion as it appears to its followers, setting aside your own opinions ("suspending judgement") so that you can understand before you evaluate.
- **Comparative approach:** compare religions on the same points, such as beliefs about God, festivals or rites of passage, looking for similarities and differences.
- **Multi-faith approach:** study several religions side by side with equal respect, as this subject does with Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam.
- **Non-confessional and non-proselytising:** the aim is knowledge and understanding, not belief. You are not asked to join or leave any religion.

:::example{title="Using the approaches on one festival"}
A class in Mutare studies the festival of Eid al-Fitr.

1. **Phenomenological:** they describe it as Muslims do: it marks the end of Ramadan, with special prayers, charity and a family meal.
2. **Comparative:** they compare it with Christian Easter and Jewish Pesach, noting that each is a festival of thanks and renewal connected with sacred history.
3. **Insider voice:** Yusuf explains what Eid means to his family.
4. **Outcome:** each learner understands more, and nobody is asked to change their own belief.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a belief correctly"}
Compare two ways of writing about the same belief.

1. **Preaching:** "Jesus rose from the dead, so Christianity is the true religion." This asserts a religious claim and ranks religions.
2. **Describing:** "Christians believe that Jesus rose from the dead on the third day, and they celebrate this at Easter." This reports a belief accurately.
3. **Result:** the second is the style expected in this subject.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a religion 'false', 'primitive' or 'pagan'"}
These are judgements. Describe beliefs as the beliefs of their followers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up atheism and agnosticism"}
Atheism says there is no God. Agnosticism says we cannot know. Neither is a religion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Christian belief in the Trinity polytheism"}
Christians teach one God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. This is understood as monotheism.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing secularism with atheism"}
A secular state does not favour a religion, but it can protect religion. Secularism is about how public life is organised, not about denying God.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1"}
For *distinguish between* or *explain the meaning of* terms, give a definition and an example for each, for instance "Monotheism is belief in one God, as in Islam". Always name the religion you use as an example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining the approach"}
In a question about why the subject is non-proselytising, say that it describes and understands beliefs fairly, respects freedom of conscience (section 60 of the Constitution) and does not ask learners to change belief.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Monotheism is belief in one God, polytheism in many, henotheism worships one among many, and pantheism holds that God and the universe are one.
- Atheism says there is no God; agnosticism says we cannot know; secularism is about keeping public life independent of any one religion.
- General revelation is knowledge of God through nature and conscience; special revelation is through prophets, visions or scriptures.
- Insiders (emic) describe their own religion; outsiders (etic) study it from outside; good study uses both.
- The phenomenological, comparative and multi-faith approaches describe religions fairly, and this subject is non-confessional and non-proselytising.
:::
