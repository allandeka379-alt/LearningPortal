## Introduction

When Rudo was twelve, she asked her grandmother, "Who taught people to pray?" Her grandmother answered that God made people and that the ancestors have always guided the family. Her classmate Yusuf answered that the Qur'an was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad. Her teacher then explained that scholars have also asked the question in another way: *what in human life leads people to have religion at all?*

This lesson looks at some of the best-known answers. They are called **theories**: attempts to explain something, which can be compared, tested against evidence and criticised. They are not facts, and none of them is accepted by every scholar. Most were written by outsiders to the religions they discuss, so many believers disagree with them. You should already know what religion is and its main features.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Theory"}
An explanation offered for something, based on reasoning and evidence, that can be discussed and challenged. A theory is not the same as a proven fact.
:::

:::definition{term="Revelation"}
In religions that teach it, God's making himself or his will known to people, for example through a prophet or a scripture. This is the religions' own account of their origin.
:::

:::definition{term="Animism"}
The word used by E. B. Tylor for belief that spirits or souls are present in people, animals, plants and natural things.
:::

:::definition{term="Totemism"}
A system in which a clan has a special bond with a totem, usually an animal or plant, which is respected and often may not be harmed.
:::

:::definition{term="Projection"}
In Freud's theory, the human tendency to project feelings, wishes and fears onto an imagined being, so that religion reflects human needs.
:::

## The religions' own account: revelation

Judaism, Christianity and Islam teach that their religions began when God made himself known. Jews believe that God revealed the Torah to Moses at Sinai. Christians believe that God was revealed in the life of Jesus. Muslims believe that the Qur'an was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibril. In Indigenous Religion, people believe that Mwari (uMlimo) created the world and its people and that the ancestors and elders have handed on the way of life since the beginning.

This is an **insider's** explanation: it is a belief, held by faith, and it is not examined as a scholar's theory. The theories that follow are **outsiders'** explanations, which look for natural causes in human life and society. A scholar who proposes one is not saying whether God exists.

## Theories compared

| Thinker (dates of main work) | Theory | Central idea |
|---|---|---|
| E. B. Tylor (*Primitive Culture*, 1871) | Animism | Religion began with belief in souls and spirits in people and nature |
| Émile Durkheim (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912) | Social theory (totemism) | Religion is created by society; the clan worships what stands for itself |
| Sigmund Freud (*The Future of an Illusion*, 1927) | Psychological theory | Religion meets deep human wishes for protection, like a child's need for a parent |
| Karl Marx (writing in the 1840s) | Religion and society | Religion comforts people in an unjust society and supports the order that harms them |
| Max Müller and others | Fear and wonder at nature | Awe and fear of the sun, storms and death led people to honour powers behind nature |

### Animism (Tylor)
Tylor suggested that early people, trying to understand dreams, death and shadows, concluded that a spirit could leave the body. They then believed that spirits live in nature too. Religion grew from this, he said, and later developed from belief in many spirits to belief in one God.

*Strengths:* it explains why belief in spirits and ancestors is so common. *Criticisms:* it assumes religion developed in stages from "simple" to "complex", which is not supported by evidence; it treats belief as a mistake in reasoning; and Tylor's older description of early religion as "primitive" is now rejected by scholars as unfair. The word *animism* is also not accepted by many Indigenous Religion believers as a label for their faith.

### The social theory (Durkheim)
Durkheim based his theory on reports about Aboriginal Australian clans, whose totems he saw as sacred symbols. He argued that when the clan gathers and feels a power greater than any individual, it is really experiencing the power of society, and religion is a way of expressing and strengthening the unity of the group. The sacred, for him, stands for society itself.

*Strengths:* it explains why religion unites groups and why rituals and festivals matter, including totems in Zimbabwe where a mutupo links families. *Criticisms:* it does not explain personal religious experience; its evidence came from a small number of cases; and it does not explain religions in which God is believed to be beyond the group.

### The psychological theory (Freud)
Freud argued that people feel helpless before nature, illness and death, and that belief in a powerful and caring Father-God meets the wish for protection, just as a child depends on its parents. He called religion an illusion: a belief based on wishing. (He did not claim that illusions must be false.)

*Strengths:* it explains why religion comforts people in distress. *Criticisms:* many believers have religion for deeper reasons than wish-fulfilment; the theory ignores community and morality; and it was drawn mainly from Freud's study of European individuals.

### Marx's view
Marx saw religion as part of the structure of an unequal society. He wrote that it comforts the suffering, as a painkiller does, but keeps people from changing the conditions that cause the pain. For Marx, religion would fade when injustice ended.

*Strengths:* it draws attention to cases in which religion has been used to defend unfair systems. *Criticisms:* it overlooks occasions when religious groups have opposed injustice; it assumes that religion has one social effect; and many religions have survived in societies with little inequality.

### Fear and wonder at nature
Some writers suggested that early people feared storms, drought and death and felt wonder at the sun, rivers and mountains, and so honoured the powers behind them. *Strengths:* it fits the importance of rain-making and thanksgiving rituals. *Criticisms:* it is hard to prove and does not explain moral teaching or why religions are so varied.

:::keyfact
Theories are **explanations to be compared**, not facts. In an answer, name the thinker, state the central idea, and add one strength or one criticism. Write "Tylor suggested that…", not "Tylor proved that…".
:::

:::example{title="Applying two theories to a rain ceremony"}
In a dry season, a community near Masvingo holds a mukwerera ceremony, led by elders and a spirit medium.

1. **Durkheim's view:** the whole community gathers, which strengthens its unity. The ceremony expresses shared identity and duty.
2. **Fear and wonder:** drought threatens the harvest, and the people turn to the power believed to send rain.
3. **Believers' own account:** participants say that they are asking Mwari and the ancestors for rain, as they have done since the elders taught them.

The three explanations are not the same: the first two are outsiders' theories, and the third is the participants' own belief.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing Freud and Marx"}
Both Freud and Marx saw religion as a comfort, but they explained it differently.

1. **Freud:** the comfort answers a personal wish for protection.
2. **Marx:** the comfort answers social suffering and, he said, keeps people from changing their conditions.
3. **Contrast:** Freud focuses on the individual mind, and Marx on society and economics.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Presenting a theory as a proven fact"}
Say "Durkheim argued that…" or "According to Tylor…". Do not write "Religion began with animism".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the thinkers"}
Tylor is animism, Durkheim is society and totems, Freud is wish and projection, and Marx is comfort and the social order.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'primitive' or 'pagan' about Indigenous Religion"}
These are loaded labels. Describe Indigenous Religion respectfully, as a religion in its own right.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the religions' own accounts"}
Always say that revelation is the believers' explanation; a full answer shows both insider and outsider views.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (a)"}
When asked to *describe* or *outline* theories, take three and give each: the thinker, the idea (two sentences) and a strength or criticism. This makes five marks for a theory and 15 for three.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain why no single theory is accepted by everyone*, set theories side by side, say what each leaves out, and mention the believers' view that religion begins in revelation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Theories of the origin of religion are explanations to compare, not proven facts.
- Religions give their own accounts through revelation: the Torah at Sinai, the Qur'an to the Prophet Muhammad, and in Indigenous Religion creation by Mwari.
- Tylor: animism. Durkheim: society worships itself through symbols such as totems. Freud: religion meets a wish for protection. Marx: religion comforts people in an unjust society.
- The fear and wonder theory says awe at nature led people to honour powers behind it.
- Each theory has strengths and criticisms, and none is accepted by all scholars.
:::
