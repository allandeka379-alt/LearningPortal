## Introduction

Walk through a Zimbabwean town on a Friday and a Sunday. On Friday you may hear the call to prayer near a mosque in Harare or Mutare. On Sunday church bells and choirs fill the suburbs. In a rural village that same weekend, a family may be holding a bira for its ancestors. In Bulawayo, a Jewish family marks Shabbat, and in some cities small Hindu and Bahá'í communities hold their own gatherings.

Many families themselves are religiously mixed: a grandmother who honours the ancestors, parents who attend church and a cousin who has become a Muslim. This lesson describes the religions found in Zimbabwe, explains what the Constitution says about religious freedom and shows why tolerance and cooperation matter. It also explains why this subject is **multi-faith and non-confessional**. You should already know the meaning of religion and the features that religions share.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Religious diversity"}
The presence of many different religions, and of different groups within them, in one society.
:::

:::definition{term="Religious pluralism"}
Accepting and valuing the existence of several religions side by side, with each given respect and equal protection.
:::

:::definition{term="Religious tolerance"}
Respecting the right of others to hold and practise beliefs different from your own, even if you do not share them.
:::

:::definition{term="Interfaith dialogue"}
Respectful conversation and cooperation between people of different religions, aimed at understanding, not at converting.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-confessional"}
Describing a subject that teaches about religions without asking learners to hold, or to give up, any belief.
:::

## Religions in Zimbabwe

| Religion | Who practises it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion (IR) | Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Kalanga and other peoples | Belief in God (Mwari, uMlimo/uNkulunkulu), the ancestors and spirits; no single founder; passed on orally |
| Christianity | A large majority of Zimbabweans identify as Christian | Catholic, Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian, Lutheran, Seventh-day Adventist, Pentecostal and many African Independent (apostolic and Zionist) churches |
| Islam | A minority community, found in cities and towns | Mosques and madrasa classes; Most Zimbabwean Muslims are Sunni |
| Judaism | A small community | Historic congregations in cities such as Bulawayo and Harare |
| Other faiths | Small communities | Hinduism and the Bahá'í faith are among those found in towns |

Many people take part in more than one tradition. For example, a Christian family may hold a funeral service at church and also perform a ceremony for the spirit of the deceased. Scholars call this overlap **religious syncretism** or "blending". Some religious leaders welcome it and others do not, so describe it fairly. For the exact numbers, you should rely on the Zimbabwe National Statistics Agency (ZIMSTAT) census and survey reports, which give figures for particular years. Avoid quoting percentages from memory.

:::keyfact
Section 60 of the **Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013** protects freedom of conscience, which includes freedom of thought, opinion, religion and belief, and the freedom to practise and express them in public or in private, alone or with others. It also protects people from being forced to take an oath contrary to their religion or belief.
:::

## Freedom of religion and tolerance

Section 60 means that:

- everyone is free to hold a religion or belief, and to practise and express it;
- religious communities may worship together and organise themselves;
- no one may be forced to take an oath that goes against their belief;
- the state may not treat people unfairly because of their religion (see section 56).

Section 56 adds that all persons are equal before the law and may not be treated unfairly on grounds that include religion. Freedom of religion is not unlimited: it does not allow harm to others. Rights are exercised with respect for the rights of other people.

International documents agree. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) protects freedom of thought, conscience and religion.

### Why tolerance matters
- It protects peace in schools, workplaces and families that include several faiths.
- It protects the dignity of every person, as the Constitution requires.
- It allows people to learn from one another's values, such as unhu, care for the needy and honesty.
- It reduces fear and prejudice, which can lead to discrimination or conflict.

### Interfaith cooperation in practice
Religious communities often work together. Church councils, Muslim organisations and other religious bodies sometimes join in prayers for rain, for peace or for victims of disaster, and in programmes for health, education and the care of orphans. Families, schools and neighbourhoods do the same on a smaller scale.

:::example{title="Two learners, one class"}
In a fictional Form 3 class in Marondera, Tatenda is a Pentecostal Christian and Yusuf is a Muslim. During Ramadan, Yusuf fasts from dawn until sunset.

1. **Understanding:** Tatenda asks why Yusuf is fasting and learns that it is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
2. **Respect:** their class avoids eating in front of him at lunch, and the teacher arranges a quiet place for him to rest.
3. **Cooperation:** when Eid al-Fitr arrives, Yusuf invites classmates to a meal, and Tatenda later invites him to the school carol evening.
4. **Result:** each learns about the other's faith without having to give up their own.
:::

:::example{title="A community project in Gwanda"}
In a fictional district of Gwanda, an elder of Indigenous Religion, a church minister and the imam of a nearby mosque agree to organise a clean-up of the river after a drought.

1. **Shared concern:** all three traditions teach care for water and neighbours.
2. **Each in their own way:** the elder explains the taboos that protect the river, the minister reads a passage about caring for creation, and the imam reminds people of the duty to avoid waste.
3. **Outcome:** the community works together, and each faith contributes its own values.
:::

## A multi-faith, non-confessional subject

This subject is **multi-faith**, because it studies Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam with equal respect. It is **non-confessional**, because it does not teach any one religion as the right one, and **non-proselytising**, because it does not try to persuade learners to change their beliefs. You are asked to describe and understand ("Muslims believe…", "In Indigenous Religion, people believe…") and to treat the beliefs of others with respect. You are free to keep your own faith, or none.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing tolerance with agreement"}
Tolerance means respecting people's right to hold different beliefs. It does not mean that you must agree with them or that all beliefs are the same.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting percentages from memory"}
Say "a large majority identify as Christian" and, if figures are needed, name the ZIMSTAT census and the year.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that freedom of religion has no limits"}
Section 60 protects belief and practice, but rights may not be used to harm others or to remove their rights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Indigenous Religion 'culture' only"}
Indigenous Religion is a religion with beliefs, worship, practitioners and morality, as well as a way of life.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1"}
When asked to *describe the religions found in Zimbabwe*, name each religion, say who practises it and give one belief or practice. For *explain why tolerance is important*, give reasons linked to the Constitution (section 60), peace and human dignity.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Developing points"}
To earn full marks for a point, link it to an example: "Tolerance helps in a class with learners of different faiths, such as one who fasts in Ramadan."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe has many religions: Indigenous Religion, Christianity (many denominations), Islam, Judaism and smaller faiths such as Hinduism and the Bahá'í faith.
- Religious pluralism means respecting several religions side by side; tolerance means respecting the right to different beliefs.
- Section 60 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013 protects freedom of conscience, including religion and belief; section 56 prohibits unfair discrimination.
- Interfaith dialogue and cooperation build understanding and peace.
- This subject is multi-faith, non-confessional and non-proselytising: we describe and understand beliefs without judging or converting.
:::
