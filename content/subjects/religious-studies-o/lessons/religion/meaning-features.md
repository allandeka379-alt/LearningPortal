## Introduction

On a single Sunday in Harare you might pass a family walking to church, a group of women singing outside a rural homestead preparing for a bira, and, on a Friday, men heading to a mosque for Jumu'ah prayers. In Bulawayo, a Jewish family may be marking Shabbat at home. These people hold different beliefs and worship in different ways, yet we call all of these activities **religion**. What do they have in common?

This lesson opens the course. It asks what religion means and sets out the features that most religions share, so that in later topics you can describe Indigenous Religion, Judaism, Christianity and Islam fairly and compare them. In this subject we **describe and understand** what people believe; we do not judge whose beliefs are right, and we do not try to persuade anyone. You should already know that Zimbabwe is home to many faiths and that families sometimes include members of more than one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Religion"}
A system of beliefs and practices, usually centred on the sacred, the divine or the supernatural, that gives a community a way of understanding life and a way of living and worshipping together. There is no single agreed definition.
:::

:::definition{term="Sacred and profane"}
Words used by the scholar Émile Durkheim. The **sacred** is what a community sets apart as holy and treats with special respect; the **profane** is the ordinary, everyday world.
:::

:::definition{term="Faith"}
Trust or commitment to a belief or to God, held without needing proof in the way a scientific claim does. The word is also used for a religion as a whole ("the Muslim faith").
:::

:::definition{term="Worship"}
Acts of respect, honour and devotion directed to God, a deity or the spirits, such as prayer, song, offerings and meditation.
:::

:::definition{term="Ritual"}
A set of actions performed in a fixed way, with symbolic meaning, such as baptism, the pouring of beer to the ancestors, or washing before prayer.
:::

:::definition{term="Myth"}
In the study of religion, a traditional sacred story that explains how the world, people, death or a custom came to be. It does not mean "a false story".
:::

:::definition{term="Symbol"}
An object, action or image that stands for something deeper, for example the cross, the crescent, the Star of David or a totem animal.
:::

## Defining religion from different angles

Scholars cannot agree on one definition, because religions are so varied. Three useful angles are shown below.

| Angle | Religion is seen as | Example of the idea |
|---|---|---|
| Belief in the sacred or supernatural | Belief in a power, God or spirits beyond ordinary human experience | Belief in Mwari and the ancestors; belief in one God in Judaism, Christianity and Islam |
| A way of life | Teaching that shapes how people eat, dress, work, marry and treat others | Observing Shabbat; fasting in Ramadan; keeping chisi, the rest day |
| A community of believers | A group that shares beliefs, worships together and supports each other | A congregation, a mosque community, a family gathered for a bira |

Each angle misses something if used alone. A definition based only on belief in God would leave out religions that centre on practice, and a definition based only on community would not distinguish a church from a football supporters' club. For this reason, most textbooks describe religion by listing its **features**.

## The features of religion

Ninian Smart, a scholar of religion, suggested that we can study any religion through seven "dimensions". They are one scholar's model, not an official list, but they help us notice what to look for.

| Feature | What it means | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Beliefs (doctrine) | What members hold to be true about God, spirits, humans, life and death | Monotheism in Islam; ancestors as intermediaries in Indigenous Religion |
| Worship and ritual | The practical side: prayer, offerings, ceremonies | Salah; the Eucharist (Holy Communion); mukwerera |
| Sacred places, objects and times | Places and things set apart, and holy days | A synagogue, mosque or church; Njelele; the mbira; Sabbath; chisi |
| Sacred texts or oral tradition | The source of teaching, written or spoken | The Torah, the Bible, the Qur'an; the oral traditions of Indigenous Religion |
| Practitioners | People with special religious roles | Rabbi, priest, imam, svikiro, n'anga |
| Moral code | Rules and values about right and wrong | The Ten Commandments; unhu; the zviera (taboos) |
| Symbols | Signs that carry meaning | The cross, the crescent, the menorah, totems |
| Community | The group of believers and its institutions | A congregation, a clan, the ummah (Muslim community) |

Smart's seven dimensions are usually named as the practical and ritual, the experiential and emotional, the narrative and mythic, the doctrinal and philosophical, the ethical and legal, the social and institutional, and the material dimension. Notice that they overlap with the table above: the material dimension covers places, objects and art, and the experiential dimension covers the feelings of awe, peace or closeness to God that believers describe.

:::keyfact
The word **religion** is used for very different traditions. Some have a founder and a written scripture (Islam and Christianity); others, such as Indigenous Religion, have no single founder and pass on teaching orally. A fair study looks at all of them on their own terms.
:::

:::example{title="Features in one ceremony: a church wedding in Gweru"}
Tafadzwa and Chipo marry in a church in Gweru. Look at the features at work.

1. **Beliefs:** the couple and the congregation believe that marriage is a gift from God.
2. **Ritual and worship:** the minister leads prayers, the couple exchange vows and rings, and the congregation sings.
3. **Sacred place and time:** the church building is set apart for worship, and the wedding is a religious service.
4. **Sacred text:** passages from the Bible are read aloud.
5. **Practitioner:** the minister conducts the service.
6. **Moral code:** the vows promise faithfulness and care.
7. **Symbols and community:** rings, a cross on the wall, and relatives and church members who witness the marriage and promise to support the couple.
:::

:::example{title="The same features in a different religion: a bira"}
In a village near Masvingo, a family holds a bira for its ancestral spirits.

1. **Beliefs:** the family believes that the spirits of its dead (midzimu) continue to care for the living and may ask for attention.
2. **Ritual and worship:** beer is brewed and offered, snuff may be shared, and people sing, clap and dance through the night to mbira music.
3. **Sacred place and objects:** the ceremony takes place at the family home, with the mbira and beer pots treated with respect.
4. **Practitioner:** a spirit medium (svikiro) is believed to speak for a spirit, and elders lead the proceedings.
5. **Moral code and community:** the relatives meet to settle matters and renew family bonds, in line with unhu.

Both ceremonies show beliefs, ritual, practitioners and community, even though the beliefs and symbols differ.
:::

## Why the features matter

Using the features gives us a fair framework. We can ask the same questions about every religion: *What do members believe? How do they worship? Where, when and with what? Who leads? How do they live? What holds them together?* This avoids judging a religion because it does not look like our own. For example, a religion with no building is still a religion if it has beliefs, worship, practitioners and a moral code.

Remember too that features vary within a religion. Orthodox and Reform Jews, Catholic and Pentecostal Christians, Sunni and Shi'a Muslims, and Shona, Ndebele and Tonga communities do not all practise in exactly the same way. Use words such as "many" and "most" rather than "all", and attribute beliefs: "Christians believe that…", "Muslims believe that…".

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing 'religion says' as though all religions agree"}
Different religions teach different things. Write "Muslims believe…" or "In Indigenous Religion…" and say which religion you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a myth as a lie"}
In the study of religion, a myth is a sacred story that explains the meaning of something. Calling it "just a myth" is disrespectful and misses the point of the term.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sacred and profane with good and bad"}
Profane does not mean evil. It simply means ordinary and not set apart. A kitchen is profane in this sense; a prayer hall is sacred.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing features without explaining them"}
Naming "symbols" earns little. Say what a symbol stands for and give an example from a named religion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify, outline and describe"}
*Identify* or *name* needs a word or short phrase. *Outline* needs a brief point for each feature. *Describe* (Paper 1 part (a), usually 15 marks) needs each feature explained with an example from at least one religion, for instance: "Practitioners are people with special roles, such as a svikiro who is believed to speak for a spirit."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain and discuss"}
For *explain* (part (b)), give a point and then say why or how it matters. For questions that ask whether a definition of religion is adequate, give more than one view and finish with a reasoned conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Religion is a system of beliefs and practices, usually about the sacred or supernatural, shared by a community; there is no single agreed definition.
- It can be defined as belief in the sacred, as a way of life, or as a community of believers.
- Common features are beliefs, worship and ritual, sacred places, objects and times, sacred texts or oral tradition, practitioners, moral codes, symbols and community.
- Ninian Smart's seven dimensions are one scholar's model for studying these features.
- Key terms are sacred and profane, faith, worship, ritual, myth and symbol.
- In this subject we describe beliefs fairly ("Muslims believe…") and do not judge or convert.
:::
