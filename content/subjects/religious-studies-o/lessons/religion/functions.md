## Introduction

When a family in Chinhoyi loses a loved one, what happens next? Relatives arrive with food and money. A minister, an imam or the family elders lead prayers or rites. Neighbours dig the grave and cook. A month later, the family may hold another ceremony to remember the person. In this one week religion has comforted the bereaved, brought people together, set out what should be done and marked a turning point in life.

Scholars call these effects the **functions** of religion: what religion does for people and society. Some functions are positive and some are harmful. This lesson takes a balanced view. You should already know the main features of religion; detailed contemporary issues such as education, gender, health and conflict are covered in later topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Function (of religion)"}
What religion does, or is used to do, for individuals and society, whether or not believers think of it in that way.
:::

:::definition{term="Social cohesion"}
The bonds that hold a society together, creating a sense of belonging and shared values.
:::

:::definition{term="Social control"}
The ways a society encourages people to follow its rules and discourages behaviour that harms others, for example through teachings, taboos and the fear of disapproval.
:::

:::definition{term="Rite of passage"}
A ceremony that marks a change in a person's life, such as birth, initiation, marriage or death.
:::

:::definition{term="Fundamentalism"}
A strict insistence on a narrow reading of a religion's teachings, sometimes with the claim that other views are not acceptable. The word is used differently by different writers, so it is best defined whenever you use it.
:::

## Functions for the individual

| Function | How religion provides it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning and purpose | Answers big questions: why we are here, what happens at death | Many Christians and Muslims believe in life after death; in Indigenous Religion the dead become ancestors |
| Identity and belonging | Gives a name, a community and a place | A child is named in a family ceremony; a young Muslim joins a madrasa class |
| Comfort and hope | Prayer and support in illness, grief and fear | A bereaved family sings and prays together |
| Moral guidance | Teaches right and wrong | The Ten Commandments; the teaching of unhu; the Five Pillars |
| Self-discipline and character | Practices such as fasting and regular prayer train the self | Ramadan fasting; Lent |

## Functions for the family, community and nation

| Function | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Social cohesion | Shared beliefs and rituals bind people together | A village gathers for mukwerera; a congregation worships together |
| Social control | Beliefs and taboos discourage harmful behaviour | Zviera (taboos) protect rivers, forests and relationships; the Ten Commandments forbid stealing |
| Welfare and care | Religious bodies help the needy | Church and mosque programmes for orphans, the sick and the poor; zakat |
| Education | Religious bodies run schools and teach values | Many mission and Islamic schools in Zimbabwe (see topic 7) |
| Health | Religious bodies run clinics and hospitals and offer healing | Mission hospitals; traditional healers |
| Marking life's stages | Rites of passage | Baptism, bar mitzvah, initiation, marriage, funerals |
| Legitimising authority | Religious language supports rulers and laws | Chiefs installed with the blessing of the spirits; prayers at official events |
| Social change | Religious leaders can challenge injustice | Religious leaders speaking against poverty, violence and unfair treatment |

:::example{title="A church and a community in Gweru"}
A church in a fictional suburb of Gweru begins a feeding programme for pupils whose families struggle to afford lunch, and its members visit homes where the elderly live alone.

1. **Welfare:** the church meets a practical need.
2. **Cohesion:** members and non-members work together.
3. **Moral guidance:** the programme grows from the teaching to care for the needy.
4. **Identity:** members find a way to live out their beliefs.

The example shows several functions working at once. Mosques, synagogues and Indigenous Religion communities, in their own ways, also care for those in need.
:::

:::example{title="Chisi and the care of the land"}
In some Shona communities a rest day, chisi, is set aside on which fields are not worked. Believers see it as respect for the land and the ancestors.

1. **Belief:** the land is cared for by spirits that must be respected.
2. **Social control:** the whole community follows the rule, and a person who breaks it may be reminded or fined by the elders.
3. **Possible effect:** the land and the people both get a rest, and everyone shares the same day.
:::

:::keyfact
Functions are **what religion does**, not what is true. A scholar can say "religion gives comfort" without deciding whether any belief is correct. Always give a religious example to support each function you name.
:::

### Religion and the nation
At national level, religion appears in public life in several ways. Leaders of different faiths are often invited to pray at national events such as Independence Day or Heroes' Day, and religious bodies speak on matters such as poverty, peace and the care of the vulnerable. Religious language can also be used to support those in power, which is why scholars ask whether a given use of religion serves justice or only authority. Each case should be judged on its evidence and described fairly, without taking sides in current politics.

## Negative effects, in a balanced way

Religion can also be used in ways that harm. A fair answer notes both sides.

- **Conflict:** differences of belief have sometimes been used to divide communities or to justify violence, though many conflicts also have political and economic causes.
- **Exploitation:** some leaders have taken money or favours from followers, for example by promising miracles in exchange for payment.
- **Fundamentalism:** a strict refusal to listen to other views can lead to intolerance.
- **Harmful practices:** in some places, religious reasons have been given for child marriage, for refusing medical care for children, or for accusing people of witchcraft. These harm the people involved, and several are illegal in Zimbabwe. Leaders within each religion also speak against them.
- **Resistance to change:** religion can slow reforms, for instance in the treatment of women, although it can also support reform.

Remember that these are cases of how religion is used or misused by some people. They are not a description of any one faith as a whole.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing functions without examples"}
"Religion gives comfort" earns little. Add "for example, a bereaved family finds comfort in funeral prayers".
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing religion only as good, or only as bad"}
A balanced answer gives positive functions and negative effects, and says that both depend on how religion is used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming one religion"}
Use examples from more than one religion, and write "some people", "some groups", not "this religion causes".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing social cohesion with social control"}
Cohesion is about unity and belonging. Control is about rules and discouraging harmful behaviour.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (a)"}
When asked to *describe the functions of religion*, give about seven developed functions (two marks each: the function and its explanation or example) and group them by individual, community and nation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1, part (b)"}
For *explain the importance of religion in society*, develop three or four functions and add a short note on negative effects to show balance. Bring in Indigenous Religion as well as at least one other religion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Functions are what religion does for individuals and society.
- For individuals: meaning and purpose, identity and belonging, comfort, moral guidance and self-discipline.
- For society: cohesion, social control, welfare, education, health, rites of passage, legitimising authority and sometimes social change.
- Religion can also be linked to conflict, exploitation, fundamentalism and harmful practices, depending on how it is used.
- A balanced answer gives both sides with examples from more than one religion.
:::
