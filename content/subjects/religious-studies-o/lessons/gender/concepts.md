## Introduction

At a family gathering in Bulawayo, the women are cooking in the kitchen and the men are sitting under a tree talking. A young girl asks, "Why can't I sit with the men?" Her cousin, a boy, is told to go and fetch firewood rather than help with the dishes. In a school in Harare, a teacher asks pupils to name jobs. One boy says a nurse, and the class laughs. These everyday scenes raise questions about gender: what is expected of boys and girls, men and women, and where those expectations come from.

This lesson gives you the vocabulary for the whole of Topic 8. It explains the difference between sex and gender, how gender roles and stereotypes are learned, what equality and equity mean, and what the Constitution of Zimbabwe says in outline. What each religion teaches about gender is taught in the next lessons, so here you should concentrate on the concepts. A good Religious Studies answer defines terms precisely and uses examples without taking sides unfairly.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sex"}
The biological differences between females and males, such as reproductive organs and hormones. Sex is the same in most societies.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender"}
The roles, behaviours, responsibilities and expectations that a society regards as suitable for women and men, or girls and boys. Gender differs between societies and changes over time.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender role"}
A set of tasks and behaviours that a society expects of a person because they are a woman or a man, for example who cooks, who herds cattle, who leads a meeting.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender stereotype"}
A fixed, oversimplified idea about what women or men are like or should do, for example "girls are weak" or "boys do not cry".
:::

:::definition{term="Gender equality"}
Women and men, girls and boys, having the same rights, opportunities and respect, so that being male or female does not decide what a person may do.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender equity"}
Fairness in the way women and men are treated, which may mean treating them differently to make up for earlier disadvantage so that the outcome is equal.
:::

:::definition{term="Patriarchy"}
A system in which men hold most power and authority in the family, community and institutions. Matriarchy would be the reverse.
:::

:::definition{term="Gender-based violence (GBV)"}
Harm done to a person because of their gender or unequal power between genders, including physical, sexual, emotional and economic abuse and harmful practices. Most victims are women and girls, but men and boys can be affected too.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn: **sex** is biological; **gender** is learned. **Equality** = same rights and opportunities; **equity** = fair treatment that may differ to reach equal outcomes. Constitution of Zimbabwe: **section 17** (gender balance), **section 56** (equality and non-discrimination), **section 80** (rights of women).
:::

## Sex and gender compared

| Feature | Sex | Gender |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Biology | Society, culture, religion and upbringing |
| Changes? | Does not change from place to place | Varies between societies and changes over time |
| Example | Only women can give birth; only men can father children | Cooking, ploughing, leading a village meeting, nursing or driving are not limited by sex |

A useful test: if a statement is true for all people everywhere (giving birth), it is about sex; if it varies between cultures or has changed over time (who does the cooking), it is about gender.

## Gender roles in everyday life

Traditionally in many Zimbabwean homes, girls were expected to fetch water, cook, care for young children and clean, while boys were expected to herd, plough and protect. Today, many families share these tasks, and women work as pilots, doctors, farmers and engineers while men work as nurses, teachers and cooks. This shows that many roles are learned, not fixed by nature.

### Stereotypes

Stereotypes limit people. A girl who believes "mathematics is for boys" may avoid the subject; a boy who believes "men do not show feelings" may hide his pain. Stereotypes can also justify unfair treatment, such as paying women less. A good answer gives an example of a stereotype and explains its effect.

## How gender roles are learned (socialisation)

| Agent | How it teaches gender roles | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Family** | Parents give different tasks, toys, clothes and expectations to sons and daughters | A mother asks her daughter, not her son, to wash dishes |
| **Religion** | Teachings, stories, leadership roles and ceremonies may present certain roles for women and men; interpretations differ widely | A congregation where only men lead worship, or one where women and men lead together |
| **School** | Teachers, textbooks, subject choices and games | A class where only girls clean the room and only boys carry desks |
| **Media** | Radio, television, social media and adverts show women and men in certain jobs and appearances | An advert in which only women advertise cleaning products |
| **Peers and community** | Friends tease those who step outside expected roles | A boy teased for joining a dance group |

:::example{title="Socialisation in one household"}
Thandeka and her brother Themba live with their grandmother in a village near Hwange. When they return from school, Thandeka is told to start the cooking while Themba is told to rest or play football. Their grandmother learnt the pattern from her own parents, and neither child has ever questioned it. At school, their teacher gives both children the same duties. Thandeka asks why the rules differ at home. The example shows that gender roles are learned from family and school, can be different in different places, and can be questioned.
:::

## Equality and equity

Equality means treating people the same; equity means being fair about differences in need. Suppose a school has a bursary only for girls because girls in that district have been leaving school early. That treats girls differently (equity) in order to reach equal opportunity (equality). Both ideas aim at justice. In Religious Studies you should explain both and give an example.

## Patriarchy and gender-based violence

Many societies throughout history, including traditional Shona and Ndebele societies and the societies in which the major religions began, have been patriarchal, with men as heads of families. Patriarchy can bring responsibility as well as power, but it can also be used to deny women property, education or voice, and it can contribute to gender-based violence. GBV includes beating, forced marriage, sexual abuse, emotional abuse and denying a person money or food. It is unlawful in Zimbabwe, and anyone affected can seek help from a trusted adult, a clinic, the police Victim Friendly Unit or a support organisation. The later lessons in Topic 8 explain religious responses.

## Gender and the Constitution of Zimbabwe (outline)

| Section | What it says (in outline) |
|---|---|
| 17 | The State must promote full gender balance in society, with women's fair representation in all spheres |
| 56 | Everyone is equal before the law and may not be treated unfairly because of sex or gender, among other grounds |
| 80 | Women have full and equal dignity with men, including equal opportunities in political, economic and social activities; laws that infringe women's rights are void |

The Constitution also protects freedom of religion (section 60), so the law has to respect both equality and belief. When a religious or customary practice conflicts with these rights, the principles of equality and the best interests of the child must be considered.

:::example{title="A bursary for girls"}
In a district near Lupane, the community notices that many more girls than boys leave school at Form 2. A local church and the school development committee start a bursary and a mentoring group for girls. Some parents say it is unfair to boys. The organisers reply that the aim is not to reduce boys' chances but to remove barriers that only girls face. This is a case of gender equity working towards gender equality.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using 'sex' and 'gender' as if they were the same"}
Sex is biological; gender is the set of roles and expectations a society attaches to it. Give the difference and an example.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that gender roles come only from religion"}
Family, school, media, peers and custom all teach gender roles, and religions themselves contain varied teachings. Name several agents.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking gender issues concern only women"}
Men and boys are also affected by stereotypes and can be victims of violence. Equality benefits everyone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing equality with equity"}
Equality is the same rights and chances; equity is fairness that may involve different treatment to reach equal outcomes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): define and explain"}
Define each term in one sentence and add an example. For *explain how gender roles are learned*, name four agents (family, religion, school, media) and give an example of each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess"}
For *how far are gender roles fixed by nature?*, give evidence of roles that differ between societies and change over time, note biological differences, and conclude fairly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sex is biological; gender is the social roles and expectations attached to being female or male.
- Gender roles and stereotypes are learned through family, religion, school, media and peers.
- Equality means the same rights and opportunities; equity means fair treatment that may differ to reach equal outcomes.
- Patriarchy and unequal power can lead to gender-based violence, which is unlawful in Zimbabwe.
- The Constitution of Zimbabwe promotes gender balance (section 17), equality (section 56) and women's rights (section 80).
:::
