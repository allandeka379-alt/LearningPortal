## Introduction

In a village in Chivi, an elderly woman is the host of a spirit possession ceremony. People come to consult the medium, and the whole family listens to what the ancestral spirit says through her. On the same afternoon, in a synagogue in Bulawayo, women light the Shabbat candles at home and prepare for the Sabbath meal, while in the synagogue men and women pray in their own sections. These two scenes come from two different religions. Both show that women and men have roles in religion, and that those roles are understood and applied in different ways.

This lesson describes the roles of men and women in Indigenous Religion (IR) and in Judaism, as adherents understand them. Your task is to report what each religion teaches and does, noting differences within each religion, and to explain the roles with evidence. You should already know the terms from the previous lesson (gender, role, stereotype, equality, patriarchy) and the main ideas of IR (Topic 2) and Judaism (Topic 3).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Svikiro / spirit medium"}
A person, often a woman or a man, through whom an ancestral or territorial spirit is believed to speak. Women have been very important spirit mediums in Shona history.
:::

:::definition{term="Vatete"}
The paternal aunt (father's sister) in Shona families. She holds an important ritual and advisory role in the family, for example in marriage negotiations and in advising the bride.
:::

:::definition{term="Orthodox and Progressive Judaism"}
Orthodox Judaism holds to traditional interpretation of the law (halakhah), including separate roles for men and women in worship. Progressive (Reform and Liberal) Judaism interprets the law more flexibly and gives men and women the same roles in worship. Conservative Judaism lies between them.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn: IR women act as **spirit mediums, healers and vatete (aunts)**; men are usually **family heads (samusha) and chiefs**. Judaism: **Genesis 1:27** (humans made in God's image), women of the Hebrew Bible **Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Ruth, Esther**. Women rabbis: **Reform** and **Conservative**; not in mainstream Orthodox Judaism.
:::

## Gender roles in Indigenous Religion

### Women's religious roles

- **Spirit mediums.** Women have served as svikiro for ancestral and territorial spirits. The most famous is Nehanda (Charwe), a leader in the First Chimurenga, who was believed to be the medium of the Nehanda spirit. Women can also be mediums of family spirits, and they hold the respect of their communities when the spirit speaks through them.
- **Healers.** Both women and men serve as n'anga/inyanga; some are midwives who care for mothers and babies.
- **Aunts (vatete).** The paternal aunt has ritual authority in her brother's family: she takes part in marriage negotiations, advises the bride, and has an important role in death rites and family ceremonies.
- **Mothers and grandmothers** teach religious values, prepare beer and food for ceremonies, and lead singing.
- Women often take part in rain-making ceremonies and in the care of sacred places in ways that vary between peoples.

### Men's religious roles

- **Family heads (samusha).** The father or eldest man leads family ceremonies and speaks to the ancestors on behalf of the family.
- **Chiefs, headmen and elders** (mostly men, though there have been and are women chiefs) lead community ceremonies and settle disputes.
- Many n'anga/inyanga and mediums of territorial spirits (mhondoro) are men.

### Division of labour

In traditional homes women were responsible for farming the household fields, fetching water and firewood, cooking and caring for children, while men were responsible for herding cattle, building, hunting and defence. Men were seen as heads of families, with wives often moving to the husband's home after marriage. Many families today share the work more equally.

### Lobola, inheritance and their effects

**Roora/lobola** (bride wealth) is a gift from the groom's family to the bride's family. Supporters say it is a sign of respect, joins two families and gives the marriage stability. Critics point out that it can be misunderstood as buying a wife and can be used to claim control over her. Traditionally property and inheritance passed through male relatives, and a widow or daughter could lose her home or property to the dead man's relatives. The Constitution (sections 56, 71 and 80) and the law now protect the rights of women to equality, property and inheritance, and customs that infringe women's rights are void. Many elders today support these changes.

:::example{title="A vatete at a marriage negotiation"}
In a family near Masvingo, Rudo's nephew Tafadzwa is to marry. During the lobola negotiations, Rudo, who is Tafadzwa's vatete (his father's sister), is asked to speak. She is consulted on the arrangements and she advises the young woman who will join the family. Her presence shows that, although the family head is usually a man, women with ritual and family authority have a voice in important decisions.
:::

## Gender roles in Judaism

### The creation accounts

Genesis 1:27 teaches that human beings were made in the image of God, "male and female". Many Jews take this to mean that men and women have equal worth before God. Genesis 2 tells of the creation of the first man and of a woman as his partner and helper; Jewish teachers have interpreted this in different ways, some stressing partnership and others differences of role.

### Women of the Hebrew Bible

| Woman | Role |
|---|---|
| Sarah | Wife of Abraham and mother of Isaac; one of the matriarchs (Genesis 12-23) |
| Miriam | Sister of Moses and Aaron, described as a prophet; led the women in song after the crossing of the sea (Exodus 15:20-21) |
| Deborah | A prophet and judge who led Israel (Judges 4-5) |
| Ruth | A Moabite woman who showed loyalty to her mother-in-law; the Book of Ruth is named after her |
| Esther | A queen who saved the Jewish people from danger; remembered at Purim in the Book of Esther |

These women show that women could hold leadership, wisdom and courage in the history of Israel.

### Orthodox practice

In Orthodox Judaism men and women have different religious roles. Men are required to pray at set times, wear the tallit and tefillin, and are counted in the minyan (the quorum of ten needed for public prayer). In the synagogue men and women are seated separately, often with a screen or balcony (mechitza). Women are responsible for many home duties, such as lighting the Shabbat candles and running the Jewish home, which has great importance in the religion, and Jewish status is passed on through the mother. A girl has a bat mitzvah at twelve. Mainstream Orthodox Judaism does not ordain women as rabbis, although some modern Orthodox communities have created other leadership and teaching roles for women. Orthodox Jews describe these roles as different but equally valuable, and many Orthodox women are teachers, scholars and leaders in education and community life.

### Progressive practice

In Reform and other Progressive synagogues, men and women sit together, count equally in the minyan, may read from the Torah and may lead services. Reform Judaism ordained women rabbis from 1972 in the USA, and Conservative Judaism from 1985; today women rabbis serve in these movements in many countries. Some Reform communities also recognise descent through the father if the child is raised as Jewish. These Jews hold that equality of men and women is consistent with the deeper message of the Torah.

:::example{title="Two synagogues on Shabbat"}
Rachel visits her cousin Miriam in another city. In Rachel's Orthodox synagogue, men and women sit in separate sections, a male rabbi leads, and Rachel prepares the Shabbat meal with her mother. In Miriam's Reform synagogue, families sit together and a woman rabbi leads the prayers and reads from the Torah. Both girls say that their way of worship is faithful to Judaism. The example shows that Jews interpret gender roles differently, so an answer should say "Orthodox Jews" or "Reform Jews", not "Judaism says".
:::

## Comparison

| Feature | Indigenous Religion | Judaism |
|---|---|---|
| Women's religious leadership | Spirit mediums, healers, aunts with ritual authority | Prophets and judges in the Hebrew Bible; women rabbis in Reform and Conservative Judaism |
| Men's roles | Family heads, chiefs, many mediums and healers | Required prayers, minyan, rabbis in all movements |
| Home | Women farm and care for children | Women light Shabbat candles and run the home |
| Differences within | Different peoples and families | Orthodox, Conservative, Reform |
| Issues | Lobola, inheritance, women's rights | Equality in worship, ordination, divorce rights |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying 'women have no role in IR'"}
Women are mediums, healers and aunts with real authority. State their roles accurately.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'Judaism says' for a disputed practice"}
Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews differ. Name the group.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting lobola only as buying a wife"}
Describe the meaning given by supporters and the concerns of critics. Note the legal protections.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing roles without evidence"}
Give names (Nehanda, Miriam, Deborah) and references (Genesis 1:27; Exodus 15; Judges 4-5).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In IR women act as mediums, healers and aunts (vatete); men are usually family heads and chiefs; labour was divided between women and men.
- Lobola and inheritance customs have both supporters and critics; the Constitution protects women's equality and property.
- Genesis 1:27 teaches that men and women are made in God's image; Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Ruth and Esther are women of the Hebrew Bible.
- Mainstream Orthodox Judaism has different roles for men and women and does not ordain women rabbis; Reform and Conservative Judaism ordain women.
- Always state which group you are describing.
:::
