## Introduction

A pastor in Gweru is asked by a woman in his congregation to pray for her marriage because her husband beats her. The pastor can answer in one of two ways. He can tell her to be patient and keep silent, or he can tell her that no one should be beaten, help her to find safety and counselling, and speak to the husband. How religious leaders answer questions like this has a great effect on whether religion helps to end violence or lets it continue. Religions have enormous influence on how families live, so they can either promote gender equality and protect the vulnerable, or, through the way teachings and customs are used, hinder it.

This lesson completes Topic 8. It looks at how religions promote or hinder gender equality today, how they respond to gender-based violence (GBV) and harmful practices, the place of women in religious leadership, the roles of men as partners, and the law. The content is sensitive: it is handled factually and with care. If anything in this lesson makes you worry about yourself or someone you know, speak to a trusted adult, a teacher, a clinic, the police Victim Friendly Unit, or Childline Zimbabwe on 116.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gender-based violence (GBV)"}
Harm done to a person because of their gender or unequal power between the genders, including physical, sexual, emotional and economic abuse and harmful practices. It is a violation of human rights.
:::

:::definition{term="Harmful practice"}
A custom or practice that damages the health, dignity or rights of a person, especially women and girls, such as child marriage, forced marriage, pledging, virginity testing and inheritance customs that leave a widow without property.
:::

:::definition{term="Domestic violence"}
Violence or abuse between members of a household or family, including between spouses. The Domestic Violence Act 2007 makes it unlawful in Zimbabwe.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
Women and men working together as equals in the family, the community and the congregation.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn the law in outline: **Constitution** (section 17 gender balance; 56 equality; 78 marriage at 18 and by free consent; 80 rights of women); **Domestic Violence Act 2007**; **Marriages Act 2022** (outline only; raises and unifies the marriage age at 18). Help: a trusted adult, a clinic, the police **Victim Friendly Unit**, **Childline 116**.
:::

## How religions can promote gender equality

- **Teachings on equal worth.** Genesis 1:27 teaches that all people are made in God's image; Galatians 3:28 teaches that all are one in Christ; Islam teaches equality before God (Qur'an 4:1; 33:35); Indigenous Religion teaches respect for mothers, aunts and female elders; Judaism honours women as matriarchs, prophets and heads of the home. These teachings give believers a basis for treating women with dignity.
- **Teachings on respect and love in marriage.** Christians are taught that husbands and wives are to love and respect one another (Ephesians 5); Islam teaches that God placed affection and mercy between spouses (Qur'an 30:21); Jewish teaching speaks of *shalom bayit*, peace in the home; unhu/ubuntu teaches respect for all people.
- **Women's leadership.** Women lead as ministers and pastors in many churches, as rabbis in Reform and Conservative Judaism, as teachers and scholars in Islam, and as spirit mediums, healers and aunts in Indigenous Religion. Women's groups such as Ruwadzano and Manyano give women a voice and organise help for families.
- **Practical help.** Many religious bodies run counselling services, safe houses, legal advice, orphan care and education programmes, and support the bursaries that keep girls in school.

## How religion can hinder equality

Religious teachings or customs, or their misuse, can also hold back equality.

- Some verses or teachings are interpreted in ways that justify male control and silence women, even when other believers reject that reading.
- Some leaders discourage abused women from leaving unsafe marriages, or treat violence as a private family matter, or counsel silence "for the sake of peace".
- Customs such as child marriage, pledging, forced marriage, virginity testing and wife inheritance without consent, and customs that deny women property, are sometimes defended as "tradition" or "religion".
- Women can be excluded from decision-making in some religious bodies.

:::example{title="A pastor's two responses"}
Tendai asks his pastor for advice. His friend Rudo has been beaten by her husband. The first pastor in the story tells Rudo to be patient and pray, and says nothing to the husband; the violence continues. A second pastor, in another church, tells Rudo that no marriage is a reason to accept abuse, helps her to find a safe place and counselling, takes the matter to the local Victim Friendly Unit, and speaks with the husband about love, respect and the law. The example shows how religious leaders can hinder or promote equality depending on what they teach and do. Most religious leaders and organisations in Zimbabwe say publicly that violence against women is wrong.
:::

## Harmful practices

| Practice | Why it is harmful | Position |
|---|---|---|
| Child marriage and pledging (kuzvarira) | The girl is married before she is ready, loses education and health, and is exposed to abuse | Unlawful; the Constitution (section 78) sets 18 as the minimum age, and the Marriages Act 2022 confirms 18 |
| Forced marriage | A person is made to marry against their will | Marriage requires free and full consent (section 78) |
| Virginity testing | It violates privacy and dignity, can stigmatise girls and harm their health, and may expose them to abuse | Harmful and contrary to children's rights and dignity; many religious leaders oppose it |
| Wife inheritance without consent | A widow is passed to a relative of the dead husband against her wishes | Contrary to the right to consent and to dignity; religious leaders and the law support the widow's choice |
| Denial of inheritance and property grabbing | Relatives take the home, land or goods of a widow or orphaned girls | The Constitution protects women's equality and property rights (sections 56, 71 and 80) |

## The law in outline

The **Constitution of Zimbabwe** protects equality (section 56), the rights of women (section 80) and children's rights (section 81), and requires gender balance (section 17). Freedom of religion (section 60) is protected, but it cannot be used to excuse violence or the denial of other people's rights. The **Domestic Violence Act 2007** defines domestic violence widely, including physical, sexual, emotional and economic abuse, and provides for protection orders and for the police to act. The **Marriages Act 2022** (outline only) sets the minimum age for marriage at 18 (this lesson gives only an outline of the Act). Survivors may report to the police Victim Friendly Unit, a clinic, or a trusted adult, and children may call Childline on 116.

## Men as partners and religious responses

Men and boys are essential to ending GBV. Men's fellowships in churches and mosques, community leaders, teachers and fathers can show by their example that respect, listening and sharing household work are a strength and not a weakness. Religious leaders can teach boys and men that real strength means protecting and respecting others, preach against violence, welcome and support survivors, refuse to bless forced or child marriages, and train as counsellors. Interfaith groups in Zimbabwe, including church councils and Muslim organisations, run campaigns to raise awareness during the annual 16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence (25 November to 10 December).

:::example{title="A mosque and a church work together"}
In a town in Marondera, an imam and a church minister agree to hold a joint evening for the community. Aisha, a Muslim nurse, talks about the effects of violence on health. Nyasha, a Christian counsellor, explains where to find help. The imam reads from the Qur'an about kindness between spouses, and the minister preaches on love in marriage. The community leaves with the numbers of the Victim Friendly Unit and Childline. The example shows how religious groups can work together for a shared purpose.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming one religion for GBV"}
GBV occurs in every society and among people of every belief. Religions each contain both resources for dignity and misuse. Avoid ranking or accusing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about women as victims"}
Men and boys can also be victims, and men are essential partners in prevention. Mention both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing harmful practices without saying they are harmful"}
State clearly that child marriage, forced marriage and similar practices are harmful and, where true, unlawful.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to name the law or the help available"}
Mention at least the Constitution and the Domestic Violence Act 2007, and say where help can be found.
:::

Describe religious responses to GBV with four to six developed points: teachings, counselling, campaigns, women's leadership and the law. Use examples from at least two religions where the question allows.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Religions promote equality through teachings on equal worth, respect in marriage, women's leadership and practical help.
- Misuse of teachings and harmful customs can hinder equality and allow GBV to continue.
- Child marriage, pledging, forced marriage, virginity testing and wife inheritance without consent are harmful and, where true, unlawful.
- The law includes the Constitution (sections 17, 56, 78, 80), the Domestic Violence Act 2007 and the Marriages Act 2022.
- Men and religious leaders are essential partners; help is available from a trusted adult, a clinic, the Victim Friendly Unit and Childline (116).
:::
