## Introduction

Imagine that Tendai, a Form 3 pupil, is told on the school bus that "everyone has rights". Her friend Farai asks, "Where do rights come from? From the government, or from God?" The question is old, and the answers differ. A lawyer might point to the Constitution. A Christian might point to Genesis 1:27, a Muslim to Qur'an 17:70, and a Shona or Ndebele elder to unhu/ubuntu, the teaching that a person becomes fully human through other people. This lesson looks at what human rights are, how the law protects them, how the religions support them, and what should happen when religious practice and rights seem to clash.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Human rights"}
The basic freedoms and protections to which every person is entitled because they are human, whatever their sex, race, religion, age or ability.
:::

:::definition{term="Human dignity"}
The worth that every person has simply because they are a person. Many religions teach that this worth comes from God, from the ancestors or from the community of people.
:::

:::definition{term="Freedom of religion"}
The right to hold, change and practise a religion or belief, or none, alone or with others, in worship, teaching and observance. In Zimbabwe it is protected by section 60 of the Constitution (freedom of conscience).
:::

:::keyfact
**Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)**: adopted by the United Nations on **10 December 1948**. **Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013**: Chapter 4 is the Declaration of Rights. Learn: **section 48** (life), **51** (human dignity), **56** (equality and non-discrimination), **60** (freedom of conscience), **81** (children), and **86** (limitation of rights).
:::

## Characteristics of human rights

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Universal | They belong to everyone, everywhere |
| Inalienable | They cannot be taken away, although they can be limited by law in certain cases |
| Equal | Everyone has the same rights, without discrimination |
| Interdependent | Rights depend on one another; for example, the right to education supports the right to work |
| Come with responsibilities | Each person must respect the rights of others |

## The law in outline

The **UDHR (1948)** lists thirty articles, including the right to life, freedom from slavery and torture, equality before the law, freedom of thought, conscience and religion, education and work. It is not a treaty, but it inspired later treaties and many national constitutions. The **Declaration of Rights** in Chapter 4 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe protects the right to life (section 48), human dignity (51), equality (56), freedom of conscience including religion (60), education (75), and the rights of women (80), children (81) and persons with disabilities (83). Under section 86 rights may be limited only by a law that is fair, reasonable and necessary in a democratic society, for example to protect the rights of other people, public order or health.

## Religious foundations of human dignity

- **Indigenous Religion:** *Unhu/ubuntu* teaches that a person is a person through other people (the Ndebele saying is *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*). Respect for elders, hospitality, sharing and care for the weak express this. In this view dignity is lived in community, and harming someone damages the community and the ancestors who guard it.
- **Judaism and Christianity:** Genesis 1:27 teaches that humans are made in the image of God. For this reason many Jews and Christians believe every person has equal worth, and that murder, slavery and cruelty offend God. Christians also point to Jesus' teaching to love one's neighbour (Mark 12:31).
- **Islam:** Qur'an 17:70 teaches that God has honoured the children of Adam. Muslims believe that every human being has dignity, that justice and mercy are duties, and that life is sacred.

:::example{title="One right, three religious reasons"}
A school debate at a fictional school in Gweru asks why everyone should be free to follow their own religion. Nyasha, a Christian, argues from Genesis 1:27 that each person is made in God's image and so deserves respect, even when they believe differently. Aisha, a Muslim, cites the teaching that there is no compulsion in religion (Qur'an 2:256). Sipho explains that in unhu you honour the person in front of you and that hospitality is owed to all. The three arguments differ in their sources, but they reach the same conclusion.
:::

## Freedom of religion and its limits

Section 60 protects the right to hold and practise a religion and to change it. This freedom is not unlimited: it must be exercised with respect for the rights of other people. If a religious practice would seriously harm another person, the law may limit it. This is the principle of section 86.

## Where religion and rights may conflict

These cases should be presented fairly, with the view of the believers as well as the legal and human-rights view.

| Case | Religious or customary view | Rights view |
|---|---|---|
| Child marriage | Some families defend it as tradition or religion | The Constitution (section 78) sets 18 as the minimum age and requires free consent; children have the right to education and protection |
| Denying a child medical care | A few groups believe that healing should come by prayer alone and refuse treatment | Children have the right to health care (section 81); advice is to seek a trained health worker and to seek both prayer and treatment |
| Gender roles in leadership | Some religions reserve certain offices for men | The Constitution protects equality (section 56), but also freedom of religion (section 60); religious bodies and the state must find a balance |
| Harmful practices (pledging, virginity testing) | Defended by some as custom | Contrary to dignity and the rights of the child; unlawful where they cause harm |

In each case the student should report what the believers hold, state what the law says, and explain how the conflict can be addressed: through dialogue, education, reform within the religion, and where necessary through the courts.

:::example{title="A parent and a clinic"}
Thandeka's neighbour believes that prayer is the only way to treat his child's fever and does not take her to the clinic. The elders of his church, and the local pastor, say that prayer is important but that a sick child should also be taken to the clinic. A health worker visits and explains the child's right to health care. The family agrees that the child will receive treatment and prayers. The example shows that dialogue and respect can protect the child's right to health without denying the family's faith.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying religion is opposed to human rights"}
Most religions support human dignity. A balanced answer shows both the support and the cases of conflict.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating freedom of religion as unlimited"}
Freedom of religion is protected but may be limited by a fair law to protect the rights of other people (section 86).
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing UDHR and the Constitution"}
The UDHR is an international declaration of 1948; the Declaration of Rights is Chapter 4 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing rights without explaining"}
When asked to explain, say what the right is, which religion or law supports it, and why it matters.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): explain how religions support human rights"}
Give about seven developed points: the meaning of human rights, unhu, Genesis 1:27, Qur'an 17:70, love of neighbour, the UDHR, and the Constitution. Name at least two religions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For "to what extent should freedom of religion be limited?" give the case for freedom, the case for limits (harm to others, children's rights), the law (sections 60 and 86), and a reasoned conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Human rights are the basic freedoms and protections of every person; they are universal, inalienable, equal and interdependent, and come with responsibilities.
- The UDHR (10 December 1948) and Chapter 4 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe protect rights, including freedom of religion (section 60).
- Religions ground dignity in unhu (Indigenous Religion), the image of God (Genesis 1:27) and the honour God gave humans (Qur'an 17:70).
- Freedom of religion may be limited by a fair law to protect others (section 86).
- Conflicts such as child marriage and denial of medical care are best addressed through dialogue, education and the law.
:::
