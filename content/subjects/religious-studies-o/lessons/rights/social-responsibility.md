## Introduction

After a cyclone or a flood, one of the first sights on the news is often a church hall or a mosque turned into a shelter, with volunteers handing out blankets and maize meal. On an ordinary Saturday, a Jewish family may set aside money for tzedakah, a Muslim family may calculate their zakat, a church member may bring a tithe, and a village may work together in the chief's field to feed orphans. These acts show that religion is not only about worship and belief. It also asks believers to care for their neighbours and nation. This lesson explains what social responsibility is, what the religions teach about it, what religious organisations do, and what good citizenship requires.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Social responsibility"}
The duty of a person or organisation to act in ways that benefit the community and society, not only themselves.
:::

:::definition{term="Good citizenship"}
Behaving as a responsible member of the nation: obeying just laws, paying taxes, respecting the rights of others, protecting public property, taking part in community life and voting where entitled.
:::

:::definition{term="Charity"}
Giving of money, goods or time to help people in need, because of compassion or religious duty.
:::

:::keyfact
Terms to learn: **tzedakah** (Judaism: righteous giving, justice), **zakat** (Islam: the third pillar, usually 2.5% of qualifying savings held for a lunar year), **tithe** (Christianity: a tenth, with offerings), **Zunde raMambo** (Shona) / **Isiphala seNkosi** (Ndebele): a field or granary kept by the chief for the needy.
:::

## What the religions teach about the duty to the community

| Religion | Teaching and practice |
|---|---|
| Judaism | Tzedakah is a duty and not only a favour: the Torah asks people to leave part of the harvest for the poor, the stranger, the widow and the orphan (Leviticus 19:9-10; Deuteronomy 24:19-21). Jews give money, food and time, and many give regularly |
| Christianity | Believers are taught to love their neighbour (Mark 12:31) and to help the hungry and the sick (Matthew 25:35-40, Jesus' teaching on caring for the least). Many give a tithe (a tenth, see Malachi 3:10) and offerings to support the church and the poor |
| Islam | Zakat is one of the Five Pillars and is paid to the poor and other recipients (Qur'an 9:60); Muslims also give voluntary charity (sadaqah). Qur'an 2:177 includes giving to relatives, orphans and the needy among the signs of righteousness |
| Indigenous Religion | The community shares: hospitality to strangers, work parties (nhimbe/ilima), and the Zunde raMambo or Isiphala seNkosi, a communal field or granary whose harvest is stored to help orphans, widows, the old and those hit by drought. Unhu teaches that the community cares for its weak |

## The Zunde raMambo and Isiphala seNkosi

In many rural areas the chief and his council set aside a field, which the community works together. The harvest goes into a granary and is used to feed orphans, the old, people living with disabilities and families hit by drought. The practice shows several values at once: sharing, the responsibility of leaders to care for all, and unity of the community. In recent years it has been revived in some districts to help vulnerable households.

:::example{title="Working the chief's field"}
In a fictional district near Lupane, the headman announces a day for working the chief's field. Households arrive with hoes, and Sipho's mother brings food for the workers. At harvest the grain is stored in the community granary. In a dry season, ten households with orphans receive grain from it. The example shows that Isiphala seNkosi is not charity from one rich person but the shared work of the whole community, and that leaders are expected to guard the weak.
:::

## Welfare work of religious organisations

Religious organisations in Zimbabwe have run many kinds of welfare service. Examples (in general terms) include:

- **Orphanages and children's homes** and programmes for orphans in the community.
- **Homes for the elderly** and care for the sick.
- **Mission hospitals and clinics**, which have provided health care in rural areas for generations.
- **Feeding schemes**, for example school feeding and soup kitchens.
- **Disaster relief**, such as shelter, food and blankets after floods, cyclones and droughts. Church relief bodies, Islamic charities and Jewish and other organisations take part.
- **Education bursaries and schools**, and skills training.
- **Care for people living with HIV and for people with disabilities**, in hospitals, clinics and home-based care.

:::example{title="Churches and a mosque after a storm"}
After a storm in a fictional town in Manicaland, Nyasha's church opens its hall as a shelter. The local mosque, led by Imam Yusuf, collects blankets and cooking oil, and a Catholic women's group cooks meals. Volunteers from all three register families and share news with the council. The example shows religious communities working together and with local government on a shared task.
:::

## Volunteering

Volunteering is giving time and skills without payment. Believers volunteer as teachers in Sunday schools and madrasah, as counsellors, as visitors to the sick, as helpers at feeding programmes and in clean-up campaigns. Volunteering builds skills, gives a sense of purpose, and strengthens bonds between people of different backgrounds.

## Duties of a good citizen

Religion and the law both ask for good citizenship.

- **Obey just laws.** Christianity teaches respect for governing authorities (Romans 13:1-7); in Judaism there is a principle that the law of the land is binding in civil matters; Muslims are taught to keep agreements and to uphold justice. Believers may also work to change unjust laws by peaceful means.
- **Pay taxes honestly.** Taxes pay for schools, clinics and roads. Romans 13:6-7 speaks of paying what is owed.
- **Protect public property** such as roads, water points, school buildings and clinics, and the environment.
- **Take part** in community meetings and elections and respect the rights of others.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing zakat, tzedakah and tithes"}
Zakat is a compulsory pillar for eligible Muslims, usually 2.5% of qualifying savings held for a lunar year. Tzedakah is Jewish righteous giving. A tithe is a Christian offering of a tenth. Do not use the words as if they mean exactly the same thing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about charity as if it were only money"}
Time, skills, hospitality and shared labour are also social responsibility.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Zunde raMambo as a private gift of the chief"}
It is a communal field, worked by the community under the chief's leadership, for the benefit of vulnerable households.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that believers must obey every law"}
Religions teach respect for just laws, and believers may protest peacefully against laws they consider unjust.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain social responsibility"}
Give about seven developed points: the meaning, teachings from at least two religions (tzedakah, zakat, tithes, Zunde raMambo), the welfare work of religious bodies, volunteering, and the duties of citizens.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Social responsibility is the duty to act for the good of the community and nation; good citizens obey just laws, pay taxes and protect public property.
- Religions teach giving: tzedakah, zakat, tithes and offerings, and sharing in Indigenous Religion.
- The Zunde raMambo/Isiphala seNkosi is a communal field or granary that supports orphans and the vulnerable.
- Religious organisations run orphanages, homes for the elderly, clinics, feeding schemes and disaster relief.
- Volunteering and good citizenship express religious values.
:::
