## Introduction

At the start of every year, children across Zimbabwe walk to school in their uniforms. Some carry a lunch, some carry a younger brother or sister, and some have to walk a long way. Each of them has rights: to be cared for, to learn, to be safe from harm. Each also has responsibilities: to respect their parents, to work at their lessons and to take part in family and community life. Religions and the law both speak about children. This lesson explains children's rights and responsibilities, what religions teach about children and parents, how children are protected from abuse, and where help can be found.

This topic includes sensitive matters such as abuse. If anything here worries you about yourself or a friend, speak to a trusted adult, a teacher, a clinic, the police Victim Friendly Unit, or call Childline Zimbabwe on **116**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Child"}
In the Constitution of Zimbabwe, a child is a person under the age of 18 years.
:::

:::definition{term="Children's rights"}
The protections and freedoms that every child has because of the child's age and vulnerability, set out in the Constitution (section 81) and in international agreements.
:::

:::definition{term="Child abuse"}
Harm done to a child by an adult or another person, including physical, emotional and sexual abuse, neglect and exploitation (for example child labour).
:::

:::definition{term="Best interests of the child"}
The principle that in every matter concerning a child, the child's welfare is the first consideration (Constitution, section 81(2)).
:::

:::keyfact
Learn the instruments: **Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013, section 81** (rights of children, best interests paramount); the **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989)**; the **African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child (1990)**. Help: **Childline Zimbabwe 116**, police **Victim Friendly Unit**, a clinic, a trusted adult.
:::

## Children's rights

The UN Convention is often summarised under four headings, and section 81 of the Constitution and the African Charter fit them well.

| Group of rights | Examples |
|---|---|
| Survival | Life, health care, nutrition, shelter, a name and nationality |
| Development | Education, play and rest, freedom of thought, conscience and religion in a way that suits the child's age |
| Protection | Protection from abuse, neglect, exploitation, harmful labour, harmful customs such as child marriage and pledging |
| Participation | The right to express views and to be heard in matters that affect the child |

Section 81 says that every child has the right to family or parental care, to education, to health care and to protection from maltreatment, neglect, abuse and exploitation. It also says that the best interests of the child are paramount. The minimum age of marriage is 18 (section 78). The African Charter is unusual in that it also lists the duties of the child.

## Children's responsibilities

Rights come with responsibilities, which grow as a child grows up.

- **To the family:** to respect and obey parents and elders, to help with household tasks, and to care for parents in old age.
- **To school:** to attend, to be punctual, to respect teachers, to do homework, and to obey school rules.
- **To the community:** to respect others' property, to help neighbours, to protect the environment and to take part in community life.
- **To religion:** to learn and respect one's religion and to respect the beliefs of others.

## Religious teachings about children

| Religion | Teaching |
|---|---|
| Judaism | Exodus 20:12 commands honour for father and mother; parents are to teach their children the Torah and the commandments (Deuteronomy 6:7) |
| Christianity | Ephesians 6:1-4 asks children to obey their parents and fathers not to provoke their children but to bring them up with care; Jesus welcomed children (Mark 10:13-16) |
| Islam | Qur'an 17:23-24 commands kindness and respect to parents, especially in old age; parents must care for and educate their children |
| Indigenous Religion | Children are a gift and the future of the lineage; they are taught respect for elders, the totem and the ancestors, and the whole family and community help to raise a child |

Notice that the teachings speak to both sides: children respect parents, and parents have a duty of care. A balanced answer mentions both.

:::example{title="Respect and care in one family"}
Tatenda's grandmother tells him each morning to greet the elders and help to fetch water. His father also makes sure that he goes to school and has food and medicine when ill. The family follows Indigenous Religion and also attends a church on Sundays. The example shows the shared responsibilities: Tatenda respects and helps the family, and the family cares for his health and education.
:::

## Child abuse and protection

Child abuse takes several forms.

| Form | Examples |
|---|---|
| Physical | Hitting or hurting a child |
| Emotional | Insults, threats, constant humiliation |
| Sexual | Any sexual contact or exploitation of a child; the law protects children under 18 |
| Neglect | Failing to provide food, shelter, health care or schooling |
| Exploitation | Child labour, child marriage, pledging (kuzvarira), trafficking |

Religions teach that abuse is wrong. Religious leaders can protect children by teaching that violence against children is wrong, by training volunteers, by refusing to bless child marriages and by telling children and parents where to find help. Children and adults who are worried can speak to a trusted adult, a teacher, a clinic, the police **Victim Friendly Unit** or **Childline Zimbabwe on 116**.

:::example{title="Rudo asks for help"}
Rudo hears a friend say that she is afraid to go home because an adult hurts her. Rudo does not keep the secret or laugh it off. She tells her friend that she is not to blame, takes her to see their teacher, and the teacher contacts the Victim Friendly Unit and Childline. The friend receives protection and counselling. The example shows that the right response to abuse is to listen, to believe, and to tell a trusted adult.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing rights without responsibilities"}
Questions often ask for both. Give rights and responsibilities, and show how they fit together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting teachings that speak only to children"}
Exodus 20:12 and Ephesians 6:1 speak to children, but Ephesians 6:4 and Deuteronomy 6:7 speak to parents. Include both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating harmful customs as protected by religion"}
Child marriage and pledging are harmful and unlawful. Freedom of religion does not remove a child's rights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting where help is available"}
When asked about child protection, name Childline (116), the police Victim Friendly Unit, a clinic and a trusted adult.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): explain rights, responsibilities and teachings"}
Give about seven developed points: rights from section 81 and the UN Convention, children's responsibilities, teachings from at least two religions (for example Exodus 20:12; Ephesians 6:1-4; Qur'an 17:23-24), and child protection.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For "how far do religious teachings protect children?" give teachings that protect (care, education, respect) and cases where customs harm children (child marriage, abuse), the law and a reasoned conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A child is a person under 18; section 81 gives children rights to care, education, health care and protection, and the child's best interests are paramount.
- The UN Convention (1989) and the African Charter (1990) group rights as survival, development, protection and participation; the Charter also lists duties.
- Children's responsibilities are to the family, school, community and religion.
- Religions teach respect for parents (Exodus 20:12; Ephesians 6:1-4; Qur'an 17:23-24; Indigenous Religion) and the duty of parents to care for children.
- Child abuse is wrong; help is available from a trusted adult, a clinic, the Victim Friendly Unit and Childline (116).
:::
