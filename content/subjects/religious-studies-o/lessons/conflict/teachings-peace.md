## Introduction

When Muslims greet each other they say *as-salaamu alaykum*, "peace be upon you". Jews say *shalom*. In Shona-speaking communities people ask about *rugare* (peace and well-being), and in Ndebele-speaking ones about *ukuthula*. In many Christian services the congregation shares "the peace", turning to greet one another. Peace is on the lips of believers every day. Yet the world also has wars, and believers sometimes disagree about what their religion permits when faced with violence. This lesson describes what the four religions teach about peace and conflict, in their own terms and with equal respect, and how these teachings guide believers today.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Peace"}
More than the absence of war: a state of harmony, justice, safety and well-being in which relationships between people are right. The religions use words such as shalom, salaam, rugare and ukuthula to describe this fuller peace.
:::

:::definition{term="Pacifism"}
The belief that violence and war are always wrong and that conflicts should be resolved without them. Some Christian groups, such as the Quakers and Mennonites, are pacifist.
:::

:::definition{term="Just war"}
A Christian tradition, developed by thinkers such as Augustine and Thomas Aquinas, that sets conditions under which war may be justified: a just cause, a legitimate authority, a last resort, a right intention, proportionate means and protection of civilians.
:::

:::definition{term="Sulh"}
Arabic word for reconciliation or settlement between disputing parties, encouraged in Islam.
:::

:::keyfact
Learn the references: **Isaiah 2:4** and **Micah 4:3** (swords into ploughshares); **Matthew 5:9** (blessed are the peacemakers) and **5:38-48** (love of enemies); **Matthew 18:21-22** (forgiveness); **Qur'an 49:9-10** (make peace between believers).
:::

## Indigenous Religion

In Indigenous Religion peace means harmony between the living, the ancestors and the land. Unhu/ubuntu teaches respect, hospitality and the sharing of problems with others. Elders and chiefs are expected to settle disputes in the *dare* (Shona) or *inkundla* (Ndebele), through listening and compensation, so that relationships are restored. Quarrels within a family may be taken to the aunts and uncles. Offences are thought to disturb the spiritual order, so ceremonies of cleansing and reconciliation restore peace between people and with the ancestors.

## Judaism

Peace (*shalom*) means wholeness, safety and right relationships. The prophets looked forward to a time when peoples would "beat their swords into ploughshares" (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3), so that nations would no longer learn war. Psalm 34:14 urges people to seek peace and pursue it, and Proverbs 15:1 teaches that a gentle answer turns away anger. Jewish teaching values *shalom bayit*, peace in the home, and the duty to make peace between neighbours. Judaism also recognises a right to defend life, and Jews hold different views about the use of force.

## Christianity

Jesus taught, "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9), and in the Sermon on the Mount he told his followers to love their enemies and to answer harm without revenge (Matthew 5:38-48). Peter was told to forgive again and again (Matthew 18:21-22), and Paul urged believers to live at peace with everyone as far as possible (Romans 12:18). Christians interpret these teachings in different ways.

- **Pacifists** hold that Christians must never use violence and should resolve conflict by non-violent means.
- **Just war** supporters hold that, as a last resort, force may be used to defend the innocent, provided strict conditions are met.
- Most Christians agree that war is a terrible evil and that peace-making and forgiveness are central duties.

## Islam

*Islam* and *salaam* come from the same Arabic root, which has to do with peace and submission to God. *As-Salam* ("the Source of Peace") is one of the names of God, and Muslims greet one another with *salaam*. Qur'an 49:9-10 teaches that if two groups of believers fight, others should make peace between them, and that believers are brothers. *Sulh* (reconciliation) is encouraged in disputes in families and communities. Qur'an 2:256 teaches that there is no compulsion in religion.

*Jihad* means "striving". For Muslims it includes the inner struggle to live well and the struggle for justice in daily life. Islamic teaching also permits armed defence against aggression under strict conditions and rules, which protect civilians, and the great majority of Muslims and scholars reject extremism and the killing of innocent people.

:::example{title="A dispute at a boundary"}
Two families in a fictional village near Mutare quarrel about the boundary between their fields. Tafadzwa's family follows Indigenous Religion, and Ibrahim's family is Muslim. The headman invites both to the dare. Ibrahim's father recalls the Islamic encouragement of sulh, the village elders speak of restoring good relations with neighbours and ancestors, and a Christian neighbour reminds both of Matthew 5:9. The families agree on a boundary marked with stones, and share a meal. The example shows that teachings from different religions can reinforce the same practical step: talk, make peace and restore the relationship.
:::

## How the teachings guide believers today

Believers apply these teachings by forgiving wrongs, speaking gently, refusing revenge, mediating disputes, joining interfaith groups, supporting reconciliation after conflict and campaigning for justice by peaceful means. Where believers disagree, for example on whether force can be justified, a student should report the range of views without ranking them.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing that Islam, or any religion, is violent or peaceful by nature"}
Report what each religion teaches and how believers interpret it. Avoid stereotypes, and remember that extremists do not speak for the great majority of believers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Translating jihad only as holy war"}
Jihad means striving. Armed defence is permitted only under strict conditions in Islamic teaching.
:::

:::mistake{title="Missing the difference between pacifism and just war"}
Pacifists reject violence; the just war tradition permits force under strict conditions. Both are Christian views.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting without explaining"}
When you give a reference, say what it teaches and how it guides believers today.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe teachings on peace"}
Give teachings from at least two religions with references (for example Isaiah 2:4; Matthew 5:9; Qur'an 49:9-10; unhu), one explanation of each, and an example of practice.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): assess or discuss"}
For "religious teachings on peace are enough to end conflict", argue that the teachings are powerful (they shape values) but not enough alone (believers disagree, and conflict has political and economic causes). End with a reasoned judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The religions share a rich vocabulary of peace: shalom, salaam, rugare/ukuthula, and "the peace" in Christian worship.
- Indigenous Religion links peace with harmony with the ancestors, unhu and the role of elders.
- Judaism: swords into ploughshares (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3). Christianity: peacemakers (Matthew 5:9), love of enemies (Matthew 5:38-48), forgiveness (Matthew 18:21-22), with pacifist and just war views.
- Islam: salaam, sulh, Qur'an 49:9-10; jihad means striving, and armed defence is limited by strict conditions.
- Teachings guide believers to forgive, mediate and seek justice by peaceful means.
:::
