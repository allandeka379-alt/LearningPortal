## Introduction

Chipo and her cousin have not spoken for a month. They quarrelled when one of them borrowed a phone and returned it with a cracked screen. Their grandmother notices, calls them to the kitchen, asks each to speak without interruption, and then asks, "What would put this right?" By the end of the evening the cousins are laughing. Without knowing it, the grandmother has used several of the methods of conflict resolution that this lesson explains. Some methods are modern and some are traditional; some are used by the law, and some by religious communities. All of them aim to turn a conflict into a solution that people can accept.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conflict management"}
Keeping a conflict from becoming violent or from getting worse, for example by calming the people involved and setting rules for the discussion.
:::

:::definition{term="Conflict resolution"}
Reaching a lasting solution to the problem that caused the conflict, so that the parties accept the outcome.
:::

:::definition{term="Mediation"}
Help from a neutral third person (the mediator) who helps the parties to talk and to find their own agreement. The mediator does not impose a decision.
:::

:::definition{term="Arbitration"}
A neutral person or panel (the arbitrator) hears both sides and makes a decision that the parties agree in advance to accept.
:::

:::keyfact
Methods to learn: **dialogue**, **negotiation**, **mediation**, **arbitration**, **reconciliation**, **forgiveness**. Traditional methods: the **dare** (Shona) / **inkundla** (Ndebele), chiefs' courts, elders, aunts and uncles, compensation and cleansing. The Constitution, section 282, lists resolving disputes among the functions of chiefs.
:::

## The main methods

| Method | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue | Open conversation in which each side speaks and listens | Two neighbours sit down to talk about a fence |
| Negotiation | The parties bargain directly to reach a compromise | A school and parents agree on dates for religious holidays |
| Mediation | A neutral person helps them find an agreement | An elder helps two brothers to divide an inheritance |
| Arbitration | A neutral person decides and the parties accept | Both families agree that a respected elder's ruling on a boundary is final |
| Reconciliation | Restoring relationships after the dispute is settled | A shared meal or ceremony for two families |
| Forgiveness | Letting go of anger and the wish for revenge | A student forgives a classmate who apologises |

Between them these methods move from the least formal (talking) to the most formal (court judgement). The ideal is to try the gentler methods first.

## Traditional methods in Zimbabwe

Traditional communities had well-developed ways of handling conflict.

- **The family.** Aunts (*vatete*) and uncles (*sekuru*, maternal uncles) are respected mediators in family disputes, especially over marriage, because they are neutral among the families and are trusted by both sides.
- **The headman and the chief.** The *dare* (Shona) or *inkundla* (Ndebele) is a gathering at which elders hear both sides openly, question witnesses and propose a solution. The aim is to restore harmony and not just to decide who is right. Under the Constitution (section 282) one of the functions of chiefs is to resolve disputes among people in their communities, and chiefs' and village courts can hear certain civil cases under the law.
- **Compensation.** If a person has caused harm, the family may be asked to compensate the family of the injured person, often with cattle or other goods. This recognises the loss and restores the balance between the families. Customs that gave a girl to settle a death or a debt (pledging) were harmful and are unlawful today.
- **Cleansing and appeasement.** In Indigenous Religion a killing or serious wrong is believed to disturb the spiritual order. If a person is believed to have died unjustly, the spirit (*ngozi*) may be thought to seek redress until the family makes amends. Ceremonies of cleansing and appeasement, together with compensation, are performed to put relationships right with the living and the dead.

:::example{title="A dispute taken to the dare"}
A boundary dispute between two families in a fictional village near Gwanda is brought to the inkundla. Each family speaks, witnesses are asked about the old marker stones, and an elder reminds everyone that the neighbours will live side by side long after the dispute is forgotten. The headman proposes that the boundary follow the old stones, and the families agree. They end with a shared meal. The example shows how the traditional method seeks a settlement and the restoration of relationships.
:::

## Religious institutions as peacemakers

- **Churches.** Matthew 18:15-17 sets out steps for settling a quarrel between believers: speak to the person privately, then take one or two others, and only then bring the matter to the congregation. Churches counsel couples, run mediation committees and hold reconciliation services.
- **Mosques.** An imam and other respected Muslims often act as mediators in family and community disputes, encouraging *sulh* (reconciliation).
- **Judaism.** Jewish communities have traditionally had a religious court (*beth din*) that arbitrates disputes between members.
- **Interfaith councils.** Leaders of different religions meet to prevent and resolve tension in towns, schools and the nation.

## The courts and the Constitution (outline)

If other methods fail, a dispute may be taken to a court. The courts decide according to the law, and the Constitution protects the rights of everyone before the law (section 56) and the right to a fair hearing (section 69). Courts are usually a last resort because they are slower, more expensive and more likely to leave the parties as winner and loser. Modern law also encourages mediation and other methods before or during a case.

## Skills for resolving conflict at school and at home

- **Listen** carefully and do not interrupt.
- **Speak calmly** and use "I" statements ("I felt hurt when ...") rather than blame.
- **Separate the problem from the person.**
- **Look for common interests** and for solutions that both sides can accept (a "win-win" outcome).
- **Apologise and forgive** when appropriate.
- **Ask for help** from a teacher, an elder or a counsellor when the conflict is serious.

:::example{title="A conflict at school"}
Nokuthula and Farai both want to use the school computer at lunch time for a project. They begin arguing. A prefect asks each to explain her reason, and they agree to take turns. The teacher thanks them and adds a second timetable. The example shows listening, negotiation, a compromise and the benefit of calm, early action.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing mediation and arbitration"}
A mediator helps the parties to agree; an arbitrator decides and the parties accept the decision.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting the courts as the only way"}
Many conflicts are best settled by dialogue, mediators or elders. Describe the full range of methods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing traditional methods as backward"}
Traditional methods are respected for restoring relationships. Mention harmful practices (such as pledging a girl) only to say that they are harmful and unlawful.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the religious dimension"}
Name at least one religious method or institution, such as Matthew 18:15-17, an imam's mediation or an interfaith council.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): explain methods"}
Give about seven developed points: dialogue, negotiation, mediation and arbitration (with the difference), traditional methods (dare/inkundla, aunts and uncles, compensation, cleansing), religious institutions, and the courts.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For "traditional methods are better than the courts", give the strengths of each (restoring relationships, speed and cost; fairness, written law and protection of rights), their limits, and a conclusion such as "they work best together".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conflict management keeps conflict from worsening; conflict resolution reaches a lasting solution.
- Methods include dialogue, negotiation, mediation, arbitration, reconciliation and forgiveness.
- Traditional methods include the dare/inkundla, chiefs' courts, elders, aunts and uncles, compensation and cleansing rituals.
- Churches (Matthew 18:15-17), mosques, Jewish courts and interfaith councils act as peacemakers; courts are a last resort.
- Skills such as listening, calm speech, compromise and forgiveness help to resolve conflict at school and at home.
:::
