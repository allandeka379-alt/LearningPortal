## Introduction

Two brothers in a village near Chinhoyi quarrel over who should inherit their late father's field. Two neighbours in Mutare argue about a boundary fence. Two pupils fall out because of a rumour. A government in one country and a government in another disagree over a river. All of these are conflicts, at very different scales. Conflict is part of human life: wherever people live together, their needs, interests and beliefs sometimes collide. What matters is how conflict is understood and handled. This lesson is about what conflict is, its types, its causes and its effects. The next lessons look at religious teachings on peace and at methods of resolving conflict.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conflict"}
A disagreement or struggle between people or groups whose needs, interests, beliefs or values seem to be in opposition. Conflict may be spoken, hidden or violent.
:::

:::definition{term="Violence"}
The use of physical force or threat to harm people or property. Not every conflict is violent, and conflict can be managed without violence.
:::

:::definition{term="Injustice"}
Unfair treatment, for example when a person or group is denied a right, a resource or an opportunity that others receive.
:::

:::keyfact
Types of conflict to learn: **intrapersonal** (within a person), **interpersonal** (between individuals), **family**, **community**, **religious**, **ethnic**, **political** and **international**. Religious conflict in particular is treated in the lesson on religion as a source of conflict and of peace-building.
:::

## Types of conflict

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Intrapersonal | A struggle within one person | A student torn between joining friends and doing homework |
| Interpersonal | Between two or more individuals | Two friends who fall out over a rumour |
| Family | Between members of a family | Brothers who disagree over an inheritance, or a couple who disagree about money |
| Community | Between groups in a village, school or town | Neighbouring villages that dispute grazing land or a water point |
| Religious | Between religious groups or within a religion | Disagreement within a congregation about leadership or worship |
| Ethnic | Between ethnic or language groups | A dispute over political representation between groups |
| Political | Over power, policies or elections | Rival parties that do not accept an election result |
| International | Between countries | A dispute between neighbouring countries over borders or a river |

## Causes of conflict

1. **Competition for resources.** When land, water, grazing, jobs or money are scarce, people compete. Disputes over a water point or boundary are examples.
2. **Struggles for power and leadership.** Disputes over who should be chief, headman, leader of a church or chairperson of a school committee.
3. **Differences in beliefs and values.** People may disagree about religion, customs, how to bring up children or what is right and wrong.
4. **Misunderstanding and poor communication.** Rumours, gossip and failure to listen create or deepen conflicts.
5. **Injustice and unfair treatment.** Favouritism, discrimination, corruption and denial of rights provoke resentment and sometimes resistance.
6. **Inheritance disputes.** When a family member dies, relatives may disagree about who should receive land, cattle, a house or other property, and widows and orphans can be treated unfairly.

:::example{title="Abram and Lot in Genesis 13"}
In Genesis 13:1-12 the herders of Abram and of his nephew Lot quarrel because the land cannot support the flocks of both. Abram, whom Jews, Christians and Muslims honour as Abraham, proposes that they separate: Lot may choose the part of the land he prefers, and Abram will take another. The story shows a conflict over resources (grazing land), and it also shows a peaceful way of settling it: speaking openly, being generous and finding a solution that both can accept. The example is a Biblical account and is read by Jews and Christians; Muslims also honour Abraham as a prophet.
:::

## Effects of conflict

| Destructive effects | Constructive effects |
|---|---|
| Hurt, fear and loss of life or property | Problems are brought into the open and can be solved |
| Broken relationships and distrust | Relationships may become stronger when conflict is handled well |
| Families and communities divided | People learn to listen, to negotiate and to forgive |
| Poverty, displacement and school drop-out | Unjust systems may be changed (for example laws that denied rights) |
| Hunger and disease when war disrupts farming and health care | New understanding of others' views |

Conflict can therefore be both destructive and an opportunity for change. The result depends on how people respond: with violence and insults, or with listening and fair solutions.

:::example{title="An inheritance dispute in a fictional family"}
After the death of their father, Chipo and her brothers argue over his field and cattle. Chipo's uncles tell the widow that she must leave the homestead. The family elders call a meeting, and an aunt reminds everyone that the widow and the orphans have rights under the law. After a careful discussion the family agrees on a written plan: the widow remains in the home, each child's share is recorded, and the cattle are divided fairly. The example shows a conflict caused by property and by injustice towards the vulnerable, resolved by talking, by respect for rights and by the elders' guidance.
:::

## Seeing the causes together

Real conflicts rarely have a single cause. A quarrel over a field may begin as a dispute about inheritance, grow because of rumours and misunderstanding, and become bitter because one relative believes that the widow and orphans have been treated unfairly. When you analyse a conflict, ask four questions: who is involved, what do they want, what do they fear, and what is the underlying cause? Looking beneath the first argument often shows a deeper need, such as security, respect or fairness, and that is the need a lasting solution must meet.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating conflict with violence"}
Conflict is a disagreement; violence is one way (a harmful way) of responding. Many conflicts are settled without violence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about war"}
Conflict also occurs within a person, a family, a school and a community. Give examples at several levels.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that conflict is always bad"}
Handled well, conflict can bring change and understanding. A good answer gives both destructive and constructive effects.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining"}
Do not stop at "land" or "power". Say how competition for land or power leads to conflict.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe types and causes"}
Define conflict and give about six developed points: types with examples, causes (resources, power, beliefs, misunderstanding, injustice, inheritance), and effects. Use a local example for each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For "conflict is always destructive", give destructive effects, constructive effects and a judgement that the outcome depends on how conflict is handled.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conflict is a disagreement or struggle between people or groups with opposing needs, interests, beliefs or values.
- Types include intrapersonal, interpersonal, family, community, religious, ethnic, political and international conflict.
- Causes include scarce resources, struggles for power, differences in beliefs and values, misunderstanding, injustice and inheritance disputes.
- Conflict can be destructive (hurt, division, poverty) or an opportunity for change.
- Genesis 13:1-12 gives an example of a conflict over grazing land settled by generosity and agreement.
:::
