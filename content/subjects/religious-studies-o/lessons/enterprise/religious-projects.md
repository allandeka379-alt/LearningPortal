## Introduction

Travel through Zimbabwe and you will see signs of religious organisations at work beyond worship: a mission school with its fields and workshops, a church-run clinic, a mosque that supports a small bakery, a congregation with a poultry project to pay school fees for orphans, a women's group that meets every month to save money in a round. These are religious organisations in enterprise. They show that faith and work can be linked, and they raise questions about money and trust.

In this lesson you learn what enterprise projects religious organisations run, how they serve members and communities, how believers act as entrepreneurs, and what problems can arise. You should already know the religious teachings on work, wealth and giving from the previous lesson, which are not repeated here. The lesson describes projects in general and does not name or judge any particular real organisation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Enterprise"}
An activity that produces goods or services to meet needs and earn an income, often involving risk and planning.
:::

:::definition{term="Income-generating project"}
A project, such as a farm or a shop, run to earn money for a purpose, for example to pay for the work of a church or the fees of orphans.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-reliance"}
Meeting one's own needs through one's own work and resources instead of depending wholly on others.
:::

:::definition{term="Mukando (round)"}
A savings club in which members contribute regularly and each member receives the pooled money in turn, or can borrow from it. It is known as "mukando" in Shona and as a "round" in English.
:::

:::keyfact
**Types of project:** farms and gardens, schools, hospitals and clinics, publishing, transport, microfinance, skills centres, savings and burial societies, welfare funds. **Examples in scripture:** Proverbs 31:16 and 31:24 (a woman who buys a field and sells goods); Acts 18:3 (Paul worked as a tentmaker). **Muslim tradition:** the Prophet Muhammad worked in trade, and Khadijah was a businesswoman.
:::

## What projects religious organisations run

| Type of project | Examples | Who it serves |
|---|---|---|
| Farming and gardens | Church or mission farms, gardens, poultry or fish projects | Members, schools and clinics; surplus sold for income |
| Education | Mission schools, colleges, early childhood centres | Pupils and communities |
| Health | Mission hospitals and clinics | Patients of all faiths |
| Publishing and media | Printing of books, church magazines, radio programmes | Members and the public |
| Transport | Buses or vehicles to carry members and goods | Members, and clients who pay a fee |
| Skills and training | Sewing, carpentry, agriculture and computer training centres | Young people, women, those out of work |
| Savings and welfare | Savings clubs (mukando), burial societies, welfare funds, Islamic endowments for charity | Members in need of loans, funeral costs or support |
| Microfinance | Small loans to small businesses | Women, farmers, small traders |

## How the projects serve members and communities

Religious projects meet needs that governments and businesses sometimes do not meet, especially in rural areas. A mission **school** or **clinic** gives access to education and health care. A **farm** supplies food and gives work to the unemployed. A **skills centre** trains young people to earn a living. A **savings club** or **burial society** helps members to cover emergencies such as a funeral without falling into debt. Income from the projects can pay for the work of the church, mosque or synagogue: school fees for orphans, support for the elderly, repairs to buildings.

In **Islam**, an endowment (*waqf*) is a property whose income is set aside permanently for charity, and zakat committees can support the poor and small enterprises. In **Judaism**, community funds give interest-free loans to people in need. In **Indigenous Religion**, communal projects such as the chief's field (zunde ramambo) and shared work (nhimbe/ilima) express the same idea of enterprise serving the community. In **Christianity**, churches have long run mission stations with a school, a clinic and a farm. These projects put into practice teachings about service, justice and care for the needy.

## Believers as entrepreneurs

Scripture and tradition contain examples of honest enterprise. Proverbs 31 praises a capable woman who considers a field and buys it and who makes garments and sells them (Proverbs 31:16, 31:24). The apostle Paul supported himself by making tents (Acts 18:3). Muslims remember that the Prophet Muhammad worked in the caravan trade and that Khadijah was a respected businesswoman. In Zimbabwe, believers start tuck shops, farming ventures, bakeries and sewing businesses, often with training from a church or mosque, and with a commitment to honest dealing. Entrepreneurship builds **self-reliance**: it reduces dependence on handouts and gives dignity.

## Problems and challenges

Religious enterprise can also face difficulties:

- **Mismanagement**: lack of record-keeping, poor planning or misuse of funds can cause projects to fail.
- **Exploitation of followers**: members may be asked to work without pay or to give more than they can afford, or may be pressured to buy goods from the organisation.
- **Commercialisation of religion**: the sale of religious items, services or "miracles" at high prices turns faith into business, and critics say this exploits the poor.
- **Lack of accountability**: if leaders control all money and no one checks, funds can be misused.
- **Conflict of interest**: leaders may favour relatives or friends in jobs and contracts.
- **Competition and neglect**: a congregation busy with business may neglect its spiritual purpose.

Good practice includes honest records, audits, committees that include members, openness about how money is used, fair pay, respect for the law and a clear purpose that serves the community.

:::example{title="A church poultry project"}
A congregation in Gweru starts a poultry project. Members buy chicks, the youth group feeds and cleans the runs, and the church sells eggs and birds at the market. A treasurer keeps records and the church council checks them each month. The profit pays school fees for orphans and is reported at the annual meeting. The project provides food, work and income, and its openness protects against misuse.
:::

:::example{title="A mosque savings scheme"}
Fatima belongs to a women's savings group linked to her mosque in Harare. Each member contributes a fixed amount every month, and one member receives the full amount in turn to buy materials for her small business. The group also keeps a small fund for a member who faces a funeral or illness. The scheme encourages thrift and mutual help.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing only about churches"}
Enterprise is also run by mosques, synagogues and Indigenous Religion communities (zunde ramambo, nhimbe). Refer to more than one religion when the question asks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming real organisations and accusing them"}
Describe projects and problems in general. Do not accuse a named organisation or leader of wrongdoing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing projects without saying how they serve people"}
For each project say who is served and how, for example that a clinic gives health care to rural patients.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *describe the enterprise projects of religious organisations*, give at least four types, say who each serves, and link at least one to a religious teaching on service or care.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *assess the benefits and problems of religious enterprise*, give benefits (services, jobs, funds), problems (mismanagement, exploitation, commercialisation), and reach a supported conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Religious organisations run farms, schools, clinics, publishing, transport, skills centres, savings schemes and welfare funds.
- These projects serve members and communities and fund the work of the organisation.
- Savings clubs, burial societies and endowments (*waqf*) help members to cope with emergencies and to start businesses.
- Believers can be entrepreneurs, following examples such as Proverbs 31, Paul the tentmaker and Khadijah.
- Problems include mismanagement, exploitation of followers and commercialisation; accountability and honesty reduce them.
:::
