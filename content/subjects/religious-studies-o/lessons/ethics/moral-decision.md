## Introduction

A learner finds a purse on the way home from school. A shopkeeper is asked by an official to pay a small amount "for the paperwork". A group chat fills with a rumour about a classmate. Each situation asks the same question: what is the right thing to do, and how do I decide? People do not decide in the same way. Some ask what their scripture says, some what their elders expect, some what their conscience tells them, and some what will lead to the best result for everyone.

In this lesson you learn the main ways in which people make moral decisions, and what the four religions in your syllabus teach about several contemporary issues: corruption, honesty and theft, gambling and get-rich-quick schemes, violence and bullying, and social media. You should already know the meaning of morality and of unhu/ubuntu. Issues of health, sexuality and life are covered in other lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conscience"}
A person's inner sense of right and wrong, which guides decisions. Many religions teach that conscience should be informed by teaching and reflection.
:::

:::definition{term="Situation ethics"}
A view that the loving action in the particular situation is the right one, even if it means setting a rule aside. It is associated with the Christian writer Joseph Fletcher.
:::

:::definition{term="Consequences"}
The results of an action. Some people judge an action by whether it brings the greatest good to the greatest number.
:::

:::definition{term="Corruption"}
Misusing a position of trust for personal gain, for example by taking or giving a bribe.
:::

:::keyfact
**Scripture:** Romans 2:15 (conscience); Exodus 20:15-16 (do not steal; do not give false testimony); Exodus 23:8 (a bribe blinds the officials); Proverbs 13:11 (wealth gained in haste dwindles); Qur'an 2:188 (do not take others' wealth wrongly or bribe judges); Qur'an 5:90 (gambling); Qur'an 49:6 and 49:12 (verify news; avoid backbiting).
:::

## How people make moral decisions

People use several **sources** of moral guidance, often together.

| Source | How it guides | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Scripture | The sacred text gives commands and examples | Exodus 20:15 forbids stealing; Qur'an 2:188 forbids bribery |
| Religious authority and tradition | Leaders, teachers, elders and community rules explain how to apply teaching | A rabbi, imam, pastor or elder is consulted |
| Conscience | Inner sense of right and wrong (Romans 2:15) | A learner returns a found purse because it feels wrong to keep it |
| Reason | Thinking through the situation and the principles | Weighing why honesty matters for trust |
| Consequences | Judging by the results for those affected | Asking who will be harmed if a bribe is paid |
| Situation ethics | The loving action in this situation | Setting a rule aside to help a person in real need |
| Unhu/ubuntu | The community's values of respect, sharing and responsibility | Elders teach that a person is a person through others |

Religions differ in how they weigh these. Many Christians, Jews and Muslims give first place to scripture and its interpreters, and stress that conscience should be formed by teaching. Situation ethics is debated, and some Christians criticise it for weakening rules. In Indigenous Religion, community values and the guidance of elders and ancestors are central. In practice most people use more than one source.

## Contemporary moral issues

**Corruption.** Taking or giving a bribe damages trust, and it harms the poor most. Exodus 23:8 says that a bribe blinds those who see clearly and corrupts the words of the righteous, and Qur'an 2:188 forbids taking others' wealth wrongly or bribing judges. Unhu teaches honesty and fairness, and leaders in all four religions condemn corruption and urge officials to serve the people.

**Honesty and theft.** The commandments forbid stealing and false testimony (Exodus 20:15-16), and Islam also forbids cheating and theft. In Indigenous Religion, theft shames a family, and the elders traditionally required compensation and reconciliation. Honesty builds trust in families, schools and business.

**Gambling and get-rich-quick schemes.** Islam forbids gambling (Qur'an 2:219 and 5:90). Many Christian churches discourage gambling as it promotes greed and may lead to poverty; Proverbs 13:11 warns that wealth gained in haste dwindles, and 1 Timothy 6:10 warns that love of money brings trouble. Jewish teachers criticise gambling as wasteful. Schemes that promise quick money, such as pyramid schemes, often cheat people, and religious teachers advise honest, patient work.

**Violence and bullying.** The religions teach love of neighbour (Leviticus 19:18), peacemaking (Matthew 5:9) and respect for others. Qur'an 49:11 forbids mocking people. Bullying, in school or online, harms victims and the bully's own character. Unhu teaches that a person must treat others with respect, and schools and religious groups can help bullied learners and correct bullies.

**Social media ethics.** Social media makes it easy to spread rumours, insult people and share private photographs. The religions give principles that apply: truthfulness (Exodus 20:16), verifying news before sharing (Qur'an 49:6), avoiding backbiting and spying (Qur'an 49:12), and respect for privacy and the dignity of others. A believer should ask: Is it true? Is it kind? Is it necessary? Could it harm someone?

## Making a moral decision

A useful method is to ask: What are the facts? Who is affected? What does my religion teach? What does my conscience say? What are the likely results? Whom can I consult? In an examination, you can show that you understand several sources of guidance by applying them to a case.

:::example{title="Tendai finds a purse"}
Tendai finds a purse with money and an identity card on the bus. Scripture tells him not to steal (Exodus 20:15). His conscience troubles him, and his grandmother taught him unhu. He thinks about the owner, who may need the money for school fees. He hands the purse to the conductor and tells his teacher. His decision uses scripture, conscience, tradition and an awareness of consequences.
:::

:::example{title="A rumour in a group chat"}
A rumour appears in a school group chat that a classmate, Chipo, has been caught stealing. Aisha remembers Qur'an 49:6 and Qur'an 49:12 and asks who started it and whether it is true. She does not forward it and tells the others to wait for facts. Her action protects Chipo's dignity and shows how religious teaching can guide behaviour online.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that all people use only one source of guidance"}
People use several sources together, such as scripture, conscience and community values. Mention at least two.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting religious teaching on gambling as the same everywhere"}
Islam forbids it clearly; many Christian churches discourage it; views among other groups differ. Say who holds each view.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing issues without applying teaching"}
Name the issue, give a text or teaching, and say what it means for behaviour, for example for bribery or bullying.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *describe how a believer makes a moral decision*, name at least three sources (scripture, religious authority, conscience, reason, unhu), explain each and give an example.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *how far is conscience the best guide?*, give strengths (personal, immediate), limits (can be wrong or uninformed), compare with scripture and tradition, and reach a supported conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- People use scripture, religious authority, conscience, reason, consequences, situation ethics and unhu to make moral decisions.
- Religions condemn corruption (Exodus 23:8; Qur'an 2:188) and teach honesty and respect for others' property.
- Islam forbids gambling, and many Christian churches discourage it and the pursuit of quick money.
- The religions teach love of neighbour and peacemaking, which apply to bullying and violence.
- Social media ethics apply truthfulness, verification, privacy and respect for dignity.
:::
