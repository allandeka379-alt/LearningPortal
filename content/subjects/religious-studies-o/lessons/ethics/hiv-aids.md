## Introduction

Almost every Zimbabwean school and community has been touched by HIV. A teacher may lose a colleague, a grandmother may raise grandchildren after the death of their parents, a church may run a support group for people living with HIV, and a clinic may offer free testing on the day of a village meeting. Religious bodies have been among the first to respond with prayer, food, home visits and support, and have also, at times, needed to challenge stigma among their own members.

In this lesson you learn the basic facts about HIV and AIDS, the effects of stigma, the teaching of the four religions about care for the sick, and how religious bodies respond. You should already know what the four religions teach about sex and marriage and about health and healing. The facts follow the Ministry of Health and Child Care and the World Health Organization. Language is non-stigmatising: we speak of **people living with HIV**, not of "victims" or "infected people". **Anyone who is worried about HIV should visit a clinic, where testing and counselling are available.**

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="HIV"}
The Human Immunodeficiency Virus, which attacks the body's immune system, the defence against infections.
:::

:::definition{term="AIDS"}
Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome, the most advanced stage of HIV infection, when the immune system is badly damaged. With treatment, a person with HIV can stay healthy and may never develop AIDS.
:::

:::definition{term="Antiretroviral therapy (ART)"}
Daily medicine that reduces the amount of virus in the body, allowing a person living with HIV to stay healthy and live a long life. A person on effective treatment who has a very low level of virus cannot pass HIV to a sexual partner.
:::

:::definition{term="Stigma"}
A mark of shame attached to a person, leading to rejection or unfair treatment. Discrimination is acting on stigma.
:::

:::keyfact
**Transmission:** unprotected sex with a person who has HIV; blood (shared needles or unscreened blood); from mother to child in pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. **Not spread by:** hugging, shaking hands, sharing plates, toilets or mosquitoes. **Prevention:** abstinence, faithfulness, condoms, testing, treatment, preventing mother-to-child transmission, medical male circumcision. **Scripture:** James 1:27 (care for orphans and widows); Matthew 25:35-40 (visiting the sick); Qur'an 93:9 (do not oppress the orphan).
:::

## Basic facts

HIV is **transmitted** in three main ways: through unprotected sex, through blood (for example shared needles), and from a mother to her child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It is not spread by hugging, shaking hands, sharing food or plates, toilets, or by mosquitoes. **Prevention** includes abstinence from sex until marriage, being faithful to one uninfected partner, using condoms correctly, avoiding shared needles, voluntary medical male circumcision, and treatment during pregnancy so that babies are born free of HIV. **Testing** is voluntary and confidential: a person who knows their status can start ART early and protect partners. **Treatment** with ART does not cure HIV, but it lets people stay healthy and work, study and raise families.

## Stigma and discrimination

Stigma stops people from testing, taking medicine and asking for help. A person living with HIV may be rejected by relatives, lose a job, or be blamed as though they have sinned. A child who has lost a parent may be avoided at school. Stigma is wrong because HIV is a health condition, not a measure of a person's worth. It also makes the epidemic worse, because people who fear rejection avoid the clinic. All the religions in the syllabus teach compassion, and many leaders now speak openly against stigma.

## Religious teaching on care and compassion

| Religion | Teaching on care for the sick and vulnerable | Source or idea |
|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion | The extended family and community care for the sick and for orphans | Unhu/ubuntu; kinship duty |
| Judaism | Visit the sick, give charity, protect life | *Bikur cholim*; *tzedakah*; *pikuach nefesh* |
| Christianity | Care for the sick, orphans and strangers as service to Jesus | Matthew 25:35-40; James 1:27 |
| Islam | Care for the sick, orphans and the poor | Qur'an 93:9; Zakat and sadaqah |

In **Indigenous Religion**, unhu and ubuntu teach that a person is a person through other people, so the extended family should look after the sick and the orphaned. In **Judaism**, *bikur cholim* and *tzedakah* are duties, and saving a life comes first. In **Christianity**, Matthew 25:35-40 teaches that visiting the sick is service to Jesus himself, and James 1:27 says that real religion includes caring for orphans and widows. In **Islam**, the Qur'an commands care for the orphan (Qur'an 93:9), and zakat can be used to help the poor and sick. In all four, the sick are treated with dignity.

## Religious responses

Religious organisations are often the largest providers of community support:

- **Prevention messages**: teaching abstinence before marriage and faithfulness within marriage, and, in some traditions, encouraging couples to test before marriage on a voluntary basis.
- **Testing and treatment**: church and mission clinics offer testing and ART, and many congregations announce clinic days.
- **Home-based care**: volunteers from churches, mosques and community groups visit the sick, help with food and washing, and support families.
- **Care for orphans and vulnerable children**: school fees, food and counselling provided by congregations, and children's groups.
- **Support groups** for people living with HIV, which often meet in a church or mosque hall.
- **Speaking out against stigma**: leaders preach that people living with HIV deserve respect.

Religions differ on **condoms**. The Catholic Church teaches abstinence before marriage and faithfulness within marriage, and does not approve of artificial contraception. Many Protestant churches, many Muslim and Jewish teachers and the health services accept condom use as a way of protecting life, especially where a partner may be at risk. Health workers say that condoms, used correctly and every time, are effective in preventing transmission. Religions also differ on whether a mandatory test before marriage should be required; health workers insist that testing must be **voluntary** and confidential.

## Harmful beliefs

Some beliefs have made the epidemic worse. Some people have blamed HIV on witchcraft, on ancestors or on punishment from God, and have therefore delayed going to the clinic. A few preachers have told members that prayer alone will remove HIV and that they should stop taking ART, and some people have died after stopping. Most religious leaders reject this teaching, and say that **prayer and medicine belong together**. Practices such as wife inheritance without consent, and the belief that sex with a virgin cures HIV, which is false, have also harmed people. A person living with HIV should continue taking medicine as directed by the health worker.

:::example{title="A church support group"}
At a church in Marondera, volunteers set up a support group for people living with HIV. Nokuthula, a nurse and a member of the congregation, explains how ART works, and the pastor preaches on Matthew 25:35-40 and tells the congregation that anyone who is unwell should be visited, not shunned. Members take food to families with sick relatives and help children with school fees. The example shows prevention, care and a challenge to stigma working together.
:::

:::example{title="Two views on a school talk"}
A school in Bulawayo invites a health worker to talk about condoms. Sipho, a Catholic, says that his church teaches abstinence and faithfulness. Ibrahim, a Muslim, says that his teacher accepts protection for a married couple where one partner has HIV. The health worker explains how HIV is spread and how it can be prevented. The students learn that religions share a concern for life, even where they differ on methods.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using stigmatising language"}
Write "people living with HIV", not "victims" or "AIDS sufferers", and do not suggest that HIV is a punishment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that all religions have the same view on condoms"}
Views differ. Say that the Catholic Church teaches abstinence and fidelity, while many others accept condom use for protection.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that prayer can replace treatment"}
Religious leaders and health workers say that prayer and medicine belong together, and that people should continue ART.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- HIV weakens the immune system; AIDS is the advanced stage; ART lets people living with HIV stay healthy.
- HIV is spread by unprotected sex, blood and from mother to child, not by everyday contact.
- Stigma stops people testing and seeking care; all four religions teach compassion for the sick and orphans.
- Religious bodies respond with prevention messages, testing, home-based care, support for orphans and support groups.
- Religions differ on condoms, and the belief that prayer replaces treatment is harmful.
:::
