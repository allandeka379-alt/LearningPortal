## Introduction

When a child in a Zimbabwean family falls ill with a high fever, relatives often do several things at once. A grandmother may ask whether something has been left undone at home, an aunt may suggest a visit to the church prophet or the n'anga, and a parent may carry the child to the clinic. Religion and health are closely linked in many people's lives because illness raises the biggest questions: why has this happened, who can help, and what do we owe the sick?

In this lesson you learn how the four religions in your syllabus understand illness and healing, and how religious practice relates to modern medicine. You should already know the main beliefs of each religion from earlier lessons. The religions are described as their followers understand them, and no religion is ranked above another. The health information here follows public messages of the Ministry of Health and Child Care and the World Health Organization: **anyone who is unwell should seek a trained health worker**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Healing"}
Restoring a person to health in body, mind and relationships. Religions often speak of healing of the whole person, not only of the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Traditional healer"}
A practitioner of Indigenous Religion (n'anga in Shona, inyanga in Ndebele) who diagnoses and treats illness using herbs, divination and ritual, and who is believed to work with the spirits.
:::

:::definition{term="Faith healing"}
Prayer, the laying on of hands or other religious acts carried out in the belief that God heals through them. Many churches practise it alongside medicine.
:::

:::definition{term="Pikuach nefesh"}
A Jewish principle, "saving of a life", which says that saving life overrides almost every other commandment.
:::

:::keyfact
**Christianity:** healing accounts in Mark 2:1-12 and Mark 10:46-52; anointing the sick in James 5:14-15. **Islam:** the body is a trust from God; Qur'an 2:184 allows a sick person not to fast. **Judaism:** *pikuach nefesh*; *bikur cholim* (visiting the sick). **Indigenous Religion:** illness may have natural, spiritual and social causes.
:::

## Indigenous Religion

In Indigenous Religion, illness is rarely seen as a purely physical matter. Many families distinguish three kinds of cause, and often consider them together. **Natural causes** include accidents, bad food and ordinary infections. **Spiritual causes** include the displeasure of the ancestral spirits (midzimu in Shona, amadlozi in Ndebele) when a ritual has been neglected or a taboo broken, and an angry spirit of a person wronged in life, such as ngozi in Shona. **Social causes** include quarrels in the family and the harm believed to be done by witchcraft. Witchcraft accusations have caused great suffering, and the law protects people from being accused.

A **n'anga or inyanga** is consulted to find the cause. The healer may use divination, such as throwing bones, and may prescribe herbs and a ritual such as a bira ceremony to put right a broken relationship with the ancestors. Healing therefore aims at restoring harmony between the person, the family and the spirit world. Healers are organised in associations and many work with health workers. Responsible healers refer patients with serious illness to the clinic, and nothing here claims that any remedy cures a particular disease.

## Judaism

Jewish tradition treats the preservation of life as a very high duty. **Pikuach nefesh** allows a person to break nearly all commandments, even the Shabbat rules, when a life is in danger; for example, a sick person may travel to hospital on Shabbat. Doctors are respected, and Jewish teachers say that going to a physician is permitted and in fact required. The duty of **bikur cholim**, visiting the sick, is taught as an act of kindness, and Jewish communities have long supported hospitals and care. Prayer for the sick is part of synagogue worship, alongside medical treatment.

## Christianity

The Gospels report that Jesus healed many people, such as the man let down through the roof (Mark 2:1-12) and blind Bartimaeus (Mark 10:46-52). Christians see these accounts as signs of God's compassion and as examples to follow. **James 5:14-15** tells the elders of the church to pray over the sick and anoint them with oil. Catholic and Orthodox churches keep this as a sacrament, the Anointing of the Sick.

Churches express this ministry in several ways:

- **Mission hospitals and clinics**, built by churches since the colonial period, still serve many rural areas.
- **Prayer and healing services** in Pentecostal churches.
- **African Independent Churches (AICs)**, where prophets pray for the sick, and many use holy water, ash or blessed objects as symbols of God's power.
- **Home visits and care** for the sick and for orphans.

Christians differ on how healing relates to medicine. Most churches teach that God can work through doctors, nurses and medicine as well as through prayer, but a few small groups discourage clinics or particular treatments.

## Islam

In Islam the body is an **amanah**, a trust from God, so Muslims must look after their health. Hadith, the reported sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad, encourage seeking treatment, and Muslims understand that God has provided a remedy for illness. Teachings known as **prophetic medicine** include the use of honey, which the Qur'an mentions as a source of healing (Qur'an 16:69), though Muslim doctors stress that these do not replace a trained doctor. The Qur'an also gives allowances to the sick: a person who is ill need not fast in Ramadan but may make up the days later (Qur'an 2:184). Muslims also pray for the sick, and Muslim doctors and hospitals serve people of all faiths.

|---|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion | Natural, spiritual and social causes | n'anga/inyanga; the family | Many healers refer serious cases to clinics |
| Judaism | A test and a call to care; life must be saved | Doctors; community visitors | Medicine encouraged; *pikuach nefesh* |
| Christianity | Part of a fallen world; God can heal | Prayer, elders, prophets, mission hospitals | Most churches support medicine; a few discourage it |
| Islam | Health is a trust; illness may be a test | Doctors; family; prayer | Treatment encouraged |

## Religion and modern medicine

Religion and medicine usually work together. Churches run hospitals, and healers and prayer leaders comfort patients. Problems arise when a belief **replaces** treatment. A few groups discourage vaccination or clinic visits. Health workers in Zimbabwe have appealed to all families, including those in churches that discourage clinics, to vaccinate children after outbreaks of illnesses such as measles. The Constitution of Zimbabwe 2013 protects the right to health care (section 76), and says that children have rights that include protection from harm (section 81), so adults cannot make a child go without treatment simply because of their own beliefs. Where a person is an adult and refuses a treatment, health workers explain the risks but respect the person's decision, within the law.

:::example{title="Rudo's family and the clinic"}
Rudo, aged 8, has a high fever and a rash. Her grandmother prays for her and says the family should visit the n'anga, while her father insists on the clinic. Together they agree that Rudo goes to the clinic first and that the family will pray and perform any ritual afterwards. The nurse treats her. This shows how a family can respect both its religious tradition and medical advice, which matches the practice of many believers.
:::

:::example{title="A synagogue and a hospital visit"}
Miriam's grandfather is taken to hospital on a Saturday. Jewish teaching on *pikuach nefesh* means that her family may travel to the hospital even though it is Shabbat. After he recovers, members of the synagogue visit him, which they call *bikur cholim*. The example shows that Judaism puts saving life first.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing that Indigenous Religion is opposed to medicine"}
Healers and families often use both herbs and ritual, and many encourage patients to attend the clinic. Describe the three kinds of cause and the role of the healer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that all Christians reject medicine"}
Most churches run hospitals and support medicine. Only a few small groups discourage clinics. Say "some" and give the range of views.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a remedy as a cure"}
Describe what believers do and believe. Do not write that a herb, water or prayer cures a disease.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *describe how two religions understand healing*, choose two, name the practitioners or practices (n'anga, anointing, prayer, doctors), give a reference or principle for each, and say what it means for believers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *how far can religion and modern medicine work together?*, give ways they cooperate (mission hospitals, referral by healers), ways they conflict (refusal of treatment), and reach a supported conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous Religion sees illness as having natural, spiritual and social causes, and n'anga/inyanga treat the whole person.
- Judaism holds that saving life overrides almost every commandment (*pikuach nefesh*) and encourages visiting the sick.
- Christians follow Jesus' healing ministry and James 5:14-15, through prayer, anointing, AIC healing and mission hospitals.
- Islam teaches that health is a trust and encourages treatment, with allowances for the sick (Qur'an 2:184).
- Religion and medicine usually cooperate, but belief that replaces treatment can harm, and children have a right to health care.
:::
