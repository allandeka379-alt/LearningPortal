## Introduction

News reports from Zimbabwean towns and townships regularly describe young people who have fallen into drug use: a student who stops attending lessons, a young man whose family can no longer trust him, a mother who is afraid to leave money in the house. Teachers, church leaders and chiefs all say that substance abuse is a growing worry for families and communities. At the same time, many homes, congregations and clubs are working to prevent it and to help those affected.

In this lesson you learn what substance abuse is, what causes it, what harm it does, what the four religions teach, and how religious communities respond. You should already know what religions teach about the body and about moral responsibility. The information follows public health messages: **a person who is struggling with drugs or alcohol should speak to a trained health worker or a trusted adult, because help and treatment exist.**

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Substance abuse"}
The harmful or excessive use of alcohol, drugs or other substances, whether legal or illegal, in a way that damages health, relationships or work.
:::

:::definition{term="Addiction"}
A condition in which a person feels unable to stop using a substance even though it causes harm. It is a health problem as well as a behaviour.
:::

:::definition{term="Intoxicant"}
Any substance that clouds the mind and affects behaviour. In Islam it is called *khamr*.
:::

:::definition{term="Rehabilitation"}
Help given to a person to stop using substances and to rebuild health, relationships and a future, through counselling, treatment and community support.
:::

:::keyfact
**Substances:** alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, crystal meth (called mutoriro in Shona), cough syrups such as bronclee, inhalants such as glue. **Scripture:** Proverbs 20:1; Ephesians 5:18; Qur'an 5:90 and 2:219. **Responses:** education, counselling, rehabilitation, support from family and faith groups, and the law.
:::

## Substances and their effects

Young people may meet several substances. **Alcohol** and **tobacco** are legal for adults but harmful. **Cannabis** is an illegal drug, and the law controls dangerous drugs. **Crystal meth**, locally known as *mutoriro*, is a powerful stimulant, and **cough syrups** with codeine, such as bronclee, and **inhalants** such as glue are misused for their intoxicating effects. All can cause harm.

**Causes** include peer pressure, curiosity, stress, unemployment and poverty, problems at home, loneliness, easy availability, and the belief that substances solve problems. **Effects** are felt at three levels:

| Level | Examples of effects |
|---|---|
| The individual | Damage to the brain, liver or lungs; addiction; mental health problems; poor school results; accidents; risky behaviour |
| The family | Quarrels, violence, loss of money and trust, neglect of children, shame |
| Society | Crime, road accidents, unemployment, loss of productivity, strain on health services |

## What the religions teach

**Indigenous Religion.** Traditional beer (doro in Shona, utshwala in Ndebele) has a place in rituals: beer is brewed for the ancestors at a bira or at kurova guva/umbuyiso, and elders share it. It is taken in a community setting and with respect. Drunkenness is condemned: a person who is drunk may quarrel, neglect the family or disrespect elders, and this goes against unhu/ubuntu. Today elders and healers advise that harmful modern drugs are a different matter and are no part of tradition.

**Judaism.** Wine is used in blessings, at Shabbat and at festivals, and its use is controlled and brought into a sacred setting. The Torah and the Writings warn against excess: Proverbs 20:1 describes wine as a mocker and strong drink as a brawler, and Genesis 9:20-21 reports Noah's drunkenness. Judaism teaches that the body is a trust and that a person must not harm health, so dangerous drugs are discouraged, and moderation is stressed.

**Christianity.** The Bible warns against drunkenness: Proverbs 20:1; Ephesians 5:18 tells believers not to get drunk with wine but to be filled with the Spirit. Christians also teach that the body is the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19-20), so it should not be damaged. Churches differ: the Catholic and Orthodox churches and many others permit moderate drinking while condemning drunkenness, but some, such as the Seventh-day Adventist Church, the Salvation Army, and many Pentecostal and African Independent Churches, teach complete abstinence from alcohol and drugs.

**Islam.** Islam forbids intoxicants. The Qur'an says that khamr (intoxicants) and gambling bring more harm than benefit (Qur'an 2:219) and tells believers to avoid them as the work of Satan (Qur'an 5:90). Muslim scholars apply the prohibition to other drugs that cloud the mind. Muslims also stress protection of the mind and body as a trust from God.

| Religion | Alcohol | Other drugs | Main teaching |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Religion | Beer in ritual; drunkenness condemned | Not part of tradition | Moderation; respect; unhu |
| Judaism | Wine in blessings; excess condemned | Discouraged because they harm the body | Moderation (Proverbs 20:1) |
| Christianity | Churches differ: moderation or abstinence | Harmful; the body is a temple | Do not be drunk (Ephesians 5:18) |
| Islam | Forbidden | Forbidden if they intoxicate | Avoid intoxicants (Qur'an 5:90) |

## Responses to substance abuse

Religious and community responses include:

- **Prevention**: teaching in churches, mosques and youth groups, scripture clubs in schools, and sports and music activities that keep young people busy.
- **Counselling and support**: pastors, imams, elders and teachers who listen to young people and refer them to health workers.
- **Rehabilitation**: church and community programmes that work with clinics to help people stop using drugs and rebuild their lives. Families are encouraged to support, not reject, a person in recovery.
- **Working with the law and the community**: leaders support police and schools in reducing the supply of drugs, and chiefs and councillors take part in awareness campaigns.

Teachers of all four religions stress that a person struggling with addiction should be treated with compassion and offered a path to recovery, and that the first step is to ask for help from a trained health worker.

:::example{title="Tafadzwa's youth group"}
Tafadzwa's friend Sipho starts skipping school and coming home late, and his family discovers that he has been using cough syrup for its effects. Tafadzwa tells their youth leader at church. The leader speaks with Sipho's parents, who take him to the clinic, and the youth group invites Sipho to football on Saturdays and to Bible study. The example shows prevention, support and referral working together, and it shows compassion rather than rejection.
:::

:::example{title="Ibrahim explains his faith to the class"}
Ibrahim, a Muslim student in Gweru, explains to his class that Muslims avoid intoxicants because of Qur'an 5:90. He adds that his imam talks to young men after Friday prayers about staying away from drugs and about asking for help if someone is struggling. The class sees that a religious teaching is supported by community action.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all religions forbid all alcohol"}
Islam forbids it, and some Christian churches teach abstinence, but Indigenous Religion uses beer in ritual, Judaism uses wine in blessings, and many Christian churches allow moderate drinking while condemning drunkenness.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating addiction only as a moral failing"}
Addiction is also a health problem. Religious leaders and health workers both say that people need compassion and treatment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing effects without explaining"}
Say what the effect is and why it matters, for example that addiction causes violence at home and loss of money, so the family suffers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (a): describe or explain"}
For *explain the teaching of two religions on drug and alcohol use*, choose two religions, give a reference (such as Ephesians 5:18 or Qur'an 5:90) and explain the reason behind it.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2 (b): discuss or assess"}
For *how effective are religious responses to substance abuse?*, give ways they help (education, support, rehabilitation), limits (reaching all young people, stigma), and reach a supported conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Substance abuse is the harmful use of alcohol, drugs or other substances; addiction is a health problem as well as a behaviour.
- Common substances include alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, crystal meth (mutoriro), cough syrups such as bronclee and inhalants.
- Effects harm the individual, the family and society.
- Indigenous Religion uses beer in ritual but condemns drunkenness; Judaism stresses moderation; Christian churches differ between moderation and abstinence; Islam forbids intoxicants.
- Religious bodies respond with prevention, counselling and rehabilitation, and urge people to seek help from a trained health worker.
:::
