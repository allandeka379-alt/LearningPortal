## Introduction

Nyasha sells second-hand clothes from a stall at Mbare Musika. Every Saturday evening she counts the money she has taken and subtracts what she paid for the bales of clothes, the stall fee and her transport. What is left is her **profit**: her reward for the work and the risk of running the stall.

Profit is the main objective of most private-sector businesses, from a tuck shop to a large company listed on the stock exchange. Examiners expect you to define profit, calculate it from simple data and explain why it matters to the owners and to the business itself. This lesson also prepares you for the income statement (statement of profit or loss), where profit is calculated in a formal layout.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Revenue"}
The income a business earns from selling its goods or services in a period (selling price × quantity sold). Also called sales revenue, sales or turnover.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
The surplus when revenue is greater than total costs in a period. If total costs are greater than revenue, the business makes a **loss**.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross profit"}
Revenue minus the cost of sales (the cost of the goods that were sold), before expenses such as rent and wages are deducted.
:::

:::definition{term="Retained profit"}
The part of profit kept in the business after the owners have been paid, used to finance future spending.
:::

## Calculating profit

:::formula{name="Profit"}
Profit = revenue − total costs
:::

Total costs include everything the business spent to earn the revenue: the goods or materials, wages, rent, transport, electricity and so on. When the cost of the goods sold is taken off first, the result is **gross profit**; when the other expenses are then deducted, the result is **profit** (often called **net profit** in ZIMSEC).

:::formula{name="Gross profit and profit"}
Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales

Profit = gross profit − expenses
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: profit for a market stall]
Nyasha's figures for one month are shown below.

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | 3,600 |
| Stock bought and sold | (2,300) |
| Stall fee | (120) |
| Transport | (180) |
| Airtime and data | (40) |
| Total costs | (2,640) |
| Profit | 960 |

1. Total costs = 2,300 + 120 + 180 + 40 = US\$2,640.
2. Profit = US\$3,600 − US\$2,640 = US\$960.
3. Gross profit (before the stall fee, transport and airtime) = US\$3,600 − US\$2,300 = US\$1,300.
:::

## Why profit matters to private-sector businesses

### A reward for risk-taking

The owners of a business put their own money at risk. If the business fails they may lose everything they invested. Profit is their **reward for taking that risk** and for the effort of running the business. Without the chance of profit, few people would start a business at all.

### A source of finance

Profit that is not paid to the owners can be kept in the business as **retained profit**. It is the cheapest source of finance: no interest is paid, nothing has to be repaid and the owners do not give up any control. A business can use retained profit to buy equipment, open a new branch or survive a difficult period.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: retaining profit for growth]
Thandeka's printing company makes a profit of US\$18,000 this year. The shareholders decide to receive US\$7,000 as dividends.

1. Retained profit = US\$18,000 − US\$7,000 = US\$11,000.
2. The company can use the US\$11,000 towards a new printing machine without borrowing, so it pays no interest.
3. If the shareholders had taken all the profit as dividends, the company would need a loan, and the interest would reduce future profits.
:::

### A measure of success

Profit shows whether the business is using its resources well. Owners compare profit with previous years, with competitors and with the money invested. A rising profit suggests the business is growing or becoming more efficient; a falling profit warns that costs are rising or sales are weak.

### Attracting investors and lenders

Investors buy shares in profitable companies because they expect dividends and a rising share price. Banks are more willing to lend to a profitable business because it is more likely to repay. A record of profit therefore makes it easier and cheaper to raise finance.

### Other reasons

- Profit allows a business to **pay higher wages** and bonuses, which helps it keep good employees.
- Profitable businesses pay **tax**, which funds public services.
- Profit gives a **cushion** against bad years, so the business can survive a fall in sales.

## Profit is not the only objective

Not every organisation aims mainly for profit. A new business may aim simply to **survive**; a social enterprise or co-operative may aim to help its members or community; a public-sector organisation aims to provide a service. Even profit-seeking businesses may accept lower profit for a time to win market share. But in the long run a private-sector business that never makes a profit cannot survive, because its owners will not keep putting money in.

## Profit and cash

Profit is not the same as cash. A business can make a profit but have no cash if customers have not paid, or if it has spent cash on new equipment. The lesson on cash-flow problems explains this difference in detail.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing revenue with profit"}
Revenue is the money from sales before any costs are taken off. A business with high revenue can still make a loss if its costs are higher.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting some costs"}
Profit is revenue minus **all** costs. Leaving out rent, transport or wages overstates profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying profit is the same as cash in the bank"}
Profit is a calculation for a period; cash is the money actually held. They are usually different amounts.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show the formula"}
For any profit calculation, write the formula first (profit = revenue − total costs), then the figures, then the answer with US\$. Method marks are often available.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain, do not list"}
"Explain why profit is important" needs a reason **and** what it allows the business to do, for example "retained profit is a source of finance, so the business can buy new machinery without paying interest".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board terms"}
Cambridge 0264 uses **profit** and statement of profit or loss; ZIMSEC uses **net profit** and income statement. Both mean revenue minus all costs.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Profit = revenue − total costs; a loss occurs when costs are greater than revenue.
- Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales; profit (net profit) = gross profit − expenses.
- Profit rewards owners for risk and effort, provides retained profit as cheap finance, measures success and attracts investors and lenders.
- Not all organisations aim mainly for profit, but private-sector businesses must make a profit in the long run to survive.
- Profit is not the same as cash.
:::
