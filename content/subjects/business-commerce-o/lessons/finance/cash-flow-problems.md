## Introduction

Tatenda runs a busy furniture workshop in Chinhoyi. His order book is full, his customers like his tables, and his accounts show a profit every month. Yet last month he could not pay his two carpenters on time, and the timber supplier refused to deliver until an old invoice was settled. How can a profitable business be short of money?

The answer is that **profit and cash are not the same thing**. A business can sell goods at a good price and still have an empty cash box, because customers have not yet paid, or because the owner has spent cash on stock or equipment. In this lesson you learn why cash matters, how cash differs from profit, what causes short-term cash-flow problems and how a business can solve them. Examiners often give a cash flow forecast (cash budget) with a negative closing balance and ask you to explain the cause and recommend a solution, so this topic links closely to the lesson on cash flow forecasts.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cash"}
Money held by the business in notes and coins, in its bank account and in mobile-money wallets, which can be used immediately to make payments.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash flow"}
The movement of cash into a business (cash inflows) and out of a business (cash outflows) over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
The surplus when revenue earned from sales is greater than the costs of making those sales in a period (revenue − total costs).
:::

:::definition{term="Liquidity"}
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts when they are due.
:::

:::definition{term="Insolvent"}
Unable to pay debts when they are due. An insolvent business may be forced to close even if it is profitable.
:::

## Why cash matters

Cash is often described as the "lifeblood" of a business. Without it a business cannot:

- **pay suppliers**, who may stop deliveries or refuse credit in future;
- **pay wages**, so employees lose motivation or leave;
- **pay rent, electricity and other bills**, leading to penalties or disconnection;
- **repay loans and interest**, so the bank may take the security or refuse future loans;
- **take advantage of opportunities**, such as a cash discount or a bulk-buying offer.

If a business cannot pay its debts it is **insolvent**. Creditors may take legal action to recover what they are owed, and the business may have to close, even if its products sell well.

## Cash is not the same as profit

Profit is calculated when a sale is made, whether or not the customer has paid. Cash is counted only when money actually moves. The two differ for several reasons:

- **Credit sales:** goods sold on credit add to revenue and profit now, but the cash arrives later.
- **Credit purchases:** goods bought on credit are a cost now, but cash leaves later.
- **Buying non-current assets:** paying for a vehicle or oven uses a large amount of cash at once, but only part of its cost is counted against profit each year.
- **Building up inventory:** cash spent on stock that has not yet been sold is not yet a cost of sales.
- **Owner's capital and loans:** cash received from a loan or from the owner is not revenue, so it raises cash but not profit.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: profitable but short of cash]
In March Tatenda makes and sells 10 tables at US\$150 each to a school, which will pay in 60 days. The timber, varnish and wages for the tables cost US\$900, all paid in cash in March.

1. Revenue for March = 10 × US\$150 = US\$1,500.
2. Profit on the order = US\$1,500 − US\$900 = US\$600.
3. Cash in March: no money is received yet, but US\$900 is paid out, so cash **falls** by US\$900.
4. The order is profitable, but in March it causes a cash outflow of US\$900. Tatenda needs enough cash to survive until the school pays in May.
:::

## Causes of cash-flow problems

| Cause | Why it reduces cash |
| --- | --- |
| Allowing customers too long to pay | Cash from sales arrives late while bills must be paid now |
| Customers who pay late or never pay (bad debts) | Expected inflows do not arrive |
| Overtrading (expanding too quickly) | Cash is spent on stock, wages and equipment before extra sales bring in cash |
| Holding too much inventory | Cash is tied up in goods on the shelves |
| Large one-off payments | Buying a vehicle or machine takes a large sum at once |
| Poor forecasting and planning | The owner does not see a shortage coming and has no overdraft arranged |
| Seasonal demand | Cash comes in during busy months, but costs continue in quiet months |
| Falling sales or unexpected cost rises | Inflows fall or outflows rise, for example when suppliers raise prices |

## Ways to overcome cash-flow problems

| Solution | How it helps | Drawback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrange or increase a bank overdraft | Cash is available immediately to pay bills; interest only on the amount used | Interest charges are high; the bank can ask for repayment at short notice |
| Delay payments to suppliers (ask for longer credit) | Cash stays in the business for longer | Suppliers may refuse, charge more or stop supplying; discounts lost |
| Ask customers to pay sooner (or offer a discount for prompt payment) | Cash arrives earlier | Customers may go to rivals who give longer credit; discount reduces revenue |
| Delay or cancel purchases of non-current assets | Avoids a large cash outflow now | Efficiency or growth may suffer without the new equipment |
| Lease or hire purchase instead of buying outright | Spreads the cost over many months | Total cost is higher over time |
| Reduce inventory (sell off slow-moving stock) | Turns stock back into cash | May need price cuts; risk of running out |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: spotting and solving a problem in a forecast]
A bakery in Masvingo has prepared this forecast. It plans to buy a new oven in May.

| US\$ | April | May | June |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Cash inflows** |  |  |  |
| Cash sales | 1,800 | 1,900 | 2,000 |
| Receipts from credit customers | 600 | 700 | 2,400 |
| Total inflows | 2,400 | 2,600 | 4,400 |
| **Cash outflows** |  |  |  |
| Payments to suppliers | 1,500 | 1,600 | 1,500 |
| Wages | 700 | 700 | 700 |
| New oven | 0 | 1,200 | 0 |
| Other expenses | 300 | 300 | 300 |
| Total outflows | 2,500 | 3,800 | 2,500 |
| Net cash flow | (100) | (1,200) | 1,900 |
| Opening balance | 400 | 300 | (900) |
| Closing balance | 300 | (900) | 1,000 |

1. **Identify the problem:** the closing balance in May is negative, (US\$900). The bakery would need an overdraft of US\$900.
2. **Find the cause:** in May the oven costs US\$1,200 and most of the money from credit customers does not arrive until June (US\$2,400).
3. **Option A, delay the oven to June:** May's outflows fall to US\$2,600, so net cash flow is zero and May closes at US\$300. June's net cash flow becomes US\$700 and June still closes at US\$1,000.
4. **Option B, arrange an overdraft of at least US\$900 for one month:** the oven arrives on time; interest is paid for a short period only.
5. **Judgement:** if the old oven still works, delaying the purchase by one month is cheapest. If the new oven is needed to meet orders, a short overdraft is better, because the forecast shows the bakery can repay it in June.
:::

## Choosing the best solution

The right solution depends on the **cause** and the **length** of the problem. A short gap of one or two months is best covered by an overdraft or by delaying a payment. A long-term problem caused by falling sales cannot be solved by an overdraft: the business must raise its sales, cut its costs or bring in long-term finance. Always ask: how much cash is needed, for how long, and what will it cost?

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating profit as cash"}
Writing "the business made a profit, so it has plenty of cash" loses marks. Credit sales and buying assets mean a profitable business can still run short of cash.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending a long-term loan for a one-month gap"}
A loan for several years is unsuitable for a short shortage. Match the solution to the size and length of the problem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing solutions without drawbacks"}
Every solution has a cost: interest on an overdraft, lost supplier goodwill or lost customers. Evaluation needs both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the forecast figures"}
When a forecast is given, name the month of the problem, the size of the shortage and its cause from the table ("in May the closing balance is (US\$900) because the oven costs US\$1,200").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend with a reason"}
For "recommend" or "justify" questions, compare two solutions and choose one for this business, explaining why it suits the cause and length of the problem.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board terms"}
ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills calls the forecast a **cash budget**; Cambridge 0264 calls it a **cash flow forecast**. Both boards examine the difference between cash and profit and ways of solving short-term problems.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cash is needed to pay suppliers, employees, bills and loans; a business that cannot pay its debts is insolvent and may have to close.
- Profit is not cash: credit sales, credit purchases, buying non-current assets, building up inventory and loans make the two differ.
- Causes of cash-flow problems include long credit to customers, bad debts, overtrading, too much inventory, large one-off payments, poor planning and seasonal demand.
- Short-term solutions are an overdraft, delaying payments to suppliers, asking customers to pay sooner and delaying the purchase of non-current assets; each has a drawback.
- The best solution depends on the cause, the size and the length of the shortage.
:::
