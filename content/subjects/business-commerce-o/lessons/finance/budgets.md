## Introduction

Rudo and Tatenda have opened a small bakery in Mutare. Before each quarter they sit down and write out how many loaves they expect to sell, how many they must bake, how much flour they must buy and how much cash they will have each month. This written plan is a **budget**. It tells them whether they can afford a second oven, warns them when cash will be tight and gives them targets to aim at.

Every enterprise, from a market stall in Sakubva to a large manufacturer, needs to plan its money. In ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) you must know the meaning of budgeting, the main types of budget (cash, sales, production and purchases), how to prepare a simple budget, and why budgets are useful and what their limits are. Questions often give you planned figures and ask you to complete a budget, calculate a difference from the actual result and comment on it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Budget"}
A financial plan for a future period, expressed in money or units, showing the income, spending or output an enterprise expects.
:::

:::definition{term="Budgeting"}
The process of preparing budgets, using them to guide decisions and comparing them with what actually happens.
:::

:::definition{term="Budget holder"}
The manager or person responsible for keeping to a particular budget, for example the sales manager for the sales budget.
:::

:::definition{term="Variance"}
The difference between a budgeted (planned) figure and the actual figure. It is **favourable** when it improves profit and **adverse** when it reduces profit.
:::

## Types of budget

| Budget | What it shows | Who uses it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales budget | Expected units sold and sales revenue each month (units × price) | Sales or marketing manager |
| Production budget | Number of units to be made each month to meet planned sales and inventory | Production manager |
| Purchases budget | Quantity and cost of raw materials or goods for resale to be bought | Purchasing (buying) manager |
| Cash budget | Expected cash inflows, outflows and balances each month | Owner or finance manager |

The budgets are linked. The **sales budget** is usually prepared first, because it decides how much must be produced. The **production budget** decides how many materials must be bought, which gives the **purchases budget**. All of them feed into the **cash budget**, because sales bring cash in and purchases and wages take cash out.

## Preparing a simple budget

The steps are the same for any budget:

1. Decide the period (for example the next three months) and set objectives.
2. Collect information: last year's figures, market research, price lists, planned changes.
3. Estimate each figure month by month.
4. Total the figures and check that the budget is realistic and affordable.
5. Agree the budget with the people who must work to it (the budget holders).
6. Compare actual results with the budget during the period and take action.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a sales budget]
The bakery expects to sell 400 loaves in January, 450 in February and 520 in March at US\$2.50 each.

| Sales budget | January | February | March | Total |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Loaves sold | 400 | 450 | 520 | 1,370 |
| Price per loaf (US\$) | 2.50 | 2.50 | 2.50 |  |
| Sales revenue (US\$) | 1,000 | 1,125 | 1,300 | 3,425 |

1. January revenue = 400 × US\$2.50 = US\$1,000.
2. February = 450 × US\$2.50 = US\$1,125; March = 520 × US\$2.50 = US\$1,300.
3. Total planned revenue for the quarter = 1,000 + 1,125 + 1,300 = US\$3,425.
:::

A **production budget** works out how many units must be made. A business wants some finished goods in store at the end of each month (closing inventory), and it already has some at the start (opening inventory):

:::formula{name="Units to produce"}
Units to produce = planned sales + planned closing inventory − opening inventory
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a production budget]
A furniture workshop in Bulawayo plans its chair production. Each month's opening inventory is the previous month's closing inventory.

| Production budget (chairs) | January | February | March |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Planned sales | 300 | 340 | 380 |
| Add planned closing inventory | 60 | 70 | 50 |
| Less opening inventory | (40) | (60) | (70) |
| Units to produce | 320 | 350 | 360 |

1. January: 300 + 60 − 40 = 320 chairs.
2. February: 340 + 70 − 60 = 350 chairs.
3. March: 380 + 50 − 70 = 360 chairs.
4. The **purchases budget** follows from this. If each chair uses US\$12 of timber, January's timber purchases are 320 × US\$12 = US\$3,840 (ignoring any timber kept in store).
:::

The **cash budget** brings everything together: cash sales and money from debtors are inflows; purchases, wages, rent and other payments are outflows. It is covered in detail in the lesson on cash flow forecasts and cash budgets.

## Comparing the budget with actual results

A budget is only useful if the enterprise checks what really happened. The difference is the **variance**.

- **Favourable variance:** actual revenue is higher than budget, or actual costs are lower than budget.
- **Adverse variance:** actual revenue is lower than budget, or actual costs are higher than budget.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: variances for one month]
| Item | Budget US\$ | Actual US\$ | Variance US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Sales revenue | 6,000 | 5,400 | 600 |
| Materials | 2,400 | 2,550 | 150 |
| Wages | 1,500 | 1,500 | 0 |
| Advertising | 300 | 200 | 100 |

1. Sales revenue: actual is US\$600 below budget, so the variance is **adverse**.
2. Materials: actual cost is US\$150 above budget, so the variance is **adverse**.
3. Wages: no variance.
4. Advertising: actual cost is US\$100 below budget, so the variance is **favourable**. But spending less on advertising may be one reason why sales were below budget, so a favourable variance is not always good news.
:::

The manager then asks **why** each variance happened (for example, a rival cut prices, or flour prices rose) and decides what to do: change the price, find a cheaper supplier, or revise the budget.

## Why budgeting is important

- **Planning:** owners think ahead about sales, costs and cash, and spot problems early.
- **Control:** comparing budgets with actual results shows where money is being wasted.
- **Targets and motivation:** budget holders have clear targets; meeting them can be rewarded.
- **Coordination:** sales, production and purchasing work to the same plan, so the right amount is made and bought.
- **Raising finance:** banks and other lenders ask for budgets before lending.
- **Responsibility:** each budget has a holder who is accountable for it.

## Limits of budgeting

- Budgets are based on **forecasts**, which may be wrong, especially when prices or demand change quickly.
- Preparing budgets takes **time** and skill, which small enterprises may lack.
- Budgets can be **inflexible**: managers may refuse sensible spending because "it is not in the budget".
- Targets set too high can **demotivate** staff; targets set too low encourage little effort.
- Managers may spend everything left in a budget at the end of the period so that it is not cut next time.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every lower figure favourable"}
Lower **costs** are favourable, but lower **revenue** is adverse. Always ask whether the difference raises or lowers profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the production budget the wrong way round"}
Add the closing inventory and subtract the opening inventory. Reversing them gives the wrong number of units to make.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a budget with a record"}
A budget is a plan for the future; accounts record what has already happened.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show the calculation layout"}
Set out a budget as a table with months across the top and a total. Marks are given for correct method as well as the final figure.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain a variance, then act"}
For a variance question, state whether it is favourable or adverse, suggest a likely cause from the case, and recommend an action.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A budget is a financial plan for a future period; budgeting is preparing budgets and comparing them with results.
- The sales budget is prepared first; it drives the production budget (sales + closing inventory − opening inventory), the purchases budget and the cash budget.
- A variance is the difference between budget and actual: favourable if it raises profit, adverse if it lowers it.
- Budgets help planning, control, motivation, coordination and raising finance.
- Budgets rely on forecasts, take time, can be inflexible and can demotivate if targets are unrealistic.
:::
