## Introduction

At the end of each year Farai, who owns a hardware shop in Mutare, wants to know one thing: did the business make a profit, and how big was it? His accountant answers with an **income statement**. It starts with everything the shop sold, takes off the cost of the goods sold to show the gross profit, and then takes off the running expenses to show the profit for the year.

ZIMSEC calls this document the **income statement**; Cambridge 0264 calls it the **statement of profit or loss**. They are the same thing. Cambridge candidates must understand its main features and use it to make simple calculations and decisions; ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills candidates (Forms 2 and 3) also **prepare** a simple one, including the cost of sales from opening and closing inventory.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Income statement (statement of profit or loss)"}
A financial statement that shows a business's revenue, costs and profit (or loss) over a period of time, usually one year.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue"}
The value of sales made during the period (also called sales or turnover).
:::

:::definition{term="Cost of sales"}
The cost of the goods that were actually sold during the period (also called cost of goods sold).
:::

:::definition{term="Expenses"}
The other costs of running the business in the period, such as rent, wages, electricity, insurance and transport (sometimes called overheads).
:::

:::definition{term="Profit for the year"}
Gross profit minus expenses (ZIMSEC: **net profit**). If expenses are greater than gross profit, the result is a loss.
:::

## The layout

An income statement always follows the same order:

1. **Revenue**: the value of all sales in the year.
2. **Cost of sales**: what the goods sold cost the business.
3. **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales.
4. **Expenses**: listed and totalled.
5. **Profit for the year** = gross profit − expenses.

A limited company's statement may go further: tax is deducted to give profit after tax, and the profit is then split between **dividends** to shareholders and **retained profit**.

### Calculating the cost of sales (ZIMSEC)

A trader does not sell every item bought in the year, and starts the year with some goods already in store. So the cost of sales is not simply the purchases:

:::formula{name="Cost of sales"}
Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: preparing an income statement]
Mutare Hardware has these figures for the year ended 31 December: revenue US\$48,000; opening inventory US\$5,000; purchases US\$30,000; closing inventory US\$6,000; rent US\$4,800; wages US\$6,000; electricity US\$1,200; transport US\$900.

**Mutare Hardware: income statement for the year ended 31 December**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue (sales) |  | 48,000 |
| Opening inventory | 5,000 |  |
| Add purchases | 30,000 |  |
|  | 35,000 |  |
| Less closing inventory | (6,000) |  |
| Cost of sales |  | (29,000) |
| Gross profit |  | 19,000 |
| Less expenses: |  |  |
| Rent | 4,800 |  |
| Wages | 6,000 |  |
| Electricity | 1,200 |  |
| Transport | 900 |  |
| Total expenses |  | (12,900) |
| Profit for the year |  | 6,100 |

1. Cost of sales = 5,000 + 30,000 − 6,000 = US\$29,000.
2. Gross profit = 48,000 − 29,000 = US\$19,000.
3. Total expenses = 4,800 + 6,000 + 1,200 + 900 = US\$12,900.
4. Profit for the year = 19,000 − 12,900 = US\$6,100.
:::

## Using the income statement to make decisions

The statement is useful only if someone uses it. Owners and managers compare it with **previous years**, with **budgets** and with **competitors**, and ask why the figures changed.

- If **gross profit** is falling compared with revenue, the business may be paying more for its goods or selling at lower prices. Action: find a cheaper supplier, buy in bulk or raise prices.
- If **expenses** are rising faster than revenue, the business should look for savings, for example cheaper premises or lower electricity use.
- If **profit** is rising, the owners may decide to expand, pay dividends or reward employees.
- Investors and banks use the statement to decide whether to invest or lend.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two years]
A stationery wholesaler in Harare has these results.

| US\$ | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | 60,000 | 72,000 |
| Cost of sales | 36,000 | 46,800 |
| Gross profit | 24,000 | 25,200 |
| Expenses | 15,000 | 16,200 |
| Profit | 9,000 | 9,000 |

1. Revenue rose by US\$12,000 (20%), but profit stayed at US\$9,000.
2. Gross profit as a percentage of revenue fell from 24,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 40% to 25,200 ÷ 72,000 × 100 = 35%. The business kept less from each dollar of sales: perhaps suppliers raised prices, or the business cut its own prices to win more sales.
3. Decision: the owner should check supplier prices and pricing policy before spending on further growth, because extra sales have not produced extra profit.
:::

These percentages are **profitability ratios** (gross profit margin and profit margin), covered in detail in their own lesson.

## Limitations of the income statement

- It describes the **past**; next year may be different.
- It shows profit, **not cash**: a profitable business can still run short of cash.
- It does not show non-financial factors such as staff morale, customer loyalty or the quality of the product.
- Figures such as the value of closing inventory involve **estimates**.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using purchases as the cost of sales"}
Purchases are not the cost of sales unless inventory did not change. Add opening inventory and subtract closing inventory.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting cost of sales among the expenses"}
Cost of sales is deducted **before** gross profit; expenses are deducted **after** it. Mixing them gives the wrong gross profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including non-current assets as expenses"}
Buying a vehicle or machine is not an expense in the income statement; it appears in the statement of financial position.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Label every line"}
In a "prepare" question, give the statement a heading (name, "income statement for the year ended …"), label each line and total each section. Marks are awarded for the layout as well as the answers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain changes with causes and actions"}
When comparing two years, quote the figures, give a likely cause and suggest an action. A statement such as "profit stayed the same although revenue rose by US\$12,000" earns more than "profit is the same".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 does not ask you to construct the statement; it asks you to read, calculate from and interpret it. ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills asks you to prepare a simple income statement for a trader.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The income statement (statement of profit or loss) shows revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, expenses and profit for a period.
- Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales; profit (net profit) = gross profit − expenses.
- Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory.
- Owners, managers, investors and lenders compare statements over time and with rivals to make decisions about prices, costs, expansion and lending.
- The statement describes the past, shows profit not cash, and ignores non-financial factors.
:::
