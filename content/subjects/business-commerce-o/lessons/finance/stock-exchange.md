## Introduction

Nyasha owns 2,000 shares in a fictional listed company, Mukuyu Juices Ltd. She needs money for her daughter's university fees and wants to sell them. She does not need to find a buyer herself: she instructs a **stockbroker**, who sells the shares on the **Zimbabwe Stock Exchange (ZSE)** in Harare. A stock exchange is a market where existing shares and other securities are bought and sold, and it is the place where companies raise capital from the public.

This Form 4 ZIMSEC Commerce topic asks you to explain the functions and importance of a stock exchange and of the ZSE, describe the people who deal there, and explain the behaviour of speculators: bulls, bears and stags.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stock exchange"}
An organised market where shares, debentures, government stocks and other securities of listed organisations are bought and sold under strict rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Listed (quoted) company"}
A public limited company whose shares are traded on a stock exchange, having met the exchange's listing requirements.
:::

:::definition{term="Stockbroker"}
A member of the stock exchange who buys and sells securities on behalf of clients in return for a commission (brokerage), and gives investment advice.
:::

:::definition{term="Jobber (market maker)"}
A dealer who buys and sells securities on their own account, holding shares and quoting prices at which they will buy and sell, and earning a profit from the difference (the "turn" or spread).
:::

:::definition{term="Speculator"}
A person who buys or sells securities hoping to make a quick profit from changes in their prices, rather than holding them as a long-term investment.
:::

## Functions of a stock exchange

1. **A market for second-hand securities.** Investors can sell their shares quickly (liquidity), which makes people more willing to buy them in the first place.
2. **Raising capital for companies.** Public limited companies sell new shares and debentures to the public through the exchange when they list or issue more securities.
3. **Raising money for the government.** Government stocks and bonds can be traded, helping the government borrow.
4. **Valuing companies and securities.** Share prices, published daily, show what investors think a company is worth.
5. **Protecting investors.** Listing rules require companies to publish accounts and information; dealing is regulated to prevent fraud.
6. **Channelling savings into investment.** Savings of individuals, pension funds and insurance companies are directed to productive companies.

## Importance of the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange

| To companies | To investors | To the government and economy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise large amounts of long-term capital | Safe, regulated place to invest savings | Mobilises savings for investment and growth |
| Status and publicity from being listed | Can sell shares easily when cash is needed | Attracts local and foreign investment |
| Shares can be used to take over other companies | Earn dividends and capital gains | Share prices act as an indicator of economic confidence |
| Wider share ownership | Information published on listed companies | Allows government borrowing and privatisation through share sales |

## Who deals on the stock exchange

- **Stockbrokers** act as **agents** for the public. A member of the public cannot deal directly; the broker buys or sells for the client, charges **brokerage** (commission) and advises on investments.
- **Jobbers (market makers)** act as **principals**, dealing on their own account. They hold stocks of shares and quote two prices: a lower **bid** price at which they buy and a higher **offer** price at which they sell. The difference is their profit. In many modern exchanges the jobber's role is carried out by market makers or by electronic order-matching systems.

## Speculators

### Bulls

A **bull** expects share prices to **rise**. He **buys** shares now, hoping to sell them later at a higher price. When many investors are bulls, prices rise: a **bull market**.

### Bears

A **bear** expects share prices to **fall**. She **sells** shares now (often shares she already owns, or shares she promises to deliver later), hoping to buy them back later at a lower price. When many investors are bears, prices fall: a **bear market**.

### Stags

A **stag** applies for **new** shares when a company first offers them to the public, expecting the price to rise as soon as dealing begins, and then sells them quickly for a profit.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a stag's gain]
A company offers new shares to the public at US\$0.50 each. Tatenda, acting as a stag, buys 2,000 shares. On the first day of dealing the price rises to US\$0.58, and he sells all of them. (Ignore brokerage.)

1. Cost: 2,000 × US\$0.50 = US\$1,000.
2. Sale: 2,000 × US\$0.58 = US\$1,160.
3. Gain: US\$1,160 − US\$1,000 = US\$160, or 8 cents per share.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a bear's gain]
Chipo owns 1,000 shares priced at US\$1.20. She expects the price to fall, so she sells them. Two months later the price has fallen to US\$1.05 and she buys 1,000 shares back. (Ignore brokerage.)

1. Sale: 1,000 × US\$1.20 = US\$1,200.
2. Purchase: 1,000 × US\$1.05 = US\$1,050.
3. Gain: US\$1,200 − US\$1,050 = US\$150; she has her 1,000 shares again plus US\$150.
4. If the price had **risen** instead, buying back would have cost more and she would have made a loss.
:::

**Effects of speculation.** Speculators make the market more active, so it is easier to buy and sell. But heavy speculation can cause sharp rises and falls in prices that do not reflect how well companies are performing, and inexperienced speculators can lose heavily.

:::example{title="Why a family business might list on the ZSE"}
A fictional food-processing company, owned by one family in Bulawayo, wants to build a new factory but its bank will not lend more. By becoming a public company and listing on the stock exchange it can sell shares to thousands of investors. The family gains capital and a public profile, but must publish its accounts, follow listing rules, pay listing costs, and share control with new shareholders.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the stock exchange is where all companies get finance"}
Only **listed public limited companies** raise share capital on the stock exchange. Sole traders, partnerships and private companies cannot.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up bulls and bears"}
Bulls **buy** expecting a **rise**; bears **sell** expecting a **fall**. Remember: a bull tosses prices up with its horns; a bear swipes them down with its paws.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing brokers and jobbers"}
Brokers are **agents** for clients who earn commission; jobbers are **principals** who deal on their own account and earn the spread.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A stock exchange is a regulated market for securities; it provides liquidity, raises capital for companies and government, values securities and protects investors.
- The ZSE mobilises savings for investment and gives companies, investors and the government a market for securities.
- Stockbrokers act as agents for clients and earn brokerage; jobbers (market makers) deal on their own account and earn the spread.
- Bulls buy expecting prices to rise, bears sell expecting prices to fall, and stags buy new issues to sell quickly at a profit.
- Speculation adds activity to the market but can cause unstable prices and losses.
:::
