## Introduction

A supplier of building materials in Harare is asked by a new customer, a construction company, for 60 days' credit on a large order. Before agreeing, the supplier's credit manager asks one question: will this customer be able to pay its bills when they fall due? A business that cannot pay its short-term debts may be forced to close, even if it is profitable, so the supplier needs a way to measure this.

The ability to pay short-term debts is called **liquidity**. It is measured with two ratios from the statement of financial position: the **current ratio** and the **acid test ratio**. Cambridge 0264 prints both formulas in its syllabus and expects you to calculate them, interpret them and suggest how liquidity could be improved. (ZIMSEC uses the same ideas through working capital; the acid test is sometimes called the quick ratio.)

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Liquidity"}
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts (current liabilities) when they are due.
:::

:::definition{term="Current ratio"}
Current assets divided by current liabilities, written as x : 1.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid test ratio"}
Current assets minus inventory, divided by current liabilities, written as x : 1. Also called the quick ratio.
:::

:::definition{term="Liquid assets"}
Assets that are cash or can quickly be turned into cash, such as money in the bank and trade receivables.
:::

## The formulas

:::formula{name="Current ratio"}
Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities
:::

:::formula{name="Acid test ratio"}
Acid test ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities
:::

Both answers are written as a ratio to 1, usually to two decimal places, for example **1.75 : 1**.

### Why the acid test leaves out inventory

Inventory is the **least liquid** current asset. Goods must first be sold, and often the customer is given credit, before the business receives cash. Some inventory may be out of date or hard to sell. The acid test asks a stricter question: could the business pay its current liabilities **without** selling any inventory?

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating both ratios]
A wholesaler has inventory US\$10,000, trade receivables US\$8,000 and cash US\$6,000. Its current liabilities are US\$12,000.

1. Current assets = 10,000 + 8,000 + 6,000 = US\$24,000.
2. Current ratio = 24,000 ÷ 12,000 = **2.00 : 1**. It has US\$2 of current assets for every US\$1 it owes in the short term.
3. Acid test ratio = (24,000 − 10,000) ÷ 12,000 = 14,000 ÷ 12,000 = **1.17 : 1** (to two decimal places).
4. Even without selling any inventory, the wholesaler could pay all its current liabilities. Its liquidity is good.
:::

## Interpreting the ratios

As a rough guide:

| Ratio | Result | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current ratio | Below 1 : 1 | Current liabilities exceed current assets: serious risk of not paying debts |
| Current ratio | About 1.5 : 1 to 2 : 1 | Usually comfortable |
| Current ratio | Much above 2 : 1 | Safe, but too much may be tied up in inventory or idle cash that could earn a return |
| Acid test ratio | Below 1 : 1 | Depends on selling inventory to pay debts: a warning sign, unless inventory sells very quickly |
| Acid test ratio | About 1 : 1 | Can pay current liabilities from liquid assets |

These are guides, not rules. A supermarket that sells its inventory for cash every day can work safely with a low acid test, while a furniture maker whose stock sells slowly needs a higher one. Always **compare** with the same business in previous years and with similar businesses.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two businesses]
| | Business X | Business Y |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Current assets (US\$) | 30,000 | 18,000 |
| of which inventory (US\$) | 21,000 | 4,000 |
| Current liabilities (US\$) | 15,000 | 12,000 |

1. Business X: current ratio = 30,000 ÷ 15,000 = **2.00 : 1**; acid test = (30,000 − 21,000) ÷ 15,000 = 9,000 ÷ 15,000 = **0.60 : 1**.
2. Business Y: current ratio = 18,000 ÷ 12,000 = **1.50 : 1**; acid test = (18,000 − 4,000) ÷ 12,000 = 14,000 ÷ 12,000 = **1.17 : 1**.
3. X looks safer on the current ratio, but most of its current assets are inventory. Without selling stock it could pay only 60 cents of each dollar it owes. Y has fewer current assets but more of them are liquid, so Y is the more liquid business.
4. A supplier deciding whether to give credit should prefer Y, or ask X for a shorter credit period.
:::

## Causes of poor liquidity

- Too much cash tied up in **inventory** (slow-selling stock).
- Customers taking a long time to pay (high **trade receivables**).
- Heavy short-term borrowing, such as a large **overdraft**.
- Using cash or an overdraft to buy **non-current assets** instead of long-term finance.
- Losses that drain cash from the business.

## How to improve liquidity

| Action | Effect | Drawback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sell off excess inventory (clearance sale) | Turns stock into cash | Lower prices reduce profit |
| Collect trade receivables faster | More cash sooner | Customers may go elsewhere |
| Sell unused non-current assets | Raises cash | Asset may be needed later |
| Raise long-term finance (loan or shares) to repay the overdraft | Fewer current liabilities | Interest or loss of control |
| Delay buying non-current assets | Keeps cash in the business | Growth or efficiency may suffer |

Note: holding very large amounts of cash also has a cost. Idle cash earns little, so a very high current ratio can mean resources are not being used well.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing the ratio as a percentage"}
Liquidity ratios are written as x : 1, for example 1.17 : 1, not 117%.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to subtract inventory"}
The acid test always uses current assets **minus inventory**. If your acid test equals your current ratio, check this step.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'higher is always better'"}
A very high current ratio may mean too much stock or idle cash. Judge what is suitable for the type of business.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Calculate both, then compare"}
When given a statement of financial position, calculate both ratios and say what the difference between them shows about inventory.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link liquidity to decisions"}
Use the ratios to answer the question in the case: should the supplier give credit? Should the bank lend? Should the business buy a new asset with its cash?
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
The current ratio and acid test are Cambridge 0264 content (Topic 5). ZIMSEC learners meet liquidity through working capital (current assets − current liabilities).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Liquidity is the ability to pay short-term debts when they are due.
- Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities; acid test = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities; both written as x : 1.
- The acid test leaves out inventory because it is the least liquid current asset.
- Below 1 : 1 is a warning sign; very high ratios may mean idle resources. Compare with past years and similar businesses.
- Liquidity can be improved by selling stock, collecting debts faster, selling unused assets and replacing short-term borrowing with long-term finance.
:::
