## Introduction

Tafara Cement Ltd (a fictional company) needs a large sum to build a new kiln. It can invite people to become part-owners by buying **shares**, or it can borrow from investors by issuing **debentures**. Investors, in turn, choose between ordinary shares, which may earn high dividends but carry more risk, preference shares, which pay a fixed dividend first, and debentures, which pay fixed interest whether or not the company makes a profit.

This Form 3 ZIMSEC Commerce topic asks you to describe these securities, distinguish between them, explain the types of share capital, and calculate dividends and debenture interest.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Share"}
A unit of ownership in a limited company. Shareholders are owners and share in the profits through dividends.
:::

:::definition{term="Ordinary share (equity)"}
A share whose holder has voting rights and receives a dividend that varies with profits, paid only after preference shareholders have been paid.
:::

:::definition{term="Preference share"}
A share that receives a fixed rate of dividend before ordinary shareholders are paid, and is repaid before them if the company is wound up; holders usually have no vote.
:::

:::definition{term="Debenture"}
A long-term loan to a company, usually at a fixed rate of interest and for a fixed period, often secured on the company's assets. Debenture holders are creditors, not owners.
:::

:::definition{term="Dividend"}
The part of a company's profit paid to shareholders, usually stated as an amount per share or as a percentage of the nominal (face) value.
:::

## Ordinary shares

- Holders are the **real owners** and risk-bearers of the company.
- They have **voting rights** at the annual general meeting (one vote per share), so they elect the directors.
- Dividends are **not fixed**: high in good years, low or nil in bad years.
- If the company is wound up, they are paid **last**, after creditors, debenture holders and preference shareholders.

## Preference shares

- Dividend at a **fixed rate** (for example 8% of the nominal value), paid **before** ordinary dividends.
- Repaid **before** ordinary shareholders if the company is wound up.
- Usually **no voting rights** (except when their dividend is unpaid).

**Types of preference share:**

| Type | Feature |
| --- | --- |
| Cumulative | Unpaid dividends from a bad year are carried forward and paid later, before ordinary dividends |
| Non-cumulative | A missed dividend is lost |
| Participating | Receives the fixed dividend plus a share of extra profits |
| Redeemable | Can be bought back by the company at a future date |

## Debentures

- A debenture is a **loan**: holders are **creditors** of the company.
- **Fixed interest** must be paid every year, **whether or not** there is a profit.
- Repaid at the end of an agreed period.
- **Secured (mortgage) debentures** are backed by specific assets that can be sold if the company defaults; **unsecured (naked) debentures** have no specific security.
- Debenture holders have **no vote**, but they are repaid before all shareholders if the company is wound up.

## Comparing the securities

| Point | Ordinary shares | Preference shares | Debentures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Holder is | Owner | Owner | Creditor (lender) |
| Return | Variable dividend | Fixed-rate dividend | Fixed interest |
| Paid when there is a loss? | No | No (cumulative: carried forward) | Yes: interest must be paid |
| Voting rights | Yes | Usually no | No |
| Order of repayment on winding up | Last | Before ordinary shares | Before all shareholders |
| Risk to the investor | Highest | Medium | Lowest |
| Repaid by the company? | No | Only if redeemable | Yes, at the end of the term |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: dividing profit between preference and ordinary shareholders]
Tafara Cement Ltd has issued 50,000 ordinary shares of US\$1 each and 20,000 8% preference shares of US\$1 each. The directors decide that US\$9,600 of profit is available for dividends.

1. Preference dividend first: 8% of US\$20,000 = US\$1,600.
2. Amount left for ordinary shareholders: US\$9,600 − US\$1,600 = US\$8,000.
3. Ordinary dividend per share: US\$8,000 ÷ 50,000 = US\$0.16 (16 cents) per share.
4. As a percentage of the nominal value: 0.16 ÷ 1 × 100 = 16%.
5. In this good year the ordinary shareholders earn a higher rate (16%) than the preference shareholders (8%). In a poor year the order could be reversed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: debenture interest]
The company has issued US\$30,000 of 10% debentures.

1. Annual interest: 10% of US\$30,000 = US\$3,000.
2. The US\$3,000 must be paid every year even if the company makes a loss, because debenture holders are lenders.
3. Debenture interest is paid **before** any dividend can be declared.
:::

## Types of share capital

| Type of capital | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Authorised capital (maximum the company may issue) | 100,000 |
| Issued capital (shares actually issued) | 80,000 |
| Called-up capital (amount asked for so far) | 60,000 |
| Paid-up capital (amount received) | 55,000 |
| Calls in arrear (called up but not yet paid) | 5,000 |

- **Authorised (nominal or registered) capital:** the maximum share capital the company may issue, stated in its founding documents.
- **Issued capital:** the part of authorised capital actually issued to shareholders.
- **Called-up capital:** the part of issued capital that the company has asked shareholders to pay so far (shares can be paid for in instalments called "calls").
- **Paid-up capital:** the part of called-up capital actually received.
- **Uncalled capital:** issued capital not yet called up (here US\$80,000 − US\$60,000 = US\$20,000).
- **Calls in arrear:** called-up capital that shareholders have not yet paid (here US\$5,000).

So: authorised ≥ issued ≥ called-up ≥ paid-up.

### Shares and the stock exchange

The shares of a public limited company can be bought and sold on a stock exchange, so a shareholder who wants cash can sell to another investor; the company itself does not repay the money. A rise in the share price gives the holder a capital gain in addition to dividends.

## Why companies choose one or the other

- **Shares** bring permanent capital with no fixed charge, but new ordinary shares dilute control.
- **Debentures** keep control with existing shareholders, and interest is a business expense, but they add a fixed charge that must be paid in bad years.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling debenture holders owners"}
Debenture holders are **lenders** (creditors). Shareholders are owners.
:::

:::mistake{title="Paying ordinary dividends first"}
Preference dividends are paid **before** ordinary dividends. Always take the preference dividend off first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong base for a dividend percentage"}
A dividend percentage is normally calculated on the **nominal (face) value** of the shares, not on their market price.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Distinguish point by point"}
"Distinguish between shares and debentures" earns marks for each contrast: owner versus lender, dividend versus interest, vote versus no vote, paid in loss years or not, order of repayment.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Show the order in calculations"}
Write: profit available → less preference dividend → left for ordinary → per share → percentage.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ordinary shareholders own and control the company and receive a variable dividend, paid last.
- Preference shareholders receive a fixed dividend before ordinary shareholders and are repaid before them; they usually cannot vote.
- Debenture holders are lenders who receive fixed interest, even in loss years, and are repaid before shareholders.
- Authorised ≥ issued ≥ called-up ≥ paid-up capital; calls in arrear are called-up amounts not yet paid.
- To share out profit, deduct the preference dividend first and divide the rest among ordinary shares.
:::
