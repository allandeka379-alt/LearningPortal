## Introduction

Chipo owns a grocery shop in Masvingo. She buys maize meal, cooking oil and soap from a wholesaler and sells them at a higher price. At the end of the year she wants to know: How much did I sell? What did the goods I sold cost me? How much profit did I make on them? How quickly did my stock move off the shelves? Am I adding enough to my buying prices?

These questions are answered by the **business calculations** in the ZIMSEC Commerce syllabus (Form 4): turnover, cost of goods sold, gross and net profit, average stock, rate of stock turnover, and mark-up and margin. They appear regularly in Paper 2, usually as a short set of calculations from a trader's figures followed by a part asking what the results show. Learn each formula, set out your working and always state the unit (US\$, %, or times).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Turnover"}
The total value of sales in a period (net sales: sales minus any goods returned by customers).
:::

:::definition{term="Cost of goods sold (cost of sales)"}
The cost price of the goods actually sold during the period.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross profit"}
Turnover minus the cost of goods sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Net profit"}
Gross profit minus the expenses of running the business (rent, wages, electricity, transport and so on).
:::

:::definition{term="Rate of stock turnover"}
The number of times a trader sells and replaces its average stock in a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Mark-up"}
Gross profit expressed as a percentage of the cost price (cost of goods sold).
:::

:::definition{term="Margin"}
Gross profit expressed as a percentage of the selling price (turnover).
:::

## The formulas

:::formula{name="Cost of goods sold"}
Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock
:::

:::formula{name="Profit"}
Gross profit = turnover − cost of goods sold

Net profit = gross profit − expenses
:::

:::formula{name="Stock turnover"}
Average stock = (opening stock + closing stock) ÷ 2

Rate of stock turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average stock (answer in times)
:::

:::formula{name="Mark-up and margin"}
Mark-up = gross profit ÷ cost of goods sold × 100

Margin = gross profit ÷ turnover × 100
:::

Mark-up and margin use the **same** gross profit but divide by different figures. Because turnover is always larger than cost of goods sold (when there is a profit), the margin is always **smaller** than the mark-up.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a full set of calculations]
Chipo's figures for the year: opening stock US\$3,000; purchases US\$18,000; closing stock US\$5,000; turnover US\$20,000; expenses US\$2,600.

1. Cost of goods sold = 3,000 + 18,000 − 5,000 = US\$16,000.
2. Gross profit = 20,000 − 16,000 = US\$4,000.
3. Net profit = 4,000 − 2,600 = US\$1,400.
4. Average stock = (3,000 + 5,000) ÷ 2 = US\$4,000.
5. Rate of stock turnover = 16,000 ÷ 4,000 = **4 times** a year (about every three months).
6. Mark-up = 4,000 ÷ 16,000 × 100 = **25%**.
7. Margin = 4,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = **20%**.
:::

## Converting between mark-up and margin

If you know one, you can find the other without any money figures. Think of cost as 100:

- A mark-up of 25% means cost 100, profit 25, selling price 125. Margin = 25 ÷ 125 × 100 = 20%.
- A margin of 20% means selling price 100, profit 20, cost 80. Mark-up = 20 ÷ 80 × 100 = 25%.

:::formula{name="Converting"}
Margin = mark-up ÷ (100 + mark-up) × 100

Mark-up = margin ÷ (100 − margin) × 100
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: converting and pricing]
1. A trader adds a mark-up of 50%. Margin = 50 ÷ 150 × 100 = **33.3%** (to one decimal place).
2. Another trader works on a margin of 25%. Mark-up = 25 ÷ 75 × 100 = **33.3%**.
3. A radio costs a shop US\$80 and it adds a mark-up of 25%. Profit = 25% × 80 = US\$20; selling price = 80 + 20 = **US\$100**. Margin = 20 ÷ 100 × 100 = **20%**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: working back from turnover and margin]
A shop's turnover is US\$12,000 and it works on a margin of 30%.

1. Gross profit = 30% × 12,000 = US\$3,600.
2. Cost of goods sold = 12,000 − 3,600 = US\$8,400.
3. Mark-up = 3,600 ÷ 8,400 × 100 = 42.9% (to one decimal place).
:::

## Interpreting the results

### Rate of stock turnover

A **higher** rate means stock sells quickly. This is good: less money is tied up in stock, goods stay fresh and the trader earns gross profit more often. Fast-moving goods such as bread, milk and airtime have a very high rate; furniture, jewellery and hardware have a low rate. A trader's rate should be compared with **previous years** and with **similar shops**.

A falling rate may mean that the trader is buying too much stock, that some goods are out of fashion or poorly displayed, or that customers are going to competitors.

**Ways to improve the rate of stock turnover:** advertise and use sales promotions; reduce prices or hold a clearance sale for slow-moving goods; stock the goods customers want; buy smaller quantities more often; improve display and customer service.

### Gross and net profit, mark-up and margin

- A rise in turnover with no rise in gross profit means the trader is paying more for goods or charging less.
- A large gap between gross and net profit means the expenses are high; the trader should control rent, wages and other running costs.
- A low mark-up with a high rate of stock turnover can still produce a good profit (for example, a supermarket). A high mark-up with a low rate is typical of a jeweller.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Swapping mark-up and margin"}
Mark-up divides by **cost**; margin divides by **selling price**. If your margin is larger than your mark-up, you have them the wrong way round.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using turnover in the rate of stock turnover"}
Stock is valued at cost, so divide **cost of goods sold** (not turnover) by average stock.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using purchases as cost of goods sold"}
Purchases include goods still on the shelves. Adjust for opening and closing stock.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong units"}
Profits are in US\$, mark-up and margin are percentages, and the rate of stock turnover is a number of **times**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Write formula, substitution, answer"}
Each calculation usually carries a method mark. Write the formula, put the figures in, then give the answer with its unit.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use earlier answers"}
Many questions build step by step: cost of goods sold, then gross profit, then mark-up. If you make an error early, examiners usually give "own figure" marks for correct later working, so always show it.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
These calculations are in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 4, business calculations). Cambridge 0264 uses gross profit margin and profit margin (see the lesson on profitability ratios) but not mark-up or the rate of stock turnover.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cost of goods sold = opening stock + purchases − closing stock; gross profit = turnover − cost of goods sold; net profit = gross profit − expenses.
- Average stock = (opening + closing stock) ÷ 2; rate of stock turnover = cost of goods sold ÷ average stock (times).
- Mark-up = gross profit ÷ cost × 100; margin = gross profit ÷ selling price × 100; the margin is always smaller.
- Margin = mark-up ÷ (100 + mark-up) × 100; mark-up = margin ÷ (100 − margin) × 100.
- A higher rate of stock turnover means stock sells faster; compare results with past years and similar businesses.
:::
