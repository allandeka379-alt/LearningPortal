## Introduction

Thandeka is opening a tuck shop at a busy bus terminus in Gweru. She expects good sales, but in August she must also pay for a new fridge. Will she have enough cash in the bank every month to pay for stock, rent, wages and the fridge? A **cash flow forecast** answers this question before it becomes a crisis. It predicts the cash coming into and going out of the business month by month and shows the expected bank balance at the end of each month.

Cash flow forecasts are examined in Business Enterprise and Skills (Forms 2 and 3, cash budgets) and in Cambridge 0264 (5.2.1). ZIMSEC learners prepare simple cash budgets. **Cambridge does not assess constructing a forecast**, but candidates must amend, complete and interpret one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cash flow"}
The movement of cash into and out of a business over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash flow forecast (cash budget)"}
An estimate of the cash a business expects to receive and pay out over a future period, usually month by month, showing the expected cash balance at the end of each month.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash inflows"}
Cash received by the business, for example cash sales, payments from credit customers, owners' capital, loans received and the sale of assets.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash outflows"}
Cash paid out by the business, for example purchases of stock, wages, rent, electricity, loan repayments and purchases of equipment.
:::

:::definition{term="Net cash flow"}
Total cash inflows minus total cash outflows for a period. It can be positive or negative.
:::

:::formula{name="Cash flow forecast calculations"}
Net cash flow = total inflows − total outflows

Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow

Next month's opening balance = this month's closing balance
:::

## Why cash is important

- Cash pays **suppliers, wages, rent and bills**. Without it, a business cannot operate, even if it is profitable.
- Running out of cash damages relationships with suppliers and workers, and creditors may force the business to close.
- Banks and investors ask for a forecast before they lend or invest.

**Cash is not profit.** A sale on credit increases profit now but brings in cash only when the customer pays. Buying equipment uses cash now but is not an expense in full in this year's profit.

## Layout of a cash flow forecast

A forecast has three parts:

1. **Cash inflows** and their total.
2. **Cash outflows** and their total.
3. **Net cash flow**, **opening balance** and **closing balance**.

Negative figures (a net outflow or an overdraft) are shown in **brackets**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: completing a three-month forecast]
Thandeka opens her tuck shop with US\$400 in the bank and puts in US\$500 of her own capital in July.

| US\$ | July | August | September |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **Cash inflows** |  |  |  |
| Cash sales | 2,600 | 2,400 | 3,100 |
| Owner's capital | 500 | 0 | 0 |
| Total inflows | 3,100 | 2,400 | 3,100 |
| **Cash outflows** |  |  |  |
| Purchases of stock | 1,800 | 1,700 | 2,100 |
| Rent | 350 | 350 | 350 |
| Wages | 600 | 600 | 600 |
| New fridge | 0 | 900 | 0 |
| Total outflows | 2,750 | 3,550 | 3,050 |
| Net cash flow | 350 | (1,150) | 50 |
| Opening balance | 400 | 750 | (400) |
| Closing balance | 750 | (400) | (350) |

1. July: inflows 2,600 + 500 = 3,100; outflows 1,800 + 350 + 600 = 2,750; net cash flow 3,100 − 2,750 = 350; closing 400 + 350 = US\$750.
2. August: inflows 2,400; outflows 1,700 + 350 + 600 + 900 = 3,550; net cash flow (1,150); opening 750 (July's closing); closing 750 − 1,150 = (400).
3. September: inflows 3,100; outflows 3,050; net cash flow 50; opening (400); closing −400 + 50 = (350).
4. **Interpretation:** the fridge in August causes a cash shortage of US\$400, and the account is still overdrawn by US\$350 at the end of September. Thandeka should arrange an overdraft of at least US\$400 before August, or buy the fridge on hire purchase, or delay it.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: amending a forecast]
Thandeka now expects August cash sales to be 20% lower than forecast because schools are closed.

1. New August cash sales: 2,400 × 0.8 = US\$1,920 (a fall of US\$480).
2. New August net cash flow: 1,920 − 3,550 = (1,630).
3. New August closing balance: 750 − 1,630 = (880).
4. New September opening balance: (880); closing: −880 + 50 = (830).
5. A change in one month affects **every later** month's balances. Thandeka now needs an overdraft of about US\$900, more than double the earlier estimate.
:::

## Using a cash flow forecast

- **Plan ahead:** see when cash will be short, and arrange an overdraft or delay spending in time.
- **Support a loan application:** banks want to see how and when the loan will be repaid.
- **Control:** compare actual cash flows with the forecast each month and investigate differences.
- **Plan surpluses:** decide how to use spare cash, for example to repay loans or buy equipment.

## Interpreting a forecast

When asked to interpret a forecast, look for:

- months with a **negative net cash flow** and why (a large one-off payment, seasonal sales);
- months with a **negative closing balance** (the business needs an overdraft or other finance);
- whether the business **recovers** in later months;
- the **largest** shortage, which shows how much short-term finance is needed.

## Limitations of forecasts

Forecasts are only estimates. Sales may be lower than expected, costs may rise (for example fuel or electricity prices), customers may pay late, and unexpected events (a breakdown, a new competitor) can change the figures. A forecast is most useful when it is **updated** regularly.

## Ways to improve a forecast cash position (outline)

Increase inflows (sell slow stock, collect debts faster, owner adds capital) or reduce and delay outflows (delay buying equipment, lease instead of buying, ask suppliers for longer credit), or arrange short-term finance such as an overdraft. Cash flow problems and their solutions are developed further in a separate lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to carry the closing balance forward"}
Each month's opening balance **must** equal the previous month's closing balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding a negative balance as if it were positive"}
An opening balance of (400) is minus 400. Closing = −400 + 50 = (350), not 450.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including non-cash items or credit sales as cash"}
Only cash actually received or paid in that month appears. Credit sales appear in the month the customer **pays**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show every step when completing a forecast"}
Write the net cash flow, then opening + net = closing, month by month. One error early on is followed through by examiners if your method is clear.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Interpret with figures"}
Quote the month and the amount: "In August the closing balance is (400), so Thandeka needs an overdraft of at least US\$400."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
Business Enterprise and Skills learners may be asked to prepare a simple cash budget from given information. Cambridge 0264 candidates amend, complete and interpret forecasts but are **not** asked to construct one from scratch.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A cash flow forecast estimates cash inflows and outflows month by month and the expected closing balance.
- Net cash flow = total inflows − total outflows; closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow; it becomes next month's opening balance.
- Negative figures are shown in brackets; a negative closing balance means an overdraft or other finance is needed.
- Forecasts help plan finance, support loan applications and control cash, but are only estimates and must be updated.
- Changing one month's figure changes every later month's balances.
:::
