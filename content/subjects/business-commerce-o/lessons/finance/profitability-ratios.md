## Introduction

Two transport businesses in Bulawayo each made a profit of US\$15,000 last year. Which performed better? You cannot tell from the profit figure alone. One may have sold US\$50,000 of services and the other US\$300,000; one may have needed US\$40,000 of capital and the other US\$250,000. **Profitability ratios** compare profit with the sales that produced it, or with the capital used to earn it, so that businesses of different sizes, or the same business in different years, can be compared fairly.

You need three ratios: the **gross profit margin**, the **profit margin** (ZIMSEC: net profit margin) and the **return on capital employed (ROCE)**. Cambridge 0264 prints these formulas in its syllabus and expects you to calculate and interpret them. ZIMSEC Commerce includes gross and net profit and ROCE among its Form 4 business calculations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Profitability"}
How well a business turns its sales or its capital into profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross profit margin"}
Gross profit as a percentage of revenue: the share of each dollar of sales left after paying for the goods sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit margin (net profit margin)"}
Profit as a percentage of revenue: the share of each dollar of sales left after all costs.
:::

:::definition{term="Return on capital employed (ROCE)"}
Profit as a percentage of the capital employed: the return earned on the long-term finance invested in the business.
:::

## The formulas

:::formula{name="Gross profit margin"}
Gross profit margin (%) = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100
:::

:::formula{name="Profit margin"}
Profit margin (%) = profit ÷ revenue × 100
:::

:::formula{name="Return on capital employed"}
ROCE (%) = profit ÷ capital employed × 100
:::

Capital employed is the equity plus non-current liabilities (for a sole trader, often simply the owner's capital). Profit here means profit for the year (net profit), before dividends are paid.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating the three ratios]
Chinhoyi Feeds Ltd: revenue US\$120,000; gross profit US\$48,000; profit for the year US\$18,000; capital employed US\$150,000.

1. Gross profit margin = 48,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **40%**. For every US\$1 of sales, 40 cents is left after paying for the goods sold.
2. Profit margin = 18,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = **15%**. After all costs, 15 cents of every US\$1 of sales is profit.
3. ROCE = 18,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **12%**. Each US\$100 of capital employed earned US\$12 of profit.
:::

## Interpreting the ratios

**Higher is better** for all three, but a ratio means little on its own. Compare it with:

- the same business in **previous years** (is performance improving?);
- **competitors** in the same industry (margins differ greatly between industries);
- for ROCE, the **interest rate** on savings or loans (if a business earns 6% while a bank pays 8% on deposits, the owners could do better elsewhere).

### What changes in the gross profit margin tell you

The gross profit margin falls when the cost of sales rises faster than revenue: suppliers have raised prices, or the business has cut its selling prices. It rises when the business finds cheaper supplies, buys in bulk or raises its prices.

### What the gap between the two margins tells you

The difference between the gross profit margin and the profit margin is caused by **expenses**. If the gross profit margin is steady but the profit margin falls, expenses such as rent, wages or advertising are rising faster than sales.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two years]
| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue (US\$) | 120,000 | 150,000 |
| Gross profit (US\$) | 48,000 | 54,000 |
| Profit (US\$) | 18,000 | 15,000 |
| Capital employed (US\$) | 150,000 | 160,000 |

1. Gross profit margin: Year 1 = 40%; Year 2 = 54,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **36%**. It fell, so the cost of goods sold rose faster than revenue (or prices were cut to win the extra sales).
2. Profit margin: Year 1 = 15%; Year 2 = 15,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = **10%**. It fell more than the gross margin, so expenses also rose faster than sales.
3. ROCE: Year 1 = 12%; Year 2 = 15,000 ÷ 160,000 × 100 = 9.375% = **9.4%** (to one decimal place). More capital earned less profit.
4. Conclusion: although revenue grew by US\$30,000, profitability **worsened** on every measure. Managers should review supplier prices, pricing and expenses.
:::

## How a business can improve its profitability

| Action | Ratio mainly affected | Possible drawback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise selling prices | Gross and profit margins | Sales may fall if customers switch to rivals |
| Find cheaper suppliers or buy in bulk | Gross margin | Quality may fall; more cash tied up in stock |
| Cut expenses (rent, energy, advertising) | Profit margin, ROCE | Lower advertising may reduce sales |
| Sell unused non-current assets | ROCE (lower capital employed) | Assets may be needed later |
| Improve efficiency (less waste, better methods) | All three | May need investment and training first |

## Limits of profitability ratios

Ratios are based on past accounts, ignore non-financial factors such as customer loyalty, and are only useful when compared with suitable figures. Two businesses may also calculate figures differently. The lesson on users of accounts looks at these limitations in more detail.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the wrong figure"}
Both margins divide by **revenue**; ROCE divides by **capital employed**. Never divide a margin by cost of sales (that would be a mark-up).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a ratio rose when the profit rose"}
Profit can rise while the margin falls, if revenue rose even faster. Always calculate the ratio before commenting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the answer without %"}
All three ratios are percentages. State the rounding the question asks for (for example, to one decimal place).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Calculate, compare, explain, recommend"}
A full answer calculates the ratio, compares it with another year or business, explains a likely cause from the case and recommends an action.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the printed formulas"}
Cambridge 0264 prints the formulas for gross profit margin, profit margin and ROCE; you still need to know which figures to use and how to interpret the results.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board terms"}
ZIMSEC questions say **net profit** and **net profit margin**; Cambridge says **profit** and **profit margin**. They are the same measures.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100; profit margin = profit ÷ revenue × 100; ROCE = profit ÷ capital employed × 100.
- Higher ratios mean better profitability, but compare with previous years, competitors and interest rates.
- A falling gross margin points to higher cost of sales or lower prices; a falling profit margin with a steady gross margin points to rising expenses.
- Profitability can be improved by raising prices, cutting costs, using assets better and improving efficiency, each with drawbacks.
:::
