## Introduction

Rudo wants to open a dressmaking workshop in Gweru. Before she sells a single dress she must buy sewing machines, fabric and thread, pay a deposit on a workshop, and keep some cash to pay bills until customers start paying her. Five years later, if the business does well, she will want a bigger workshop and more machines. At every stage the business needs **finance**: money to pay for assets and activities before the income from them arrives.

This topic is the starting point of business finance in all three syllabi: ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3, business finance), Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 1, the need for finance) and Cambridge 0264 (5.1.1, the need for business finance). Questions ask you to identify and explain the reasons a business needs finance and to classify needs as short-term or long-term.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Finance"}
Money that a business obtains to pay for its activities and assets.
:::

:::definition{term="Start-up capital"}
The finance needed to set up a new business: to buy its first non-current assets and inventory and to pay its first expenses.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital expenditure"}
Spending on non-current assets that will be used in the business for more than one year, such as buildings, machinery and vehicles.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue expenditure"}
Spending on the day-to-day running of the business, such as wages, rent, electricity and raw materials.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital"}
The finance available for day-to-day spending: current assets minus current liabilities.
:::

## Reasons why businesses need finance

### 1. Starting a business (start-up capital)

A new business needs money for premises (purchase or rent deposit), equipment, an opening stock of goods or materials, registration and licences, and a cash reserve for the first months, when sales are low and customers may not yet have paid.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: estimating start-up capital]
Rudo lists what she needs to open her workshop.

| Start-up need | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Equipment (two sewing machines and a table) | 2,400 |
| Opening stock of fabric and thread | 900 |
| Rent deposit for a workshop | 600 |
| Cash reserve for the first months | 300 |
| Total start-up capital | 4,200 |

1. Add the items: 2,400 + 900 + 600 + 300 = US\$4,200.
2. The equipment (US\$2,400) is a **long-term** need: it will be used for years.
3. The stock and the cash reserve (US\$900 + US\$300 = US\$1,200) are **short-term** needs: they are used up and replaced within months.
:::

### 2. Expansion (growth)

A growing business needs finance to buy larger premises, more machinery and vehicles, open new branches, take over another business, or enter new markets. Expansion normally needs large sums for long periods.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: finance needed for expansion]
Tatenda's bakery in Masvingo wants a second oven costing US\$3,500. He has US\$1,500 of retained profit available.

1. Finance needed from outside the business: US\$3,500 − US\$1,500 = US\$2,000.
2. Because the oven will last many years, this is a long-term need, so Tatenda should look for a long-term source (for example a bank loan or hire purchase), not an overdraft.
:::

### 3. Replacing non-current assets

Machinery, vehicles and computers wear out or become out of date (depreciation). Businesses need finance to replace them, otherwise output and quality fall.

### 4. New technology and research and development

Buying new technology (for example computerised tills, solar power, online ordering systems) or developing new products requires finance before any benefit is received.

### 5. Working capital (day-to-day running costs)

Even an established business needs money to buy inventory, pay wages, rent and electricity, and wait for credit customers to pay. A shortage of working capital can force a profitable business to close because it cannot pay its bills.

### 6. Other reasons

Overcoming a temporary cash shortage (for example a seasonal fall in sales); paying for unexpected costs such as repairs; meeting the cost of a new legal requirement.

## Short-term and long-term finance needs

| Short-term needs (usually repaid within one year) | Long-term needs (more than about five years) |
| --- | --- |
| Buying inventory | Buying land and buildings |
| Paying wages and bills while waiting for customers to pay | Buying machinery and vehicles |
| Covering a seasonal cash shortage | Expansion: new branches, takeovers |
| Small, unexpected expenses | Major new technology |

Some textbooks also use **medium-term** (one to five years) for items such as vehicles and computers.

**Matching rule:** long-term needs should be financed by long-term sources (share capital, long-term loans, debentures, retained profit) and short-term needs by short-term sources (overdrafts, trade credit). Using an overdraft to buy a building is risky because the bank can demand repayment at short notice.

### Long-term and short-term capital (ZIMSEC Commerce)

ZIMSEC Commerce refers to **long-term capital** (also called fixed or permanent capital), which is invested in non-current assets and stays in the business for many years, and **short-term capital**, which finances current assets and day-to-day operations and is turned over quickly. Owners' capital, long-term loans and debentures are long-term capital; bank overdrafts and trade credit are short-term capital.

:::example{title="A farmer's finance needs through the year"}
Chipo grows tomatoes near Chinhoyi. Each September she needs short-term finance for seed, fertiliser and casual labour, which is repaid when she sells her crop in December. She is also planning a borehole and drip irrigation, a long-term need, because the equipment will serve the farm for many years. Using one overdraft for both would leave her exposed if the bank reduced her limit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing finance with profit"}
A profitable business can still need finance, for example to expand or to pay suppliers before customers pay. Finance is about timing as well as amounts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling inventory a long-term need"}
Inventory is sold and replaced within months, so it is a short-term (working capital) need, even if the business always holds some.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mismatching need and source"}
Do not recommend an overdraft for a new building or share capital for a week's wages. Match the length of the need to the length of the finance.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify and explain"}
Cambridge questions often say "Identify two reasons why X needs finance" (1 mark each) and then "Explain…" (development and application to the business). Use the case details: name the asset or activity.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Classify with a reason"}
When you classify a need as short-term or long-term, give the reason: how long the asset or spending lasts.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
All three syllabi cover this concept. ZIMSEC Commerce uses the terms long-term and short-term capital; Cambridge 0264 asks you to explain why businesses need finance and to distinguish short-term from long-term needs, and links this to working capital.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses need finance to start up, expand, replace non-current assets, buy new technology and fund working capital.
- Capital expenditure buys non-current assets; revenue expenditure pays day-to-day costs.
- Short-term needs (inventory, wages, seasonal shortages) are repaid within a year; long-term needs (buildings, machinery, expansion) last many years.
- Match long-term needs with long-term finance and short-term needs with short-term finance.
- Estimating the finance needed means listing and adding each need and subtracting the funds already available.
:::
