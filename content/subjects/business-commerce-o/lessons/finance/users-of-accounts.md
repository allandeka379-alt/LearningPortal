## Introduction

When Masvingo Dairies Ltd publishes its accounts, many different people read them. The managers want to see which products made money. A bank deciding on a loan studies its debts and liquidity. A farmer who supplies milk on credit wants to know if she will be paid. A retired teacher who owns some shares wants to know whether dividends will rise. The workers' representatives want to know whether the company can afford a pay rise. The same statement of profit or loss and statement of financial position answer different questions for each of them.

Cambridge 0264 asks you to identify these **users of accounts**, explain how they use the accounts and ratio results to make decisions, and explain the **limitations** of accounts and ratio analysis. Questions are often applied: "Explain how the bank could use the ratios you calculated to decide whether to lend to the company."

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Users of accounts"}
The individuals and groups who read a business's financial statements to make decisions.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal users"}
People inside the business, mainly managers, directors and employees.
:::

:::definition{term="External users"}
People and organisations outside the business, such as shareholders, banks and other lenders, suppliers, customers, government and competitors.
:::

:::definition{term="Ratio analysis"}
Using ratios calculated from the accounts, such as margins, ROCE, the current ratio and the acid test, to judge performance and compare businesses or years.
:::

## The users and their decisions

| User | What they want to know | Ratios and figures they use | Decisions they make |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Managers and directors | Which activities are profitable; whether targets are met; whether the business can pay its debts | All ratios; comparisons with budgets and past years | Set prices, control costs, plan expansion, decide how to finance growth |
| Shareholders (current and potential) | Is the business profitable and growing? Will dividends rise? Is it safe? | Profit, profit margin, ROCE | Whether to buy, keep or sell shares |
| Banks and other lenders | Can the business repay the loan and interest? Has it already borrowed a lot? | Liquidity ratios, profit, non-current liabilities, assets for security | Whether to lend, how much, at what rate, with what security |
| Suppliers (trade payables) | Will they be paid on time? | Current ratio, acid test | Whether to give credit, and for how long |
| Customers | Will the business survive to supply goods, honour guarantees and provide spare parts? | Profit and liquidity | Whether to place large or long-term orders |
| Employees and trade unions | Is the job secure? Can the business afford a pay rise? | Profit, profit margin | Whether to stay; how much pay to ask for |
| Government and tax authority | How much tax is due; is the business obeying the law? | Profit | Tax assessment; support for an industry |
| Competitors | How well is the rival performing? | Margins, ROCE | Pricing and strategy |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a bank uses the ratios]
Kopje Motors Ltd asks a bank for a three-year loan. The bank calculates: profit margin 9% (last year 11%); ROCE 14%; current ratio 1.60 : 1; acid test 0.70 : 1. Assume the loan interest rate is 12%.

1. **Profitability:** the profit margin has fallen, but ROCE of 14% is above the assumed interest rate of 12%, so money invested in the business has been earning more than the loan will cost.
2. **Liquidity:** the current ratio is comfortable, but the acid test of 0.70 : 1 shows the company depends on selling vehicles and parts (inventory) to pay its short-term debts.
3. **Decision:** the bank may lend, but ask for security (for example, the company's premises), set a limit on the amount, or ask the company to improve its acid test first.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a supplier decides on credit]
A supplier of fabric is asked for 60 days' credit by two clothing factories. Factory A has a current ratio of 0.90 : 1 and an acid test of 0.40 : 1. Factory B has a current ratio of 1.80 : 1 and an acid test of 1.10 : 1.

1. Factory A's current liabilities are greater than its current assets, and without inventory it could pay only 40 cents of each dollar owed.
2. Factory B could pay all of its current liabilities from its liquid assets.
3. Decision: give B the 60 days' credit; offer A a shorter period, a lower credit limit or cash on delivery.
:::

## Limitations of accounts

- **Historical:** accounts show what happened last year. Conditions may now be different (a new competitor, higher costs, a change in the exchange rate).
- **Snapshot:** the statement of financial position shows one date only; liquidity may be much worse a month later.
- **Non-financial factors are missing:** the skill and morale of employees, the quality of the product, customer loyalty, the reputation of the brand and the effect on the environment do not appear.
- **Estimates and valuations:** the value of inventory and of non-current assets involves judgement.
- **Limited detail:** published accounts of a large company combine many products and branches, so a user cannot see which parts are doing well.

## Limitations of ratio analysis

- A ratio on its own means little; it must be **compared** with past years, competitors or the industry.
- Comparisons between businesses are difficult when they are of **different sizes**, in **different industries** or use **different accounting methods**.
- Ratios show **what** has changed, not **why**; the user must look for causes.
- Inflation can make figures from different years hard to compare.
- Ratios are based on past data and cannot **predict** the future with certainty.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing users without decisions"}
"Banks use accounts" earns little. Say what the bank looks at and what it decides: "a bank checks the acid test and existing loans to decide whether the company can repay a new loan".
:::

:::mistake{title="Matching the wrong ratio to the user"}
Suppliers giving credit care most about **liquidity**; shareholders care most about **profitability** and returns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a limitation 'ratios are hard to calculate'"}
Limitations are about what ratios cannot show: causes, non-financial factors, the future, and fair comparisons.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Apply to the user named"}
If the question names a user (a bank, a supplier, an investor), use the ratios in the case and finish with the decision that user should make.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Balance the evidence"}
Evaluation answers should use more than one ratio and recognise a limitation, for example "the accounts are a year old, so the bank should also ask for a cash flow forecast".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
Users of accounts and the limits of ratio analysis are Cambridge 0264 content (Topic 5). ZIMSEC questions on accounts focus on preparing and calculating rather than on users.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Internal users (managers, employees) and external users (shareholders, lenders, suppliers, customers, government, competitors) read accounts for different reasons.
- Shareholders focus on profitability and returns; lenders and suppliers on liquidity and ability to pay; managers on all ratios to make decisions.
- Accounts are historical, are snapshots, involve estimates and leave out non-financial factors.
- Ratios must be compared, show what changed but not why, are hard to compare between different businesses, and cannot predict the future.
:::
