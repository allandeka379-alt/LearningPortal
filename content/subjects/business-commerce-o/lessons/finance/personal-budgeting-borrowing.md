## Introduction

Nyasha works at a supermarket in Bulawayo and also sells snacks from a small tuck shop at weekends. By the third week of every month her money has run out, and she borrows from a neighbour who charges heavy interest. Nyasha's problem is not only how much she earns but how she plans and controls her spending. A personal budget, sensible borrowing and good advice could change her situation.

This Form 2 ZIMSEC Commerce topic asks you to prepare a simple personal budget, explain reasons for borrowing and when not to borrow, compare borrowing options, name sources of financial advice and explain the consequences of mismanaging money.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Personal budget"}
A plan of a person's or household's expected income and expenditure for a future period, such as a month.
:::

:::definition{term="Surplus"}
The amount by which income is greater than expenditure; it can be saved.
:::

:::definition{term="Deficit"}
The amount by which expenditure is greater than income; it must be covered by savings or borrowing, or spending must be cut.
:::

:::definition{term="Borrowing"}
Receiving money now that must be repaid later, usually with interest.
:::

:::definition{term="Collateral (security)"}
An asset a borrower pledges to the lender, which the lender may sell if the loan is not repaid.
:::

## Drawing up a personal budget

Steps:

1. List all expected **income** for the period (wages, profit, rent, allowances).
2. List all planned **expenditure**: first essential needs (rent, food, transport, school fees, utilities), then wants.
3. Total each side and find the **surplus or deficit**.
4. If there is a deficit, cut wants, look for extra income or plan to use savings. If there is a surplus, save it.
5. During the month, record **actual** spending and compare it with the plan.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Nyasha's monthly budget]
Nyasha expects wages of US\$420 and profit from her tuck shop of US\$80.

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| **Income** |  |
| Wages | 420 |
| Tuck-shop sales profit | 80 |
| Total income | 500 |
| **Expenditure** |  |
| Rent | 150 |
| Food | 140 |
| Transport | 60 |
| School fees | 70 |
| Electricity and water | 35 |
| Airtime and data | 25 |
| Total expenditure | 480 |
| Surplus (available to save) | 20 |

1. Total income = 420 + 80 = US\$500.
2. Total expenditure = 150 + 140 + 60 + 70 + 35 + 25 = US\$480.
3. Surplus = 500 − 480 = US\$20, which Nyasha can save for emergencies instead of borrowing.
:::

### Needs and wants

A good budget separates **needs** (essentials such as food, rent, school fees, transport to work and medical care) from **wants** (things that are pleasant but not essential, such as new clothes for an event, takeaway meals or a more expensive phone). When money is short, wants are cut first. Planned saving should also be treated as a priority, not as whatever happens to be left at the end of the month: many people "pay themselves first" by setting aside a fixed amount as soon as income arrives.

**Benefits of a personal budget:** spending is planned and controlled; essential needs are paid first; saving becomes possible; shortages are seen in advance; unnecessary borrowing is avoided.

## Borrowing

### Good reasons for borrowing

- To buy an **asset that lasts or earns income**: a house, a sewing machine, stock for a business.
- To meet a genuine **emergency** such as hospital bills when there are no savings.
- To pay for **education** that will raise future income.

### When not to borrow

- To pay for everyday consumption, such as groceries, month after month.
- When the repayments cannot be met from expected income.
- To repay another loan (this leads to a debt trap).
- When the interest charged is very high, as with some informal lenders.
- For impulse purchases of luxuries.

### Borrowing options

| Option | Features | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bank overdraft | Current-account holder spends more than the balance, up to a limit | Flexible; interest only on the amount overdrawn | Interest rate high; bank can demand repayment |
| Bank (personal) loan | Fixed sum repaid in instalments over an agreed period | Planned repayments; larger sums | Needs proof of income, often collateral; interest on the whole loan |
| Trade credit (store account) | Buy now from a shop or supplier, pay at month end | Often no interest if paid on time | Temptation to overspend; account can be closed |
| Hire purchase | Deposit, then instalments; own after last payment | Use goods while paying | Costs more than cash price; repossession |
| Microfinance institution | Small loans to people with little collateral | Reaches the informal sector | Interest rates may be high |
| Informal lenders, family and friends | Money lent privately | Quick; few formalities | Informal lenders may charge very high interest; disputes can damage relationships |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the cost of a bank loan]
Tatenda borrows US\$1,200 from a bank for one year. Assume the bank charges flat interest of 15% on the amount borrowed, and the total is repaid in 12 equal monthly instalments.

1. Interest: 15% of US\$1,200 = US\$180.
2. Total to repay: US\$1,200 + US\$180 = US\$1,380.
3. Monthly instalment: US\$1,380 ÷ 12 = US\$115.
4. Tatenda should borrow only if US\$115 a month fits into his budget after essential spending.
:::

## Sources of financial advice

- **Banks and building societies**: advice on accounts, loans and savings products.
- **Microfinance institutions** and small-enterprise finance bodies: advice for small traders.
- **Insurance companies and brokers**: advice on cover and pension plans.
- **Accountants and financial advisers**: advice on tax, investment and business finance.
- **Stockbrokers**: advice on buying and selling shares.
- **Consumer organisations**, such as the Consumer Council of Zimbabwe: advice on credit agreements and consumers' rights.
- **Family, teachers and trusted community members**, and newspapers and reliable websites.

Advice from a person who earns commission from selling a product may not be impartial; check more than one source.

## Consequences of financial mismanagement

| For the individual | For the family and community |
| --- | --- |
| Debt problems and high interest charges | Children's school fees unpaid |
| Assets repossessed or sold | Stress, conflict and broken relationships |
| Poor credit record, so future loans refused | Reliance on relatives for support |
| Legal action by creditors | Loss of savings needed for emergencies |
| No savings for emergencies or retirement | Lower standard of living |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting savings in the expenditure total without explanation"}
Savings are what is left over, not a cost. If a budget shows a savings line, label it clearly as planned saving.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying borrowing is always bad"}
Borrowing to buy a productive asset or to pay for education can be sensible if the repayments are affordable. Explain the conditions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing loans only by the amount borrowed"}
Compare the **total repayment** and the **monthly instalment** against income, not just the sum received.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Lay out budgets clearly"}
Use headings for income and expenditure, total each, and label the final line "surplus" or "deficit". A deficit can be shown in brackets.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Advise in context"}
When asked to advise a person, use the figures given: can the instalment be paid from the surplus? Recommend and justify.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
Personal budgets and borrowing are in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2). Business Enterprise and Skills and Cambridge 0264 examine business budgets and finance instead.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A personal budget plans income and expenditure, shows a surplus or deficit and helps control spending.
- Borrow for assets, emergencies or education when repayments are affordable; avoid borrowing for everyday spending or at very high interest.
- Options include overdrafts, bank loans, trade credit, hire purchase, microfinance and informal lenders.
- Advice can come from banks, financial advisers, insurers, stockbrokers and consumer organisations.
- Mismanaging money leads to debt, repossession, a poor credit record and hardship for the family.
:::
