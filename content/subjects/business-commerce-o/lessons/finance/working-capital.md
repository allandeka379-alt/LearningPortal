## Introduction

Nyasha's hardware shop in Mutare is profitable: customers like her prices and sales are rising. Yet last month she could not pay a supplier on time because her cash was tied up in cement and roofing sheets on the shelves and in money owed by builders who buy on credit. Nyasha has a **working capital** problem. Working capital is the money available to run the business from day to day, and managing it well is as important as making a profit.

Working capital is in all three syllabi: ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 4, business calculations), Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) and Cambridge 0264 (5.1.1, the concept and importance of working capital). The formula is printed in the Cambridge syllabus.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Current assets"}
Assets that are expected to be turned into cash within one year: inventory (stock), trade receivables (debtors) and cash at bank and in hand.
:::

:::definition{term="Current liabilities"}
Debts that must be paid within one year: trade payables (creditors), bank overdraft, and short-term loans and other amounts due soon.
:::

:::definition{term="Working capital"}
The finance available for day-to-day spending: current assets minus current liabilities.
:::

:::definition{term="Liquidity"}
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts as they fall due.
:::

:::formula{name="Working capital"}
Working capital = current assets − current liabilities
:::

## Calculating working capital

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Nyasha Hardware]
| | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| **Current assets** |  |  |
| Inventory (stock) | 6,500 |  |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | 3,200 |  |
| Cash and bank | 1,300 |  |
| Total current assets |  | 11,000 |
| **Current liabilities** |  |  |
| Trade payables (creditors) | 4,800 |  |
| Bank overdraft | 1,200 |  |
| Total current liabilities |  | 6,000 |
| Working capital |  | 5,000 |

1. Current assets = 6,500 + 3,200 + 1,300 = US\$11,000.
2. Current liabilities = 4,800 + 1,200 = US\$6,000.
3. Working capital = 11,000 − 6,000 = US\$5,000.
4. Nyasha has enough to cover her short-term debts on paper, but only US\$1,300 is cash; most of her working capital is in stock and debtors, which explains her difficulty in paying the supplier.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: negative working capital and a long-term loan]
A transport business has current assets of US\$7,400 and current liabilities of US\$8,100.

1. Working capital = 7,400 − 8,100 = −US\$700, shown as (700). The business does not have enough current assets to pay its current liabilities: it has a liquidity problem.
2. The owner obtains a **three-year** bank loan of US\$2,000, paid into the bank account. Cash (a current asset) rises by US\$2,000; the loan is not a current liability because it is repaid over three years.
3. New working capital = (7,400 + 2,000) − 8,100 = US\$1,300.
4. Note: paying a creditor in cash would **not** change working capital (cash and creditors both fall by the same amount); neither would buying stock on credit (stock and creditors both rise).
:::

## The working capital cycle

Working capital flows round the business in a cycle:

1. **Cash** is used to buy raw materials or goods (or they are bought on credit from suppliers).
2. The goods become **inventory** (and, in a factory, work in progress and finished goods).
3. Inventory is **sold**, often on credit, creating **trade receivables** (debtors).
4. Debtors **pay**, and the business has **cash** again to pay suppliers, wages and other costs.

The longer the cycle, the more working capital the business needs. A supermarket that sells for cash and pays suppliers after 30 days needs little working capital; a furniture maker that holds timber for months and sells on credit needs a lot.

## Why working capital matters

**Too little working capital** (poor liquidity):

- cannot pay suppliers on time, so loses discounts, credit and goodwill;
- cannot pay wages, rent or electricity; staff and landlords lose patience;
- cannot buy stock to meet orders, so loses sales;
- may have to borrow at high cost or sell assets cheaply;
- in the worst case, creditors force the business to close even if it is profitable.

**Too much working capital** is also wasteful:

- cash lying idle earns little or nothing;
- large inventory risks theft, damage and going out of date, and costs money to store;
- large debtors increase the risk of bad debts;
- the money could be used to expand or repay loans.

### Working capital in different businesses

The amount of working capital a business needs depends on its type. A market vendor selling fresh vegetables for cash needs very little, because stock is sold daily. A builders' merchant that holds large stocks and sells on credit needs much more. A seasonal business, such as a shop selling school uniforms, needs extra working capital before the start of each school term.

## Managing working capital

| Area | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| Inventory | Hold only the stock needed; order little and often; sell slow-moving stock at a discount; use stock records |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | Check customers before giving credit; shorten credit periods; offer cash discounts for prompt payment; chase late payers; use debt factoring |
| Trade payables (creditors) | Negotiate longer credit periods with suppliers, without damaging relationships |
| Cash | Prepare a cash flow forecast (cash budget); arrange an overdraft in advance for short gaps; keep a cash reserve |
| Long-term finance | Use long-term loans or capital, not the overdraft, for non-current assets so that cash is not drained |

:::example{title="Improving working capital at a hardware shop"}
Nyasha reduces her stock of slow-selling paint by holding a sale, asks builders to pay within 30 days instead of 60 and offers a small discount to those who pay in cash. She also negotiates 45 days' credit with her main cement supplier. Within two months she has more cash in the bank and pays suppliers on time, without any fall in sales.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing working capital with profit"}
A profitable business can have too little working capital, and a loss-making one can temporarily have plenty. Working capital is about **liquidity**, not profitability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including non-current assets or long-term loans"}
Only **current** assets and **current** liabilities are used. Buildings, vehicles and long-term loans are left out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking more is always better"}
Too much working capital means money is idle in stock, debtors or cash instead of earning a return.
:::

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain importance in context"}

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities; it measures the finance available for day-to-day spending.
- Current assets: inventory, trade receivables, cash. Current liabilities: trade payables, overdraft, short-term debts.
- The working capital cycle runs cash → inventory → sales and debtors → cash.
- Too little working capital threatens survival; too much leaves money idle.
- Manage it by controlling stock and debtors, negotiating credit from suppliers, forecasting cash and using long-term finance for long-term assets.
:::
