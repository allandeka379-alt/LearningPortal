## Introduction

Gweru Engineering Ltd wants to borrow money from a bank to buy a new lathe. The bank manager asks for its **statement of financial position**. In one table she can see what the company owns (its premises, machines, inventory and cash), what it owes (to suppliers and to other lenders) and how much belongs to its shareholders. From this she judges whether the company can afford another loan and whether it has assets that could be offered as security.

The statement of financial position (once called the balance sheet) is a "snapshot" of a business on one date, usually the last day of its financial year. Cambridge 0264 candidates must understand its main elements, make simple calculations from it and interpret it. ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills candidates (Forms 2 and 3) also **prepare** a simple statement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Statement of financial position"}
A financial statement showing the assets, liabilities and equity (capital) of a business on a particular date.
:::

:::definition{term="Asset"}
Something owned by the business that has value. **Non-current assets** are kept for more than one year (premises, machinery, vehicles); **current assets** are expected to be turned into cash within a year (inventory, trade receivables, cash).
:::

:::definition{term="Liability"}
A debt the business owes. **Current liabilities** must be paid within a year (trade payables, overdraft); **non-current liabilities** are due after more than a year (long-term bank loans, debentures).
:::

:::definition{term="Equity (capital)"}
The owners' investment in the business: share capital plus retained profit for a company, or the owner's capital for a sole trader.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital employed"}
The total long-term finance used by the business: equity plus non-current liabilities (equal to total assets minus current liabilities).
:::

## The elements of the statement

| Element | Examples | ZIMSEC term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-current assets | Land and buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture and fittings | Fixed assets |
| Current assets | Inventory, trade receivables, cash at bank and in hand | Stock, debtors |
| Current liabilities | Trade payables, bank overdraft, tax owed | Creditors |
| Non-current liabilities | Bank loans repayable after more than a year, debentures | Long-term liabilities |
| Equity | Share capital, retained profit (company); capital (sole trader) | Capital, owner's equity |

The statement always balances, because everything the business owns has been financed either by its owners or by lenders:

:::formula{name="The accounting equation"}
Total assets − total liabilities = equity (net assets)
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a company's statement]
**Gweru Engineering Ltd: statement of financial position at 31 December**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Non-current assets |  |  |
| Premises | 40,000 |  |
| Equipment | 12,000 |  |
| Vehicle | 8,000 |  |
| Total non-current assets |  | 60,000 |
| Current assets |  |  |
| Inventory | 6,000 |  |
| Trade receivables (debtors) | 3,500 |  |
| Cash | 1,500 |  |
| Total current assets |  | 11,000 |
| Total assets |  | 71,000 |
| Current liabilities |  |  |
| Trade payables (creditors) | 4,000 |  |
| Bank overdraft | 1,000 |  |
| Total current liabilities |  | (5,000) |
| Non-current liabilities |  |  |
| Bank loan (repayable in 5 years) | 15,000 |  |
| Total non-current liabilities |  | (15,000) |
| Net assets |  | 51,000 |
| Equity (capital) |  |  |
| Share capital | 40,000 |  |
| Retained profit | 11,000 |  |
| Total equity |  | 51,000 |

1. Total assets = 60,000 + 11,000 = US\$71,000.
2. Total liabilities = 5,000 + 15,000 = US\$20,000.
3. Net assets = 71,000 − 20,000 = US\$51,000, which equals total equity (40,000 + 11,000). The statement balances.
4. Working capital = current assets − current liabilities = 11,000 − 5,000 = US\$6,000.
5. Capital employed = equity + non-current liabilities = 51,000 + 15,000 = US\$66,000 (also 71,000 − 5,000).
:::

## A sole trader's capital

For a sole trader, the equity section shows how the owner's capital has changed during the year. Profit increases capital; **drawings** (cash or goods the owner takes for personal use) reduce it.

:::formula{name="Closing capital of a sole trader"}
Closing capital = opening capital + profit for the year − drawings
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a sole trader's capital]
Chipo's sewing business in Kwekwe started the year with capital of US\$9,000. She made a profit of US\$4,500 and took drawings of US\$2,400.

1. Closing capital = 9,000 + 4,500 − 2,400 = US\$11,100.
2. This figure appears in the equity section of her statement of financial position at the year end.
3. If her total liabilities are, say, US\$3,000, her total assets must be 11,100 + 3,000 = US\$14,100, because assets − liabilities = capital.
:::

## Using the statement to make decisions

- **Liquidity:** comparing current assets with current liabilities (working capital, and the liquidity ratios) shows whether the business can pay its short-term debts.
- **Borrowing:** a business with large non-current liabilities already has heavy borrowing, so a bank may refuse a further loan or charge a higher rate. Valuable non-current assets can be offered as security.
- **Growth:** retained profit in equity shows how much past profit has been kept to finance the business.
- **Inventory and receivables:** large amounts may mean cash is tied up in slow-selling goods or in customers who pay late.
- **Return:** capital employed is used to calculate the return on capital employed (ROCE) with the profit from the income statement.

## Limitations

- It is a snapshot of **one date**; the position may be quite different a week later.
- Assets may be shown at values that differ from what they would sell for.
- It does not show valuable things that are hard to value, such as a skilled workforce or customer loyalty.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting an overdraft under non-current liabilities"}
An overdraft can be recalled at short notice, so it is a **current** liability, even if the business has had it for years.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating inventory as a non-current asset"}
Inventory is meant to be sold within the year, so it is a **current** asset. Machinery used to make goods is non-current.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding drawings to capital"}
Drawings reduce the owner's capital. Profit increases it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Classify, then total"}
In "prepare" or "calculate" questions, first sort each item into its group (non-current assets, current assets, current liabilities, non-current liabilities, equity). Then total each group. Check that net assets equal equity.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Interpret with the figures"}
When asked what the statement shows, quote figures: "current assets of US\$11,000 cover current liabilities of US\$5,000 more than twice, so the business can pay its short-term debts".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board terms"}
Cambridge uses inventory, trade receivables and trade payables; ZIMSEC questions may say stock, debtors and creditors. Cambridge does not ask you to construct the statement, only to read, calculate from and interpret it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The statement of financial position shows assets, liabilities and equity on one date.
- Non-current assets last more than a year; current assets turn into cash within a year. Current liabilities are due within a year; non-current liabilities after a year.
- Total assets − total liabilities = net assets = equity.
- Working capital = current assets − current liabilities; capital employed = equity + non-current liabilities.
- A sole trader's closing capital = opening capital + profit − drawings.
- The statement is used to judge liquidity, borrowing, security and return, but it is only a snapshot.
:::
