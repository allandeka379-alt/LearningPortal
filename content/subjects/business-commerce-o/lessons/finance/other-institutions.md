## Introduction

Commercial banks are not the only places where people and businesses save and borrow. A large mining company raising millions for a new plant will talk to a **merchant bank**. A family buying a house in Bulawayo may go to a **building society**. A retailer selling fridges on hire purchase relies on a **finance house**. A woman selling vegetables at Sakubva market may get her first loan from a **microfinance institution**. Each institution specialises in particular customers and services.

This topic is in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3, financial institutions). Examiners ask you to describe the services of each institution and to choose the right one for a given person or business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Financial institution"}
An organisation that deals in money: it accepts savings, lends, or helps people and businesses raise and move funds.
:::

:::definition{term="Merchant bank"}
A bank that serves mainly large businesses, providing large loans, advice on raising capital, mergers and takeovers, and finance for foreign trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Building society"}
A financial institution that accepts savings deposits and lends mainly for buying, building or improving houses (mortgages).
:::

:::definition{term="Discount house"}
An institution that buys (discounts) bills of exchange and treasury bills for less than their face value and holds them until they mature, providing short-term funds to the money market.
:::

:::definition{term="Microfinance institution"}
An organisation that provides small loans, savings and other services to low-income people and small businesses who cannot get finance from ordinary banks.
:::

## Merchant banks

Merchant banks deal in large sums with relatively few, mainly corporate, customers. Their services include:

- **large loans** to companies, often arranged with other banks;
- **accepting** (guaranteeing) bills of exchange, so that the bills can be sold easily;
- **finance for foreign trade**: letters of credit, foreign currency and advice on exporting and importing;
- **issuing houses**: arranging new share and debenture issues for companies going to the stock exchange;
- **advice on mergers and takeovers** and managing investments for wealthy clients and pension funds.

Merchant banks do not usually serve ordinary individuals or run large branch networks.

## Finance houses

Finance houses (finance companies) provide **hire purchase** finance and **leasing**. When a customer buys a car, a fridge or a tractor on hire purchase, the finance house pays the dealer the cash price, then collects the deposit and instalments with interest. Finance houses also lease equipment and vehicles to businesses. They obtain funds from deposits, from banks and from their own capital, and they usually charge higher interest than banks because their lending is riskier.

## Discount houses

Discount houses work in the **money market**. They:

- buy **treasury bills** (short-term government borrowing) and **bills of exchange** at a discount and hold them until they are repaid at face value; the difference is their income;
- borrow short-term surplus money from commercial banks and lend it out, helping banks to manage their cash;
- help the central bank carry out monetary policy, because it can buy and sell bills through them.

:::example{title="Discounting a bill"}
A clothing wholesaler in Harare holds a bill of exchange from a retailer, payable in three months. The wholesaler needs cash now, so it sells the bill to a discount house for slightly less than its face value. Three months later the discount house collects the full amount from the retailer. The wholesaler gets cash early; the discount house earns the difference.
:::

## Building societies

Building societies accept savings and lend mainly on **mortgages** for houses. The house is the **security** for the loan: if the borrower stops paying, the society may sell it. Mortgages are long-term (often 10 to 25 years). Building societies also offer savings accounts and fixed deposits, and some offer current accounts and other banking services. They encourage home ownership and the construction industry.

## The post office savings bank

A post office savings bank allows people to save small amounts through the network of post offices, which reaches small towns and rural areas where bank branches are few. It is a safe, convenient place for small savers; the funds are often lent to the government.

## Development and small-enterprise finance institutions

Development finance institutions provide **long-term finance** for projects that ordinary banks see as too risky or too long-term: infrastructure, agriculture, mining, manufacturing and the growth of small and medium enterprises. Some are owned or supported by government. Services may include medium- and long-term loans, equity (taking shares in a business), loan guarantees, and business training and advice. Agencies that support small enterprises also help entrepreneurs prepare business plans before they borrow.

## Microfinance institutions

Microfinance institutions (MFIs) serve people who are **excluded** from ordinary banking: vendors, small-scale farmers, home-industry workers and other informal traders.

- They lend **small amounts** for short periods.
- Loans are often given to **groups**, whose members guarantee one another, instead of requiring collateral.
- Many offer **savings** accounts, mobile money repayments, insurance and **training** in record-keeping.

**Advantages:** access to credit for the poor; growth of small businesses; employment and income for women and rural households. **Disadvantages:** interest rates can be high because the loans are small and costly to manage; borrowers can fall into debt if a business fails.

## Matching customers with institutions

| Need | Most suitable institution |
| --- | --- |
| A mining company wants advice on issuing new shares | Merchant bank (issuing house) |
| A teacher wants a 20-year loan to buy a house | Building society |
| A farmer buys a tractor and pays in instalments | Finance house |
| A wholesaler wants cash now for a three-month bill of exchange | Discount house |
| A rural pensioner wants a safe place for small savings near home | Post office savings bank |
| A manufacturer needs long-term finance for a new factory | Development finance institution |
| A market vendor with no collateral needs US\$200 for stock | Microfinance institution |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying merchant banks serve the general public"}
Merchant banks serve mainly large companies and wealthy clients. Ordinary individuals use commercial banks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing finance houses and building societies"}
Finance houses fund **hire purchase and leasing** of goods; building societies fund **houses** through mortgages.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a discount house as a shop that gives discounts"}
A discount house buys bills of exchange and treasury bills for less than their face value. It has nothing to do with discounts in shops.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name, then describe the service"}
For "describe two services of a merchant bank" name each service and say what it does for the customer: "Issuing house: arranges the sale of new shares for a company."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the scenario"}
When asked which institution suits a person, match the size of the need, the period and the customer's security to the institution, and justify.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) both cover these institutions. Cambridge 0264 does not examine them by name; it covers the sources of finance they provide.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Merchant banks serve large companies: large loans, accepting bills, foreign trade finance, share issues and advice on mergers.
- Finance houses fund hire purchase and leasing; discount houses buy treasury bills and bills of exchange at a discount.
- Building societies take savings and lend long-term on mortgages for houses.
- Post office savings banks serve small savers through a wide network.
- Development institutions give long-term finance for projects and enterprises; microfinance institutions give small loans, often to groups, to people excluded from banks.
:::
