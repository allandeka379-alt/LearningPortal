## Introduction

Some projects are too large for a country's own banks: a national road network, a power station, a water supply scheme for a city, or support for a currency in a crisis. Developing countries such as Zimbabwe can turn to **international financial institutions**, organisations owned by many member governments that lend money, give advice and provide technical help.

This Form 4 ZIMSEC Commerce topic asks you to describe the roles of the **World Bank** (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development), the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** and **regional development banks**, and to explain their activities in developing countries. Avoid stating figures or recent loans as facts; explain the roles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="International financial institution"}
An organisation set up and owned by the governments of many countries to provide finance, advice and technical assistance across borders.
:::

:::definition{term="World Bank (IBRD)"}
An international institution that lends to and advises member governments for long-term development projects such as roads, power, water, health and education. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development is its main lending arm.
:::

:::definition{term="International Monetary Fund (IMF)"}
An international institution that promotes stability in exchange rates and international payments, lends to member countries with balance of payments problems, and monitors and advises on their economic policies.
:::

:::definition{term="Regional development bank"}
A development bank owned by the countries of a region (and some others) that finances development projects and trade within that region.
:::

:::definition{term="Conditionality"}
Conditions a lender attaches to a loan, such as reforms to government spending or policy, that the borrowing country must meet.
:::

## Background

The World Bank and the IMF were founded at a conference held at **Bretton Woods** in the United States in 1944, near the end of the Second World War. The World Bank's first task was to help **reconstruct** war-damaged Europe; its focus later moved to **development** in poorer countries. The IMF was set up to prevent the currency crises and trade wars of the 1930s by promoting cooperation and stable international payments. Both are owned by member countries, which pay in a subscription (quota) and receive votes broadly according to their contribution.

## The World Bank

Main activities:

- **Long-term loans** at relatively low interest for development projects: roads, railways, dams, electricity, water and sanitation, schools, hospitals and agriculture.
- **Grants and very low-interest credits** to the poorest countries through its associated agencies.
- **Technical assistance and advice**: helping governments plan and manage projects, and training officials.
- **Research and data** on development, poverty and economies.
- **Encouraging private investment** in developing countries through related agencies that lend to and insure private companies.

## The International Monetary Fund

Main activities:

- **Surveillance**: monitoring the economies of member countries and the world economy, and advising governments on policies for stable growth.
- **Lending to countries with balance of payments difficulties**, so that they can pay for essential imports and stabilise their currency while they adjust. Loans usually come with **conditions** (for example, reducing a budget deficit or reforming exchange rate policy).
- **Technical assistance and training** in tax administration, central banking, statistics and public finance.
- **Promoting cooperation** on exchange rates and international trade.

## Regional development banks

For Africa, the main regional development bank is the **African Development Bank**, and southern African countries also use regional institutions linked to their trading blocs. Activities include:

- loans and grants for **infrastructure**: regional roads, power links between countries, water projects;
- finance for **agriculture**, industry and small and medium enterprises, often lent through local banks;
- support for **regional trade and integration**;
- technical help and policy advice.

### How the institutions work with a member country

A member country deals with these institutions mainly through its ministry responsible for finance and its central bank. Before lending, the institution studies the economy and the project, agrees targets with the government and then checks progress regularly. Much of their help is not money at all: training officials, improving statistics, strengthening tax collection and advising on how to run public finances efficiently.

## World Bank and IMF compared

| Point | World Bank | IMF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main aim | Long-term development and reducing poverty | Stability of exchange rates and international payments |
| Type of lending | Long-term loans for projects | Shorter-term loans for balance of payments support |
| Who it lends to | Developing member countries | Any member country in payment difficulty |
| Other work | Technical advice for projects, research | Surveillance of economies, technical help in finance |

:::example{title="How a development loan might work"}
Suppose a developing country wants to rebuild a rural road network so that small-scale farmers can transport crops to markets in Harare and Mutare. Its own revenue is not enough. A development institution agrees a 25-year loan at a low interest rate, sends engineers to help plan the work and requires open tendering for contracts. Farmers' transport costs fall, traders reach new markets, and the country repays the loan over many years from the extra economic activity and taxes.
:::

## Benefits and criticisms for developing countries

| Benefits | Criticisms |
| --- | --- |
| Finance for large projects that local banks cannot fund | Loans must be repaid; debts can become a heavy burden |
| Lower interest and longer periods than commercial lenders | Conditions may require cuts in public spending, hurting the poor |
| Expert advice and training | Policies may not suit local conditions |
| Support in a currency or payments crisis | Rich countries have most of the votes |
| Confidence for other investors and lenders | Delays and bureaucracy in approving projects |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the World Bank and the IMF"}
The World Bank funds **long-term development projects**. The IMF deals with **balance of payments and currency stability** and monitors economies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling their money free aid"}
Most of their finance is **loans** that must be repaid with interest, even if at low rates. Only some help is given as grants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing facts"}
Do not quote amounts lent or dates of recent programmes unless the question gives them. Explain the roles instead.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- International financial institutions are owned by many member governments and provide finance, advice and technical help.
- The World Bank (IBRD) lends long-term for development projects and gives advice and research.
- The IMF monitors economies, lends to countries with balance of payments problems (with conditions) and gives technical help.
- Regional development banks, such as the African Development Bank, finance infrastructure, agriculture, enterprise and regional trade.
- Benefits include finance and expertise; criticisms include debt burdens, strict conditions and limited voting power of poor countries.
:::
