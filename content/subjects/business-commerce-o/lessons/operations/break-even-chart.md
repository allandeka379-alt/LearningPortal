## Introduction

Rudo wants to start making school uniforms in Mutare and must convince a bank to lend her money. Instead of a page of calculations, she shows the bank manager a **break-even chart**: a graph with three lines that shows, at a glance, how many uniforms she must sell to cover her costs, how much profit she will make at her expected sales, and how far sales could fall before she makes a loss.

ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) examines the break-even point, the profit and loss regions and the margin of safety; Cambridge 0264 asks you to construct, complete, amend and interpret a simple break-even chart and to explain the uses and limitations of break-even analysis. In this lesson the chart is described in words and as a table of values, because that is how you plot it: work out the points, then draw straight lines through them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Break-even chart"}
A graph showing fixed costs, total costs and total revenue at different levels of output, used to find the break-even point.
:::

:::definition{term="Break-even point"}
The point where the total revenue line crosses the total cost line: revenue equals total costs.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit region"}
The area to the right of the break-even point, where the revenue line is above the total cost line.
:::

:::definition{term="Loss region"}
The area to the left of the break-even point, where the total cost line is above the revenue line.
:::

## Constructing a break-even chart

1. Draw the axes. **Output (units)** goes on the horizontal (x) axis; **costs and revenue (US\$)** go on the vertical (y) axis. Label both and give the chart a title.
2. Choose a maximum output (often the business's capacity) and work out the figures at zero and at the maximum, plus one or two points between.
3. Draw the **fixed cost line**: a horizontal straight line at the level of fixed costs.
4. Draw the **total cost line**: it starts on the y-axis at the level of fixed costs (at zero output, total cost = fixed cost) and slopes upwards, because variable costs rise with output.
5. Draw the **total revenue line**: it starts at the origin (zero output, zero revenue) and slopes upwards more steeply than the total cost line (as long as price is above variable cost).
6. Mark the **break-even point** where revenue crosses total cost, and read off the break-even output (down to the x-axis) and break-even revenue (across to the y-axis).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: data for a chart]
A uniform maker has fixed costs of US\$4,000 a month. Each uniform sells for US\$10 and has a variable cost of US\$6. Capacity is 2,000 uniforms a month.

| Output (units) | Fixed costs US\$ | Total costs US\$ | Revenue US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 4,000 | 4,000 | 0 |
| 500 | 4,000 | 7,000 | 5,000 |
| 1,000 | 4,000 | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| 1,500 | 4,000 | 13,000 | 15,000 |
| 2,000 | 4,000 | 16,000 | 20,000 |

1. Total cost at 500 = 4,000 + (500 × 6) = US\$7,000; revenue at 500 = 500 × 10 = US\$5,000.
2. On the chart, the fixed cost line is flat at US\$4,000; the total cost line runs from (0, 4,000) to (2,000, 16,000); the revenue line runs from (0, 0) to (2,000, 20,000).
3. The revenue and total cost lines cross at **1,000 units and US\$10,000**: the break-even point. Check: 4,000 ÷ (10 − 6) = 1,000.
:::

## Reading the chart

- **Break-even output:** read down from the crossing point to the x-axis.
- **Profit or loss at any output:** the vertical gap between the revenue and total cost lines. To the right of break-even the revenue line is higher (profit); to the left the cost line is higher (loss).
- **Margin of safety:** mark the expected (actual) output on the x-axis; the distance between it and the break-even output is the margin of safety.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: reading profit, loss and margin of safety]
Using the chart from Example 1:

1. At 1,500 uniforms, revenue = US\$15,000 and total cost = US\$13,000, so the gap shows a **profit of US\$2,000**.
2. At 500 uniforms, total cost = US\$7,000 and revenue = US\$5,000, so the gap shows a **loss of US\$2,000**.
3. If expected sales are 1,500 uniforms, the margin of safety = 1,500 − 1,000 = **500 uniforms**.
:::

## Amending a chart

Examiners often ask what happens to the chart when something changes. The lines **pivot** or **shift**:

| Change | What happens on the chart | Break-even point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price rises | Revenue line pivots upwards from the origin (steeper) | Moves left (lower output) |
| Price falls | Revenue line becomes less steep | Moves right |
| Fixed costs rise | Fixed cost line and total cost line shift upwards in parallel | Moves right |
| Variable cost per unit rises | Total cost line pivots upwards from its starting point (steeper) | Moves right |

For example, if the uniform price rises from US\$10 to US\$12, revenue at 2,000 units becomes US\$24,000; the new revenue line runs from (0, 0) to (2,000, 24,000) and crosses total cost at about 667 units (4,000 ÷ 6 = 666.7, rounded up). At sales of 1,500 the margin of safety grows to about 833 units, but only if customers still buy at the higher price.

## Uses of break-even analysis

- **Planning a new business or product:** shows whether expected sales are well above break-even.
- **Supporting a loan application:** lenders can see the risk and the expected profit.
- **"What if?" analysis:** shows the effect of changing price or costs before the decision is made.
- **Choosing between options:** compares break-even points of different locations, machines or suppliers.
- **Setting targets:** sales staff know the minimum they must sell.

## Limitations of break-even analysis

- It assumes **all output is sold**; in reality some goods may remain unsold.
- It assumes the **selling price is the same** at every level of output, but businesses often give discounts for bulk orders.
- It assumes costs rise in a **straight line**; in reality bulk-buying discounts lower variable cost per unit at high output, and fixed costs may jump when a new machine or building is needed.
- It is only as good as the **data**: forecasts of costs and sales may be wrong.
- It suits a business with **one product**; with many products, fixed costs are hard to divide between them.
- It ignores **non-financial factors**, such as competitors' reactions and the quality of the product.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Starting the total cost line at zero"}
At zero output the business still pays its fixed costs, so the total cost line starts at the fixed cost level on the y-axis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading profit as the height of the revenue line"}
Profit is the **gap** between the revenue line and the total cost line, not the height of the revenue line.
:::

:::mistake{title="Unlabelled charts"}
Lose marks by missing a title, axis labels (output in units; costs and revenue in US\$) or line labels. Label every line.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Plot three points per line"}
Work out values at zero output, at maximum output and at one point in between. Two points make a straight line; the third checks your arithmetic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the chart, then check by formula"}
After reading the break-even point from the chart, check it with fixed costs ÷ contribution. If they differ, look for a plotting error.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Limitations need context"}
When evaluating, apply the limitations to the case: "the bakery gives discounts to schools, so price is not the same for every loaf, and the chart overstates revenue".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A break-even chart has output on the x-axis and costs/revenue (US\$) on the y-axis, with fixed cost, total cost and revenue lines.
- Total cost starts at the fixed cost level; revenue starts at the origin; they cross at the break-even point.
- The vertical gap between revenue and total cost shows profit (right of break-even) or loss (left); the margin of safety is the distance from break-even output to actual output.
- A price change pivots the revenue line; a fixed cost change shifts the total cost line; a variable cost change pivots the total cost line.
- Break-even helps planning, lending and "what if?" decisions, but assumes all output is sold at one price with straight-line costs and relies on forecasts.
:::
