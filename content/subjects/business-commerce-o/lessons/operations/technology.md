## Introduction

Tendai's family has run a small furniture workshop in Mutare for twenty years. His father cut every piece by hand from paper drawings. Tendai now designs tables on a laptop, shows customers a 3D image before they order, and is thinking of buying a computer-controlled cutting machine. At the tills of the hardware shop next door, every item is scanned and the stock records update automatically. Technology is changing how goods are made and how services are delivered.

Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) studies ICTs in operations management: bar codes, radio frequency identification, computer-aided design and online billing. Cambridge 0264 (topic 4.2) covers the use of technology in producing goods and services, including automation, CAD/CAM, 3D printing, electronic stock control and contactless payment, and their impact on businesses and employees.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mechanisation"}
Using machines to help workers do tasks that were done by hand, with workers still operating the machines.
:::

:::definition{term="Automation"}
Using machines controlled by computers to carry out tasks with little or no human involvement.
:::

:::definition{term="Computer-aided design (CAD)"}
Using computer software to design products, producing accurate drawings and 3D models that can be changed easily.
:::

:::definition{term="Computer-aided manufacture (CAM)"}
Using computers to control the machines that make products, often from CAD designs.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour productivity"}
The output per worker in a given time: total output ÷ number of employees.
:::

## Technology in producing goods

| Technology | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanisation | Machines help workers (tractors, power saws, sewing machines) | A farmer using a tractor instead of oxen |
| Automation and robots | Computer-controlled machines work with little human input | An automated bottling line filling and capping bottles |
| CAD | Designs on computer; easy to change, share and show to customers | Designing furniture, buildings or clothing patterns |
| CAM | Computer-controlled machines cut, shape or sew from the design | A computer-controlled router cutting table parts |
| 3D printing | Builds an object layer by layer from a digital design | Spare parts, prototypes, dental moulds |
| Electronic stock control | Computer records stock levels and reorders automatically | A factory reordering packaging when stock falls below a set level |

## Technology in services

| Technology | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bar codes and scanners | Identify each product at the till; price is charged and stock updated | Supermarket tills |
| Radio frequency identification (RFID) | Tags send data by radio, read without line of sight | Tracking pallets in a warehouse, tagging cattle, anti-theft tags in clothing shops |
| Online billing | Bills and invoices are sent and paid electronically | Utility and internet bills sent by email or SMS |
| Contactless and mobile payments | Customers tap a card or phone, or pay by mobile money | Paying at a pharmacy without cash |
| Online booking and ordering | Customers book or order through websites or apps | Bus tickets, hotel rooms, takeaway food |

:::example{title="Bar codes and electronic stock control in a hardware shop"}
When a customer buys a bag of cement, the cashier scans its bar code. The till charges the correct price, records the sale and reduces the stock record by one bag. When stock falls to a set reorder level, the system alerts the manager (or orders automatically). The owner can see at any time which items sell fastest.
:::

## Measuring the effect: labour productivity

Technology often raises **labour productivity**: more output from each worker.

:::worked-solution[Labour productivity before and after automation]
A juice factory produced 4,800 bottles a day with 12 workers. After installing an automated filling line, it produced 7,200 bottles a day with 10 workers.
1. Productivity before = 4,800 ÷ 12 = 400 bottles per worker per day.
2. Productivity after = 7,200 ÷ 10 = 720 bottles per worker per day.
3. Increase = 720 − 400 = 320 bottles per worker.
4. Percentage increase = 320 ÷ 400 × 100 = **80%**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Labour cost per unit]
In the same factory each worker is paid US\$15 a day.
1. Before: labour cost per bottle = (12 × US\$15) ÷ 4,800 = US\$180 ÷ 4,800 = US\$0.0375.
2. After: (10 × US\$15) ÷ 7,200 = US\$150 ÷ 7,200 = about US\$0.0208.
3. Labour cost per bottle has almost halved, although the business must also pay for the new machine, its maintenance and electricity.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages for the business

**Advantages**

- Higher productivity and output, lower cost per unit in the long run.
- Better and more consistent quality; fewer human errors.
- Faster production and service; machines can work longer hours.
- Accurate, up-to-date information (stock, sales, customers) for decisions.
- Products can be designed and changed quickly (CAD); prototypes made cheaply (3D printing).
- Better customer service: quick payment, online ordering, fewer queues.

**Disadvantages**

- High initial cost of equipment and software; may need borrowing.
- Training costs; staff may resist change.
- Breakdowns, power cuts and network failures can stop production or sales.
- Maintenance and upgrade costs; technology becomes outdated.
- Security risks: hacking, fraud and loss of data.

## Advantages and disadvantages for employees

- **Positive**: less boring, dangerous or heavy work; new skills and better-paid technical jobs; safer conditions.
- **Negative**: some jobs are lost (unemployment, especially for unskilled workers); need to retrain; fear of change and job insecurity; work may be monitored more closely.

## Recommending ICT tools for an enterprise

Ask about the business:

1. **What problem must be solved?** Long queues → faster tills or contactless payment; stock running out → electronic stock control; slow design changes → CAD.
2. **Can the business afford it?** Compare the cost with savings and extra revenue.
3. **Are there reliable power and internet?** Back-up power may be needed.
4. **Do staff have the skills?** Budget for training.
5. **What will customers accept?** For example, whether they use mobile payments.

:::example{title="An ICT recommendation for a small enterprise"}
Sipho runs a busy tuck shop in Bulawayo. Customers often have no cash, and he often runs out of popular items. A mobile-money payment option and a simple stock app on his phone would cost little, accept cashless payments and show which items to reorder. A full bar-code till system would be too expensive for his turnover.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing mechanisation and automation"}
In mechanisation workers operate machines. In automation computers control the machines with little human involvement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming technology always cuts costs"}
Technology lowers cost per unit only if output is high enough to cover the cost of buying, running and maintaining it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the effect on workers"}
Questions often ask about employees as well as the business. Mention job losses, retraining and changes in working conditions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Labour productivity = output ÷ number of employees (a Cambridge formula). Show both before and after figures and state the units (units per worker per day).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate new technology"}
Weigh the gains (productivity, quality, speed) against cost, training, breakdowns and job losses, then make a judgement for the business in the case: is it large enough, and are power and skills available?
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Production technology: mechanisation, automation, CAD, CAM, 3D printing and electronic stock control.
- Service technology: bar codes, RFID, online billing, contactless and mobile payments, online booking.
- Labour productivity = output ÷ number of employees; technology often raises it and lowers cost per unit.
- For businesses: higher output, quality and information, but high costs, training, breakdowns and security risks.
- For employees: safer, more skilled work but job losses and the need to retrain. Recommend tools that solve the business's problem and are affordable.
:::
