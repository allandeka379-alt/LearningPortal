## Introduction

Thandeka buys a pair of school shoes for her son in January. By March the soles have split. She returns to the shop, complains, and tells her friends not to buy that brand. The manufacturer has lost more than one sale: it has lost a customer, damaged its reputation and must replace the shoes.

**Quality** is not about luxury. A cheap product can be good quality if it does what customers expect for the price they pay. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) studies quality management, including total quality management and quality circles; Cambridge 0264 (topic 4.5) asks why quality is important and how it is achieved through quality control, quality assurance and TQM. You must be able to recommend a method for a business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Quality"}
Producing a good or service that meets customers' expectations and is fit for its purpose, free from faults.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality control"}
Checking finished products (or samples of them) at the end of production, or at stages, to find and remove faulty items before they reach customers.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality assurance"}
Checking quality at every stage of production, by the workers themselves, against agreed standards, to prevent faults happening.
:::

:::definition{term="Total quality management (TQM)"}
An approach in which every employee in every department is responsible for quality all the time, with the aim of zero defects and complete customer satisfaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Quality circle"}
A small group of workers who meet regularly to identify quality problems in their area and suggest solutions to management.
:::

## Why quality matters

| Benefit of good quality | Cost of poor quality |
| --- | --- |
| Customers are satisfied and return (loyalty) | Customers complain, return goods and switch to rivals |
| A good reputation attracts new customers by word of mouth | Bad reviews spread quickly, especially on social media |
| A higher price can be charged | Prices must be cut to sell goods |
| Less waste and fewer reworked or scrapped products, so lower costs | Wasted materials, time spent reworking, replacement and refund costs |
| Easier to sell to large retailers and export markets that demand standards | Retailers stop stocking the product; possible legal action |

## Quality control

Inspectors check products at the end of the line, or at set points, against a standard. Faulty items are rejected, reworked or scrapped. For large outputs, a **sample** is checked.

**Advantages**: faulty products are found before customers receive them; workers need little training in checking; it is simple to understand.
**Disadvantages**: it finds faults after they have happened, so materials and labour are already wasted; inspectors are an extra cost; it does not find the **cause** of faults; workers may feel quality is "someone else's job".

:::worked-solution[Measuring the reject rate]
A furniture workshop made 1,200 chairs in a month. Inspectors rejected 36.
1. Reject rate = 36 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **3%**.
2. After introducing quality assurance, rejects fell to 9 chairs out of 1,200: 9 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = **0.75%**.
3. The workshop now wastes the materials and labour of 27 fewer chairs a month.
:::

## Quality assurance

Quality is built in at **every stage**: suppliers must meet standards, workers check their own work, and procedures are written down so that each task is done the same way. Some businesses gain certificates from standards bodies to show customers that their processes meet recognised standards.

**Advantages**: faults are prevented, so less waste and lower costs; workers take responsibility, which can motivate them; customers and retailers trust products made under agreed standards.
**Disadvantages**: workers need training; setting up procedures takes time and money; it depends on workers' commitment.

:::example{title="Quality assurance in a bakery"}
A bakery in Gweru agrees standards with its flour supplier, trains bakers to weigh ingredients and check oven temperatures for every batch, and records the results. Instead of finding burnt or under-weight loaves at the end, faults are prevented at each step.
:::

## Total quality management and quality circles

TQM extends quality assurance to the **whole business**: every employee, including office staff, drivers and cleaners, aims to satisfy "customers", including **internal customers** (the next department in the process). The aim is **zero defects** and continuous improvement.

**Quality circles** support TQM: small groups of workers meet regularly, discuss problems such as waste or delays in their area, and suggest improvements. Workers know the job best, so they often find simple, cheap solutions.

**Advantages of TQM**: quality improves throughout the business; waste and costs fall; workers feel valued (linking to motivation theories); reputation improves.
**Disadvantages**: expensive training; needs commitment from all staff and managers; benefits take time; meetings take time away from production.

## Choosing a quality method

| Situation | Suitable method |
| --- | --- |
| Small business, simple product, little money for training | Quality control by the owner or a supervisor, with simple checks |
| Products where a fault is dangerous (food, medicines, electrical goods) | Quality assurance, with checks at every stage and records |
| Large business wanting continuous improvement and a strong reputation | TQM with quality circles |
| Service business | Training, customer feedback, mystery shoppers, clear service standards |

## Quality in services

Quality in a service means reliability, speed, politeness, cleanliness and accurate information. A bus company, bank or hotel measures quality through **customer feedback**, complaints, waiting times and repeat customers, and improves it through **training** and clear standards.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying quality means expensive"}
Quality means meeting customers' expectations. A low-priced product can be high quality for its price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up control and assurance"}
Quality control **finds** faults, usually at the end. Quality assurance **prevents** faults at every stage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the cost of quality systems"}
Training, inspectors and procedures cost money. A good recommendation weighs these costs against the savings and the benefits to reputation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend control or assurance"}
Consider the size of the business, the type of product (is a fault dangerous?), its budget and its workers' skills. Explain one advantage and one drawback of your choice and why it suits the case better than the alternative.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills names TQM and quality circles (and links quality with just-in-time). Cambridge 0264 asks about quality control, quality assurance and TQM and their advantages and disadvantages, often with data such as reject rates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Quality means meeting customers' expectations and being fit for purpose; it builds loyalty, reputation and lowers waste.
- Quality control inspects products to find and remove faults, but faults are found after the waste has happened.
- Quality assurance checks at every stage to prevent faults; workers take responsibility.
- TQM makes every employee responsible for quality, aiming at zero defects; quality circles involve workers in solving problems.
- Choose a method by business size, product risk, budget and workers' skills.
:::
