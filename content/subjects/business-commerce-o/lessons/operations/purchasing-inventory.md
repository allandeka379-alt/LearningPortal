## Introduction

A furniture factory in Chinhoyi cannot make a single chair without timber, glue, screws and varnish. If the timber arrives late, the carpenters stand idle. If too much is bought, money is tied up in a yard full of planks that may warp in the rain. If it is bought at a high price, every chair costs more to make. Getting purchasing and inventory right is therefore one of the most important jobs in any business that makes or sells goods.

ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) covers the purchasing function and cycle, e-purchasing, types of inventory and the costs and benefits of holding inventory. Cambridge 0264 covers inventory as part of the production process, including why it is held and how it is managed (linked to just-in-time, covered in the lesson on lean production).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Purchasing"}
The business function responsible for buying the materials, components, goods for resale and services a business needs, on the best terms.
:::

:::definition{term="Inventory (stock)"}
The materials and goods a business holds: raw materials, work in progress and finished goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Lead time"}
The time between placing an order with a supplier and receiving the goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Buffer (safety) stock"}
A minimum level of inventory kept in case of delays in delivery or an unexpected rise in demand.
:::

:::definition{term="E-purchasing"}
Buying from suppliers using the internet and electronic systems: online catalogues, electronic orders, invoices and payments.
:::

## The importance of purchasing

Purchases are often the largest cost of a manufacturer or retailer, so good buying has a direct effect on profit. Good purchasing:

- keeps production running by making sure materials are available on time;
- keeps costs low, raising profit margins or allowing lower prices;
- protects quality, because poor materials lead to faulty products and complaints;
- builds good relationships with reliable suppliers.

## The five rights of purchasing

| Right | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Right quality | Materials fit for the purpose, neither too poor nor unnecessarily expensive | A bakery buys flour that meets food-safety standards |
| Right quantity | Enough to meet needs without overstocking | Buying for two weeks' production, not six months |
| Right price | The best value, including discounts, delivery and credit terms | Comparing quotations from several suppliers |
| Right time | Goods arrive when needed, allowing for lead time | Ordering school-uniform fabric well before January |
| Right source (supplier) | A reliable supplier who delivers on time and gives good service | Choosing a supplier with a record of on-time delivery |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: comparing suppliers]
A bakery needs 500 kg of a special baking ingredient. Supplier A charges US\$4.20 per kg plus US\$60 delivery and delivers in two days. Supplier B charges US\$4.00 per kg plus US\$180 delivery and delivers in eight days.

1. Supplier A: 500 × US\$4.20 + US\$60 = US\$2,100 + US\$60 = **US\$2,160**.
2. Supplier B: 500 × US\$4.00 + US\$180 = US\$2,000 + US\$180 = **US\$2,180**.
3. A is US\$20 cheaper in total **and** delivers six days sooner. Although B's price per kilogram is lower, A gives the right price and the right time. Choose A, unless B offers better quality or credit terms.
:::

## The purchasing cycle

1. **Identify the need**: a department or the stock records show materials are needed (a requisition is raised).
2. **Find suppliers**: search catalogues, websites and trade contacts.
3. **Ask for quotations** and compare price, quality, delivery time and credit terms.
4. **Choose the supplier and place an order** (a purchase order).
5. **Receive and check the goods** against the order and the delivery note.
6. **Check the invoice and pay** the supplier on the agreed terms.
7. **Review the supplier's performance** for future orders.

### E-purchasing

Many businesses now order through suppliers' websites or electronic ordering systems, and pay electronically.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Easy to compare prices of many suppliers, including distant ones | Needs reliable internet and electricity |
| Faster ordering and fewer paperwork errors | Risk of fraud and online scams |
| Orders and payments can be made at any time | Cannot inspect goods before buying |
| Records are kept automatically | Staff need training |

## Types of inventory

- **Raw materials and components**: inputs waiting to be used, such as timber, fabric, flour or electronic parts.
- **Work in progress**: partly finished goods still being made, such as chairs that are assembled but not varnished.
- **Finished goods**: completed products waiting to be sold or delivered.

Retailers and wholesalers hold mainly **goods for resale**.

## Why businesses hold inventory

- To keep production running if a delivery is late (buffer stock).
- To meet sudden or seasonal rises in demand, so sales are not lost.
- To take advantage of bulk-buying discounts.
- To give customers choice and immediate delivery.
- To protect against expected price rises or shortages.

## Costs and benefits of holding inventory

| Costs of holding inventory | Costs of holding too little |
| --- | --- |
| Money (working capital) tied up that could be used elsewhere | Production stops; workers and machines stand idle |
| Storage: rent, security, insurance, staff | Lost sales and unhappy customers who go to rivals |
| Damage, theft, waste and goods going out of date or out of fashion | Small, frequent orders cost more to place and deliver |
| Interest on money borrowed to finance the stock | Bulk-buying discounts are lost |

The aim is a **balance**: enough inventory to keep production and sales going, but not so much that money and space are wasted.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: when to re-order]
A clothing factory uses 30 metres of a fabric each day. Its supplier's lead time is 5 days, and it keeps a buffer stock of 50 metres.

1. Fabric used during the lead time = 30 × 5 = 150 metres.
2. To avoid falling below the buffer stock while waiting, it should re-order when stock falls to 150 + 50 = **200 metres**.
3. If the supplier becomes less reliable, the factory could raise its buffer stock, but more cash would be tied up.
:::

## Factors affecting how much inventory is held

- **Type of product**: perishable goods (milk, bread, vegetables) cannot be stored for long.
- **Demand**: high or seasonal demand needs more stock at certain times.
- **Lead time and reliability of suppliers**: long or uncertain deliveries need more buffer stock (common with imported materials).
- **Storage space and costs**.
- **Finance available**: a business short of cash cannot tie up much in inventory.
- **Bulk discounts and expected price rises**.
- **Method of production**: just-in-time keeps inventory to a minimum.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing the cheapest supplier automatically"}
The right price is only one of the five rights. Delivery cost, reliability, quality and credit terms matter too.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying holding inventory has no cost"}
Inventory ties up cash and costs money to store, insure and protect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing work in progress with finished goods"}
Work in progress is still being made; finished goods are complete and ready for sale.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the five rights to structure answers"}
When asked how a business should choose a supplier, work through quality, quantity, price, time and source, using the case figures.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Balance costs and benefits"}
For "should the business hold more inventory?" questions, give the benefit (fewer stock-outs, bulk discounts) and the cost (cash tied up, storage, waste) before deciding.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Purchasing buys materials and goods on the best terms; it affects costs, quality and the smooth running of production.
- The five rights: right quality, quantity, price, time and source.
- The purchasing cycle runs from identifying a need through quotations, ordering, receiving and paying to reviewing the supplier; e-purchasing makes this faster but brings risks.
- Inventory is raw materials, work in progress and finished goods; it is held to avoid stoppages, meet demand and gain discounts.
- Holding inventory ties up cash and costs money; holding too little risks stoppages and lost sales. The right level depends on the product, demand, suppliers, space and finance.
:::
