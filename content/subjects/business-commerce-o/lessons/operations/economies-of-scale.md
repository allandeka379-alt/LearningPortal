## Introduction

A small stock-feed mixer near Gweru buys maize and soya in 50 kg bags from a local dealer. A national feed company buys whole truckloads directly from farmers and processors. The large company pays far less per tonne, borrows money more cheaply, advertises on national radio and runs automated mixing plants. Its cost of making each bag of feed is much lower, so it can charge less and still earn a good profit.

These cost advantages of being large are called **economies of scale**. But growth also brings problems: as a business becomes very large, communication and control become harder, and costs per unit can start to rise again. These are **diseconomies of scale**. Cambridge 0264 asks you to identify and explain each type and apply them to a business in a case, for example when it plans to expand or merge.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economies of scale"}
The reductions in average (unit) cost that a business gains as it increases its scale of operation.
:::

:::definition{term="Diseconomies of scale"}
The increases in average cost that can arise when a business grows too large.
:::

:::definition{term="Scale of operation"}
The size of a business, measured for example by its output, capacity, number of employees or capital employed.
:::

## Economies of scale

### Purchasing (bulk-buying) economies

Large businesses buy raw materials and components in very large quantities, so suppliers give them **bulk discounts**. They may also buy direct from producers, cutting out wholesalers. A supermarket chain pays less per carton of cooking oil than a small tuck shop.

### Marketing economies

The cost of an advertising campaign or a sales team is spread over a much larger output, so the marketing cost **per unit** falls. A large business can afford national television and radio advertising, and its own delivery vehicles carry full loads, lowering transport cost per unit.

### Financial economies

Banks see large businesses as less risky, because they have more assets for security and a longer record. They can therefore borrow **more easily and at lower interest rates**. Large public limited companies can also raise finance by selling shares.

### Managerial economies

A large business can afford to employ **specialist managers** (for finance, marketing, human resources, production), who make better decisions than one owner trying to do everything. Each manager's salary is spread over a large output.

### Technical economies

Large businesses can afford advanced, **specialised machinery** and flow production. A large machine may cost twice as much as a small one but produce four times the output, so the cost per unit falls. Large firms can also spend on research and development.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how average cost falls with scale]
A feed mill expands in two stages. As it grows, its fixed costs rise (bigger premises, more machines, specialist managers), but bulk buying and better machinery cut its variable cost per bag.

| Output (bags a month) | Fixed costs US\$ | Variable cost per bag US\$ | Total cost US\$ | Average cost per bag US\$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1,000 | 4,000 | 2.50 | 6,500 | 6.50 |
| 5,000 | 6,000 | 2.10 | 16,500 | 3.30 |
| 20,000 | 10,000 | 1.90 | 48,000 | 2.40 |

1. At 5,000 bags: total cost = 6,000 + (5,000 × 2.10) = 6,000 + 10,500 = US\$16,500; average cost = 16,500 ÷ 5,000 = US\$3.30.
2. At 20,000 bags: total cost = 10,000 + (20,000 × 1.90) = 10,000 + 38,000 = US\$48,000; average cost = 48,000 ÷ 20,000 = US\$2.40.
3. Average cost falls from US\$6.50 to US\$2.40: purchasing economies cut the variable cost per bag, and technical and managerial economies spread the fixed costs over far more bags.
:::

## Diseconomies of scale

### Poor communication

In a large business there are more layers of management and more people, so messages take longer to pass along and may be distorted. Decisions are slower and mistakes more frequent, raising costs.

### Low commitment of employees

Workers in a very large organisation may feel unimportant and far from the managers who make decisions. They may lose motivation, leading to lower productivity, more absence and higher labour turnover.

### Weak coordination

It is harder to make sure that all departments, factories and branches work towards the same objectives. Departments may duplicate work or follow conflicting plans.

### Lack of control

Senior managers cannot check everything. Waste, inefficiency and even theft may go unnoticed, and more supervisors are needed, adding to costs.

:::example{title="Growth that went too far"}
A fictional bakery chain grew from one bakery in Mutare to forty branches across the country in five years. Bulk-buying flour cut its ingredient costs, and national radio adverts were cheap per loaf sold. But head office instructions reached branch managers late, some branches ran out of flour while others threw away stale stock, and staff in distant branches felt ignored. Average costs began to rise. The owners responded by dividing the business into regions, each with its own manager.
:::

## Avoiding diseconomies of scale

- **Decentralise**: give regional or branch managers authority to make decisions.
- **Improve communication**: use clear procedures and digital systems, and reduce the number of management layers.
- **Motivate staff**: teamwork, consultation and recognition so employees feel valued.
- **Set clear objectives** and monitor results with budgets and targets.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing economies of scale with spreading fixed costs in the short run"}
Spreading existing fixed costs over more output happens at any size. Economies of scale come from **growing the scale** of the business: bulk buying, specialist managers, better machinery and cheaper finance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying total costs fall"}
Economies of scale lower **average** (unit) cost. Total costs still rise as output grows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing types without applying them"}
"It gets purchasing economies" earns little. Say what the business buys in bulk and how this lowers its cost per unit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the chain"}
For each economy of scale give: what the business can do because it is large → why that lowers cost per unit → what this means for its price or profit.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use both sides when evaluating growth"}
If a case asks whether a business should expand, weigh likely economies of scale against possible diseconomies and other factors such as finance and demand.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economies of scale are falls in average cost as a business grows.
- Types: purchasing (bulk discounts), marketing (advertising and distribution spread over more units), financial (cheaper, easier borrowing), managerial (specialist managers), technical (efficient machinery and research).
- Diseconomies of scale are rises in average cost when a business becomes too large: poor communication, low commitment of employees, weak coordination and lack of control.
- Decentralisation, better communication and motivation can reduce diseconomies of scale.
:::
