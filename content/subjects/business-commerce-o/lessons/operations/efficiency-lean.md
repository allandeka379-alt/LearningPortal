## Introduction

Two furniture workshops in Kwekwe each employ ten carpenters. One makes 120 chairs a week; the other makes 170. The second workshop is more **productive**: each worker produces more, so the labour cost of each chair is lower, and it can charge lower prices or earn a bigger profit. In a competitive market, the less productive workshop will struggle to survive.

Production is not only about making goods; it is about making them **efficiently**, using as few resources as possible for each unit. Cambridge 0264 expects you to calculate labour productivity, explain why efficiency matters and how it can be improved, and explain **lean production**, especially **just-in-time (JIT)** inventory control and **Kaizen**, with their advantages and disadvantages.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production"}
The process of turning inputs (land, labour, capital, enterprise) into outputs of goods and services that satisfy customers' wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
The output produced compared with the inputs used to produce it.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour productivity"}
The output per employee in a given period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Efficiency"}
Producing output with the minimum use of resources, so that cost per unit is as low as possible.
:::

:::definition{term="Lean production"}
Techniques that aim to cut waste of every kind (materials, time, space, effort, stock) in order to raise efficiency.
:::

## Calculating labour productivity

:::formula{name="Labour productivity"}
Labour productivity = output (per period) ÷ number of employees
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: measuring an improvement]
A workshop's 12 carpenters make 1,800 chairs a month. After training and new equipment, 10 carpenters make 1,700 chairs a month.

1. Labour productivity before = 1,800 ÷ 12 = **150 chairs per worker per month**.
2. Labour productivity after = 1,700 ÷ 10 = **170 chairs per worker per month**.
3. Increase = 170 − 150 = 20 chairs per worker; as a percentage: 20 ÷ 150 × 100 = **13.3%** (to one decimal place).
4. Total output fell slightly, but each worker now produces more, so the labour cost of each chair is lower.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: productivity and cost per unit]
A bakery employs 8 workers, each paid US\$120 a week, and they produce 960 loaves a week.

1. Labour productivity = 960 ÷ 8 = 120 loaves per worker per week.
2. Labour cost per loaf = (8 × US\$120) ÷ 960 = US\$960 ÷ 960 = **US\$1.00**.
3. If better organisation raises output to 1,200 loaves with the same workers and wages, labour cost per loaf = US\$960 ÷ 1,200 = **US\$0.80**.
4. Higher productivity lowers cost per unit even though wages have not changed.
:::

## Why efficiency matters

- **Lower costs per unit**, so the business can cut prices to compete or keep prices and earn more profit.
- **Competitiveness**, especially against imports and large rivals.
- **Higher profits** can be used to pay higher wages, which helps keep good staff.
- **Less waste** of materials, energy and water, which is better for the environment.

## How to raise productivity and efficiency

| Method | How it helps | Possible problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Automation and technology | Machines work faster and more accurately than people | High cost; workers may fear job losses |
| Training workers | Skilled workers make fewer mistakes and work faster | Cost; trained workers may leave for better pay |
| Improving motivation | Motivated workers put in more effort | Rewards cost money |
| Better organisation of the workplace | Less time walking, searching or waiting | Takes management time to plan |
| Better inventory control | Fewer delays caused by shortages; less money tied up | Needs reliable suppliers |
| Better quality control | Fewer rejects and less rework | Requires training and checks |

## Lean production

Lean production cuts out every activity that does not add value for the customer. Common types of waste are: making more than customers want, waiting time, unnecessary transport and movement, too much inventory, faults that must be corrected, and over-complicated processes. Two important lean techniques are just-in-time and Kaizen.

### Just-in-time (JIT) inventory control

With JIT, materials and components arrive **exactly when they are needed** in production, and finished goods are made only when there is an order, so little or no inventory is held.

| Advantages of JIT | Disadvantages of JIT |
| --- | --- |
| Less money tied up in inventory, so cash flow improves | Depends completely on reliable suppliers; a late delivery stops production |
| Less storage space and lower warehousing costs | Higher ordering and delivery costs (many small deliveries) |
| Less waste from damaged, stolen or out-of-date stock | Cannot easily meet a sudden rise in demand |
| Business responds quickly to customer orders | Loses bulk-buying discounts |

JIT works best where suppliers are close and reliable and transport is dependable. Where deliveries are often delayed, for example by long distances or border hold-ups, a business may keep a small buffer stock instead.

### Kaizen (continuous improvement)

Kaizen is a Japanese word meaning **continuous improvement**. Instead of a few large changes, workers and managers look for many small improvements every day. Workers often meet in small groups to suggest ideas, such as rearranging tools to save time or changing a process to reduce faults.

| Advantages of Kaizen | Disadvantages of Kaizen |
| --- | --- |
| Many small improvements add up to large gains in productivity | Takes time for meetings and discussion |
| Workers are involved and feel valued, raising motivation | Some workers may resist change or not want extra responsibility |
| Little expensive investment needed | Small steps may not be enough when a big change is needed |
| Problems are solved by the people who know the work best | Needs training and support from managers |

:::example{title="Lean production in a garment factory"}
A clothing factory in Harare used to buy fabric for three months at a time and kept a store full of rolls. It moved to weekly deliveries from a nearby textile supplier (JIT), freeing space and cash. Machinists meet for fifteen minutes each Monday to suggest improvements (Kaizen); one group rearranged its workstations so that half-finished garments move a shorter distance, saving several minutes per garment.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing production with productivity"}
Production is total output; productivity is output **per worker** (or per input). Output can rise while productivity falls if many more workers are hired.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying JIT means no inventory at all"}
JIT aims to keep inventory to a minimum, not necessarily zero. Many businesses keep a small safety stock.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing Kaizen as one big change"}
Kaizen is many **small**, continuous improvements, usually suggested by workers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show the productivity formula"}
Write output ÷ number of employees, state the period ("per worker per week") and compare with another figure to show the change.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Judge JIT in context"}
In a case study, ask: are suppliers reliable and close? Is demand steady? Can the business afford to stop production if a delivery is late? Your recommendation should depend on these facts.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Productivity compares output with inputs; labour productivity = output ÷ number of employees.
- Higher efficiency lowers cost per unit, improves competitiveness and profit, and reduces waste.
- Productivity can be raised through automation, training, motivation, better organisation, inventory control and quality control.
- Lean production cuts waste; JIT keeps inventory to a minimum but depends on reliable suppliers; Kaizen uses many small improvements suggested by workers.
:::
