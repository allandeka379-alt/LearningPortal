## Introduction

Walk through the industrial areas of Bulawayo and you will see three very different ways of making things. A tailor makes one wedding suit, cut to fit a single customer. A bakery makes 300 loaves of white bread, then changes the mixer and makes 200 loaves of brown bread. A bottling plant fills thousands of identical bottles of soft drink every hour on a production line that hardly ever stops. These are **job**, **batch** and **flow** production.

Choosing the right method affects a business's costs, the price it can charge, the quality of the product and how it treats its workers. Cambridge 0264 expects you to describe each method, give its advantages and disadvantages, and recommend the most suitable method for a business in a case. The best answers link the method to the **size of the market**, the **type of product** and the **resources** of the business.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Job production"}
Making a single product, or a very small number, to meet the individual needs of each customer. Each job is different.
:::

:::definition{term="Batch production"}
Making a limited quantity of one identical product, then switching the equipment to make a batch of a different product.
:::

:::definition{term="Flow production"}
Making large quantities of a standardised product continuously, with the product moving from one stage to the next along a production line.
:::

:::definition{term="Standardised product"}
A product where every unit is the same, so it can be made in large numbers.
:::

## Job production

**Examples:** a made-to-measure suit, a wedding cake, a house built to an architect's plan, a carved wooden door, a website for one client, repairing a car.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Product meets the customer's exact needs | High cost per unit: skilled labour and much time per item |
| A high price can usually be charged | Production is slow |
| Work is varied, so skilled workers are often well motivated | Few or no economies of scale |
| Easy to change the design during production | Needs a wide range of tools and skilled staff |

Job production suits **small businesses** and products where customers want something unique. Most new businesses, such as a dressmaker or welder, start with job production.

## Batch production

**Examples:** a bakery making batches of different breads and cakes, a clothing factory making 500 school shirts in one size then 500 in another size, a furniture factory making 50 chairs of one design, a printer printing 2,000 copies of one book.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Lower cost per unit than job production: machines can be used for many identical items | Time is lost (and costs incurred) when machines are cleaned or reset between batches |
| Gives some variety and choice to customers | Inventory of raw materials and finished goods must be stored between batches |
| Flexible: easy to respond to changes in demand by changing batch sizes | Work can be repetitive within a batch, lowering motivation |
| If one product sells badly, the business can switch to another | Planning and scheduling batches is complicated |

Batch production suits businesses with **moderate demand** for a **range** of similar products.

## Flow production

**Examples:** soft drinks, cooking oil, cigarettes, cars, cement, maize meal, mobile phones.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Very low cost per unit: economies of scale and specialist machinery | Very high cost to set up the production line |
| Large output to meet high demand | Products are standardised: little variety |
| Machines can run continuously; less labour needed (automation) | Work is boring and repetitive, so motivation may be low |
| Consistent quality, easy to check | A breakdown at one stage stops the whole line |
| Raw materials can be bought in bulk at lower prices | Needs high, steady demand; large stocks if demand falls |

Flow production suits **large businesses** selling **standardised products** in **mass markets**.

## Comparing the three methods

| Feature | Job | Batch | Flow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quantity of each product | One or a few | Limited quantities | Very large, continuous |
| Variety | Every item different | Several versions | Little or none |
| Cost per unit | Highest | Medium | Lowest |
| Labour | Highly skilled | Semi-skilled | Mostly unskilled, or automated |
| Capital equipment | General tools | Adjustable machines | Specialist production line |
| Typical market | Individual customers | Medium-sized markets with some choice | Mass market |

:::example{title="One business, changing methods"}
Thandeka starts a business in Gweru making leather sandals to order, measuring each customer's feet: **job production**. As word spreads, she makes 40 pairs in each of five standard sizes at a time and sells them at markets: **batch production**. Years later, a large retailer orders 20,000 identical pairs a month, so her company invests in machines that cut, stitch and pack sandals continuously: **flow production**. The method changed as her market grew.
:::

:::example{title="A food factory using more than one method"}
A biscuit factory in Harare may use flow production for its best-selling plain biscuit, made every day on a dedicated line, and batch production for seasonal or flavoured biscuits made a few times a year. Large businesses often combine methods.
:::

## Production methods, employees and technology

The method chosen also shapes the work people do. In job production a skilled craftsperson sees a product through from start to finish and takes pride in it. In batch production workers may specialise in one stage for a whole batch. In flow production each worker usually repeats one small task, or simply watches over machines, so managers need to reduce boredom, for example by rotating jobs or giving teams responsibility for quality. Modern technology is also blurring the lines: computer-controlled machines can switch quickly from one design to another, so some businesses can now produce small batches, or even individual designs, at a cost close to that of flow production.

## How to choose a method

When recommending a method in an exam, consider:

1. **Size of the market:** is demand large and steady enough to justify flow production?
2. **Nature of the product:** does each customer want something unique, or is the product standardised?
3. **Finance available:** can the business afford specialist machinery?
4. **Labour:** are skilled workers available, or will machines and less-skilled workers be used?
5. **Competition and price:** if rivals sell cheaply, a low cost per unit may be essential.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all small-scale production 'job production'"}
A small bakery that bakes 100 identical loaves and then 100 rolls is using **batch** production. Job production makes individual items to each customer's needs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying flow production is always best because costs are lowest"}
Flow production is only suitable if demand is high and steady. A business with a small market would waste money on a production line.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing features without linking them to the business"}
In a recommendation, use facts from the case: the size of orders, the number of designs, the money available.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend and justify"}
A good recommendation says which method, why it suits **this** business (with case evidence), and why the other methods are less suitable.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use precise advantages"}
Instead of "it is cheaper", write "flow production has a lower cost per unit because specialist machines produce large quantities and materials are bought in bulk".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
Methods of production are Cambridge 0264 content (Topic 4). ZIMSEC Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills learners may meet the same ideas when studying production and division of labour.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Job production makes one-off products to individual needs: high quality and price, high cost per unit, slow.
- Batch production makes limited quantities of identical products, then changes: some variety and lower costs, but time lost between batches and stock to store.
- Flow production makes large quantities of standardised products continuously: lowest cost per unit, but high set-up cost, no variety, repetitive work and needs high demand.
- The right method depends on the size of the market, the type of product, the finance and labour available, and competition.
:::
