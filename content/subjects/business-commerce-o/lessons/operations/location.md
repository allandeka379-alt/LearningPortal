## Introduction

Why are so many sugar mills in the Lowveld, close to the cane fields? Why do clothing shops cluster in city centres, while timber processing happens near the plantations of the Eastern Highlands? Where a business locates affects its costs, its sales and sometimes whether it survives at all.

Location is a long-term decision: buildings, machinery and staff are hard to move. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 1) studies location and relocation factors; Cambridge 0264 (topic 4.6) asks about the factors influencing the location of manufacturing and service businesses, the choice of country and the role of government. You must be able to **recommend** a location using evidence from a case.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Location"}
The place where a business carries out its activities: its factory, office, shop, farm or warehouse.
:::

:::definition{term="Relocation"}
Moving a business, or part of it, from one location to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Bulk-reducing industry"}
An industry whose raw materials are heavier or bulkier than its finished products (such as ore processing or sugar milling), so it usually locates near its raw materials.
:::

:::definition{term="Bulk-increasing industry"}
An industry whose finished products are heavier, bulkier or more fragile than its raw materials (such as soft drinks or furniture), so it usually locates near its market.
:::

## Factors influencing the location of manufacturing businesses

| Factor | Explanation and example |
| --- | --- |
| Raw materials | Bulk-reducing and perishable inputs: locate close to them (a dairy near farms, a sawmill near forests) |
| Market | Bulk-increasing or perishable products, or where customers need frequent contact: locate near customers (a bakery, a bottling plant) |
| Transport and communication | Good roads, rail, airports and network coverage lower costs and speed up delivery |
| Labour | Availability of workers with the right skills, and wage rates |
| Power and water | Factories need a reliable electricity supply; some need large amounts of water |
| Cost and availability of land | Large factories need affordable, serviced land |
| Government policy | Incentives such as tax relief or grants in some areas; planning rules and restrictions |
| Competitors and related firms | Being near suppliers and similar firms gives access to services and skilled labour |
| Climate | Important for agriculture and for some processing |

## Factors influencing the location of service businesses

Service businesses usually need to be **close to their customers**:

- **Customers and footfall**: shops, banks, hairdressers and restaurants need many passers-by, such as near bus terminuses and city centres.
- **Rent and costs**: busy sites cost more, so the business must balance footfall against rent.
- **Competitors**: some services cluster (furniture shops in one street, so customers compare); others avoid rivals.
- **Personal convenience**: small businesses often start near the owner's home.
- **Security, parking and access**: important for customers carrying goods or money.
- **Technology**: online businesses and call centres can be located almost anywhere with good internet and power.

:::example{title="Location of a hair salon"}
Nyasha compares two sites for her salon in Chinhoyi. Site 1 is in a quiet side street with low rent. Site 2 is near the bus terminus where hundreds of people pass every hour, but the rent is double. Because a salon depends on walk-in customers, Nyasha chooses Site 2: she expects the extra customers to more than cover the extra rent.
:::

## Comparing sites using costs

A business can compare the costs of possible sites. Here is a firm that delivers bottled water to customers:

| Monthly cost US\$ | Site A (city centre) | Site B (industrial area) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Rent | 1,500 | 700 |
| Wages | 600 | 650 |
| Transport of goods to customers | 400 | 900 |
| Total | 2,500 | 2,250 |

:::worked-solution[Comparing the total cost of two sites]
1. Site A total = 1,500 + 600 + 400 = US\$2,500 a month.
2. Site B total = 700 + 650 + 900 = US\$2,250 a month.
3. Site B is cheaper by 2,500 − 2,250 = **US\$250 a month**, or US\$3,000 a year.
4. But costs are only part of the decision: if Site A attracts walk-in customers, its extra sales could outweigh the extra cost. Qualitative factors (security, space to expand) also matter.
:::

:::worked-solution[Annual saving from relocating]
A clothing factory pays rent of US\$2,800 a month in Harare. A site in Kwekwe would cost US\$1,600 a month, but transport to its Harare customers would rise by US\$500 a month.
1. Saving on rent = 2,800 − 1,600 = US\$1,200 a month.
2. Net saving after extra transport = 1,200 − 500 = US\$700 a month.
3. Annual net saving = 700 × 12 = **US\$8,400**.
4. The firm must weigh this against the one-off cost of moving and the risk of losing skilled workers who will not move.
:::

## Deciding which country to operate in

When a business locates abroad (for example a factory in another country, or a mine owned by a foreign company), it considers:

- **Costs**: wages, land, energy and transport.
- **Markets**: being close to large or growing markets, and inside a trading bloc to avoid tariffs.
- **Government**: political stability, taxes, incentives, rules on foreign ownership and on taking profits home.
- **Skills and infrastructure**: workers, roads, ports, power and communications.
- **Exchange rates and currency stability.**
- **Laws and culture**: labour laws, environmental rules, language.

## Why businesses relocate

- **Raw materials run out** (a mine is exhausted, forests are cut down).
- **Markets decline or move** (customers move to new suburbs, or demand falls in the old area).
- **Costs rise** (rents, wages, rates) or cheaper sites become available.
- **Natural disasters** (floods, droughts, cyclones) damage the site or make it risky.
- **Government policy** (new incentives elsewhere, new rules, compulsory acquisition of land, rezoning).
- **Need to expand** when the existing site is too small.

Relocation has costs: moving equipment, lost production during the move, redundancy or relocation payments for staff, and the risk of losing customers who were used to the old site.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving every factor the same weight"}
For a bakery, nearness to customers matters most; for a mine, the raw material decides. Explain which factor matters **most** for the business in the case.
:::

:::mistake{title="Choosing the cheapest site without thinking about revenue"}
A cheap site with few customers may earn less profit than a costly site with many customers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the costs of relocating"}
Moving saves money only if the savings outweigh one-off moving costs and lost sales.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend a location"}
Compare the options using the case's data (costs, distance to customers, labour), add at least one qualitative factor, and give a justified decision. Show your working if you calculate total or annual costs.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 separates manufacturing, service and country decisions and asks about the role of government. Business Enterprise and Skills focuses on location and relocation factors for enterprises; apply them to small Zimbabwean businesses.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Manufacturing location depends on raw materials, markets, transport, labour, power and water, land, government policy and related firms.
- Bulk-reducing industries locate near raw materials; bulk-increasing and perishable-product industries locate near markets.
- Service businesses usually locate near customers, balancing footfall against rent.
- Choosing a country involves costs, markets, government, skills, infrastructure and currency.
- Businesses relocate when raw materials run out, markets change, costs rise, disasters strike, policy changes or they need space.
:::
