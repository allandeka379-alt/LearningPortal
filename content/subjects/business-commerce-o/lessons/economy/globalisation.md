## Introduction

Walk through a Zimbabwean supermarket and you will find rice from Asia, tinned fish from southern Africa, phones assembled in China and clothes made in Bangladesh, next to local maize meal, cooking oil and soap. Zimbabwean tobacco, flowers, minerals and macadamia nuts are sold all over the world. Small businesses order stock online from other continents and sell crafts to tourists who pay by card. This growing connection between the economies of the world is **globalisation**.

Cambridge IGCSE Business (0264, topic 6.2.1) asks for the **reasons** for globalisation and the **opportunities and threats** it creates for businesses. Import tariffs and import quotas, the other part of 6.2.1, are covered in the lesson on protectionism. Paper 2 expects you to apply these ideas to a business in a case study and to recommend what it should do.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Globalisation"}
The increasing worldwide integration of economies through trade in goods and services, the movement of capital and people, and the spread of technology and ideas, so that the world acts more like one market.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade agreement"}
An agreement between countries to reduce or remove barriers to trade, such as tariffs and quotas, between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Newly industrialised country (NIC)"}
A country whose economy has moved quickly from relying mainly on agriculture and raw materials towards manufacturing and services, and which now trades heavily with the rest of the world.
:::

:::definition{term="Multinational company (MNC)"}
A business with its headquarters in one country and production or service operations in more than one country.
:::

## Reasons for globalisation

### Improved transport links

Container ships, larger aircraft, better roads and rail links, and containerised intermodal transport have made moving goods between continents cheaper, faster and more reliable. A container can travel from a factory in Asia to an inland terminal in southern Africa without being opened.

### Technological change, including communication

The internet, mobile phones, email, video calls and e-commerce let businesses find suppliers and customers anywhere, place orders instantly and pay electronically. Managers can control operations in several countries from one office. Information about products and prices spreads quickly.

### Free trade agreements

Countries have joined regional and international agreements that lower tariffs and other barriers. Firms can sell into larger markets on better terms, and imports become cheaper. Zimbabwe, for example, belongs to regional groupings in southern and eastern Africa that aim to reduce barriers to trade between members.

### Newly industrialised countries

Countries that have industrialised rapidly produce large quantities of manufactured goods at low cost and have growing numbers of consumers with rising incomes. They are both strong competitors and large new markets.

## Opportunities of globalisation for businesses

- **New markets**: a business can sell abroad, increasing sales and spreading risk across countries. Example: a Zimbabwean macadamia grower sells to buyers on several continents.
- **Cheaper inputs**: raw materials, components and machinery can be bought from the cheapest suppliers worldwide, lowering costs.
- **Economies of scale**: selling to a larger market allows larger output and lower average costs.
- **Locating production abroad**: firms can set up operations where costs are lower or nearer to markets, avoiding transport costs and trade barriers.
- **Access to technology and ideas**: firms learn new methods and can buy modern equipment.
- **Partnerships and investment**: joint ventures with foreign firms bring capital and expertise.

## Threats of globalisation for businesses

- **More competition at home**: imports, sometimes cheaper or of higher quality, compete with local products. Local firms may lose sales and have to cut prices.
- **Competition for workers and resources**: multinationals may attract the best workers with higher pay.
- **Takeover risk**: larger foreign firms may buy out successful local businesses.
- **Exchange-rate and international risks**: trading abroad exposes firms to changing exchange rates, foreign laws and political events.
- **Dependence on distant suppliers**: problems far away (a port closure, a shortage) can stop supplies.
- **Pressure to cut costs**: firms competing with low-cost producers may need to automate or cut jobs.

:::example{title="Opportunity and threat for one business"}
Rudo's company in Chitungwiza makes school shoes. Cheap imported shoes now fill the market, and her sales have fallen: a threat. But globalisation also lets her buy modern stitching machines and synthetic soles from overseas at lower prices, and she has started selling to retailers in neighbouring countries through a regional trade agreement: opportunities. Her response is to compete on durability and fit, which buyers of cheap imports often find poor, rather than on price alone.
:::

## How businesses respond to globalisation

1. **Improve quality and efficiency**: invest in machinery and training to match foreign rivals.
2. **Focus on a niche**: offer products imports do not, for example local tastes, fast service or after-sales support.
3. **Market the local advantage**: "made locally", fresher, supporting local jobs.
4. **Export**: use trade agreements and e-commerce to sell abroad.
5. **Form partnerships**: joint ventures or supply agreements with foreign firms.
6. **Ask for protection**: some businesses lobby government for tariffs or quotas (see protectionism).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing reasons without explaining the link"}
"Technology" alone is not an explanation. Explain how it causes globalisation: the internet lets firms find foreign customers and suppliers and trade with them cheaply.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
Globalisation brings both opportunities and threats, and the balance differs between businesses. An exporter with low costs gains; a high-cost local producer facing cheap imports may lose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing globalisation with multinationals"}
Multinationals are one result and driver of globalisation, not the same thing. Globalisation also includes trade by small firms, flows of capital and the spread of technology.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 2: apply to the case"}
All 0264 Paper 2 part (a) questions are applied. Name the business, its products and its customers in every point: "Imports of cheaper shoes reduce the company's sales in its home market."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend with justification"}
For "Do you think globalisation is good for this business? Justify your answer", weigh one opportunity against one threat for this business and decide which matters more, with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Globalisation is the growing integration of the world's economies through trade, investment, people and technology.
- Causes: improved transport links, technology and communication, free trade agreements and newly industrialised countries.
- Opportunities: new markets, cheaper inputs, economies of scale, production abroad, new technology and partnerships.
- Threats: more competition from imports, competition for workers, takeovers, exchange-rate risk and dependence on distant suppliers.
- Businesses respond by improving quality and efficiency, finding a niche, exporting and forming partnerships.
:::
