## Introduction

A residents' association in a Bulawayo suburb objects to plans for a quarry next to its homes. An environmental group campaigns against a company that is clearing woodland. A consumer organisation publishes test results showing that some brands of cooking oil are not what their labels claim. Each of these groups has no power to make the business change, yet each may succeed. They are **pressure groups**.

Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.5) expects you to explain what pressure groups are, how they influence business decisions and how businesses respond. Examiners often describe a campaign in a case study and ask whether the business should give in to the pressure group's demands.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pressure group"}
An organisation of people with a common interest who try to influence the decisions of businesses, governments or the public, without seeking to form a government.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumer boycott"}
When customers refuse to buy a business's products in order to force it to change its behaviour.
:::

:::definition{term="Lobbying"}
Trying to persuade government officials and members of parliament to change laws or policies.
:::

## Types of pressure groups

| Type | What they want | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Environmental groups | Protection of land, water, forests, wildlife; less pollution | Groups campaigning against deforestation or river pollution |
| Consumer groups | Fair prices, safe and honest products | National consumer organisations such as the Consumer Council of Zimbabwe |
| Community and residents' groups | Protect a local area from noise, traffic, pollution or unwanted developments | A residents' association opposing a quarry |
| Human rights and labour groups | Fair wages, safe conditions, no child labour | Groups campaigning for workers' rights |
| Animal welfare groups | Better treatment of animals | Groups opposing cruel farming methods |
| Business groups | The interests of their members, e.g. lower taxes | Chambers of commerce, associations of manufacturers or farmers |

Trade unions also act as pressure groups for their members. Some groups exist only for a single issue (one quarry, one road); others campaign on many issues for years.

## Methods used by pressure groups

- **Publicity and media campaigns**: newspaper articles, radio interviews, social media posts, petitions and reports that damage a business's reputation.
- **Consumer boycotts**: urging customers to stop buying the business's products.
- **Lobbying the government**: persuading officials to pass laws, refuse planning permission or enforce existing rules.
- **Protests and demonstrations**: peaceful marches or gatherings outside premises to attract attention.
- **Legal action**: taking a business to court for breaking environmental or consumer laws.
- **Working with businesses**: advising firms on better practices, certifying good products, or buying shares to raise issues at shareholder meetings.

:::example{title="A campaign against a polluting factory"}
A chemical factory near Kwekwe releases waste into a stream. A local environmental group tests the water and publishes the results on social media and in a newspaper. It organises a petition, asks residents to stop buying the factory's products and asks local officials to enforce pollution laws. Within months, sales in the area fall and inspectors visit the factory. The factory installs a waste-treatment system and announces it publicly.
:::

## What makes a pressure group effective?

| More effective when … | Less effective when … |
| --- | --- |
| It has many members and strong public support | It is small and little known |
| It gets wide media coverage | The media ignore it |
| The business depends on consumers who care about the issue | The business sells to other businesses or has few alternatives for customers |
| Consumers can easily switch to rivals | The product is essential and has no substitutes |
| Its claims are backed by evidence | Its claims are weak or exaggerated |
| The government is sympathetic | The government supports the business (for jobs or taxes) |
| It has money to fund campaigns and legal action | It has little finance |

## How businesses respond to pressure groups

| Response | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accept** the demands and change | Protects reputation; ends the campaign; may win new customers | Costs money; may reduce profit; may encourage further demands |
| **Negotiate** a compromise | Shows willingness to listen; limits costs | May not satisfy the group or the public |
| **Ignore** the campaign | No immediate cost | Risk of boycott, bad publicity, legal action and new laws |
| **Counter-campaign**: publicise the business's side (jobs, benefits, actions already taken) | Corrects false claims; keeps support | May look defensive; costs money; can make the dispute more public |

## Why pressure groups have become more powerful

Pressure groups have always existed, but several changes have made them harder for businesses to ignore:

- **Social media** lets a small group share photographs, videos and test results with thousands of people in a few hours, at almost no cost. A single post about a polluted stream or an unsafe product can spread across the country.
- **Better-informed consumers** are more likely to ask where products come from, how workers are treated and what happens to waste.
- **Mobile phones** make it easy to collect evidence and to organise petitions and meetings.
- **Investors and lenders** increasingly check whether the businesses they finance treat the environment and workers responsibly, so a campaign can affect a firm's access to finance.

:::example{title="How one business turned a campaign into an opportunity"}
A fictional soft-drinks company in Harare was criticised by a local environmental group because its empty plastic bottles littered streets and blocked drains. At first the company ignored the campaign, and sales in some suburbs fell as photographs of bottle-choked drains spread online. The directors then met the group and agreed a plan: a deposit on every bottle, collection points at shops and a partnership with informal waste collectors, who are paid for each bottle returned. Litter fell, collectors gained an income and the company advertised its new scheme. Sales recovered, and the group publicly praised the change. Meeting the group early would have saved the company several months of lost sales.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of pressure groups for business

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Warn the business of public concerns early | Bad publicity can reduce sales quickly |
| Push businesses towards better practices that customers value | Changes may raise costs |
| Can help businesses avoid future legal problems | Claims are sometimes exaggerated or unfair |
| Some certify good practice, helping marketing | Campaigns take management time |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing pressure groups with government"}
Pressure groups have **no legal power** to make a business change. They influence it through customers, the media and the government.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a business must always give in"}
Whether to give in depends on the cost of the change, the strength of the campaign, the evidence and the effect on sales. Evaluate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing methods without effects"}
Explain how each method affects the business: "a boycott reduces sales revenue; lobbying may lead to stricter laws that raise costs".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Judge the group's power"}
Before deciding how a business should respond, use the case to judge how powerful the group is: media coverage, public support, how easily customers can switch.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Weigh costs against risks"}
"Changing will cost US\$X, but a boycott could cost much more in lost sales and reputation" is the kind of judgement examiners reward.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge 0264 only"}
Pressure groups are in the Cambridge syllabus (6.5.1). Questions often combine them with environmental issues and ethics.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pressure groups try to influence businesses, governments and the public without seeking political power.
- They use publicity, boycotts, lobbying, protests and legal action.
- They are most effective with wide support, media coverage, evidence and customers who can easily switch.
- Businesses may accept, negotiate, ignore or counter-campaign, each with costs and risks.
- Pressure groups can push businesses towards better practices but can also damage sales and raise costs.
:::
