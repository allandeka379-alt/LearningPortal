## Introduction

Governments try to manage the economy: to keep prices stable, reduce unemployment, encourage growth and keep a healthy balance with other countries. To do this they change **taxes**, **government spending** and **interest rates**. Every one of these changes affects businesses: their costs, their customers' spending and their plans to invest.

Imagine a furniture maker in Gweru with a bank loan. If the interest rate rises, its loan becomes more expensive. If income tax rises, its customers have less to spend. If the government spends more on building schools, it may win an order for desks. Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.1.2) expects you to explain how these policies affect businesses and how businesses may respond. Rates used in this lesson are **assumptions for practice**, not actual current rates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fiscal policy"}
Government decisions on taxation and government spending.
:::

:::definition{term="Monetary policy"}
Decisions on interest rates (and the supply of money), usually made by the central bank.
:::

:::definition{term="Interest rate"}
The cost of borrowing money, or the reward for saving, shown as a percentage of the amount per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Disposable income"}
The income people have left to spend or save after paying income tax.
:::

## Taxes

| Tax | Who pays | Main effect on businesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax on profits (corporation tax) | Businesses, on their profits | Less profit left for owners and for reinvestment; may discourage investment |
| Income tax | Individuals, on their income | Changes customers' disposable income and so demand for products |
| Taxes on spending (e.g. VAT) | Paid by consumers through prices; collected by businesses | Higher prices reduce demand; more administration for businesses |
| Import duties (tariffs) | Importers | Imported materials cost more; local producers protected from foreign competition |

### Higher tax on profits

A higher tax on profits leaves less **profit after tax** for the owners and less **retained profit** to invest in the business. Investors may be less willing to set up or expand businesses.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a rise in tax on profits]
A clothing company makes a profit of US\$48,000. Assume that the tax on profits rises from 25% to 30%.

1. Tax at 25% = 25% of US\$48,000 = US\$12,000; profit after tax = US\$48,000 − US\$12,000 = US\$36,000.
2. Tax at 30% = 30% of US\$48,000 = US\$14,400; profit after tax = US\$48,000 − US\$14,400 = US\$33,600.
3. The company has US\$36,000 − US\$33,600 = US\$2,400 less to pay its owners or reinvest, for example in new machines.
:::

### Higher income tax

When income tax rises, people's **disposable income** falls, so they spend less. Businesses selling luxuries are hit hardest; demand for necessities changes little. A cut in income tax has the opposite effect. Higher income tax may also reduce workers' incentive to work longer hours, and workers may ask for pay rises to protect their take-home pay.

:::example{title="Income tax and take-home pay"}
Assume a factory worker in Kwekwe earns US\$600 a month. With income tax at 20% her take-home pay is US\$480. If the rate rises to 25%, it falls to US\$450. She has US\$30 a month less to spend in local shops, and thousands of workers in the same position mean a noticeable fall in demand.
:::

### Higher taxes on spending

A rise in a tax such as VAT increases the price customers pay. Demand falls, especially for products where customers can easily switch or do without. Businesses may absorb part of the tax by cutting their own margin to protect sales.

## Government spending

The government spends on schools, hospitals, roads, the police and defence, and on the wages of public sector workers.

- **More government spending** increases demand: construction firms win contracts to build roads and clinics; suppliers of desks, uniforms and medicines gain orders; public sector workers spend their wages in local businesses.
- **Better infrastructure** (roads, power, water) reduces business costs.
- **Cuts in government spending** reduce orders for businesses that depend on government contracts and reduce the income of public sector workers, so demand falls.

## Interest rates

### Effects of a rise in interest rates

- **Higher cost of loans and overdrafts**: businesses with variable-rate loans pay more interest, so profit falls.
- **Less investment**: new projects become more expensive to finance, so businesses delay expansion.
- **Lower consumer spending**: customers with loans pay more interest and have less to spend; goods usually bought on credit (cars, furniture, houses) are hit hardest.
- **More saving**: higher rates encourage people to save rather than spend.
- **Exchange rate**: higher interest rates can raise the value of the currency, making exports dearer.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a rise in interest rates]
A transport business has a bank loan of US\$40,000. Assume the interest rate rises from 8% to 12% a year. Its profit before the change was US\$26,000.

1. Interest at 8% = 8% of US\$40,000 = US\$3,200 a year.
2. Interest at 12% = 12% of US\$40,000 = US\$4,800 a year.
3. Extra interest = US\$4,800 − US\$3,200 = US\$1,600 a year.
4. If nothing else changes, profit falls to US\$26,000 − US\$1,600 = US\$24,400. The business may also delay buying another vehicle.
:::

A **fall** in interest rates has the opposite effects: cheaper loans, more investment and more consumer spending.

## How businesses may respond

| Policy change | Possible business responses |
| --- | --- |
| Higher tax on profits | Cut costs; reduce dividends; delay investment; look for tax allowances on investment |
| Higher income tax | Offer cheaper products; promotions; focus on necessities; target customers less affected |
| Higher VAT | Absorb part of the tax; emphasise value for money |
| Higher interest rates | Repay loans early; use retained profit instead of loans; reduce stock; delay expansion; offer customers their own credit terms |
| More government spending | Bid for government contracts; increase capacity |
| Less government spending | Find private-sector customers; cut costs |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing tax on profits and income tax"}
Tax on profits is paid by the **business**. Income tax is paid by **people**; it affects the business through its customers' spending (and its workers' pay demands).
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one effect of interest rates"}
A rise affects the business's own loans **and** its customers' spending. Explain both when the question allows.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating real tax rates as facts"}
In answers and calculations, use the rates given in the question. Do not invent current national rates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Chain your analysis"}
"Interest rates rise → customers' loan repayments rise → less disposable income → fewer furniture sales → lower revenue for the business." Each link earns analysis marks.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Match the effect to the product"}
Products bought on credit and luxuries are most sensitive to interest rates and income tax. Necessities are least sensitive.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge 0264 only"}
This topic is in the Cambridge syllabus (6.1.2). It is often combined with the business cycle (6.1.1): for example, a government raising interest rates to reduce inflation in a boom.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Governments use taxes, government spending (fiscal policy) and interest rates (monetary policy) to manage the economy.
- Higher tax on profits reduces profit after tax and investment; higher income tax reduces disposable income and demand.
- More government spending raises demand and improves infrastructure; cuts reduce orders and demand.
- Higher interest rates raise borrowing costs, reduce investment and reduce consumer spending, especially on goods bought on credit.
- Businesses respond by cutting costs, changing prices and products, changing finance and timing investment.
:::
