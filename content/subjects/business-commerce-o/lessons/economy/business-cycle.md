## Introduction

Over several years, an economy rarely grows at a steady pace. There are periods when incomes rise, shops are busy and businesses expand, and periods when spending falls, firms cut jobs and some close. These ups and downs in the level of economic activity are called the **business cycle**.

A business cannot control the economy, but it must respond to it. A car dealer in Harare, a furniture maker in Bulawayo and a supermarket in Gweru are all affected differently when the economy changes. Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.1) expects you to describe the stages of the business cycle, explain how each affects businesses and explain the effects of changes in employment, inflation and economic growth. Examiners often give a case and ask how a business should respond to a recession or to rising inflation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business cycle"}
The regular pattern of rises and falls in economic activity (output, incomes and employment) over time.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross domestic product (GDP)"}
The total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. A rise in real GDP is **economic growth**.
:::

:::definition{term="Inflation"}
A sustained rise in the general level of prices, which reduces the purchasing power of money.
:::

:::definition{term="Unemployment"}
When people who are willing and able to work cannot find a job.
:::

## The stages of the business cycle

| Stage | What happens in the economy | Typical effects on businesses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Growth** (recovery) | GDP rises; incomes and spending increase; unemployment falls | Sales rise; businesses invest and recruit; profits improve |
| **Boom** | GDP is high and rising fast; demand may exceed supply; prices and wages rise (inflation); skills shortages | High sales and profits, but rising costs; difficulty finding skilled workers; capacity stretched |
| **Recession** | GDP falls for a period; incomes and spending fall; unemployment rises | Sales and profits fall; businesses cut costs, delay investment, reduce staff; some fail |
| **Slump** | A long, deep fall in GDP; very high unemployment; very low spending | Many businesses close; prices may be cut to attract customers; investment stops |

After a slump the economy usually recovers, and the cycle begins again with growth. Not every business is affected in the same way: sellers of **luxuries** (cars, holidays, furniture) are hit much harder in a recession than sellers of **necessities** (basic food, soap, medicines).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: luxuries and necessities in a recession]
During a recession, a furniture shop's annual sales fall from US\$48,000 to US\$33,600. A grocery shop's sales fall from US\$52,000 to US\$49,400.

1. Furniture shop: fall = US\$48,000 − US\$33,600 = US\$14,400; percentage fall = 14,400 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 30%.
2. Grocery shop: fall = US\$52,000 − US\$49,400 = US\$2,600; percentage fall = 2,600 ÷ 52,000 × 100 = 5%.
3. Furniture is a luxury that people can put off buying, so its sales fall six times as fast. Basic groceries are needed whatever happens to incomes.
:::

## How businesses respond to each stage

### In growth and boom

- **Expand capacity**: new machinery, more staff, new branches.
- **Raise prices** if demand exceeds supply.
- **Launch new products** to take advantage of higher incomes.
- **Watch costs**: rising wages and materials can squeeze profit; plan for the next downturn.

### In recession and slump

- **Cut costs**: reduce overtime, delay investment, negotiate with suppliers, and in the last resort reduce staff.
- **Lower prices or offer cheaper products** to keep customers.
- **Protect cash**: reduce stock, chase debts, avoid new borrowing.
- **Look for new markets**, such as exports to countries whose economies are growing.
- **Diversify** into products whose demand is more stable.

## Effects of changes in employment, inflation and economic growth

### Unemployment

- **Rising unemployment** means lower incomes, so demand falls, especially for luxuries.
- But it is **easier to recruit** workers and wage rises are smaller.
- Falling unemployment raises demand but makes skilled staff harder and dearer to find.

### Inflation

- **Costs rise**: materials, fuel and wages cost more.
- Businesses may raise prices, but if customers' incomes do not keep up, **sales fall**.
- Prices of exports rise compared with foreign competitors, so they become **less competitive**.
- **Planning is harder**: it is difficult to set prices and budgets when costs keep changing.
- Businesses with loans may gain slightly because the real value of the debt falls, but lenders often raise interest rates.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: inflation squeezes profit]
A printing business has annual sales revenue of US\$70,000 and total costs of US\$58,000. Inflation raises its total costs by 10%, but competition stops it raising its prices, so revenue stays the same.

1. Profit before inflation = US\$70,000 − US\$58,000 = US\$12,000.
2. New total costs = US\$58,000 + 10% of US\$58,000 = US\$58,000 + US\$5,800 = US\$63,800.
3. Profit after inflation = US\$70,000 − US\$63,800 = US\$6,200.
4. Profit margin falls from 12,000 ÷ 70,000 × 100 = 17.1% to 6,200 ÷ 70,000 × 100 = 8.9%. A 10% rise in costs has almost halved profit.
:::

### Economic growth

- Rising incomes increase **demand**, so sales and profits rise.
- Businesses can **expand** and create jobs, with more confidence to invest.
- Fast growth can lead to **inflation** and skills shortages (the boom stage).
- Falling growth (a recession) reduces demand and causes the problems above.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying every business suffers equally in a recession"}
Sellers of luxuries suffer most. Sellers of necessities, and of cheaper alternatives (second-hand goods, budget brands), may be less affected or even gain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating inflation as only bad for sales"}
Explain both sides: costs rise and planning becomes harder; firms may raise prices, but customers' real incomes fall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up boom and growth"}
Growth is the recovery stage when GDP starts rising. A boom is the peak, when demand is so high that prices and wages rise quickly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name the stage and link to the product"}
"In a recession, demand for Zeta Motors' new cars will fall sharply because cars are luxuries that customers can delay buying."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the figures"}
If a case gives sales or costs, calculate the change: a percentage fall or a fall in profit makes your analysis precise.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge 0264 only"}
The business cycle and the effects of employment, inflation and economic growth are in the Cambridge syllabus (6.1.1). Questions often combine them with government policy (6.1.2).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The business cycle has four stages: growth, boom, recession and slump.
- In growth and boom, sales and profits rise but costs and skills shortages increase.
- In recession and slump, sales fall, unemployment rises and businesses cut costs; luxuries suffer most.
- Inflation raises costs, makes planning difficult and can reduce export competitiveness.
- Unemployment reduces demand but eases recruitment; economic growth raises demand and confidence.
:::
