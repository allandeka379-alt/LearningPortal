## Introduction

A cross-border trader from Bulawayo buys goods in South Africa priced in rand and sells them in Zimbabwe for US dollars. A tobacco exporter receives US dollars, but some of its buyers pay in other currencies. A Harare hardware shop imports tools from China. For all of them, the **exchange rate**, the price of one currency in terms of another, decides how much they pay and how much they earn.

Prices in this lesson are written in US dollars, the main currency for business in Zimbabwe; ZiG is also used in the country. **All exchange rates in this lesson are assumed for practice and are not real rates.**

ZIMSEC Commerce covers exchange rates in Form 4 with the balance of payments, including converting amounts. Cambridge IGCSE Business (0264, 6.2.4) covers appreciation and depreciation and their effects on importers and exporters; **Cambridge does not assess exchange-rate calculations**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Exchange rate"}
The price of one currency expressed in terms of another, for example "US\$1 = 18 rand".
:::

:::definition{term="Appreciation"}
A rise in the value of a currency against another: one unit of it buys **more** of the other currency.
:::

:::definition{term="Depreciation"}
A fall in the value of a currency against another: one unit of it buys **less** of the other currency.
:::

:::definition{term="Competitiveness"}
How attractive a business's products are to buyers compared with those of rivals, especially in price.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign exchange (forex)"}
Foreign currency used to pay for imports and received from exports.
:::

## Reading an exchange rate

If US\$1 = 18 rand, then one US dollar buys 18 rand, and one rand is worth $\frac{1}{18}$ of a dollar.

- If the rate moves to US\$1 = 20 rand, each dollar buys **more** rand: the **US dollar has appreciated** against the rand, and the **rand has depreciated** against the dollar.
- If the rate moves to US\$1 = 16 rand, each dollar buys **less** rand: the dollar has **depreciated** and the rand has **appreciated**.

Exchange rates change because of the demand for and supply of currencies, influenced by trade, interest rates, inflation, investment and confidence.

## How exchange-rate changes affect businesses

Think about the business's **own** currency (the one it keeps its accounts in).

### When the home currency appreciates

- **Exporters**: their goods become **more expensive** for foreign buyers in the buyers' currency. Sales may fall, or the exporter must cut its home-currency price and accept lower profit. Exports become **less competitive**.
- **Importers**: foreign goods and raw materials become **cheaper** in the home currency. Costs fall, and an importer can lower prices or earn a higher profit margin.

### When the home currency depreciates

- **Exporters**: their goods become **cheaper** abroad. Sales and competitiveness rise, or the exporter can raise prices and margins.
- **Importers**: imported goods and materials become **dearer**. Costs rise; prices may have to rise, so sales may fall.

| Change in the home currency | Exporters | Importers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appreciation (stronger) | Prices abroad rise; less competitive | Imports cheaper; costs fall |
| Depreciation (weaker) | Prices abroad fall; more competitive | Imports dearer; costs rise |

A memory aid often used: **SPICED** = **S**trong **P**ound (currency), **I**mports **C**heap, **E**xports **D**ear. It works for any currency.

:::example{title="A business that both imports and exports"}
Sipho's company in Bulawayo makes leather shoes. It buys tanning chemicals from a supplier in Botswana, priced in pula, and exports some shoes to Botswana. When the US dollar appreciates against the pula, the chemicals cost fewer dollars, which lowers costs; but its shoes become dearer for Botswana customers, so export sales may fall. The overall effect depends on which matters more to the business, and on how sensitive Botswana customers are to price.
:::

### Other effects

- **Uncertainty**: frequent changes make it hard to set prices and plan, and contracts agreed months in advance may become unprofitable.
- **Competition at home**: when the home currency appreciates, imports become cheaper and local producers face more competition.
- **Price elasticity matters**: if foreign buyers are not very sensitive to price (for example because of quality or brand loyalty), an appreciation reduces sales only a little.

## ZIMSEC Commerce: converting between currencies

When the rate is "US\$1 = $r$ units of foreign currency":

$$
\text{foreign amount} = \text{dollar amount} \times r \qquad \text{dollar amount} = \frac{\text{foreign amount}}{r}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 1: converting both ways]
Assume US\$1 = 18.50 rand. Thandeka buys stock priced at 9,250 rand in Johannesburg. She also has US\$640 to change into rand for her next trip.

1. US\$ cost of the stock $= \frac{9{,}250}{18.50} = 500$, so US\$500.
2. Rand received for US\$640 $= 640 \times 18.50 = 11{,}840$ rand.
3. Check: $500 \times 18.50 = 9{,}250$ rand. ✓
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the effect of a change in the rate]
A Zimbabwean exporter sells a product for US\$40 to buyers in South Africa. An importer buys a machine priced at 27,000 rand. The rate changes from US\$1 = 16 rand to US\$1 = 18 rand.

| | Before: US\$1 = 16 rand | After: US\$1 = 18 rand |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Exporter: price of a US\$40 item in rand | 640 | 720 |
| Importer: US\$ cost of a machine priced 27,000 rand | 1,687.50 | 1,500 |

1. The US dollar has **appreciated** (each dollar buys 2 more rand).
2. Exporter: $40 \times 16 = 640$ rand before; $40 \times 18 = 720$ rand after. South African buyers pay 80 rand more, so the product is less competitive.
3. Importer: $\frac{27{,}000}{16} = 1{,}687.50$ dollars before; $\frac{27{,}000}{18} = 1{,}500$ dollars after. The machine costs US\$187.50 less.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Multiplying when you should divide"}
With "US\$1 = 18 rand", rand amounts are larger numbers. To change rand into dollars **divide** by 18; to change dollars into rand **multiply**. Check that the answer makes sense: 9,250 rand cannot be worth more than 9,250 dollars.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction of change wrong"}
If US\$1 buys **more** rand than before, the dollar has appreciated, not depreciated. Say which currency you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying appreciation is always good"}
A stronger currency helps importers but hurts exporters. Always say which kind of business you are discussing.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge: explain the chain of effects"}
Use a chain: appreciation → exports dearer in foreign currency → fewer sales abroad → lower revenue and profit. No calculations are assessed, but you must apply the effect to the business in the case.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Commerce: show your working"}
Write the rate, the operation and the answer with the currency: "9,250 ÷ 18.50 = US\$500". Round money to the nearest cent only at the end.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.
- Appreciation: a currency buys more of another; depreciation: it buys less.
- A stronger home currency makes exports dearer and imports cheaper; a weaker one does the opposite.
- Exchange-rate changes affect prices, costs, profit, competitiveness and planning.
- ZIMSEC Commerce: multiply by the rate to get foreign currency, divide to get US dollars; Cambridge does not assess the calculation.
:::
