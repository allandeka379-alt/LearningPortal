## Introduction

A company wants to open a new lithium mine near a rural community. Its directors calculate the cost of machinery, wages and transport, and the revenue from selling the ore. If revenue is greater than cost, the mine is profitable for the company. But the decision affects many people who are not part of the company: villagers who gain jobs, farmers whose water becomes polluted, shopkeepers who gain customers, travellers on roads damaged by heavy lorries. These effects on outsiders are **external costs and benefits**.

Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.2) expects you to identify the external costs and benefits of a business decision, explain who is affected, and judge whether a decision is good for society as a whole, not only for the business. Case studies often describe a new factory, mine, road, hotel or shopping centre.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Private costs"}
Costs paid by the business itself as a result of its decision, such as wages, materials, buildings and machinery.
:::

:::definition{term="Private benefits"}
Benefits gained by the business itself, such as revenue and profit.
:::

:::definition{term="External costs"}
Costs of a business decision paid by others (the community, other businesses, the environment, the government) and not by the business itself. Examples: pollution, noise, congestion, damage to roads, loss of farmland.
:::

:::definition{term="External benefits"}
Benefits of a business decision gained by others and not by the business itself. Examples: new jobs, better roads built for the project, more customers for local shops, training of local people.
:::

:::definition{term="Social costs and social benefits"}
The total effect of a decision on society: social cost = private costs + external costs; social benefit = private benefits + external benefits.
:::

## Private and external effects of a new factory

:::example{title="A new cement factory near Gweru"}
| | Private (to the business) | External (to others) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Costs** | Building the factory, machinery, wages, limestone, power, transport | Dust and air pollution affecting health and crops; noise; heavy lorries damaging roads and causing congestion; loss of land used for grazing |
| **Benefits** | Revenue and profit from cement sales | Jobs for local people; more customers for local shops and transport operators; cheaper local cement for builders; tax revenue for the government; roads and water supplies built by the company that the community can also use |
:::

The business decides on the basis of its **private** costs and benefits. Society is interested in **all** the costs and benefits, so a decision that is profitable for the business might still be bad for society if the external costs are very large, and a decision that looks unprofitable might be good for society if the external benefits are large.

## Common external costs

- **Pollution** of air, water and land: smoke, chemicals, mine waste, plastic litter.
- **Noise** from factories, mines, nightclubs and airports.
- **Congestion** and accidents from extra traffic.
- **Damage to infrastructure**, such as roads worn out by heavy lorries.
- **Loss of natural resources**: deforestation, loss of wildlife habitats, lower water tables.
- **Loss of business for others**: a large supermarket may cause small local shops to close.
- **Health costs**: illness from pollution may raise costs for clinics and families.

## Common external benefits

- **Jobs** for local people, reducing unemployment and raising incomes.
- **The multiplier effect**: workers spend their wages in local shops, which then employ more people.
- **Improved infrastructure**: roads, electricity, water and clinics built by the business and used by the community.
- **Training**: skills learned by workers can be used elsewhere.
- **Tax revenue** for the government, which can fund public services.
- **New suppliers**: local businesses grow by supplying the new firm.
- **Cheaper or better products** available locally.

## Weighing up a decision

To judge whether a decision is good for society, compare **social benefits** with **social costs**.

| Question to ask | Example |
| --- | --- |
| How large is each effect? | Hundreds of jobs vs a small rise in noise |
| How long will it last? | Construction dust for a year vs river pollution for decades |
| Who is affected, and can they cope? | Poor farmers losing their only water source |
| Can the external costs be reduced? | Dust filters, replanting, building a bypass road |
| Can the harmed groups be compensated? | Payment for lost land, new boreholes |

:::example{title="Should a new hotel be built near a wildlife area?"}
External benefits: jobs for local people, more customers for craft sellers and guides, tax revenue, a new access road. External costs: disturbance to wildlife, more waste and water use, higher land prices for local people. If the hotel limits building, treats its waste and employs and trains local people, the social benefits are likely to be greater than the social costs; if it drains a wetland and imports all its staff, they may not be.
:::

## How governments respond

Governments may try to reduce external costs and encourage external benefits:

- **Laws and regulations**: limits on pollution, planning permission, environmental impact assessments.
- **Fines and taxes** on polluters, so the business pays part of the external cost.
- **Grants and subsidies** for projects with large external benefits, such as training schemes.

These links are developed in the lesson on business and the environment.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing the business's own costs as external"}
Wages and machinery are **private** costs paid by the business. External costs fall on **other people**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Only seeing costs"}
Most decisions have both external costs and external benefits. Balanced answers earn evaluation marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague effects"}
"It is bad for the environment" earns little. State the effect and who suffers: "dust from the quarry damages the crops of nearby farmers".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the stakeholders in the case"}
Name the people affected: "the 300 households in the village", "the tourists who use the road", "small shops in the town centre".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Judge the overall effect"}
For "should the government allow the factory to be built?", weigh the main external benefits against the main external costs, consider whether costs can be reduced, and decide.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge 0264 only"}
External costs and benefits are in the Cambridge syllabus (6.2.3) and are often combined with location decisions and environmental issues.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Private costs and benefits fall on the business; external costs and benefits fall on others.
- Social cost = private + external costs; social benefit = private + external benefits.
- External costs include pollution, noise, congestion, damage to roads and loss of resources.
- External benefits include jobs, the multiplier effect, infrastructure, training and tax revenue.
- A decision is good for society when social benefits outweigh social costs; governments use laws, fines and grants to influence external effects.
:::
