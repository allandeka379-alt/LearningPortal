## Introduction

Business activity provides jobs, goods and services, but it can also harm the natural environment. Smoke from factories, chemicals from mines entering rivers, plastic bags littering roadsides, forests cut down for timber and firewood, and overuse of water by large farms all damage the environment that communities and future businesses depend on.

Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.3) expects you to explain how business activity affects the environment, why and how businesses respond to environmental issues, and how environmental laws affect business decisions and costs. Examiners usually set a case in which a business must decide whether to spend money on reducing its environmental impact, and ask you to weigh the costs against the benefits.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollution"}
Harmful substances or noise released into the air, water or land as a result of human activity, including business production and transport.
:::

:::definition{term="Depletion of resources"}
Using up natural resources (minerals, forests, fish, water, soil fertility) faster than they can be replaced.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable production"}
Producing in a way that meets present needs without damaging the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental impact assessment (EIA)"}
A study, often required by law before a large project is approved, of how the project will affect the environment and how the harm can be reduced.
:::

## How business activity damages the environment

| Type of damage | Business examples | Effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air pollution | Factory chimneys, diesel lorries, burning waste, mine dust | Breathing problems, damage to crops, climate change |
| Water pollution | Mine and factory waste in rivers, fertiliser running off farms | Unsafe drinking water, death of fish, harm to farming |
| Land pollution | Dumped waste, plastic packaging, chemical spills | Unusable land, harm to livestock and wildlife |
| Noise pollution | Quarries, factories, nightclubs, airports | Stress and poor health for residents |
| Depletion of resources | Cutting forests for timber or tobacco curing, overfishing, overuse of groundwater, mining | Resources run out; soil erosion; loss of wildlife and tourism |
| Visual pollution | Mine dumps, billboards, poorly planned buildings | Damaged landscapes, fewer tourists |

## Why businesses respond to environmental issues

- **Legal requirements**: laws set limits on pollution and waste; breaking them leads to fines, closure or prosecution.
- **Customer pressure**: many customers prefer environmentally friendly products and may stop buying from polluters.
- **Pressure groups and the media**: campaigns and bad publicity damage a business's reputation.
- **Cost savings**: using less energy, water and materials and recycling waste can reduce costs.
- **Long-term survival**: businesses that depend on natural resources (farms, timber, tourism) must protect them to stay in business.
- **Ethical reasons**: owners and managers may believe it is the right thing to do (social responsibility).
- **Attracting staff and investors** who prefer responsible businesses.

## How businesses can reduce environmental damage

- **Cleaner production methods**: filters on chimneys, treating waste water before release.
- **Reduce, reuse, recycle**: less packaging, returnable bottles, recycled materials.
- **Efficient use of energy and water**: solar power, efficient machines, drip irrigation.
- **Sustainable sourcing**: timber from replanted forests; buying from responsible suppliers.
- **Proper waste disposal** and avoiding dumping.
- **Planning location** carefully, away from homes and sensitive areas.
- **Rehabilitation**: refilling and replanting land after mining.

Each of these usually has a **cost** (new equipment, more expensive materials), so it may raise prices or reduce profit in the short run. But some measures, such as saving energy or selling waste for recycling, may **lower** costs over time.

## Legal controls and their effects

Governments use laws to make businesses take responsibility for environmental damage. In Zimbabwe, an environmental agency enforces environmental law; for example, large projects such as mines and factories need an environmental impact assessment before they start. Legal controls include:

- **Limits** on the amount of pollution a business may release.
- **Rules on waste disposal** and bans on some materials or products.
- **Licences and permits** for activities such as mining, quarrying and cutting timber.
- **Fines and taxes** for polluters, and requirements to repair damage.
- **Planning rules** on where certain activities may take place.

| Legal controls affect … | Example | Effect on the business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What** is produced or sold | A ban on certain thin plastic packaging | Must change products or packaging; may lose a product line |
| **How** it is produced | Limits on smoke from chimneys | Must install filters or use cleaner fuels; change production methods |
| **Where** it produces or sells | Factories not allowed near rivers or homes | Location choices limited; may have to move |
| **Costs** | Fees for EIAs and permits; new equipment; fines | Costs rise; prices may rise or profit fall; some small firms cannot afford to comply |

:::example{title="A brick maker and new environmental rules"}
Farai makes bricks in kilns fired with firewood near Gweru. New rules require a permit to cut trees and limit smoke near homes. Farai must buy firewood from licensed suppliers, which costs more, and may need to move his kilns away from a housing area. His costs rise, so he may raise his prices. A rival that switches to coal or to cement blocks may gain customers. In the long run, the rules protect the forests on which Farai's supply of fuel depends.
:::

## Weighing environmental decisions

When a business decides whether to spend money on reducing its environmental impact, consider:

- **The cost** of the measure and the effect on price and profit.
- **The benefits**: avoiding fines, better reputation, cost savings, long-term supply of resources.
- **What customers want**, and whether competitors are doing the same.
- **The law**: whether the measure is required now or likely to be required soon.
- **The time scale**: short-term costs against long-term gains.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming environmental action only costs money"}
Many measures also save money (energy efficiency, recycling) or protect sales. Show both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing external costs with environmental issues"}
Environmental damage is one kind of external cost. This topic also asks why and how businesses respond and how laws change what they do.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating how the law affects business without detail"}
Be specific: does the law change **what** the business makes, **how** it makes it, **where** it operates, or its **costs**?
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the four effects of legal controls"}
For "explain how environmental laws could affect this business", use what, how, where and costs, applied to the case.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate the decision"}
"Should the business switch to more expensive environmentally friendly packaging?" needs costs, benefits, customers' views, competitors and a justified decision.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge 0264 only"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business activity causes air, water, land, noise and visual pollution and depletes natural resources.
- Businesses respond because of laws, customers, pressure groups, cost savings, long-term survival and ethics.
- Ways to reduce damage include cleaner methods, recycling, efficient use of energy and water, sustainable sourcing and rehabilitation.
- Legal controls affect what, how and where businesses produce and sell, and usually raise costs.
- Environmental decisions weigh short-term costs against long-term benefits.
:::
