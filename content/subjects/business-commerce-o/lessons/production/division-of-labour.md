## Introduction

In many rural homes a family grows its own maize, keeps a few chickens, repairs its own hut and makes its own clay pots. Nothing is sold, and nothing is bought. In a town, by contrast, a bus driver drives a bus all day and uses her wages to buy food, clothes and school fees from other people who specialise in producing those things.

The difference between these two ways of living is the difference between **direct** and **indirect production**. Indirect production depends on **specialisation** and the **division of labour**, and it is the reason trade and commerce exist. This Form 2 Commerce topic is a favourite for short "state" and "explain" questions and for essays that ask you to discuss advantages and disadvantages.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Direct production"}
Producing goods and services for one's own use (or one's family's use) rather than for sale. Also called subsistence production.
:::

:::definition{term="Indirect production"}
Producing goods and services for exchange (sale), and using the income to buy what others produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialisation"}
Concentrating on producing one particular good or service, or doing one particular task.
:::

:::definition{term="Division of labour"}
Splitting the production of a good into separate tasks or processes, with each worker (or group of workers) doing only one task.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass production"}
Producing very large quantities of identical (standardised) goods, usually using machines and an assembly line.
:::

## Direct and indirect production

| Feature | Direct production | Indirect production |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Own use | Sale or exchange |
| Skills | Many tasks done by one person | Each person specialises |
| Standard of living | Usually low: limited variety | Usually higher: wide variety of goods |
| Need for trade | Little or none | Trade, money and aids to trade are essential |
| Surplus | Little or none | A surplus is produced for sale |

Direct production is limited because one person cannot be good at everything and has no surplus to exchange. As people specialise and produce a **surplus**, they need a way to exchange it: first barter, then money, and then all the services of commerce.

## Types of specialisation

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| By person (by craft or profession) | Each person follows one occupation | A carpenter, a nurse, a mechanic |
| By process (division of labour) | Making one product is split into stages, each done by different workers | In a shirt factory: cutting, sewing, buttonholing, ironing, packing |
| By region | Areas produce what suits their climate, soil and resources | Tea and timber in the Eastern Highlands; cattle ranching in Matabeleland; mining along the Great Dyke |
| By country (international specialisation) | Countries produce what they are best at and trade | Zimbabwe exporting tobacco and minerals and importing vehicles and machinery |

:::example{title="Division of labour in a bakery"}
A large bakery in Harare does not have each worker make whole loaves. One team weighs and mixes the dough, another shapes it, a third operates the ovens, a fourth slices and wraps the bread and a fifth loads the delivery trucks. Each worker repeats one task and becomes very quick at it.
:::

## Advantages of the division of labour

**To the producer (employer):**

- **Higher output** per worker, because workers become skilled and fast at one task ("practice makes perfect").
- **Time saved** as workers do not move from one task or tool to another.
- **Machinery** can be used for repetitive tasks, raising output further.
- Workers can be placed in the task that suits their **abilities**.
- **Lower cost per unit**, so prices can be lower and profits higher.

**To the worker:**

- Training is quicker and cheaper because only one task must be learned.
- Workers become expert in their task.

**To the consumer and the country:**

- More goods of a standard quality at lower prices; a higher standard of living.

## Disadvantages of the division of labour

**To the worker:**

- **Monotony and boredom** from repeating the same task, which can lower motivation and lead to accidents.
- **Loss of craftsmanship**: no pride in making a complete product.
- **Risk of unemployment**: a worker with one narrow skill may struggle to find another job, and machines may replace workers.

**To the producer:**

- **Interdependence**: if one stage stops (a strike, an absent worker, a broken machine), the whole production line stops.
- Standardised products: less variety.

**To the country:**

- **Overdependence** on a few products: regional or national specialisation is risky if demand or prices fall (for example, a mining town when a mine closes).

## Mass production

Division of labour makes **mass production** possible. Goods are made in very large numbers, to the same design, often on an assembly line with machines doing much of the work.

Conditions needed for mass production:

- a **large market** (high, steady demand) to buy the output;
- **standardised products** that buyers accept;
- enough **capital** for machinery and large factories;
- reliable supplies of raw materials and power;
- good transport to distribute the goods.

:::example{title="Why a market size matters"}
A fictional company in Bulawayo installs an assembly line that can fill thousands of bottles of cooking oil a day. If it can sell only a few hundred bottles, the machines stand idle and the cost per bottle is high. Mass production only lowers costs when the market is large enough to buy the output.
:::

Mass production links to commerce: large quantities of goods must be stored (warehousing), transported, insured, advertised and sold through wholesalers and retailers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating specialisation and division of labour as identical"}
Specialisation is the broad idea (by person, process, region or country). Division of labour is specialisation **by process** within the production of one good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages without saying who gains"}
Many questions ask for advantages "to the worker" or "to the employer". An advantage to the employer (lower costs) is not an advantage to the worker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying mass production is always cheaper"}
Mass production lowers the cost per unit only if there is a large enough market and enough capital.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Point, then explain"}
"Higher output" earns a mark; "higher output because workers become skilled and quick through repeating one task" earns the explanation mark too.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Balanced essays"}
For "discuss the division of labour", give advantages and disadvantages, ideally grouped by who is affected, and finish with a conclusion (for example, that the gains in output usually outweigh the costs if job rotation is used to reduce boredom).
:::

This topic is in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2). It is not a separate topic in Business Enterprise and Skills or Cambridge 0264, but the ideas help when you explain productivity and efficiency.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Direct production is for one's own use; indirect production is for exchange and depends on trade.
- Specialisation can be by person, by process (division of labour), by region and by country.
- Division of labour raises output, saves time, allows machinery and lowers unit costs.
- It can cause monotony, loss of craftsmanship, unemployment risk and overdependence.
- Mass production needs a large market, standardised products, capital, materials and transport.
:::
