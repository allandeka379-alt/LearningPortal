## Introduction

A farmer in Mutoko who sells fresh tomatoes by the bucket earns far less per kilogram than a small factory that turns the same tomatoes into tomato sauce in labelled bottles. The factory has **added value**: customers are willing to pay much more for the processed, packaged, branded product than the raw materials cost.

Adding value is at the heart of every business. It is in all three syllabi: Cambridge 0264 asks what added value is and how a business can increase it; ZIMSEC Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills also ask about **value addition and beneficiation** of Zimbabwe's minerals and farm produce, and their benefits to the country. Expect a short calculation, an "explain how" question and, in ZIMSEC papers, an essay on beneficiation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Added value"}
The difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials (and components) used to make it.
:::

:::formula{name="Added value"}
Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials
:::

:::definition{term="Bought-in materials"}
Raw materials, components and other inputs that a business buys from other businesses to make its product.
:::

:::definition{term="Value addition"}
Processing or improving a product at any stage of production so that it can be sold for more than the cost of its inputs.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiation"}
Processing a country's raw materials, especially minerals, inside the country before export (for example refining ore into metal), so that more of the value is created at home.
:::

## Calculating added value

Added value is **not** the same as profit. The added value must still pay for wages, rent, electricity and other overheads; whatever is left is profit. A business with high added value has more room to cover these costs and make a profit.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: added value in a bakery]
Rudo's bakery sells each loaf for US\$1.20. The flour, yeast, sugar, oil and wrapper bought for each loaf cost US\$0.55. She sells 400 loaves a day.

1. Added value per loaf = selling price − cost of bought-in materials.
2. Added value per loaf = US\$1.20 − US\$0.55 = US\$0.65.
3. Added value per day = US\$0.65 × 400 = US\$260.
4. Rudo's wages, rent and fuel for the oven must be paid out of this US\$260; what remains is her profit.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: value added along a chain of production]
A cotton shirt passes through four businesses. The farmer buys seed and fertiliser worth US\$0.60 for the cotton in one shirt. The shirt maker buys the cloth for US\$5.00 plus buttons and thread for US\$0.50.

| Stage | Cost of bought-in materials US\$ | Selling price US\$ | Value added US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cotton farmer (primary) | 0.60 | 2.00 | 1.40 |
| Spinning and weaving mill (secondary) | 2.00 | 5.00 | 3.00 |
| Shirt maker (secondary) | 5.50 | 9.00 | 3.50 |
| Clothing retailer (tertiary) | 9.00 | 14.00 | 5.00 |
| **Total value added** |  |  | **12.90** |

1. Each stage: value added = selling price − cost of bought-in materials (for example the mill: US\$5.00 − US\$2.00 = US\$3.00).
2. Total value added = 1.40 + 3.00 + 3.50 + 5.00 = US\$12.90.
3. Check: final price US\$14.00 − materials from outside the chain (US\$0.60 + US\$0.50) = US\$12.90.
4. The farmer, who only grows the cotton, receives the smallest share of the value. A country that exports raw cotton gives away the later stages to other countries.
:::

## How a business can increase added value

A business can increase added value by **raising the price customers will pay**, by **reducing the cost of bought-in materials**, or both.

| Method | How it adds value | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Branding | A trusted brand name lets the business charge more | A local honey producer sells under its own label instead of in unlabelled jars |
| Higher quality | Better materials or workmanship justify a higher price | Hand-finished leather shoes |
| Processing | Turning a raw material into a more useful product | Peanuts into peanut butter |
| Packaging and presentation | Attractive, convenient packs | Dried vegetables in sealed, labelled packets |
| Convenience | Saving the customer time | Ready-cut, washed vegetables; home delivery |
| Customer service | Advice, after-sales service, guarantees | A hardware shop that installs the solar panels it sells |
| Cheaper bought-in materials | Bulk buying or a cheaper supplier, if quality is kept | A bakery buying flour in bulk |

Each method has a cost. Branding needs advertising, and better quality needs better materials; added value rises only if the extra price is greater than the extra cost of materials. Cheaper materials can backfire if quality falls and customers stop buying.

## Value addition and beneficiation in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe has rich natural resources: minerals such as gold, platinum, chrome, diamonds and lithium, and farm produce such as tobacco, cotton, maize, tea, coffee and fruit. Selling these unprocessed earns the least value. Value addition and beneficiation mean doing more of the processing at home.

:::example{title="Value addition at each stage"}
- **Farm produce:** maize into maize meal and stock feed; cotton into yarn, cloth and clothing; milk into yoghurt and cheese; fruit into juice and jam; hides into leather and shoes.
- **Minerals (beneficiation):** chrome ore smelted into ferrochrome; gold refined and made into jewellery; granite cut and polished into tiles; lithium ore processed into concentrate and chemicals.
:::

### Benefits

| To the producer | To the community | To the country |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher selling price and profit per unit | More local jobs in processing | More export earnings: processed goods sell for more |
| Products last longer (processed food), so less waste | Skills and training | Less dependence on raw-material prices |
| Access to new markets | Growth of supporting businesses (transport, packaging) | Industrial growth and technology transfer |
| A brand that customers recognise | Better infrastructure (roads, power) | More tax revenue for government |

### Challenges

Value addition needs **capital** for factories and machinery, a reliable supply of **electricity and water**, **skilled workers**, good **transport** and access to **markets** that will buy the processed product. Processed goods may also face competition from large foreign producers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling added value profit"}
Added value is selling price minus bought-in materials only. Wages, rent and other overheads are paid out of it; profit is what is left.
:::

:::mistake{title="Subtracting all costs"}
Do not subtract wages or rent when calculating added value: they are not bought-in materials.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a higher price always adds value"}
If the business raises its price and customers stop buying, total added value falls. Value is added only when customers accept the higher price.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Show the formula"}
Write "added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials" before you substitute numbers. If your arithmetic slips you can still earn the method mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain how, not just what"}
"Branding" alone earns one mark. "Branding allows the business to charge a higher price because customers trust the name, increasing added value" earns the full explanation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 (1.1) asks for the concept of adding value and how it can be increased, applied to the business in the stimulus. ZIMSEC Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills add value addition at each stage of production and beneficiation of local resources, with benefits to the producer, community and nation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials; it is not profit.
- Value is added at every stage of the chain of production.
- Businesses increase added value through branding, quality, processing, packaging, convenience, service or cheaper materials.
- Beneficiation processes minerals and farm produce at home before export.
- Benefits include jobs, skills, export earnings and tax revenue; challenges include capital, power, skills and markets.
:::
