## Introduction

Think about a cotton T-shirt sold in a Bulawayo clothing shop. The cotton was grown by a small-scale farmer in the Gokwe area, ginned and spun into yarn, woven or knitted into cloth, cut and sewn into a T-shirt, transported to a wholesaler and finally sold by a retailer. Many different businesses were involved, each doing one stage of the work.

Economists group these businesses into three **sectors of production**. Questions on the sectors appear in every paper: "classify these businesses", "explain how the sectors depend on each other", and, in Cambridge 0264, "explain why the importance of the sectors changes". This lesson covers all of them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary sector"}
Businesses that extract natural resources from the earth, the sea or the air: farming, mining, fishing and forestry. Also called extractive production.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary sector"}
Businesses that turn raw materials into finished or semi-finished goods: manufacturing and construction.
:::

:::definition{term="Tertiary sector"}
Businesses that provide services to other businesses and to consumers, such as transport, banking, insurance, retailing, education, health care and tourism.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of production"}
All the stages a product passes through from the raw material to the final consumer.
:::

## The three sectors in detail

| Sector | Also called | Zimbabwean examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Extractive | Tobacco and maize farming, gold and platinum mining, fishing on Lake Kariba, timber plantations in the Eastern Highlands |
| Secondary | Manufacturing and construction | Food processing, clothing factories, cement making, building houses and roads |
| Tertiary | Services | Supermarkets, buses and haulage trucks, commercial banks, insurance companies, schools, hotels |

### Tertiary production: commercial and direct services

In ZIMSEC Commerce the tertiary sector is split into two kinds of service:

- **Commercial services** (commerce) help goods move from producers to consumers: **trade** (retail and wholesale, home and foreign) and the **aids to trade** (transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising).
- **Direct services** are provided personally to people rather than to goods: teachers, nurses, doctors, lawyers, hairdressers, police officers.

:::example{title="Classifying businesses"}
- A dairy farm near Marondera milks cows: **primary**.
- A factory in Harare turns the milk into yoghurt: **secondary**.
- A haulage company transports the yoghurt to Mutare: **tertiary** (commercial service: transport).
- A supermarket sells the yoghurt: **tertiary** (commercial service: trade).
- A doctor treats the supermarket manager: **tertiary** (direct service).
:::

## The chain of production

The chain of production shows how the sectors link together to make one product.

![Chain of production for a loaf of bread: a farmer grows wheat (primary), a miller makes flour and a bakery makes bread (secondary), then transport and a retailer deliver and sell it (tertiary); value is added at every stage and a bank and insurer support the chain.](media:diagrams/sector-chain-of-production.png "Figure: the chain of production for a loaf of bread, from primary to tertiary")

:::example{title="Chain of production for a loaf of bread"}
1. **Primary:** a farmer grows wheat.
2. **Secondary:** a miller turns the wheat into flour; a bakery turns the flour into bread.
3. **Tertiary:** transport carries the bread, a retailer sells it, a bank handles the payments and an insurer covers the delivery vans.
:::

At every stage something is done to the product to make it more useful or more available, so **value is added** at each stage. A product can pass through the secondary stage several times (spinning, then weaving, then sewing).

## Interdependence of the sectors

**Interdependence** means that the sectors rely on each other. No sector can work alone.

- The **primary** sector needs the secondary sector for tools, tractors and fertiliser, and the tertiary sector for transport, finance and insurance, and to sell its output.
- The **secondary** sector needs raw materials from the primary sector and services such as transport, banking, insurance and advertising from the tertiary sector.
- The **tertiary** sector needs goods from the other two sectors to transport, store, insure and sell, and people working in all sectors are its customers.

:::example{title="When one link fails"}
A drought reduces the maize harvest (primary). Millers have less grain to process and may lay off workers (secondary). Transporters carry fewer loads, and retailers have less to sell or must import (tertiary). A problem in one sector spreads to the others.
:::

## How commerce relates to production

In Commerce, **production** is the whole process of making goods available to consumers. Industry (primary and secondary) makes the goods; **commerce** distributes them. Commerce is made up of trade and the aids to trade, which remove the "hindrances" between producer and consumer:

| Hindrance (barrier) | Aid to trade that removes it |
| --- | --- |
| Producer and consumer are far apart (place) | Transport |
| Goods are produced before they are needed (time) | Warehousing |
| Risk of loss or damage | Insurance |
| Payment between buyer and seller | Banking |
| Consumers do not know about the product | Advertising |
| Buyer and seller need to contact each other | Communication |

## Changes in the importance of the sectors

The importance of a sector can be measured by the number of people it employs or the value of the output it produces. As economies develop, the balance usually changes:

- In a **less developed** economy most people work in the **primary** sector, often in small-scale farming.
- As the economy **industrialises**, more people move into the **secondary** sector: factories offer regular wages and output is more valuable.
- In a **developed** economy the **tertiary** sector employs the most people, because higher incomes are spent on services such as banking, tourism, health care and entertainment.

Reasons for the change include mechanisation of farming (fewer farm workers needed), rising incomes, better education and skills, cheaper manufactured imports from other countries (deindustrialisation in some developed countries) and new technology that creates service jobs. Many countries, including Zimbabwe, have large numbers of people in all three sectors, with the informal sector important in trade and services.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting construction in the tertiary sector"}
Building houses, roads and dams makes physical structures, so construction is **secondary**, even though builders "provide a service" to their customer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sectors of production with the private and public sectors"}
Primary, secondary and tertiary describe the **type of activity**. Private and public describe **who owns** the business. A state-owned railway is public sector and tertiary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing interdependence too vaguely"}
"The sectors need each other" earns little. Show **how**: name what one sector supplies to another, with an example.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Classify with a reason"}
When asked to classify a business, state the sector and give the reason ("secondary, because it processes raw cotton into cloth").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the chain"}
A chain of production with one example at each stage is a quick way to show interdependence and value added.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce divides tertiary production into commercial and direct services and links it to trade and the aids to trade. Business Enterprise and Skills studies the stages of production with the production function. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.2) expects you to explain why the importance of each sector changes as an economy develops.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Primary production extracts natural resources; secondary production manufactures and constructs; tertiary production provides services.
- Tertiary production includes commercial services (trade and aids to trade) and direct services.
- The chain of production links the sectors from raw material to consumer, adding value at each stage.
- The sectors are interdependent: each supplies and buys from the others.
- As an economy develops, employment usually shifts from primary to secondary and then to tertiary.
:::
