## Introduction

Rudo runs a small bakery in a high-density suburb of Masvingo. Every morning she turns flour, sugar, yeast and firewood into bread and buns that her neighbours buy before work. Rudo is doing what every business does: she uses scarce resources to make something that people want and are willing to pay for.

This lesson is the starting point of Commerce, Business Enterprise and Skills, and Cambridge IGCSE Business. Examiners regularly ask you to define needs and wants, to explain scarcity and opportunity cost, and to identify the factors of production in a short case. The ideas are simple, but marks are lost through vague definitions, so learn the exact meanings.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Needs"}
Goods and services that people must have to survive, such as food, clean water, shelter and clothing.
:::

:::definition{term="Wants"}
Goods and services that people would like to have but can live without, such as a smartphone, a car or a holiday. Wants are unlimited.
:::

:::definition{term="Scarcity"}
The basic economic problem: there are not enough resources to satisfy everybody's unlimited wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost"}
The next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
:::

:::definition{term="Production"}
The process of turning inputs (resources) into outputs (goods and services) that satisfy needs and wants. In Commerce, production includes every activity up to the point where the product reaches the consumer.
:::

:::definition{term="Factors of production"}
The resources needed to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and enterprise.
:::

## Needs, wants and scarcity

People's needs are limited: once a family has enough food and a roof, its basic needs are met. Wants, however, keep growing. A family with a radio wants a television; a vendor with a stall wants a shop. Because land, labour and capital are limited, not every want can be satisfied. This is **scarcity**.

Scarcity forces every person, business and government to **choose**. Choosing one thing means giving up something else, and the value of the best alternative given up is the **opportunity cost**.

:::example{title="Opportunity cost for a business and a household"}
Tendai has enough money saved either to buy a second-hand deep freezer for his tuck shop or to restock his shelves for the festive season. He chooses the freezer so that he can sell cold drinks. The opportunity cost is the extra festive-season stock he could have sold.

A farmer near Chinhoyi has one hectare left. If she plants tobacco there, the opportunity cost is the maize she could have grown on the same land.
:::

Notice that opportunity cost is **one** alternative (the next best), not a list of everything given up, and it is not simply the money spent.

## Goods and services

Production satisfies wants by providing goods and services.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer goods | Goods bought by households for their own use | Bread, soap, school shoes, a television |
| Producer (capital) goods | Goods used by businesses to make other goods and services | A grinding mill, a delivery truck, an oven |
| Services | Intangible activities done for people or businesses | Hairdressing, banking, transport, teaching |

Some consumer goods are used up at once (bread, airtime), while others last for years (furniture, a fridge). The same item can be either a consumer good or a producer good depending on who uses it: a sewing machine used at home to mend family clothes is a consumer good; the same machine in a dressmaking workshop is a producer good.

## The production function: inputs, process, outputs

Every business can be described as a simple system:

- **Inputs**: the factors of production and materials, such as Rudo's flour, her two assistants, her oven and her own ideas.
- **Process**: what is done to the inputs, such as mixing, baking and packing.
- **Outputs**: the finished goods or services, such as loaves of bread, sold to customers.

A business is successful when customers value the outputs more than the cost of the inputs. That difference is the basis of **added value**, studied in a later lesson.

## The four factors of production and their rewards

| Factor | What it is | Example in a Zimbabwean business | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land | All natural resources | Farmland, minerals, timber, water from a river | Rent |
| Labour | Human effort, physical and mental | Mine workers, a shop assistant, an accountant | Wages and salaries |
| Capital | Man-made goods used in production | Machines, tools, buildings, vehicles | Interest |
| Enterprise | Organising the other factors and taking the risk | The person who starts a poultry project | Profit |

:::example{title="Factors of production in a market garden"}
Farai grows vegetables for sale at a market in Mutare. The **land** is his plot and the borehole water. The **labour** is Farai and two workers he pays weekly. The **capital** is the pump, the hoes and the wheelbarrow. The **enterprise** is Farai's decision to grow tomatoes and covo for sale and his acceptance that a drought or low prices could cause a loss. If the crop sells well, his reward is profit.
:::

### Why enterprise is special

Land, labour and capital do nothing on their own. The entrepreneur (called an enterpriser in Business Enterprise and Skills) brings them together, decides what to produce and how, and bears the **risk** that the business may fail. Profit is the reward for taking this risk; a loss is the penalty.

### Capital is not money

In business studies, capital as a factor of production means **capital goods** such as machinery and buildings. Money is used to buy these goods. (In accounting, "capital" also means the owner's investment in the business; read the question carefully to see which meaning is intended.)

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up needs and wants"}
A need is essential for survival; a want is something extra. "A phone" is normally a want for a household, even if it is very useful. Give clear examples when asked.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing several items as the opportunity cost"}
Opportunity cost is the **next best alternative** given up, a single option. Do not write "everything else the money could buy".
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling money a factor of production"}
Money finances production but is not itself a factor. Capital means tools, machines, vehicles and buildings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Matching the wrong reward"}
Land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest and enterprise earns profit. Profit is not the reward for capital.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the case"}
When a question gives a business, identify each factor of production **from that business** (for example "the oven is capital"). General answers that ignore the case earn fewer marks, especially in Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Define, then apply"}
For "explain the term opportunity cost", give the definition (next best alternative forgone) and then a short example. Two marks are often split exactly this way.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce links production to commerce (trade and the aids to trade) and expects producer and consumer goods. Business Enterprise and Skills uses the word "enterpriser". Cambridge 0264 places factors of production and opportunity cost in topic 1.1 and expects them applied to a business in the stimulus.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Needs are essential for survival; wants are unlimited, so resources are scarce.
- Scarcity forces choice, and every choice has an opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up.
- Production turns inputs into outputs (goods and services) that satisfy needs and wants.
- The factors of production are land, labour, capital and enterprise, rewarded by rent, wages, interest and profit.
- The entrepreneur organises the other factors and bears the risk of the business.
:::
