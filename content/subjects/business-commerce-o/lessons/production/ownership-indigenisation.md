## Introduction

The factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) are sometimes called the **means of production**. Whoever owns them decides what is produced, who is employed, and who receives the rent, interest and profit. That is why ownership of land, minerals and businesses has been an important issue in Zimbabwe since independence in 1980.

ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 1) asks you to explain the means of production as **heritage**, to describe **land reform** and **indigenisation**, and to discuss their benefits and problems. Write about policy in a balanced, factual way: examiners reward clear reasons and effects, not political opinions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Means of production"}
The resources used to produce goods and services: land and natural resources (minerals, water, forests), capital goods and the businesses that combine them.
:::

:::definition{term="Heritage"}
Resources and traditions passed down from one generation to the next. Land and natural resources are seen as a national heritage to be used for the benefit of present and future citizens.
:::

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
Government action to change who owns or uses land, usually by redistributing land from a few large owners to many more people.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenisation"}
Policy that increases the share of businesses and resources owned and controlled by indigenous (local) citizens.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic empowerment"}
Giving previously disadvantaged people the means (land, capital, skills, finance, markets) to take part fully in the economy as owners and producers.
:::

## Why ownership matters

Ownership decides who **controls** production and who **benefits** from it.

- **Income:** owners receive the rent, interest and profit; if most owners live abroad, much of the profit leaves the country.
- **Decisions:** owners choose what to produce, where to invest and whom to employ.
- **Distribution of wealth:** when a small group owns most resources, income and wealth are unequal.
- **Sustainability:** owners who see resources as a long-term heritage are more likely to conserve soil, water, forests and minerals.

:::example{title="Two ways of exploiting a mineral deposit"}
Suppose a fictional company owned entirely abroad mines chrome near Shurugwi and exports the raw ore. Local people earn wages, but the profit and the jobs in processing the chrome go overseas. If local investors share in ownership and the ore is processed in Zimbabwe, more of the profit, the skilled jobs and the tax revenue stay in the country. This is the thinking behind indigenisation and beneficiation.
:::

## Land and natural resources as heritage

Land in Zimbabwe has cultural as well as economic value: it is linked to ancestors, communities and identity. Treating land, minerals, wildlife and forests as **heritage** means:

- they belong to the nation as a whole, including future generations;
- they should be used productively, not left idle or wasted;
- they should be conserved, for example by preventing soil erosion, illegal gold panning in rivers and deforestation;
- the benefits should be shared widely among citizens.

## Land reform

At independence most good commercial farmland was owned by a small number of large-scale farmers, while most of the population farmed in crowded communal areas. Land reform aimed to correct this imbalance. In the 1980s and 1990s land was bought for resettlement; from 2000 the Fast Track Land Reform Programme redistributed large areas of commercial farmland to small-scale (A1) and medium- to large-scale (A2) farmers.

| Possible benefits | Possible problems |
| --- | --- |
| More people own land and can produce for themselves and for sale | Many new farmers lacked capital, equipment and farming skills at first |
| Less inequality in land ownership | Output of some crops fell during the change-over |
| Rural employment and self-employment | Without secure, transferable title, banks may be reluctant to lend against the land |
| Land seen as restored heritage | Some farm workers lost jobs and homes |

The success of land reform depends on support for new farmers: training (agricultural extension), inputs such as seed and fertiliser, finance, irrigation and markets for their produce.

## Indigenisation and economic empowerment

Indigenisation policy aims to increase local ownership of businesses, especially in sectors based on natural resources such as mining. Zimbabwe passed an Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act; the original law set a target of majority (51%) local ownership for larger businesses, and later amendments narrowed its scope. Check current rules with an up-to-date source: they change.

Methods governments use to widen local ownership include:

- requiring or encouraging foreign-owned firms to sell shares to local people, employees or communities;
- **community share ownership schemes**, where communities near a mine own part of it;
- **employee share ownership schemes**;
- reserving certain small businesses for local citizens;
- loans, grants and training for local entrepreneurs and small businesses;
- encouraging **value addition and beneficiation** so that more processing happens locally.

| Possible benefits | Possible problems |
| --- | --- |
| More profit and income stays in the country | Foreign investors may be discouraged, so less new capital and technology |
| Local people gain business ownership and experience | Local buyers may lack the money to buy shares |
| Wider distribution of wealth | Benefits may go to a small, well-connected group rather than to the majority |
| Communities benefit from resources in their area | Uncertainty about rules can delay investment |

## The role of government

The government influences ownership of the means of production by passing laws (land and indigenisation laws), allocating land, providing finance and training to new owners, supporting small and medium enterprises, and protecting resources for future generations through environmental laws. It must balance wider local ownership against the need to attract investment that creates jobs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving opinions instead of reasons"}
"Land reform was good" or "bad" earns nothing on its own. State a specific effect and explain why it happened.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing indigenisation with nationalisation"}
Indigenisation transfers ownership to **local citizens**, communities and employees. Nationalisation transfers ownership to the **state**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the support needed"}
Owning land or shares is not enough. Answers on success should mention skills, finance, inputs and markets.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Two-sided answers"}
"Discuss the effects of indigenisation" needs benefits **and** problems, then a conclusion such as "the policy succeeds only if new owners have finance and skills".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link to heritage"}
When asked why land is regarded as heritage, mention its cultural value, that it is passed between generations, and that it must be conserved and used productively.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
This topic is in ZIMSEC Commerce only. Cambridge 0264 does not examine it, though it does examine ownership in the private and public sectors.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The means of production are land, natural resources, capital and businesses; their owners control production and receive the income.
- Land and natural resources are regarded as heritage: to be used productively, conserved and shared across generations.
- Land reform redistributed land to more people; its success depends on skills, finance, inputs and markets.
- Indigenisation and economic empowerment aim to increase local ownership, through share schemes, support for local businesses and value addition.
- Benefits (wider ownership, profits kept at home) must be balanced against problems (lower foreign investment, lack of finance).
:::
