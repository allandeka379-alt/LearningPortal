## Introduction

A clothing factory in Bulawayo keeps finished shirts in a store behind the factory. A wholesaler in Harare's industrial area holds thousands of cartons of cooking oil and soap. A supermarket chain runs a distribution centre that supplies its branches in Gweru, Kwekwe and Masvingo. An importer leaves a container of electrical goods in a government-approved store at the border until she can pay the import duty. All four are warehouses, but they are owned by different people and do different jobs.

This lesson builds on the functions of warehousing. ZIMSEC Commerce teaches the types and location of warehouses in Form 3; Business Enterprise and Skills (BES) asks you to state the types, explain their functions and explain the factors to consider when locating a warehouse in Form 4.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Private warehouse"}
A warehouse owned and used by one business for its own goods, for example a manufacturer's, wholesaler's or retailer's warehouse.
:::

:::definition{term="Public warehouse"}
A warehouse owned by a warehousing business (or a public authority) that stores goods for anyone who pays a storage charge.
:::

:::definition{term="Bonded warehouse"}
A warehouse approved and supervised by the customs authority (ZIMRA in Zimbabwe) where imported goods on which customs duty has not yet been paid are stored under bond.
:::

:::definition{term="Bond"}
A guarantee given to the customs authority by the owner of a bonded warehouse that duty will be paid before goods leave the warehouse for the home market.
:::

## Private warehouses

### Manufacturers' warehouses

Manufacturers store **raw materials** (so that production does not stop) and **finished goods** (until they are sold). A cement or fertiliser factory needs large stores of finished bags before the building or planting season.

### Wholesalers' warehouses

The wholesaler's warehouse is at the centre of the wholesaler's work: goods are bought in bulk from many manufacturers, stored, **broken into smaller lots**, sometimes packed and labelled, and sent to retailers. A cash-and-carry wholesaler's warehouse is also where retailers come to buy.

### Retailers' warehouses

Large retailers and supermarket chains run **distribution centres** (central warehouses) that receive goods in bulk and supply each branch as needed. A small shop may simply use a stockroom at the back.

:::example{title="A chain of hardware shops"}
Tatenda's business has five hardware shops in the Midlands. Instead of each shop ordering separately, suppliers deliver to one central warehouse in Gweru, which sends a lorry to each shop twice a week. The business buys in larger quantities at lower prices, keeps less stock in each shop and can move goods to the branch that needs them.
:::

**Advantages of a private warehouse**: the owner controls it fully, can design it for the goods (cold rooms, high racks), and can use it whenever needed. **Disadvantages**: high cost to build or rent and run, and space may stand empty when stock is low.

## Public warehouses

Public warehouses are run by businesses that rent out storage space, or by public bodies (for example grain storage depots and stores at ports and railway stations). Anyone may use them on payment of a fee.

They are useful to:

- small firms that cannot afford their own warehouse;
- firms that need extra space only at peak times (for example after harvest);
- importers and exporters waiting for transport.

Public warehouses often provide services: grading, packing, insurance cover, transport arrangements, and a **warehouse warrant** that the owner of goods can use as security for a loan or transfer to a buyer.

## Bonded warehouses

When goods are imported, customs duty is normally payable before they enter the country. A bonded warehouse allows the importer to **delay** paying the duty until the goods are released for sale in the home market.

Functions and advantages:

- The importer pays duty **in instalments**, only on the quantity taken out each time, which helps cash flow.
- Goods that are **re-exported** (sent on to another country) from bond pay no Zimbabwean import duty.
- Goods can be **blended, sorted, repacked or labelled** in bond under customs supervision.
- Buyers can **inspect** goods while they are in bond.
- Customs officials can **check** goods and collect duty efficiently, which protects government revenue.

:::example{title="Importing in bond"}
Farai imports 2,000 cartons of electrical goods. He leaves them in a bonded warehouse at the border town and takes out 200 cartons at a time, paying duty on each 200 as he sells them in Harare. If a buyer in Zambia orders 300 cartons, those can be sent on from bond without Zimbabwean import duty being paid on them.
:::

| Type | Owned by | Used by | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manufacturer's | the manufacturer | the manufacturer | raw materials and finished goods |
| Wholesaler's | the wholesaler | the wholesaler (and buying retailers) | bulk storage and breaking bulk |
| Retailer's | a large retailer | its own branches | distribution to branches |
| Public | a warehousing firm or public body | anyone who pays | storage space and services for hire |
| Bonded | a licensed operator, under customs control | importers | storing imports before duty is paid |

## Locating a warehouse

The right site keeps storage and delivery costs low and service to customers fast. Factors to consider:

- **Nearness to customers (the market)**: shorter, cheaper deliveries and faster service to retailers or branches.
- **Nearness to suppliers or sources of goods**: for example near farming areas for grain, or near a factory.
- **Transport links**: good roads, a railway siding, closeness to a border post or airport; access for large lorries.
- **Cost of land and rent**: industrial sites on the edge of towns are usually cheaper than city centres.
- **Availability of services**: reliable electricity (essential for cold rooms), water, security services and communication links.
- **Labour**: enough workers within reach.
- **Security and safety**: low risk of theft, flooding or fire; access for fire services.
- **Space for expansion** and for lorries to turn and park.
- **Government regulations**: zoning laws (warehouses belong in industrial areas) and, for bonded warehouses, customs approval and closeness to the customs office or border.

:::example{title="Choosing between two sites"}
Chipo's fruit wholesale business needs a cold store. Site 1 is in the city centre near her retail customers but is expensive and has no space for lorries. Site 2 is on an industrial site beside the main highway, has a reliable power supply and room to expand, but is 6 km from most customers. For a cold store, reliable electricity, lorry access and space are critical, so Site 2 is the better choice; the extra delivery distance is small.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying goods in a bonded warehouse are duty-free"}
Duty is **delayed**, not removed, for goods sold in the home market. Only goods re-exported from bond avoid the import duty.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up public and private"}
"Public" refers to who may **use** the warehouse (anyone who pays), not that the government must own it. Many public warehouses are run by private businesses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Generic location factors"}
"Near the market" alone earns little. Explain the benefit in the context: lower delivery costs, faster supply to branches, fresher goods.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe each type with owner, user and purpose"}
For each type of warehouse say who owns it, who uses it and what it is for. A comparison table in your answer is acceptable if the question allows notes.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Location questions"}
In an 'advise' or 'recommend' question, choose a site, give two or three reasons from the case and say why the drawback of your choice matters less. A justified decision earns the top marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Private warehouses belong to manufacturers, wholesalers or retailers and store their own goods.
- Public warehouses rent space and services to anyone who pays; they help small firms and seasonal users.
- Bonded warehouses store imports under customs control so that duty is paid only when goods are released; re-exports from bond pay no import duty.
- Location factors: market, suppliers, transport links, cost of land, services (power, water), labour, security, space and regulations.
- A good site keeps costs low and gets goods to customers quickly.
:::
