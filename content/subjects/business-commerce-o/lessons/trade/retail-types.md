## Introduction

Walk down any street in Zimbabwe and you meet retailers of every size: a vendor selling airtime and sweets from a small table, a tuck shop at the corner of a residential street, a fruit and vegetable stall at Sakubva market, and a large supermarket with a car park and twenty tills. All of them do the same basic job: they sell goods in small quantities to the final consumer.

Retail trade is the last link in the chain of distribution. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2) expects you to know the functions of retailers, describe small-scale and large-scale retailers, advise someone opening a shop, and explain why small retailers survive despite competition from supermarkets.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Retailer"}
A trader who buys goods from wholesalers or producers and sells them in small quantities to final consumers.
:::

:::definition{term="Small-scale retailer"}
A retailer with little capital, few or no employees and a small range of goods, usually run by the owner.
:::

:::definition{term="Large-scale retailer"}
A retailer with large capital, many employees and a wide range of goods, often organised as a company with many branches.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain store (multiple shop)"}
One of many similar shops owned by one company, selling similar goods with the same name, layout and prices in different towns.
:::

## Functions of the retailer

**To the consumer:**

- sells goods in **small quantities** that consumers can afford;
- stocks a **variety** of goods from many producers in one place;
- is **located near** consumers and opens at convenient times;
- gives **advice** and information about products;
- may offer **credit**, delivery and after-sales service.

**To the wholesaler and the producer:**

- buys goods from them and so helps them sell their output;
- passes on **information** about consumers' tastes and complaints;
- **advertises** and displays goods;
- **stores** goods, reducing the wholesaler's storage needs;
- bears risks such as theft, damage and changes in fashion.

## Small-scale retailers

| Type | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Tuck shop (unit or independent shop) | Owner-run shop in a residential area; groceries and household goods; long opening hours |
| Kiosk | Very small fixed booth selling airtime, newspapers, sweets and cold drinks |
| Market stall | Stall in an open or covered market (e.g. Mbare or Sakubva); fresh produce, clothes, hardware |
| Hawkers and vendors | Sell from a tray, cart or table, or move from place to place; very little capital |
| Mobile shop | A vehicle fitted as a shop that travels to farms, villages and new suburbs |

Common features: little capital, owner-managed, personal service, small range of goods, often credit to known customers, no or few employees.

## Large-scale retailers

| Type | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Supermarket | Large self-service store selling mainly food and household goods; many checkouts |
| Hypermarket | Very large self-service store, often outside the town centre with a big car park; food, clothing, furniture and electrical goods |
| Chain store (multiple shop) | Many branches of one company with the same name and layout; centralised buying |
| Department store | Large shop divided into departments (clothing, furniture, cosmetics), each run like a separate shop under one roof; high level of service |

**Organisation of large-scale retailers:** head office buys centrally in bulk and sets prices and policies; branch managers run each shop; departments may have their own buyers. Large retailers can buy in bulk at discounts, advertise nationally and use technology such as electronic point of sale (EPOS).

:::example{title="Comparing two retailers"}
Chipo's tuck shop in Dzivarasekwa opens from 06:00 to 21:00, sells bread, milk and airtime in small amounts, and lets regular customers pay at the end of the week. A supermarket in the town centre charges lower prices for most goods because it buys in bulk, and offers a much wider choice, but it is a bus ride away and gives no credit. Each serves a different need.
:::

### Small-scale and large-scale retailers compared

| Feature | Small-scale retailer | Large-scale retailer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital | Little | Large |
| Range of goods | Narrow | Wide |
| Prices | Usually higher | Usually lower (bulk buying) |
| Service | Personal, counter service | Mostly self-service |
| Credit | Often to known customers | Rarely, except store cards |
| Location | Near homes, markets, bus stops | Town centres, shopping malls |

Large-scale retailers enjoy **economies of scale**: they buy in bulk at a discount, spread advertising costs across many branches and can afford modern technology. Their disadvantages are high running costs (rent, wages, electricity), impersonal service and slow decision-making, because many decisions are made at head office rather than in the branch.

Small-scale retailers are flexible and close to their customers, but they lack capital to expand, cannot buy in bulk, often keep poor records and depend heavily on the owner's health and effort.

## Factors to consider when opening a retail outlet

| Factor | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Location | Is it near customers? Is there passing trade, parking and public transport? |
| Capital | How much is needed for premises, fittings and opening stock? Where will it come from? |
| Demand and type of goods | What do people in the area want and how much can they afford? |
| Competition | How many similar shops are nearby, and what do they charge? |
| Premises and rent | Is the building suitable, secure and affordable? |
| Legal requirements | Shop licence, health regulations for food, registration and tax |
| Suppliers | Are wholesalers or producers nearby and reliable? |
| Security | Risk of theft; insurance |
| Skills | Does the owner have the skills to keep records, buy and sell? |

:::example{title="Choosing a site"}
Rudo plans to open a small grocery in Masvingo. A shop in the town centre would attract many shoppers but costs a high rent and faces three supermarkets nearby. A shop beside a busy kombi rank in a high-density suburb has a lower rent, steady passing trade from early morning, and no large competitor within walking distance. For a small grocery selling everyday items, the suburban site near the rank is likely to be the better choice.
:::

## Why small-scale retailers survive

Despite the buying power of supermarkets, small retailers remain very common because they:

- are **close to customers' homes**, saving time and transport costs;
- open **long and flexible hours**, including early mornings, evenings and holidays;
- give **personal service** and know their customers;
- offer **credit** to regular customers;
- sell in **very small quantities** (single cigarettes, a cup of sugar, small packets) that low-income customers can afford;
- are **flexible**: they can quickly stock what customers ask for;
- sell **specialist or local products** that large retailers do not stock;
- need **little capital** to start, so new ones open easily;
- join **voluntary chains** or buying groups to get lower prices from wholesalers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a chain store with a supermarket"}
A supermarket describes **how** a store sells (large and self-service). A chain store describes **ownership** (many branches of one company). Many supermarkets are also chain stores.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying small shops survive because they are cheaper"}
Small shops are usually **more** expensive than supermarkets; they survive because of convenience, credit, hours and personal service.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the case"}
When advising someone opening a shop, use the information given (location, capital, competitors) rather than writing a general list.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name, then explain"}
"Location" alone is a point; "location, because a shop near a bus terminus attracts passing trade" is a developed point.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Comparisons"}
For "compare a supermarket with a tuck shop", write the features side by side (prices, range, service, credit, hours) rather than two separate descriptions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
This is ZIMSEC Commerce content. Cambridge 0264 includes retailers only as a distribution channel in the marketing mix.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Retailers sell goods in small quantities to final consumers and serve consumers, wholesalers and producers.
- Small-scale retailers include tuck shops, kiosks, market stalls, hawkers and vendors and mobile shops.
- Large-scale retailers include supermarkets, hypermarkets, chain stores and department stores, with centralised buying.
- Opening a shop requires thinking about location, capital, demand, competition, premises, legal rules, suppliers and security.
- Small retailers survive through convenience, long hours, personal service, credit, small quantities and flexibility.
:::
