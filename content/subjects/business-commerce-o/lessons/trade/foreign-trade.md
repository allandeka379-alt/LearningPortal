## Introduction

Zimbabwe's tobacco is smoked in Asia and Europe, its gold and platinum are used around the world, and its citrus and macadamia nuts appear on foreign supermarket shelves. At the same time, Zimbabweans drive imported cars, use imported phones and take imported medicines. No country can produce everything it needs, so countries trade with each other.

Foreign trade is a large Form 3 topic in ZIMSEC Commerce. This lesson covers why countries trade, the problems importers and exporters face, entrepot trade, and the intermediaries who help. Documents, protection, trading blocs and the balance of payments follow in later lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign (international) trade"}
The exchange of goods and services between countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Imports"}
Goods and services bought from other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Exports"}
Goods and services sold to other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Entrepot trade"}
Importing goods in order to re-export them, often after storage, sorting, grading, repacking or light processing.
:::

:::definition{term="Interdependence of countries"}
Countries rely on each other for goods, services, markets and resources.
:::

## Why countries trade

- **Different natural resources:** minerals, soils and climate differ. Zimbabwe has platinum, chrome and good tobacco-growing conditions but no oil fields, so it imports fuel.
- **Specialisation:** countries produce what they are best at and trade for the rest, so more is produced in total and living standards rise.
- **Goods that cannot be produced at home** (or only at very high cost), such as advanced machinery, aircraft and some medicines.
- **Larger markets:** exporters can sell far more than the home market would buy, gaining economies of scale.
- **Variety and choice** for consumers.
- **Foreign currency earnings** from exports pay for imports.
- **Friendly relations** and co-operation between countries.

## Difficulties of foreign trade

| Difficulty | Why it is a problem | How it is overcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance | Long transport routes, higher costs, delays and risk of damage | Containerisation, air freight for valuable goods, insurance |
| Different currencies | Payment must be converted; exchange rates change | Banks deal in foreign exchange; agree the currency in the contract |
| Language differences | Misunderstandings in orders and contracts | Translators, agents in the foreign country, trade in a common language |
| Different laws, regulations and customs procedures | Import licences, standards, documents and duties | Clearing and forwarding agents, customs brokers |
| Payment risk | Seller may not know whether the foreign buyer can pay | Letters of credit, credit insurance, confirming houses |
| Different tastes, standards and measures | Goods may need changing for the foreign market | Market research, agents |
| Trade restrictions | Tariffs, quotas, embargoes | Trading bloc agreements |
| Lack of information about foreign markets | Exporters do not know buyers | Trade fairs, embassies, export promotion agencies, agents |

Zimbabwe is **landlocked**, so goods travel by road or rail through neighbouring countries to seaports such as Beira, Durban or Dar es Salaam. This adds transport costs, border delays and extra documents.

## Differences between home trade and foreign trade

| Point | Home trade | Foreign trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Currency | One currency | Currencies must be exchanged |
| Laws and regulations | One set of laws | Laws of at least two countries, plus customs procedures |
| Distance and transport | Usually short; road or rail | Often long; sea, air, road and rail, with transit through other countries |
| Documents | Order, invoice, delivery note and so on | Many extra documents: bill of lading, certificate of origin, customs declarations |
| Risk | Lower | Higher: payment, damage, political and currency risks |
| Taxes | Local taxes such as VAT | Customs duties on imports, sometimes on exports |

Because foreign trade is more complex and riskier, specialist services and intermediaries have grown up to help traders.

## Advantages and disadvantages of foreign trade to a country

Foreign trade brings a wider variety of goods, larger markets for producers, foreign currency, jobs in export industries and the transfer of new technology and ideas. However, a country may become **over-dependent** on a few exports (for example minerals or tobacco) whose prices can fall sharply; local industries may be harmed by cheaper imports; and spending more on imports than is earned from exports can cause a balance of payments deficit. Governments therefore try to encourage exports, often through export promotion and value addition, while watching the level of imports.

## Entrepot trade

Entrepot centres are usually ports or hubs on major trade routes with good warehousing, transport links, banking and low or no import duties on goods for re-export (often stored in **bonded warehouses** or free zones). Well-known international examples include Singapore, Dubai and Rotterdam.

Benefits of entrepot trade to the country: jobs in warehousing, transport, banking and insurance; income from storage and handling; growth of the port and related businesses.

## Intermediaries in foreign trade

Exporters and importers often use specialist middlemen who know foreign markets, documents and procedures.

| Intermediary | What they do | How they are paid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Merchant (import or export merchant) | Buys goods **on their own account** and resells them abroad or at home, taking the risk | Profit on resale |
| Agent | Acts **on behalf of** a principal, arranging sales or purchases without owning the goods | Commission |
| Del credere agent | An agent who also guarantees that the buyer will pay | Extra commission for the risk |
| Broker | Brings buyers and sellers together, especially for commodities, shipping and insurance; does not handle goods | Brokerage (commission) |
| Factor | An agent who has possession of the goods, sells them in their own name and may advance money to the seller | Commission (factorage) |
| Confirming house | Buys on behalf of foreign importers and guarantees payment to the exporter | Commission |
| Indent house | Buys goods for foreign buyers on receipt of an order (an indent) | Commission |
| Clearing and forwarding agent | Handles customs clearance, documents and transport arrangements | Fees |

:::keyfact{title="Merchant versus agent"}
A **merchant** owns the goods and earns profit; an **agent** never owns the goods and earns commission. This difference is the most common exam question on intermediaries.
:::

:::example{title="An exporter uses an agent"}
A fictional macadamia nut co-operative near Chipinge wants to sell in Europe. It appoints an agent in the Netherlands who finds buyers, negotiates prices and arranges for orders to be sent. The co-operative still owns the nuts until they are sold and pays the agent a commission on each sale. A clearing and forwarding agent in Mutare handles the export documents and transport to Beira.
:::

### Choosing an intermediary

An exporter chooses an intermediary by weighing **cost**, **risk** and **control**. Selling outright to a merchant is simplest and safest: the exporter is paid at home and the merchant bears the risks, but the merchant's profit means a lower price for the exporter. Using an agent keeps ownership and a larger share of the final price, but the exporter carries the risk of non-payment unless it uses a del credere agent or a letter of credit. Large, experienced exporters often set up their own sales offices abroad, which gives the most control but is the most expensive option.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying agents buy and sell goods themselves"}
Agents act for someone else and do not own the goods. Merchants buy and resell on their own account.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing difficulties without explanation"}
"Language" earns one mark; "language differences may cause orders to be misunderstood, so the wrong goods are sent" earns more.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing entrepot trade with exports"}
Entrepot goods were **imported first** and are then re-exported; ordinary exports are produced in the country.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Problem plus solution"}
Questions often ask how a difficulty of foreign trade is overcome. Pair each problem with a service: payment risk with letters of credit, distance with insurance and containerisation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use Zimbabwe's position"}
Being landlocked is a strong, specific point when explaining the difficulties Zimbabwean exporters face.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Countries trade because resources differ, specialisation raises output and some goods cannot be produced at home.
- Difficulties include distance, currencies, language, regulations, payment risk and lack of information; Zimbabwe is also landlocked.
- Entrepot trade imports goods to re-export them, often through bonded warehouses.
- Merchants buy and resell on their own account (profit); agents, brokers and factors act for others (commission).
- Confirming houses, indent houses and clearing and forwarding agents also help traders.
:::
