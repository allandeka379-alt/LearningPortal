## Introduction

Tatenda runs a small factory in Bulawayo that makes school shoes. Imported shoes are cheaper than his, and his sales are falling. He asks the government to put a tax on imported shoes. A shoe retailer in Harare, which sells the imported shoes, argues the opposite: a tax would raise her costs and her customers' prices. Who is right?

This is the debate between **free trade** and **protectionism**. ZIMSEC Commerce asks you to describe the methods of protection and the reasons for them. Cambridge 0264 (topic 6.2) asks specifically about **import tariffs and import quotas** and their **effects on businesses**, as part of globalisation. Both boards like questions that ask you to evaluate who gains and who loses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Free trade"}
International trade with no (or very few) government restrictions such as tariffs or quotas.
:::

:::definition{term="Protectionism"}
Government policies that restrict imports to protect domestic businesses and jobs from foreign competition.
:::

:::definition{term="Import tariff (customs duty)"}
A tax on imported goods, which raises their price in the home market.
:::

:::definition{term="Import quota"}
A limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported in a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Embargo"}
A complete ban on trade in certain goods or with a certain country.
:::

:::definition{term="Exchange control"}
Government limits on the amount of foreign currency that can be bought, which restricts how much can be paid for imports.
:::

## Free trade

Arguments for free trade:

- countries **specialise** in what they produce best, so total output and living standards rise;
- consumers enjoy **lower prices** and **more choice**;
- businesses can buy **cheaper raw materials and components** from abroad;
- **competition** forces domestic firms to become more efficient and innovative;
- exporters gain access to **larger markets**;
- it avoids **retaliation** by other countries.

## Methods of protection

| Method | How it restricts imports |
| --- | --- |
| Tariff (customs duty) | Raises the price of imports. It may be *ad valorem* (a percentage of value) or *specific* (a fixed amount per unit) |
| Quota | Limits the quantity of imports |
| Embargo | Bans imports of a good or from a country |
| Exchange control | Limits the foreign currency available to pay for imports |
| Import licences and red tape | Importers need permits; slow, complicated procedures discourage imports |
| Subsidies to domestic producers | Lower local firms' costs so they can compete with imports |
| Product standards | Strict health, safety or quality rules that some imports cannot meet |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how a tariff changes a price]
Blankets imported from overseas cost a Harare retailer US\$20 each, including transport. A locally made blanket of similar quality sells for US\$25. The government introduces a 40% tariff on imported blankets.

1. Tariff per blanket: 40% of US\$20 = US\$8.
2. Cost of an imported blanket after the tariff: US\$20 + US\$8 = US\$28.
3. Before the tariff, the import was US\$5 cheaper than the local blanket; after it, the import is US\$3 more expensive.
4. The local manufacturer becomes price-competitive; the retailer's costs and consumers' prices for imported blankets rise.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: specific and ad valorem tariffs compared]
An importer brings in 500 bags of fertiliser valued at US\$12 a bag. Compare a specific tariff of US\$1.50 a bag with an ad valorem tariff of 15%.

1. Specific tariff: 500 × US\$1.50 = US\$750.
2. Value of the consignment: 500 × US\$12 = US\$6,000.
3. Ad valorem tariff: 15% of US\$6,000 = US\$900.
4. Here the ad valorem tariff raises US\$150 more. If the price per bag rose, the ad valorem duty would rise too, while the specific duty would stay at US\$1.50 a bag.
:::

## Reasons for protection

- **Protecting infant industries:** new industries need time to grow large enough to compete.
- **Protecting jobs** in industries threatened by cheap imports.
- **Preventing dumping:** foreign firms selling below their cost of production to capture a market.
- **Improving the balance of payments:** reducing imports reduces the outflow of foreign currency.
- **Raising government revenue** from tariffs.
- **Strategic reasons:** keeping key industries such as food, energy and defence at home.
- **Protecting health and safety:** banning harmful or poor-quality goods.
- **Retaliation** against restrictions imposed by other countries.

Each argument has a weakness. Infant industries may never "grow up" if they are protected for too long, jobs saved in one industry may be lost in others that use imported inputs, and revenue from tariffs falls if imports fall. Many economists accept some of these arguments in particular cases, such as temporary support for a genuinely new industry or duties to stop proven dumping, but warn that protection given for too long can keep inefficient firms alive at consumers' expense.

## Effects of tariffs and quotas

| Group | Possible effects |
| --- | --- |
| Domestic producers competing with imports | More sales and higher prices, so more jobs; but less pressure to be efficient |
| Businesses that import raw materials or components | Higher costs, so lower profits or higher prices; may find a local supplier |
| Importers and retailers of imported goods | Higher costs (tariff) or limited stock (quota); may lose sales |
| Exporters | Other countries may retaliate with their own tariffs, so exporters lose markets |
| Consumers | Higher prices and less choice |
| Government | Tariffs raise revenue; quotas do not (unless import licences are sold) |
| Economy | Possibly more domestic jobs and a better balance of payments; but less competition, smuggling and retaliation |

:::example{title="Tariff or quota?"}
A **tariff** lets any quantity in but at a higher price, so consumers who are willing to pay can still buy imports, and the government earns revenue. A **quota** fixes the quantity: once it is filled, no more can come in, however much consumers want them. Prices of the limited imports often rise because supply is restricted, and the extra money goes to the importers lucky enough to have quota, not to the government.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying tariffs only harm foreign firms"}
Tariffs also raise costs for local businesses that use imported materials and raise prices for local consumers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a quota with an embargo"}
A quota **limits** imports; an embargo **bans** them completely.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation"}
"Protection saves jobs" is incomplete. Consider jobs lost in businesses that use imports and in exporters facing retaliation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Effects on a business"}
Cambridge questions often ask how a tariff affects a business in the case. Decide first whether the business **competes with** imports (gains) or **uses** imports (loses), then explain the effect on costs, prices, sales and profit.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Show the tariff working"}
In calculations show the tariff amount and the new price separately. State whether the tariff is ad valorem (percentage) or specific (per unit).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce covers all the methods of protection (including embargoes and exchange control) and reasons for protection. Cambridge 0264 concentrates on import tariffs and quotas and their effects on businesses within globalisation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Free trade allows specialisation, lower prices, more choice and competition.
- Protection methods include tariffs, quotas, embargoes, exchange control, licences, subsidies and standards.
- Reasons for protection include infant industries, jobs, dumping, the balance of payments, revenue and strategic needs.
- Tariffs raise import prices and earn revenue; quotas limit quantities and earn no revenue.
- Domestic competitors gain, while importers, businesses using imported inputs, consumers and exporters (through retaliation) may lose.
:::
