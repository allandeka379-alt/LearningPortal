## Introduction

Nyasha owns a hardware shop in Masvingo and wants to restock cement, nails and paint from a wholesaler in Harare. She cannot just phone and hope for the best: both businesses need written records of what was asked for, what was promised, what was delivered, what is owed and what has been paid. These records are the **documents of home trade**.

ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2) examines the purpose, contents and order of these documents, and the calculation of **trade** and **cash discounts**. Structured questions often give you an invoice with blanks to complete, or ask "which document would the buyer send when…".

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade discount"}
A reduction in the list (catalogue) price given by a seller to traders who buy for resale, often larger for bigger orders. It is shown on the invoice.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash discount"}
A reduction offered to encourage the buyer to pay promptly, within a stated period (for example "5% if paid within 14 days"). It is calculated on the amount after trade discount.
:::

:::definition{term="Invoice"}
The document sent by the seller to the buyer stating the goods supplied, their prices, discounts and the total amount owed. For the buyer it is a bill; for the seller it is a record of a credit sale.
:::

:::definition{term="Statement of account"}
A summary sent by the seller, usually monthly, showing the balance owed at the start, invoices and debit notes, credit notes and payments, and the balance now due.
:::

## The sequence of documents

| Step | Document | Sent by | Purpose and main contents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Letter of enquiry | Buyer | Asks about goods available, prices, terms of payment and delivery |
| 2 | Quotation, price list or catalogue | Seller | Quotation: prices and terms for the goods asked about. Price list: current prices. Catalogue: pictures, descriptions and prices of goods |
| 3 | Order | Buyer | Formal request for goods: order number, quantities, descriptions, prices, delivery address |
| 4 | Pro forma invoice (if needed) | Seller | An invoice sent **before** the goods, when the seller wants payment in advance (new customers) or as a quotation; also used for import licences |
| 5 | Advice note | Seller | Tells the buyer the goods have been sent, and how and when they will arrive |
| 6 | Delivery note | Seller (with the goods) | Lists the goods delivered; the buyer checks and signs one copy as proof of delivery |
| 7 | Invoice | Seller | Bill for the goods: quantities, prices, trade discount, terms of payment, total due |
| 8 | Debit note | Seller | Increases the amount owed (the buyer was **undercharged** on the invoice) |
| 9 | Credit note | Seller | Reduces the amount owed (goods returned, damaged or short, or the buyer was **overcharged**) |
| 10 | Statement of account | Seller | Monthly summary of the account and the balance due |
| 11 | Payment and receipt | Buyer pays; seller issues receipt | The receipt is proof of payment |

:::note{title="Debit or credit note?"}
Think from the **buyer's** point of view: a debit note adds to the debt; a credit note gives the buyer credit (reduces the debt). Credit notes are often printed in red.
:::

### Why documents matter

Documents provide **evidence** if there is a dispute about what was ordered, delivered or paid. They let both businesses **check** each other's figures, they are the source of entries in the **accounts**, and they are needed to prove income and expenses to the tax authority. Many businesses now send these documents electronically, by email or through accounting software, but their purpose and order stay the same.

### Example of a statement of account

A wholesaler's statement for Nyasha's shop for March:

| Date | Details | Debit US\$ | Credit US\$ | Balance US\$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 Mar | Balance brought forward |  |  | 350 |
| 5 Mar | Invoice 214 | 960 |  | 1,310 |
| 12 Mar | Credit note 31 (returns) |  | 120 | 1,190 |
| 20 Mar | Payment received |  | 350 | 840 |
| 28 Mar | Invoice 229 | 410 |  | 1,250 |

Invoices (and debit notes) increase the balance; credit notes and payments reduce it. Nyasha owes US\$1,250 at the end of March.

## Trade discount and cash discount

**Trade discount** is given because the buyer is a trader who needs a margin to resell the goods, and to encourage large orders. **Cash discount** is given to encourage prompt payment, which improves the seller's cash flow and reduces the risk of bad debts.

The order of calculation matters: **trade discount first**, on the list price; **cash discount second**, on the amount after trade discount.

:::formula{name="Discounts"}
Amount after trade discount = list price × (100 − trade discount %) ÷ 100

Amount paid with cash discount = amount after trade discount × (100 − cash discount %) ÷ 100
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: trade and cash discount on cement]
A wholesaler's list price for an order of cement is US\$2,400. Nyasha receives 15% trade discount, and a further 5% cash discount if she pays within 14 days.

1. Trade discount: 15% of US\$2,400 = US\$360.
2. Invoice total after trade discount: US\$2,400 − US\$360 = US\$2,040.
3. Cash discount: 5% of US\$2,040 = US\$102.
4. If she pays within 14 days she pays US\$2,040 − US\$102 = US\$1,938. If she pays later she pays US\$2,040.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: completing an invoice and avoiding the common error]
An invoice is for 50 cartons of paint brushes at US\$24 a carton, less 20% trade discount; terms 2.5% cash discount within 30 days.

1. Gross (list) value: 50 × US\$24 = US\$1,200.
2. Trade discount: 20% of US\$1,200 = US\$240, so the invoice total is US\$960.
3. Cash discount: 2.5% of US\$960 = US\$24, so prompt payment is US\$936.
4. Common error: adding the two rates (22.5%) and taking them off the list price gives US\$1,200 × 0.775 = US\$930, which is wrong, because the cash discount applies to US\$960, not to US\$1,200.
:::

Trade discount appears on the invoice and is never recorded in the accounts as a separate item; cash discount is stated in the **terms of payment** on the invoice and only taken if the buyer pays in time.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calculating cash discount on the list price"}
Cash discount is always calculated **after** trade discount has been taken off.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding the percentages"}
15% trade and 5% cash discount is **not** a 20% discount on the list price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the advice note and the delivery note"}
The advice note is sent ahead of the goods to say they are on the way; the delivery note travels **with** the goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the buyer sends the invoice"}
Invoices, debit notes, credit notes and statements are all sent by the **seller**. The buyer sends the enquiry, order and payment.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name the sender"}
For "state the purpose of a credit note", add who sends it and why: "sent by the seller to reduce the amount owed when goods are returned or the buyer was overcharged".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Lay out calculations"}
Write list price, trade discount, invoice total, cash discount and amount paid on separate lines. Each line can earn a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
These documents and discount calculations are ZIMSEC Commerce content. Cambridge 0264 does not examine trade documents.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The usual sequence is enquiry, quotation (price list or catalogue), order, pro forma invoice if needed, advice note, delivery note, invoice, debit or credit note, statement and receipt.
- Debit notes increase what the buyer owes; credit notes reduce it.
- The statement of account summarises the month and shows the balance due.
- Trade discount is given to traders on the list price; cash discount rewards prompt payment.
- Take trade discount first, then calculate cash discount on the reduced amount.
:::
