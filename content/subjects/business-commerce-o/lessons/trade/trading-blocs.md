## Introduction

A clothing manufacturer in Bulawayo wants to sell to shops in Zambia and Botswana. A cross-border trader from Mutare buys goods in Mozambique to sell at home. Both are affected by agreements that Zimbabwe has made with its neighbours to make trade easier. Groups of countries that make such agreements are called **trading blocs**.

Trading blocs appear in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3, foreign trade) and in Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4, economic integration). Expect questions that ask you to name blocs and members, state their aims, and discuss how they affect Zimbabwean businesses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic integration"}
The process by which countries reduce or remove barriers to trade and co-operate on economic policies.
:::

:::definition{term="Trading bloc"}
A group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers (such as tariffs and quotas) between themselves.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A trading bloc in which members remove tariffs and quotas on trade between themselves but each keeps its own tariffs on imports from non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Customs union"}
A free trade area in which members also charge the **same** (common external) tariff on imports from non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Common market"}
A customs union that also allows free movement of labour and capital between members.
:::

## Stages of economic integration

| Stage | Free trade between members | Common external tariff | Free movement of labour and capital | Common economic policies or currency |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Free trade area | Yes | No | No | No |
| Customs union | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Common market | Yes | Yes | Yes | No |
| Economic (and monetary) union | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Each stage includes everything in the stage before it. Many African blocs aim to move through these stages over time.

## Examples of trading blocs

| Bloc | Region | Examples of members | Main aims |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SADC (Southern African Development Community), headquarters in Gaborone, Botswana | Southern Africa | Zimbabwe, South Africa, Botswana, Zambia, Mozambique, Namibia, Malawi | Regional integration, economic growth, poverty reduction, peace and security, development of infrastructure; a SADC free trade area |
| COMESA (Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa), headquarters in Lusaka, Zambia | Eastern and southern Africa | Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Kenya, Uganda, Egypt | Free trade area moving towards a customs union and common market; larger regional market |
| ECOWAS (Economic Community of West African States), headquarters in Abuja, Nigeria | West Africa | Nigeria, Ghana, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire | Economic integration, free movement of people, common external tariff, regional peace |
| AU (African Union), headquarters in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia | Whole of Africa | Almost all African countries, including Zimbabwe | Unity, peace and development across Africa; supports a continental free trade area |
| EU (European Union), headquarters in Brussels, Belgium | Europe | France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands | Single market with free movement of goods, services, people and capital; many members share the euro |

:::note{title="Membership changes"}
Countries sometimes join or leave blocs. Learn several clear examples rather than trying to memorise every member, and check up-to-date lists before quoting numbers.
:::

## Why countries form trading blocs

Most African economies are small on their own. By joining together, member countries create a much larger market in which their businesses can sell without tariffs, which encourages investment and allows firms to produce on a larger scale. Neighbouring countries also share transport routes, rivers, power grids and borders, so co-operation on roads, railways, electricity and security benefits all of them. Acting as a group gives members a stronger voice when negotiating trade agreements with large economies and other blocs. Finally, closer economic ties encourage peace and political co-operation in the region.

## Advantages of trading blocs

**To businesses:**

- a **larger market** without tariffs, so more sales and economies of scale;
- **cheaper raw materials and components** imported from other members;
- **simpler procedures** at borders (fewer documents, quicker clearance);
- easier investment in other member countries.

**To consumers:**

- **lower prices** and **more choice** as tariffs fall and competition rises.

**To member countries:**

- more trade, investment and jobs;
- **stronger bargaining power** in negotiations with large economies and other blocs;
- co-operation on infrastructure (roads, rail, power) and on peace and security;
- free movement of labour in common markets.

## Disadvantages of trading blocs

- **More competition** for local firms from stronger firms in member countries; weak firms may close and jobs may be lost.
- **Loss of tariff revenue** for governments.
- **Trade diversion:** members may buy from a less efficient member instead of a cheaper non-member, because non-members still face tariffs.
- **Loss of independence:** members must follow common rules and cannot freely set their own tariffs (in a customs union).
- **Unequal benefits:** larger, more industrialised members may gain most, while smaller members mainly export raw materials.
- **Overlapping membership:** belonging to several blocs with different rules can be confusing for traders.

## Effects on Zimbabwe's economy and businesses

| Positive effects | Negative effects |
| --- | --- |
| Zimbabwean manufacturers can sell to regional markets duty-free or at reduced duty if their goods meet the rules of origin | Local manufacturers face cheaper imports from more efficient regional producers |
| Cheaper imported inputs from member countries | Lower customs revenue for the government |
| Easier movement of goods along regional transport corridors to ports | Some industries may relocate to larger neighbouring economies |
| Opportunities for cross-border traders and exporters of value-added products | Rules of origin and paperwork can still be complicated for small traders |

To benefit, exporters must obtain a **certificate of origin** proving their goods qualify for the reduced duty.

Rules of origin exist to stop goods from non-members being sent through a member country simply to avoid tariffs. Goods usually qualify if they were wholly produced in a member country, or if enough processing or value was added there. This links trading blocs to value addition: a Zimbabwean firm that processes local raw materials is more likely to meet the rules than one that only repacks imported goods.

:::example{title="A Zimbabwean exporter in SADC"}
A fictional food processor in Mutare makes fruit juice and jams. Because its products meet the SADC rules of origin, it can sell them in Zambia and Botswana at reduced or zero duty, which makes its prices competitive there. At the same time, a larger juice producer from another member country can sell in Zimbabwe on the same terms, so the Mutare firm must keep its costs down and its quality high to protect its home market.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a free trade area with a customs union"}
Both remove tariffs between members. Only a customs union adds a **common external tariff**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying trading blocs remove all trade barriers with the world"}
Barriers are removed between **members** only. Non-members still face tariffs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages without saying who gains"}
Specify businesses, consumers or the country, and explain the effect.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use real blocs carefully"}
Name the bloc in full the first time ("the Southern African Development Community (SADC)") and give two or three members you are sure of.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Balanced essays"}
"Discuss the effects of SADC on Zimbabwean businesses" needs both opportunities (larger market, cheaper inputs) and threats (competition), then a conclusion that depends on how competitive the business is.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
ZIMSEC Commerce studies trading blocs within foreign trade; Business Enterprise and Skills studies them as regional economic integration (SADC, COMESA, ECOWAS). Cambridge 0264 refers only to free trade agreements as a cause of globalisation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trading blocs are groups of countries that reduce trade barriers between members.
- Stages of integration: free trade area, customs union, common market, economic union.
- Examples: SADC, COMESA, ECOWAS, the AU and the EU; Zimbabwe belongs to SADC, COMESA and the AU.
- Benefits include larger markets, cheaper inputs, lower prices and bargaining power.
- Drawbacks include more competition for local firms, lost tariff revenue, trade diversion and loss of independence.
:::
