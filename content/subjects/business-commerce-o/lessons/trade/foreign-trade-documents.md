## Introduction

When Thandeka's company in Bulawayo imports industrial sewing machines from overseas, the goods may travel by ship to a port in Mozambique or South Africa, then by road or rail to Zimbabwe. Along the way the goods pass through several countries' customs, change hands between carriers and must be paid for across currencies. Every step needs a document that proves ownership, value, origin or payment.

ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) expects you to know the purpose and main contents of the documents of foreign trade and the functions of the customs authority. Questions are usually "state the purpose of…", "distinguish between…" and short scenario questions asking which document is needed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indent"}
An order sent by an importer (often through an indent house or agent) to buy goods from abroad. An **open indent** lets the agent choose the supplier; a **closed (specific) indent** names the supplier.
:::

:::definition{term="Bill of lading"}
A document issued by a shipping company for goods sent by sea. It is a receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage and a **document of title**: whoever holds it can claim the goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Airway bill (air waybill)"}
A document issued by an airline for goods sent by air: a receipt and evidence of the contract of carriage, but **not** a document of title.
:::

:::definition{term="Letter of credit"}
A promise by the importer's bank to pay the exporter once the exporter presents the documents required (such as the bill of lading and invoice).
:::

:::definition{term="Customs authority"}
The government body that controls goods entering and leaving the country and collects duties. In Zimbabwe this is the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority (ZIMRA).
:::

## The documents of foreign trade

| Document | Prepared by | Purpose and key information |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indent | Importer | Order for goods from abroad: description, quantity, price limits, delivery and payment terms |
| Pro forma invoice | Exporter | Shows the importer the price before shipment; used for payment in advance and to apply for an import licence or foreign currency |
| Consular invoice | Exporter, certified by the importing country's consul (embassy) in the exporting country | Confirms the value and description of goods so that duty is charged correctly and dumping is prevented |
| Certificate of origin | Exporter, certified by an official body (e.g. a chamber of commerce) | States the country where goods were made; needed for preferential duty under trade agreements (for example SADC or COMESA) and to enforce restrictions |
| Bill of lading | Shipping company | Sea transport: receipt, contract of carriage and document of title; lists goods, ports, ship, shipper and consignee |
| Airway bill | Airline | Air transport: receipt and contract of carriage; not a document of title |
| Customs declaration (customs specification or entry) | Exporter or importer, or their clearing agent | Declares the type, quantity, value and origin of goods to customs so that duty can be calculated and trade statistics compiled |
| Letter of credit | Importer's bank | Guarantees payment to the exporter on presentation of the agreed documents |
| Bill of exchange | Exporter (drawer) | An order to the importer (or the importer's bank) to pay a stated sum on demand or at a future date; once accepted, it can be discounted at a bank for early cash |
| Marine insurance certificate | Insurer | Proof that goods are insured during transport |

:::example{title="How a letter of credit protects both sides"}
A fictional Harare firm orders packaging machinery from a foreign supplier who has never dealt with it. The supplier fears not being paid; the Harare firm fears paying and receiving nothing. The firm's bank issues a letter of credit: the bank will pay the supplier as soon as the supplier presents a bill of lading, invoice, insurance certificate and certificate of origin proving the goods were shipped as ordered. The supplier is sure of payment and the importer pays only when the goods are on their way.
:::

### The order in which the documents are used

A typical import by sea follows these steps:

1. The importer sends an **indent** (often through an indent house or agent).
2. The exporter may reply with a **pro forma invoice**, which the importer uses to arrange foreign currency or an import licence and, if required, a **letter of credit** with its bank.
3. The exporter ships the goods and receives a **bill of lading** from the shipping company; it insures the goods and obtains a **certificate of origin** and, where required, a **consular invoice**.
4. The exporter sends the shipping documents through the banks, often with a **bill of exchange**; the importer's bank pays (or the importer accepts the bill) and releases the documents.
5. The importer, usually through a clearing agent, presents the documents and a **customs declaration** to customs, pays any duty and collects the goods.

Knowing this order helps you answer questions that ask which document is needed at a particular stage.

### Bill of lading and airway bill compared

| Point | Bill of lading | Airway bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transport | Sea | Air |
| Document of title | Yes: can be transferred to sell the goods in transit | No |
| Speed of goods | Slow; the bill often arrives before the goods | Fast; the bill travels with the goods |

## Customs authorities: ZIMRA

The Zimbabwe Revenue Authority (ZIMRA) collects taxes for the government and administers customs at border posts, airports and inland ports. Its customs functions include:

- **collecting customs duties** (import duties and, where they apply, export duties) and other taxes charged on imports, which are government revenue;
- **preventing smuggling** of goods brought in without paying duty, and of prohibited goods such as illegal drugs and weapons;
- **enforcing trade restrictions**: quotas, embargoes and import or export permits;
- **controlling bonded warehouses**: warehouses where imported goods are stored **without paying duty** until they are released for sale at home (duty is then paid) or re-exported (no duty is paid);
- **collecting trade statistics** from customs declarations, which are used to calculate the balance of trade;
- checking documents such as certificates of origin so that goods from trading-bloc partners receive the agreed lower duty;
- protecting public health and safety by stopping goods that do not meet standards.

### Bonded warehouses

Bonded warehouses help importers and entrepot traders:

- duty is paid only when goods are released, a little at a time, which helps cash flow;
- goods for re-export pay no duty at all;
- goods can be repacked, blended or sampled while in bond;
- customs officers can check the goods easily.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the airway bill a document of title"}
Only the bill of lading is a document of title. Goods sent by air are delivered to the named consignee.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the certificate of origin and the consular invoice"}
The certificate of origin states **where** goods were made; the consular invoice confirms their **value**, certified by the importing country's consul.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking goods in a bonded warehouse never pay duty"}
Duty is **delayed**, not removed: it is paid when goods are released for home use. Only re-exported goods escape it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Purpose questions"}
For "state the purpose of a bill of lading", give two or three functions: receipt for goods, evidence of the contract of carriage, document of title.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Link customs to other topics"}
Customs links to protectionism (duties, quotas), trading blocs (certificates of origin) and the balance of payments (trade statistics). Examiners like these links in essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
These documents and customs functions are ZIMSEC Commerce content; Cambridge 0264 does not examine them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Importers order with an indent; exporters may send a pro forma invoice first.
- The consular invoice confirms value; the certificate of origin confirms where goods were made.
- The bill of lading (sea) is a receipt, contract of carriage and document of title; the airway bill (air) is not a document of title.
- Letters of credit and bills of exchange arrange safe payment between countries.
- ZIMRA collects duties, prevents smuggling, enforces restrictions, controls bonded warehouses and collects trade statistics.
:::
