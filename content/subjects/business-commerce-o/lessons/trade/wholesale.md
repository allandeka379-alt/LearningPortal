## Introduction

A soap factory in Harare produces hundreds of thousands of bars each month. Thousands of tuck shops, supermarkets and rural stores around Zimbabwe each want only a few cartons at a time. Somebody has to bridge that gap. That is the job of the **wholesaler**: buying in bulk from producers and selling in smaller quantities to retailers.

This Form 1 Commerce topic is examined in nearly every paper. You need the services of the wholesaler to each group, the types of wholesaler, the reasons for "cutting out the middleman", and the work of marketing boards and commodity markets.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Wholesaler"}
A trader who buys goods in large quantities from producers (or importers) and sells them in smaller quantities to retailers.
:::

:::definition{term="Breaking bulk"}
Buying in large quantities and dividing them into smaller quantities for sale.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of distribution"}
The route goods take from the producer to the consumer, for example producer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing board"}
A body set up by government (or by law) to buy, store and sell particular farm produce, often at set or guaranteed prices.
:::

:::definition{term="Commodity market"}
An organised market where large quantities of raw materials (commodities) of standard grades are bought and sold.
:::

## Services of the wholesaler

**To the manufacturer (producer):**

- Buys in bulk, so the manufacturer has fewer, larger orders and lower selling costs.
- Stores goods (warehousing), saving the manufacturer storage space.
- Pays promptly, sometimes in advance, which helps the manufacturer's cash flow.
- Bears risks of price changes, damage and goods going out of fashion.
- Passes on market information (what retailers are asking for).
- May advertise and transport the goods.

**To the retailer:**

- **Breaks bulk**: sells in small quantities, so the retailer does not need large capital or storage.
- Offers **variety**: goods from many manufacturers in one place.
- Gives **credit**, so the retailer can sell before paying.
- **Delivers** goods and gives advice on new products and display.
- Holds stock, so goods are available when needed.

**To the consumer:**

- Goods are available all year because wholesalers store them.
- Prices are steadier because wholesalers release stock when supplies are short.
- Grading, packing and labelling make goods easier to buy.

## Types of wholesaler

| Type | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| General wholesaler | Stocks a wide range of goods (groceries, hardware, clothing) |
| Specialist wholesaler | Stocks one line of goods, such as pharmaceuticals or building materials |
| Cash and carry | Self-service warehouse; retailers choose goods, pay cash and transport them themselves; no credit or delivery, so prices are lower |
| Co-operative wholesale society | Owned by retail co-operatives; supplies its member shops and shares profits with them |
| Regional or national wholesaler | Serves one region or the whole country from one or more depots |

:::example{title="A cash and carry"}
Nyasha owns a tuck shop in Chitungwiza. Every Monday she drives to a cash and carry in Harare, walks the aisles with a trolley, buys cartons of sugar, cooking oil and soap, pays cash and takes them back in her own car. She gets lower prices than she would from a delivery wholesaler, but she must pay at once and spend time and fuel collecting the goods.
:::

## Eliminating (bypassing) the wholesaler

Sometimes the wholesaler is "cut out" of the chain of distribution.

| Route | When it is used |
| --- | --- |
| Producer → retailer → consumer | Large retailers (supermarket chains) buy in bulk directly |
| Producer → consumer | Perishable goods (fresh milk, bread), goods made to order (furniture, tailored suits), farm-gate sales, factory shops, online sales |

Reasons for bypassing the wholesaler:

- Large retailers can buy in bulk and store goods themselves.
- Perishable goods must reach consumers quickly.
- Goods made to order or needing technical advice are better sold directly.
- Manufacturers want control over how their goods are presented and priced.
- Removing a middleman's profit may lower prices or raise the producer's profit.
- E-commerce makes it easy for producers to sell directly.

Bypassing the wholesaler does **not** remove the wholesaler's **functions**: someone must still store, transport, break bulk and bear risk. The manufacturer or retailer simply takes these over, and pays their costs.

## Marketing boards

Marketing boards are set up to organise the buying and selling of important farm produce. In Zimbabwe, the **Grain Marketing Board (GMB)** buys and stores grain such as maize and wheat and holds strategic grain reserves for food security.

Functions of marketing boards:

- buying produce from farmers, often at announced (sometimes guaranteed) prices;
- **grading** produce and checking its quality;
- **storing** produce in depots and silos;
- holding **strategic reserves** for times of drought;
- selling produce to processors (millers) and, in some cases, exporting it;
- providing farmers with inputs, advice or finance in some schemes;
- helping to keep prices stable.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Farmers have a guaranteed buyer | Payments to farmers may be delayed |
| Price stability helps farmers plan | Set prices may be below what private buyers offer |
| Reserves protect the country against food shortages | May be slow and costly to run |
| Grading improves quality | Farmers far from depots face high transport costs |

## Commodity markets

Commodity markets bring together large buyers and sellers of standard raw materials such as metals, grains, coffee, cotton and tobacco. Goods are sold by **grade** or **sample**, so buyers need not inspect every load. Examples include international metal exchanges and grain exchanges, and tobacco auction floors where buyers bid for farmers' bales.

Functions of commodity markets:

- bring buyers and sellers together in one place, physical or electronic;
- set a market price through bidding (**price discovery**);
- grade commodities so they can be traded without inspection;
- allow **spot** deals (immediate delivery) and **futures** deals (delivery later at a price agreed now), which help businesses reduce the risk of price changes (**hedging**);
- provide information on prices and supplies.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying bypassing the wholesaler removes costs entirely"}
The functions (storage, transport, breaking bulk) still have to be done and paid for by someone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing services to the wrong group"}
"Breaks bulk" is a service to the **retailer**; "buys in bulk" is a service to the **manufacturer**. Read which group the question asks about.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a cash and carry as giving credit"}
A cash and carry offers no credit and no delivery; that is why its prices are lower.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the benefit"}
"The wholesaler stores goods" earns one mark; add "so the manufacturer does not need large warehouses" for the second.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss the middleman"}
Essays often ask whether the wholesaler should be eliminated. Give the reasons for bypassing, the functions that must still be done, and a conclusion that it depends on the size of the retailer and the type of goods.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board coverage"}
This topic is in ZIMSEC Commerce. Cambridge 0264 covers channels of distribution under place in the marketing mix; there, wholesalers appear only as part of a channel.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wholesalers buy in bulk from producers and sell in smaller quantities to retailers.
- They serve manufacturers (bulk buying, storage, risk, finance), retailers (breaking bulk, credit, variety, delivery) and consumers (availability, stable prices).
- Types include general, specialist, cash and carry and co-operative wholesalers.
- The wholesaler is bypassed by large retailers, for perishable or made-to-order goods and through e-commerce, but its functions remain.
- Marketing boards buy, grade, store and sell farm produce; commodity markets trade graded raw materials at market prices, spot or futures.
:::
