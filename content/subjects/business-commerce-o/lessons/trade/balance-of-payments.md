## Introduction

When a tobacco farmer in Karoi sells her crop to a foreign buyer, foreign currency flows into Zimbabwe. When a Harare company buys a tractor from overseas, or when a tourist from Zimbabwe pays for a hotel abroad, money flows out. A country keeps a record of all these flows in its **balance of payments**.

This Form 4 Commerce topic combines definitions with straightforward calculations. Examiners expect you to classify items as visible or invisible, calculate the balance of trade, the invisible balance and the current account balance, and explain how a deficit can be corrected.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Visible trade"}
Trade in **goods** (things that can be seen and touched), such as tobacco, gold, machinery and fuel.
:::

:::definition{term="Invisible trade"}
Trade in **services**, such as tourism, transport (freight), insurance, banking and consultancy.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of trade (visible balance)"}
The difference between the value of visible exports and the value of visible imports over a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Invisible balance"}
The difference between the value of invisible exports and the value of invisible imports.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of payments"}
A record of all the financial transactions between a country and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. The **current account** records trade in goods and services (and income and transfer payments); the **capital (and financial) account** records flows of investment and loans.
:::

:::formula{name="Balances"}
Balance of trade = visible exports − visible imports

Invisible balance = invisible exports − invisible imports

Current account balance = balance of trade + invisible balance
:::

A positive result is a **surplus** (favourable); a negative result is a **deficit** (unfavourable), written in brackets or with a minus sign.

## Visible and invisible exports and imports

The direction of the **money** decides whether an item is an export or an import:

- money flows **in** to the country: an **export** (visible or invisible);
- money flows **out** of the country: an **import**.

| Item (from Zimbabwe's point of view) | Classification |
| --- | --- |
| Platinum sold to a foreign refinery | Visible export |
| Fuel bought from abroad | Visible import |
| Foreign tourists spend money at Victoria Falls hotels | Invisible export (money flows in) |
| A Zimbabwean family on holiday abroad | Invisible import |
| A Zimbabwean firm pays a foreign shipping line freight charges | Invisible import |
| A foreign firm buys insurance from a Zimbabwean insurer | Invisible export |

:::keyfact{title="Tourism trap"}
Foreign tourists visiting Zimbabwe are an **invisible export** for Zimbabwe, because they bring foreign currency **in**, even though nothing leaves the country.
:::

## Calculating the balances

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Country K's current account]
A fictional country, Country K, records the following for one year (US\$ million): visible exports 4,800; visible imports 5,600; invisible exports 900; invisible imports 1,300.

1. Balance of trade = 4,800 − 5,600 = (800): a deficit of US\$800 million.
2. Invisible balance = 900 − 1,300 = (400): a deficit of US\$400 million.
3. Current account balance = (800) + (400) = (1,200): a deficit of US\$1,200 million.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: classifying items first]
Country L's transactions for a year (US\$ million) are listed below. Calculate its balance of trade, invisible balance and current account balance.

| Item | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Tobacco exports | 2,100 |
| Mineral exports | 3,400 |
| Fuel imports | 1,900 |
| Machinery imports | 2,600 |
| Spending by foreign tourists in Country L | 650 |
| Banking and insurance services sold to foreign firms | 300 |
| Freight charges paid to foreign transport firms | 480 |
| Insurance premiums paid to foreign insurers | 170 |

1. Visible exports = 2,100 + 3,400 = 5,500; visible imports = 1,900 + 2,600 = 4,500.
2. Balance of trade = 5,500 − 4,500 = 1,000: a surplus of US\$1,000 million.
3. Invisible exports = 650 + 300 = 950; invisible imports = 480 + 170 = 650.
4. Invisible balance = 950 − 650 = 300: a surplus of US\$300 million.
5. Current account balance = 1,000 + 300 = 1,300: a surplus of US\$1,300 million.
:::

In full national accounts the current account also includes income (such as interest and profits paid abroad) and transfers such as money sent home by citizens working abroad. In Commerce calculations you will normally work with visible and invisible trade only, unless the question gives other items.

## Why the balance of payments always "balances"

Over all its accounts, a country's payments must be financed. A current account deficit is paid for by borrowing from abroad, by foreign investment flowing in, or by using the country's reserves of foreign currency. That is why the full balance of payments always balances in an accounting sense, while the **current account** can be in surplus or deficit.

## Why a persistent deficit matters

A deficit for a year or two can be financed easily, but a deficit that continues for many years means the country keeps borrowing or running down its foreign currency reserves. Debts must be repaid with interest, and shortages of foreign currency make it hard for businesses to import fuel, machinery and raw materials.

## Causes of a current account deficit

- **High demand for imports**, for example fuel, machinery, vehicles and food after a drought.
- **Low export earnings**, because of falling world prices for minerals or crops, poor harvests or low production.
- **Uncompetitive exports**: high production costs or poor quality.
- **A strong currency** (high exchange rate) that makes exports dear and imports cheap.
- **Large payments for services**, such as freight and insurance for a landlocked country.

## Correcting a deficit

| Measure | How it helps | Possible problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Import restrictions (tariffs, quotas, exchange control) | Reduce spending on imports | Retaliation; higher prices; shortages of inputs |
| Export promotion (trade fairs, incentives, export finance) | Raise export earnings | Takes time; costs government money |
| Encouraging value addition and beneficiation | Exports earn more per unit | Needs capital, power and skills |
| Import substitution (producing locally what was imported) | Reduces imports | Local goods may be dearer at first |
| Devaluation or depreciation of the currency | Exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home | Imported inputs and fuel cost more; inflation |
| Promoting tourism | Raises invisible exports | Depends on the country's image and infrastructure |
| Reducing domestic spending (higher taxes, interest rates) | Less spending on imports | Slower growth and job losses |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Classifying tourism the wrong way round"}
Foreign tourists in Zimbabwe = invisible **export**; Zimbabweans holidaying abroad = invisible **import**. Follow the money.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the balance of trade the balance of payments"}
The balance of trade covers **goods only**. The current account adds services (and income and transfers).
:::

:::mistake{title="Losing the sign"}
A deficit must be shown as negative, in brackets or with the word "deficit". "800" alone suggests a surplus.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Visible trade is in goods; invisible trade is in services such as tourism, transport, insurance and banking.
- Balance of trade = visible exports − visible imports; invisible balance = invisible exports − invisible imports.
- Current account balance = balance of trade + invisible balance; negative means a deficit.
- Deficits result from high imports, weak exports, uncompetitive goods or a strong currency.
- Remedies include import restrictions, export promotion, value addition, import substitution and devaluation, each with costs.
:::
