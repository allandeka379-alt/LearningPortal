## Introduction

Every day at Mbare Musika in Harare, farmers from Mutoko, Murehwa and Chimanimani sell vegetables to traders, who sell them on to vendors, who sell them to families across the city. Each person gives up something they have in exchange for something they want. This is **trade**.

Trade is the core of Commerce. This Form 1 topic introduces the words you will use for the rest of the course (home trade, foreign trade, retail, wholesale, aids to trade), so examiners test it often in Paper 1 multiple-choice questions and in the opening parts of structured questions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade"}
The buying and selling (exchange) of goods and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Barter"}
The direct exchange of goods or services for other goods or services, without using money.
:::

:::definition{term="Money"}
Anything that is generally accepted as a means of payment and in settlement of debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Home trade"}
Trade between buyers and sellers within one country. It is also called domestic or internal trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign trade"}
Trade between people and businesses in different countries. It is also called international or external trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Aids to trade"}
Services that help trade to take place: transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising.
:::

## Why people trade

People trade because of **specialisation**. A person who specialises produces more of one thing than they need (a **surplus**) and none of many other things they want. Trade lets them:

- exchange their surplus for goods they do not produce;
- enjoy a wider variety of goods and a higher standard of living;
- obtain goods that cannot be produced locally, for example because of climate or a lack of minerals;
- earn an income from what they produce.

## Barter and its problems

Before money was widely used, people exchanged goods directly. A farmer might exchange a goat for several bags of maize. Barter still happens today, for example when a villager exchanges vegetables for a neighbour's help with ploughing.

Barter has serious problems:

| Problem | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Double coincidence of wants | Each person must want what the other has, at the same time | Tendai wants shoes and has chickens; the shoemaker must want chickens |
| No common measure of value | It is hard to agree how many of one good equal another | How many bags of maize is one cow worth? |
| Indivisibility | Some goods cannot be divided without losing value | A cow cannot be cut up to buy a single pair of shoes |
| Difficulty of storing wealth | Many goods are perishable or costly to keep | Tomatoes rot; cattle need grazing and water |
| No standard for deferred payment | Lending and borrowing are hard to measure | A loan of maize repaid next year: how much, and of what quality? |
| Bulky goods are hard to carry | Moving goods to exchange is costly | Taking cattle to a distant market |

## Trade using money (monetary trade)

Money overcomes the problems of barter because it performs four functions:

- **Medium of exchange:** people sell goods for money and use the money to buy what they want, so a double coincidence of wants is no longer needed.
- **Measure of value (unit of account):** every good has a price, so values can be compared easily.
- **Store of value:** money can be saved for future use without rotting (although inflation can reduce its value).
- **Standard of deferred payment:** debts can be stated in money and repaid later.

Money also makes specialisation and large-scale trade possible. Today money includes notes and coins, bank deposits, and mobile money and card payments.

## The branches of trade

Trade has two main branches, each with smaller branches.

- **Home trade**
  - **Retail trade:** selling goods in small quantities to final consumers (for example a supermarket or a tuck shop).
  - **Wholesale trade:** buying in bulk from producers and selling in smaller quantities to retailers (for example a cash and carry).
- **Foreign trade**
  - **Import trade:** buying goods and services from other countries.
  - **Export trade:** selling goods and services to other countries.
  - **Entrepot trade:** importing goods and re-exporting them to other countries, often after storing, sorting or repacking.

:::example{title="Branches of trade in one product"}
A fictional company in Harare imports bicycles from overseas (**import trade**). It sells most of them in bulk to shops across Zimbabwe (**wholesale trade**), and the shops sell them one at a time to customers (**retail trade**). It sends some of the imported bicycles on to buyers in Zambia (**re-export**, an example of entrepot trade).
:::

### How home trade and foreign trade differ

Home trade uses one currency, one set of laws and usually one language, and goods travel relatively short distances. Foreign trade involves converting currencies, obeying the customs rules of at least two countries, longer and riskier journeys, and often different languages and business customs. For this reason foreign trade needs extra documents and specialist services such as clearing agents and marine insurance, which you will study in later lessons.

:::example{title="Trade in a rural service centre"}
At a growth point in Gokwe, a cotton farmer sells his crop to a buyer for cash, pays a grinding mill owner to mill his maize, buys fertiliser from an agro-dealer and sends money to his daughter in Harare by mobile money. In one morning he takes part in selling, buying and paying for services: trade made easy by money and by the aids to trade.
:::

## The aids to trade

Trade would be very difficult without services that remove the barriers between producers and consumers.

| Aid to trade | How it helps trade |
| --- | --- |
| Transport | Moves goods from where they are made to where they are wanted |
| Communication | Lets buyers and sellers contact each other: phones, the internet, post |
| Banking | Provides safe ways of paying and loans to finance trade |
| Insurance | Covers the risk of loss or damage to goods and premises |
| Warehousing | Stores goods until they are needed |
| Advertising | Informs consumers about goods and persuades them to buy |

Together, trade and the aids to trade make up **commerce**, which is part of tertiary production.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all foreign trade 'imports'"}
Foreign trade includes imports, exports and entrepot trade. Learn all three.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing barter's problem as just 'it is difficult'"}
Name the problem precisely (double coincidence of wants, no measure of value, indivisibility) and give a short example.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting wholesale in foreign trade"}
Wholesaling and retailing are branches of **home** trade, even when a wholesaler sells imported goods.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Diagram-style answers"}
If asked to "show the branches of trade", use an indented list like the one above: trade → home (retail, wholesale) and foreign (import, export, entrepot).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Problem plus solution"}
When explaining how money overcomes barter, pair each problem with the function that solves it: double coincidence of wants → medium of exchange; no measure of value → unit of account.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trade is the exchange of goods and services; it is needed because people specialise and produce a surplus.
- Barter suffers from the double coincidence of wants, no common measure of value, indivisibility and storage problems.
- Money is a medium of exchange, a measure of value, a store of value and a standard of deferred payment.
- Home trade is retail and wholesale; foreign trade is import, export and entrepot.
- The aids to trade are transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising.
:::
