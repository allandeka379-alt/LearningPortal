## Introduction

A tea estate in Chipinge pays its pickers for each kilogram of tea they pick. A bank in Harare pays its clerks a monthly salary and gives them a medical-aid contribution. A clothing shop in Gweru pays its sales assistants a small basic wage plus a share of what they sell. Each business has chosen a way of paying that suits its work, and each also uses rewards that are not money at all: praise, training and promotion.

Business Enterprise and Skills (Forms 2 and 4) and Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.1.2) ask you to explain financial and non-financial methods of motivation and to **recommend** a method for a particular business or group of workers. Simple pay calculations, such as working out wages under time rate, piece rate or commission, also appear in case studies.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Time rate"}
Pay for each hour (or day) worked, regardless of how much is produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Piece rate"}
Pay for each unit produced or task completed.
:::

:::definition{term="Salary"}
A fixed annual amount, usually paid monthly, that does not depend on hours worked or output.
:::

:::definition{term="Commission"}
Payment, usually a percentage of the value of sales made, paid to sales staff in addition to or instead of a basic wage.
:::

:::definition{term="Bonus"}
An extra lump-sum payment for meeting a target or for good performance.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit sharing"}
Paying part of the business's profit to employees, usually once a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Fringe benefits"}
Non-wage rewards given to employees, such as a company car, free meals, housing, medical-aid contributions, school fees or staff discounts.
:::

## Financial methods

| Method | Suits | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time rate | Work where output is hard to count (security guards, cleaners) | Simple; workers know their pay; quality not sacrificed for speed | No reward for effort; supervision needed to keep output up |
| Piece rate | Countable output (sewing, picking, packing) | Encourages high output; cost per unit is fixed | Quality may fall; pay falls if machines break down; stressful |
| Salary | Managers, office and professional staff | Secure income; easy to budget | No direct link to effort |
| Commission | Sales staff | Encourages selling; low cost if sales are low | Pushy selling; unstable income for staff |
| Bonus | Most jobs with targets | Rewards achievement | Targets may be unfair; becomes expected |
| Profit sharing | All employees | Workers share the business's success and care about costs | Small amount per worker; paid long after effort |
| Fringe benefits | Most staff, especially managers | Valued by staff; help recruitment | Costly for the business |

### Calculating pay

:::worked-solution[Time rate with overtime]
Rudo works in a packing warehouse. She is paid US\$2.50 an hour for a 42-hour week. Overtime is paid at time and a half. One week she works 6 hours of overtime.
1. Basic pay = 42 × US\$2.50 = US\$105.
2. Overtime rate = US\$2.50 × 1.5 = US\$3.75 an hour.
3. Overtime pay = 6 × US\$3.75 = US\$22.50.
4. Gross pay for the week = US\$105 + US\$22.50 = **US\$127.50**.
:::

| Method | Calculation | Pay US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Time rate (basic) | 42 hours × 2.50 | 105 |
| Overtime at time and a half | 6 hours × 3.75 | 22.50 |
| Gross pay | | 127.50 |

:::worked-solution[Piece rate]
Tatenda sews school shirts and is paid US\$0.60 for each shirt that passes inspection. This week 180 shirts passed.
1. Pay = number of units × rate per unit.
2. Pay = 180 × US\$0.60 = **US\$108**.
3. Shirts that fail inspection earn nothing, so piece rate here also encourages quality.
:::

:::worked-solution[Basic pay plus commission]
Chipo sells solar equipment. She earns a basic US\$300 a month plus 4% commission on her sales. In March she sold US\$12,500 worth of equipment.
1. Commission = 4% × US\$12,500 = 4 ÷ 100 × 12,500 = US\$500.
2. Total pay = basic + commission = US\$300 + US\$500 = **US\$800**.
:::

## Non-financial methods

| Method | What it means | Why it motivates (theory link) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job enrichment | Giving workers more responsibility and more challenging tasks, such as a whole unit of work | Responsibility and achievement are Herzberg motivators |
| Job rotation | Moving workers between similar tasks in turn | Reduces boredom; workers learn several skills |
| Training | Developing workers' skills | Personal growth; self-actualisation (Maslow) |
| Promotion opportunities | A clear path to more senior posts | Status and esteem |
| Praise and recognition | Thanks, certificates, employee of the month | Esteem needs; Herzberg's recognition |
| Team working | Workers organised in teams with shared targets | Social needs; workers support each other |
| Job enlargement | Adding more tasks at the same level | More variety, though not more responsibility |

:::example{title="Job rotation and enrichment in a hotel"}
A hotel in Kariba moves its reception staff between the front desk, reservations and guest relations every month (**job rotation**), so the work is less repetitive. Senior receptionists are also made responsible for training new staff and handling complaints from start to finish (**job enrichment**), which gives them responsibility and recognition.
:::

## Recommending a method

Ask four questions about the case:

1. **Can output be measured?** If yes, piece rate or a bonus may work; if not, time rate or salary.
2. **Does quality matter more than quantity?** If so, avoid pure piece rate or add a quality check.
3. **What can the business afford?** Non-financial methods such as praise and job rotation are cheap; fringe benefits and profit sharing are costly.
4. **What do the workers need?** Low-paid workers value money (Maslow's lower levels, Taylor); well-paid workers are more likely to be motivated by responsibility and recognition (Herzberg).

For example, a commission-only scheme for a new salesperson in a quiet season could leave him with almost no income, so a basic wage plus a smaller commission is fairer and keeps him in the job. Often the best answer is a **combination**: a fair basic wage plus a bonus, together with training and recognition.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling commission a fixed payment"}
Commission depends on the value of sales. A salary is fixed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing job rotation with job enrichment"}
Job rotation moves workers between tasks at the same level. Job enrichment adds responsibility and challenge.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the overtime rate"}
Overtime at "time and a half" is 1.5 times the normal hourly rate; "double time" is 2 times. Work out the overtime rate before multiplying by the overtime hours.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend a method"}
Name one method, explain why it suits the workers and the work in the case, give one drawback and how to reduce it, then conclude. Linking to Maslow, Taylor or Herzberg adds analysis.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 lists wages, salaries, bonuses, commission, profit sharing and fringe benefits, and job rotation, job enlargement, job enrichment, team working, training and promotion. ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills uses the same ideas and may ask for simple wage calculations, so show your working.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Financial methods: time rate, piece rate, salary, commission, bonus, profit sharing and fringe benefits.
- Pay calculations: hours × rate (overtime at a higher rate), units × piece rate, basic pay + commission %.
- Non-financial methods: job enrichment, job rotation, job enlargement, training, promotion, praise and team working.
- Choose a method that fits the work (can output be measured?), quality needs, cost and the workers' needs.
- The best schemes usually combine fair pay with recognition and responsibility.
:::
