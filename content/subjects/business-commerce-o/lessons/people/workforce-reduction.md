## Introduction

A grain-milling company in Bulawayo installs an automated packing line. Work that needed twelve packers now needs three machine operators. At the same time, a rival mill has taken some of its customers, so it needs fewer delivery drivers. The company must reduce the size of its workforce. How should it decide who goes, and what is the difference between losing a job because the work has gone and losing it because of bad behaviour?

Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.2.4) asks why a business may need to reduce its workforce, the difference between **redundancy** and **dismissal**, and how to recommend which employees to make redundant from information in a case. These decisions affect stakeholders deeply, so good answers also consider motivation of the remaining staff and the business's reputation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Downsizing"}
Reducing the number of employees in a business, usually to cut costs or because less work is needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Redundancy"}
Losing a job because the job is no longer needed, through no fault of the employee.
:::

:::definition{term="Dismissal"}
Ending an employee's contract because of the employee's conduct or ability, for example theft, violence or repeated poor work after warnings.
:::

:::definition{term="Redundancy payment"}
A payment (compensation) made to an employee whose job has been made redundant, often based on length of service.
:::

## Why a business may reduce its workforce

| Reason | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Automation and new technology | Machines or computer systems do work that people used to do |
| Fall in demand | Fewer customers, a recession or a stronger competitor means less output is needed |
| Cost cutting | Losses or falling profits force the business to reduce wage costs |
| Reorganisation or delayering | Removing a level of management or merging departments |
| Closure or relocation | A branch, factory or department is closed or moved |
| Merger or takeover | Two businesses combine and duplicate jobs are cut |

## Redundancy compared with dismissal

| | Redundancy | Dismissal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | The job is no longer needed | The employee's behaviour or ability |
| Employee's fault? | No | Yes |
| Compensation | Usually a redundancy payment | Usually none (but must be fair, or it is unfair dismissal) |
| Example | A bank closes a branch and the tellers' jobs disappear | A teller is caught taking money from the till |

Even a dismissal must be fair: the employer needs a good reason and a fair procedure, such as warnings and a hearing, or it risks a claim for unfair dismissal. A dismissed worker may be replaced by a new worker in the same job. A redundant worker is **not** replaced, because the job itself has gone.

## Alternatives to redundancy

Before making employees redundant, a business may:

- stop recruiting and let numbers fall as people leave or retire (**natural wastage**);
- offer **voluntary redundancy** or early retirement to those who want to leave;
- cut overtime or move some staff to **part-time** hours;
- **retrain** and redeploy workers to other departments.

These protect morale and the business's reputation, but they may be slower or not reduce costs enough. Voluntary redundancy also carries a risk: the most skilled workers, who know they can find another job easily, may be the first to volunteer, leaving the business weaker. Managers may therefore keep the right to refuse a volunteer whose skills are essential.

## Deciding which employees to make redundant

A fair decision uses clear, objective criteria applied equally to everyone in the affected group, for example:

- **skills and qualifications** needed for the future;
- **performance** records and appraisals;
- **attendance and discipline** records;
- **length of service** ("last in, first out" is sometimes used, though it may lose good young workers);
- willingness to be **flexible** or retrain.

:::example{title="Selecting a packer for redundancy"}
The grain mill must cut one job from three packers. Its managers score each packer from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on three criteria.

| Packer | Skills for the new machines | Attendance | Performance | Total (out of 15) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tendai | 4 | 3 | 4 | 11 |
| Rudo | 2 | 4 | 3 | 9 |
| Farai | 3 | 5 | 4 | 12 |

On the totals, Rudo has the lowest score (9), so a fair, consistent system would select her. However, managers should also consider whether Rudo could be retrained for the new machines, and whether she would like voluntary redundancy. They must not use reasons that are discrimination, such as her gender or age.
:::

## Effects of downsizing

**On the business:**
- Lower wage costs and higher productivity if the right people remain.
- Redundancy payments are a one-off cost.
- The remaining workers may fear for their jobs, so motivation may fall.
- Loss of experienced staff and their skills.
- Possible damage to reputation in the local community.

**On employees:**
- Those made redundant lose income, though they may receive a redundancy payment.
- Those who remain may have heavier workloads and less job security.

:::example{title="Communicating downsizing"}
A mobile-phone repair chain closes two of its six shops in Harare. It explains the reasons to all staff, offers voluntary redundancy first, moves some technicians to the remaining shops and gives a reference and a fair payment to those who leave. Clear, honest communication keeps the remaining staff motivated.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating redundancy as a punishment"}
Redundancy is not the employee's fault. Dismissal is the result of the employee's conduct or ability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a redundant worker is replaced"}
If a new person is hired to do the same job, the job was not redundant.
:::

:::mistake{title="Choosing on one criterion only"}
When recommending who to make redundant from a table, use all the criteria given, show the comparison and justify the choice, noting any alternative such as retraining.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend questions"}
Work through the data for each employee, state who scores lowest or is least needed for the future, add a qualification (could they be retrained? voluntary redundancy?) and reach a clear conclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Downsizing and redundancy are in Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.2.4). Link answers to motivation (job security is a safety need in Maslow's hierarchy) and to stakeholders.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses downsize because of automation, falling demand, cost cutting, reorganisation or closure.
- Redundancy: the job is no longer needed, not the employee's fault, usually with a payment.
- Dismissal: because of the employee's conduct or ability; the job may be refilled.
- Alternatives include natural wastage, voluntary redundancy, part-time work and retraining.
- Fair selection uses objective criteria such as skills, performance, attendance and service.
- Downsizing cuts costs but may lower the morale of remaining staff.
:::
