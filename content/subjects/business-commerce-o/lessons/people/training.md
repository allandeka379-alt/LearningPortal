## Introduction

A new cashier at a supermarket in Masvingo is shown where the fire exits are, introduced to her supervisor and given her uniform on her first morning. That afternoon an experienced cashier shows her how to use the till. A year later she is sent on a two-week course at a college in Harare to learn stock control so that she can become a supervisor. In one year she has had three different kinds of **training**.

Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.3.3) asks why training is important and expects you to compare induction, on-the-job and off-the-job training and to recommend the most suitable type in a case. Training also links to motivation (Herzberg's personal growth, Maslow's self-actualisation) and to recruitment (new recruits need induction).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Training"}
Improving the knowledge and skills of employees so that they can do their jobs well.
:::

:::definition{term="Induction training"}
An introduction for new employees to the business: its layout, people, rules, health and safety procedures and their job.
:::

:::definition{term="On-the-job training"}
Training given at the workplace while the employee does the job, usually by watching and working with an experienced employee.
:::

:::definition{term="Off-the-job training"}
Training given away from the employee's normal work, for example at a college, training centre or a separate training room.
:::

## Why training is important

**To the business:**
- Employees work faster and make fewer mistakes, so **productivity** and quality rise.
- Employees can use new technology and methods.
- Better customer service wins repeat customers.
- Fewer accidents when workers know safety procedures.
- Trained, motivated workers are less likely to leave, so **labour turnover** falls.
- Future managers can be developed from within.

**To employees:**
- New skills make work easier and more interesting.
- Better chances of promotion and higher pay.
- Greater confidence and job satisfaction (personal growth).

## Induction training

Induction usually lasts from a few hours to a few days. It covers the business's history and aims, a tour of the premises, introductions to colleagues and the supervisor, health and safety rules, hours, breaks and pay arrangements, and the basics of the job.

- **Advantages:** new employees settle quickly, become productive sooner and know safety rules from the start; they are less likely to leave in the first weeks.
- **Disadvantages:** costs time and wages while the new employee is not yet productive; too much information at once may be forgotten.

## On-the-job training

The trainee learns by watching and then doing the job, guided by an experienced worker or supervisor.

- **Advantages:** cheap (no course fees or travel); the trainee produces something while learning; training is specific to the business's own machines and methods.
- **Disadvantages:** the experienced worker's output falls while teaching; bad habits may be passed on; the trainee may make costly mistakes on real work or with real customers; the trainer may not be a good teacher.

## Off-the-job training

The trainee attends a course away from the workplace, run by a college, a professional body, an equipment supplier or the business's own training centre.

- **Advantages:** taught by experts, often to a recognised qualification; trainees can concentrate without the pressure of work; wider skills and new ideas are brought back; no risk to real products or customers.
- **Disadvantages:** expensive (fees, travel, accommodation and wages while away); no output during training; skills may be general, not specific to the business; qualified employees may leave for better-paid jobs elsewhere.

| | Induction | On-the-job | Off-the-job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who | New employees | New and existing employees | Mainly existing employees |
| Where | At the workplace | At the workplace, doing the job | Away from the workplace |
| Cost | Low | Low | High |
| Best for | Settling in, safety, rules | Practical, business-specific skills | New technology, management, qualifications |

:::example{title="Choosing the type of training"}
A bus company in Bulawayo is buying new buses with automatic gearboxes and electronic ticket machines.

- Drivers who already hold licences can learn the new gearbox **on the job**, driving with an experienced instructor on quiet routes.
- The ticket machines' supplier offers a one-day course; sending conductors **off the job** gives expert training before the machines go into service, so no fares are lost through mistakes.
- Any newly recruited drivers receive **induction** on company rules, routes and safety first.
:::

## Recommending a type of training

Consider:

1. **Who needs training?** New staff always need induction.
2. **What skill?** Simple, practical, business-specific skills suit on-the-job training; complex, technical or managerial skills and qualifications suit off-the-job training.
3. **Cost and budget**: small businesses may only afford on-the-job training.
4. **Risk**: if mistakes would be dangerous or costly (with machinery, money or customers), learn off the job first.
5. **Risk of losing trained staff**: the business may ask staff to stay for a period after an expensive course.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying induction is for existing staff"}
Induction is only for **new** employees. Existing staff receive on-the-job or off-the-job training.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all off-the-job training external"}
Off-the-job training can happen inside the business, for example in its own training centre, as long as it is away from the normal job.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the cost of lost output"}
On-the-job training seems free, but the trainer's output falls and mistakes cost money. Include these hidden costs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend a method"}
State the type, give two reasons from the case (the skill needed, the cost, the risk of mistakes), recognise one drawback and explain why it matters less here, and conclude.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Training improves employees' knowledge and skills.
- It raises productivity, quality and service, reduces accidents and labour turnover, and helps promotion.
- Induction introduces new employees to the business, its rules and safety procedures.
- On-the-job training is cheap and specific but can pass on bad habits and cause costly mistakes.
- Off-the-job training uses experts and gives qualifications but is costly and trained staff may leave.
:::
