## Introduction

A single nurse who asks her hospital for better pay or safer shifts may be ignored. If thousands of nurses ask together, through an organisation that speaks for all of them, employers and the government must listen. That organisation is a **trade union**. Unions represent workers in many sectors in Zimbabwe, including mining, agriculture, transport, teaching and health.

Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.2.5) asks what a trade union is and the benefits to employees of membership. Questions may also ask you to analyse how a union affects a business, for example in negotiations over pay or redundancies.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade union"}
An organisation of workers that acts on their behalf to protect and improve their pay, working conditions and job security.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective bargaining"}
Negotiation between a trade union, acting for all its members, and an employer (or employers' organisation) over pay and working conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Industrial action"}
Action taken by workers to put pressure on an employer during a dispute, such as a strike, a go-slow or an overtime ban.
:::

:::definition{term="Shop steward"}
An employee elected by union members in a workplace to represent them in dealings with managers.
:::

## What trade unions do

- **Negotiate pay**: bargain for higher wages and better benefits.
- **Improve working conditions**: hours, holidays, rest breaks, health and safety.
- **Protect job security**: negotiate over redundancies, redundancy payments and fair procedures.
- **Represent individual members**: support a member at a disciplinary or grievance hearing.
- **Provide services**: legal advice, training, education and sometimes financial help.
- **Influence government**: speak for workers on laws about employment, minimum wages and safety.

## Benefits to employees of union membership

| Benefit | Why it matters to the employee |
| --- | --- |
| Strength in numbers | One worker has little bargaining power; a union speaking for many can win higher pay and better conditions |
| Support in disputes | A union official or shop steward helps the member at hearings, so the member is treated fairly |
| Legal advice | Members get help if dismissed unfairly or injured at work |
| Safer workplaces | Unions press for safety equipment and training |
| Job security | Unions negotiate over redundancies and push for alternatives and fair payments |
| Training and benefits | Courses, information and sometimes discounts or welfare funds |

:::example{title="Collective bargaining at a sugar estate"}
Workers at a fictional sugar estate in the Lowveld want a pay rise and better transport to the fields. Their union meets the estate's managers. The union asks for a 12% rise; the managers offer 4%. After several meetings they agree a 7% rise and new buses for the morning shift. No individual worker could have won this alone.
:::

### How a negotiation usually proceeds

1. Members tell their shop steward or union officials what they want, for example a pay rise or safer equipment.
2. The union presents a **claim** to the employer, often supported by evidence such as rising living costs or accident records.
3. The employer replies with an **offer**, usually lower than the claim, explaining what it can afford.
4. Both sides meet, sometimes several times, and move towards each other. Each side gives something up: the union may accept a smaller rise in return for better conditions, and the employer may agree to changes in return for more flexible working.
5. If agreement is reached, it is written down and applies to all the workers covered.
6. If talks fail, the two sides may ask an independent third party to help (conciliation or arbitration), and only as a last resort does the union consider industrial action.

:::example{title="A union and new technology"}
A fictional printing company in Harare wants to replace old presses with digital machines that need fewer operators. Rather than simply opposing the change, the union negotiates: no compulsory redundancies for a year, retraining for operators who want to learn the new machines, and voluntary redundancy with a fair payment for those who prefer to leave. The company gets its new machines with the cooperation of its workers, and members keep as much job security as possible. This shows that unions can help, not only hinder, change.
:::

### Drawbacks of membership to employees

- Members pay a **subscription** (membership fee), often deducted from wages.
- During **industrial action** such as a strike, members may lose pay.
- Members are expected to follow the union's decisions, even if they disagree.
- Strikes can harm the business and, in the long run, threaten jobs.

## Effects of trade unions on a business

**Possible benefits:**
- Managers negotiate with one body instead of hundreds of individual workers, which saves time.
- Unions communicate the workers' views clearly and can help introduce change, for example agreeing new working methods in return for training.
- Better conditions may improve motivation and reduce labour turnover.

**Possible drawbacks:**
- Higher wages raise costs.
- Industrial action stops or slows production, so orders may be lost.
- Changes such as new technology or redundancies may be resisted or delayed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a trade union with a trade association"}
A **trade union** represents employees. A **trade association** represents businesses in the same industry (for example, retailers or manufacturers).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying unions only organise strikes"}
Most union work is negotiation, advice and representation. Strikes are a last resort.
:::

:::mistake{title="Only giving benefits"}
If a question asks you to discuss membership, include the subscription, lost pay during strikes and the duty to follow union decisions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A trade union is an organisation of workers that protects and improves their pay, conditions and job security.
- Collective bargaining gives workers strength in numbers.
- Benefits to members: higher pay, better conditions, representation, legal advice, safety and job security.
- Drawbacks: subscriptions, lost pay during strikes, following majority decisions.
- For businesses, unions simplify negotiation but can raise costs and resist change.
:::
