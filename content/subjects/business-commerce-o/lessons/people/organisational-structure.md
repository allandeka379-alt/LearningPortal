## Introduction

When Tendai started making school uniforms in Harare, she did everything herself: buying fabric, sewing, selling and keeping the books. Ten years later her business employs sixty people. Workers now need to know who their supervisor is, who makes which decisions and which department deals with customers' complaints. Tendai has had to create an **organisational structure**.

Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) and Cambridge 0264 (topic 2.2.1) both expect you to explain the parts of a structure, read an organisation chart, compare tall and flat structures and comment on flexible working. Questions often give a chart and ask you to count levels, identify a manager's span of control or explain the effect of removing a layer of management.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organisational structure"}
The way the jobs, departments and lines of authority in a business are arranged, usually shown in an organisation chart.
:::

:::definition{term="Hierarchy"}
The levels of authority in a business, from the most senior managers at the top to the workers at the bottom.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of command"}
The line through which instructions pass down the hierarchy and information passes up it.
:::

:::definition{term="Span of control"}
The number of subordinates who report directly to one manager or supervisor.
:::

:::definition{term="Tall structure"}
A structure with many levels of hierarchy and narrow spans of control.
:::

:::definition{term="Flat structure"}
A structure with few levels of hierarchy and wide spans of control.
:::

## Functional departments

Most medium and large businesses group jobs into **functional areas**:

| Department | Main tasks |
| --- | --- |
| Operations (production) | Producing goods or services, quality control, buying materials |
| Marketing | Market research, pricing, promotion, sales and distribution |
| Finance | Recording transactions, paying wages and suppliers, budgets, raising finance |
| Human resources | Recruitment, training, pay, welfare and discipline of employees |

Each department has a manager who reports to the managing director, so specialists can concentrate on what they do best.

## Reading an organisation chart

Charts are drawn as boxes and lines; here one is written as an indented list. Each person reports to the person one level above.

**Chart A: Tendai's Uniforms (part of the business)**

- Managing director
  - Operations manager
    - Cutting supervisor → 8 machinists
    - Sewing supervisor → 8 machinists
    - Finishing supervisor → 8 machinists
  - Marketing manager
  - Finance manager
  - Human resources manager

:::worked-solution[Reading Chart A]
1. Levels of hierarchy: managing director, managers, supervisors, machinists = **4 levels**.
2. Span of control of the managing director: four managers report to her, so the span is **4**.
3. Span of control of the operations manager: three supervisors, so **3**.
4. Span of control of each supervisor: **8** machinists.
5. Chain of command for a cutting machinist: machinist → cutting supervisor → operations manager → managing director.
:::

:::worked-solution[Effect of removing a level]
1. Tendai removes the three supervisor posts. The 24 machinists now report directly to the operations manager.
2. Levels of hierarchy fall from 4 to **3**.
3. The operations manager's span of control rises from 3 to **24**.
4. The structure is now flatter: messages pass through fewer people, but the operations manager may struggle to supervise 24 people.
:::

## Tall and flat structures

| | Tall structure | Flat structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levels of hierarchy | Many | Few |
| Span of control | Narrow | Wide |
| Communication | Slow; messages may be distorted as they pass many levels | Faster and more accurate between top and bottom |
| Supervision | Close supervision of each worker | Less supervision; workers need to be trusted |
| Promotion | Many steps, so more chances of promotion | Fewer posts to be promoted to |
| Cost | More managers, so higher salary costs | Fewer managers, lower costs |
| Motivation | Workers may feel far from decision-makers | Workers given more responsibility, which may motivate them |

:::example{title="Delayering at a bank"}
A commercial bank decides to remove the assistant branch manager level in all its branches. Its structure becomes flatter. It saves salaries, and branch staff report directly to the branch manager, so decisions are faster. However, each branch manager now supervises many more people, and some staff lose a chance of promotion.
:::

### Wide or narrow span: which is better?

A **narrow span** suits work that needs close supervision, such as new or unskilled employees or dangerous tasks. A **wide span** suits skilled, trusted employees doing similar work, and it is cheaper. There is no single right answer: in an exam, relate your choice to the type of work and the skill of the employees.

## Flexible working, part-time and full-time employees

- **Full-time employees** work the business's normal week (for example 40 or more hours). They are usually more committed and easier to train and supervise, but cost more.
- **Part-time employees** work fewer hours. The business can match staff to busy periods (Saturday mornings in a supermarket) and pays less in total, but part-time staff may be less committed and harder to communicate with.
- **Flexible hours** let employees choose start and finish times around core hours. This helps parents and students and can reduce lateness, but makes meetings harder to arrange.
- **Home working** (remote working) lets employees work from home using phones and the internet. It saves office space and travel time, but managers find it harder to supervise and some workers feel isolated.

:::example{title="A supermarket in Mutare"}
The supermarket is busiest at month-end and on Saturdays. It keeps a core of full-time staff and employs part-time cashiers for busy periods. Its accounts clerk works from home two days a week. This lowers wage costs and keeps skilled staff, but the manager must plan rosters carefully.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting the span of control wrongly"}
Count only the people who report **directly** to the manager, not everyone below them. A managing director with four managers has a span of 4, even if 200 workers are below the managers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a flat structure has a narrow span"}
Flat structures have few levels and **wide** spans of control. Tall structures have many levels and **narrow** spans.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the chain of command one-way"}
Instructions travel down the chain of command, but information and feedback travel up it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Using organisation charts"}
Before answering, count the levels and the spans carefully from the organisation chart in the question. When asked about the effect of a change in structure, say what happens to levels, spans, communication, cost and motivation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) asks you to describe organisational structures of enterprises. Cambridge 0264 expects you to interpret simple charts, explain the role of each department and analyse the effect of a change in structure; flexible working and part-time employees are also examined in Cambridge.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An organisational structure sets out jobs, departments and lines of authority.
- Common departments are operations, marketing, finance and human resources.
- Hierarchy is the levels of authority; the chain of command carries instructions down and information up.
- Span of control is the number of direct subordinates of one manager.
- Tall structures: many levels, narrow spans. Flat structures: few levels, wide spans.
- Flexible hours, home working and part-time staff help match labour to needs but are harder to supervise.
:::
