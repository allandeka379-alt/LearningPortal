## Introduction

When Nyasha's sole-trader bakery in Bulawayo grew to three shops, she needed more capital than she could borrow, and she worried that a failed expansion could cost her the family home. Many growing businesses reach this point. The usual answer is to form a **limited company**: an **incorporated** business owned by **shareholders**, with **limited liability**.

Limited companies range from family firms with a handful of shareholders to large businesses whose shares are traded on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange. Examiners ask you to describe their features, explain who owns and who controls them, compare private and public companies, weigh their advantages and disadvantages, and recommend a suitable type of organisation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Limited company"}
A business that is incorporated (registered as a company), so it has a separate legal identity from its owners, who are shareholders with limited liability.
:::

:::definition{term="Shareholder"}
A person or organisation that owns one or more shares in a company and is therefore a part-owner. Shareholders may receive a dividend and can vote at the annual general meeting.
:::

:::definition{term="Director"}
A person elected by the shareholders to the board of directors, which controls the company and appoints managers to run it day to day.
:::

:::definition{term="Limited liability"}
Shareholders can lose only the money they have invested in the company's shares; their personal possessions are safe if the company cannot pay its debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Dividend"}
The part of a company's profit paid out to shareholders, usually as an amount per share.
:::

## Features of all limited companies

- **Separate legal personality:** the company owns property, signs contracts, sues and is sued in its own name.
- **Limited liability** for shareholders.
- **Continuity:** the company continues even if shareholders die or sell their shares.
- **Ownership** by shareholders; **control** by a board of directors elected at the annual general meeting (AGM).
- Must be **registered** with the Registrar of Companies, with formation documents (see the lesson on formation and liability).
- Must keep proper accounts, have them audited and send returns to the Registrar.

### Ownership and control

In a sole trader the owner is also the manager. In a company, ownership and control can be **separated**:

- **Shareholders own** the company. Usually one share gives one vote, so whoever holds more than half the voting shares can elect the board.
- **Directors control** it. They set policy and appoint managers.
- **Managers run** it day to day.

In a small private company the same family members may be shareholders, directors and managers. In a large public company thousands of shareholders own it, but few take part; the directors and managers may pursue their own aims, such as growth, rather than the highest dividends.

:::worked-solution{title="Shares, control and dividends"}
Mopane Feeds (Private) Limited has 50,000 shares. Nyasha owns 20,000 of them. The directors pay a total dividend of US\$7,500.

1. Nyasha's share of the company = 20,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = **40%**. She does not have more than half the votes, so she cannot elect the board on her own.
2. Dividend per share = US\$7,500 ÷ 50,000 = **US\$0.15**.
3. Nyasha's dividend = 20,000 × US\$0.15 = **US\$3,000** (40% of US\$7,500).
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Limited liability in figures"}
Thandeka bought 2,000 shares at US\$1.50 each in a company that later fails, owing creditors US\$40,000 more than its assets.

1. Thandeka's investment = 2,000 × US\$1.50 = **US\$3,000**.
2. With limited liability, the most she can lose is this **US\$3,000**.
3. The creditors cannot claim the US\$40,000 shortfall from her house or savings: the debts belong to the company, not to its shareholders.
:::

## Private and public limited companies compared

| Feature | Private limited company | Public limited company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name ends with | (Private) Limited, (Pvt) Ltd | Limited, Ltd (and is a public company) |
| Selling shares | Shares **cannot** be offered to the general public; sold privately, often to family and friends | Shares **can** be offered to the public through a prospectus and may be listed on a stock exchange |
| Transfer of shares | Restricted: usually needs the other shareholders' approval | Freely bought and sold, for example on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange |
| Number of shareholders | Limited by law; often a family | Can be very large |
| Capital | Medium | Can raise very large amounts |
| Accounts | Filed with the Registrar but less widely seen | Published for all shareholders and the public |
| Can start trading | On incorporation | Needs a trading certificate (certificate to commence business) in ZIMSEC Commerce |
| Control | Owners often keep control | Risk of takeover if others buy enough shares |

## Advantages and disadvantages

### Private limited company

**Advantages**

- Limited liability encourages owners to invest.
- More capital than a partnership, from more shareholders.
- Continuity; easier to borrow, as banks deal with a legal entity.
- Owners keep control because shares are not sold to the public.

**Disadvantages**

- Legal formalities and costs of formation.
- Cannot raise capital from the general public.
- Shares are hard to sell, so investors may be reluctant.
- Accounts are less private than a sole trader's.

### Public limited company

**Advantages**

- Can raise very large amounts of capital by selling shares to the public.
- Shares are easy to sell, which attracts investors.
- Size brings economies of scale and easier borrowing.
- High public profile.

**Disadvantages**

- Expensive and complex to form and run (prospectus, audits, published accounts).
- **Separation of ownership and control:** shareholders may have little say.
- Risk of **takeover** because anyone can buy the shares.
- Size can make decisions slow and staff relations impersonal.
- Must disclose information to competitors through published accounts.

## Recommending a type of organisation

Ask: How much capital is needed? How risky is the business? Do the owners want to keep control? How much do formalities matter?

:::example{title="Recommending for Sipho"}
Sipho runs a successful printing business as a sole trader in Mutare. He needs US\$60,000 for new presses. Three relatives will invest but want limited liability. A **private limited company** is suitable: it brings in the relatives' capital, gives everyone limited liability, and keeps control in the family because shares are not sold to the public. A public limited company would be far too costly to form for this amount, and Sipho would risk losing control.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the public sector and a public limited company"}
A public limited company is privately owned by its shareholders: it is in the private sector.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying shareholders run the company"}
Shareholders **own** it and elect directors; directors **control** it; managers **run** it day to day.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying limited liability means the company's debts are limited"}
The **company** must pay all its debts as far as its assets allow. It is the **shareholders'** liability that is limited to their investment.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Distinguish clearly"}
In "distinguish between" questions, write matching pairs: "A private company cannot sell shares to the public, whereas a public company can offer shares to anyone."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Recommendations earn the top marks"}
Use evidence from the case (capital needed, risk, control) and say why the other option is less suitable.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) covers formation, ownership, control and the similarities and differences of incorporated units. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 2) covers the features, benefits and limitations of incorporated enterprises. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.4) requires the main features, limited liability and a recommendation for a given situation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Limited companies are incorporated: a separate legal identity, limited liability for shareholders and continuity.
- Shareholders own the company, directors control it and managers run it.
- Private companies cannot sell shares to the public; public companies can and may be listed on a stock exchange.
- Public companies raise more capital but face high formation costs, published accounts, takeover risk and separation of ownership and control.
- Recommend a type by weighing capital needs, risk, control and formalities.
:::
