## Introduction

Walk down any street in Gweru and you pass many kinds of business organisation: a vendor selling vegetables from a table, a hair salon run by two friends, a branch of a commercial bank, a city council office collecting rates, and a bus belonging to a state-owned transport company. They differ in size and in who owns them, but every one of them exists to provide goods or services that people want.

Examiners start the topic of business organisations with two questions: **why do business organisations exist?** and **how can we classify them?** You must be able to explain their purposes and sort any business into the **private** or **public** sector and into the **formal** or **informal** sector, with a reason. This lesson builds the foundation for the later lessons on sole traders, partnerships, companies and public enterprises.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business organisation (business unit)"}
Any organisation that combines resources (land, labour, capital and enterprise) to produce goods or services for others. ZIMSEC calls it a business unit or business enterprise.
:::

:::definition{term="Private sector"}
The part of the economy owned and controlled by private individuals or groups of individuals, usually with the aim of making a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Public sector"}
The part of the economy owned and controlled by the government (central or local) on behalf of the people, usually to provide essential services.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal sector"}
Businesses that are registered with the authorities, pay taxes, keep records and follow employment laws.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Businesses that are not registered or only partly registered, often small, run from homes, streets or markets, and outside many tax and employment rules.
:::

## Why business organisations exist

A business organisation brings together the factors of production and turns them into goods and services. In doing so it serves several purposes at once.

1. **Satisfying needs and wants.** People need food, clothing and shelter, and want many other goods and services. Businesses produce them and make them available where and when people want them.
2. **Creating employment.** Businesses hire workers, so people earn wages and can support their families. Even a small market stall can employ the owner and one helper.
3. **Generating income and profit.** Owners earn profit as a reward for taking risks; workers earn wages; landlords earn rent.
4. **Paying taxes.** Formal businesses pay taxes to ZIMRA, which the government uses for schools, clinics and roads.
5. **Developing infrastructure.** Large businesses may build roads, bridges, housing and water supplies near mines, estates and factories, which local communities also use.
6. **Economic development.** Businesses add value to raw materials, earn foreign currency through exports and introduce new skills and technology.
7. **Social responsibility.** Some businesses support schools, clinics or sports clubs in their community.

:::example{title="One business, many purposes"}
Nyasha opens a small furniture workshop in Kwekwe. She **satisfies wants** (beds and tables), **creates jobs** for two carpenters, **earns profit**, pays **tax** once registered, and buys timber from local suppliers, which keeps money circulating in the town.
:::

## The private sector and the public sector

The key test is **ownership**: who owns the business and who controls it?

| Feature | Private sector | Public sector |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owners | Private individuals or shareholders | The state (central government) or a local authority, on behalf of citizens |
| Main aim | Usually profit | Usually providing an essential service; may also aim to break even or make a profit |
| Source of capital | Owners' savings, partners, shares, loans | Government funds (taxes), loans, charges for services |
| Control | Owners or directors elected by shareholders | Ministers, boards appointed by government, or elected councillors |
| Who receives profit | Owners | The government / used for the service |

### Business units in the private sector

- **Sole traders** (sole proprietorships)
- **Partnerships**
- **Private limited companies** and **public limited companies**
- **Co-operatives**, **franchises** and **multinational companies**

### Business units in the public sector

- **Government departments** (run directly by a ministry)
- **Public corporations** (parastatals): state-owned businesses with their own board, such as a national railway or electricity supplier
- **Local authorities**: city, town and rural district councils that provide water, refuse collection and markets
- **Marketing boards**: public bodies that buy and market particular crops, such as the Grain Marketing Board for grain

:::example{title="Classifying business units"}
- A tuck shop owned by Tendai: **private** sector, because it is owned by an individual.
- A public limited company listed on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange: **private** sector. "Public" refers to who can buy its shares, not who owns it.
- A city council's water department: **public** sector, owned by a local authority.
- A state-owned railway company: **public** sector, owned by the government.
:::

## The formal and informal sectors

A second way to classify businesses is by whether they are **registered and regulated**.

| Formal sector | Informal sector |
| --- | --- |
| Registered with the authorities and licensed | Often unregistered or only partly licensed |
| Pays taxes and keeps proper records | Pays little or no tax; few written records |
| Workers have contracts, set hours and benefits | Usually self-employment or family labour; little job security |
| Fixed premises such as offices, shops or factories | Streets, markets, homes, home-industry sites |
| Easier access to bank loans | Hard to borrow from banks; relies on savings, family and informal lenders |

In Zimbabwe the informal sector is very large. It includes vendors at Mbare Musika in Harare and Sakubva market in Mutare, cross-border traders, carpenters and welders at home-industry sites, and people selling airtime or offering mobile money services.

### Why the informal sector matters

- It provides a living for many people who cannot find formal jobs.
- It supplies cheap goods and services in small quantities, close to where people live.
- It develops enterprise skills; some informal businesses grow and become formal.

However, the government loses tax revenue, working conditions can be poor and unsafe, and informal businesses find it hard to raise finance or grow. Governments therefore encourage informal businesses to register (**formalise**), for example by simplifying registration and offering workspace.

:::note{title="Two classifications, not one"}
Private/public is about **ownership**. Formal/informal is about **registration and regulation**. Primary/secondary/tertiary (studied separately) is about the **type of activity**. One business can be described in all three ways: an unregistered vegetable vendor is private, informal and tertiary.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a public limited company is in the public sector"}
A public limited company (plc) is owned by private shareholders, so it is in the **private** sector. "Public" means its shares can be sold to the general public.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying public sector organisations never make a profit"}
Many public corporations charge for their services and aim to cover costs or make a surplus. Their **main** aim is the service, but profit is not forbidden.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing purposes without explaining them"}
"Employment" alone earns one mark at most. Explain the effect: "it employs workers, who earn wages to support their families and spend in the local economy".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Classify with a reason"}
When asked to classify, name the sector **and** the deciding fact: "public sector, because it is owned by a local authority on behalf of residents".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the case"}
In "explain the importance of this business to the community" questions, link each purpose to details in the case: the jobs, the product, the road it built.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 1) asks you to describe the private and public sectors and classify business units. Business Enterprise and Skills stresses the purposes of business enterprises, including employment creation and infrastructure development. Cambridge 0264 places private and public sector organisations within its topic on types of economic sector.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business organisations combine resources to satisfy needs and wants, create jobs, generate income and taxes, and help develop the economy and infrastructure.
- The private sector is owned by individuals or shareholders and usually aims for profit; the public sector is owned by central or local government and usually aims to provide services.
- A public limited company belongs to the private sector.
- The formal sector is registered, taxed and regulated; the informal sector is largely unregistered and very important for jobs in Zimbabwe.
- Always classify with a reason based on ownership or registration.
:::
