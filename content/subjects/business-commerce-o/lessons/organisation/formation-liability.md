## Introduction

Farai and three friends want to turn their poultry project near Chinhoyi into a company so that they can borrow from a bank and protect their personal savings. Before the company exists in law, they must complete a set of **legal formalities**: prepare formation documents, register them and receive a certificate from the Registrar of Companies.

This lesson explains two ideas that make a company different from a sole trader or partnership, **corporate legal personality** and **limited liability**, and then the documents used to form a company. Both ZIMSEC syllabuses examine them: Commerce lists the documents by name, and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) adds the risks of forming a new enterprise.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Corporate (separate) legal personality"}
A company is treated in law as a person separate from its owners: it can own property, make contracts, employ people, sue and be sued in its own name.
:::

:::definition{term="Limited liability"}
Shareholders are liable for the company's debts only up to the amount they invested (or agreed to pay) for their shares.
:::

:::definition{term="Incorporation"}
The legal process of registering a business as a company, which creates the new legal person.
:::

:::definition{term="Memorandum of association"}
The document that sets out the company's relationship with the outside world: its name, registered office, objects and liability.
:::

:::definition{term="Articles of association"}
The document of internal rules that governs how the company is run.
:::

## Corporate legal personality

Once incorporated, the company is a separate person. This has several consequences:

- The company **owns its assets**. If a shareholder dies, the assets stay with the company.
- The company **signs contracts** and **employs workers** in its own name.
- **Creditors sue the company**, not the shareholders.
- The company has **perpetual succession** (continuity): it continues until it is formally wound up, however often its shares change hands.

A sole trader or partnership has no separate personality: the owner **is** the business.

## Limited liability and why it matters

With limited liability a shareholder's maximum loss is the money paid for the shares. It matters for several reasons:

1. **For investors:** people are more willing to buy shares when they know their houses and savings are safe, so companies can raise large amounts of capital.
2. **For enterprise:** entrepreneurs can take reasonable risks with new ideas without risking ruin, which encourages new businesses and innovation.
3. **For the stock exchange:** shares can be bought and sold freely, because a buyer does not take on unknown personal liabilities.
4. **For growth:** with more capital, companies can expand and create jobs.

There are drawbacks. **Creditors bear more risk**: if a company fails, suppliers and lenders may not be paid in full. For this reason banks often ask the directors of small companies for **personal guarantees**, which removes the protection for that loan. Companies must also publish information so that creditors can judge the risk, and the name must show "Limited" as a warning.

:::example{title="Who loses when a company fails?"}
Chiedza Poultry (Private) Limited fails owing a feed supplier US\$9,000 and has assets of only US\$5,000. The supplier receives part of the debt from the company's assets. The shareholders lose their investment, but the supplier cannot take their personal property. The supplier bears the loss, which is why suppliers check a company's credit record before giving credit.
:::

## Risks of forming a new enterprise

Whatever form is chosen, starting a business is risky. Business Enterprise and Skills expects you to identify these risks:

- **Financial risk:** losing the capital invested; debts the business cannot repay.
- **Market risk:** too few customers; competitors cut prices; tastes change.
- **Operational risk:** breakdowns, theft, fire, unreliable suppliers.
- **Legal risk:** fines for trading without the correct licences or for breaking regulations.
- **Personal risk:** stress, long hours and loss of a steady wage.

Choosing an incorporated form reduces the **personal** financial risk, but not the risk that the business itself fails.

## Documents for forming a company

Company law sets out the exact procedure, and the names of some documents can change when the law is updated. Learn the documents named in your syllabus:

| Document | Prepared or issued by | Purpose and main contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Memorandum of association** | Promoters (founders) | The company's "external constitution": name (ending in Limited or (Private) Limited), registered office address, objects (what the company will do), a statement that liability is limited, the share capital and how it is divided, and the signatures of the first members |
| **Articles of association** | Promoters | The internal rules: rights of each class of share, how shares are issued and transferred, how directors are appointed and removed and their powers, how meetings are called and votes taken, how profits are distributed, how accounts are kept |
| **Certificate of incorporation** | Registrar of Companies | Confirms the company has been registered and exists as a legal person; a private company can then begin trading |
| **Trading certificate** (certificate to commence business) | Registrar of Companies | Allows a **public** company to start trading, once it shows the minimum capital has been raised |
| **Prospectus** | A public company (directors) | An invitation to the public to buy shares: the company's history, directors, plans, past and forecast profits, number and price of shares offered, and how to apply |

### Steps in forming a company

1. The **promoters** agree to form the company and choose a name, checking with the Registrar that it is available.
2. They prepare the **memorandum** and **articles of association** and other required forms.
3. The documents are lodged with the **Registrar of Companies** with the registration fee.
4. If everything is in order, the Registrar issues a **certificate of incorporation**: the company now exists.
5. A **private** company can start trading. A **public** company issues a **prospectus**, raises the minimum capital and obtains a **trading certificate** before it can trade.

:::example{title="Reading the memorandum and the articles"}
Farai's group writes "to rear and sell broiler chickens and to process poultry products" as the **objects** in the memorandum. In the articles they agree that each share has one vote, that shares may be transferred only with the board's approval, and that the board will have three directors elected each year at the AGM.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Swapping the memorandum and the articles"}
The **memorandum** faces outwards (name, objects, liability, capital). The **articles** face inwards (meetings, voting, directors, share transfers).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying every company needs a prospectus"}
Only a company inviting the **public** to buy shares issues a prospectus. A private company cannot.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying limited liability removes all risk"}
The shareholder can still lose all the money invested, and the business can still fail.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Match document to purpose"}
Questions often give a purpose and ask for the document ("Which document invites the public to buy shares?"). Learn one key purpose for each.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate limited liability from two sides"}
"Evaluate the importance of limited liability" needs benefits to investors and the economy, the cost to creditors, and a conclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) lists the memorandum, articles, certificate of incorporation and trading certificate, and the prospectus in its finance work. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) adds the risks of forming an enterprise and corporate legal personality. Cambridge 0264 does not examine company formation documents.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A company has corporate legal personality: it owns assets, makes contracts and is sued in its own name, and has continuity.
- Limited liability caps a shareholder's loss at the investment, encouraging investment and enterprise but shifting risk to creditors.
- Forming a business carries financial, market, operational, legal and personal risks.
- The memorandum (external) and articles (internal rules) are lodged with the Registrar, who issues a certificate of incorporation.
- A public company also issues a prospectus and needs a trading certificate before trading.
:::
