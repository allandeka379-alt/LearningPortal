## Introduction

When you switch on a light, catch a train or pay your water bill, you are probably dealing with a **public enterprise**: a business unit owned by the state or by a local authority. In Zimbabwe these include state-owned corporations providing electricity, railways and water, city and town councils, rural district councils and marketing boards such as the Grain Marketing Board.

Public enterprises are part of the **public sector**. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) both expect you to describe their features, explain why they are formed, and weigh up their merits and demerits. This lesson covers the four main types.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Public enterprise"}
A business unit owned and controlled by central government or a local authority on behalf of the public.
:::

:::definition{term="Public corporation (parastatal)"}
A state-owned business set up by an Act of Parliament, with its own legal identity and a board appointed by the responsible minister.
:::

:::definition{term="Local authority"}
An elected council (city, town, municipal or rural district council) that provides services in its area and is financed by rates, charges and government grants.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing board"}
A public body set up to buy, store and market particular agricultural products, often at prices announced in advance.
:::

:::definition{term="Government department"}
A unit run directly by a government ministry and financed from the national budget, such as an office that issues identity documents.
:::

## Why governments form public enterprises

1. **Essential services:** water, electricity and railways are too important to be left to private firms that may not serve poorer or remote areas.
2. **Natural monopolies:** it would be wasteful to have several companies laying water pipes or railway lines side by side.
3. **Strategic industries:** the state may want control of energy, transport or communications for national security.
4. **Very large capital:** some projects (dams, power stations) need more capital than private businesses can raise, with long payback periods.
5. **Protecting consumers:** to prevent private monopolies from charging very high prices.
6. **Social aims:** jobs, development of rural areas, food security and stable producer prices.

## Public corporations (parastatals)

### Features

- **Ownership:** the state, on behalf of the people. There are no private shareholders.
- **Formation:** by an Act of Parliament, which sets out its functions and powers.
- **Control:** a **board of directors** appointed by the responsible **minister**; managers run it day to day. The minister sets general policy; the board handles daily decisions.
- **Separate legal personality:** it can sue and be sued, own property and sign contracts.
- **Finance:** government grants and loans, charges to customers and borrowing.
- **Aim:** to provide a service at a reasonable price; it may aim to break even or make a surplus, which goes back into the service or to the state.
- **Accountability:** reports and accounts presented to the minister and to Parliament.

### Merits

- Provides essential services across the whole country, including unprofitable areas.
- Prices can be kept affordable.
- Can raise the very large capital needed for national projects.
- Creates employment and supports development.
- Profits, if any, benefit the public rather than a few owners.

### Demerits

- **Inefficiency:** there may be little competition and no profit motive.
- **Political interference** in appointments, pricing and investment.
- Losses must be covered by **taxpayers** through subsidies.
- Slow decision-making and bureaucracy (red tape).
- Large size may lead to poor service and weak responses to complaints.

## Local authorities

City, town and rural district councils are run by **elected councillors**, with a town clerk or chief executive and officials who manage daily work. They provide **water and sewerage, refuse collection, roads and street lighting, markets and bus termini, clinics, housing stands, parks and fire services**. They also issue shop and vendor licences.

They are financed by **rates** on property, **charges** (water bills, market fees, licence fees), **government grants** and loans.

:::example{title="A local authority as a business unit"}
A town council in Masvingo builds market stalls and rents them to vendors for a monthly fee. The fees cover cleaning and security at the market, and any surplus helps pay for road repairs. The council aims to provide a service to residents rather than maximise profit.
:::

## Marketing boards

A marketing board buys certain products from farmers, stores, grades and sells them at home or abroad. The syllabus example is the **Grain Marketing Board**, which buys grain such as maize.

**Functions:** buying from farmers; grading; storing; transporting; selling locally and exporting; announcing producer prices; keeping strategic reserves for food security; sometimes giving farmers inputs or advice.

**Merits:** a guaranteed market and stable prices for farmers, food security through reserves, orderly marketing and grading standards.

**Demerits:** late payments to farmers, prices set below market levels, inefficiency and political influence.

## Government departments

These are run directly by a ministry, financed by the national budget, and have **no separate legal identity** or independent board. Examples include an office that issues passports and birth certificates, or a department that runs government schools. They are controlled by civil servants who report to the minister.

| Feature | Government department | Public corporation | Local authority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Controlled by | Ministry (civil servants) | Board appointed by a minister | Elected council |
| Legal identity | None separate from government | Separate | Separate |
| Main finance | National budget | Charges, grants, loans | Rates, charges, grants |
| Area served | National | National | Local |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a public limited company a public enterprise"}
A public limited company is owned by private shareholders. A public enterprise is owned by the state or a local authority.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ministers run parastatals day to day"}
The minister sets policy and appoints the board; the **board and managers** run the corporation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing merits without explaining"}
Explain who benefits and how: "prices are kept low so poorer households can afford electricity", not just "cheap".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use ownership, control, finance and aim"}
To describe any public enterprise, write one point on each: who owns it, who controls it, how it is financed and what it aims to do.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss, then conclude"}
"Discuss the merits and demerits of parastatals" needs both sides developed and a judgement, for example that they are justified for essential services if well managed.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce examines parastatals, local authorities and marketing boards and the reasons for forming public organisations. Business Enterprise and Skills studies public corporations alongside joint ventures and franchises as forms of enterprise. Cambridge 0264 does not examine them in this detail.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Public enterprises are owned by central government or local authorities on behalf of the public.
- Parastatals are formed by Acts of Parliament, controlled by boards appointed by ministers and have separate legal personality.
- Local authorities are elected councils financed by rates, charges and grants; marketing boards buy and market farm products.
- Merits: essential services for all, affordable prices, large capital, jobs; demerits: inefficiency, political interference, losses borne by taxpayers, red tape.
- Describe any public enterprise by its ownership, control, finance and aim.
:::
