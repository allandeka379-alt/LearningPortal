## Introduction

Most businesses in Zimbabwe are small and owned by one person or a few people: the tuck shop at the bus stop, the welding workshop at a home-industry site, the hair salon in a shopping centre, a firm of lawyers or accountants in Bulawayo. These are **unincorporated** businesses: in law, the business and its owners are the same.

Every board examines the two main unincorporated forms, the **sole trader** (sole proprietorship) and the **partnership**. You need their features, how they are set up, who owns and controls them, their advantages and disadvantages, and, most importantly, how to **recommend** a form of organisation for particular owners.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sole trader (sole proprietorship)"}
A business owned and controlled by one person, who takes all the profit and bears all the risk. The owner may employ other people.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership"}
A business owned by two or more people (commonly two to twenty) who agree to share the capital, the work, the decisions and the profits or losses.
:::

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business. If the business cannot pay, the owners' personal possessions can be taken to pay its debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Partnership deed (deed of partnership)"}
A written agreement between partners setting out how the partnership will be run.
:::

:::definition{term="Unincorporated business"}
A business that has no separate legal identity from its owners.
:::

## The sole trader

### Features

- Owned by **one person**, who provides the capital (usually from savings, family or a small loan).
- The owner **controls** the business and makes all decisions.
- The owner keeps **all the profit** and bears all losses.
- **Unlimited liability.**
- **No separate legal personality:** the owner sues and is sued personally.
- **Few legal formalities:** choose a name, obtain a trading licence from the local authority and register for tax where required.

### Advantages

- Easy and cheap to set up.
- The owner keeps all the profit, which is a strong motive to work hard.
- Quick decisions, as there is no one to consult.
- Personal contact with customers, so the owner knows what they want and can offer credit to trusted regulars.
- Accounts are private: they need not be published.

### Disadvantages

- **Unlimited liability**: personal assets are at risk.
- Limited capital, so it is hard to expand; banks may refuse loans because there is little security.
- The owner must do many jobs (buying, selling, records) and may lack some skills.
- Long hours; illness or holidays can close the business.
- **Lack of continuity:** the business may end when the owner dies or retires.

:::worked-solution{title="What unlimited liability means in figures"}
Tendai's hardware shop in Masvingo fails, owing suppliers and the bank US\$18,000. When the shop's stock and equipment are sold they raise US\$11,000.

1. Amount still owed = debts − business assets sold = US\$18,000 − US\$11,000 = **US\$7,000**.
2. Because Tendai has **unlimited liability**, the creditors can claim this US\$7,000 from his **personal assets**: savings, a car or even his house.
3. If the shop had been a limited company, its owners would lose only the money they put in.
:::

## The partnership

### Features

- Owned by **two or more partners** (commonly two to twenty; some professional firms may have more).
- Partners contribute capital and often skills.
- Partners usually share control; **each partner can bind the firm** in business deals.
- Profits and losses are shared as agreed in the deed, or **equally** if there is no agreement.
- **Unlimited liability** for the general partners, jointly for the debts of the firm.
- **No separate legal personality**; the partnership usually ends or must be re-formed if a partner dies, retires or goes bankrupt.

A **limited partnership** may have **limited (sleeping) partners** who provide capital but take no part in running the firm; their liability is limited to their capital. At least one partner must be a **general partner** with unlimited liability.

### The partnership deed

A partnership can be formed by a simple verbal agreement, but a written **deed** prevents disputes. It usually states:

- the name, address and purpose of the business;
- the capital each partner contributes;
- how profits and losses are shared (the **profit-sharing ratio**);
- partners' salaries, drawings and any interest on capital;
- the duties of each partner and how decisions are made;
- how a partner may join or leave, and how disputes are settled.

### Sharing profit

Partners share profit in the agreed ratio. If one partner works full time, the deed may give her a salary first and then share the rest.

:::worked-solution{title="Profit shared in the ratio of capital"}
Rudo invests US\$12,000 and Farai US\$8,000 in a printing partnership. The deed shares profit in the ratio of capital. Profit for the year is US\$15,000.

1. Ratio of capital = 12,000 : 8,000 = **3 : 2** (5 parts).
2. Rudo's share = 3/5 × US\$15,000 = **US\$9,000**.
3. Farai's share = 2/5 × US\$15,000 = **US\$6,000**.
4. Check: US\$9,000 + US\$6,000 = US\$15,000.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Salary first, then the ratio"}
Tatenda, Chipo and Sipho share profit 2 : 2 : 1 after Sipho, who manages the business full time, receives a salary of US\$3,000. Profit is US\$23,000.

1. Profit after the salary = US\$23,000 − US\$3,000 = US\$20,000 (5 parts of US\$4,000).
2. Tatenda 2 parts = US\$8,000; Chipo 2 parts = US\$8,000; Sipho 1 part = US\$4,000.
3. Sipho's total = US\$3,000 + US\$4,000 = **US\$7,000**.

| Partner | Salary US\$ | Share of remaining profit US\$ | Total US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Tatenda | 0 | 8,000 | 8,000 |
| Chipo | 0 | 8,000 | 8,000 |
| Sipho | 3,000 | 4,000 | 7,000 |
| **Total** | 3,000 | 20,000 | 23,000 |
:::

### Advantages of a partnership

- More capital than a sole trader, as each partner contributes.
- Shared skills and specialisation (one partner sells, another keeps the books).
- Shared workload, responsibility and losses; the business can run when one partner is away.
- Still easy to form, and accounts are private.

### Disadvantages of a partnership

- **Unlimited liability**, and a partner is liable for debts caused by the other partners' decisions.
- **Disagreements** between partners can slow decisions or end the business.
- Profits must be shared.
- Capital is still limited compared with a company.
- **Lack of continuity** when a partner dies or leaves.

## Recommending a form of organisation

Base your recommendation on the owners' situation:

| Question to ask | Points towards |
| --- | --- |
| Does the owner want full control and all the profit? | Sole trader |
| Is a lot of capital needed, or are different skills needed? | Partnership (or company) |
| Is the business risky, with large possible debts? | A form with limited liability (company) |
| Do the owners trust each other and agree on aims? | Partnership is workable |

:::example{title="A recommendation"}
Chipo, a qualified accountant, and Thandeka, who has worked in sales, want to open a bookkeeping service in Chinhoyi. Each has US\$3,000. A **partnership** suits them: they combine capital and complementary skills, the business is low-risk (little stock and few debts), so unlimited liability is less of a worry, and a deed can set out how they share profit and settle disputes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a sole trader works alone"}
A sole trader has **one owner** but may employ many workers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying profit is always shared equally"}
Profit is shared **as the deed says**. Only when there is no agreement is it shared equally.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending without the case"}
"A partnership is better because it has more capital" is generic. Use the owners' capital, skills, risk and wishes from the case, and conclude.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
"State" needs a feature only. "Explain" needs the feature plus why it helps or harms the owner. "Recommend" or "justify" needs a decision supported by evidence from the case and a reason the other option is weaker.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Show working"}
For profit-sharing calculations, write the ratio, the value of one part and each share, and check that the shares add up to the profit.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2) covers formation, ownership, control and distinguishing the two forms. Business Enterprise and Skills includes setting up a sole proprietorship or partnership. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.4) wants the main features and a recommendation for a given situation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A sole trader has one owner who controls the business, keeps all profit and has unlimited liability.
- A partnership has two or more owners who share capital, skills, control and profit, usually as set out in a partnership deed.
- Both are unincorporated: no separate legal personality, unlimited liability (except limited partners) and limited continuity.
- Profit is shared in the agreed ratio, after any partner's salary; equally if nothing is agreed.
- Recommend a form by weighing the owners' capital, skills, risk and wish for control.
:::
