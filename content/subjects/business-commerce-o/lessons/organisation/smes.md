## Introduction

Visit a home-industry site in Harare or Bulawayo and you will see rows of small workshops: welders making burglar bars and scotch carts, carpenters building wardrobes, tailors sewing school uniforms, mechanics repairing cars. Walk through a busy market and you meet vendors, cross-border traders and food sellers. Most of these are **small to medium enterprises** (SMEs), and together they employ a very large share of working Zimbabweans.

ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 4) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) ask you to describe the features of SMEs, explain their importance to the economy, outline the challenges they face and **evaluate** government programmes that help them. The Commerce syllabus names SME zones such as **Siyaso** (Mbare) and **Glen View Area 8** as examples of workspace provided for SMEs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small to medium enterprise (SME)"}
An independently owned business that is small or medium in size, measured by number of employees, annual turnover (sales) or capital employed.
:::

:::definition{term="Micro enterprise"}
The smallest type of business, often one owner with a few helpers, such as a vendor or a home-based tailor.
:::

:::definition{term="Home industry"}
A cluster of small manufacturing and repair workshops, often on a site provided by a local authority, where small businesses work side by side.
:::

:::definition{term="Formalisation"}
The process by which an informal business registers, obtains licences and starts paying taxes.
:::

:::note{title="How 'small' is defined"}
Governments set official size limits for micro, small and medium enterprises, usually by number of employees and turnover, and these limits differ between countries and change over time and between sectors. In the exam, describe the **measures** used rather than quoting particular numbers.
:::

## Features of SMEs

- **Small number of employees**, often family members.
- **Owner-managed:** the owner makes most decisions and does many jobs.
- **Small capital**, mainly from savings, family and friends, or informal lenders.
- Usually **sole traders or partnerships**, some private limited companies or co-operatives.
- **Serve local markets**, often with personal service.
- Many operate in the **informal sector**: unregistered, few written records.
- Use **simple technology** and are **labour-intensive**.
- **Flexible:** quick to change products in response to customers.

## Importance of SMEs to Zimbabwe's economy

1. **Employment creation:** SMEs provide jobs and self-employment for school leavers, retrenched workers and people who cannot find formal jobs.
2. **Income and poverty reduction:** owners and workers earn incomes that support households.
3. **Goods and services close to people:** in small quantities, at low prices and in places large firms do not serve, including rural areas.
4. **Developing enterprise skills:** many large businesses started as SMEs; they train future entrepreneurs.
5. **Using local resources:** SMEs use local materials, scrap and skills, adding value.
6. **Linkages with large firms:** they supply parts, repairs and services to bigger businesses and distribute their products.
7. **Competition and innovation:** they offer alternatives to large firms, which keeps prices down.
8. **Tax revenue** once they are formalised; and **exports**, for example of crafts or processed foods through cross-border trade.

## Challenges faced by SMEs

| Challenge | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Lack of finance** | Banks ask for collateral and records that SMEs do not have, so they rely on savings and expensive informal loans |
| **Lack of suitable workspace** | Operating from homes or streets; harassment or removal by authorities; no power or water |
| **Poor management and record keeping** | Owners lack bookkeeping, costing and marketing skills, so they misjudge profit |
| **Limited markets** | Competition from many similar SMEs and from cheap imports; difficulty reaching bigger customers |
| **High costs** | Small orders mean no bulk discounts; high transport and power costs |
| **Out-of-date technology** | Old tools reduce quality and output |
| **Regulations and costs of registration** | Licences, fees and paperwork discourage formalisation |
| **Economic instability** | Changes in prices, currency and demand hit small businesses hardest |

## Government assistance to SMEs

Governments support SMEs because they create jobs and develop the economy. Assistance includes:

- **Workspace:** building SME zones and home-industry sites with stalls, power and water, such as Siyaso and Glen View Area 8.
- **Finance:** low-interest loans, loan guarantees and grants through government agencies, development banks and special funds for youth and women.
- **Training and advice:** courses in bookkeeping, marketing, technical skills and business planning; business advisory centres.
- **Simpler registration** and lower licence fees to encourage formalisation.
- **Market access:** trade fairs and exhibitions, help with exporting, and **government procurement** that reserves some contracts for SMEs.
- **Infrastructure:** roads, power and water near SME clusters.
- **Protection** from unfair competition, for example rules on imports of goods SMEs produce.

:::example{title="Evaluating a programme"}
A council builds a new SME zone with 200 workshop bays in Gweru. **Benefits:** welders and carpenters gain secure premises with electricity, so output and quality rise; customers know where to find them; the council can reach them with training and licences, which helps formalisation. **Limitations:** rents may be too high for the smallest businesses; the site may be far from customers; there may be too few bays for all who need them; and workspace alone does not solve lack of finance or skills. Overall the programme is useful if rents are affordable and it is combined with training and access to credit.
:::

### How else SMEs can help themselves

- Join **traders' associations** to share information, buy in bulk and negotiate with authorities.
- Form **co-operatives** or **savings clubs** to pool capital.
- Keep **records**, which helps them obtain loans.
- Use **mobile money** and social media to reach customers cheaply.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Measuring size by profit"}
Profit is not a measure of size: a small business can be very profitable and a large one can make a loss. Use employees, turnover or capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating SMEs as only informal"}
Many SMEs are registered, formal businesses. The informal sector is one part of the SME sector.
:::

:::mistake{title="Evaluating without a judgement"}
"Evaluate government programmes" needs strengths, limitations and a conclusion on how well they work.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Link challenge to solution"}
A strong answer pairs each challenge with a matching form of help: lack of collateral → loan guarantees; no workspace → SME zones.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use Zimbabwean examples"}
Vendors, home-industry welders, cross-border traders and SME zones named in the syllabus make answers specific.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 4) covers features, importance, challenges and government programmes, including SME zones. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) covers SMEs and government assistance within business size. Cambridge 0264 treats small businesses under business size, not as a separate topic.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SMEs are independently owned businesses that are small or medium by employees, turnover or capital.
- They are owner-managed, use little capital and simple technology, and many are informal.
- They create jobs and incomes, supply local markets, use local resources and develop entrepreneurs.
- Challenges include finance, workspace, management skills, markets, costs and regulation.
- Government helps with workspace, finance, training, simpler registration and market access; evaluate each with strengths and limits.
:::
