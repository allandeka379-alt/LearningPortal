## Introduction

Governments regularly change the way they run state-owned businesses. A loss-making parastatal may be told to run "on business lines" and charge prices that cover its costs. Another may be sold, wholly or in part, to private investors. Occasionally the opposite happens, and the state takes over a private business it considers vital.

These changes are called **public sector reforms**. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 4) asks you to define **commercialisation**, **privatisation** and **nationalisation**, and to explain the reasons for and against each. Questions are usually set on a fictional parastatal, so you must apply the arguments to its situation and reach a conclusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Commercialisation"}
Running a public enterprise on business lines, so that it covers its costs or makes a profit from its own charges, without changing its ownership: it stays state-owned.
:::

:::definition{term="Privatisation"}
The transfer of ownership of a public enterprise, or of some of its activities, from the state to the private sector.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationalisation"}
The transfer of ownership of a private business to the state, usually with compensation for the former owners.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidy"}
Money paid by the government to a business to cover its losses or keep its prices low.
:::

## Commercialisation

When a public enterprise is commercialised, the government stays the owner but changes how the enterprise is run:

- It must **cover its costs** from its own revenue, and later perhaps make a profit.
- **Subsidies** from the government are reduced or stopped.
- Prices are set to reflect **costs**.
- Managers are given more **freedom** and are judged on financial performance, often on contracts with targets.
- It may be turned into a company owned by the state, with a board responsible for results.

Commercialisation is often a **first step towards privatisation**.

| Arguments for | Arguments against |
| --- | --- |
| Reduces the burden on taxpayers as subsidies fall | Prices rise, so poorer consumers may lose access to essential services |
| Encourages efficiency, cost control and better service | Unprofitable services, such as rural routes, may be cut |
| Managers become accountable for results | Jobs may be lost as costs are cut |
| Prepares the enterprise for private investment | The state still bears losses if the reform fails |

## Privatisation

### Methods

- **Selling shares to the public**, for example by listing the enterprise on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange.
- **Selling the enterprise** (or part of it) to a private company, sometimes a foreign one.
- **Selling shares to employees** and management.
- **Contracting out** services: the state pays a private firm to do work it used to do itself, such as refuse collection or cleaning.
- **Deregulation:** allowing private firms to compete in a market the state used to control.

### Arguments for privatisation

- **Efficiency:** the profit motive and competition encourage private owners to cut waste and improve service.
- **Revenue for the government:** the sale raises money, and the government no longer has to pay subsidies.
- **Investment:** private owners can raise capital to modernise equipment.
- **Less political interference** in pricing and appointments.
- **Wider share ownership** if shares are sold to the public and employees.
- **Tax:** a profitable private business pays tax.

### Arguments against privatisation

- **Higher prices**, especially where the privatised firm is a monopoly.
- **Job losses** as new owners cut costs.
- **Unprofitable services** (remote areas, poor customers) may be stopped.
- **Loss of control** over strategic industries; foreign ownership may send profits abroad.
- **Selling cheaply:** assets built with public money may be sold below their true value.
- A public monopoly may simply become a **private monopoly**, so the state must still regulate it.

:::example{title="Applying the arguments"}
A fictional state-owned bus company runs routes across Matabeleland and needs government subsidies every year. Privatisation could raise money for the state, end the subsidies and bring in new buses. But private owners may stop running buses on quiet rural routes where few passengers travel, and fares may rise. A sensible conclusion: privatise the profitable urban routes, but contract private operators to run rural routes with a small subsidy so that rural communities keep a service.
:::

## Nationalisation

Governments nationalise for reasons that mirror the arguments against privatisation.

### Arguments for nationalisation

- **Control of strategic industries** such as energy, minerals or transport.
- **Protect jobs** when a vital private business is about to close.
- Provide **essential services to all**, including unprofitable areas.
- Prevent **exploitation** by private monopolies charging high prices.
- Keep **profits in the country** rather than sending them to foreign owners.
- Plan the economy and develop particular regions.

### Arguments against nationalisation

- **Compensation** to former owners can be very costly for the government.
- Nationalised businesses may become **inefficient** without the profit motive.
- **Political interference** and bureaucracy.
- Losses become a **burden on taxpayers**.
- It may discourage **foreign investment**, as investors fear their businesses could be taken.

:::note{title="Indigenisation is a different idea"}
Policies that require local people to own a share of businesses change **who in the private sector** owns them; they are studied in the lesson on ownership and indigenisation, not here.
:::

## Comparing the three reforms

| Reform | Ownership after the reform | Main aim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercialisation | Stays with the state | Run on business lines; reduce subsidies |
| Privatisation | Moves from the state to the private sector | Efficiency, revenue, investment |
| Nationalisation | Moves from the private sector to the state | Control, protection of jobs and services |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing commercialisation with privatisation"}
In commercialisation the state **keeps** ownership; in privatisation ownership **passes** to private owners.
:::

:::mistake{title="Arguing only one side"}
"Discuss" questions need arguments for and against and a justified conclusion; one-sided answers are capped at a low level.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying privatisation always lowers prices"}
Prices fall only if competition increases or efficiency gains are passed on. A private monopoly may raise prices.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Define precisely"}
Each definition earns its mark through the **direction of ownership**: state to private (privatisation), private to state (nationalisation), no change (commercialisation).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Consider the stakeholders"}
Strong answers look at the effect on consumers, workers, taxpayers, the government and investors, then judge which matters most in the case.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Only ZIMSEC Commerce examines this concept (Form 4: public sector reforms). Business Enterprise and Skills and Cambridge 0264 cover public sector organisations but not these reforms in depth.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Commercialisation keeps state ownership but runs the enterprise on business lines with fewer subsidies.
- Privatisation transfers ownership to the private sector by share sales, outright sale, contracting out or deregulation.
- Privatisation can raise efficiency, revenue and investment, but may raise prices, cut jobs and end unprofitable services.
- Nationalisation transfers ownership to the state to control strategic industries and protect jobs and services, but is costly and may be inefficient.
- Apply the arguments to the case and give a justified conclusion.
:::
