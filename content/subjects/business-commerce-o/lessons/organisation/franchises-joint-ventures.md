## Introduction

Rudo wants to open a fast-food outlet in Masvingo. She could start her own brand from nothing, or she could pay to use the name, recipes and systems of a well-known chicken-and-chips chain. A mining company and a local construction firm want to build a processing plant together without merging. A group of young people in Bulawayo want to run a recycling business whose main purpose is to clean up their community and create jobs.

These are three forms of business enterprise beyond the sole trader, partnership and company: the **franchise**, the **joint venture** and the **social enterprise**. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) and Cambridge 0264 both examine them, often by asking you to weigh up advantages and disadvantages for one party in a case.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Franchise"}
An agreement in which one business (the franchisor) allows another (the franchisee) to use its brand name, products and way of doing business in return for fees and royalties.
:::

:::definition{term="Franchisor"}
The business that owns the brand and business format and sells franchises.
:::

:::definition{term="Franchisee"}
The person or business that buys a franchise and runs an outlet under the franchisor's brand.
:::

:::definition{term="Royalty"}
A regular payment from the franchisee to the franchisor, often a percentage of sales.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint venture"}
An agreement between two or more businesses to share the capital, risks, control and profits of a particular project or new business, while each remains independent.
:::

:::definition{term="Social enterprise"}
A business that sells goods or services but whose main aims are social or environmental; most of its surplus is reinvested in those aims.
:::

## How a franchise works

1. The franchisee pays an **initial fee** to join.
2. The franchisor provides the **brand**, the products or recipes, equipment specifications, **training**, a manual of how to run the outlet, **national advertising** and often supplies.
3. The franchisee provides the **capital** for the premises and equipment, employs staff and runs the outlet day to day.
4. The franchisee pays **royalties** (often a percentage of sales) and must follow the franchisor's **rules** on prices, menu, layout and quality.

Franchising is common in fast food, petrol stations, hotels, courier services, printing and educational services.

### Advantages and disadvantages for the franchisee

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Lower risk of failure: the product and brand are already known and trusted | Initial fee and royalties reduce profit |
| Training and support from the franchisor | Little freedom: must follow rules on products, prices and layout |
| National advertising paid for by the franchisor | Poor performance by other outlets can damage the brand |
| Banks may lend more readily for a proven format | The franchisor may not renew the agreement |
| Bulk-bought supplies may be cheaper | Must buy supplies from the franchisor, sometimes at high prices |

### Advantages and disadvantages for the franchisor

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Rapid expansion using franchisees' capital | Shares profit with franchisees |
| Income from fees and royalties | One poorly run outlet can harm the brand's reputation |
| Franchisees are motivated owners, often more committed than salaried managers | Less direct control over day-to-day operations |
| Local franchisees understand local markets | Must spend on training, monitoring and support |

:::example{title="Rudo's choice"}
If Rudo buys the franchise, she gains a recipe customers already like, training and the chain's advertising, so her outlet should attract customers quickly. But she must pay the joining fee, a royalty on every sale and buy supplies from the chain, and she cannot change the menu even if Masvingo customers ask for local dishes. If she starts her own brand she keeps all the profit and freedom, but must build a reputation from nothing, which is riskier.
:::

## Joint ventures

In a joint venture each partner contributes something the other lacks: capital, technology, local knowledge, licences or access to customers. They share the costs, risks and profits of the project. Joint ventures are common when a foreign business enters a new country with a local partner, or when a project is too large or risky for one business.

**Advantages**

- Shared costs and risks of a large or risky project.
- Combines skills, technology and local knowledge.
- Easier entry into a new market or country.
- Each business keeps its own independence outside the project.

**Disadvantages**

- Profits must be shared.
- Disagreements over decisions, management styles or aims.
- One partner may gain the other's technology or knowledge and later compete.
- Unclear responsibility if the project goes wrong.

:::example{title="A joint venture"}
A foreign solar-panel maker forms a joint venture with a Harare electrical contractor. The foreign firm supplies technology and panels; the local firm provides installation teams, knowledge of local customers and its business contacts. Both share the cost of a warehouse and split the profits as agreed.
:::

## Social enterprises

A social enterprise trades like a business, but its main aims are **social** (jobs for disadvantaged people, community services) or **environmental** (recycling, clean energy). It may also aim to make a surplus, which is reinvested in the cause rather than paid out to owners. It may take the legal form of a company, co-operative or trust.

Social enterprises often have three aims: an **economic** aim (cover costs and make a surplus), a **social** aim and an **environmental** aim.

:::example{title="A social enterprise"}
A Bulawayo group collects plastic waste from the townships, pays collectors per kilogram and sells the plastic to a recycler. Its surplus pays for more collection points. It creates income for unemployed young people and cleans the environment, while still needing to cover its costs.
:::

## Comparing forms of business enterprise

| Form | Owners | Liability | Capital | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited | Small | Full control by owner |
| Partnership | 2 or more partners | Unlimited (general partners) | Small to medium | Shared |
| Private limited company | Shareholders (private) | Limited | Medium | Directors, often the owners |
| Public limited company | Shareholders (public) | Limited | Large | Board of directors |
| Franchise | Franchisee owns the outlet | Depends on franchisee's legal form | Franchisee's capital plus franchisor's brand | Shared: franchisor's rules |
| Joint venture | Two or more businesses | Depends on agreement and legal form | Pooled | Shared by the partners |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the franchisee owns the brand"}
The franchisee owns the outlet and its equipment; the **franchisor** owns the brand and recipes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a joint venture with a merger"}
In a merger two businesses become one. In a joint venture they remain separate and cooperate only on one project.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying social enterprises do not want profit"}
They need a surplus to survive; the difference is that it is used for the social or environmental aim.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Say whose point of view"}
Franchise questions usually ask for advantages to the **franchisee** or to the **franchisor**. Read carefully and answer from the right side.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend with evidence"}
For "should Rudo buy a franchise?", balance lower risk and support against costs and loss of freedom, using the case figures, and conclude.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) covers public corporations, joint ventures and franchises as forms of enterprise. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.4) covers franchises, joint ventures and social enterprises. ZIMSEC Commerce does not examine this concept directly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A franchisee pays fees and royalties to use the franchisor's brand, products and system.
- Franchisees gain a proven format and support but lose freedom and part of their profit; franchisors expand quickly with others' capital but risk their reputation.
- A joint venture shares the costs, risks and profits of a project between independent businesses.
- Social enterprises trade to achieve social and environmental aims and reinvest their surplus.
- Compare forms by ownership, liability, capital and control.
:::
