## Introduction

Tendai started making peanut butter in her kitchen in Gweru and selling it to neighbours. Five years later she rents a small factory, employs twelve people and supplies supermarkets. Now a larger food company wants to merge with her business. Should she grow by expanding on her own, or join with another firm?

Growth is one of the most common themes in business case studies. Examiners ask why owners want growth, which method of growth a business should choose, and what problems growth can bring. Business Enterprise and Skills covers factors influencing growth in Form 2 and methods and effects of growth in Form 4; Cambridge 0264 covers growth in topic 1.3.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal growth (organic growth)"}
Growth from within the business: expanding its own operations by selling more, developing new products or entering new markets.
:::

:::definition{term="External growth"}
Growth by joining with another business through a merger or a takeover.
:::

:::definition{term="Merger"}
When the owners of two businesses agree to join together to form one business.
:::

:::definition{term="Takeover (acquisition)"}
When one business buys enough shares in another to gain control of it, with or without the agreement of its managers.
:::

## Why owners want their businesses to grow

- **Higher profits**: more sales can bring more profit.
- **Economies of scale**: bulk buying and specialist machinery lower average costs.
- **Larger market share**: more power in the market and stronger brands.
- **Security**: a larger business is less likely to fail or be taken over, and it can spread risk across more products and markets.
- **Status and rewards** for owners and managers.

## Factors that influence growth

| Helps growth | Limits growth |
| --- | --- |
| Growing demand for the product | Small or shrinking market |
| Access to finance (retained profit, loans, shares) | Lack of finance |
| Skilled managers and workers | Shortage of skills; owner wants control |
| Good economic conditions (boom) | Recession; high interest rates |
| Government support for SMEs and exporters | Strict regulations, high taxes |
| New technology and good infrastructure | Strong competition |

## Internal growth

A business grows internally by:

- **Increasing sales** of existing products (more promotion, more outlets, opening branches).
- **Developing new products** to sell to existing customers.
- **Entering new markets**: new towns, new customer groups, exports or selling online.

| Advantages of internal growth | Disadvantages of internal growth |
| --- | --- |
| Lower risk: the business grows at its own pace | Slow |
| Owners keep control | Needs finance from profit or loans |
| Keeps the business's own culture and methods | Limited by the size of the existing market |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: internal growth into a new market]
Tendai's peanut butter business has sales revenue of US\$96,000 and profit of US\$18,000 a year. Supplying supermarkets in Bulawayo is expected to add US\$30,000 of revenue and US\$21,000 of extra costs (transport, ingredients and an extra worker).

1. New sales revenue = US\$96,000 + US\$30,000 = US\$126,000.
2. Extra profit = US\$30,000 − US\$21,000 = US\$9,000.
3. New profit = US\$18,000 + US\$9,000 = US\$27,000.
4. The new market increases revenue and profit, and Tendai keeps full control, but she must find the money for the extra costs before the revenue arrives.
:::

## External growth: mergers and takeovers

External growth is classified by the stage of production of the two businesses.

| Type of integration | Meaning | Example | Main benefits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | Two businesses at the **same stage** of production in the same industry join | Two bakeries merge | Larger market share, fewer competitors, economies of scale |
| Vertical backward | A business joins with a **supplier** (earlier stage) | A bakery buys a flour mill | Secure supplies, control of quality and cost of inputs |
| Vertical forward | A business joins with a **customer** (later stage) | A clothing factory buys a chain of clothing shops | Secure outlets, keeps the retailer's profit, controls how products are sold |
| Conglomerate (diversification) | Businesses in **unrelated** industries join | A hotel group buys a transport company | Spreads risk across different markets |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: market share after a horizontal merger]
Two milling companies sell maize meal in a regional market worth US\$900,000 a year. Mill X sells US\$162,000 and Mill Y sells US\$117,000. They agree to merge.

1. Mill X's market share = 162,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 18%.
2. Mill Y's market share = 117,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 13%.
3. Combined market share = (162,000 + 117,000) ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 279,000 ÷ 900,000 × 100 = 31%.
4. The merged business becomes much larger in its market, with one fewer competitor, which may let it buy maize in bulk at lower cost.
:::

| Advantages of external growth | Disadvantages of external growth |
| --- | --- |
| Fast: the business gains another firm's customers, staff and assets at once | Expensive to buy another business |
| Can reduce competition and increase market share | Clash of management styles and cultures |
| Secures supplies or outlets (vertical integration) | Job losses and low morale as duplicate jobs are cut |
| Spreads risk (conglomerate) | Original owners may lose some control |

## Problems linked to growth and how to overcome them

| Problem | How to reduce it |
| --- | --- |
| Poor communication and coordination as the business gets bigger | Clear structure, delegation, regular meetings, ICT |
| Shortage of cash: money is spent on expansion before revenue rises | Plan cash flow; use long-term finance for long-term expansion |
| Loss of control by the owner | Recruit skilled managers; delegate |
| Lower motivation of workers who feel less valued | Team working, training, recognition |
| Diseconomies of scale: average costs rise in a very large business | Divide the business into smaller units (divisions or branches) |

## Effects of growth

Growth affects stakeholders: owners may earn more profit, employees may gain promotion or lose jobs after a merger, customers may gain from lower costs but lose choice if competition falls, and the government may investigate if a merger gives one firm too much power over prices.

:::example{title="Choosing a method of growth"}
A bus operator in Masvingo with eight buses wants to grow. Buying a rival's fleet (horizontal integration) would double its size at once, but it would need a large loan and would have to combine two groups of drivers with different working methods. Adding two buses a year from retained profit (internal growth) is slower but keeps control and avoids debt. The right choice depends on how quickly the market is growing, how much finance is available and how much risk the owners will accept.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up horizontal and vertical integration"}
Horizontal: the **same** stage (bakery and bakery). Vertical: **different** stages of the same chain (bakery and flour mill, or bakery and shop).
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every joining of firms a merger"}
A merger is agreed by both businesses. A takeover happens when one business buys control of another, and it may be hostile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the problems of growth"}
Questions on growth often reward balance: growth brings economies of scale and market power but also cash shortages, communication problems and culture clashes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name the type and apply it"}
"This is backward vertical integration because the bakery is buying its supplier, the flour mill, which gives it control over the price and quality of flour."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Recommend a method"}
For "should the business grow internally or externally?", compare speed, cost, risk and control in the context of the case, then decide.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Owners want growth for profit, economies of scale, market share, security and status.
- Growth depends on demand, finance, skills, the economy, government policy and competition.
- Internal growth is slower and lower-risk; owners keep control.
- External growth (mergers and takeovers) is fast but costly and risks culture clashes; it can be horizontal, vertical (backward or forward) or conglomerate.
- Growth brings problems (communication, cash, control, motivation, diseconomies) that good management can reduce.
:::
