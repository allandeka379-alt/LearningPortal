## Introduction

When a new brick-making business opens on the edge of Masvingo, many people are affected. The owners hope for profit. Local people hope for jobs. Builders hope for cheaper bricks. Neighbours worry about smoke from the kilns. The bank that lent the money wants to be repaid, and the local authority wants rates and the government wants taxes. Each of these groups has an interest in what the business does: they are its **stakeholders**.

Stakeholders appear in almost every case study. Examiners ask you to identify stakeholders, explain their objectives, explain how a decision affects them and, most often, to analyse **conflicts** between them. Business Enterprise and Skills covers stakeholders in Form 1; Cambridge 0264 in topic 1.5.2.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stakeholder"}
Any person or group that has an interest in the activities of a business because they are affected by what it does.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal stakeholder"}
A stakeholder inside the business: its owners, managers and employees.
:::

:::definition{term="External stakeholder"}
A stakeholder outside the business: customers, suppliers, lenders, government, the local community and competitors.
:::

:::definition{term="Shareholder"}
A person who owns shares in a limited company and so owns part of it. Shareholders are stakeholders, but not every stakeholder is a shareholder.
:::

## Stakeholder groups and their objectives

| Stakeholder | Internal or external | Main objectives (what they want) | How they help the business |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owners (sole trader, partners, shareholders) | Internal | Profit, dividends, growth in the value of the business | Provide capital and take the risk |
| Managers | Internal | Good salaries and bonuses, job security, status, growth | Plan, organise and control the business |
| Employees | Internal | Fair wages, safe working conditions, job security, training | Provide the labour and skills |
| Customers | External | Good quality products at fair prices, good service, choice | Provide the sales revenue |
| Suppliers | External | Regular orders, fair prices, prompt payment | Supply materials and stock, often on credit |
| Lenders (banks, microfinance institutions) | External | Repayment of loans with interest on time | Provide finance |
| Government | External | Taxes, jobs, legal behaviour, growth in the economy | Provides infrastructure and laws, and may give grants or support |
| Local community | External | Jobs, little pollution or noise, support for local projects | Provides workers and customers, and accepts the business locally |
| Competitors | External | Fair competition, information about rivals' plans | Push the business to improve (and may cooperate in trade associations) |

:::example{title="Stakeholders of a bus company in Bulawayo"}
- **Owners** want profit from fares.
- **Drivers and conductors** want regular wages and safe vehicles.
- **Passengers** want cheap, safe and punctual buses.
- **The fuel supplier** wants to be paid on time.
- **The bank** that financed the buses wants its instalments.
- **The government** wants vehicles that are roadworthy and taxes paid.
- **Residents** along the routes want less noise and fewer accidents.
:::

## Why stakeholders matter to a business

A business cannot succeed without its stakeholders. Without customers there is no revenue; without employees no work gets done; without suppliers there is nothing to sell; without lenders many businesses cannot start or grow. Stakeholders also have **power**: customers can switch to rivals, employees can leave or strike, the government can fine or close a business and the community can protest or report pollution. Good businesses therefore try to understand what each group wants.

## Conflicts between stakeholder objectives

Stakeholders often want different things, so a decision that pleases one group may upset another.

| Decision | Stakeholders who gain | Stakeholders who lose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise prices | Owners (higher profit per item) | Customers (pay more) |
| Pay higher wages | Employees (more income) | Owners (lower profit), customers (prices may rise) |
| Replace workers with machines | Owners (lower costs in the long run), customers (cheaper products) | Employees (job losses), community (less income spent locally) |
| Build a larger factory | Owners and managers (growth), local job seekers | Neighbours (noise, traffic, pollution) |
| Pay suppliers later | The business (better cash flow) | Suppliers (wait for their money) |

:::example{title="A conflict at a clothing factory in Harare"}
Shareholders want higher profits, so the directors plan to buy cheaper cotton from a supplier that pays its workers very little. Customers who care about ethical sourcing may stop buying, the current local supplier loses orders, and the business's reputation could suffer. The directors must weigh the extra profit against these effects.
:::

Not every objective conflicts. Many stakeholder objectives **overlap**: when a business is successful, owners earn profit, employees keep their jobs, suppliers get regular orders and the government collects more tax.

### How businesses deal with conflicts

- **Prioritise** the stakeholders who matter most for the decision (often owners and customers), while trying to limit the harm to others.
- **Communicate and consult**: explain decisions to employees, the community and suppliers and listen to their concerns.
- **Compromise**: for example, introduce machines gradually and retrain workers instead of dismissing them all.
- **Act responsibly**: corporate social responsibility can reduce conflict with the community and government.

## ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills: stakeholders of an enterprise

In BES you study the internal and external stakeholders of an enterprise and their roles. A typical question gives a small enterprise, such as a poultry project or a welding workshop, and asks you to "identify four stakeholders and state the role or interest of each". Remember that a sole trader's family and the local community are often important stakeholders of a small enterprise.

:::example{title="Stakeholders of a small poultry enterprise"}
Thandeka keeps broiler chickens near Chinhoyi. Her **family** (owners) want income to pay school fees. Her two **workers** want regular pay. **Customers** at the local market want fresh, affordable chickens. Her **feed supplier** wants prompt payment. The **microfinance lender** wants loan repayments. **Neighbours** want no bad smells, and the **local authority** wants her to keep to health rules. If she expands, she must think about every one of these groups, not only her own profit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing stakeholders with shareholders"}
Shareholders own part of a company. Stakeholders are everyone affected by the business, including shareholders. A customer is a stakeholder but not usually a shareholder.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing groups without their interests"}
"Customers are stakeholders" earns little. Say what they want ("low prices and good quality") and how the decision affects them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating competitors and government as internal"}
Only owners, managers and employees are internal stakeholders. Everyone else is external.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the case"}
Name the stakeholder as the case does ("the 40 farmers who supply milk") and link its objective to the decision in the question.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Conflict questions"}
For "explain a conflict", name two groups, say what each wants and show why both cannot be fully satisfied. For "evaluate", say which group should be given priority and why.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 expects the roles and objectives of internal and external stakeholder groups and how their objectives conflict. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 1) concentrates on identifying stakeholders and their roles in an enterprise.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stakeholders are people or groups affected by a business.
- Internal stakeholders: owners, managers, employees. External: customers, suppliers, lenders, government, community, competitors.
- Each group has its own objectives and contributes something the business needs.
- Stakeholder objectives often conflict (prices, wages, automation, expansion), though many overlap when the business succeeds.
- Businesses manage conflict by prioritising, consulting, compromising and acting responsibly.
:::
