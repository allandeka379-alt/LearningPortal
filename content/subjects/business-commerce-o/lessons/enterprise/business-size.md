## Introduction

Is a welding workshop in Gweru with fifteen workers bigger or smaller than a software firm in Harare with eight programmers? It depends on how you measure. The workshop has more workers, but the software firm may sell far more. Governments, banks and investors all need to compare businesses by size, for example to decide which firms qualify for support given to small and medium enterprises (SMEs).

Examiners ask you to name and explain methods of measuring size, to calculate or compare sizes from data, to explain why profit is **not** a measure of size and to discuss the advantages of being small or large. Business Enterprise and Skills covers small and large businesses in Form 3 and measuring size in Form 4; Cambridge 0264 covers both in topic 1.3.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Capital employed"}
The total long-term finance invested in a business (owners' capital plus long-term loans), used to buy its assets and run it.
:::

:::definition{term="Market share"}
The percentage of total sales in a market made by one business: sales of the business ÷ total market sales × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Small and medium enterprises (SMEs)"}
Businesses below a size limit (usually set by number of employees, annual turnover or the value of assets) that governments often treat differently, for example with special loans and training.
:::

## Methods of measuring business size

| Method | How it works | Problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of employees | Count the workers | Capital-intensive firms (e.g. an automated bottling plant) employ few people but are large; part-time workers make counting unclear |
| Value or volume of output | Value or quantity of goods produced | Hard to compare firms making different products; value depends on prices |
| Value of sales (sales revenue) | Total revenue in a year | Firms selling expensive items (cars, gold) look large with few customers; not useful for comparing different industries |
| Capital employed | Total long-term finance invested | A labour-intensive service business needs little capital but may be large; capital is hard to value |
| Market share | Sales as a percentage of the market | Only compares firms in the same market; a large share of a tiny market may still be a small business |

**Profit is not a measure of size.** Profit depends on how efficiently a business is run, on its prices and on the economy, not on how big it is. A small, well-run firm can earn more profit than a large, inefficient one, and a large business can make a loss.

Because each method has problems, it is best to use **more than one** method when comparing businesses.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: different measures give different answers]
Compare Chipo's furniture workshop in Gweru with Farai's software firm in Harare.

| Measure | Chipo's furniture workshop | Farai's software firm |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Number of employees | 12 | 35 |
| Capital employed (US\$) | 180,000 | 90,000 |
| Annual sales revenue (US\$) | 240,000 | 310,000 |

1. By **number of employees**, the software firm is larger (35 against 12).
2. By **capital employed**, the furniture workshop is larger (US\$180,000 against US\$90,000): it needs expensive machines and timber stocks, while the software firm mainly needs people and computers.
3. By **sales revenue**, the software firm is larger (US\$310,000 against US\$240,000).
4. Conclusion: two of three measures suggest the software firm is larger, but the answer depends on the measure, so state which one you use.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: market share and why profit is not a measure]
Two bakeries in Kwekwe compete in a market worth US\$560,000 a year. Bakery A sells US\$84,000 and earns a profit of US\$21,000. Bakery B sells US\$140,000 and earns a profit of US\$14,000.

1. Bakery A's market share = 84,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = 15%.
2. Bakery B's market share = 140,000 ÷ 560,000 × 100 = 25%.
3. Bakery B is larger by sales and market share, yet Bakery A earns more profit. Profit shows how well a business is doing, not how large it is.
:::

## Small businesses

### Advantages of being small

- **Personal service** and close contact with customers, who are often known by name.
- **Flexibility**: quick decisions and quick responses to changes in demand.
- **Easy communication** between owner and the few employees; good working relationships.
- **Low start-up costs** and fewer legal formalities.
- They can serve **niche markets** too small to interest large firms.

### Disadvantages of being small

- **Higher average costs**: no bulk-buying discounts or specialist machinery.
- **Difficulty raising finance**: banks see small firms as risky and may demand collateral.
- **Limited management skills**: the owner may have to do everything.
- **Vulnerable** to the loss of one large customer or the arrival of a large competitor.

## Large businesses

### Advantages of being large

- **Economies of scale**: bulk buying, specialist managers and machines lower average costs.
- **Easier access to finance**: share issues and loans on better terms.
- **Market power**: strong brands and the ability to influence prices.
- **Spreading risk** by selling many products in many markets.

### Disadvantages of being large

- **Diseconomies of scale**: slow communication, long chains of command, poor coordination.
- **Less personal** contact with customers and employees; workers may feel unimportant and less motivated.
- **Slower decisions** because of many levels of management.
- More **public and government attention**, for example over prices or pollution.

## Why some businesses remain small

- **Owners' choice**: they want independence, control and a manageable workload.
- **Small market**: a niche product or a local service (a hairdresser, a rural tuck shop) has limited demand.
- **Personal service** is the main attraction and would be lost with growth.
- **Lack of finance** to expand.
- **Type of industry**: some services, such as plumbing or tailoring, depend on the owner's skills.

## ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills: SMEs and government assistance

BES (Form 4) studies small to medium enterprises and how government helps them: training, advice, loans on easier terms, workspace in designated SME sites and simplified registration. SMEs are important in Zimbabwe because they create many jobs, especially in the informal sector, use local resources and develop enterprising skills. A government ministry is responsible for SMEs; questions focus on the types of help rather than on any agency's current name.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using profit to measure size"}
Profit measures performance, not size. Use employees, output, sales revenue, capital employed or market share.
:::

:::mistake{title="Relying on one measure"}
A conclusion based on one measure may be misleading. Compare at least two and explain why they differ.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming bigger is always better"}
Large firms gain economies of scale but face diseconomies; small firms are more flexible and personal. Answer "advantages and disadvantages" questions with both sides.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the problem with each method"}

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Show working"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business size can be measured by employees, output, sales revenue, capital employed or market share, never by profit.
- Every method has problems, so compare more than one.
- Small businesses are flexible and personal but have higher costs and find finance harder to raise.
- Large businesses gain economies of scale and finance but face diseconomies and slower decisions.
- Many businesses stay small by choice, because their market is small or because finance is limited.
:::
