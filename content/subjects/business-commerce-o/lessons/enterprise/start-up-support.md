## Introduction

Thandeka has a good idea: a small business making school uniforms in Bulawayo. She has sewing skills but only two second-hand machines, no business training and too little money to buy cloth in bulk. Like most new enterprises, her business is fragile in its first years. Support from her family, a youth loan scheme, a training course and a business mentor could make the difference between success and failure.

Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) asks you to justify why start-ups need support and to identify ways they are supported. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.3) asks why governments support business start-ups and how. Exam answers must explain **why** support is needed, **who** provides it and **how** each kind of support helps.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Start-up"}
A newly created business in its first years of trading.
:::

:::definition{term="Grant"}
Money given to a business, usually by the government or a development organisation, that does not have to be repaid if the conditions are met.
:::

:::definition{term="Low-cost (soft) loan"}
A loan at a lower interest rate, with easier security or longer repayment than a normal bank loan.
:::

:::definition{term="Business incubator"}
A place or programme that gives new businesses low-cost workspace, shared equipment, advice and mentoring.
:::

:::definition{term="Crowdfunding"}
Raising small amounts of money from a large number of people, often through an online platform, to finance a project.
:::

## Why new enterprises need support

1. **Lack of finance:** banks see start-ups as risky because they have no trading record and little collateral.
2. **Lack of experience and skills:** owners may lack bookkeeping, marketing or management skills.
3. **No customer base:** the business is unknown and must win customers from established firms.
4. **High start-up costs:** equipment, premises, licences and first stock must be paid for before any revenue.
5. **Cash-flow problems:** costs come before income, and customers may pay late.
6. **High failure rate:** many new businesses close within their first few years.
7. **Difficulty with regulations:** registration, tax and licensing can be confusing.

## Why governments support start-ups

- **Employment:** new businesses create jobs and reduce unemployment, especially for young people.
- **Economic growth:** more output, more competition and more innovation.
- **Tax revenue** in the future, once start-ups grow and register.
- **Future large firms:** today's start-ups can become tomorrow's large employers and exporters.
- **Regional development:** businesses in rural areas or small towns spread jobs and services.
- **Formalisation:** support encourages informal businesses to register.
- **Social aims:** support for women, young people and disadvantaged groups to earn incomes.

## Ways start-ups are supported

### Support from government

| Type of support | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Grants** | Provide capital that does not need to be repaid, reducing financial risk |
| **Low-cost loans and loan guarantees** | Give access to finance at lower interest; a guarantee persuades banks to lend |
| **Advice** | Business advisory centres help with registration, planning and finding markets |
| **Training schemes** | Courses in bookkeeping, marketing, technical skills and business planning |
| **Workspace and incubators** | Cheap premises, shared equipment and mentoring reduce start-up costs |
| **Tax incentives** | Lower taxes or allowances in the first years improve cash flow |
| **Simpler registration** | Reduces the cost and time of setting up |
| **Government contracts** | A share of public purchasing reserved for small or new businesses guarantees some sales |

### Support from family and friends

Most start-ups in Zimbabwe begin with money from the owner's own savings and from **family and friends**: gifts, interest-free loans or working without pay in the business. Family members may also provide premises, such as a room at home, and advice from their own experience.

- **Advantages:** quick, flexible, cheap or interest-free, no collateral.
- **Disadvantages:** small amounts; disputes can damage relationships; relatives may expect a say in decisions.

### Fundraising and other sources

- **Savings clubs** (rounds) and **burial societies** that lend to members.
- **Crowdfunding** through online platforms, where people give small sums for a project.
- **Competitions** and **prizes** for business ideas, sometimes run by banks or development organisations.
- **Non-governmental organisations (NGOs)** and development agencies offering grants, equipment or training, especially for women and young people.
- **Mentoring** by experienced business people, sometimes arranged by business organisations.

:::example{title="Thandeka's support package"}
Thandeka's aunt lends her money interest-free to buy a third machine (**family**). She joins a youth enterprise training course on costing and record keeping (**training**). With a business plan from the course, she obtains a youth loan at a low interest rate (**low-cost loan**). A retired tailor visits monthly to advise her (**mentoring**). Each type of support solves a different problem: capital, skills, finance and experience.
:::

## Evaluating support

Support is most effective when:

- it is matched to the business's main problem (training will not help if the real problem is no workspace);
- loans come with training, so the money is used well;
- the application process is simple and fair, so it reaches small and rural businesses;
- the business has a sound plan; support cannot rescue an idea with no demand.

Limitations include limited government funds, long waiting times, loans that must still be repaid, support that reaches only well-connected applicants, and dependency on grants instead of building a self-sustaining business.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a grant is a loan"}
A grant does **not** have to be repaid; a loan does, with interest (even if the rate is low).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing sources without the reason"}
Explain how each kind of support solves a start-up problem: "training improves record keeping, so the owner knows whether the business is profitable".
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming support guarantees success"}
Support reduces risk but the business still needs customers, good management and a viable idea.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Why, then how"}
Strong answers explain **why** the business needs support (its problem) before saying **how** the support helps.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Government's point of view"}
For "why do governments support start-ups?", answer from the economy's side: jobs, growth, tax, future large firms.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) includes support from friends, family members and government. Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.3) asks why and how governments support start-ups. ZIMSEC Commerce does not examine this concept.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Start-ups need support because they lack finance, experience, customers and cash, and many fail early.
- Governments support them for jobs, growth, future tax revenue, regional development and formalisation.
- Government support includes grants, low-cost loans and guarantees, advice, training, workspace, tax incentives and contracts.
- Family and friends provide quick, cheap finance and help; fundraising includes savings clubs, crowdfunding, competitions and NGOs.
- Support works best when matched to the business's real problem and combined with a sound plan.
:::
