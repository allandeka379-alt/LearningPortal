## Introduction

Rudo opens a small bakery in Chinhoyi. In her first year she only wants to cover her costs and keep the doors open. Three years later, with loyal customers, she wants to open a second shop and supply tuck shops across town. Her goals have changed because her business has changed.

Every business, from a market stall at Mbare to a large company listed on the Zimbabwe Stock Exchange, works towards **objectives**. Examiners ask you to identify objectives, explain why they matter, suggest suitable objectives for a business in a case study and explain why objectives change. Business Enterprise and Skills studies objectives in Form 1; Cambridge 0264 covers them in topic 1.5.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business objective"}
A target or goal that a business aims to achieve, which guides the decisions of its owners and managers.
:::

:::definition{term="Mission statement"}
A short, general statement of what a business is for and what it values. Objectives turn the mission into specific targets.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate social responsibility (CSR)"}
The idea that a business should consider the interests of society and the environment, not only its owners, when it makes decisions.
:::

## The main business objectives

| Objective | What it means | Typical business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Survival | Stay in business: cover costs and avoid running out of cash | New businesses; any business in a recession or facing a strong new rival |
| Profit | Earn revenue greater than total costs | Most private sector businesses, especially once established |
| Growth | Become larger: more outlets, more output, more employees | Established businesses that want economies of scale and more security |
| Sales revenue | Increase the value of sales | Businesses whose managers are rewarded for sales, or that want to grow |
| Market share | Increase the business's percentage of total sales in its market | Businesses in competitive markets that want to become market leaders |
| Social and ethical aims | Treat workers, suppliers, customers and the environment fairly | Social enterprises, co-operatives, and businesses protecting their reputation |

### Survival

A new business has few customers and is not yet known, and often has loans to repay. Its first aim is to survive: to sell enough to pay its bills. An established business may also return to survival as its main objective in a recession or when a large competitor arrives.

### Profit

Profit rewards the owners for the risk they take, provides **retained profit** to pay for expansion and attracts investors and lenders. A business may aim to **maximise** profit, or for a "satisfactory" profit that keeps its owners content.

### Growth, sales revenue and market share

Growth brings lower average costs (economies of scale), more security against takeover and higher pay and status for managers. Growth is often measured by sales revenue or market share. **Market share** is calculated as:

Market share (%) = sales of the business ÷ total market sales × 100

### Social and ethical objectives

Some businesses aim to provide a service to the community, pay fair wages, buy from local farmers or protect the environment. Public sector organisations aim to provide services such as water and electricity. A **social enterprise** has social objectives as well as profit objectives, and uses its profit to help a community.

## Why objectives are important

- They give the business a **clear direction**: everyone works towards the same goal.
- They help managers **make decisions**: a choice is good if it helps to meet the objective.
- They **motivate** employees, who know what they are aiming for.
- They allow managers to **measure performance** by comparing results with the target.
- They help to persuade **lenders and investors**, who want to see clear targets in a business plan.

Objectives work best when they are **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic and Time-bound. "Increase sales revenue by 10% in the next 12 months" is SMART; "do better" is not.

## Judging whether an objective has been met

Because good objectives are measurable, you can check them with simple calculations.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a sales revenue objective]
Rudo set her bakery the objective "increase sales revenue by 10% this year". Last year sales revenue was US\$150,000; this year it was US\$163,500.

1. Increase in revenue: US\$163,500 − US\$150,000 = US\$13,500.
2. Percentage increase: 13,500 ÷ 150,000 × 100 = 9%.
3. Revenue needed for a 10% rise: US\$150,000 + 10% of US\$150,000 = US\$165,000.
4. Rudo missed her objective by US\$165,000 − US\$163,500 = US\$1,500 (one percentage point).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: market share and profit objectives]
Tatenda's printing business in Masvingo aims for a 15% share of the town's printing market and a profit this year. Its sales were US\$72,000, the total market was worth US\$480,000 and its total costs were US\$61,500.

1. Market share = 72,000 ÷ 480,000 × 100 = 15%: the market share objective is met.
2. Profit = revenue − total costs = US\$72,000 − US\$61,500 = US\$10,500: the profit objective is also met.
:::

## How and why objectives change

Objectives are not fixed. They change when the situation of the business changes:

- **Age of the business:** survival at first, then profit and growth once it is established.
- **Economic conditions:** in a recession the aim may move back to survival; in a boom, growth.
- **Competition:** a new rival may force a business to protect its market share or simply to survive.
- **Change of ownership:** a sole trader who forms a company may face shareholders who want higher profit and dividends.
- **Public opinion and laws:** pressure on businesses to act responsibly may add environmental or social objectives.

:::example{title="Objectives of different organisations"}
- A new tuck shop in Bulawayo: **survival** in its first year.
- A family transport business with three lorries: **profit**, then **growth** by adding vehicles.
- A dairy co-operative near Marondera: fair prices for its members and services to them (a **social** objective) as well as a surplus.
- A state-owned water utility: providing a reliable **service** to the public at an affordable price.
:::

### Conflicting objectives

A business may find that its objectives pull against each other. Cutting prices to gain **market share** may reduce **profit** in the short run. Paying higher wages or buying from responsible suppliers (an ethical objective) raises costs. Managers must decide which objective comes first at that time.

## ZIMSEC Business Enterprise and Skills: objectives of an enterprise

The BES syllabus links objectives to the enterprise and its owner. Expect questions such as "state four objectives of a small enterprise" or "explain why an enterprise should set objectives". Personal objectives of an enterpriser (independence, income, using a talent) often sit alongside business objectives.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying every business only aims for profit"}
New businesses aim to survive, social enterprises and co-operatives have social aims, and public sector organisations aim to provide a service. Match the objective to the business in the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sales revenue and profit"}
Sales revenue is the value of sales; profit is revenue minus total costs. A business can increase its revenue and still make a loss.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calculating market share from the wrong total"}
Divide the business's sales by the total sales of the **whole market**, not by the sales of one rival.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Apply to the business in the case"}
"Identify two objectives" earns marks for naming them. "Explain two objectives suitable for this business" needs a reason linked to the case: "survival, because it opened only six months ago and has a large bank loan".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain why objectives change"}
Use a trigger and an effect: "a new competitor has arrived, so the business may change from growth to protecting its market share".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 (1.5.1) expects the full range of objectives, why they are important, how they change and the objectives of social enterprises and the public sector. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 1) concentrates on the objectives of an enterprise and why they are set.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Objectives are targets that guide a business's decisions.
- Common objectives: survival, profit, growth, sales revenue, market share, and social and ethical aims.
- Objectives give direction, guide decisions, motivate staff, allow performance to be measured and reassure lenders.
- Good objectives are SMART, so you can check them with calculations such as market share and profit.
- Objectives change with the age of the business, the economy, competition, ownership and public opinion, and they can conflict.
:::
