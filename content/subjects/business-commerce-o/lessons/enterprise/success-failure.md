## Introduction

Many new businesses open every year in Zimbabwe, from tuck shops and welding workshops to transport services and online sellers. Some grow into well-known local names; many close within their first few years. Why does one hair salon in Mutare prosper while another on the same street closes after a few months?

Understanding the causes of failure helps an enterpriser avoid them. Examiners ask you to explain causes of failure, to identify the cause from a case study, to suggest solutions and to explain why new businesses are especially at risk. Business Enterprise and Skills studies the causes of failure in Form 2; Cambridge 0264 covers why businesses succeed and fail in topic 1.3.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business failure"}
When a business has to close because it cannot pay its debts or cannot make enough profit to continue.
:::

:::definition{term="Insolvency"}
Being unable to pay debts when they fall due. A profitable business can still become insolvent if it runs out of cash.
:::

:::definition{term="Liquidation"}
Closing a company and selling its assets to pay its creditors.
:::

## Causes of business failure

### Internal causes (within the control of the business)

- **Poor management**: lack of planning, poor decisions, no clear objectives, inexperienced owners who try to do everything themselves.
- **Poor record keeping**: no accurate records of sales, costs, stock and debtors, so the owner does not know whether the business is making a profit or who owes money. Mixing personal and business money is a common example.
- **Lack of finance and poor cash flow**: too little start-up capital, too much stock, too much credit given to customers, so the business cannot pay suppliers, rent or wages.
- **Unsuitable product or poor quality**: products customers do not want, or of poor quality, so customers do not return.
- **Poor location**: too few customers pass by, or rent is too high.
- **Poor marketing**: customers do not know the business exists, or prices are wrong.

### External causes (outside the control of the business)

- **Low or falling demand**: customers' tastes change or incomes fall.
- **Economic change**: recession, rising inflation, rising interest rates or a change in exchange rates.
- **Competition**: a large rival opens nearby with lower prices or better service.
- **Changes in government policy and laws**: higher taxes, new regulations, licence costs.
- **Natural disasters and other shocks**: drought for farmers, floods, power cuts.

:::example{title="Why Nyasha's restaurant failed"}
Nyasha opened a restaurant in Gweru with a small loan. She did not keep records, so she did not notice that the cost of ingredients was rising faster than her prices. She gave credit to regular customers who paid late. When a fast-food outlet opened across the road, her sales fell. She could not pay her rent and had to close. Her failure had **internal** causes (poor records, poor cash management, prices too low) and an **external** cause (new competition).
:::

## Why new businesses are especially at risk

| Problem | Why it is greater for a new business |
| --- | --- |
| Lack of experience | The owner may not know how to manage staff, stock or money |
| Little finance | Savings and small loans leave no reserve for bad months |
| Few customers | The business is not yet known and has no loyal customers |
| Cash-flow problems | Start-up costs are paid before sales build up |
| Strong established rivals | Competitors have loyal customers and lower costs |

## Why businesses succeed

Successful businesses tend to:

- **Understand their market**: research what customers want and how much they will pay.
- **Plan**: a business plan with clear objectives, forecasts and budgets.
- **Keep accurate records** and separate business and personal money.
- **Manage cash carefully**: control stock and credit, and keep a cash reserve.
- **Offer a good product and service** that meets customers' needs, often with something that makes them different (a unique selling point).
- **Adapt** to change: new products, new markets, new technology.
- **Have good management and motivated staff.**

## Solutions to the causes of failure

| Cause | Possible solution |
| --- | --- |
| Poor management | Training in business skills; hire experienced managers; seek advice from support agencies and mentors |
| Poor record keeping | Keep books every day; use simple accounting software or a bookkeeper; separate personal and business money |
| Lack of finance or cash | Plan with a cash budget (cash flow forecast); limit credit to customers; negotiate credit from suppliers; keep a cash reserve |
| Unsuitable product, low demand | Market research before launching; improve quality; develop new products; find new markets |
| Competition | Differentiate (better service, quality, location); control costs; build customer loyalty |
| Economic change | Plan for different situations; reduce costs; spread risk with more products or markets |

Note that the business can control the internal causes, but it can only **respond** to external causes, not prevent them.

:::example{title="Why Chipo's catering business succeeded"}
Chipo started a catering business in Harare with a small loan. Before opening, she asked event organisers what they wanted and set prices from her costs. She kept a record of every order and payment, asked customers for a deposit before each event, and saved part of her profit as a reserve. When the price of cooking oil rose, her records showed the effect at once, so she adjusted her prices. When a rival opened, she kept customers by offering reliable delivery and menus for small family events. Planning, records and cash control helped her survive changes that closed less careful rivals.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying \"lack of profit\" without explaining why"}
"The business failed because it made no profit" restates the problem. Explain the cause: poor records, high costs, low demand, competition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming profitable businesses cannot fail"}
A business can be profitable on paper but fail because it runs out of **cash**, for example if customers pay late.
:::

:::mistake{title="Solutions that do not match the cause"}
Advertising will not solve a cash-flow problem caused by giving too much credit. Match each solution to the cause in the case.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Find the evidence in the case"}
Case studies hide clues: "she kept no receipts" (poor records), "a supermarket opened nearby" (competition), "customers paid late" (cash flow). Quote the clue and explain its effect.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Internal or external?"}
Sorting causes into internal and external helps you explain which ones the owner could have prevented.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 2) asks for causes of business failure and solutions, often for a small enterprise. Cambridge 0264 (1.3.4) asks why businesses succeed and fail, and why new businesses are at greater risk.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses fail when they cannot pay their debts or make enough profit to continue.
- Internal causes: poor management, poor records, lack of finance and cash, unsuitable products, poor location and marketing.
- External causes: low demand, economic change, competition, government policy, natural shocks.
- New businesses are at greater risk because of inexperience, little finance, few customers and established rivals.
- Success comes from understanding the market, planning, good records, cash control, quality and adaptability; each cause of failure has a matching solution.
:::
