## Introduction

Chipo wants to open a small bakery in Gweru and needs a loan. When she visits the bank, the first thing the loans officer asks for is her **business plan**. Without one, the bank cannot judge whether her idea will work or whether she can repay the loan. Writing the plan also forces Chipo to think through every part of her business before she spends any money.

All three syllabuses examine the business plan. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) asks for its contents and design; Business Enterprise and Skills asks you to draft a mini business plan (Form 1) and develop a full one (Form 3); Cambridge 0264 (topic 1.3) asks about its main parts and why it helps an entrepreneur.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business plan"}
A written document that describes a business, its objectives, its market and its strategies, and contains financial forecasts showing how the objectives will be achieved.
:::

:::definition{term="Executive summary"}
A short overview at the start of the plan that summarises the whole plan, so a reader can quickly understand the idea and the finance needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Financial plan (financial forecast)"}
The part of the plan that sets out start-up costs, sources of finance, and forecasts of sales, costs, profit and cash flow.
:::

:::definition{term="Break-even output"}
The level of output at which total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss.
:::

## Purpose and importance of a business plan

1. **To obtain finance:** banks, investors and grant schemes require a plan before they lend or invest.
2. **To think things through:** the owner researches the market, costs and competitors, reducing the risk of failure.
3. **To set objectives:** the plan gives clear targets for sales and profit.
4. **To guide decisions:** it acts as a road map once the business starts.
5. **To monitor performance:** actual results can be compared with the forecasts, so problems are spotted early.
6. **To communicate:** it explains the idea to partners, employees, suppliers and landlords.

**Limitations:** forecasts are estimates and may be wrong; conditions change, so the plan must be updated; writing a good plan takes time and skill; and a plan does not guarantee success.

## Main sections of a business plan

| Section | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| **Executive summary** | The business idea, objectives, target market, finance needed and expected profit, in a page or less |
| **Business (organisational) background** | Name, location, form of ownership, the owners and their experience, mission and objectives |
| **Products or services** | What will be sold, its features and what makes it different (unique selling point) |
| **Market research and analysis** | Target customers, size of the market, competitors, results of surveys |
| **Marketing plan** | Price, promotion, place (how customers will be reached) and product; sales forecast |
| **Operations plan** | Premises, equipment, suppliers, production method, opening hours |
| **Management and people** | Who will manage, staff needed, their skills, wages and organisation chart |
| **Financial plan** | Start-up costs, sources of finance, forecast income statement (profit), cash flow forecast (cash budget), break-even |

Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills list "strategy and implementation" (how and when the plan will be carried out) as a section; Cambridge groups the same content under marketing and operations.

## The financial plan: worked examples

The financial plan shows a reader whether the business can make a profit and how much money is needed.

:::worked-solution{title="Start-up costs and the finance needed"}
Chipo lists the costs of opening her bakery.

| Start-up cost | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Oven and trays | 3,200 |
| Mixer | 950 |
| Shop fittings | 1,100 |
| First month's flour and ingredients | 700 |
| Licence and registration | 500 |
| **Total start-up costs** | 6,450 |

1. Total start-up costs = 3,200 + 950 + 1,100 + 700 + 500 = **US\$6,450**.
2. Chipo has savings of US\$4,000.
3. Finance needed from other sources = US\$6,450 − US\$4,000 = **US\$2,450**. Her plan asks the bank for this amount and shows how it will be repaid.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Forecast profit and break-even"}
Chipo plans to sell bread at US\$1.20 a loaf. Variable cost (ingredients, packaging) is US\$0.70 a loaf. Fixed costs (rent, wages, loan repayment) are US\$1,500 a month. She forecasts sales of 4,000 loaves a month.

1. Contribution per loaf = price − variable cost per unit = 1.20 − 0.70 = **US\$0.50**.
2. Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit = 1,500 ÷ 0.50 = **3,000 loaves a month**.
3. Revenue = 4,000 × 1.20 = US\$4,800.
4. Total costs = fixed costs + total variable costs = 1,500 + (4,000 × 0.70) = 1,500 + 2,800 = US\$4,300.
5. Forecast profit = revenue − total costs = 4,800 − 4,300 = **US\$500 a month**.
6. Margin of safety = 4,000 − 3,000 = **1,000 loaves**: sales could fall by 1,000 loaves before she makes a loss.
:::

A lender reading this would see that Chipo expects a profit but that it depends on selling at least 3,000 loaves, so the market research section must convince the reader that this is realistic.

## Drafting a simple (mini) business plan

For a school or community project, a mini plan on one or two pages may include:

1. **Name and idea:** what you will sell and to whom.
2. **Objectives:** for example "sell 200 freezits a week in Term 2".
3. **Market:** who your customers are; competitors nearby.
4. **Marketing:** price, where you will sell, how you will advertise.
5. **Resources:** materials, equipment, people and who does what.
6. **Finance:** start-up costs, where the money comes from, expected revenue, costs and profit.
7. **Timeline:** when each task will be done.

:::example{title="A mini plan in outline"}
Form 2 pupils at a school in Mutare plan to make and sell freezits at break time. Objective: a profit to buy sports kit. Market: pupils; the tuck shop sells only cold drinks. Marketing: a price below the cold drinks, posters in classrooms. Resources: a borrowed freezer, a parent's kitchen, four pupils sharing jobs. Finance: start-up costs from a class contribution, repaid in the first three weeks. Timeline: research in week 1, production from week 2, review each Friday.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking the plan is only for the bank"}
The plan also helps the owner make decisions, set targets and check progress.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing profit with cash"}
The forecast income statement shows **profit**; the cash flow forecast shows whether the business will have **cash** to pay its bills each month. Both are needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting fixed costs in the profit forecast"}
Profit is revenue minus **total** costs, including fixed costs such as rent and wages.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Purpose questions"}
"Explain why a business plan is important to an entrepreneur" needs reasons from the owner's point of view (planning, objectives, monitoring) as well as finance.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the formulas"}
Show the formula, the substitution and the answer with units: contribution per unit, break-even output, profit, margin of safety.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills list the plan's sections as executive summary, organisational background, products, market analysis, strategy and implementation, management and financial plan. Cambridge 0264 expects the main parts and why the plan helps an entrepreneur obtain finance and reduce risk.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A business plan describes the business, its market and strategies, with financial forecasts.
- It helps obtain finance, reduce risk, set objectives, guide decisions and monitor performance.
- Main sections: executive summary, background, products, market analysis, marketing, operations, management and people, financial plan.
- The financial plan shows start-up costs, finance needed, forecast profit, cash flow and break-even.
- A plan is only as good as its forecasts and must be reviewed as conditions change.
:::
