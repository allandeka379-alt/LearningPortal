## Introduction

When a new university campus opened in a fictional town, Nyasha saw an opportunity: hundreds of students would need cheap meals, printing and phone repairs. But she also saw risks: students go home during holidays, other traders would arrive quickly, and the council might not grant a licence near the gate.

Every enterprise begins with an **opportunity** and every enterprise faces **risks**. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) asks you to explain how opportunities arise, to identify the risks enterprisers face, and to **assess the likelihood and the impact** of those risks. This lesson gives you a simple method for doing that.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business opportunity"}
A favourable situation in which a business could profitably meet a need or want that is not yet being met, or met well.
:::

:::definition{term="Business risk"}
The possibility that an event will cause a business to make a loss or fail to reach its objectives.
:::

:::definition{term="Likelihood"}
How probable it is that a risk will actually happen.
:::

:::definition{term="Impact (magnitude)"}
How serious the effect on the business would be if the risk happened.
:::

:::definition{term="Real income"}
Income measured by what it can buy, after allowing for changes in prices.
:::

## How opportunities arise

| Source of opportunity | How it creates an opportunity | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unmet need or demand** | Customers want something that is not available nearby, or is poor quality | No hardware shop in a growing township |
| **Ability to meet the demand** | The enterpriser has the skills, resources or contacts others lack | A trained mechanic opens a repair workshop |
| **New technology** | New products and cheaper ways of working | Mobile money agents; online selling; solar equipment |
| **Research and development (R&D)** | Invention of new products or improved methods | A new drought-tolerant seed variety creates demand for seed sellers |
| **Areas for expansion** | Existing businesses spot related products or new markets | A bakery adds cakes for weddings |
| **Changes in taste and fashion** | Consumers want new styles or healthier products | Demand for natural hair products or traditional foods |
| **Changes in size and structure of population** | More people, more young people or more urban dwellers change demand | Growth of towns increases demand for transport and rented rooms |
| **Changes in real income** | Rising incomes increase demand for services and better goods; falling incomes increase demand for cheaper goods and repairs | More demand for second-hand clothes when incomes fall |
| **Globalisation** | Access to foreign suppliers and export markets | Exporting crafts or importing cheaper inputs |
| **Government policy** | Incentives, contracts, infrastructure or new rules | A new rule requiring safety equipment creates demand for suppliers |

:::example{title="One change, several opportunities"}
A new tarred road links a farming area to Mutare. Farmers can now reach the city market (opportunity for transporters), city traders can come to buy produce (opportunity for a collection point), and travellers pass through (opportunity for a fuel station and food stalls).
:::

## Risks enterprisers face

- **Market risks:** too little demand; customers' tastes change; new competitors cut prices.
- **Financial risks:** running out of cash; being unable to repay loans; customers not paying debts; rising costs.
- **Operational risks:** machines breaking down; unreliable suppliers; power or water cuts; theft; fire.
- **Economic risks:** inflation, changes in exchange rates, falling incomes, recession.
- **Legal and political risks:** new taxes or regulations; loss of a licence; changes in government policy.
- **Natural risks:** drought, floods, pests and disease (especially for farming enterprises).
- **People risks:** losing a key worker; dishonest staff; disputes between partners.
- **Growth risks:** expanding too fast, losing control of quality or cash.

## Judging likelihood and impact

Not every risk deserves the same attention. A simple way is to rate each risk on two scales and multiply:

- **Likelihood:** 1 = unlikely, 2 = possible, 3 = likely.
- **Impact:** 1 = minor, 2 = serious, 3 = severe.
- **Risk rating** = likelihood × impact (1 to 9).

| Risk rating | Action |
| --- | --- |
| 6 to 9 (high) | Act now: reduce the risk or avoid it |
| 3 to 4 (medium) | Plan a response and monitor |
| 1 to 2 (low) | Accept and watch |

:::worked-solution{title="Rating Nyasha's risks"}
Nyasha rates three risks for her student food stall.

1. **Sales fall in the holidays:** almost certain (3); serious, as sales drop sharply (2). Rating 3 × 2 = **6 (high)**. Response: sell to staff and nearby workers, or reduce stock in holiday months.
2. **A competitor opens next door:** possible (2); serious (2). Rating 2 × 2 = **4 (medium)**. Response: build customer loyalty with quality and student meal deals.
3. **Fire destroys the stall:** unlikely (1); severe (3). Rating 1 × 3 = **3 (medium)**. Response: buy a fire extinguisher and insure the equipment.

The holiday fall in sales is her top priority, even though fire is the most severe event.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Comparing two business ideas"}
Tatenda compares two ideas by adding the ratings of their main risks.

1. **Idea A, mobile phone repairs:** parts unavailable (2 × 2 = 4); customers' phones damaged (1 × 3 = 3). Total = **7**.
2. **Idea B, broiler chickens:** disease kills the flock (2 × 3 = 6); feed prices rise (3 × 2 = 6). Total = **12**.
3. Idea B is riskier on these ratings. Tatenda should choose Idea A, or start Idea B only with strict hygiene, vaccination and a feed supplier contract.
:::

### Ways of reducing risk

- **Research** the market before starting (market research reduces market risk).
- **Start small** and grow step by step.
- **Insure** against fire, theft and accidents.
- **Diversify:** offer more than one product or serve more than one market.
- **Keep records** and a **cash budget** to spot financial problems early.
- Choose a form of business with **limited liability** for high-risk ventures.
- Sign **contracts** with reliable suppliers and customers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing an opportunity with a product idea"}
An opportunity is a **situation** (unmet demand, a change). A product idea is a way of using it. Explain the situation first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rating only impact"}
A severe but very unlikely risk may matter less than a moderate risk that is almost certain. Consider both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming risks without a response"}
Strong answers state the risk, judge it and say how to reduce it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Link source to example"}

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Show the rating"}
If asked to assess risks, state the likelihood and impact you have chosen, multiply, and say which risk needs action first.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Opportunities arise from unmet demand, new technology, R&D, changes in taste, population and real income, globalisation and government policy.
- Enterprisers face market, financial, operational, economic, legal, natural and people risks.
- Assess each risk by its likelihood and its impact; a rating of likelihood × impact ranks them.
- Act first on high-rated risks; plan for medium risks; accept low risks.
- Reduce risk through research, starting small, insurance, diversification, records and contracts.
:::
