## Introduction

A clothing business in Harare is offered cotton fabric at a very low price by a supplier that is known to employ children. A grocer in Bulawayo could earn more by adding water to the cooking oil he sells loose. A building contractor is told that a "gift" to an official will win a government contract. Each of these is legal or illegal in different ways, but all of them raise the same question: **is it right?**

Business ethics appear in all three syllabuses. Examiners ask you to define ethics, to identify ethical issues in a case, to explain why behaving ethically matters and to evaluate whether a business should take an ethical decision that costs money. ZIMSEC Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills study ethics in Form 2; Cambridge 0264 covers ethical issues in topic 6.4.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business ethics"}
Moral principles that guide how a business behaves: doing what is right and fair, not only what is legal or profitable.
:::

:::definition{term="Corporate social responsibility (CSR)"}
When a business takes responsibility for the effects of its decisions on society and the environment, going beyond what the law requires.
:::

:::definition{term="Ethical code (code of conduct)"}
A written document setting out how employees of a business are expected to behave, for example on gifts, honesty and treatment of customers.
:::

## Main ethical issues in business

| Issue | Unethical behaviour | Ethical behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wages and working conditions | Paying very low wages, long hours, unsafe workplaces | Fair wages, safe conditions, reasonable hours |
| Child labour | Employing children, so they miss school | Refusing to employ children or buy from suppliers who do |
| Fair prices to suppliers | Forcing small farmers to accept prices below their costs | Paying a fair price and paying on time |
| Honesty with customers | Short weights, false descriptions, misleading adverts, hiding faults | Accurate labels, honest advertising, clear prices |
| Bribery and corruption | Paying officials to win contracts or avoid inspections | Winning business fairly; refusing bribes |
| The environment | Dumping waste, using suppliers that destroy forests | Responsible disposal; environmentally responsible suppliers |
| Tax | Hiding income to avoid paying tax owed | Keeping honest records and paying taxes due |

Some unethical actions are also **illegal** (bribery, short weights, tax evasion). Others are legal but still considered wrong by many people, such as paying the legal minimum when the business could easily afford more, or advertising unhealthy food to children.

:::example{title="An ethical dilemma at a tea estate"}
A tea estate in the Eastern Highlands can buy fertiliser cheaply from a supplier that dumps chemical waste in a river. A more expensive supplier disposes of its waste safely. Buying from the cheaper supplier raises profit, but it supports pollution that harms communities downstream. An ethical business chooses the responsible supplier even though costs rise.
:::

## Corporate social responsibility

CSR means a business considers all its stakeholders, not only its owners. Examples include:

- Building a classroom or a borehole for the local community.
- Training and developing employees.
- Recycling waste and reducing energy use.
- Buying from local suppliers and paying them fairly.
- Supporting health programmes for workers and their families.

## Why ethics matter

### To society

- Workers are treated fairly and safely; children stay in school.
- Consumers are protected from harmful or misleading products.
- The environment is protected for future generations.
- Corruption is reduced, so public money is spent properly.

### To the business

- A good **reputation** attracts customers who prefer to buy from responsible firms.
- It is easier to **recruit and keep** good employees, who are more motivated.
- **Investors and lenders** may prefer ethical businesses.
- It avoids **fines, legal action and boycotts** by pressure groups.
- **Suppliers** treated fairly are loyal and reliable.

## Advantages and disadvantages of being ethical

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Better reputation and customer loyalty | Higher costs (fair wages, responsible suppliers, safe waste disposal) |
| Motivated, loyal employees | Possibly higher prices, so some price-sensitive customers buy elsewhere |
| Fewer legal problems and less pressure-group action | Lower short-term profit, which may upset shareholders |
| Can be used in marketing ("fair trade", "locally sourced") | Rivals that cut corners may undercut the business |
| Attracts some investors | Checking suppliers takes time and money |

Many businesses find that ethical behaviour pays in the **long run**, even if it costs more in the **short run**. Judging this trade-off is the key to evaluation questions.

## Why some businesses behave unethically

Businesses may act unethically because of pressure to cut costs, the hope of short-term profit, weak enforcement of laws, or the belief that customers will not find out. Strong competition can tempt firms to copy a rival's cheaper but harmful practices. Ethical businesses resist these pressures with clear ethical codes, training for employees, careful checks on suppliers and managers who set a good example.

## ZIMSEC Commerce: ethics in trade

The Commerce syllabus links ethics to fair trading: honest weights and measures, accurate descriptions, fair prices, proper receipts, not selling expired goods, and fair treatment of customers who complain. These connect with consumer protection. Questions may ask "describe four unethical practices of traders" or "explain why traders should be ethical".

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating ethical and legal as the same"}
The law sets the minimum. Ethics asks whether an action is right. Some legal actions are unethical, so explain the moral issue, not just the law.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation"}
Ethical behaviour has costs. A strong answer weighs higher costs and lower short-term profit against reputation, loyalty and long-term profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague examples"}
"Be good to people" earns little. Name the issue: "pay suppliers a fair price for their maize", "refuse to buy cocoa produced using child labour".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify the stakeholders affected"}
Ethical questions are really about stakeholders: say who is harmed by the unethical choice and who benefits from the ethical one.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate with time"}
"In the short term costs rise and profit falls, but in the long term the business gains loyal customers and avoids boycotts" is the kind of judgement examiners reward.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 (6.4.1) uses examples such as fair wages, child labour and environmentally responsible suppliers, and asks for the advantages and disadvantages of ethical decisions. ZIMSEC Commerce links ethics to fair trading and consumer protection. Business Enterprise and Skills treats ethics as part of running an enterprise responsibly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business ethics are moral principles guiding what is right, not only what is legal or profitable.
- Key issues: wages and conditions, child labour, fair prices to suppliers, honest advertising, bribery, the environment and tax.
- CSR means taking responsibility for effects on society and the environment.
- Ethics benefit society and the business (reputation, staff, investors, fewer legal problems).
- Being ethical usually costs more in the short run but can increase long-run profit.
:::
