## Introduction

Chipo sells second-hand clothes at a market in Mutare. A few years ago she had to travel to Harare to see what stock a supplier had. Today the supplier sends photographs of new bales to her phone, she orders by voice call or message, pays with mobile money and advertises to her customers on a messaging group. Her phone has become her most important business tool.

**Telecommunication** means sending information over a distance by electronic means: telephone lines, radio signals, satellites, fibre-optic cables and mobile networks. It is an important **aid to trade**, because trade depends on buyers and sellers exchanging information quickly. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 1) asks you to describe telecommunication devices and services, give their advantages and disadvantages, and describe the types of service provider and the services they offer.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Telecommunication"}
The sending and receiving of information (voice, text, pictures, data) over a distance by electronic means.
:::

:::definition{term="Fixed-line operator"}
A business that provides telephone and internet services through cables connected to homes and offices.
:::

:::definition{term="Mobile network operator"}
A business that provides voice calls, text messages, mobile data and related services (such as mobile money) through a wireless network of masts and base stations.
:::

:::definition{term="Internet service provider (ISP)"}
A business that connects customers to the internet, for example through fibre, wireless or satellite links, usually for a monthly fee or data bundle.
:::

## Telecommunication devices and services

| Device or service | What it does | Business uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed (landline) telephone | Voice calls through cables | Office switchboards, customer enquiries, ordering |
| Mobile phone | Voice calls, text messages (SMS), data and apps from almost anywhere with network coverage | Orders, deliveries, payments, contacting staff in the field |
| Internet | Global network linking computers and phones | Email, websites, online selling, research, online banking |
| Email | Electronic messages with attachments | Sending quotations, invoices and documents in seconds |
| Messaging apps and voice-over-internet calls | Text, voice notes, pictures and calls over data | Customer groups, cheap calls, sharing product photographs |
| Video conferencing | Live video meetings over the internet | Meetings with staff, suppliers or customers in other places |
| Fax | Sends a copy of a printed document over a phone line | Now rare; replaced by email and scanned documents |
| Data services (bundles, Wi-Fi, fibre) | Access to the internet | Running online systems and point-of-sale machines |
| Mobile money and USSD services | Payment and transfer of money using a phone | Paying suppliers, receiving payment from customers |

## Advantages of telecommunication for business

- **Speed**: messages arrive instantly, so orders, enquiries and complaints are dealt with quickly.
- **Distance does not matter**: traders can contact suppliers and customers in other towns and countries without travelling.
- **Lower costs**: fewer journeys, less paper and postage.
- **Two-way communication**: instant feedback and negotiation.
- **Wider markets**: businesses can sell online and advertise on social media.
- **Records**: emails and messages provide written evidence of orders and agreements.
- **Payments**: mobile money and online banking speed up trade.

## Disadvantages and problems

- **Cost of equipment and data**: phones, computers, airtime and data bundles cost money.
- **Network problems**: poor coverage in some rural areas, dropped calls and slow connections.
- **Power cuts** stop computers, routers and base stations unless there is back-up power.
- **Security risks**: hacking, scams, viruses and fraud.
- **No written record** for an ordinary voice call unless notes are kept.
- **Misunderstandings**: short messages can be unclear; there is no body language on a phone call.
- **Information overload and distraction**: too many messages; staff using phones for private matters.

:::example{title="Choosing the right service"}
Tendai owns a hardware shop in Masvingo. He uses a landline for the shop's published number, so customers always reach someone at the counter. He uses mobile phones and a messaging group to contact his delivery drivers. He uses email to send quotations to building contractors, because they need a written record. And he uses an internet connection for his point-of-sale machine and online banking.
:::

## Types of service provider

Telecommunication services in Zimbabwe are provided by several kinds of business. Service providers are licensed and regulated by the Postal and Telecommunications Regulatory Authority of Zimbabwe (POTRAZ).

| Provider | Main services offered |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed-line operator | Landline telephones, leased lines for businesses, fixed broadband internet (copper or fibre), public telephones |
| Mobile network operators | Voice calls, SMS, mobile data bundles, mobile money transfers and payments, airtime and bundles sold through agents and vendors |
| Internet service providers | Home and business internet by fibre, wireless or satellite, email services, web hosting |
| Internet cafés and business centres | Internet access, printing, scanning and typing for people without their own equipment |

Mobile network operators have changed how many Zimbabweans trade: vendors sell airtime and data bundles, mobile money agents provide cash-in and cash-out services, and small businesses accept payments by phone.

:::example{title="A vendor's day with mobile services"}
Farai runs a tuck shop in Gweru. In the morning he buys bread from a bakery and pays with mobile money. He also sells airtime and data bundles to his customers, earning a small commission. In the afternoon a customer who has no cash pays for groceries by phone. At night Farai orders stock from a wholesaler by sending a message with his list.
:::

## Choosing a service and a provider

A business chooses telecommunication services in the same way it chooses other methods of communication. It asks how **urgent** the message is, whether a **written record** is needed, how many people must be reached, how **far away** they are, and what it can **afford**. When choosing a provider it compares network **coverage** in the areas where it works, the **cost** of calls and data bundles, the **reliability** of the service during busy periods, and the **extra services** offered, such as mobile money, business lines or fibre connections. Many businesses use more than one provider, so that they can still communicate if one network fails.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing devices without uses"}
"Mobile phone" alone earns little. Say what a business uses it for, such as taking orders from customers or contacting drivers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying telecommunication has no drawbacks"}
Network problems, power cuts, fraud and costs are real problems in many areas. A balanced answer includes them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the regulator with the providers"}
The regulator licenses and supervises providers; it does not sell phone or internet services to the public.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Advantages questions"}
For "explain the advantages of telecommunication to a trader", give a point (speed), then develop it for a trader (orders are placed and confirmed in minutes, so shelves are restocked before goods run out).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Service providers"}
When asked to describe service providers, name the type (fixed-line operator, mobile network operator, internet service provider) and give at least two services each offers. You do not need to name particular companies.
:::
