## Introduction

The managing director of a large company in Harare sends an instruction to change a delivery schedule. It passes through a general manager, a depot manager and a supervisor before reaching the drivers in Mutare. By then the date has been changed, one supervisor has forgotten to pass it on and two drivers misunderstood a technical term. Lorries leave on the wrong day and customers complain. Nothing was wrong with the instruction itself; the problem was **barriers** to communication.

ZIMSEC Commerce studies the enhancers of and barriers to effective communication in Form 2, and Cambridge 0264 covers communication barriers in topic 2.3.2. Examiners ask you to identify a barrier in a case, explain the problems it causes and recommend how to overcome it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Effective communication"}
Communication in which the right message reaches the right receiver at the right time, is understood correctly and receives feedback where needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Communication barrier"}
Anything that stops a message from being received or understood as the sender intended.
:::

:::definition{term="Jargon"}
Technical or specialist words understood by a particular group but not by others.
:::

:::definition{term="Chain of command"}
The line through which instructions pass from the top of an organisation down to the workers.
:::

## What makes communication effective (enhancers)

- A **clear and simple message**, with no unnecessary technical terms.
- The **right medium** for the message and receiver (urgent: phone; record needed: written).
- The **right receiver**, and at the **right time**.
- **Feedback**, so the sender knows the message was understood.
- **Trust and good relationships** between sender and receiver.
- **Short chains of command**, so messages are not distorted.
- **Reliable equipment and networks** (phones, internet, power).
- **Active listening** and attention by the receiver.

## Barriers to communication

| Barrier | Example | Problem caused |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Jargon and technical language** | An accountant tells shop staff to "reduce receivables days" | Receivers do not understand, so they cannot act |
| **Language differences** | A supplier who speaks a different language from the buyer | Messages are misunderstood; orders are wrong |
| **Noise** (physical) | Machines in a factory drown out instructions | Messages not heard or only partly heard |
| **Poor choice of channel** | Sending urgent news by post; a complicated message by text | Message arrives late or is unclear |
| **Faulty equipment and networks** | Power cuts, poor mobile signal, internet failure | Messages are delayed or lost |
| **Attitudes and emotions** | An employee who distrusts the manager ignores instructions; an angry customer will not listen | Messages are ignored or rejected |
| **Long chain of command** | An instruction passes through five levels of management | Message is delayed and distorted at each level |
| **Information overload** | Staff receive dozens of emails a day | Important messages are missed |
| **Lack of feedback** | A notice with no chance to ask questions | Misunderstandings are not discovered |
| **Distance and time** | Branches in different towns; different working hours | Delays in sending and receiving messages |
| **Poorly expressed message** | Vague, badly written or too long | Receiver is confused |

## Problems caused by communication barriers

- **Mistakes** in production, orders and deliveries, which waste time and materials.
- **Lost sales and unhappy customers** when orders are wrong or late.
- **Poor decisions** because managers receive inaccurate or late information.
- **Low motivation** and conflict when employees feel ignored or misinformed.
- **Higher costs**: correcting errors, repeating work, compensating customers.
- **Damage to reputation** with customers and suppliers.

## How to reduce or remove barriers

| Barrier | Solution |
| --- | --- |
| Jargon | Use simple language suited to the receiver; explain technical terms |
| Language differences | Use interpreters or translations; use pictures and diagrams; staff training |
| Noise | Communicate in a quiet place; use written or visual messages in noisy areas |
| Poor channel | Choose the medium to suit urgency, need for a record and receiver |
| Faulty equipment | Maintain equipment; back-up power such as generators or solar; alternative channels |
| Attitudes | Build trust; involve staff in decisions; train managers in listening and communication |
| Long chain of command | Communicate directly with those affected; reduce the number of levels (delayering); use group messages or meetings |
| Information overload | Keep messages short and relevant; mark urgent messages; avoid copying everyone |
| No feedback | Use two-way methods; ask receivers to confirm or repeat key points |

:::example{title="Removing barriers at a manufacturing company"}
A clothing factory in Bulawayo found that machinists often made the wrong garments. Investigation showed three barriers: instructions were shouted over the noise of sewing machines, orders were written in technical language, and messages from the sales office passed through three supervisors. The factory now posts each order on a board with a picture of the garment, uses plain language, and has the production manager brief machinists directly at the start of each shift. Errors fell sharply.
:::

## Barriers in different business situations

| Situation | Typical barriers | Useful responses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large organisation with many levels | Long chain of command, information overload, departments not sharing information | Fewer levels, direct briefings, clear reporting lines, shared online systems |
| Communicating with customers | Jargon in adverts or manuals, language differences, poor timing, unreliable networks | Plain language, local languages, pictures, several channels (phone, messaging, social media) |
| Trading with suppliers in other countries | Language and culture, different time zones, distance, poor connections | Written confirmation of orders, standard order forms, interpreters, agreed times for calls |
| Factory or workshop floor | Noise, workers on different shifts, low literacy among some workers | Briefings before shifts, visual signs, notice boards, supervisors who check understanding |

:::example{title="A misunderstanding with an export customer"}
Nyasha's company in Bulawayo sells packaged dried fruit to a buyer in Mozambique. A telephone order for "two hundred" cartons was heard as "two thousand" over a poor connection, and the extra cartons were packed before anyone checked. Since then, every telephone order is followed by a short written confirmation by email or messaging app, which the buyer must answer before goods are packed. The cost is a few minutes of staff time; the saving is avoiding expensive errors and keeping a valuable customer.
:::

Barriers often occur together. A message from head office to a depot in another town may suffer from a long chain of command, a poor channel and jargon at the same time, so the most effective improvements usually tackle more than one barrier: a short, plain message sent directly to everyone affected, with a request for confirmation.

## Checking that communication has worked

Managers can test whether their communication is effective. They can ask receivers to repeat the key points of an instruction, watch whether the task is then done correctly, count errors in orders and deliveries, and ask staff and customers for their views in short surveys or meetings. If the same mistakes keep happening, the business should look for the barrier behind them rather than blaming individual workers. A falling number of complaints and errors is good evidence that barriers have been reduced.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming barriers without explaining the problem"}
"Jargon" alone earns little. Explain: "the drivers did not understand the technical term, so they loaded the wrong goods".
:::

:::mistake{title="Generic solutions"}
Match the solution to the barrier in the case. Training will not solve a power cut; a generator will not solve jargon.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting feedback"}
Many communication failures happen because the sender never checks understanding. Two-way communication is one of the strongest enhancers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Find the barrier in the case"}
Look for clues: "the factory is very noisy", "the message passed through several managers", "the supplier speaks Portuguese", "the internet was down".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the effect on the business"}
Link the barrier to a business problem: wrong orders, lost sales, lower motivation or higher costs.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2) uses the terms enhancers and barriers of effective communication. Cambridge 0264 (2.3.2) asks for examples and causes of barriers, the problems they cause and how to reduce them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Effective communication delivers a clear message to the right receiver at the right time and is understood.
- Enhancers: clear message, right medium, feedback, trust, short chains of command, reliable equipment.
- Barriers: jargon, language, noise, poor channels, faulty equipment, attitudes, long chains of command, overload, no feedback.
- Barriers cause mistakes, lost sales, poor decisions, low motivation and higher costs.
- Each barrier has a matching solution, such as plain language, the right medium, two-way methods and fewer levels of management.
:::
