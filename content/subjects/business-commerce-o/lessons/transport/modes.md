## Introduction

Tomatoes grown in Mutoko are sold at Mbare Musika the same morning. Coal from Hwange reaches factories and power users hundreds of kilometres away. Flowers cut in the morning can be on sale in Europe the next day. None of this happens without **transport**, the aid to trade that moves goods and people from where they are produced to where they are wanted.

ZIMSEC Commerce teaches the importance and modes of transport in Form 1; Business Enterprise and Skills (BES) covers the importance, modes and choice of transport in Form 2. Questions ask you to list modes, describe their features, compare advantages and disadvantages and match each mode to suitable goods.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transport"}
The movement of goods and people from one place to another. In commerce it is an aid to trade that bridges the gap in **place** between producers and consumers.
:::

:::definition{term="Mode of transport"}
A method of moving goods or people: road, rail, air, water (inland waterways and sea) or pipeline.
:::

:::definition{term="Place utility"}
The extra value goods have because they are where consumers want them. A bag of oranges is worth more in a Harare supermarket than on a farm far from any buyer.
:::

## Importance of transport to commerce

- **Bridges the gap in place**: moves raw materials to factories and finished goods to markets.
- **Widens the market**: producers can sell all over the country and abroad, which encourages large-scale production and specialisation.
- **Makes trade possible**: wholesalers, retailers, importers and exporters all depend on moving goods.
- **Helps stabilise prices**: goods can be moved from areas of surplus to areas of shortage.
- **Creates employment**: drivers, mechanics, railway, airline and port workers.
- **Moves people**: workers travel to jobs, and tourists to places such as Victoria Falls and Nyanga.
- **Supports perishable trade**: fast transport lets fresh produce, flowers and medicines reach buyers in good condition.

## The development of transport

Early trade relied on **human porterage** (head loads), **pack animals** such as donkeys, and later **ox-drawn sledges and scotch carts**, which are still used in many rural areas. Rivers and lakes were crossed by canoe. Railways in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries linked mines and towns to sea ports; motor vehicles and tarred roads followed; air transport and pipelines are the most recent. Each development made transport faster, carried larger loads and reached new markets.

## Road transport

Lorries, vans, buses, cars, motorcycles and scotch carts.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Door-to-door delivery: no need to reload | Small loads compared with rail and sea |
| Flexible routes and times | Delays from traffic, breakdowns and poor roads |
| Reaches rural areas without a railway | Higher accident and theft risk |
| Cheap and fast over short distances | Expensive over long distances for bulky goods |
| Owners can run their own vehicles | Fuel costs and pollution |

**Suitable goods**: perishables over short distances (vegetables, milk, bread), small consignments, goods for shops and homes.

## Rail transport

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Carries heavy, bulky goods cheaply over long distances | Fixed routes and timetables |
| Not affected by road traffic | Not door-to-door: goods often need road transport at each end |
| Safer for bulk goods; fewer accidents | Extra handling at stations increases damage and cost |
| Less pollution per tonne carried | Costly to build and maintain track; slow if wagons are delayed |

**Suitable goods**: coal, minerals, cement, fertiliser, fuel in tankers, grain in bulk, containers.

## Air transport

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Fastest mode over long distances | Most expensive mode |
| Ideal for perishables and urgent goods | Small carrying capacity |
| Low risk of damage and theft; less packing needed | Needs airports; road transport at each end |
| Reaches distant countries quickly | Affected by bad weather; limits on dangerous goods |

**Suitable goods**: cut flowers, fresh fruit and vegetables for export, medicines, mail, documents, valuable or urgent goods (spare parts, jewellery).

## Water transport

**Inland water transport** uses rivers, lakes and canals (for example ferries and boats on Lake Kariba). **Sea transport** uses **liners** (regular routes and timetables), **tramps** (no fixed route, hired for a cargo) and **tankers** and **container ships**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Cheapest mode for very large, heavy loads over long distances | Slowest mode |
| Huge carrying capacity | Limited to coasts, ports and navigable water |
| Main link for international trade | Risks of storms, piracy and delays at ports |
| | A landlocked country such as Zimbabwe must also use foreign ports and land transport |

**Suitable goods**: bulky goods in international trade: machinery, vehicles, fuel, grain, minerals and containers.

## Pipeline transport

Pipelines carry liquids and gases (and some slurries) continuously.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Low running cost once built; continuous flow | Very high cost to build |
| Not affected by traffic or weather | Carries only liquids and gases |
| Few workers needed; little theft or loss when secure | Fixed route; leaks and vandalism cause loss and pollution |

**Suitable goods**: fuel (petrol, diesel), water, natural gas, sewage.

## Combining modes

Many consignments use more than one mode. Road transport usually collects goods at the start and delivers them at the end of a rail, air or sea journey, because only road reaches the door. Using two or more modes for one journey is called **intermodal** transport. It lets a business enjoy the low cost of rail or sea over the long middle stage and the flexibility of road at each end, but every transfer between modes adds handling time, cost and the risk of damage or theft. Containers, studied under trends in transport, reduce these problems.

:::example{title="Matching goods to modes"}
- 600 tonnes of coal from a mine to a factory 400 km away: **rail** (bulky, low value, long distance).
- Crates of fresh tomatoes from Mutoko to Harare: **road** (perishable, short distance, door-to-door).
- Boxes of cut roses to Europe: **air** (highly perishable, long distance, high value).
- Diesel from a port to an inland storage depot, all year: **pipeline** (liquid, continuous flow).
- Tractors from overseas: **sea** to a port, then **road or rail** to Zimbabwe.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="One-word advantages"}
"Road is flexible" earns more when explained: lorries can change route and time and deliver to the customer's door, so goods are not reloaded.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying air is never suitable for cheap goods"}
Air suits goods that are **perishable, urgent or valuable relative to their weight**. Fresh flowers are cheap per stem but perish quickly, so air is the right choice.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that Zimbabwe is landlocked"}
Sea transport still matters to Zimbabwe: imports and exports go through sea ports in neighbouring countries and then by road or rail.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Advantages and disadvantages"}
For "discuss" or "compare", give both sides and link each point to the goods in the question. In a table answer, still use full phrases, not single words.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Suitable goods"}
Learn one or two goods for each mode with the reason (bulky, perishable, liquid, urgent, valuable). These make quick marks in Paper 1 multiple-choice questions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transport bridges the gap in place, widens markets, makes specialisation possible and creates jobs.
- Transport developed from porterage and pack animals to rail, road, air and pipelines.
- Road: flexible and door-to-door, best for short distances and small loads.
- Rail: cheap for bulky goods over long distances, but fixed routes and extra handling.
- Air: fastest, for perishable, urgent or valuable goods; most expensive. Sea: cheapest for huge loads, slowest. Pipeline: liquids and gases, continuous and cheap to run.
:::
