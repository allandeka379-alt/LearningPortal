## Introduction

Consumer organisations can advise and persuade, but only **the law** can force every business to behave fairly. Governments pass laws that make it an offence to sell short measures, describe goods falsely or sell dangerous products, and they appoint inspectors to enforce them. These **legal controls** protect consumers, but they also change how businesses produce, label, price and promote their products.

This topic is shared by two syllabuses. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) asks you to describe the **methods of protecting consumers**, explain the **role of government** and explain how consumers can **protect themselves**. Cambridge IGCSE Business (0264, topic 3.4.1) asks for the **purpose** of legal controls (to protect customers from misleading promotion and faulty goods) and their **effects on marketing**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Legal controls"}
Laws and regulations, enforced by government, that set the rules businesses must follow when they produce, describe, price, promote and sell goods and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Misleading promotion"}
Advertising or other promotion that gives a false impression of a product, its price or its benefits, for example false claims, hidden charges or a "sale" price that was never really reduced.
:::

:::definition{term="Faulty goods"}
Goods that do not work properly, are not of satisfactory quality, are not as described or are unsafe.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-protection"}
The steps consumers take themselves to avoid being cheated or harmed, such as checking goods, reading labels and keeping receipts.
:::

## Methods of protecting consumers by law

### Laws against misleading descriptions and promotion

It is an offence to describe goods or services falsely (their contents, quantity, origin, performance) or to make misleading price claims. Advertisements must not make false claims, for example that a product cures illnesses.

### Laws on quality, safety and faulty goods

Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for their purpose and as described. If they are faulty, the consumer is entitled to a repair, replacement or refund. Dangerous goods can be banned or recalled, and safety standards set for products such as electrical goods, toys and building materials.

### Weights and measures

Laws require scales and measures used in trade to be accurate, and inspectors test and stamp them. Selling less than the stated quantity is an offence.

### Labelling

Packaged goods must show information such as the contents or ingredients, quantity, name and address of the producer, date marks (use-by or best-before) and instructions or warnings.

### Food, drugs and health

Food sold must be fit to eat; health inspectors check food premises; medicines must be approved before sale.

### Price controls

At times governments set maximum prices for essential goods, or require prices to be displayed clearly so consumers can compare.

### Credit and contracts

Laws require credit and hire purchase agreements to show the cash price, the deposit, the instalments and the total cost, and protect buyers from unfair repossession.

## The role of government

Government protects consumers by:

- **passing laws** (legislation) such as those above;
- **enforcing** them through inspectors (weights and measures, health, standards) and the police and courts, with fines and closure for offenders;
- **setting up and funding** consumer protection bodies and regulators;
- **setting standards** for products, often through a national standards body;
- **controlling prices** of some essential goods and services when necessary;
- **educating** consumers through campaigns;
- **licensing** businesses such as food sellers, pharmacies and transport operators.

## Self-protection by consumers

The law works best when consumers protect themselves: check goods, weights and expiry dates; read labels and contracts; compare prices; keep receipts and guarantees; buy from reputable sellers; and report offences.

## Purpose of legal controls (Cambridge focus)

1. **Misleading promotion**: consumers make choices from advertisements and labels. If these are false, consumers waste money and may buy unsuitable or harmful products. Controls make sure customers can trust what they are told.
2. **Faulty goods**: consumers cannot test most products before buying. Controls give them the right to goods that work, are safe and match their description, and to a remedy when they do not.

## Effects of legal controls on businesses and their marketing

| Area of the marketing mix | Effect of legal controls |
| --- | --- |
| Product | Must meet safety and quality standards; testing and redesign raise costs; recalls of faulty products are costly |
| Price | Prices displayed honestly; no misleading "sale" claims; maximum prices may cut profit margins |
| Promotion | Claims must be true and provable; some promotions may be restricted; adverts may need to be changed or withdrawn |
| Place (and packaging) | Labels must give required information; packaging redesigned to meet rules |

**Negative effects on business**: higher costs (testing, labelling, legal advice, refunds), less freedom in promotion, risk of fines, legal cases and bad publicity if rules are broken.

**Positive effects**: honest firms compete on a fair basis; consumer trust increases sales; a reputation for safety and quality can be used in marketing; fewer complaints and returns in the long run.

:::example{title="A juice maker changes its marketing"}
Chipo's business sells fruit drinks in Mutare. Her label says "100% natural fruit juice", but the drink contains added sugar and water. Under laws against false descriptions she must change the label to describe the drink accurately and list the ingredients. Her packaging costs rise and she loses her strongest advertising claim. To compete, she promotes the drink's taste and local fruit instead, and later develops a genuine pure juice that she can honestly advertise.
:::

:::example{title="A recall"}
Tatenda's company imports electric heaters. Tests show that one model can overheat. The law requires unsafe goods to be withdrawn, so he recalls the heaters and refunds buyers. The recall is costly and gets bad publicity, but acting quickly protects his customers, avoids fines and legal claims, and helps him keep customers' trust.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Only describing the law"}
Cambridge questions about **effects** want consequences for the business: costs, changes to the marketing mix, effects on sales and reputation. Describing the law alone earns little.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating legal controls as only bad for business"}
Legal controls add costs, but they also protect honest firms from unfair competition and build consumer confidence. A balanced answer earns evaluation marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing methods with organisations"}
Methods of protection by law (legislation, inspectors, labelling rules) are different from protection bodies (the consumer council, the standards association). Answer what the question asks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge: apply to the case"}
In Paper 2, link each effect to the business in the case: its product, its customers, its promotion. Generic points about "businesses" score low on applied parts.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Commerce: methods and the role of government"}
For "describe methods of protecting consumers", give a range: legislation, enforcement by inspectors, standards, labelling, price controls, consumer bodies and self-protection. For "the role of government", say what government **does**: legislates, enforces, funds bodies, educates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Laws protect consumers against misleading descriptions and promotion, faulty and unsafe goods, short measures, poor labelling, unfair prices and unfair credit terms.
- Government legislates, enforces through inspectors and courts, funds protection bodies, sets standards, controls some prices and educates consumers.
- Consumers protect themselves by checking, comparing, reading labels and keeping receipts.
- Legal controls aim to protect customers from misleading promotion and faulty goods.
- They raise business costs and limit promotion, but also build trust and protect honest firms.
:::
