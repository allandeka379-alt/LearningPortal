## Introduction

Think of how people in Zimbabwe have listened to music: radios and cassette players, then CDs, then music on mobile phones. Each product was new once, sold more and more, reached a peak and then faded as something better arrived. This pattern is called the **product life cycle**.

The life cycle helps a business decide what to do with each product: when to spend heavily on promotion, when to cut prices, when to refresh a product and when to withdraw it. It appears in ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3), Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) and Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.3.1), which asks you to draw and interpret the cycle, explain extension strategies and recommend one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Product life cycle"}
The stages a product passes through from its launch to its withdrawal from the market: introduction, growth, maturity (including saturation) and decline.
:::

:::definition{term="Extension strategy"}
An action taken to stop sales falling and extend the life of a product, usually during maturity, such as changing the product, finding new markets or new uses.
:::

:::definition{term="Saturation"}
The point in maturity when almost every customer who wants the product already has it and many competitors share the market, so sales stop growing.
:::

## The stages of the life cycle

The life cycle is usually drawn as a curve of **sales against time**. Here it is described as a table: sales rise slowly, then quickly, level off and then fall.

| Stage | Sales | Profit | Typical marketing mix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Development (before launch) | None | Losses: research and design costs | Research and testing |
| Introduction | Low, rising slowly | Usually a loss: high launch costs | Informative advertising; skimming or penetration price; few outlets |
| Growth | Rising quickly | Profit begins and rises | Persuasive advertising to build the brand; more outlets; rivals enter |
| Maturity (and saturation) | Highest, then levelling off | Highest; may begin to fall as competition grows | Competitive prices; reminder advertising; extension strategies |
| Decline | Falling | Falling | Less promotion; lower prices; withdraw from some outlets; decide whether to withdraw |

Cash flow follows a similar pattern: it is negative during development and introduction, and only becomes strongly positive in growth and maturity. This is why a business needs products at **different stages**: profits from mature products pay for developing and launching new ones.

:::example{title="A solar lantern through its life cycle"}
A Harare company launches a solar lantern. In **introduction** it advertises on radio to explain how it works and sells through a few hardware shops. In **growth** rural demand rises fast, so it adds more outlets and rivals appear. In **maturity** most households that want one have bought one, so the company cuts its price slightly and runs reminder adverts. When cheaper lanterns with phone-charging points arrive, sales enter **decline**.
:::

## Reading a product life cycle

Exam questions often give a description or a table of sales over several years and ask you to identify the stage:

- Sales low and slow to rise → **introduction**.
- Sales rising steeply each period → **growth**.
- Sales high but increasing only slightly or flat → **maturity / saturation**.
- Sales falling steadily → **decline**.

:::example{title="Identifying the stage from data"}
A drinks firm's sales of a flavoured water (in thousands of bottles) were 20, 60, 140, 210, 230 and 232 in six years. The early years show slow then rapid growth; the last two years show only small increases. The product is in **maturity**, close to saturation, so this is the time to plan an extension strategy.
:::

## Extension strategies

When sales reach maturity, a business can try to delay decline:

| Extension strategy | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Change or improve the product | A new flavour, a larger size, an improved formula, a new design |
| Change the packaging | A modern design or a smaller, cheaper pack size |
| Sell in new markets | New regions, other countries or a new segment (for example, selling to hotels as well as households) |
| Find new uses | Promoting a baking ingredient as a snack topping |
| Change the promotion | A new advertising campaign, a celebrity endorsement, special offers |
| Change the price | A temporary price cut to win customers from rivals |

**Advantages**: extension strategies are usually cheaper and less risky than developing a completely new product, and they keep revenue coming in from a product customers already know.

:::example{title="Extending the life of a cooking oil"}
A cooking-oil brand has sold steadily for years, but sales have stopped growing and two cheaper brands have arrived. The firm launches a 500 ml bottle for customers who cannot afford the 2-litre size, starts supplying restaurants as well as households, and runs a radio campaign stressing that the oil is made from local sunflowers. Sales rise again for several years before levelling off once more.
:::

**Disadvantages**: they cost money; they only **delay** decline, and if the product is outdated (like a cassette player), the money may be wasted. A business must judge whether to extend the life of a product or to let it decline and invest in a new one.

## ZIMSEC: links to the rest of the marketing mix

ZIMSEC Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills questions often ask how the marketing mix changes over the life cycle. Use the table above: price, promotion and place all change stage by stage, so your answer should describe what happens to each P.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying profit is highest at introduction"}
At introduction sales are low and launch costs high, so the product usually makes a loss. Profit is usually highest in maturity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using extension strategies in the introduction stage"}
Extension strategies are used when sales stop growing, usually in maturity, not when the product has just been launched.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming every product follows the same timing"}
Some products, such as fashion items, pass through the cycle in months; others, such as basic foods, stay in maturity for many years.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommending an extension strategy"}
Choose one strategy, explain why it suits the product and its customers in the case, state its cost or risk, and compare it with another option (or with withdrawing the product). End with a clear recommendation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing the diagram in words"}
If asked to draw or describe the life cycle, label the axes (sales on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal) and the four stages in order, with sales rising slowly, then steeply, levelling off and then falling.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The product life cycle has four stages after development: introduction, growth, maturity (saturation) and decline.
- Sales are low at introduction, rise fast in growth, peak in maturity and fall in decline; profit is usually highest in maturity.
- The marketing mix changes at each stage: informative then persuasive then reminder promotion; price and outlets adjusted.
- Extension strategies (new versions, packaging, markets, uses, promotion, price) delay decline, usually in maturity.
- Extension strategies cost money and only delay decline; sometimes a new product is the better choice.
:::
