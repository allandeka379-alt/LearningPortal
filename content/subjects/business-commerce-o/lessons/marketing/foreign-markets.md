## Introduction

Rudo's company makes chilli sauce in Harare. Sales in Zimbabwe are good but no longer growing: most shops already stock it and several rivals compete for the same customers. Rudo notices that travellers take bottles to Zambia, Botswana and South Africa as gifts. Should she start selling there? Selling abroad could double her market, but it brings new problems: different tastes, different labelling rules, foreign currencies and customers who have never heard of her brand.

Entering foreign markets is a method of **growth**. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 2) studies foreign markets as one type of market. Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.3.7) asks about the opportunities and problems of entering new markets abroad and the methods of overcoming them: joint ventures, franchising, local agents and adapting the marketing mix. Exchange rates and trading blocs are covered in other lessons; here the focus is the business decision.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign (international) market"}
Customers in countries other than the one where the business is based.
:::

:::definition{term="Exporting"}
Selling goods or services produced in the home country to customers in other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint venture"}
Two or more businesses, often one local and one foreign, agreeing to share the capital, costs, risks and profits of a new project.
:::

:::definition{term="Franchise"}
An agreement allowing a business (the franchisee) to use another business's (the franchisor's) name, products and methods in return for fees or a share of revenue.
:::

:::definition{term="Agent"}
A person or firm in the foreign country that arranges sales on the business's behalf, usually for a commission.
:::

## Why sell in other countries?

- **More sales and growth**: the home market may be saturated or too small.
- **Spreading risk**: if sales fall at home (for example during a recession or drought), sales abroad may hold up.
- **Economies of scale**: larger output can lower the average cost of each unit.
- **Extending the product life cycle**: a product in maturity at home may be new, and in growth, elsewhere.
- **Higher prices or less competition** in some markets.
- **Earning foreign currency** to pay for imported materials and machines.

:::example{title="Spreading risk across markets"}
Sipho's company makes school furniture in Bulawayo. In a year when schools at home postpone purchases because budgets are tight, orders from schools in Botswana keep the factory busy. Because the business sells in two countries, a fall in demand in one market does not force it to lay off workers or leave machines idle, and fixed costs are spread over more output.
:::

## Problems of entering foreign markets

| Problem | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Cultural differences | Language, religion, tastes, colours and customs differ; a product name, picture or advert that works at home may offend or confuse |
| Lack of knowledge of the market | The business does not know customers, competitors, distribution channels or prices |
| Legal requirements | Different rules on product safety, labelling, ingredients, advertising, import licences and permits |
| Payment and currency | Customers pay in other currencies; exchange rates change; the seller may be paid late or not at all |
| Costs of transport and documents | Distance, border delays, customs documents and import duties raise costs |
| Competition from local firms | Local firms know customers and may have loyal buyers |
| Distance and control | It is harder to manage staff, quality and stock far away |

:::example{title="When a product meets a new culture"}
A Zimbabwean biscuit maker tries to sell a biscuit named after a word that, in the local language of the new market, has an unpleasant meaning. Sales are poor until the product is renamed. Research into language and culture before entry would have avoided the cost.
:::

## Ways of reducing the problems

| Method | How it helps | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market research in the target country | Reveals tastes, prices, competitors and rules before entry | Costly; hard to do from a distance |
| Using a local agent or distributor | Local knowledge, contacts, language and access to shops | Commission reduces profit; less control |
| Joint venture with a local firm | Shares costs and risks; local partner knows the market and the law | Profits shared; partners may disagree |
| Franchising | Local franchisees invest their own money and know the area; fast growth | Less control over quality; fees are only part of the profit |
| Adapting the marketing mix | Change the product, packaging, language on labels, price and promotion to suit local customers | Adaptation costs money and loses some economies of scale |
| Selling online first | Tests demand at low cost | Delivery, customs and trust problems |
| Using secure payment methods and insurance | Reduces the risk of non-payment and loss in transit | Adds costs |

:::example{title="Reducing the problems for chilli sauce"}
Rudo decides to start in Zambia, where many customers already know Zimbabwean products. She appoints a Lusaka distributor who supplies supermarkets (local knowledge), changes her labels to meet Zambian labelling rules (legal requirements) and asks to be paid in advance for the first orders (payment risk). She keeps her recipe but offers a milder version after the distributor reports that some customers find it too hot (adapting the product).
:::

## Deciding whether to enter a foreign market

A good decision weighs:

- The **size and growth** of the foreign market compared with the home market.
- The **costs** of entry (research, adaptation, transport, documents, duties) against expected revenue.
- The **risks** (currency, payment, political and legal) and how they can be reduced.
- The business's own **resources**: finance, production capacity and managers with time and skills.
- Whether a **partner** (agent, joint venture, franchise) would reduce risk enough.

Starting small (one country, one channel, one product) and expanding when successful is often the safest approach.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming the same marketing mix works everywhere"}
Tastes, incomes, laws and media differ. Explain what must change and why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a joint venture with a franchise"}
In a joint venture, both businesses invest and share profits in a new project. In a franchise, the franchisee pays to use an existing business's name and methods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without solutions"}
Questions often ask how a problem can be **overcome**. Pair each problem with a practical method.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Apply to the case"}
Name the country or customers in the case, the specific problem (for example, labelling rules for food) and a specific solution (a local distributor who knows those rules). Then judge whether the benefits outweigh the costs.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Cambridge 0264 names joint ventures, franchising, local agents and adapting the marketing mix as ways of overcoming problems. Business Enterprise and Skills treats foreign markets alongside local and regional markets, so link your answer to regional trade within Southern Africa where it fits.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses enter foreign markets to grow, spread risk, gain economies of scale, extend the product life cycle and earn foreign currency.
- Problems include cultural differences, lack of market knowledge, legal requirements, payment and currency risks, costs and local competition.
- Solutions include research, local agents or distributors, joint ventures, franchising, adapting the marketing mix and secure payment.
- Each solution has drawbacks: shared profit, commission, less control or extra cost.
- A sound decision compares market size and profit with costs, risks and the business's resources, often starting small.
:::
