## Introduction

A cellphone network does not sell the same bundle to everyone. It offers cheap night-time data bundles that appeal to students, business bundles with extra calls for traders and small firms, and WhatsApp-only bundles for customers who mainly send messages. The network has divided its customers into groups with different needs. This is **market segmentation**.

Segmentation is examined in all three syllabi: ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2), Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) and Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.1.4). Expect questions that ask you to identify how a market has been segmented, explain why segmentation helps a business, and suggest how a particular business could segment its market.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market segment"}
An identifiable group of customers within a market who share similar characteristics and needs.
:::

:::definition{term="Market segmentation"}
Dividing a market into segments so that a business can target each group with suitable products, prices, promotion and distribution.
:::

:::definition{term="Target market"}
The segment (or segments) of customers that a business chooses to sell to.
:::

## Ways of segmenting a market

| Basis | How customers are grouped | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age | Children, teenagers, young adults, middle-aged, elderly | Toy makers target children (and parents); funeral policies target older adults |
| Income | Low, middle and high income groups | Small, cheap sachets of washing powder for low-income buyers; luxury cars for high-income buyers |
| Location (geographic) | Urban and rural areas, provinces, countries, climate | Farm inputs marketed in rural areas; heavier blankets marketed in colder highland areas |
| Gender | Male and female customers | Separate ranges of toiletries and clothing |
| Lifestyle | How people live: health-conscious, busy workers, sporty, environmentally aware | Ready-to-eat meals for busy office workers; gym memberships for the sporty |
| Interests (hobbies) | What people enjoy doing | Fishing equipment for anglers at Kariba; football kits for supporters of a club |

Businesses often combine several bases. A private school, for example, targets families with **children** of a certain **age**, with **high incomes**, living in or near a particular **location**.

:::example{title="Segmenting the market for bread"}
A bakery in Harare could segment its market:
- **by income**: a cheap standard loaf for low-income households and a premium seeded loaf for higher-income buyers;
- **by lifestyle**: wholemeal and low-sugar breads for health-conscious customers;
- **by location**: deliveries to tuck shops in high-density suburbs and to supermarkets in low-density suburbs;
- **by age**: soft, sweet buns packed for school lunchboxes.

Each segment gets a product, price, promotion and place suited to it.
:::

### What makes a useful segment?

Not every group of customers is worth targeting. A useful segment is:

- **identifiable**: the business can tell who belongs to it, for example by age or where they live;
- **large enough** to be profitable after the extra costs of a special product and promotion;
- **reachable**: the business can communicate with it, for example through a radio programme, a school or a social-media group;
- **different** in its needs from other segments, so that a special product or price makes sense.

A bakery might notice that a few customers want bread shaped like animals, but if there are only a handful of them it would not pay to create a separate product for them.

## Why businesses segment their markets

1. **Meet customers' needs more closely**: products are designed for each group, so customers are more satisfied and more likely to stay loyal.
2. **Target marketing spending**: promotion reaches the people most likely to buy, so less is wasted. For example, advertising school shoes on a radio programme that parents listen to.
3. **Set suitable prices**: high-income segments may pay more for premium versions; low-income segments need affordable sizes.
4. **Find new opportunities**: a segment whose needs are not met (a gap in the market) may be profitable.
5. **Increase sales and market share** by selling different versions to several segments.

Once a business has chosen its target segments, it adapts the whole marketing mix for each one: the **product** (size, features, design), the **price**, the **promotion** (message and media) and the **place** where the product is sold. Segmentation is therefore the first step in planning a marketing strategy.

## Advantages and disadvantages of segmentation

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Products match customers' needs, increasing sales and loyalty | Market research to identify segments is expensive and takes time |
| Promotion is targeted, so it is more cost-effective | Several product versions raise production, stock and design costs |
| Prices can be set to suit each group, increasing revenue | Smaller output per version means fewer economies of scale |
| Gaps in the market can be identified | Segments may be too small to be profitable |
| Helps a small business focus on a niche | Customers in a segment do not all behave alike, so targeting may miss |

## Segmentation, niche and mass marketing

A business that targets one small segment is using **niche marketing**. A business that ignores segments and sells one product to everyone is using **mass marketing**. Many large businesses segment their market and target several segments with different versions of their product.

:::example{title="Segmentation at a bus company"}
An intercity bus company running between Harare and Bulawayo offers standard seats at low fares for students and price-conscious travellers (income), luxury coaches with meals and Wi-Fi for business travellers (lifestyle and income), and a parcel service for traders who send goods to customers in other towns (interests and needs). It advertises student fares at colleges and luxury coaches in business areas: promotion matched to each segment.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a segment with a product"}
A segment is a group of **customers**, not a product. "Budget bread" is a product; "low-income households" is a segment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing bases without examples"}
For "explain how a business could segment its market", name the basis, give the groups and show how the business would treat each group differently.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the costs"}
Segmentation is not free. Research, extra product versions and separate promotions all cost money.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Apply to the business"}
Choose bases that fit the product in the case. Income and age suit most consumer goods; location suits farm inputs and transport; lifestyle and interests suit leisure and health products.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 2) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) ask for the bases and reasons for segmentation. Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.1.4) also asks for the benefits and limitations and how segmentation helps a business identify its target market.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Segmentation divides a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics and needs.
- Bases include age, income, location, gender, lifestyle and interests.
- Segmentation helps meet needs, target promotion, set prices and find gaps in the market.
- It costs money for research and extra product versions, and may reduce economies of scale.
- Targeting one segment is niche marketing; ignoring segments is mass marketing.
:::
