## Introduction

Early on a Saturday morning, tomato farmers arrive at Mbare Musika in Harare with their crates. Traders and vendors arrive to buy. If many farmers bring tomatoes and few buyers come, prices fall quickly. If the rains have damaged crops and few crates arrive, buyers compete and prices rise. Nobody sets the price on a notice board: it is set by **demand and supply**.

Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 1) asks for the meaning of market, product, demand and supply and how they interact to set price. You will not have to draw a diagram here: questions give a **demand and supply schedule** (a table) or describe a diagram in words, and ask you to find the price at which the market settles, or to explain what happens when demand or supply changes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market"}
Any place or arrangement where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods or services. It may be a physical place, such as a market stall, or a virtual one, such as a website.
:::

:::definition{term="Product"}
A good or service offered to customers to satisfy their needs and wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a product that consumers are willing and able to buy at each price over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply"}
The quantity of a product that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each price over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium price"}
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, so the market clears with no surplus or shortage.
:::

## The law of demand

As the price of a product **falls**, the quantity demanded usually **rises**; as the price rises, the quantity demanded falls. At a lower price more people can afford the product and some switch from substitutes. A demand curve therefore slopes **down** from left to right.

### What else changes demand?

When one of these changes, the whole demand for the product changes at **every** price:

- **Income**: higher incomes usually raise demand for most products.
- **Prices of substitutes**: if the price of rice rises, demand for maize meal may rise.
- **Prices of complements**: if fuel becomes dearer, demand for cars may fall.
- **Tastes and fashion**, often influenced by advertising.
- **Population**: more people, or more people in a particular age group.
- **Season and weather**: demand for umbrellas rises in the rainy season.

## The law of supply

As the price of a product **rises**, the quantity supplied usually **rises**, because producing and selling it becomes more profitable. A supply curve therefore slopes **up** from left to right.

### What else changes supply?

- **Costs of production** (wages, raw materials, fuel): higher costs reduce supply.
- **Weather and natural conditions**, especially for farm products.
- **Technology**: better methods increase supply.
- **Number of producers** in the market.
- **Taxes and subsidies**: a tax reduces supply; a subsidy increases it.

## How demand and supply set the price

The equilibrium price is found where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

| Price per crate US\$ | Quantity demanded (crates) | Quantity supplied (crates) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 120 | 40 |
| 5 | 100 | 60 |
| 6 | 80 | 80 |
| 7 | 60 | 100 |
| 8 | 40 | 120 |

:::worked-solution[Finding the equilibrium price from a schedule]
1. Look along each row for the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
2. At US\$6, quantity demanded = 80 crates and quantity supplied = 80 crates.
3. Equilibrium price = **US\$6** per crate; equilibrium quantity = **80 crates**.
4. At US\$4, demand (120) is greater than supply (40): a **shortage** of 120 − 40 = 80 crates. Buyers compete, so the price rises.
5. At US\$7, supply (100) is greater than demand (60): a **surplus** of 100 − 60 = 40 crates. Farmers cut prices to sell, so the price falls.
6. The price keeps moving until it reaches US\$6, where there is no shortage or surplus.
:::

In a diagram, this is the point where the downward-sloping demand curve **crosses** the upward-sloping supply curve.

:::worked-solution[An increase in demand]
Before a public holiday, demand for tomatoes rises by 40 crates at every price, while supply stays the same.

| Price per crate US\$ | New quantity demanded (crates) | Quantity supplied (crates) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 160 | 40 |
| 5 | 140 | 60 |
| 6 | 120 | 80 |
| 7 | 100 | 100 |
| 8 | 80 | 120 |

1. At the old price of US\$6, demand is now 120 but supply is only 80: a shortage of 40 crates.
2. Buyers compete, so the price rises.
3. The new equilibrium is where the new demand equals supply: at US\$7, 100 = 100.
4. New equilibrium price = **US\$7**; quantity = **100 crates**. Higher demand has raised both price and quantity.
:::

### Summary of changes

| Change | Effect on equilibrium price | Effect on quantity sold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand increases | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases | Rises | Falls |

:::example{title="A poor harvest"}
A drought reduces the supply of maize to millers. With demand unchanged, supply falls, so the price of maize rises and less is bought. Millers pass on some of the higher cost, so maize-meal prices rise in shops.
:::

## Why this matters to an enterprise

An enterprise that understands demand and supply can:

- set a price customers will accept;
- expect lower prices when many producers supply the same product (for example, many vendors selling the same vegetables);
- plan production around seasons when supply is low and prices are higher;
- see how a rise in its costs will affect what it can supply and the price it can charge.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing demand with want"}
Demand means being **willing and able** to buy. Wanting a car without the money to buy it is not demand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher price reduces demand for all reasons"}
A change in the product's own price changes the **quantity demanded** (a movement along the curve). A change in income, tastes or the price of a substitute changes **demand** at every price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up shortage and surplus"}
Shortage: demand is greater than supply (price below equilibrium). Surplus: supply is greater than demand (price above equilibrium).
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A market is where buyers and sellers meet; a product is a good or service that satisfies needs.
- Demand: what consumers are willing and able to buy at each price; it falls as price rises.
- Supply: what producers are willing and able to sell at each price; it rises as price rises.
- The equilibrium price is where demand equals supply; shortages push prices up, surpluses push them down.
- More demand raises price; more supply lowers price.
:::
