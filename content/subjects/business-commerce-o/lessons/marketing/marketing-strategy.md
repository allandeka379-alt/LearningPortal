## Introduction

Imagine a business that makes excellent organic baby food, sells it at a high price that matches its quality, but distributes it only through rural tuck shops and advertises it on a late-night radio programme. Each decision might make sense alone, but together they fail: the customers who would pay for organic baby food rarely shop or listen there. A marketing mix only works when the **four Ps fit together** and fit the **target market**.

This lesson draws together the separate lessons on product, price, place and promotion. ZIMSEC Commerce (Form 3) and Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 4) study the marketing mix as a whole. Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.3.6) asks you to explain the importance of each element, evaluate how the mix affects a business, and recommend an appropriate marketing strategy. This is one of the most common evaluation questions in Cambridge Paper 2.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marketing mix"}
The combination of product, price, place and promotion that a business uses to meet the needs of its target market.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing strategy"}
A plan that combines the elements of the marketing mix to achieve a marketing objective, such as raising sales or entering a new market segment.
:::

:::definition{term="Target market"}
The group of customers a business aims to sell to.
:::

:::definition{term="Marketing objective"}
A goal for the marketing department, for example to increase market share, launch a new product successfully or raise brand awareness.
:::

## The importance of each element

| Element | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Product | It must meet the customers' needs; without a product customers want, the other Ps cannot save it |
| Price | The only P that earns revenue; it must be affordable for the target market and cover costs; it signals quality |
| Place | Customers must find the product easily, in the right outlets, at the right time |
| Promotion | Customers must know the product exists and want to buy it; it builds the brand image |

## Making the mix consistent

A good mix sends **one consistent message** to the target market:

- A **luxury** product needs a high price, exclusive outlets and stylish promotion in media wealthy customers use.
- A **low-cost** product needs a low price, wide distribution through many outlets and simple, informative or price-based promotion.

:::example{title="Two consistent mixes"}
**Mix A: Premium coffee from the Eastern Highlands.** Product: single-estate coffee in an attractive resealable pack. Price: high (skimming-style, quality image). Place: hotels, upmarket supermarkets and online sales to tourists. Promotion: social media with high-quality photographs, tastings at hotels, the story of the farm.

**Mix B: Budget washing powder.** Product: plain, effective powder in small, affordable sachets. Price: low, competitive with rivals. Place: wholesalers supplying tuck shops and markets across the country. Promotion: radio adverts, posters and "buy 3, get 1 free" offers.
:::

## Building a marketing strategy

A simple process:

1. **Set the marketing objective**: what does the business want to achieve?
2. **Identify the target market**: who are the customers? What do they need, earn and value? Where do they shop and what media do they use? (Market research helps here.)
3. **Design each P** to suit those customers.
4. **Check that the four Ps fit together** and that the business can afford the plan.
5. **Review the results** and adjust: sales, market share, customer feedback.

The strategy also depends on the **stage of the product life cycle** (inform at launch, remind in maturity), on **competitors** (how to be different), and on the business's **budget**.

## Evaluating a marketing mix

When asked to evaluate the impact of a mix, consider:

- **Does each P suit the target market?** For example, is the price affordable for the segment?
- **Are the Ps consistent?** Does the price match the quality and image?
- **What is the effect on sales and profit?** A lower price may raise sales but cut profit per unit; more promotion raises costs.
- **How will competitors react?** A price cut may start a price war.
- **Is the plan affordable** for the business, and will it achieve the objective?

:::example{title="Evaluating a change in the mix"}
Tendai sells packaged maize snacks in Harare. To raise market share, he plans to cut the price from US\$0.50 to US\$0.40 and to spend more on social media adverts aimed at teenagers. Evaluation: the lower price suits teenagers with little money and the social media fits their habits, so sales should rise. But each packet now earns less, the advertising raises costs, and rivals may cut their prices too. If sales rise enough to cover the lower margin and extra costs, the strategy works; Tendai should test it for a month before committing.
:::

## Recommending a marketing strategy

Exam questions often give two options (for example, "cut the price" or "spend more on promotion") or ask for a complete marketing mix for a new product. A strong answer:

- Applies each P to the **business and its customers** in the case.
- Uses **evidence** from the case: market research results, sales figures, competitors' prices.
- Shows **costs and risks** as well as benefits.
- Ends with a **justified recommendation** that says which element matters most and why.

## ZIMSEC: the marketing mix in Commerce and Business Enterprise and Skills

ZIMSEC questions may ask you to "describe the marketing mix" for a named product or business, or to explain how a trader could use each of the four Ps to increase sales. Give one specific action for each P and explain how it would attract customers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the four Ps separately"}
Describing each P without linking them misses the point. Explain how they support each other, for example "the high price matches the premium packaging and exclusive outlets".
:::

:::mistake{title="Generic recommendations"}
"Use social media and lower the price" could apply to any business. Use the case: who the customers are, what rivals charge, what the budget is.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring cost"}
Every change in the mix has a cost or a risk. A recommendation without considering affordability is weak evaluation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Cambridge Paper 2 structure"}
For "Recommend a marketing strategy… Justify your answer", consider at least two elements (or two options), analyse each in the context of the case, and finish with a decision that explains why it is better than the alternative.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
"Describe the marketing mix" asks for what each P is for this product. "Evaluate" asks you to weigh benefits against drawbacks and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The marketing mix is product, price, place and promotion, combined to meet the needs of a target market.
- Each P matters: product meets needs, price earns revenue and signals quality, place gives access, promotion informs and persuades.
- The four Ps must be consistent with each other and with the target market.
- A marketing strategy sets an objective, identifies the target market, designs and checks the mix, and reviews results.
- Evaluate a mix by its fit with customers, its effect on sales, costs and profit, competitors' reactions and affordability.
:::
