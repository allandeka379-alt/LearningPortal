## Introduction

Twenty years ago, a shopper in Harare queued at a counter while an assistant fetched each item from the shelves and added up the bill by hand. Today she picks goods from the shelves herself, the cashier scans each bar code, she pays by card or mobile money, and she may order her next delivery of groceries on her phone. Retailing has changed enormously, and technology is the main reason.

ZIMSEC Commerce asks about **trends in retailing**; Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.3.5) asks for **examples of e-commerce** and its **advantages and disadvantages for businesses and for customers**. Both appear as short-answer and "evaluate" questions, often based on a small business considering selling online.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="E-commerce"}
Buying and selling goods and services using the internet or other electronic networks, including mobile phones.
:::

:::definition{term="Electronic point of sale (EPOS)"}
A computerised till system that scans bar codes, records each sale, prints receipts and updates stock records automatically.
:::

:::definition{term="Bar code"}
A pattern of lines (or a square code) printed on a product that a scanner reads to identify the item and its price.
:::

:::definition{term="Loyalty scheme"}
A scheme that rewards customers with points, discounts or gifts for buying repeatedly from the same retailer.
:::

## Trends in retailing

| Trend | What it is | Benefit to the retailer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-service | Customers select goods from open shelves and pay at a checkout | Fewer assistants, more customers served, impulse buying |
| EPOS and bar coding | Scanning items at the till | Fast, accurate checkouts; automatic stock control and re-ordering; sales data |
| Electronic payment | Cards, mobile money, point-of-sale swipe machines | Less cash handled, so less theft; customers without cash can still buy |
| Vending machines | Machines that sell drinks, snacks or airtime automatically | Sales 24 hours a day without staff |
| Loyalty schemes | Points or discounts for regular customers | Repeat custom and data about shopping habits |
| Out-of-town shopping centres and hypermarkets | Large stores with parking outside town centres | Space, lower rents, one-stop shopping |
| Online shopping and delivery | Ordering through websites, apps or social media | New customers, lower premises costs |

:::example{title="EPOS in a supermarket"}
When a cashier scans a 2 kg bag of sugar, the EPOS system adds the price to the bill, deducts one bag from the stock record and, when stock falls below a set level, can produce an order for the supplier. The manager can also see which products sell best on which days.
:::

## Examples of e-commerce

- **Online shopping:** ordering goods through a retailer's website, an app or a social-media page, with delivery or collection.
- **Mobile phone and internet banking:** checking balances, transferring money and paying bills from a phone or computer; mobile money wallets let people without bank accounts send and receive money and pay merchants.
- **Online ticketing:** buying bus, air, cinema or event tickets online.
- **Online marketplaces:** websites or apps where many sellers list goods.
- **Business-to-business (B2B) ordering:** a retailer ordering stock from a wholesaler's website.

## Advantages and disadvantages for businesses

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Reach customers across the country and abroad, 24 hours a day | Cost of setting up and maintaining a website or app, and hiring skilled staff |
| Lower costs: less need for expensive shop premises and staff | Delivery costs and problems, especially to rural areas |
| Easy to update prices and products | Strong competition: customers compare prices instantly |
| Data on what customers view and buy | Risk of fraud, hacking and payment problems |
| Small businesses can compete with large ones | Customers may return goods; some customers lack internet access |

## Advantages and disadvantages for customers

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Shop at any time from home, saving travel time and cost | Cannot see, touch or try goods before buying |
| Easy price comparison; wider choice | Delivery charges and waiting time |
| Reviews from other buyers | Risk of fraud and of personal data being stolen |
| Bills paid and money sent without queuing | Needs a phone or computer, data and internet coverage |
| Goods not available locally can be bought | Returns and complaints can be difficult |

:::example{title="A craft business goes online"}
Thandeka makes beaded jewellery in Victoria Falls and sells it to tourists from a stall. She sets up a social-media shop page and accepts mobile money and card payments. She now receives orders from customers in Harare and Bulawayo, but must pay courier charges, answer messages quickly and take good photographs, because buyers cannot handle the jewellery before they buy.
:::

## E-commerce and small businesses

E-commerce lowers the barriers to entry: a vendor with a smartphone can advertise on social media and accept mobile money without renting a shop. However, success depends on reliable internet, trusted payment methods, good photographs and descriptions, quick delivery and building trust through reviews.

### Which products suit e-commerce?

Some products are much easier to sell online than others. Products that are **light, durable and standardised** (books, phone accessories, clothing in standard sizes, airtime and data bundles, event tickets) suit online selling, because customers know what they will receive and delivery is cheap and safe. Products that are **bulky, fragile or perishable** (furniture, glassware, fresh food) are harder, because delivery is costly and goods may be damaged or spoil. Services that can be delivered electronically, such as banking, bill payments and tickets, suit e-commerce best of all, because nothing physical needs to be delivered.

### Effects on traditional retailers

E-commerce puts pressure on shops. Customers may look at goods in a shop and then buy more cheaply online, and some shoppers stop visiting town centres. Many retailers respond by becoming "multi-channel": they keep their shops but also take orders online, offer click-and-collect, accept mobile payments and use social media to advertise special offers. A retailer that ignores these trends risks losing younger, connected customers to competitors.

### Security and trust

Customers will buy online only if they trust the seller and the payment method. Businesses build trust by using secure payment systems, publishing clear prices, delivery charges and return policies, answering questions quickly and showing genuine customer reviews. Customers protect themselves by buying from known sellers, never sharing passwords or PINs, and checking that goods have arrived before confirming payment where possible.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing advantages for the wrong group"}
Cambridge questions specify "for businesses" or "for customers". "Customers can shop at any time" is a customer advantage; "the business can reach more customers" is a business advantage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming online selling is always cheaper"}
A website reduces premises costs, but delivery, packaging, returns, website maintenance and online advertising add new costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing EPOS with e-commerce"}
EPOS is a till system inside a shop. E-commerce is buying and selling through electronic networks such as the internet.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Apply to the business"}
In Cambridge Paper 2, every answer should refer to the business in the case: what it sells, who its customers are and whether they are likely to shop online.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evaluate"}
For "should the business start selling online?", give at least one advantage and one disadvantage, then a justified decision that depends on the product and the customers ("jewellery photographs well and is light to post, so online selling suits it").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
ZIMSEC Commerce links this topic to retail trends (self-service, EPOS, vending machines, loyalty schemes). Cambridge 0264 focuses on e-commerce examples and its advantages and disadvantages for businesses and customers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Retailing trends include self-service, EPOS and bar codes, electronic payments, vending machines and loyalty schemes.
- E-commerce is buying and selling electronically: online shopping, mobile and internet banking, online ticketing.
- Businesses gain wider reach, lower premises costs and customer data, but face website, delivery and security costs and more competition.
- Customers gain convenience, choice and easy comparison, but cannot inspect goods and face delivery and fraud risks.
- E-commerce helps small businesses but depends on internet access, trust and reliable delivery.
:::
