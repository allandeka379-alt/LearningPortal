## Introduction

Nyasha wants to open a stall selling fresh fruit smoothies near a college in Mutare. Before she spends her savings on a blender, a fridge and a gazebo, she needs answers. Will students buy smoothies? Which flavours? What price will they pay? Who else sells drinks nearby? Finding these answers is **market research**.

Market research reduces the risk of a business decision by replacing guesses with information. Business Enterprise and Skills (Form 3) covers types and sources of data, sampling, data-collection tools and presenting and analysing results. Cambridge 0264 (topic 3.2) asks for primary and secondary methods, their advantages and disadvantages, and the accuracy of research data. Expect to recommend a method for a business in a case and to read results from a table or chart.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market research"}
Collecting, recording and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market to help a business make marketing decisions.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary research (field research)"}
Collecting new, first-hand information for a specific purpose, for example by questionnaire, interview, focus group or observation.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary research (desk research)"}
Using information that has already been collected by someone else, for example government statistics, newspapers, trade association reports, the internet or the business's own sales records.
:::

:::definition{term="Sample"}
The group of people selected from the target population to answer the research, because asking everyone would be too costly and slow.
:::

:::definition{term="Quantitative and qualitative data"}
Quantitative data are numbers that can be counted and compared (how many, how often, what price). Qualitative data are opinions, feelings and reasons (why customers prefer a product).
:::

## Why businesses carry out market research

- To find out whether there is **demand** for a new product and how big the market is.
- To learn what customers **want**: features, flavours, sizes, prices they are willing to pay and where they shop.
- To understand **competitors**: their prices, products and promotion.
- To identify **market segments** and target the right customers.
- To find the reasons for **falling sales** and test reactions to changes before spending money.

The aim is better decisions: research costs money, but launching a product nobody wants costs far more.

## Primary research methods

| Method | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Questionnaire / survey | Set questions answered on paper, by phone or online | Many people reached; answers easy to count | Poorly worded questions give misleading answers; low response rates online |
| Interview | One-to-one questions, face to face or by phone | Can explain questions and follow up answers | Slow and costly; interviewer may influence answers |
| Focus group | A small group discusses a product with a leader | Detailed qualitative opinions; ideas for new products | Small group may not represent the market; strong members dominate |
| Observation | Watching and counting behaviour, such as people passing a site | Shows what people actually do, not what they say | Does not explain **why** people behave as they do |
| Test marketing | Selling a product in one small area first | Real buying behaviour before a full launch | Competitors see the product early; costly |

Primary research is **up to date** and **specific** to the business's own question, and competitors do not have it. But it is **expensive and slow** to collect.

## Secondary research sources

- **Internal**: the business's own sales records, customer complaints and accounts.
- **External**: government publications such as population census reports, trade and industry association reports, newspapers and magazines, market reports and the internet.

Secondary research is **cheap and quick**, and sometimes covers a whole country. However, it may be **out of date**, collected for a different purpose, and equally available to competitors. A small enterprise usually starts with secondary research and then fills the gaps with simple primary research.

## Sampling

A business cannot ask every possible customer, so it chooses a **sample**.

- **Random sample**: everyone in the population has an equal chance of being chosen (for example, names drawn from a customer list).
- **Quota sample**: the researcher chooses a set number of people from each group, such as age group or suburb, in proportion to the population.

A **larger** sample is usually more accurate but costs more. A sample that leaves out important groups is **biased**.

:::worked-solution[Setting quotas for a survey]
A water-delivery business plans to survey 200 households in three suburbs of Mutare. Assume the suburbs have 1,800, 1,200 and 1,000 households, a total of 4,000.
1. Share of households in the first suburb = 1,800 ÷ 4,000 = 0.45.
2. Quota for the first suburb = 0.45 × 200 = **90** households.
3. Second suburb: 1,200 ÷ 4,000 × 200 = **60** households.
4. Third suburb: 1,000 ÷ 4,000 × 200 = **50** households.
5. Check: 90 + 60 + 50 = 200.
:::

## Designing a questionnaire

Good questions are **short, clear and unbiased**. Use **closed questions** (tick a box: yes/no, a price range) for answers that are easy to count, and a few **open questions** ("What would make you buy more often?") for reasons and ideas. Avoid leading questions such as "Don't you agree that our juice is delicious?". Ask only what the business needs; long questionnaires are abandoned. Include questions that identify the respondent's segment (age group, area) without asking for names.

:::example{title="Improving a question"}
Weak: "Do you like cheap, healthy smoothies?" (leading, and "cheap" means different things to different people).
Better: "What is the highest price you would pay for a 300 ml smoothie? US\$0.50 / US\$0.75 / US\$1.00 / US\$1.25".
:::

## Presenting and analysing results

Results are presented in **tables**, **bar charts** (comparing groups), **pie charts** (shares of a total) and **line graphs** (changes over time). The most common analysis is the **percentage** of the sample giving each answer.

| Highest price willing to pay | Number of people | Percentage of sample % |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| US\$0.50 | 40 | 16 |
| US\$0.75 | 95 | 38 |
| US\$1.00 | 85 | 34 |
| US\$1.25 | 30 | 12 |

:::worked-solution[Reading a survey table]
Nyasha asked 250 students the price question above.
1. Percentage willing to pay at most US\$0.75 = 95 ÷ 250 × 100 = **38%**.
2. People willing to pay **at least** US\$1.00 = 85 + 30 = 115.
3. As a percentage: 115 ÷ 250 × 100 = **46%** of the sample.
4. Interpretation: a price of US\$1.00 would lose more than half of the students asked, so a price nearer US\$0.75 may sell more, unless costs are too high.
:::

## Accuracy of research data

Research can be wrong. Accuracy depends on:

- **Sample size and choice**: a small or biased sample may not represent the market.
- **Question design**: leading or unclear questions give misleading answers.
- **Honesty of respondents**: people may give the answer they think is expected.
- **Age of the data**: secondary data may be out of date.
- **Who collected it and why**: a source with an interest in the result may exaggerate.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling sales records primary research"}
A business's own sales records already exist, so using them is secondary (desk) research from an internal source.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying primary research is always better"}
Primary research is specific and up to date but expensive and slow. For a small business, cheap secondary data may be enough for some questions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong total for percentages"}
Always divide by the number of people in the sample who answered, not by the population or by another answer's count.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Recommending a method"}
Name the method, apply it to the business (who would be asked, how, where), give one limitation, and say why it is still the best choice given the business's budget and time. Cambridge Paper 2 rewards answers that use the case's figures.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Board differences"}
Business Enterprise and Skills may ask you to design a short questionnaire or observation sheet and to present results in a table or chart. Cambridge 0264 focuses on choosing methods and judging accuracy. Both reward a clear link between the method and the decision the business has to make.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Market research gives information about customers, competitors and the market, reducing the risk of decisions.
- Primary research (questionnaires, interviews, focus groups, observation) is specific and up to date but costly and slow.
- Secondary research (internal records, government statistics, reports, the internet) is cheap and quick but may be out of date or not fit the purpose.
- A sample (random or quota) must be large enough and unbiased; questions must be clear and not leading.
- Present results in tables and charts, work out percentages of the sample, and judge how accurate the data are.
:::
