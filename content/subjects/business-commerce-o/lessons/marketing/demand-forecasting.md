## Introduction

Tatenda runs a small business in Gweru making freezits (frozen fruit drinks in plastic sleeves) that he sells to tuck shops and vendors. If he makes too few, shops run out on hot days and buy from a rival. If he makes too many, his freezer is full of stock that ties up cash, and he may have to sell it cheaply. Tatenda needs a sensible estimate of how many he will sell next month: a **demand forecast**.

Demand forecasting is a Form 3 topic in Business Enterprise and Skills. You need to explain why enterprises forecast demand, describe simple qualitative and quantitative methods, carry out a simple trend calculation from past sales and judge how reliable a forecast is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a good or service that customers are willing and able to buy at a given price over a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand forecasting"}
Estimating the future demand (sales) for a product over a given period, using judgement, research or past sales data.
:::

:::definition{term="Trend"}
The general direction in which a set of figures, such as sales, is moving over time: rising, falling or steady.
:::

:::definition{term="Seasonal demand"}
Demand that rises and falls at the same times each year, such as school uniforms in January or cold drinks in October.
:::

## Why enterprises forecast demand

A forecast is the starting point for many other plans:

- **Production planning**: how much to make, and when, so that output matches demand.
- **Purchasing and stock**: how many raw materials and how much stock to hold, avoiding both shortages and waste.
- **Staffing**: whether extra or seasonal workers are needed.
- **Cash budgeting**: expected sales revenue is the main cash inflow in a cash budget.
- **Finance and investment**: whether to borrow to buy a bigger machine or open a new branch.
- **Marketing**: when to run promotions and how much stock to order before a busy season.

Without a forecast, an enterprise reacts too late: it runs out of stock in busy months and is left with unsold goods in quiet ones.

## Qualitative methods: using judgement

Qualitative methods rely on people's knowledge and opinions rather than calculations. They are useful for **new products** with no sales history.

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opinion of sales staff | Sales staff estimate what their customers will buy | Staff are close to customers and notice changes early | Staff may give low estimates to make targets easy to beat |
| Expert opinion | Advice from experienced people in the industry, consultants or trade associations | Wide knowledge of the market | Costly; experts can disagree |
| Consumer surveys | Asking a sample of customers what they intend to buy | Direct evidence from buyers | People do not always buy what they say they will |

:::example{title="Forecasting for a new product"}
Chipo plans to sell school bags at a stall in Bulawayo. She has no past sales, so she asks three uniform shop owners how many bags they sell each January (expert opinion) and asks 80 parents whether they plan to buy a new bag this year (consumer survey). She combines the two to plan her first order.
:::

## Quantitative methods: using past sales

When an enterprise has a sales history, it can look for the **trend** and extend it into the future. The simplest methods are:

1. **Average change**: find how much sales changed each period, take the average, and add it on.
2. **Percentage growth**: if sales grow by a steady percentage, apply it to the latest figure.
3. **Moving average**: smooth out ups and downs by averaging a few periods at a time, to see the underlying trend.

Sales can also be shown on a **line graph**; drawing a line through the points and extending it gives a forecast.

:::worked-solution[Forecasting from the average monthly change]
Tatenda's sales of freezits (in crates) were March 620, April 655, May 690 and June 725.
1. Changes: 655 − 620 = 35; 690 − 655 = 35; 725 − 690 = 35.
2. Average change = (35 + 35 + 35) ÷ 3 = 35 crates a month.
3. Forecast for July = 725 + 35 = **760 crates**.
4. Forecast for August = 760 + 35 = **795 crates**, assuming the trend continues.
:::

| Month | Sales (crates) | Change on previous month |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| March | 620 | - |
| April | 655 | +35 |
| May | 690 | +35 |
| June | 725 | +35 |

:::worked-solution[Forecasting from percentage growth]
A sewing co-operative in Masvingo sold 2,400 school shirts in Year 1, 2,640 in Year 2 and 2,904 in Year 3.
1. Growth in Year 2 = (2,640 − 2,400) ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 10%.
2. Growth in Year 3 = (2,904 − 2,640) ÷ 2,640 × 100 = 10%.
3. Sales are growing by a steady 10% a year, so forecast for Year 4 = 2,904 × 1.10 = 3,194.4.
4. Forecast: about **3,194 shirts**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Finding the trend with a three-month moving average]
A hair salon's customers over six months were 180, 210, 240, 195, 225 and 255.
1. First average (months 1-3) = (180 + 210 + 240) ÷ 3 = 210.
2. Months 2-4: (210 + 240 + 195) ÷ 3 = 215.
3. Months 3-5: (240 + 195 + 225) ÷ 3 = 220.
4. Months 4-6: (195 + 225 + 255) ÷ 3 = 225.
5. The moving averages rise by 5 each month, so the underlying trend is upward even though month 4 fell.
:::

## Why forecasts can be wrong

A forecast is an **estimate**, not a fact. It may be wrong because:

- **Changes in tastes and fashion** reduce demand for a product.
- **New competitors** enter the market or rivals cut prices.
- **The economy changes**: incomes fall, prices rise or exchange rates move.
- **Weather and natural events**: a drought reduces farmers' incomes; a cool summer cuts drink sales.
- **Government action**: new taxes, regulations or bans.
- **Past trends do not continue**: a product reaches the end of its growth.

The further ahead the forecast, the less reliable it is. Enterprises should **review** forecasts regularly and compare them with actual sales.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating a forecast as certain"}
A forecast is only an estimate. Always say what could make it wrong and how the business can reduce the risk (holding a little safety stock, reviewing monthly).
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding the total change instead of the average"}
To forecast one period ahead, add the **average** change per period, not the total change over all periods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring seasonal patterns"}
Sales of uniforms jump every January. A trend calculated from busy months only will overestimate demand in quiet months.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Show each step: list the changes, find the average, then add it to the **latest** figure. State the assumption that the trend continues.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining limitations"}
For "explain why the forecast may be inaccurate", give a reason that fits the case (a new competitor, a drought, falling incomes) and say whether it would make actual sales higher or lower than forecast.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Demand forecasting estimates future sales so that production, stock, staffing, cash and finance can be planned.
- Qualitative methods use judgement: sales staff, experts and consumer surveys (useful for new products).
- Quantitative methods use past sales: average change, percentage growth and moving averages show the trend.
- Forecasts can be wrong because of changes in tastes, competition, the economy, weather or government policy.
- Review forecasts regularly and compare them with actual sales.
:::
