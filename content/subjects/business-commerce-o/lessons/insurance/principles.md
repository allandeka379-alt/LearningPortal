## Introduction

Farai insures his grocery shop in Masvingo against fire. A fire damages stock worth US\$4,000. Can he claim US\$6,000 because the policy says the stock is insured for that much? Could his neighbour have insured Farai's shop and claimed too? What if Farai never told the insurer that he stores paraffin at the back? The answers come from the **principles of insurance**, the rules that every insurance contract follows.

ZIMSEC Commerce teaches the principles in Form 2: **utmost good faith**, **insurable interest**, **indemnity** and **proximate cause**, together with **subrogation** and **contribution**, which follow from indemnity. Examiners give short cases and ask you to identify and explain the principle involved, or to decide how much an insurer will pay.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei)"}
Both parties to an insurance contract must disclose all material facts honestly. If the insured hides or misstates a material fact, the insurer can refuse to pay a claim or cancel the policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Material fact"}
Any fact that would influence an insurer's decision to give cover or the premium it charges, for example the use of a building, the goods stored in it or a driver's accident record.
:::

:::definition{term="Insurable interest"}
The insured must stand to suffer a financial loss if the insured event happens. You can insure only what you own or have a financial interest in.
:::

:::definition{term="Indemnity"}
The insured is restored to the same financial position as just before the loss, no better and no worse. The insured cannot make a profit from insurance.
:::

:::definition{term="Proximate cause"}
The insurer pays only if the main (nearest, dominant) cause of the loss is a risk covered by the policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Subrogation"}
After paying a claim in full, the insurer takes over the insured's rights to the damaged property (salvage) and to recover the loss from anyone responsible.
:::

:::definition{term="Contribution"}
When the same property is insured with more than one insurer, the insurers share the loss between them, usually in proportion to the sums each insured, so the insured receives no more than the loss.
:::

## Utmost good faith

The insurer depends on what the proposer says in the **proposal form**. A shop owner must disclose that paraffin is stored on the premises; a driver must disclose previous accidents. Honesty is required from the insurer too: it must explain the terms and exclusions of the policy.

:::example{title="A hidden fact"}
Nyasha insures her house and says it has an iron roof. In fact the kitchen is thatched. After a fire starts in the kitchen, the insurer discovers the truth and refuses the claim because a material fact was misstated.
:::

## Insurable interest

You must suffer a financial loss from the event. A person has an insurable interest in their own property and life, a spouse's life, goods they own or are responsible for, and property on which they have lent money. A creditor has an insurable interest in a debtor's life up to the amount of the debt. Without this rule people could "bet" on others' misfortune, and some might even cause it.

:::example{title="No interest, no cover"}
Tatenda cannot insure his neighbour's lorry, because he would lose nothing if it crashed. The haulage company that owns the lorry, and a bank that lent money to buy it, both can.
:::

## Indemnity

Insurance compensates; it does not reward. If goods insured for US\$6,000 are destroyed but were worth only US\$4,000, the insurer pays US\$4,000, the actual loss. The insurer may pay in cash, repair the item or replace it.

**Indemnity does not apply to life assurance**, because a life has no money value: the insurer pays the agreed sum assured.

### Under-insurance and the average clause

If property is insured for less than its full value, many policies contain an **average clause**: the insurer pays only the same proportion of the loss as the sum insured bears to the true value.

$$
\text{amount paid} = \frac{\text{sum insured}}{\text{value of the property}} \times \text{loss}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 1: under-insurance]
A building worth US\$80,000 is insured for only US\$60,000, under a policy with an average clause. A fire causes damage of US\$20,000.

1. Proportion insured $= \frac{60{,}000}{80{,}000} = \frac{3}{4}$.
2. Amount paid $= \frac{3}{4} \times 20{,}000 = 15{,}000$, so the insurer pays US\$15,000.
3. The owner bears the remaining $20{,}000 - 15{,}000 = 5{,}000$ dollars, because the building was under-insured by a quarter.
:::

## Subrogation

After paying a claim, the insurer steps into the insured's shoes. If a stolen car is recovered after the owner has been paid its full value, the car belongs to the insurer. If another driver caused the accident, the insurer, not the insured, can claim from that driver. This prevents the insured from being paid twice.

## Contribution

If a trader insures the same stock with two insurers, she can still recover only her actual loss. The insurers share it in proportion to the sums insured with each.

$$
\text{insurer's share} = \frac{\text{sum insured with that insurer}}{\text{total sum insured}} \times \text{loss}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 2: contribution between two insurers]
Chipo insures her stock with Insurer A for US\$30,000 and with Insurer B for US\$20,000. A burglary causes a loss of US\$15,000.

1. Total sum insured $= 30{,}000 + 20{,}000 = 50{,}000$ dollars.
2. Insurer A pays $\frac{30{,}000}{50{,}000} \times 15{,}000 = 9{,}000$ dollars.
3. Insurer B pays $\frac{20{,}000}{50{,}000} \times 15{,}000 = 6{,}000$ dollars.
4. Check: $9{,}000 + 6{,}000 = 15{,}000$, the actual loss. Chipo is indemnified but makes no profit.
:::

## Proximate cause

When several events lead to a loss, the insurer looks for the **dominant** cause. If it is covered, the claim is paid.

:::example{title="Finding the proximate cause"}
A fire breaks out in Sipho's clothing shop. The fire brigade puts it out with water, which soaks stock that the flames never reached. Sipho's fire policy does not mention water damage, but the fire is the proximate cause of the water damage (the water was used only because of the fire), so the fire policy pays for it. By contrast, if a burst pipe floods the shop with no fire at all, a fire policy pays nothing: the proximate cause is not an insured risk.
:::

| Principle | Short meaning | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utmost good faith | Disclose all material facts | Protects the insurer against hidden risks |
| Insurable interest | Must suffer a financial loss | Stops people gambling on the losses of others |
| Indemnity | No profit from a loss | Stops anyone profiting from a loss |
| Proximate cause | Main cause must be covered | Limits payment to the risks covered |
| Subrogation | Insurer takes over rights after paying | Prevents the insured being paid twice |
| Contribution | Insurers share a loss | Insurers share; no double recovery |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Applying indemnity to life assurance"}
Indemnity applies to property and liability insurance. Life assurance pays the agreed sum assured because a life cannot be valued in money.
:::

:::mistake{title="Insurers sharing the loss equally"}
Under contribution, insurers share **in proportion to the sums insured**, not equally (unless the sums happen to be equal).
:::

:::mistake{title="Paying the sum insured instead of the loss"}
The sum insured is the maximum the insurer will pay. It pays the actual loss, which may be less.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Name it, explain it, apply it"}
For a case question: name the principle, explain what it means, then say how it decides this case ("the insurer pays only US\$4,000, the value of the goods destroyed, because of indemnity").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Show the fraction and the multiplication, then check that the payments do not exceed the loss.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Utmost good faith: disclose every material fact, or the insurer may refuse to pay.
- Insurable interest: you can insure only what you would lose money from.
- Indemnity: compensation restores you to your position before the loss, no profit; under-insurance with an average clause reduces the payout.
- Proximate cause: the dominant cause of the loss must be an insured risk.
- Subrogation and contribution follow from indemnity and prevent the insured being paid twice.
:::
