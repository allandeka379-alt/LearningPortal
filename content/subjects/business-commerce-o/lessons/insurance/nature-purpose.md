## Introduction

A fire destroys the stock of a hardware shop in Chinhoyi. A cross-border trader's goods are stolen on the way home. A bus overturns and passengers are injured. A breadwinner dies, leaving children without income. No one can stop every misfortune, but people and businesses can protect themselves against the **financial loss** it causes. That is the purpose of **insurance and assurance**.

Long before insurance companies, Zimbabwean communities shared risks: under **zunde ramambo** the produce of the chief's field fed families in need, and **burial societies** still collect small contributions so that a member's family can afford a funeral.

This is the ZIMSEC Commerce Form 1 introduction to insurance. Later lessons cover the principles of insurance, procedures and documents, and types of policy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Insurance"}
A contract in which the insured pays a premium to an insurer, who agrees to compensate the insured for a financial loss caused by a specified risk **that may or may not happen** (for example fire, theft or an accident).
:::

:::definition{term="Assurance"}
A contract that provides cover against an event **that is certain to happen**, but at an uncertain time, mainly death, or that pays out after a fixed period. Life assurance pays an agreed sum.
:::

:::definition{term="Premium"}
The amount the insured pays to the insurer, usually monthly or yearly, in return for cover.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk"}
The chance of a loss happening.
:::

:::definition{term="Pooling of risks"}
Collecting premiums from many people who face the same risk into one fund, from which the few who suffer a loss are compensated.
:::

## Insurance and assurance compared

| Feature | Insurance | Assurance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Event covered | May or may not happen (fire, theft, accident) | Certain to happen (death) or a fixed date |
| Payment | Only if the loss happens | Certain to be paid (in most policies) |
| Amount paid | The actual loss, up to the sum insured (indemnity) | The agreed sum assured |
| Examples | Fire, burglary, motor, goods in transit | Whole life, endowment |
| Main purpose | Compensation for loss | Protection of dependants and saving |

## Business risks and life risks

**Business risks** threaten a firm's property, income or liability: fire, theft and burglary, damage to goods in transit, accidents to vehicles, injury to employees at work, injury to members of the public, dishonest employees, and customers who fail to pay.

**Life risks** affect individuals: death, illness, injury, disability and old age (loss of earning power).

## Insurable and non-insurable risks

A risk is **insurable** when:

- the loss can be measured in money;
- it happens by **chance**, not deliberately;
- many people face it, so **statistics** exist to predict how often it happens;
- the insured has an **insurable interest** (would suffer a financial loss);
- the loss is not so widespread that all policyholders suffer at once.

**Non-insurable risks** fail these tests. Examples:

- **Losses from poor management** or bad business decisions.
- **Changes in fashion or demand**, so goods do not sell.
- **Competition** from a new rival.
- **Trading losses** in general: the risk of not making a profit.
- **Changes in government policy**, such as a new tax.
- **War** and widespread events that affect everyone at once (usually excluded).
- **Deliberate damage** by the insured.

These are risks that a business must bear itself; the reward for bearing them is profit.

## How pooling of risks works

Many people pay a small premium each into a common fund. In any year only a few of them suffer a loss, and they are compensated from the fund. Each person exchanges an uncertain large loss for a certain small cost.

## Using statistics to set premiums

Insurers keep records of how often losses happen and how large they are. **Actuaries** use these statistics to predict the likely total claims for the coming year and set premiums so the fund can pay all claims, cover running costs and leave a profit. The higher the risk, the higher the premium: a thatched house pays more for fire cover than a brick house with a tiled roof.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: setting a premium from statistics]
An insurer covers 4,000 tuck shops against fire. Its records show that about 20 shops a year suffer a fire, with an average loss of US\$6,000. It adds US\$10 per shop for running costs and profit.

1. Expected claims $= 20 \times 6{,}000 = 120{,}000$ dollars.
2. Share per shop $= \frac{120{,}000}{4{,}000} = 30$ dollars.
3. Premium $= 30 + 10 = 40$, so each shop pays US\$40 a year.
4. A shop owner pays US\$40 for certain instead of facing a possible loss of thousands of dollars.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a burial society contribution]
A burial society has 80 members. From past years it expects about 4 funerals a year and pays US\$600 for each.

1. Expected payments $= 4 \times 600 = 2{,}400$ dollars a year.
2. Contribution per member $= \frac{2{,}400}{80} = 30$ dollars a year.
3. Per month $= \frac{30}{12} = 2.50$, so each member pays US\$2.50 a month.
:::

## Purposes of insurance and assurance

- **Compensation**: the insured is restored to his or her financial position before the loss.
- **Peace of mind and confidence**: businesses take reasonable risks, invest and trade knowing that losses are covered.
- **Encourages trade**: importers, exporters and lenders insist on cover for goods and assets.
- **Security for loans**: banks lend more readily against insured property.
- **Protection of dependants**: life assurance provides for a family after a death.
- **Saving and investment**: assurance policies are a form of saving, and insurers invest premiums in the economy.
- **Loss prevention**: insurers advise on and reward safety measures (fire extinguishers, tracking devices).
- **Employment**: insurers, brokers and assessors employ many people.

## Communal systems of insurance and assurance

| System | How it works | Risk shared |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zunde ramambo (isiphala senkosi) | Villagers work the chief's field; the harvest is stored and given to orphans, widows, the elderly and families hit by drought | Hunger after crop failure, loss of a breadwinner |
| Burial societies | Members pay regular small contributions; the society pays for the funeral of a member or family member | Funeral costs at death |
| Contributions at funerals and other occasions | Relatives and neighbours contribute money, food or labour | Sudden costs of a death or disaster |

These systems use the same idea as modern insurance: many contribute so that the unlucky few are helped. They are based on trust and community membership rather than legal contracts and statistics.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using insurance and assurance as the same word"}
Insurance covers events that **may** happen; assurance covers events that **will** happen (death) or a fixed date. Use the right term.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a business can insure against making a loss"}
Trading losses, poor decisions, changes in fashion and competition are non-insurable: there are no reliable statistics and the business controls the outcome.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the premium equals the loss"}
The premium is a small share of the expected losses of everyone in the pool, plus costs. Pooling makes it far smaller than any one loss.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Distinguish clearly"}
"Distinguish between insurance and assurance" needs a point about **each**: the event (may or will happen) and the payment (only if loss happens or certain to be paid).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain pooling with numbers"}
A short numerical illustration (many pay a little, a few are paid a lot) makes an explanation of pooling clear and earns full marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Insurance compensates for losses that may happen; assurance pays on events that will happen or at a fixed date.
- Business risks include fire, theft, accidents and liability; life risks include death, illness and old age.
- Insurable risks are measurable, accidental and common enough for statistics; trading losses, fashion changes and poor management are not insurable.
- Pooling: many pay premiums into a fund from which the few who suffer losses are paid; statistics and actuaries set premiums.
- Communal systems such as zunde ramambo and burial societies share risk within the community.
:::
