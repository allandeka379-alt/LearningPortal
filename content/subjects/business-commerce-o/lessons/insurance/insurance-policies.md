## Introduction

A wholesaler in Bulawayo holds stock worth thousands of dollars, runs a fleet of delivery lorries, employs forty workers, handles cash every day and has customers walking around its premises. Each of these brings a different risk, and each risk is covered by a different **insurance policy**. Choosing the right combination of policies is an important management decision: too little cover can ruin a business after one disaster, while unnecessary cover wastes money on premiums.

ZIMSEC Commerce teaches types of insurance policies in Form 3. Expect questions that ask you to explain what a policy covers, match policies to risks, or advise a named business which policies it needs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Insurance policy"}
A contract of insurance covering one or more named risks, for a stated period and up to a stated sum insured.
:::

:::definition{term="Liability"}
A legal responsibility to pay compensation to another person for injury, loss or damage that one has caused.
:::

:::definition{term="Third party"}
A person who is not one of the two parties to the insurance contract (the insurer and the insured) but who suffers injury or damage, for example a pedestrian hit by the insured's vehicle.
:::

:::definition{term="Comprehensive policy"}
A policy that covers a wide range of risks under one contract, for example motor insurance covering damage to one's own vehicle, theft, fire and third-party claims.
:::

## Property insurance

### Fire insurance

Covers buildings, machinery, furniture and stock against damage by fire, and often lightning and explosion. Extra cover can be added for storms, floods and burst pipes. A **consequential loss** (business interruption) policy can be added to cover lost profit and continuing expenses while the business is closed after a fire.

### Burglary and theft insurance

Covers loss of goods and damage to premises caused by forcible entry (burglary) or theft. Insurers may require burglar bars, alarms or security guards before giving cover.

### Cash in transit

Covers money being carried to or from the bank, for example a supermarket's daily takings, against robbery.

### All risks

Covers valuable portable items (jewellery, cameras, laptops) against most risks of loss or damage, wherever they are.

## Motor insurance

- **Third party only**: covers the insured's legal liability for injury to other people and damage to their property. This is the minimum cover required by law in Zimbabwe for vehicles used on public roads.
- **Third party, fire and theft**: adds cover for the insured's own vehicle if it is stolen or damaged by fire.
- **Comprehensive**: also covers accidental damage to the insured's own vehicle, whoever was at fault.

## Goods in transit and marine insurance

**Goods in transit** insurance covers goods while they are carried by road, rail or air, against theft, accident and damage, for example a wholesaler delivering to shops around the country.

**Marine insurance** covers risks at sea and is important to importers and exporters. Its main kinds:

- **Hull insurance**: covers the ship itself.
- **Cargo insurance**: covers the goods carried.
- **Freight insurance**: covers the shipowner's loss of freight charges if goods are lost and not delivered.
- **Ship owner's liability**: covers damage the ship causes to other ships or to ports.

Because Zimbabwean imports and exports by sea pass through foreign ports, cargo insurance usually covers the whole journey, by sea and by road or rail.

## Insurance of people and liabilities

### Fidelity guarantee insurance

Covers the employer against loss caused by the **dishonesty of employees**, such as cashiers, clerks and storekeepers who steal money or goods.

### Public liability insurance

Covers the business's legal liability to **members of the public** who are injured or whose property is damaged on its premises or by its activities, for example a customer who slips on a wet supermarket floor.

### Employer's liability insurance

Covers the employer's legal liability for **injury or illness of employees** caused by their work. (Workers in Zimbabwe are also covered by the national workers' compensation scheme run by NSSA, covered in the next lesson.)

### Other business policies

- **Bad debts (credit) insurance**: covers losses when customers fail to pay.
- **Personal accident insurance**: pays benefits if the insured is injured.
- **Professional indemnity**: covers professionals such as accountants against claims for negligent advice.

| Risk to the business | Policy |
| --- | --- |
| Fire destroys the factory | Fire (with consequential loss) |
| Stock stolen in a break-in | Burglary and theft |
| Cashier steals takings | Fidelity guarantee |
| Delivery lorry hits a pedestrian | Motor (third party) |
| Goods damaged on the way to a customer | Goods in transit |
| Imported cargo lost at sea | Marine (cargo) |
| Customer injured in the shop | Public liability |
| Worker injured by a machine | Employer's liability |
| Daily takings robbed on the way to the bank | Cash in transit |

## Which risks should a business insure?

A business should consider:

- **Legal requirements**: some cover is compulsory, such as third-party motor insurance.
- **The size of the possible loss**: risks that could ruin the business (fire, liability claims) should always be insured.
- **The likelihood of the loss**: frequent losses (for example theft in a high-crime area) make cover more valuable.
- **The cost of premiums** compared with the cost of bearing the loss itself.
- **The nature of the business**: a transport firm needs motor and goods in transit cover; a shop needs public liability; a business with many cashiers needs fidelity guarantee.
- **Requirements of lenders and partners**: banks usually insist that property used as security is insured.

:::example{title="Advising a supermarket"}
Rudo opens a supermarket in Chinhoyi with a delivery van, 12 staff and a busy shop floor. She should take: **fire** cover for the building and stock (a fire could destroy everything); **burglary and theft**; **motor** insurance for the van (third party at least, because the law requires it; comprehensive if she cannot afford to replace it); **public liability** because many customers visit; **fidelity guarantee** because cashiers handle money; and **cash in transit** for banking the takings. Marine insurance is unnecessary because she buys from local wholesalers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up public liability and employer's liability"}
Public liability covers members of the public (customers, visitors, passers-by); employer's liability covers employees injured at work.
:::

:::mistake{title="Fidelity guarantee for burglars"}
Fidelity guarantee covers dishonesty by **employees**. Theft by outsiders is covered by burglary and theft insurance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending every policy"}
An 'advise' question rewards choosing the policies that fit the business, with a reason for each, and saying which are unnecessary.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Match risk, policy and reason"}
For each policy in your answer, name the risk in the case and explain the consequence avoided: "Public liability: customers walk around the shop and could be injured, and a claim for compensation could be very large."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Marine insurance"}
Know the four kinds of marine insurance and who each protects: the shipowner (hull, freight, liability) or the owner of the goods (cargo).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Property policies: fire (and consequential loss), burglary and theft, cash in transit, all risks.
- Motor insurance: third party (compulsory), third party fire and theft, or comprehensive.
- Goods in transit covers goods on land and in the air; marine covers hull, cargo, freight and shipowner's liability.
- Fidelity guarantee covers employee dishonesty; public liability covers the public; employer's liability covers employees.
- Choose policies by legal requirement, size and likelihood of loss, premium cost and the nature of the business.
:::
