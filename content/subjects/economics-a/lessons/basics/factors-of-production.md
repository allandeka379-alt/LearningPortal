## Introduction

A bakery in Masvingo needs a building and flour (from land and natural resources), bakers and cashiers (labour), ovens and delivery vans (capital) and someone who takes the risk and organises it all (the entrepreneur). These inputs are called the **factors of production**. Every good and service, from a loaf of bread to a tourist trip to Victoria Falls, is made by combining them. Because the factors are scarce, how they are used is the heart of economics.

This lesson is for ZIMSEC Form 5 and Cambridge AS Level. You should know the idea of scarcity and opportunity cost. The *rewards* paid to the factors are in the lesson on factor rewards, and the entrepreneur has a lesson of its own.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Factors of production"}
The resources used to produce goods and services: land (natural resources), labour, capital and enterprise.
:::

:::definition{term="Land"}
All natural resources that are not made by people: the surface of the earth, minerals, forests, water, wildlife and the climate and fertility of the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour"}
The physical and mental effort of people used in production.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital"}
Man-made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machines, tools, factories, roads and vehicles. In economics, capital does not mean money.
:::

:::definition{term="Enterprise"}
The skill of organising the other factors and bearing the risk of producing; the person who does this is the entrepreneur.
:::

## The four factors

| Factor | Examples (Zimbabwe) | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Platinum and gold deposits at Great Dyke mines, farmland, Hwange coal, Lake Kariba | Rent |
| Labour | Teachers, miners, tobacco graders, nurses | Wages and salaries |
| Capital | Tractors, irrigation pipes, a tobacco barn, a bus | Interest (and a return on investment) |
| Enterprise | A person who starts and manages a business | Profit |

### Physical and human capital

**Physical capital** is the stock of machines, buildings and equipment. **Human capital** is the knowledge, skills and health of workers that make them more productive, built up through education, training and experience. A nurse trained for three years has more human capital than someone who has had no training. Spending on education and training is an investment in human capital.

### Renewable and non-renewable resources

- **Renewable resources** can regenerate if managed well: forests, fish, soil fertility and water in rivers. They run out only if used faster than they replace themselves.
- **Non-renewable resources** cannot be replaced within human time scales: coal, gold, platinum and diamonds. Every tonne extracted leaves less for the future.

**Sustainable use** means using resources so that future generations can use them too. ZIMSEC expects you to discuss it, for example by replanting, regulating harvests and recycling.

:::worked-solution{title="When does use of a renewable resource become unsustainable?"}
A fictional woodland covers 2,000 hectares of trees and regrows at 4% a year if it is not cut.

1. Annual regrowth: $2000 \times 0.04 = 80$ hectares.
2. Suppose 100 hectares are cleared each year for firewood.
3. Cutting minus regrowth: $100 - 80 = 20$ hectares lost each year.
4. After one year there are $2000 + 80 - 100 = 1{,}980$ hectares, so the woodland is shrinking and the regrowth in later years also falls.
5. A sustainable level is no more than the regrowth of 80 hectares a year (if the regrowth rate stays at 4%).
6. At 100 hectares a year the use is unsustainable: the stock keeps falling.
:::

## Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production

Firms can combine factors in different proportions.

- **Labour-intensive** production uses a large amount of labour relative to capital (for example a hand-picked tea estate or a hairdressing salon).
- **Capital-intensive** production uses a large amount of capital relative to labour (for example a car plant or an automated bottling line).

One way to classify is by the share of labour in total costs.

:::worked-solution{title="Classifying firms by the share of labour costs"}
Two fictional firms have the following monthly costs.

| Firm | Labour cost (US\$) | Capital cost (US\$) | Total cost (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Bright Sewing | 18,000 | 6,000 | 24,000 |
| Zandia Bottling | 5,000 | 20,000 | 25,000 |

1. Bright Sewing: labour share $= 18000 \div 24000 \times 100 = 75\%$.
2. Zandia Bottling: labour share $= 5000 \div 25000 \times 100 = 20\%$.
3. Bright Sewing is labour-intensive; Zandia Bottling is capital-intensive.
4. Choosing the right combination depends on the relative prices of the factors and the technology available.
:::

Labour-intensive methods suit economies with abundant labour and scarce capital, and they create more jobs per dollar invested. Capital-intensive methods can give higher output per worker and lower unit costs when output is large.

## Mobility of factors

**Mobility** is how easily a factor can move between uses.

- *Occupational mobility*: a worker changing to a different type of job, which depends on skills and training.
- *Geographical mobility*: a worker or machine changing location, which depends on housing, transport and family ties.
- Land is geographically immobile (a mine cannot be moved), and some capital, such as a blast furnace, is occupationally immobile because it can be used only for one purpose.

When factors are immobile, unemployment may remain in one region while another has shortages of workers, and resources are not moved to where they are most valued.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing capital with money"}
In economics, capital means man-made equipment used to produce, not cash or savings. Money is a financial asset, not a factor of production.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a bus used by a family 'capital'"}
A vehicle is capital only if used in production, such as a minibus taxi business. A family car used for private travel is a consumer good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing renewable means unlimited"}
Renewable resources can be destroyed if used faster than they regenerate (overfishing, deforestation).
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up enterprise and labour"}
The entrepreneur organises production and bears the risk of loss; workers are paid wages whatever the profit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper coverage"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to identify factors from a description, to define capital correctly, and to explain why a method is labour- or capital-intensive. Essays on development often use the quality and quantity of factors.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify*: name the factor and its type. *Distinguish*: give both meanings and the difference. *Explain*: link the factor to the production in the stem. *Assess the choice of method*: weigh costs, output and jobs and reach a conclusion.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Sustainable use of renewable and non-renewable resources is ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge expects the four factors, human and physical capital, and mobility, and may ask about quantity and quality of factors as causes of growth.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The four factors of production are land, labour, capital and enterprise.
- Capital means man-made productive equipment, not money; human capital is skills and health.
- Renewable resources can regenerate but run out if overused; non-renewable resources cannot be replaced.
- Labour-intensive and capital-intensive production differ in the share of labour in costs.
- Factor mobility, occupational and geographical, affects how well resources are used.
:::
