## Introduction

A **production possibility curve** (PPC), also called a production possibility frontier, is the economist's simplest picture of scarcity and choice. It shows the maximum amounts of two goods (or of two groups of goods, such as consumer goods and capital goods) that an economy can produce in a given period when all its resources are fully and efficiently employed and technology is fixed. Because it can be described in words and a table, you can use it in every part of the course, from opportunity cost to economic growth and development.

You should already know scarcity, choice and opportunity cost. This lesson applies them to the whole economy and is examined by both ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge (AS Level). The trading possibility curve is covered in the lesson on comparative advantage.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production possibility curve"}
A curve showing the maximum combinations of two goods or services an economy can produce with its existing resources and technology, when resources are fully and efficiently used.
:::

:::definition{term="Productive efficiency"}
Producing on the PPC, so that more of one good can be produced only by producing less of the other.
:::

:::definition{term="Potential output and actual output"}
Potential output is the maximum the economy could produce (a point on the PPC); actual output is what it does produce, which may be inside the curve.
:::

## Reading a PPC

Take a fictional economy, Zandia, which makes consumer goods (food, clothing) and capital goods (machines, tools). All figures are thousands of units per year.

| Combination | Consumer goods | Capital goods |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | 20 | 98 |
| C | 40 | 92 |
| D | 60 | 80 |
| E | 80 | 56 |
| F | 100 | 0 |

**The diagram in words.** Put consumer goods on the horizontal axis and capital goods on the vertical axis, each labelled with its units. The PPC starts at 100 on the vertical axis (A), slopes downwards from left to right and meets the horizontal axis at 100 (F). It is *concave to the origin* (bowed outwards), steeper as consumer goods increase.

- A point **on** the curve is attainable and efficient.
- A point **inside** it shows unemployed or inefficiently used resources.
- A point **outside** it is unattainable with present resources and technology.
- Moving along the curve shows the **opportunity cost**: more of one good means less of the other.

## Opportunity cost and the shape of the curve

:::worked-solution{title="Opportunity cost from the schedule"}
Opportunity cost per unit of consumer goods $=$ capital goods given up $\div$ consumer goods gained.

1. A to B: gain 20, give up $100 - 98 = 2$; cost per unit $= 2 \div 20 = 0.1$.
2. B to C: give up $98 - 92 = 6$; cost per unit $= 6 \div 20 = 0.3$.
3. C to D: give up $92 - 80 = 12$; cost per unit $= 12 \div 20 = 0.6$.
4. D to E: give up $80 - 56 = 24$; cost per unit $= 24 \div 20 = 1.2$.
5. E to F: give up $56 - 0 = 56$; cost per unit $= 56 \div 20 = 2.8$.
6. The cost rises at every step: this is **increasing opportunity cost**.
:::

**Why costs increase.** Resources are not equally suitable for every use. Land suited to growing tobacco is less suited to growing maize; skilled machinists make poor farmers. The first resources moved are those best suited to the new good; later ones are less suitable, so more of the other good must be given up for each extra unit.

| Shape | Opportunity cost | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Straight line | Constant | Resources are equally suited to both goods |
| Concave to the origin (bowed out) | Increasing | Resources are specialised; the usual case |
| Convex to the origin (bowed in) | Decreasing (ZIMSEC detail) | Gains from specialising or economies of scale |

:::worked-solution{title="A straight-line PPC"}
A fictional farm can produce 40 kg of cloth or 80 kg of food, with every 20 kg of food given up releasing 10 kg of cloth.

1. Opportunity cost of 20 kg of food $= 10 \div 20 = 0.5$ kg of cloth, at every point on the line.
2. Opportunity cost of 1 kg of cloth $= 20 \div 10 = 2$ kg of food.
3. The two opportunity costs are reciprocals: $0.5 \times 2 = 1$.
:::

For a decreasing-cost curve (ZIMSEC detail), the schedule could show 30, 12, 6 and 2 units of one good given up for each extra 10 units of the other: the cost falls as more is produced.

## Shifts of the PPC

The curve shifts when the economy's **productive capacity** changes. This is economic growth in the long run, and it is different from moving from inside the curve to the curve.

- **Outward shift (growth):** more or better resources (new mineral discoveries, a larger labour force, more capital investment), improved technology, better education and health, and improved infrastructure.
- **Inward shift (decline):** destruction of resources (war, drought, floods), emigration of skilled workers, or exhaustion of non-renewable resources.
- **Parallel shift** if capacity rises for both goods; **pivot** if capacity rises for only one good (for example a new irrigation technique that raises only the output of food).

:::example{title="Effect of a 25% rise in capacity"}
Suppose Zandia's resources and technology improve so that all its maximum outputs rise by 25%.

1. The consumer goods intercept rises from 100 to $100 \times 1.25 = 125$.
2. The capital goods intercept rises from 100 to 125.
3. At 60 thousand units of consumer goods the maximum capital goods rises from 80 to 100.
4. Zandia can produce combinations that were previously unattainable, but the improvement does not guarantee that it will use the new capacity: actual output could still be inside the new curve.
:::

## Actual output, potential output and limits of the model

If Zandia produces 60 thousand units of consumer goods and 70 thousand of capital goods, it is inside its PPC (the maximum at 60 is 80). The shortfall is $80 - 70 = 10$ thousand units, or $10 \div 80 \times 100 = 12.5\%$ of potential output. Moving from this point to the curve would give more capital goods at no opportunity cost, using idle resources.

The model has limits. It shows only two goods, so it simplifies a world with many. It assumes fixed resources and technology, so it does not show how an economy changes. It says nothing about which point on the curve is best, or about prices and distribution. Real PPCs are difficult to measure, so the diagram is a tool for reasoning rather than a forecast.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating a point inside the curve as unattainable"}
Points inside the curve are attainable but inefficient. Only points outside are unattainable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a movement along the curve growth"}
Growth is an outward shift of the curve (or a move from inside towards it, if growth is measured as actual output). A movement along the curve only changes the mix.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading the slope the wrong way"}
Opportunity cost is the amount of the *other* good given up per unit gained, so state which good is being measured.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to label the axes"}
A PPC without labelled axes, units and a labelled curve loses marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Multiple-choice questions (ZIMSEC Paper 1, Cambridge Paper 1) test points on, inside and outside the curve, opportunity cost and shifts. Data-response questions give a schedule. Essays on growth, development and unemployment often use the PPC. In a described diagram, state the axes, the shape and the labelled points.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain the opportunity cost of moving from C to D*: state both changes, then the amount given up. *Analyse a shift*: give the cause, say which resources or technology change, and describe the new curve. *Evaluate the usefulness of the PPC*: give uses (scarcity, choice, growth) and limits (two goods, fixed technology, no prices).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Decreasing opportunity cost (a convex curve) is ZIMSEC detail. Both boards require constant and increasing opportunity cost, shifts, actual versus potential output and efficiency.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods, given resources and technology.
- Points on the curve are efficient, inside inefficient (unemployed resources) and outside unattainable.
- A movement along the curve shows opportunity cost; cost is constant on a straight line and increasing on a concave curve.
- The curve shifts outward with more or better resources or technology and inward with destruction or loss.
- The PPC is a simple model with limits: two goods, fixed technology and no prices.
:::
