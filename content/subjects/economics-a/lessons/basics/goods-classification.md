## Introduction

Why can you buy a loaf of bread in a shop but not a "ticket" for the street light outside it? Why does the government pay for most vaccination campaigns but not for chocolate? Why do cattle graze on a village commons until the grass is gone? The answers depend on the **characteristics of the good**: whether it can be kept for those who pay, whether one person's use leaves less for others, and whether the buyer fully understands its benefits.

This lesson classifies goods for ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should know the ideas of scarcity and opportunity cost. The policies used to deal with the problems found here are in the lessons on government intervention.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rivalry"}
A good is **rival** if one person's use of it reduces the amount available to others. A loaf of bread is rival.
:::

:::definition{term="Excludability"}
A good is **excludable** if people who do not pay can be prevented from using it. A cinema seat is excludable.
:::

:::definition{term="Public good"}
A good that is **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce what is available to others) and **non-excludable** (non-payers cannot be stopped from using it). Examples: street lighting, national defence, a lighthouse.
:::

:::definition{term="Merit good"}
A good that society regards as beneficial for people to consume, but which tends to be under-consumed in a free market because people undervalue its benefits or lack information (for example education and vaccination).
:::

:::definition{term="Demerit good"}
A good that is harmful to the consumer or others, but which is over-consumed because people underestimate the harm (for example tobacco and alcohol).
:::

## Free and economic goods

A **free good** has no opportunity cost: it is so abundant that using it leaves no less for others, such as air in an open space. An **economic good** is scarce and has an opportunity cost. Almost all goods and services, including those given away free of charge, are economic goods because producing them uses scarce resources.

## Private and public goods

| Type | Rival? | Excludable? | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Private good | Yes | Yes | A loaf of bread, a bus ticket |
| Public good | No | No | Street lighting, national defence, flood warning system |
| Quasi-public good | Partly | Partly | A public road (can become congested; can be tolled), a park |
| Common resource | Yes | No | Open-access fishery, communal grazing land |

**The free-rider problem.** Because nobody can be excluded, people have no reason to pay for a public good if others will pay: they can "free ride". Firms therefore cannot earn revenue from supplying it, so the market provides too little or none. This is why governments provide public goods from taxation.

:::worked-solution{title="Why a community may not buy its own street lighting"}
A fictional neighbourhood of 100 households would benefit from street lighting that costs US\$2,000 to install. Each household values it at US\$30.

1. Total benefit: $100 \times 30$ = US\$3,000.
2. Total cost: US\$2,000. Benefit exceeds cost by US\$1,000, so the lighting is worthwhile.
3. Equal share of cost per household: $2000 \div 100$ = US\$20, less than the US\$30 benefit.
4. But if payment is voluntary, each household reasons: "If others pay, I enjoy the light for free; if I pay alone, the light is still shared." Many do not pay.
5. Suppose only 30 households pay US\$20: revenue is $30 \times 20$ = US\$600, which is less than US\$2,000. The lighting is not provided although it would benefit everyone.
6. Taxation overcomes this problem: every household is required to pay its share.
:::

**Quasi-public goods** have some features of public goods but can be made partly excludable or rival, for example a road that is free of congestion most of the time but crowded at rush hour. The government often provides or subsidises them.

## Merit and demerit goods

Merit and demerit goods arise because of **imperfect information** or undervaluing of long-term effects.

- **Merit goods:** people may not know how much education or immunisation benefits them or others (positive externalities), so they buy too little. A market produces less than the socially best amount. Governments subsidise or provide them, or make them compulsory.
- **Demerit goods:** people may not fully understand the health risks of smoking or may ignore effects on others (negative externalities), so they buy too much. Governments tax, regulate or ban them and run information campaigns.

The classification involves a **value judgement** (what is "good for" people), so it is partly normative; people disagree about which goods are merit or demerit.

## Common resources (ZIMSEC detail: common property)

Common resources, such as fish in a lake or communal pasture, are rival but non-excludable. Each user gains the full benefit of taking more but shares the cost of depletion with everyone, so the resource tends to be **over-used**.

:::worked-solution{title="Over-use of a common fishery"}
A fictional lake can safely supply 1,000 kg of fish a year without the stock falling. There are 10 fishers, each of whom catches 150 kg.

1. Total catch: $10 \times 150 = 1{,}500$ kg.
2. Sustainable catch: 1,000 kg.
3. Over-use: $1500 - 1000 = 500$ kg a year, which is $500 \div 1000 \times 100 = 50\%$ above the sustainable level.
4. If one fisher cut back to 100 kg, the others would simply catch more; each fisher expects to gain by catching more, so the stock falls over time.
5. A solution is to set catch quotas: $1000 \div 10 = 100$ kg per fisher, or to give the community rights to manage the lake.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that a public good is one provided by the government"}
A public good is defined by non-rivalry and non-excludability. Governments provide *education*, a merit good, even though it is private (rival and excludable).
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling any good that is free of charge a free good"}
A free school lunch has an opportunity cost, so it is an economic good provided without charge.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up merit and public goods"}
Merit goods are under-consumed because of imperfect information, and may be rival and excludable. Public goods fail the market because of free riding.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a demerit good as one that is illegal"}
Demerit goods such as alcohol are often legal. They are over-consumed relative to what is best for society.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test classification from a description, the free-rider problem and the justification for government provision, usually combined with a market-failure essay.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: give both definitions and a contrast. *Explain why the market under-provides*: free riding, no revenue, so no private firm supplies it. *Evaluate government provision*: weigh benefits (provision, fairness) against costs (taxes, inefficiency, measuring demand).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The term common property (over-use of shared resources) is ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge does not name common resources in the syllabus for this topic, but the ideas of public, merit and demerit goods are core to both.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Goods are classified by rivalry and excludability: private goods are both rival and excludable; public goods are neither.
- Public goods are under-provided because of free riding, so governments supply them through taxation.
- Merit goods are under-consumed and demerit goods over-consumed because of imperfect information.
- Common resources are rival but non-excludable and tend to be over-used.
- Free goods have no opportunity cost; most goods, even those provided free of charge, are economic goods.
:::
