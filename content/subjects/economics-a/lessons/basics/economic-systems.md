## Introduction

Every society must decide **what, how and for whom** to produce, because resources are scarce. Who makes those decisions? In one kind of economy they are made by millions of buyers and sellers responding to prices. In another they are made by a central authority that sets targets. Most economies today use a blend of both. These patterns are called **economic systems**, and comparing them is a favourite A Level topic because there are strong arguments on each side and no system is perfect.

This lesson is for ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should know the three basic questions and opportunity cost. How prices work as signals is explained in the lesson on the price mechanism, and the history of planning in Zimbabwe is in a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic system"}
The way in which a society organises the ownership and use of resources to answer the questions of what, how and for whom to produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Market economy"}
An economy in which resources are privately owned and decisions about production and consumption are made by individuals and firms through the price mechanism, with little government involvement.
:::

:::definition{term="Planned (command) economy"}
An economy in which the state owns most resources and a central authority decides what, how and for whom to produce.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixed economy"}
An economy in which both private firms and the government play important roles in allocating resources: some goods are provided through markets and some by the state, and the government regulates markets.
:::

## How each system answers the three questions

| Question | Market economy | Planned economy | Mixed economy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What? | Firms produce what is profitable; consumers vote with their money | Planners decide, using targets and quotas | Markets decide most goods; the state provides others (e.g. defence, public health) |
| How? | Firms choose the lowest-cost methods to compete | State enterprises follow the plan | Firms choose methods, within government regulations |
| For whom? | Those with money and the ability to pay | According to the plan: rationing, state wages and social needs | Market incomes, adjusted by taxes, benefits and free services |
| Ownership | Private | State | Both |

## The market economy

**Features:** private ownership, freedom of enterprise, competition, prices set by supply and demand, profit as the motive, limited government.

**Strengths**

- Prices signal what consumers want and which resources are scarce, so resources move to their best uses without planners.
- Competition and the profit motive encourage efficiency and innovation.
- Consumers have choice, and the system responds quickly to changing tastes.
- Few government workers are needed to run the system.

**Weaknesses**

- Public goods (such as street lighting) and merit goods tend to be under-provided because they are not profitable.
- Inequality may be large, because those without income cannot express demand.
- Firms may abuse market power, and external costs such as pollution are ignored.
- Unstable: booms, slumps and unemployment.

## The planned economy

**Features:** state ownership of land and capital, central planning, targets set for each industry, prices and wages often fixed by the state, little or no private competition.

**Strengths**

- Government can direct resources to national priorities such as basic health, education or industrialisation.
- Less inequality in incomes is possible, and basic goods can be guaranteed.
- Resources can in principle be coordinated to avoid duplication, and full employment can be made an explicit goal.

**Weaknesses**

- Planners lack the information that prices provide, so shortages and surpluses of particular goods are common.
- Little incentive to be efficient or to innovate when profit does not reward success.
- Limited choice for consumers and, in some cases, reduced economic freedom.
- Planning for millions of products is very difficult and slow.

## The mixed economy

Almost all economies today are mixed, differing in **how much** the state does.

- The government provides public goods and often merit goods such as education and health care.
- It regulates markets (competition law, environmental rules, minimum wages) and may tax and redistribute.
- Private firms produce most goods and services, and some industries (electricity, railways) may be state-owned.

The question is therefore one of degree, not of choice between two extremes.

:::worked-solution{title="Placing economies on a spectrum"}
Public spending as a share of total output can show how large the state's role is. Three fictional countries are compared (illustrative data).

| Country | Public spending (US\$ billion) | Total output (US\$ billion) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Alba | 12 | 80 |
| Brevia | 36 | 80 |
| Corona | 64 | 80 |

1. Alba: $12 \div 80 \times 100 = 15\%$.
2. Brevia: $36 \div 80 \times 100 = 45\%$.
3. Corona: $64 \div 80 \times 100 = 80\%$.
4. Alba's state plays a small role (close to a market economy); Corona's plays a very large role (close to a planned economy); Brevia is a mixed economy with a substantial state sector.
5. Public spending is only one indicator. Ownership, price controls and regulation also matter.
:::

## Moving between systems

Some countries have moved towards markets by **privatisation** (selling state firms), **deregulation** and removing price controls; others have increased state ownership or planning. Moves have costs and benefits: market reforms may raise efficiency but create unemployment and inequality in the short run; more planning may protect basic needs but reduce efficiency. The speed and sequence of change matter.

## The Zimbabwean economy (ZIMSEC detail)

Zimbabwe is usually described as a **mixed economy**. Private farmers, mining companies, manufacturers, traders and a large informal sector produce much of the output, while the government owns or controls state-owned enterprises in sectors such as electricity and rail, provides education and health services, and regulates trade, prices and foreign exchange to varying degrees over time. Supporters of a larger state role argue that it protects basic needs and strategic industries; supporters of a smaller role argue that private enterprise raises efficiency and investment. Examinations expect a balanced, factual description rather than a political opinion.

:::example{title="Evaluating a mixed economy"}
A fictional country is deciding whether the state should own its electricity company.

1. For state ownership: electricity is essential, so the government can keep prices low and extend supply to poor and rural households.
2. Against: state companies may be inefficient and rely on subsidies from taxpayers.
3. For private ownership: competition and profit may raise efficiency and investment.
4. Against: private owners may charge high prices and neglect unprofitable rural areas.
5. Judgement: it depends on regulation and on how well each owner performs. Many countries mix the two, for example with private generators selling to a regulated national grid.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a mixed economy as half market and half planned"}
A mixed economy has many different mixes. The state's share may be small or large.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a market economy has no government"}
Even market economies need government to protect property rights and enforce contracts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting a system as simply good or bad"}
Both extremes have strengths and weaknesses; a good answer names both and gives a reasoned judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing planned and mixed economies"}
In a planned economy the state owns most resources. In a mixed economy the private sector is large.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Multiple-choice questions on features of each system (ZIMSEC Paper 1; Cambridge Paper 1), short explanations of how a system answers the three questions, and essays asking you to evaluate a system or the role of the state in the economy.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Compare*: give similarities and differences in the same categories. *Explain how a market economy decides what to produce*: prices, profit and consumer demand. *Evaluate*: give advantages and disadvantages, then judge which is greater for the country described.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Describing the Zimbabwean system is ZIMSEC detail. Both boards expect features, strengths and weaknesses of the three systems and the idea that most economies are mixed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic systems answer what, how and for whom to produce in different ways.
- Market economies use prices and private ownership; planned economies use central planning and state ownership.
- Most economies are mixed: private firms and the government both play roles.
- Each system has strengths and weaknesses; the degree of state involvement is the key question.
- Zimbabwe is generally described as a mixed economy, with both private and state sectors.
:::
