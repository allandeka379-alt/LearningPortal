## Introduction

Every household in Zimbabwe knows that money for school fees, food, transport and airtime does not stretch to cover everything people would like. A farmer has only so much land and labour; the Ministry of Finance has only so many tax dollars to share between hospitals, roads and schools. This is the **economic problem**: human wants are unlimited, but the resources that can satisfy them are limited. Economics is the study of the choices that follow.

This lesson is the foundation of the whole course, for both ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. It will help you to recognise a trade-off, to calculate an opportunity cost from a table and to describe the three basic questions every society must answer. Production possibility curves and economic systems build on this lesson and have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Scarcity"}
The economic problem that arises because people's wants are unlimited while the resources available to satisfy them are limited.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost"}
The value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic good"}
A good that is scarce and has an opportunity cost, so that it must be produced and normally has a price. Most goods and services are economic goods.
:::

:::definition{term="Free good"}
A good with no opportunity cost to use because it is available in such abundance that using more does not reduce what others can have. Air is the standard example.
:::

## Wants, needs and scarcity

**Needs** are things required for survival or a basic decent life, such as food, water, shelter and basic health care. **Wants** go beyond needs (a smartphone, a car) and are unlimited because as one want is met, another appears. What counts as a need varies between societies and times.

Scarcity affects everyone.

- **Individuals** have limited income and time. A student has to choose between studying and working.
- **Firms** have limited capital, workers and materials. A baker must decide between producing bread or buns with the same flour and oven time.
- **Governments** have limited tax revenue and must choose between schools, hospitals, defence and debt repayment in the national budget.

Scarcity is not the same as a shortage. A **shortage** occurs when, at the current price, people want to buy more than is supplied (for example for a few weeks when fuel is hard to get). Scarcity is permanent and applies to everything that is not free.

## Choice and opportunity cost

Because resources are scarce, choosing one thing means giving up another. The opportunity cost is **only the next best alternative**, not the whole list of things given up and not the money price alone.

:::worked-solution{title="Opportunity cost of a farming choice"}
Rudo has one hectare of land. The expected profit per hectare from each crop (fictional figures) is shown.

| Crop | Expected profit (US\$ per hectare) |
| --- | ---: |
| Maize | 300 |
| Groundnuts | 450 |
| Cotton | 500 |
| Tobacco | 900 |

1. She ranks the options: tobacco (900), cotton (500), groundnuts (450), maize (300).
2. She chooses tobacco, the highest profit.
3. The next best alternative is cotton at US\$500.
4. The opportunity cost of growing tobacco is **US\$500**, the profit from cotton given up.
5. It is not US\$500 + US\$450 + US\$300 = US\$1,250, because she could only have chosen one of the other crops.
6. The extra profit from choosing tobacco rather than her next best option is 900 − 500 = US\$400.
:::

Opportunity cost is not always measured in money. A student who spends three hours at a football match gives up three hours of study; the time is the opportunity cost.

:::worked-solution{title="Opportunity cost of time"}
Tendai has 4 hours on Saturday. She can (a) tutor a neighbour's child and earn US\$3 per hour or (b) help at the family stall and earn US\$2 per hour. She chooses to tutor for the 4 hours.

1. Earnings from tutoring: 4 × 3 = US\$12.
2. The next best alternative is the stall: 4 × 2 = US\$8.
3. The opportunity cost of tutoring is therefore US\$8, the stall earnings given up.
4. If she had also considered resting, and valued rest at more than US\$8, then rest would be the next best alternative and its value to her would be the opportunity cost. The opportunity cost always depends on the best alternative that really was available.
:::

Governments face the same trade-off. If a fictional ministry has US\$100 million and builds a new hospital costing US\$100 million, the opportunity cost is the best alternative use, such as the roads or schools that the money could have paid for. This is why every spending decision should compare costs with the best alternative.

## What, how and for whom

Scarcity forces every society to answer three **basic economic questions**.

| Question | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What to produce? | Which goods and services, and how much of each | More maize or more tobacco? Roads or schools? |
| How to produce? | Which methods and resources | Labour-intensive hand picking or machines? |
| For whom to produce? | How output is shared among people | By ability to pay, by need, or by government decision? |

Different economic systems, described in a later lesson, answer these questions in different ways.

## Economic goods and free goods

Most things have an opportunity cost, so most goods are economic goods. A good may be free in one place and economic in another: clean river water in an unpopulated area may be free, but treated water in Harare uses scarce resources and has a price. Pollution can also make a previously free good scarce.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding up all the alternatives"}
Opportunity cost is the value of the *next best* alternative only, not the total of everything given up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing scarcity with poverty or shortage"}
Scarcity affects rich and poor, countries and individuals. It exists even when shops are full.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking that if something is free of charge it has no opportunity cost"}
A free school meal still uses resources (food, labour, fuel). It is free to the pupil but not free to society.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using only money"}
Time, land, and other resources have opportunity costs even if no money is paid.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it appears"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask for definitions, short calculations from a table of options and brief explanations. It also underlies every essay on choice and policy.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the exact meaning. *Calculate*: state the next best alternative and its value. *Explain*: link scarcity to the need to choose and to an opportunity cost in the example given. *Analyse a decision*: state what is given up and weigh it against the benefits.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Scarcity arises because wants are unlimited and resources are limited; it affects individuals, firms and governments.
- Because of scarcity, choices must be made, and each choice has an opportunity cost.
- Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative given up, not all alternatives.
- Every society answers what, how and for whom to produce.
- Economic goods are scarce and have an opportunity cost; free goods have none.
:::
