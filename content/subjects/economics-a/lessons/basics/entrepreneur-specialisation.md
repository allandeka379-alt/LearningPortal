## Introduction

Behind every market stall, minibus route and tobacco farm is someone who decided to start it. That person saw an opportunity, gathered land, labour and capital, and accepted the chance of losing money. Economists call this person the **entrepreneur** and the activity **enterprise**. Once a business is running, it can raise output by letting each worker concentrate on one task: **specialisation** and the **division of labour**.

This lesson covers both for ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should already know the four factors of production. Forms of business organisation and international specialisation are met in later lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Entrepreneur"}
A person who organises the other factors of production, takes the risk of producing without being sure of selling, and often introduces new ideas or methods.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialisation"}
Concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services (by individuals, firms, regions or countries) and trading for the rest.
:::

:::definition{term="Division of labour"}
Breaking a production process into separate tasks, with each worker doing only one task or a few tasks.
:::

## The role of the entrepreneur

An entrepreneur performs four main functions.

1. **Organising** land, labour and capital into production.
2. **Risk-taking:** the entrepreneur pays for inputs before knowing whether the output will sell. If the business fails, the entrepreneur loses the money invested. The reward is **profit**, which is what remains after all costs are paid.
3. **Decision-making:** what to produce, how, where and at what price.
4. **Innovation:** introducing new products, methods or markets, which can raise productivity and create jobs.

### Characteristics of a successful entrepreneur

Common qualities include willingness to take calculated risks, creativity, determination, good organising and communication skills, the ability to spot an opportunity, and knowledge of the market. Success also depends on factors outside the entrepreneur's control, such as access to finance, a stable economy and reliable electricity.

### Recognising an opportunity and starting a business (ZIMSEC detail)

ZIMSEC asks for the steps that lead from an idea to a business.

1. **Generate ideas** by noticing unmet needs, for example a lack of reliable solar-charging stations in a Mutare suburb.
2. **Test the idea** by asking potential customers, studying competitors and estimating costs.
3. **Write a business proposal (plan)** that states the product, market, costs, expected sales, the finance needed and how it will be repaid.
4. **Raise finance** for equipment and working funds.
5. **Meet legal requirements:** register the business, obtain any licences and permits that the activity requires (for example a licence to trade food), and register for tax.

## Specialisation and the division of labour

Specialisation can occur at several levels: a worker who becomes a nurse, a firm that makes only bricks, a region such as Hwange known for coal, and a country that exports tobacco and imports machinery. Within a factory, the division of labour can be seen where each worker does one stage.

:::worked-solution{title="How the division of labour raises output per worker"}
A fictional shoe workshop in Bulawayo has six workers. Working alone, each makes 2 pairs of shoes in a day. When the work is divided into cutting, stitching and finishing, with two workers on each stage, the workshop makes 42 pairs a day.

1. Output without division of labour: $6 \times 2 = 12$ pairs a day.
2. Output per worker: $12 \div 6 = 2$ pairs.
3. Output with division of labour: 42 pairs a day.
4. Output per worker: $42 \div 6 = 7$ pairs.
5. Increase in output per worker: $7 - 2 = 5$ pairs, which is $5 \div 2 \times 100 = 250\%$.
6. Unit labour cost falls if wages stay the same: with a daily wage of US\$8 per worker the wage cost per pair falls from $8 \div 2$ = US\$4.00 to $8 \div 7$ = US\$1.14.
:::

### Advantages

- **Higher output per worker** (productivity) because workers become skilled at one task through repetition.
- **Time saved** because workers do not move between tasks or change tools.
- **Suitable workers for each task:** people can be put on the task they do best.
- **Machines** can be introduced for simple, repeated tasks.
- **Lower unit costs** and, if passed on, lower prices.

### Disadvantages

- **Boredom and low motivation** from repeating one task, which can cause mistakes and absenteeism.
- **Interdependence:** if one stage stops (a machine breaks, workers are absent), the whole production line stops.
- **Loss of skills and flexibility:** workers who only know one task find it hard to change jobs.
- **Job loss from technology:** if tasks are simple, machines can replace workers.
- **Standardised products** may reduce variety.

:::example{title="Evaluating division of labour in a fictional clothing firm"}
The firm's output per worker rose by 250% after it divided the work, but absenteeism doubled.

1. For: the extra output lowers cost per pair, so profit rises or prices can fall.
2. Against: the repeated work is boring, so more workers stay away, and the whole line is slowed.
3. Judgement: the gain is probably worth keeping, but the firm should rotate workers between tasks and train them in more than one stage to reduce boredom and interdependence.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the entrepreneur is the manager or the owner only"}
An entrepreneur organises production *and* takes the risk. A hired manager who risks nothing is labour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing specialisation with division of labour"}
Specialisation is the general idea of concentrating on a limited range of activities; the division of labour is the splitting of a production process into tasks within a firm.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling profit the entrepreneur's wage"}
Profit is the reward for risk-taking and may be negative (a loss). Wages are paid regardless of profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing only advantages"}
A question that asks to *assess* division of labour needs both sides and a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and characteristics; Paper 2 may use a table of output per worker; Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 questions ask about risk and about the costs and benefits of specialisation, often in an evaluation part.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give a reason and its effect (for example repetition builds skill, so output per worker rises). *Calculate*: output per worker is total output ÷ number of workers. *Assess*: weigh advantages against disadvantages for the situation in the stem and reach a conclusion.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Generating ideas, a business proposal, licensing and registration are ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge concentrates on the role of the entrepreneur, risk-taking and the benefits and costs of specialisation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The entrepreneur organises production, takes risks and may innovate; profit is the reward.
- Successful entrepreneurs are creative, determined, organised and good at spotting opportunities.
- ZIMSEC: idea generation, a business proposal, registration and licensing lead to a new business.
- Specialisation and the division of labour raise output per worker through skill, time saving and machines.
- Disadvantages include boredom, interdependence, loss of flexibility and job loss.
:::
