## Introduction

In every country some households earn far more than others and some own far more than others. Two families in the same street can live very different lives: one with a regular salary and a house passed down from parents, another with irregular casual work and no savings. Economists want to measure this inequality, to explain why it arises, and to ask whether the government should reduce it, and how. The answers combine positive analysis (what causes inequality, what a tax does to incomes) with normative judgements (how much inequality is fair).

You should know how wages are determined by demand and supply, what taxes and subsidies do, and the difference between positive and normative statements. This lesson is for Cambridge AS Level. The Lorenz curve and the construction of the Gini coefficient belong to another lesson (development and income distribution); here you only need to describe the Gini coefficient as a measure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Income"}
A **flow** of money received over a period of time, such as wages, profits, rent, interest and benefits, measured per week, month or year.
:::

:::definition{term="Wealth"}
A **stock** of assets owned at a point in time (property, savings, shares, land, business capital) less debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Gini coefficient"}
A measure of inequality of income (or wealth) between 0 and 1 (or 0 and 100%). A value of 0 means perfect equality, where everyone has the same income; a value of 1 means perfect inequality, where one person has all of the income. A higher value means greater inequality.
:::

:::definition{term="Progressive, proportional and regressive taxes"}
A tax is **progressive** if the average rate of tax rises as income rises; **proportional** if the average rate is the same at all incomes; **regressive** if the average rate falls as income rises.
:::

:::definition{term="Transfer payment"}
A payment made by the government to individuals or households that is not a reward for producing output, such as a pension, a child grant or an unemployment benefit.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Flow and stock"}
Income is a flow (US\$ per year). Wealth is a stock (US\$ at a date). Wealth produces income (rent, interest, dividends), and saving out of income adds to wealth. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
:::

## Measuring inequality

- **The Gini coefficient** summarises the distribution in one number: for example 0.30 shows less inequality than 0.55.
- **Shares of income by group.** The population is divided into five equal groups (quintiles). The **quintile ratio** is the share of income going to the richest 20% divided by the share of the poorest 20%.

:::worked-solution{title="A quintile ratio (illustrative data)"}
In a fictional country the poorest 20% of households receive 4% of total income and the richest 20% receive 51%.

1. Quintile ratio = 51 ÷ 4 = **12.75**. The richest fifth receives nearly thirteen times as much income as the poorest fifth.
2. After taxes and transfers the shares become 6% and 44%. The ratio becomes 44 ÷ 6 = **7.33**.
3. The fall in the ratio from 12.75 to 7.33 shows that taxes and transfers have reduced inequality of disposable income.
4. The measures say nothing about wealth, and the quality of data (informal incomes are hard to record) limits their accuracy.
:::

## Economic reasons for inequality

1. **Differences in skills, education and ability.** Skilled workers have a high marginal revenue product and a restricted supply, so they earn high wages (see wage differentials).
2. **Differences in occupation and hours worked**, including unpaid or irregular work, and unemployment.
3. **Ownership of assets.** Wealth produces income, and those who own property, shares or land receive rent, interest and profit as well as wages.
4. **Inheritance.** Wealth passed from one generation to the next preserves inequality.
5. **Market power and discrimination.** Some groups face barriers (gender, location, ethnicity), and some firms and workers have market power.
6. **Age and the life cycle.** People earn less when young and old; wealth tends to peak late in life.
7. **Regional differences** in jobs, services and infrastructure.
8. **Government policy**: the tax and benefit system can widen or narrow inequality.

## Policies to redistribute income and wealth

### Progressive income taxes

Higher-income households pay a higher share of income in tax. Used with transfers, they reduce inequality of disposable income.

:::worked-solution{title="A progressive income tax (illustrative data)"}
Tax bands (per year): 0% on income up to US\$4,000; 20% on income from US\$4,000 to US\$12,000; 35% on income above US\$12,000.

| Household | Gross income (US\$) | Income tax (US\$) | Average tax rate (%) | Disposable income (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Household A | 3,600 | 0 | 0.0 | 3,600 |
| Household B | 10,000 | 1,200 | 12.0 | 8,800 |
| Household C | 30,000 | 7,900 | 26.3 | 22,100 |

1. Household B: (10,000 − 4,000) × 20% = US\$1,200. Average rate = 1,200 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **12.0%**.
2. Household C: 8,000 × 20% = US\$1,600, plus (30,000 − 12,000) × 35% = US\$6,300; total **US\$7,900**. Average rate = 7,900 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **26.3%**. The **marginal rate** on extra income is 35%.
3. The average rate rises with income (0%, 12.0%, 26.3%), so the tax is **progressive**.
4. Ratio of C's income to A's: before tax 30,000 ÷ 3,600 = **8.33**; after tax 22,100 ÷ 3,600 = **6.14**.
5. If household A also receives a cash transfer of US\$600, its disposable income is US\$4,200 and the ratio becomes 22,100 ÷ 4,200 = **5.26**.
:::

### Transfer payments

Pensions, child grants and unemployment benefits raise the incomes of poor households. They can be **universal** (paid to everyone) or **means-tested** (paid only to those below an income level). They cost money, must be financed from taxes or borrowing, and may reduce the incentive to work if benefits are withdrawn quickly as income rises.

### Minimum wage

A legal minimum wage raises the pay of the lowest paid. In a competitive labour market it may reduce employment (see the lesson on wage determination and intervention), so the effect on inequality depends on the elasticity of demand for labour and on how many workers lose jobs.

### Taxes on wealth, inheritance and capital

An inheritance tax is charged on property passed on at death; a capital gains tax is charged on profit made from selling assets; a tax on property is charged on holdings of land or buildings. They reduce the concentration of wealth and raise revenue, and are less likely to affect work incentives.

:::worked-solution{title="An inheritance tax (illustrative data)"}
An estate is worth US\$600,000. The first US\$100,000 is exempt and the rest is taxed at 20%.

1. Taxable amount = 600,000 − 100,000 = US\$500,000.
2. Tax = 500,000 × 20% = **US\$100,000**.
3. The heirs receive US\$500,000. The tax is progressive in effect, since small estates below US\$100,000 pay nothing.
4. Problems: wealthy families may find ways to avoid the tax (gifts before death, moving assets), and it may discourage saving and business succession.
:::

### State provision of essential goods and services

Free or low-cost education and health care help poor households in kind. They raise future incomes by improving skills and health, so they tackle the causes of inequality, but they cost money and quality can vary.

## Evaluating redistribution

- **Efficiency and equity.** Higher marginal tax rates may reduce the incentive to work, save and invest; but a more equal distribution may improve health, education and spending power.
- **Cost and administration.** Means testing needs information and costs to administer; universal benefits cost more but reach everyone and are easier to run.
- **Targeting.** Do policies reach the poorest, or do the better-off capture much of the benefit?
- **Time.** Education and health spending work over decades; cash transfers help immediately.
- **The informal economy.** Where many incomes are informal, income taxes and benefits are hard to apply.
- **Normative judgement.** What is "fair" depends on values. An economist can show the effects but not decide how much equality is right.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A progressive income tax combined with cash transfers can reduce the gap between high and low disposable incomes, as the quintile ratio fell from 12.75 to 7.33 in the example. However, high marginal tax rates may reduce incentives to work and encourage avoidance, and means-tested benefits are costly to run and may not reach the informal poor. State provision of education may do more to reduce inequality in the long run, as it raises earning ability, but it takes years to show results. The best policy mix depends on the causes of inequality in the country and on society's views about fairness.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing income and wealth"}
Income is a flow per period; wealth is a stock at a date. A person can have a high income and little wealth, or a large inherited estate and a low income.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a tax progressive because it raises more from the rich in cash terms"}
A tax is progressive only if the **average rate** rises with income. A flat 10% tax takes more dollars from the rich but is proportional.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting redistribution as costless"}
Redistribution has an opportunity cost, may affect incentives and has administrative costs. A balanced answer discusses both gains and costs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 1 asks for the meaning of terms (flow, stock, progressive, Gini) and simple calculations of tax and quintile ratios; Paper 2 asks for explanation of reasons for inequality and evaluation of redistribution policies in a data response or short essay.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: flow and stock. *Explain*: link a cause (skills, assets, inheritance) to a difference in income or wealth. *Calculate*: tax, average rate, disposable income, quintile ratio. *Evaluate*: weigh the effect on inequality against incentives, cost and administration, and reach a supported judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually more unequally distributed than income.
- The Gini coefficient describes inequality from 0 (equality) to 1 (one person has everything); the quintile ratio compares top and bottom fifths.
- Causes of inequality include skills, asset ownership, inheritance, discrimination, age and policy.
- Redistribution policies include progressive taxes, transfers, minimum wages, taxes on wealth and state provision; each has costs and incentives effects.
- Evaluation weighs equity against efficiency, cost, targeting and time, and recognises that views of fairness differ.
:::
