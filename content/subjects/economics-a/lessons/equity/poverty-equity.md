## Introduction

A society can choose to share its output equally, to share it in proportion to effort, or to share it according to need. Each of these can be defended as "fair", and each has different economic consequences. At the same time, a family may be poor in two different senses: it may lack the income needed for food, shelter and health (absolute poverty), or it may have much less than most people around it (relative poverty). Once a government decides to help, it must design benefits and taxes so that people are helped without losing the incentive to work, or being trapped in poverty by the benefit system itself.

You should already know about income and wealth inequality, progressive taxes and transfer payments (the AS lesson on inequality and redistribution). This lesson is for Cambridge A Level and develops the ideas of equity, poverty and the design of benefits.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Equality"}
A situation in which people receive the same income, wealth or opportunities, whatever their circumstances.
:::

:::definition{term="Equity"}
Fairness in the distribution of income, wealth or opportunities. What is fair is a **normative** judgement and may be defined as equality of outcomes, equality of opportunity, or treatment according to need or contribution.
:::

:::definition{term="Efficiency"}
The use of scarce resources to produce the most output or welfare. Allocative efficiency means producing the goods that society values most at the lowest cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Absolute poverty"}
A situation in which a person's income is below a fixed minimum needed to meet basic needs (food, shelter, clothing, health), the same standard in all time periods, often expressed as a money line per day.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative poverty"}
A situation in which a person's income is far below that of others in the same society, commonly defined as income below a fraction (for example 60%) of the median income.
:::

:::definition{term="Poverty trap"}
A situation in which a poor person who earns more loses so much in extra tax and withdrawn benefits that the gain from working more is very small, so there is little incentive to escape poverty.
:::

## Equity, equality and efficiency

- **Equity is not the same as equality.** Equal shares may be unfair if people have different needs or put in different effort. Equity is a judgement about what is fair; equality is a measurable condition.
- **Equity and efficiency may conflict.** A very high tax on high earners can reduce inequality but may reduce effort, saving and investment and so the size of the national output. Some argue instead that reducing extreme inequality improves efficiency by raising health, education and spending power among the poor, and avoiding social tension. The relationship is disputed: it depends on the size of the redistribution and the way it is carried out.
- **Opportunity or outcome.** Policies that equalise opportunity (education, health, access to credit) are widely seen as promoting both equity and efficiency; policies that equalise outcomes (high taxes and large transfers) raise bigger questions about incentives.

## Measuring poverty

:::worked-solution{title="Absolute and relative poverty (illustrative data)"}
Eleven households in a fictional town have the following annual incomes per person (US\$): 1,200; 1,800; 2,400; 3,000; 3,600; 4,200; 5,400; 6,600; 9,000; 15,000; 24,000.

1. The **absolute** poverty line is set at US\$3 a day: 3 × 365 = **US\$1,095** a year. No household has an income below this line, so absolute poverty is **0 of 11** households.
2. The median income is the middle (6th) value: **US\$4,200**.
3. The **relative** poverty line at 60% of the median = 0.6 × 4,200 = **US\$2,520**.
4. Households below US\$2,520 are those with 1,200, 1,800 and 2,400: **3 of 11**, which is 3 ÷ 11 × 100 = **27.3%**.
5. The two measures give different answers. Absolute poverty measures lack of basic needs against a fixed standard; relative poverty measures inequality at the lower end. Relative poverty can persist even when incomes rise for everyone, and falls only if the lowest incomes rise faster than the median.
:::

## The poverty trap

Means-tested benefits are reduced as earnings rise. Combined with income tax and other charges, a worker who earns more may keep only a small part of the extra.

:::worked-solution{title="The poverty trap (illustrative data)"}
A poor worker earns an extra US\$100. As a result she loses US\$60 of means-tested benefits and pays US\$15 more in tax and contributions.

1. Net gain = 100 − 60 − 15 = **US\$25**.
2. Effective marginal tax rate = (60 + 15) ÷ 100 × 100 = **75%**.
3. At such a high rate, the incentive to work more or take a better job is weak. The worker is "trapped": earning more barely raises her disposable income.
4. A smaller withdrawal rate (for example benefits reduced by 30 cents for each extra dollar earned) would reduce the trap, but it would extend benefits further up the income scale and cost more.
:::

The trap arises from the design of means-tested benefits, not from the poor being unwilling to work. It is worse where there are many overlapping benefits, each with its own withdrawal rate, and where low-paid jobs are insecure.

## Policies

### Means-tested and universal benefits

**Means-tested benefits** are paid only to those whose income or assets are below a threshold. They target money at the poorest, so they cost less per beneficiary, but they require administration, can stigmatise recipients, may fail to reach those eligible (low take-up) and create poverty traps.

**Universal benefits** are paid to everyone in a category (all children, all pensioners) regardless of income. They are simple, avoid stigma and have no poverty trap, but they cost more and a part of the money goes to people who do not need it unless it is recovered through taxation.

### Negative income tax (NIT)

A **negative income tax** combines taxes and benefits in one scheme. Below a break-even income, people receive a payment from the state; above it they pay tax. The payment is a guaranteed minimum (G) reduced by a fixed rate (t) of earnings.

:::worked-solution{title="A negative income tax (illustrative data)"}
The guaranteed minimum is G = US\$3,000 a year and the withdrawal rate is t = 40%. Payment = G − t × earnings.

| Earnings (US\$ a year) | Payment from (or tax to) the state (US\$) | Total income after NIT (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 3,000 | 3,000 |
| 2,000 | 2,200 | 4,200 |
| 5,000 | 1,000 | 6,000 |
| 7,500 | 0 | 7,500 |
| 10,000 | −1,000 | 9,000 |

1. Payment at earnings of US\$2,000: 3,000 − 0.4 × 2,000 = **US\$2,200**. Total income = **US\$4,200**.
2. Break-even income = G ÷ t = 3,000 ÷ 0.4 = **US\$7,500**. Above this, the person pays tax.
3. For every extra dollar earned the person keeps 60 cents, so work always pays: total income rises with earnings. The effective marginal rate (40%) is lower than in the poverty trap example, but a lower rate or higher guarantee raises the cost.
4. Strengths: simple, one scheme, preserves work incentives, reaches working poor. Weaknesses: cost; a generous guarantee may reduce work; needs reliable information on incomes, which is difficult where many people work informally.
:::

### Universal basic income (UBI)

A **UBI** pays a regular cash sum to every citizen, without a means test or work requirement, usually financed from taxes.

:::worked-solution{title="Cost of a UBI (illustrative data)"}
A fictional country has 8 million adults. A UBI of US\$1,200 a year per adult is proposed.

1. Gross cost = 8,000,000 × 1,200 = US\$9,600,000,000 = **US\$9.6 billion** a year.
2. Part is recovered if the extra income is taxed, and other benefits could be replaced, so the net cost is smaller; but the scheme still needs large new revenue.
3. A UBI avoids the poverty trap (it is not withdrawn as earnings rise) and has low administration costs, but it gives money to people who do not need it and may be too small to remove poverty or too costly if it is large.
:::

### Other measures

Improving education and health, minimum wages, support for small enterprises and microfinance, and policies to create jobs address the causes of poverty rather than its symptoms. They take longer to work.

## Evaluating policies

| Policy | Main benefit | Main drawback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Means-tested benefit | Targets the poorest; lower cost | Poverty trap; stigma; low take-up; administration |
| Universal benefit | Simple; no stigma; no trap | Higher cost; money goes to those who do not need it |
| Negative income tax | One scheme; work always pays | Cost; needs good income data |
| Universal basic income | No trap; low administration | Very high cost; may be too small to remove poverty |

Questions to ask in an evaluation: how many people live in poverty and in which form; how much can the economy afford; how good is the information on incomes; how do people respond to incentives; and what is the time horizon? The answers differ between countries and different people have different views of what is fair.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A negative income tax may be better than separate means-tested benefits because it reduces the poverty trap: with a 40% withdrawal rate the recipient always keeps 60 cents of each extra dollar. However, it requires reliable information on incomes, which is difficult in an economy with a large informal sector, and a high guaranteed minimum would be costly. A government might therefore combine a simple cash transfer for those who are clearly poor with investment in education and health that raises earnings in the long run.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using equity and equality as synonyms"}
Equality describes sameness; equity describes fairness. An equitable outcome may be unequal (for example payments according to need).
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating relative poverty as less serious than absolute poverty"}
They are different measures. A person can be in relative poverty even when able to afford basic needs, and relative poverty can persist when the economy is growing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming the poor for the poverty trap"}
The trap results from the design of benefits and tax rates, which can leave little reward for working more. It is a policy problem.
:::

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: equity and equality, absolute and relative poverty. *Explain*: how the poverty trap arises (withdrawal of benefits and extra tax). *Calculate*: effective marginal rate, break-even income, share below a line. *Evaluate*: compare the policies on targeting, cost, incentives and administration, and reach a justified judgement for the context.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Equality means sameness; equity means fairness, a normative judgement; efficiency is the best use of resources, and equity and efficiency may conflict, though not always.
- Absolute poverty uses a fixed standard of basic needs; relative poverty compares with the median income of society.
- The poverty trap occurs when extra earnings lead to loss of benefits and extra tax, so the effective marginal rate is high.
- Means-tested benefits target the poor but create traps; universal benefits and UBI avoid traps but cost more; a negative income tax pays a guarantee reduced at a fixed rate.
- Evaluation weighs targeting, cost, incentives, information and administration in the country's context.
:::
