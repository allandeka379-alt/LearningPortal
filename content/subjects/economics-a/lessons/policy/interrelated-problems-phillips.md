## Introduction

A government that cuts interest rates to reduce unemployment finds that prices start to rise; one that squeezes demand to control inflation finds that jobs disappear; one that stimulates growth finds that imports surge and the currency weakens. Macroeconomic problems are linked, so action on one often changes another. A famous example is the **Phillips curve**, which suggests that low unemployment and low inflation are hard to have together. This lesson sets out the links and the two versions of the Phillips curve, and explains why the trade-off may exist in the short run but not in the long run.

You should know the objectives of macroeconomic policy, AD and AS, inflation and the natural rate of unemployment from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Internal value of money"}
The purchasing power of money inside the country: what a unit of the currency buys of domestic goods and services. It falls when the price level rises.
:::

:::definition{term="External value of money"}
The purchasing power of the currency abroad, shown by the exchange rate: how much foreign currency a unit of the domestic currency buys.
:::

:::definition{term="Phillips curve"}
A curve which shows the relationship between the rate of unemployment (horizontal axis) and the rate of inflation (vertical axis). The **traditional** Phillips curve slopes downwards: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation. The **short-run Phillips curve (SRPC)** is drawn for a given expected rate of inflation; the **long-run Phillips curve (LRPC)** is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Stagflation"}
A combination of stagnant or falling output, high unemployment and high inflation at the same time.
:::

## The internal and external value of money

If prices rise by 25%, the purchasing power of US\$100 falls to 100 ÷ 1.25 = **US\$80** of the old prices: the internal value of money has fallen by 20%. If the price level in a country rises faster than in its trading partners and the exchange rate does not change, its goods become dearer abroad, which affects the external value and competitiveness. The two values are linked: if the currency's external value falls (depreciation), import prices rise, and the internal value falls through imported inflation.

## Links between macroeconomic problems

1. **The balance of payments and inflation.** Higher inflation than abroad makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account worsens. A deficit may lead to a depreciation, which raises import prices and inflation again (a spiral). Conversely, a country with low inflation tends to gain competitiveness.
2. **Growth and inflation.** Fast growth of demand raises output, but when it exceeds capacity (a positive output gap) it causes demand-pull inflation. Slow growth reduces inflationary pressure but raises unemployment.
3. **Growth and the balance of payments.** Rising incomes raise spending on imports, because the marginal propensity to import is positive, so fast growth tends to worsen the current account. A country with a deficit may need to slow demand, which reduces growth.
4. **Growth and unemployment.** Growth in output creates jobs, so unemployment falls (a relationship sometimes called Okun's law), but growth through labour-saving technology may create fewer jobs.
5. **Inflation and unemployment.** The Phillips curve trade-off (below).
6. **Unemployment and the budget.** High unemployment cuts tax revenue and raises benefit spending, so the budget deficit rises.

:::worked-solution{title="Inflation and competitiveness"}
Domestic prices in Zandia rise by 10% in a year while prices in its trading partners rise by 3%. The nominal exchange rate is unchanged.
1. Relative price of Zandian goods = 1.10 ÷ 1.03 = 1.068: they are **6.8% dearer** relative to foreign goods.
2. Exports become less competitive and imports cheaper, so the current account deficit is likely to widen.
3. If this causes the currency to depreciate, import prices rise and inflation may increase again.
:::

## The traditional Phillips curve

In 1958 A. W. Phillips found a stable inverse relationship between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages in the United Kingdom. Later versions relate unemployment and the rate of inflation. The explanation is based on demand: when aggregate demand is high, unemployment is low, labour is scarce and wages and prices rise faster; when demand is weak, unemployment is high and inflation is low.

Illustrative data for a fictional economy:

| Unemployment rate (%) | Inflation rate (%) |
| ---: | ---: |
| 3 | 6.0 |
| 4 | 4.5 |
| 5 | 3.5 |
| 6 | 2.8 |
| 8 | 2.0 |

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the trade-off"}
1. A downward-sloping curve: as unemployment falls from 6% to 4%, inflation rises from 2.8% to 4.5%, an increase of **1.7 percentage points**.
2. The trade-off is not constant. Moving from 8% to 6% unemployment costs only 0.8 percentage points of extra inflation, but from 4% to 3% costs 1.5. The curve gets steeper at low unemployment because of shortages of labour.
3. A government must choose a point on the curve: its judgement about which problem is worse determines the policy.
:::

![Traditional Phillips curve from the data: inflation falls as unemployment rises, from 6.0% at 3% unemployment to 4.5% at 4%, 3.5% at 5%, 2.8% at 6% and 2.0% at 8%; the curve is steeper at low unemployment, so the trade-off worsens.](media:diagrams/phillips-curve-data.png "Figure: the traditional Phillips curve shows lower unemployment going with higher inflation")

**The breakdown of the relationship.** In the 1970s many economies experienced high inflation and high unemployment together (stagflation), as oil price rises raised costs and expectations of inflation rose. The simple curve could not explain this. Two ideas were used to explain it: **supply shocks** (a leftward shift of short-run aggregate supply), and **expectations**.

## The expectations-augmented Phillips curve

Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that workers and firms care about **real** wages, so when they expect inflation they build it into wage and price decisions. The short-run curve therefore depends on the expected rate of inflation (πe):

:::keyfact{title="Expectations-augmented Phillips curve (illustration)"}
Inflation = expected inflation + a × (natural rate of unemployment − actual unemployment), where a measures how strongly inflation responds to the gap.
:::

If a government tries to hold unemployment below the natural rate, actual inflation exceeds expected inflation. Once people adjust their expectations, the SRPC shifts upwards, and inflation rises further each year.

:::worked-solution{title="Accelerating inflation below the natural rate"}
Natural rate = 5%, a = 0.5, expected inflation starts at 3%, and the government keeps unemployment at 4%.
1. Year 1: inflation = 3 + 0.5 × (5 − 4) = **3.5%**. Expectations adjust to 3.5%.
2. Year 2: inflation = 3.5 + 0.5 = **4.0%**.
3. Year 3: inflation = **4.5%**, year 4: **5.0%**: inflation rises by 0.5 percentage points every year.
4. At 5% unemployment (the natural rate), inflation equals expected inflation and does not accelerate. This is why the natural rate is also the **non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU)**, and the **long-run Phillips curve is vertical** at that rate: in the long run there is no trade-off.
:::

![Expectations-augmented Phillips curve with natural rate 5%: holding unemployment at 4% gives inflation 3.5% on the first short-run curve (expected inflation 3%), then each year expectations catch up and the curve shifts up by 0.5, giving 4.0%, 4.5% and 5.0%; the long-run curve is vertical at 5% unemployment.](media:diagrams/phillips-expectations-augmented.png "Figure: keeping unemployment at 4%, below the natural rate, shifts the short-run curve up every year; the long-run curve is vertical at 5%")

Describe the diagram in words: unemployment is on the horizontal axis and inflation on the vertical axis; the SRPC slopes downward and cuts the vertical LRPC at the natural rate; a rise in expected inflation shifts the SRPC upward; a fall in the natural rate (through supply-side policy) shifts the LRPC to the left.

**Shifts of the SRPC** are caused by changes in expected inflation, supply shocks (oil, exchange rate falls) and changes in productivity; the **LRPC** shifts with the natural rate, which depends on labour market flexibility, skills, benefits and hysteresis.

:::example{title="Policy implications"}
In the short run a government can reduce unemployment by raising demand but only at the cost of more inflation, and the gain disappears when expectations adjust. In the long run it can reduce the natural rate, and so escape the trade-off, by supply-side policies (training, mobility, flexible labour markets). Controlling inflation expectations through a credible policy (for example an announced inflation target) can lower the SRPC, so that disinflation costs less unemployment.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying there is a permanent trade-off between inflation and unemployment"}
The trade-off exists only in the short run. In the long run, when expectations have adjusted, the Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing the wrong axes"}
Unemployment is on the horizontal axis and inflation on the vertical axis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a movement along the SRPC with a shift"}
A change in demand moves the economy along the curve; a change in expectations or a supply shock shifts the whole curve.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 3 ask about the links between problems and the conflict between inflation and unemployment; Paper 2 may supply data on inflation and unemployment. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask you to identify the curves in a described diagram and to explain shifts in the SRPC and LRPC and their policy implications.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give the chain (for example demand rises, unemployment falls, wages rise, inflation rises). *Distinguish*: short run (trade-off) from long run (vertical). *Evaluate*: consider whether the trade-off is stable, the role of expectations and supply shocks, and the policy options.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The internal value of money is its purchasing power at home; the external value is shown by the exchange rate.
- Inflation, growth, unemployment and the balance of payments are linked: higher inflation harms competitiveness; fast growth raises imports.
- The traditional Phillips curve shows a short-run trade-off between unemployment and inflation.
- The expectations-augmented curve shifts with expected inflation; the long-run curve is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU).
- Stagflation shows that supply shocks and expectations can break the simple trade-off; supply-side policy shifts the LRPC to the left.
:::
