## Introduction

Policy-makers have a set of objectives (stable prices, jobs, growth, a sustainable external balance, fairness and a healthy environment) and a small set of tools. Each tool helps with some objectives and hurts others, works with a delay, and depends on how people react. A tax cut may raise growth but also imports; high interest rates may lower inflation but also jobs. The questions that examiners ask are therefore evaluative: *how effective is this policy, against which objective, and at what cost?*

You should know the objectives, the AD/AS model, the multiplier and the Phillips curve from the earlier lessons. The detailed workings of tax, money and supply-side policy belong to separate lessons; this one weighs them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Policy conflict (trade-off)"}
A situation in which a policy that improves one macroeconomic objective worsens another, or in which two objectives cannot both be achieved by the same policy instrument at the same time.
:::

:::definition{term="Government failure (in macroeconomic policy)"}
A situation in which government intervention fails to improve outcomes, or makes them worse, because of poor information, time lags, mistaken forecasts, crowding out, unintended side effects or political motives.
:::

:::definition{term="Laffer curve"}
A curve showing the relationship between the tax rate and tax revenue: revenue is zero at a tax rate of 0% and at 100%, and rises and then falls in between because high rates reduce the tax base (through lower effort, avoidance and evasion). The tax rate that maximises revenue is the point at the top of the curve.
:::

## The policies and the objectives

| Policy | Growth | Unemployment | Inflation | Balance of payments | Main side effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Expansionary fiscal** (more spending, lower taxes) | Raises | Reduces | Raises | Worsens (imports rise) | Deficit, debt, crowding out |
| **Contractionary fiscal** | Lowers | Raises | Reduces | Improves | Public services cut |
| **Tight monetary** (higher interest rates) | Lowers | Raises | Reduces | May improve (capital inflow, lower imports) | Higher cost of investment; currency may rise |
| **Loose monetary** | Raises | Reduces | Raises | May worsen (currency falls) | Asset bubbles, inflation |
| **Supply-side, market-based** (lower taxes on enterprise, deregulation, flexible labour markets) | Raises potential growth | Reduces (natural rate) | Reduces (costs fall) | May improve | Inequality may rise; slow; costs |
| **Supply-side, interventionist** (education, infrastructure, R&D) | Raises potential growth | Reduces structural | Reduces | Improves competitiveness | Cost, time lag, uncertain returns |
| **Exchange rate depreciation** | Raises (if exports rise) | Reduces | Raises (import prices) | Improves if demand is elastic (Marshall–Lerner) | Higher import prices; J-curve |
| **Protection** (tariffs, quotas) | May protect jobs | Short-run gain | Raises prices | Improves short term | Retaliation, inefficiency, higher prices |

(The effect on the balance of payments of exchange rate changes is studied in the trade topics.)

### Effectiveness: what to weigh

- **Cause of the problem.** Demand-side policy for demand-pull inflation and cyclical unemployment; supply-side policy for structural unemployment and cost-push inflation.
- **Size and speed.** Monetary policy can be changed quickly, but its effect is delayed; fiscal policy needs a budget decision and its effects build slowly; supply-side policy takes years.
- **Strength of the response.** If firms and households are pessimistic, lower interest rates may fail to raise spending; if consumers expect higher taxes in future, they may save a tax cut.
- **State of the economy.** Near full capacity, expansion raises prices; with spare capacity it raises output.
- **Openness.** In a small open economy a large part of any boost leaks into imports, and the exchange rate regime limits the power of monetary policy.
- **Credibility and expectations.** A policy that people believe (for example an inflation target) works faster and at a lower cost.

## The Laffer curve

Suppose that the tax base (taxable income, US\$ million) of a fictional economy falls as the tax rate rises: base = 2,000 − 20t, where t is the tax rate in percent.

| Tax rate t (%) | Tax base (US\$ million) | Tax revenue (US\$ million) |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 2,000 | 0 |
| 10 | 1,800 | 180 |
| 20 | 1,600 | 320 |
| 30 | 1,400 | 420 |
| 40 | 1,200 | 480 |
| 50 | 1,000 | 500 |
| 60 | 800 | 480 |
| 70 | 600 | 420 |
| 80 | 400 | 320 |
| 100 | 0 | 0 |

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the Laffer curve"}
1. Revenue = t ÷ 100 × base. At 30%: 0.30 × 1,400 = **US\$420 million**; at 50%: 0.50 × 1,000 = **US\$500 million**; at 70%: 0.70 × 600 = **US\$420 million**.
2. Revenue is highest at **50%** (US\$500 million). Raising the rate from 30% to 40% raises revenue (420 to 480), but raising it from 60% to 70% **reduces** revenue (480 to 420), because the base shrinks faster than the rate rises.
3. Cutting the rate from 70% to 50% would increase revenue by US\$80 million, but cutting from 30% to 20% would reduce revenue (420 to 320).
4. Caution: the curve is a model. It is hard to know where an actual economy lies on it, and the revenue-maximising rate is not necessarily the best from the viewpoint of fairness or efficiency.
:::

## Policy conflicts

1. **Inflation against unemployment** (short-run Phillips trade-off): lower unemployment through demand raises inflation.
2. **Growth against the balance of payments:** faster growth raises imports and worsens the current account.
3. **Growth against inflation:** fast demand growth may create inflationary pressure.
4. **Growth against the environment and sustainability.**
5. **Growth/efficiency against equity:** market-based reforms may raise output but widen inequality; redistribution may weaken incentives.
6. **Price stability against growth in the short run:** higher interest rates reduce inflation but also investment.
7. **Policy instruments conflict:** a central bank may want high interest rates to control inflation while the government wants low rates to reduce the cost of debt.

Governments cope by setting priorities, using several instruments for several objectives (for example, fiscal policy for demand and supply-side policy for capacity), using targeted measures, and accepting a mix of results.

## Government failure in macroeconomic policy

- **Time lags:** recognition, decision and impact lags mean a policy designed for a slump may take effect in a boom, increasing instability.
- **Poor information and forecasts:** the size of the output gap, the natural rate and the multiplier are estimates; data are late and revised.
- **Crowding out:** borrowing for fiscal expansion raises interest rates and reduces private investment.
- **Political motives:** governments may spend before elections (a political business cycle), delay unpopular measures or resist cuts.
- **Unintended consequences:** price controls cause shortages; high taxes cause evasion; protection invites retaliation.
- **Inconsistent policy** and weak credibility: frequent changes make it hard for people to plan.
- **Limited power:** small open economies cannot control world prices, interest rates or demand.

:::example{title="Evaluating policy for a developing economy such as Zimbabwe"}
For an economy with a large informal sector, a narrow tax base and heavy dependence on a few exports, fiscal policy is limited by the small share of people who pay income tax, and automatic stabilisers are weak; monetary policy can be weakened if few people use bank credit and if people hold foreign currency; supply-side measures such as infrastructure, skills and a stable policy environment may have the largest long-run effect, but they take time and cost money; and the exchange rate matters for an economy that imports fuel and machinery. A balanced judgement says which constraints are most severe and which measures are feasible, and acknowledges the trade-offs between policies rather than recommending a single remedy.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the evaluation of the main policies against each objective, policy conflicts and government failure. Laffer curve analysis is named for both. ZIMSEC (Form 6) asks for evaluation in the context of the Zimbabwean economy (ZIMSEC detail) and Cambridge (A Level) expects the market-based and interventionist forms of supply-side policy to be distinguished.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing policies without judging effectiveness"}
Say how well the policy works for the specific problem, how quickly and at what cost, and give a judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the Laffer curve as proof that tax cuts always raise revenue"}
A tax cut raises revenue only if the economy is on the downward-sloping part of the curve; otherwise revenue falls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the effects on other objectives"}
A policy aimed at one objective always affects others; a full answer considers the conflict.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays and Paper 2 final parts ask you to evaluate fiscal, monetary or supply-side policy for an objective or for Zimbabwe; Cambridge Paper 4 essays ask you to assess the effectiveness of a policy or the extent of a conflict, and Paper 3 tests the Laffer curve and government failure in shorter items.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Assess* or *evaluate*: give how the policy works, then judge using the cause of the problem, the strength and speed of the effect, the state of the economy, side effects on other objectives and the possibility of government failure. Support the judgement with the case or data given.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Policies are judged by their effect on each objective, their speed, strength and side effects.
- Demand-side policy suits cyclical problems; supply-side policy suits structural problems and raises potential output slowly.
- Common conflicts are inflation against unemployment, growth against the balance of payments and the environment, and efficiency against equity.
- The Laffer curve shows revenue rising and then falling as the tax rate rises; the top of the curve is the revenue-maximising rate.
- Government failure arises from time lags, poor information, crowding out, political motives and unintended effects.
:::
