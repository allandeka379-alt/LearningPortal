## Introduction

When the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe changes its policy interest rate, tightens the rules on bank lending or sells government securities, it is using **monetary policy**. These decisions are made by the central bank rather than by the Minister of Finance, and they affect the cost and availability of money for households and firms: the rate on a car loan, a farmer's seasonal borrowing, a company's decision to build a new factory. Changes in borrowing and spending alter aggregate demand, and so output, employment and the price level.

You should know what aggregate demand and aggregate supply are, what the central bank does (see the lesson on banks and the central bank) and the difference between nominal and real interest rates. The arithmetic of how banks create deposits is in the lesson on credit creation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monetary policy"}
The use by the central bank (or government) of the rate of interest, the supply of money and the availability of credit to influence aggregate demand and so the price level, output and employment.
:::

:::definition{term="Policy interest rate"}
The rate set by the central bank at which it lends to commercial banks (or pays them on reserves). It influences the other interest rates in the economy, such as the rates that banks charge on loans and pay on savings.
:::

:::definition{term="Expansionary and contractionary monetary policy"}
**Expansionary** (easy) monetary policy lowers interest rates or increases the money supply and credit to raise aggregate demand. **Contractionary** (tight) monetary policy raises interest rates or reduces the money supply and credit to reduce aggregate demand.
:::

## The tools of monetary policy

| Tool | How it works | Expansionary use | Contractionary use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interest rate | Changes the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving | Lower the policy rate | Raise the policy rate |
| Open-market operations | The central bank buys or sells government securities, changing banks' reserves | Buy securities (reserves rise) | Sell securities (reserves fall) |
| Reserve requirements | Sets the minimum share of deposits that banks must hold as reserves | Lower the ratio | Raise the ratio |
| Credit regulations | Limits on the amount or type of lending (credit ceilings, minimum deposits, directions to lend to certain sectors) | Relax the limits | Tighten the limits |

Interest rates are the main tool in many countries; open-market operations require well-developed markets for government securities. In economies with small financial markets, central banks often rely more on reserve requirements and direct credit controls. The tools are used in combination: a central bank that wants to tighten policy may raise its rate and sell securities at the same time.

## The transmission mechanism

The **transmission mechanism** is the chain of events from a change in the policy instrument to a change in the economy. For a **rise** in the policy rate:

1. The central bank raises its policy interest rate (or sells securities).
2. Commercial banks raise the rates they charge on loans and overdrafts, and the rates they pay on deposits.
3. Borrowing becomes more expensive, so firms cut investment and households cut spending on credit-financed goods such as cars and houses. Higher saving rates encourage households to save more and consume less.
4. Higher interest rates may attract foreign funds, raising the exchange rate; exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so net exports fall. In some countries, asset prices fall and households feel poorer (a wealth effect).
5. Consumption (C), investment (I) and net exports (X − M) all fall, so aggregate demand shifts to the left.
6. Real output and employment fall in the short run and the price level rises more slowly or falls.

A fall in the policy rate works in the opposite direction.

| Interest rate (%) | Planned investment (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: |
| 4 | 420 |
| 6 | 360 |
| 8 | 300 |
| 10 | 250 |
| 12 | 210 |

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of an interest rate cut on aggregate demand"}
Using the investment schedule above, the central bank cuts its rate so that the bank lending rate falls from 10% to 6%. The multiplier is 2.
1. Planned investment rises from US\$250 million to US\$360 million, a rise of **US\$110 million**.
2. As a percentage, the rise is 110 ÷ 250 × 100 = **44%**.
3. Maximum rise in aggregate demand = 2 × 110 = **US\$220 million**.
4. This is the size of the AD shift (the rightward shift of AD) if nothing else changes. In practice, it takes time, and output rises by less if prices rise.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Real interest rates"}
A bank charges a nominal rate of 10% and inflation is 6%.
1. Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate = 10% − 6% = **4%**.
2. If inflation rises to 12% with the same nominal rate, the real rate is 10% − 12% = **−2%**. Borrowers gain and savers lose, so borrowing is encouraged even though the nominal rate looks high.
3. A central bank that wants to reduce spending must raise nominal rates by enough to make the real rate positive and significant.
:::

## Open-market operations in more detail

When the central bank **sells** government securities to the public and banks, buyers pay with funds from their bank accounts. Banks' deposits at the central bank (their reserves) fall, so they can lend less and money supply growth slows. Buying securities does the opposite. Because banks can create several dollars of deposits for each dollar of reserves, a small change in reserves can have a large effect on lending (see the lesson on credit creation).

## Limits of monetary policy

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
Monetary policy has advantages: decisions can be taken quickly by an independent central bank, small changes in interest rates are possible and it is less open to political pressure than changes in taxes and spending. However, the effect may be weak or slow. Investment may depend more on expected sales than on interest rates, so firms may not borrow even when rates are low; banks may not lend; many people use informal finance, which the central bank cannot influence; and the effect on spending takes months to appear. Raising interest rates also does little if inflation is caused by rising costs from imports rather than by excess demand. Monetary policy is therefore most effective for controlling demand-pull inflation in an economy with a developed banking system.
:::

Other points to remember in evaluation:

- **Time lags.** Policy affects the economy after a delay of several months, and the effect on prices takes longer.
- **Confidence.** When confidence is low, firms and households will not borrow even at low rates.
- **Conflicts.** A rise in rates to control inflation may slow growth and raise unemployment; an exchange rate that rises may harm exporters.
- **Credibility.** If people expect the central bank to keep inflation low, expectations adjust and policy works better.
- **Financial inclusion.** The policy works through banks, so it is weaker where much of the economy uses cash, mobile money or informal credit.
- **Use of foreign currencies.** An economy that uses another country's currency for much of its transactions has less control over its money supply and interest rates.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up fiscal and monetary policy"}
Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes. Monetary policy uses interest rates, the money supply and credit. Do not describe a tax cut as monetary policy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the direction of the tools"}
Selling securities **reduces** banks' reserves and tightens policy. Buying securities increases reserves and eases policy. A higher reserve requirement reduces lending.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stopping at the interest rate"}
In an answer on the effects of a rate change, continue the chain: borrowing, investment and consumption, aggregate demand, then output and prices.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}

Cambridge Paper 1 tests the tools and effects; Paper 2 data responses and essays ask you to analyse and assess a change in interest rates.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: complete the chain from the instrument to AD and then to output and prices. *Calculate*: use the schedule to find the change in investment, then apply the multiplier. *Assess* or *evaluate*: balance the strengths (speed, flexibility) against the limits (lags, confidence, cost-push inflation, financial structure) and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Monetary policy uses interest rates, the money supply (open-market operations and reserve requirements) and credit regulations to influence aggregate demand.
- Raising rates or selling securities is contractionary; lowering rates or buying securities is expansionary.
- The transmission mechanism runs from the policy rate to bank rates, borrowing, consumption, investment and the exchange rate, then to AD, output and prices.
- The real interest rate (nominal minus inflation) matters for spending decisions.
- Effects are limited by time lags, low confidence, cost-push inflation, informal finance and conflicts with other objectives.
:::
