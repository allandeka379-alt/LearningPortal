## Introduction

When a finance minister presents a budget or a central bank announces an interest rate, they are trying to improve the economy against a set of goals. Households want stable prices and jobs; firms want steady growth and a predictable currency; the country as a whole wants to pay its way in the world and to share the gains fairly without damaging the environment. These are the **macroeconomic objectives**. This lesson lists them, explains how to tell whether the economy is meeting them, and shows how to judge performance from data. The next lessons look at how the objectives conflict and how policies try to achieve them.

You should know the measures of national income and growth, unemployment, inflation and the balance of payments from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Macroeconomic objective"}
A goal for the performance of the whole economy that governments try to achieve through macroeconomic policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Policy target"}
A numerical value or range for an indicator which policy-makers aim to achieve, for example an inflation rate of 3% to 5% or real growth of 5% a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Policy instrument"}
A tool which the government or central bank controls directly, such as the interest rate, tax rates, government spending or the exchange rate regime, used to influence the target.
:::

## The main objectives and how they are measured

| Objective | Why it matters | Main indicator | Typical target or aim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Price stability** | Stable prices protect savings and incomes, allow planning, support competitiveness | Inflation rate (CPI) | Low, stable inflation, for example 2% to 5% |
| **Low unemployment (full employment)** | Wasted output and hardship | Unemployment rate; employment rate | A rate close to the natural rate |
| **Economic growth** | Raises incomes and tax revenue | Growth of real GDP and real GDP per head | A stable rate of real growth, for example 4% to 6% in a developing economy |
| **Economic development** | Better health, education and incomes | Human development index, poverty rate | Reduce poverty; raise indicators |
| **Balance of payments stability** | Avoids unsustainable borrowing or loss of reserves, protects the exchange rate | Current account balance as a percentage of GDP | Keep the deficit at a sustainable level (for example below 3% to 5% of GDP) |
| **Equity (fair distribution)** | A fairer sharing of income | Gini coefficient, share of the poorest fifth, poverty rate | Reduce inequality and poverty |
| **Sustainability** | Protects the environment and future output | Emissions, resource use, adjusted net savings | Growth that does not exhaust resources |

Governments rarely pursue only one objective, and different governments weight them differently. A government that cares most about jobs may accept some inflation; one that wants a stable currency may tolerate slower growth. Targets also differ: many central banks set an **inflation target**; many governments set growth targets and limits for the budget deficit and public debt.

### Explaining each objective

1. **Price stability** does not mean zero inflation. Moderate, steady inflation is tolerated, but high or erratic inflation damages decisions and redistributes incomes. A central bank may also be concerned about deflation.
2. **Low unemployment.** Full employment means that cyclical unemployment is removed; some frictional and structural unemployment remains. The employment of young people and rural workers is often a special concern in developing economies.
3. **Growth and development.** Growth raises output per head; policy-makers also want it to be sustainable and shared, and to improve health and education.
4. **Balance of payments.** A persistent large current account deficit has to be financed by borrowing, foreign investment or running down reserves and may lead to a fall in the currency; a large surplus may mean that the country is not using its income at home.
5. **Equity.** The distribution of income and wealth, and access to services.
6. **Sustainability.** Growth that meets present needs without damaging future ability to produce.

## Measuring performance

:::worked-solution{title="Current account as a percentage of GDP"}
A fictional economy has GDP of US\$14,000 million. Its current account (US\$ million): exports of goods 4,200 and imports 5,100; services exports 800 and imports 600; primary income received 150 and paid 400; secondary income received 700 and paid 100.
1. Goods balance = 4,200 − 5,100 = **−900**. Services = 800 − 600 = **+200**. Primary income = 150 − 400 = **−250**. Secondary income = 700 − 100 = **+600**.
2. Current account balance = −900 + 200 − 250 + 600 = **−350** (a deficit of US\$350 million).
3. As a percentage of GDP = −350 ÷ 14,000 × 100 = **−2.5%**.
4. If the target is a deficit below 3% of GDP, the objective is met, although the large goods deficit is a concern.
:::

To judge overall performance, compare the indicators with targets, with earlier years and with comparable countries. The table gives an illustrative scorecard for a fictional economy.

| Indicator | Target | Actual |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real GDP growth | 5.0% | 4.8% |
| Inflation | 3% to 5% | 9.5% |
| Unemployment rate | Below 7% | 6.0% |
| Current account | Deficit below 3% of GDP | Deficit of 2.5% of GDP |
| Gini coefficient | Fall from 0.45 | 0.44 |

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the scorecard"}
1. Growth (4.8%) is **just below** target: almost met.
2. Inflation (9.5%) is **well above** the range: not met, which hurts savers and competitiveness.
3. Unemployment (6.0%) is **below** the limit: met.
4. The current account deficit (2.5% of GDP) is **within** the limit: met.
5. The Gini coefficient fell from 0.45 to 0.44: a **small** improvement.
6. Judgement: three of the five objectives are met, one is close and inflation is the main problem; policy should be directed at inflation, taking care not to damage growth and jobs.
:::

### Limits of the indicators

- Official figures can be inaccurate or revised, and informal activity is difficult to measure.
- Averages hide regional and group differences.
- One year's figures may be unrepresentative: look at trends.
- The indicators cover only part of well-being: GDP excludes the environment, leisure and unpaid work.
- The objectives conflict (the next lesson), so perfection on all of them is unlikely.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the main objectives, their indicators and the evaluation of performance from data. ZIMSEC (Form 6) emphasises growth and development, price stability, employment and balance of payments equilibrium; Cambridge (AS) also names equity and the environment (sustainability) among the objectives. Conflicts between the objectives belong to the next lesson.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that the aim is zero inflation"}
The aim is low and stable inflation. Zero or negative inflation can reduce spending and raise the real burden of debt.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that full employment means zero unemployment"}
Some frictional and structural unemployment will always remain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Judging performance on one indicator"}
Use all the relevant indicators, compare them with targets and look at trends before reaching a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the objectives and indicators; Paper 2 gives a table of indicators and asks you to judge performance; Paper 3 essays ask whether a government has been successful. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test the objectives and their measurement, and AS essays ask you to assess how far policy has achieved them.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* or *identify*: name the objective and its indicator. *Calculate*: use the formula for the indicator and give the unit. *Assess*: compare each indicator with its target and with earlier years, weigh them and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The main objectives are price stability, low unemployment, growth and development, balance of payments stability, equity and sustainability.
- Each has an indicator (inflation rate, unemployment rate, real GDP growth, current account as a percentage of GDP, Gini coefficient, emissions).
- Targets turn objectives into numbers; instruments are the tools used to reach them.
- Performance is judged by comparing indicators with targets, trends and other countries.
- Indicators have limits, and the objectives can conflict, so a balanced judgement is needed.
:::
