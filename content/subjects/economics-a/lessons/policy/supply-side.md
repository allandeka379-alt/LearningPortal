## Introduction

Zimbabwe's farms, mines and factories can produce more only if they have reliable electricity, good roads, skilled workers and modern technology. The Kariba hydroelectric station, the railways and the country's schools and colleges all affect how much the economy can produce in the long run, whatever the level of spending. **Supply-side policy** is the set of government actions aimed at raising this productive capacity, as opposed to **demand-side policy** (fiscal and monetary policy), which changes the amount of spending.

You should know the aggregate demand and aggregate supply model, the difference between the short-run (SRAS) and long-run (LRAS) aggregate supply curves, and the meaning of productivity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Supply-side policy"}
Government policies that aim to increase the productive capacity of the economy, and so shift the long-run aggregate supply curve to the right, by raising the quantity or quality of factors of production or the efficiency with which they are used.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
Output per unit of input in a period of time. **Labour productivity** is output per worker (or per hour worked): output ÷ number of workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Potential output (productive capacity)"}
The maximum level of real output that an economy can sustain when its resources are fully employed. It is shown by the position of the LRAS curve.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key formula"}
Labour productivity = $\dfrac{\text{total output}}{\text{number of workers}}$

Labour cost per unit of output = $\dfrac{\text{total labour cost per worker}}{\text{output per worker}}$. If output per worker rises and wages do not, the cost per unit falls.
:::

## Aims of supply-side policy

1. **Raise potential output**, so that the economy can grow without inflation.
2. **Raise productivity and cut costs of production**, which makes firms more competitive at home and abroad.
3. **Reduce structural unemployment**, by improving the skills and mobility of workers and the incentives to look for and offer work.
4. **Lower the price level or the rate of inflation**, because lower costs shift the aggregate supply curve to the right.

## Policy tools

| Tool | How it raises supply | Example | Typical time lag |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Education and training | Raises the skills and productivity of labour (human capital) | Funding vocational colleges and apprenticeships | Long (years) |
| Infrastructure | Lowers firms' costs and raises capacity | Roads, rail, electricity generation, ICT networks | Long (years) |
| Support for technology and research | Raises productivity and creates new products | Grants and tax relief for research; extension services for farmers | Medium to long |
| Tax incentives | Increase the reward for work, saving and investment | Lower company tax, investment allowances, lower marginal rates of income tax | Short to medium |
| Deregulation | Lowers the costs of starting and running a business | Fewer licences, faster registration, removal of unnecessary rules | Short to medium |

Other supply-side tools include policies that make labour and product markets work better (such as encouraging competition and improving information about jobs). Whether such policies should rely on markets or on government intervention is discussed in the lesson on the effectiveness of policy.

## Analysing the effects with AD and AS

A successful supply-side policy shifts the **LRAS** curve (and the SRAS curve) to the right. The schedule below is illustrative: the price level is an index and real GDP is in US\$ million.

| Price level (index) | AD (US\$ million) | SRAS before (US\$ million) | SRAS after (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 100 | 1,000 | 700 | 850 |
| 110 | 950 | 800 | 950 |
| 120 | 900 | 900 | 1,050 |
| 130 | 850 | 980 | 1,130 |
| 140 | 800 | 1,040 | 1,190 |

Before the policy, AD equals SRAS at a price level of **120** and real GDP of **US\$900 million**. The policy raises productive capacity and lowers costs, so SRAS shifts right by US\$150 million at every price level (the "after" column). At a price level of 110 the quantity of real GDP demanded (950) now equals the quantity supplied (950), so the new equilibrium is at a price level of **110** and real GDP of **US\$950 million**.

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the effect on prices and output"}
1. Change in real GDP = 950 − 900 = US\$50 million, or 50 ÷ 900 × 100 = **5.6%**.
2. Change in the price level = (110 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = **−8.3%**.
3. Higher output and a lower price level together show a benefit that demand-side policy cannot achieve: growth **without** inflation.
4. Employment is likely to rise, because firms produce more; structural unemployment falls if the workers gain the skills that firms need.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Training, productivity and unit labour costs"}
A clothing firm employs 150 workers who together make 12,000 shirts a month. After a training programme the same 150 workers make 13,500 shirts. Each worker is paid US\$600 a month.
1. Productivity before = 12,000 ÷ 150 = **80** shirts per worker. After = 13,500 ÷ 150 = **90**.
2. Percentage rise = (90 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **12.5%**.
3. Labour cost per shirt before = 600 ÷ 80 = **US\$7.50**. After = 600 ÷ 90 = **US\$6.67** (to the nearest cent).
4. Labour cost per shirt fell by (6.67 − 7.50) ÷ 7.50 × 100 = **−11.1%**, which lets the firm lower prices or raise profit without raising prices.
:::

## Time lags and costs

Supply-side policies usually act slowly. Education and training take years to produce a skilled labour force, and a power station or railway may take many years to plan and build. Tax incentives and deregulation work faster but their effects depend on how firms respond. The policies also have costs:

- **Opportunity cost.** Spending on training and infrastructure cannot be spent on other things, and it may require higher taxes or borrowing.
- **Uncertainty.** The return on spending on research or training is not guaranteed.
- **Equity.** Tax cuts may benefit high-income households more, and deregulation may reduce protection for workers or the environment.
- **Wrong skills.** Training may not match the skills that employers want.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
Supply-side policy can raise potential output and reduce costs and so allow growth with lower inflation, which demand-side policy cannot do. Investment in training and infrastructure may be particularly valuable in a country with a large number of unemployed workers and unreliable power. However, the benefits arrive slowly, often after several years, so the policies do not help a country in a sudden recession. The cost is high, the results are uncertain and training will not reduce unemployment if there are not enough jobs because demand is weak. Supply-side policy is therefore best seen as a long-term complement to demand management rather than a substitute for it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing supply-side policy with fiscal policy"}
Fiscal policy changes **aggregate demand** through spending and taxes. Supply-side policy aims at **aggregate supply**. Some policies, such as spending on education, are both, but the supply-side effect is on the quality and quantity of resources.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shifting the wrong curve"}
A successful supply-side policy shifts SRAS and LRAS to the **right**. It does not shift AD, although higher incomes may raise AD later.
:::

:::mistake{title="Expecting fast results"}
Describe time lags. Training and infrastructure take years, so these policies cannot cure a short-run recession.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 1 tests the meaning of supply-side policy, examples and the effect on the AS curve; Paper 2 data responses ask for AD/AS analysis and Paper 2 essays ask you to assess the policy against other policies.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: say how the policy raises productivity or lowers costs and then show the shift of LRAS/SRAS and the effect on output and prices. *Assess* or *evaluate*: weigh the long-term benefits against the time lags, the cost, the uncertainty and the need for adequate demand, and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply-side policy aims to raise productive capacity and shift LRAS to the right.
- Tools include education and training, infrastructure, support for technology, tax incentives and deregulation.
- A successful policy raises real GDP and lowers the price level; it reduces structural unemployment if workers gain the skills that are needed.
- Labour productivity is output per worker; higher productivity with unchanged wages lowers cost per unit.
- Time lags are long, costs are high and results are uncertain, so supply-side policy complements demand management.
:::
