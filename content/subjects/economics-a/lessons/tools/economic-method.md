## Introduction

When a minister says "the minimum wage should be raised" and an economist replies "raising it will reduce employment in small firms", they are doing two different things. The first is a statement of what someone *wants*; the second is a claim about what *would happen*. A Level Economics begins by learning to tell them apart, because every later topic, from demand curves to inflation, relies on the same way of thinking: build a simplified **model**, state its **assumptions**, test it against evidence and be honest about its limits.

This lesson is Cambridge AS Level content and is useful background for every candidate. You should know that economics is about how people, firms and governments use scarce resources. Scarcity and opportunity cost are developed in their own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economics as a social science"}
The study of how people and societies choose to use scarce resources. It is a *social* science because it studies human behaviour, which cannot be tested as precisely as chemicals in a laboratory, but economists still build theories and test them against data.
:::

:::definition{term="Positive statement"}
A statement about what is, was or will be, which can be tested against evidence and shown to be true or false (even if it turns out to be false).
:::

:::definition{term="Normative statement"}
A statement that involves a value judgement about what ought to be. It cannot be settled by evidence alone because it depends on opinions about what is fair or desirable.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceteris paribus"}
A Latin phrase meaning "other things being equal". It means studying the effect of one change on another variable while all other influences are assumed to stay the same.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic model"}
A simplified representation of reality, built on assumptions, that is used to explain relationships and predict outcomes.
:::

## Models and assumptions

A map of Harare leaves out almost every building, but it is useful because it keeps only what you need to find your way. An economic model does the same. The demand and supply model, for example, assumes that buyers and sellers are many, that the goods are identical and that nothing else changes. These assumptions are untrue in detail, yet the model correctly predicts that a poor maize harvest will push up maize prices.

The steps of economic method are:

1. Observe a situation or ask a question.
2. Build a model with assumptions and a prediction (a *hypothesis*).
3. Test the prediction against data.
4. Keep, change or reject the model.

Models are limited because real people do not always act rationally, data may be poor, and assumptions such as perfect information rarely hold. A good answer says what a model explains well and where it fails.

## Positive and normative statements

Look for clues. Words such as *should*, *ought*, *fair*, *unfair*, *better* and *worse* usually signal a normative statement. Positive statements use words such as *will*, *is*, *increases* and *causes*, and can be checked against data.

:::example{title="Classifying statements"}
| Statement | Type | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "A rise in the price of fuel reduces the number of journeys made by minibus." | Positive | Can be tested with data on fares and journeys |
| "The government should reduce taxes on fuel." | Normative | Contains the value judgement *should* |
| "Unemployment in Country X is higher than in Country Y." | Positive | Can be checked from statistics; true or false |
| "Income inequality in Country X is unfairly high." | Normative | *Unfairly* is a value judgement |
| "Education spending is the best use of public money." | Normative | *Best* depends on opinions |
:::

A statement can be positive and still be **wrong**. "A 10% rise in price will double demand" is positive because evidence can test it; the evidence will say it is false. A normative statement may rest on a positive one: "Cigarettes should be taxed" (normative) may depend on "taxes reduce smoking" (positive). Economists can advise on the positive part but not decide the value judgement.

## Ceteris paribus

In real life many things change together. If bus fares rise and journeys fall, perhaps school holidays began at the same time. Economists therefore use *ceteris paribus* to isolate one relationship at a time.

:::worked-solution{title="Testing a positive statement with data"}
A fictional bus company in Zandia raises its fare from US\$1.00 to US\$1.20, and weekly journeys fall from 5,000 to 4,500. The claim: "A rise in fares reduces the number of journeys."

1. Percentage change in fare: $(1.20 - 1.00) \div 1.00 \times 100 = +20\%$.
2. Percentage change in journeys: $(4500 - 5000) \div 5000 \times 100 = -10\%$.
3. Direction: journeys fell when the fare rose, which is consistent with the claim.
4. Ceteris paribus warning: if the fare rise took place just as schools closed for the holidays, the fall in journeys may not be due to the fare. The evidence supports the claim only if other factors (incomes, other fares, season) did not change.
5. Conclusion: the data is *consistent with* the statement but does not prove it.
:::

*Ceteris paribus* is a modelling device, not a claim that nothing else changes. In examinations, always state it when you describe a movement along a demand curve.

## Why economists disagree

Economists can reach different conclusions about the same policy for several reasons.

- **Different value judgements.** One may value equality more, another may value growth more. This is a normative difference and evidence cannot settle it.
- **Different models and assumptions.** One may think wages adjust quickly, another that they are slow to change.
- **Uncertain and incomplete data.** Statistics may be inaccurate and the future is uncertain.
- **Different time horizons.** A policy may help in the short run and harm in the long run.

Disagreement does not mean economics is useless. Many positive questions are agreed (for example that price controls below the equilibrium cause shortages), and disagreements show where evidence or values differ.

## Time periods in economics

Economists distinguish periods by what can change, not by the calendar.

| Period | What is fixed | What can change | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short run | At least one factor of production is fixed | Variable factors, such as labour or materials | A bakery in Bulawayo can hire more workers but not build a new oven |
| Long run | No factor is fixed | The quantities of all factors; technology stays the same | The bakery can build a larger factory |
| Very long run | Nothing is fixed | All factors and technology | New baking technology is invented |

The lengths differ between industries: the long run for a street vendor may be a week, for a hydroelectric station many years. In macroeconomics, the short-run and long-run aggregate supply curves use similar ideas (wages and other costs are slow to adjust in the short run), which are met later in the course.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking positive means correct"}
A positive statement is testable, not necessarily true. "The earth is flat" is positive and false.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all words with 'should' as the only normative signal"}
Statements can be normative without the word *should*, for example "The tax system is unfair". Look for any value judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ceteris paribus means nothing else ever changes"}
It is an assumption used to isolate one cause. In real data other factors usually change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the short run as a fixed number of months"}
The short run is defined by at least one fixed factor, so it differs between industries.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Paper 1 and Paper 2"}
This topic appears in Cambridge Paper 1 multiple choice (classify a statement, identify the period) and in Paper 2 as part of data-response and essay answers. The skill of stating assumptions and *ceteris paribus* earns marks throughout the paper.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: give the meaning of each and the difference. *Explain*: give a reason (a statement is normative because it contains a value judgement). *Evaluate a model*: say what it explains, name its assumptions and judge how realistic they are.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economics is a social science that builds simplified models, tests them against evidence and revises them.
- Positive statements can be tested; normative statements contain value judgements.
- *Ceteris paribus* isolates the effect of one change by holding other influences constant.
- Economists disagree because of different values, models, data and time horizons.
- In the short run at least one factor is fixed; in the long run none is; in the very long run technology also changes.
:::
