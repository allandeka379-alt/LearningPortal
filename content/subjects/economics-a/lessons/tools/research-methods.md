## Introduction

Economists, businesses and governments all need facts before they decide: how many households can afford electricity, which brand of maize meal people prefer, how many hours children spend fetching water. Opinions are not enough, so someone has to collect **data**. The way the data is collected decides how far it can be trusted, and examiners expect you to say *which* technique suits a question and *why*.

This ZIMSEC lesson (Form 5) prepares you for data-response questions in Papers 1 and 2 and for the research parts of the continuous assessment. You should already know what an economic question looks like, for example "Do higher bus fares reduce the number of trips people make?". Calculating averages and drawing charts from the data is covered in the lesson on mean, mode and median and presenting data.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary data"}
Information collected first-hand by the researcher for the purpose of the investigation, for example answers from a questionnaire the researcher designed.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary data"}
Information that already exists because someone else collected it for another purpose, for example census results, central bank reports, company accounts, newspapers and earlier surveys.
:::

:::definition{term="Population and sample"}
The **population** is the whole group the researcher wants to learn about. A **sample** is the smaller group actually studied, chosen to represent the population.
:::

:::definition{term="Reliability and validity"}
A result is **reliable** if repeating the research under the same conditions gives similar results. It is **valid** if it measures what it claims to measure and the findings can be applied to the population.
:::

## Primary and secondary data

| | Primary data | Secondary data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Collected by the researcher | Collected earlier by others |
| Fits the question | Designed for the exact question | May be out of date or collected for a different purpose |
| Cost and time | Often costly and slow | Cheap and quick to obtain |
| Control over quality | Researcher controls method and sample | Researcher cannot check how it was collected |
| Typical examples | Questionnaire, interview, observation, experiment | Statistical office publications, company reports, journals |

A good investigation often uses both: secondary data to see what is already known, primary data to fill the gaps.

## Primary research techniques

### Interviews

A researcher asks questions face to face, by telephone or by video. *Structured* interviews follow a fixed list of questions; *unstructured* interviews let the conversation develop.

- Strengths: detailed answers, the interviewer can explain a question and ask follow-up questions, a high response rate.
- Weaknesses: expensive and slow, the interviewer may influence the answers (interviewer bias), few people can be reached.

### Questionnaires and surveys

A **questionnaire** is a written set of questions; a **survey** is the wider process of asking a sample, often with a questionnaire, and analysing the results.

- *Closed questions* offer fixed answers ("Do you use mobile money? Yes / No") and are quick to analyse.
- *Open questions* allow a free answer ("Why do you prefer this shop?") and give richer but harder-to-analyse information.
- Good wording is short, clear, neutral and asks one thing at a time. A *leading* question ("Do you agree that the dreadful new tax should go?") pushes respondents towards an answer.
- A **pilot study** tries the questionnaire on a few people first so that unclear or biased questions can be corrected.
- Weaknesses: low response rates when posted, people may not tell the truth, and respondents who do reply may differ from those who do not.

### Observation

The researcher records what people actually do, for example counting customers entering a shop in Gweru each hour. It shows real behaviour rather than claimed behaviour, but it cannot explain *why* people act as they do, it takes time, and people who know they are watched may behave differently.

### Experiments

A researcher changes one factor and watches the effect, keeping everything else the same. A shop might charge a lower price in one branch for a month and compare sales with a similar branch. Experiments can show cause and effect, but real markets are hard to control and the trial may be costly.

## Sampling

Asking everyone in a population (a *census*) is usually too slow and costly, so a sample is used. A sample is useful only if it is **representative** and large enough; an unrepresentative sample is **biased**.

| Method | How it works | Main advantage | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Random | Every member has an equal chance of selection (names drawn or random numbers used) | Fair, free of researcher bias | Needs a full list of the population; may miss small groups |
| Systematic | Choose every *k*th member from a list after a random start | Simple and quick | Can mislead if the list has a pattern |
| Stratified | Divide the population into groups (strata) and sample each group in proportion to its size | Every important group is represented | Needs information about the groups |
| Quota | Interviewer finds a fixed number of people of each type until the quotas are filled | Quick and cheap | Interviewers choose whom to ask, so bias is possible |

:::worked-solution{title="Systematic and stratified samples for a fictional town"}
A researcher wants to survey 120 of the 2,400 households in a fictional town. The town has three suburbs: A with 1,200 households, B with 800 and C with 400.

1. **Systematic:** the interval is $2400 \div 120 = 20$. Start at a random household from 1 to 20 (say the 7th) and then pick every 20th household on the list.
2. **Stratified:** the sample is 5% of the population ($120 \div 2400 = 0.05$). Each suburb is sampled at the same 5% rate.
3. Suburb A: $1200 \times 0.05 = 60$ households.
4. Suburb B: $800 \times 0.05 = 40$ households.
5. Suburb C: $400 \times 0.05 = 20$ households.
6. Check: $60 + 40 + 20 = 120$.
:::

A larger sample usually reduces the chance that the results are unusual, but a large biased sample is still biased.

## Reliability, validity and choosing a technique

:::example{title="Is the finding trustworthy?"}
A researcher posts 200 questionnaires about bus fares to households in a fictional suburb and 62 are returned.

1. Response rate: $62 \div 200 \times 100 = 31\%$.
2. Only about one in three replied. People with strong views, or with the time to reply, may be over-represented, so the sample may not be representative and the result may lack **validity**.
3. If the researcher repeated the survey with a different group of 200 households and obtained very different answers, the result would also lack **reliability**.
4. Improvements: follow up non-respondents, interview a random sample face to face, pilot the questions and use a larger sample.
:::

To choose a technique, ask four questions.

1. What exactly must be found out: facts and numbers, or reasons and opinions?
2. How many people are needed, and how widely are they spread?
3. How much money and time are there?
4. Does secondary data already answer part of the question?

A study of why traders in a market in Mutare prefer mobile money might use a short structured interview (reasons) with a stratified sample of traders, supported by secondary data on mobile money use from the central bank.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing reliable with valid"}
A bathroom scale that always shows 2 kg too much is reliable (consistent) but not valid (wrong). Say what each word means in the context of the research.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a bigger sample always means a better sample"}
Size helps, but only if the sample is chosen fairly. Asking 1,000 people at one shopping centre does not represent a whole country.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a questionnaire a type of data"}
A questionnaire is a **technique**; the answers it produces are primary data.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing strengths without applying them"}
A statement such as "interviews are accurate" earns little. Link the technique to the problem: "interviews let the researcher explain terms such as inflation to small-scale farmers".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where this is tested"}
Expect short questions in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (identify a sampling method, primary or secondary data), data-response parts in Paper 2 (a described survey to criticise) and project work in continuous assessment. This is a ZIMSEC-only topic; Cambridge 9708 does not examine it.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the precise meaning. *State*: name a technique. *Explain*: give a strength or weakness and say why it matters here. *Compare*: strengths and weaknesses of both. *Evaluate or justify a choice*: weigh cost, time, accuracy and the type of information needed, then decide.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Primary data is collected first-hand for the purpose; secondary data already exists.
- Main techniques are interviews, questionnaires and surveys, observation and experiments, each with strengths and weaknesses.
- A sample is chosen by random, systematic, stratified or quota methods; it must be representative and large enough.
- Pilot studies, neutral wording and good response rates improve quality.
- Reliability means consistent results; validity means measuring what is intended.
:::
