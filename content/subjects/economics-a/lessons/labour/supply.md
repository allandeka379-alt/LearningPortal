## Introduction

Why are there more people willing to work as shop assistants in Harare than as mining engineers in Hwange, even though the engineers are paid more? The answer is partly the wage and partly everything else: the training needed, where the job is, how safe or pleasant it is and the status it carries. The **supply of labour** is the number of workers willing and able to work at each wage, and it differs depending on whether you look at one firm, one occupation or the whole economy.

You should know the law of supply, movements along against shifts of a curve and elasticity of supply. This lesson sets up the supply side that, together with the demand for labour, determines wages in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Supply of labour"}
The number of workers willing and able to work at each wage rate in a given period, other things equal.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour force and participation rate"}
The **labour force** is the people of working age who are either employed or looking for work. The **participation rate** is the labour force as a percentage of the working-age population.
:::

:::definition{term="Occupational and geographical mobility"}
**Occupational mobility** is the ease with which workers move between types of job. **Geographical mobility** is the ease with which they move between places.
:::

## Three levels of supply

| Level | Shape of the supply curve | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| To one firm in a large competitive labour market | Horizontal (very elastic) at the going wage | The firm is small, so it can hire any number at the market wage; at less it gets no one |
| To an occupation | Upward sloping | A higher wage attracts workers from other occupations and from outside the labour force |
| To the whole economy | Steep, depends on population and participation | Most adults must work whatever the wage; wage changes alter hours and participation a little |

For a firm with market power as an employer (a monopsonist), the supply curve is the market supply curve and slopes upward: the firm must raise the wage to attract more workers. The next lesson uses this.

## Wage and non-wage factors

A worker compares the **net advantages** of jobs, not only the pay.

- **Wage and bonuses:** a higher wage raises the number willing to work (a *movement along* the supply curve).
- **Hours and flexibility:** shorter hours or work from home attract more workers.
- **Working conditions and safety:** a dangerous or unpleasant job needs higher pay to attract workers.
- **Training and qualifications:** a long training raises the cost of entry and reduces supply.
- **Job security, pensions and benefits:** free housing, health care and school fees add to pay.
- **Status and job satisfaction:** a job with status may attract workers at a lower wage.
- **Travel costs and location.**

:::worked-solution{title="Comparing the net advantages of two jobs"}
A school leaver in Bulawayo is offered two jobs. Job A pays US\$420 a month, but the worker must spend US\$60 a month on travel. Job B pays US\$380 a month, with free housing worth US\$70 a month and no travel cost.

1. Net value of Job A = 420 − 60 = **US\$360**.
2. Net value of Job B = 380 + 70 = **US\$450**.
3. Job B has the greater net advantage, although its headline wage is lower. This is why the supply of workers depends on more than the wage.
:::

## Movements along and shifts of the supply curve

A change in the **wage** moves the economy or occupation along the supply curve. Changes in anything else shift it.

| Change | Effect on the supply of labour to an occupation |
| --- | --- |
| Higher wages in other occupations | Fall (shift left): workers move elsewhere |
| More people qualified (training programmes) | Rise (shift right) |
| Better working conditions or benefits | Rise (shift right) |
| Higher entry requirements or licensing | Fall (shift left) |
| Immigration or population growth | Rise (shift right) |
| Higher participation of women or young people | Rise (shift right) |

:::worked-solution{title="Elasticity of the supply of labour"}
The supply of fictional trained welders in a town is 400 at a wage of US\$5 an hour and 500 at US\$6. After a training programme it is 500 at US\$5.

1. The rise in the wage is (6 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = +20%.
2. The rise in the number of welders is (500 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = +25%.
3. Elasticity of supply of labour = 25 ÷ 20 = **1.25**: supply is elastic.
4. The training programme shifts supply right by 100 at the wage of US\$5: at US\$5 the quantity supplied rises from 400 to 500, which is a *shift*, not a movement.
:::

## Participation and the labour force

:::worked-solution{title="The participation rate"}
A fictional district has a working-age population of 1,250 thousand. The labour force is 800 thousand, of whom 80 thousand are unemployed.

1. Participation rate = 800 ÷ 1,250 × 100 = **64%**.
2. Employed = 800 − 80 = 720 thousand.
3. Unemployment rate = 80 ÷ 800 × 100 = 10%.
4. If more young people leave education and look for work, the participation rate rises and the supply of labour to the economy increases.
:::

## Labour mobility

Movement of workers is rarely perfect. **Occupational immobility** arises from lack of skills, training and qualifications, and from licences. **Geographical immobility** arises from the cost and difficulty of moving house, family ties, lack of information about jobs, differences in housing costs and, in Zimbabwe as in other countries, the strong link of many workers to rural homes. Immobility means that wages for similar jobs differ between places and that unemployment can exist in one area while vacancies exist in another. Training, information, and cheaper transport and housing raise mobility and make supply more elastic.

## The supply of land and capital (ZIMSEC detail)

The **supply of land** to the whole economy is fixed in quantity, though its supply to a particular use (for tobacco or housing) is more elastic because land can be switched between uses. It can also be increased by reclamation or irrigation. The **supply of capital** depends on saving and past investment: a higher rate of saving allows more machines to be built, but they take time to produce, so supply is inelastic in the short run.

:::example{title="Evaluating the effect of a wage rise on labour supply"}
A rise in nurses' pay should raise the supply of nurses, but how much depends on training time (a student needs years to qualify), on whether other countries offer higher pay, and on conditions of work. In the short run the supply is inelastic; over time it is more elastic. A fair evaluation notes that pay is only one factor and that the response takes time.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the three levels of supply, wage and non-wage factors, shifts and mobility. The determinants of the supply of land and capital are ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge may also use the backward-bending labour supply curve in questions on work and leisure.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the occupation's supply as one firm's supply"}
A single firm in a large market faces a horizontal supply; the occupation's supply slopes upward.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a wage change with a shift"}
Only the wage in this occupation moves along the curve. Wages elsewhere, training or conditions shift the curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equating mobility with willingness"}
Workers may be willing to move but unable to, because of cost, family ties or lack of qualifications.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for factors and mobility; Paper 2 data questions give a labour table and ask for participation rates or an elasticity; Paper 3 essays ask about the factors affecting supply and wages. Cambridge Paper 3 tests shifts and mobility, and Paper 4 uses supply in wage-determination essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: name the factor and show how it changes the number willing to work. *Calculate*: participation rate or elasticity from the data. *Distinguish*: occupational from geographical mobility. *Evaluate*: weigh the wage against non-wage factors and time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Labour supply is the number willing and able to work at each wage; it is horizontal to a firm in a competitive market and upward sloping to an occupation.
- Non-wage factors (hours, conditions, training, security, status) influence supply; workers compare net advantages.
- A change in the occupation's wage is a movement along the curve; other changes shift it.
- Participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100.
- Occupational and geographical immobility reduce the elasticity of supply; the supply of land is fixed and of capital depends on saving (ZIMSEC).
:::
