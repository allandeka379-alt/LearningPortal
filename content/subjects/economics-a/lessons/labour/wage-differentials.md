## Introduction

A mine engineer in Hwange, a primary school teacher in Masvingo, a market trader in Mutare and a bus conductor in Harare all work hard, yet their pay is very different. Economists call these gaps **wage differentials**. Some differentials can be explained by the forces of demand and supply in the labour market; others reflect imperfections, such as discrimination or the bargaining power of a union. Telling the two apart is one of the most useful skills in labour economics, and it is a favourite evaluation question.

You should already know the demand for labour (marginal revenue product), the supply of labour and how wages are determined in a competitive market. This lesson uses those ideas to explain why wages differ.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Wage differential"}
A difference in the pay received by workers in different occupations, industries, regions or groups.
:::

:::definition{term="Compensating differential"}
Extra pay that compensates workers for the unattractive features of a job, such as danger, unsocial hours, insecurity or a remote location. (Equally, lower pay may be accepted for a job with attractive features.)
:::

:::definition{term="Human capital"}
The knowledge, skills and health of workers, built up through education, training and experience, which raise their productivity.
:::

:::definition{term="Wage elasticity of labour supply"}
The percentage change in the quantity of labour supplied divided by the percentage change in the wage: %ΔL supplied ÷ %Δ wage.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Core idea"}
In a competitive market, wages differ because the demand for (the MRP of) different workers differs and because the supply of different types of labour differs. A high wage is found where **demand is high** and **supply is scarce or costly to build**.
:::

## Differences that come from demand and supply

**1. Productivity and the demand for labour.** The demand for a worker is derived from marginal revenue product (MRP = marginal physical product × the price, or marginal revenue, of the extra output). Workers whose output sells for a high price, or who work with expensive equipment, have a high MRP, so firms are willing to pay more. A trained mine engineer who directs costly machinery has a higher MRP than a casual labourer.

**2. Skills, training and human capital.** Some jobs need years of study that cost money and forgone earnings. Fewer people can or will supply that labour, so the supply curve is further to the left and more inelastic. The extra wage is partly a **return on the investment in training**.

**3. Scarcity and inelastic supply.** When a skill is rare and takes a long time to acquire, a rise in demand cannot be met quickly. The wage rises sharply with little rise in employment. When labour is plentiful and needs no training, the same rise in demand brings a small rise in the wage and a large rise in employment.

**4. Compensating differences.** Jobs with danger, night shifts, long absences from home or poor job security must pay more to attract workers: a compensating differential. Jobs with attractive features (a pleasant working environment, high status, a sense of vocation) may pay less, because many people are willing to do them.

**5. Mobility.** Wage differentials can last because labour is **occupationally immobile** (lack of skills or licences) and **geographically immobile** (housing costs, family ties). If labour moved freely, workers would move from low-paying to high-paying jobs, raising wages in the first and lowering them in the second until only compensating differences remained.

## Differences that come from imperfections

**6. Bargaining power.** A strong trade union, or a professional association that controls entry (for example by licensing), can raise the wage above the market level for its members, while workers in weakly organised jobs earn less. A monopsony employer pays less than the competitive wage (see the lesson on wage determination).

**7. Discrimination.** If employers pay workers of equal productivity less because of gender, ethnic group or disability, the differential cannot be explained by MRP. Occupational segregation, where some groups are crowded into a few low-paid occupations, has a similar effect.

**8. Tradition, custom and government.** Pay in the public sector is set by the government, not by market forces, and some differences persist because of customary ideas about the "value" of a job. Wages legislation and national minimum wages narrow the bottom of the distribution.

**9. Imperfect information.** Workers may not know what other jobs pay, or employers may not know the true productivity of a worker, so wages do not adjust.

## Worked examples

The numbers below are illustrative.

:::worked-solution{title="Measuring a differential"}
A skilled technician earns US\$12 an hour and a general labourer earns US\$4 an hour.
1. Absolute difference = 12 − 4 = US\$8 an hour.
2. The differential as a percentage of the labourer's wage = 8 ÷ 4 × 100 = **200%**.
3. The technician's wage is 12 ÷ 4 = **3 times** the labourer's wage.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Is training worth it? A return on human capital"}
Worker X leaves school at 18 and earns US\$6,000 a year for 40 years. Worker Y spends 3 years in training, earning nothing and paying US\$9,000 in fees, and then earns US\$9,500 a year for the remaining 37 years.
1. Lifetime earnings of X = 40 × 6,000 = **US\$240,000**.
2. Lifetime earnings of Y = 37 × 9,500 − 9,000 = 351,500 − 9,000 = **US\$342,500**.
3. Y is better off by 342,500 − 240,000 = **US\$102,500** over the working life (ignoring interest, tax and the time value of money).
4. Cost of training = forgone earnings (3 × 6,000 = US\$18,000) + fees (US\$9,000) = US\$27,000.
5. Extra pay each year = 9,500 − 6,000 = US\$3,500, so the cost is recovered after 27,000 ÷ 3,500 = **7.7 years** of working.
6. The higher wage is therefore a return on the investment in human capital: it is needed to persuade people to bear the cost, and it falls if more people train.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Why a demand rise raises wages more where supply is inelastic"}
Demand rises in two labour markets. Specialist engineers: the wage rises from US\$20 to US\$26 an hour and employment from 100 to 110. General workers: the wage rises from US\$5 to US\$5.50 and employment from 1,000 to 1,300.
1. Specialists: wage +30%, employment +10%. Wage elasticity of supply = 10 ÷ 30 = **0.33** (inelastic).
2. General workers: wage +10%, employment +30%. Wage elasticity of supply = 30 ÷ 10 = **3.0** (elastic).
3. The same type of rise in demand raised the specialists' wage by 30% but the general workers' wage by only 10%, because specialist labour cannot be increased quickly.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting data: a compensating differential"}
A fictional mine pays underground drillers US\$540 a month and surface clerks US\$400 a month for the same hours, a gap of (540 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = 35%. Part of the gap compensates for danger, dust and shift work. It is therefore **not** by itself evidence of discrimination or of unfairness. If the drillers were also much more productive, MRP would explain even more of the gap. To judge the cause you would compare workers with the same skills and conditions.
:::

## Evaluating the reasons

An evaluation answer should weigh how much of a differential market forces explain.

- **Efficiency.** Differentials that reflect MRP and compensating differences send a signal: they reward skills, direct workers to where they are needed and pay for training. Removing them would reduce incentives.
- **Equity.** Differentials caused by discrimination, unequal access to education or the power of a few groups are unfair and inefficient, because talented workers are not used where they would be most productive.
- **Size and time.** A differential from scarce skills in the short run can shrink in the long run as more people train, if training is affordable. For poor households, borrowing to pay for training may be impossible, so differentials persist.
- **Developing economies.** In an economy such as Zimbabwe a large informal sector, limited formal jobs and the movement of some skilled workers to other countries can raise the wages of scarce skills while keeping the pay of many others low. The balance between market forces and imperfections is a matter of evidence.
- **Policy.** Government can widen access to education, enforce equal-pay laws and set minimum wages, each with costs and trade-offs.

Both boards expect the demand and supply explanations, compensating differences and imperfections. ZIMSEC (Form 5) frames the topic as "wage differentials"; Cambridge (A Level only) places it with the labour-market analysis and asks for evaluation. Quasi-rent is ZIMSEC detail and belongs to the lesson on rent and transfer earnings.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating every differential as discrimination"}
Many differentials are explained by productivity, training and compensating differences. Discrimination is shown only when workers of **equal** productivity in similar jobs are paid differently.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying skilled workers earn more because they work harder"}
The answer is about **MRP and supply**: skilled workers produce output of higher value, and fewer people can supply the skill. Effort alone is not the economic reason.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing a movement along the supply curve with a shift"}
A higher wage in an occupation causes a **movement along** its supply curve. A change in training costs or entry rules **shifts** the supply curve.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for reasons for differentials, Paper 2 gives wage data to compare, and Paper 3 essays ask you to explain and discuss why occupations pay differently. Cambridge Paper 3 tests compensating differences and elasticity of labour supply in multiple choice, and Paper 4 asks you to assess the reasons.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the precise meaning of the term. *Explain*: link each factor to demand or supply and say which curve shifts. *Calculate*: use percentage differences and the elasticity formula. *Analyse*: build a chain (training cost, fewer suppliers, higher equilibrium wage). *Evaluate*: separate market-based reasons from imperfections and judge which matters more in the context.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wage differentials arise from differences in MRP (demand) and in the supply of different types of labour.
- Training and human capital raise productivity and make supply scarce, so skilled work pays more.
- Compensating differences pay more for unattractive jobs; they are not unfair differentials.
- Bargaining power, discrimination, tradition, government pay and immobility create differentials that markets do not explain.
- Evaluation weighs efficiency against equity, short run against long run, and market forces against imperfections.
:::
