## Introduction

Is a minimum wage good for low-paid workers? The honest answer is "it depends", and the dependence is exactly what this lesson explains. In some labour markets a minimum wage costs jobs; in others it can raise both pay and employment. To see why, you need the model of the **competitive labour market**, where wages are set by demand and supply, and then the model of **imperfect markets**, where trade unions or a dominant employer change the outcome.

You should know the demand for labour (marginal revenue product theory), the supply of labour, and how equilibrium is found. Wage differences between occupations are the subject of the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Equilibrium wage"}
The wage at which the quantity of labour demanded equals the quantity supplied, so that there is no shortage or surplus of workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade union"}
An organisation of workers that bargains with employers over pay and conditions (collective bargaining).
:::

:::definition{term="National minimum wage"}
A legal floor below which the wage rate may not be set. (ZIMSEC also treats wages legislation more widely: laws and agreements that fix wages in particular industries.)
:::

:::definition{term="Monopsony"}
A labour market with a single dominant buyer of labour, or a few employers able to influence the wage. A monopsonist must pay a higher wage to attract more workers, so the cost of an extra worker (the marginal cost of labour, MCL) is **above** the wage.
:::

## The competitive labour market

The assumptions are many employers and workers, identical workers, perfect information and free movement. The wage settles where the demand curve (MRP) cuts the supply curve. The schedule below is illustrative: wage in US\$ an hour, workers in a fictional town.

| Wage (US\$ an hour) | Workers demanded | Workers supplied |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 4 | 500 | 200 |
| 5 | 450 | 300 |
| 6 | 400 | 400 |
| 7 | 350 | 500 |
| 8 | 300 | 600 |

:::worked-solution{title="Finding equilibrium"}
1. At US\$6 an hour demand and supply are both 400: the equilibrium wage is **US\$6** and employment is **400**.
2. At US\$5 there is a shortage of 450 − 300 = 150 workers, so employers bid the wage up.
3. At US\$7 there is a surplus of 500 − 350 = 150 workers, so the wage tends to fall.
4. Equilibrium wage bill = 6 × 400 = US\$2,400 an hour.
:::

A rise in the demand for labour (for example from a higher product price) raises both the wage and employment; a rise in supply (for example from training) lowers the wage and raises employment.

## A minimum wage in a competitive market

A minimum wage has an effect only if it is set **above** the equilibrium wage. Set at US\$4, below equilibrium, it does nothing.

:::worked-solution{title="A minimum wage of US\$7"}
1. At US\$7, firms demand only 350 workers but 500 want jobs.
2. Employment falls from 400 to **350**, and **150** people are unemployed (500 − 350).
3. The 350 who keep jobs gain US\$1 an hour. The wage bill is 7 × 350 = US\$2,450 an hour, slightly **more** than the original US\$2,400.
4. Why does the wage bill rise? Between US\$6 and US\$7, labour demand falls by 12.5% while the wage rises by 16.7%. The elasticity of demand is 12.5 ÷ 16.7 = 0.75, which is inelastic, so the higher wage outweighs the loss of jobs.
5. Workers gain overall in income, but 50 lose their jobs and 100 more are attracted to the market and cannot find work.
:::

## Trade unions

A union can bargain for a wage above equilibrium by threatening to stop work. In a competitive market, if it succeeds in raising the wage to US\$7, the outcome is the same as the minimum wage above: higher pay for those still employed, fewer jobs and unemployment. A union may also:

- raise productivity through training and lower staff turnover, shifting the demand for labour right;
- offset the power of a monopsony employer (see below);
- reduce wage differences within a firm.

The disadvantages are that a strong union can protect its members (the *insiders*) at the expense of the unemployed (the *outsiders*), may reduce a firm's competitiveness, and may lead to strikes.

## Monopsony

A monopsony employer faces the whole market supply curve. To hire an extra worker it must raise the wage, and (unless it can pay each worker differently) must pay the higher wage to *all* its workers, so MCL is above the wage. It hires where **MRP = MCL** and pays the wage on the supply curve at that employment.

In our town, with demand (MRP) given by W = 14 − 0.02L and supply by W = 2 + 0.01L:

| | Employment | Wage (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Competitive market (MRP = supply) | 400 | 6.00 |
| Monopsony (MRP = MCL, where MCL = 2 + 0.02L) | 300 | 5.00 |

:::worked-solution{title="A minimum wage in a monopsony"}
1. The monopsonist sets MRP = MCL: 14 − 0.02L = 2 + 0.02L, so L = **300** and the wage is 2 + 0.01 × 300 = **US\$5**. Workers are paid less than in the competitive market, and fewer are employed.
2. A minimum wage of US\$5.50 makes the wage fixed at US\$5.50 up to the quantity supplied. At that wage 350 workers are willing to work (supply) and firms demand 425. Employment is the lower of the two: **350**, up from 300.
3. At US\$6 employment is 400, the competitive outcome. Above US\$6, say US\$7, employment falls again (to 350).
4. A minimum wage set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage raises both pay and employment, which is why the effect on jobs depends on the type of market.
:::

## Imperfections in labour markets

Real labour markets are imperfect. Wages reflect:

- **immobility** of labour (occupational and geographical);
- **imperfect information** about wages and vacancies;
- **discrimination** by employers or customers;
- **employer power** (monopsony) and **union power**;
- **government wages legislation** and bargaining arrangements, which in many countries, including Zimbabwe, set wages in particular industries through collective bargaining between employers and workers (ZIMSEC detail).

:::example{title="Evaluating a minimum wage"}
Supporters argue that a minimum wage raises the incomes of the lowest paid, reduces poverty and may raise productivity. Critics argue that it may cause unemployment and push firms into the informal sector, especially where it is set high. The effect depends on how far the wage is above equilibrium, how elastic the demand for labour is, whether employers have monopsony power, whether the rule is enforced and how large the informal sector is. In a small town with one large employer a moderate minimum wage may raise employment; in a competitive market with a high minimum, jobs are likely to be lost.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require competitive equilibrium, unions, the minimum wage and monopsony with schedules. Cambridge writes the cost of an extra worker as marginal cost of labour. Imperfections and wages legislation as a topic are ZIMSEC detail.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a minimum wage always reduces employment"}
A minimum wage below equilibrium has no effect, and in a monopsony a moderate minimum raises employment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing the monopsonist's wage at MRP = MCL"}
The employment is where MRP = MCL, but the wage is read off the **supply** curve at that employment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the unemployed 'outsiders'"}
A union wage rise can leave those in work better off and those out of work worse off. Mention both.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In a competitive market the equilibrium wage is where labour demand equals labour supply.
- A minimum wage above equilibrium reduces employment and creates unemployment; the wage bill may still rise if demand is inelastic.
- A union can raise wages and, in a competitive market, may reduce employment; it can also raise productivity or counter monopsony.
- A monopsony hires where MRP = MCL (MCL above the wage) and pays less and employs fewer than a competitive market.
- A minimum wage between the monopsony and competitive wages can raise both pay and employment.
:::
