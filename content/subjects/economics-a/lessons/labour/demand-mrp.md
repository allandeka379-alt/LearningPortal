## Introduction

A farmer near Mutare with a field of tomatoes has to decide how many workers to hire for the picking season. One worker picks a lot; a second picks a lot more; but by the time the field is crowded the eighth worker adds only a few extra crates. The farmer will hire a worker only if that worker adds more to revenue than they cost in wages. This simple idea, **marginal revenue product theory (MRPT)**, is the economist's account of what a firm is willing to pay for labour, and so of the demand for labour.

You should know the law of diminishing returns, marginal revenue, profit maximisation (MC = MR) and the idea of derived demand. The next lessons deal with the supply of labour and with how wages are determined in the market.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Derived demand"}
Demand for a factor of production that depends on the demand for the good or service the factor helps to produce. The demand for tomato pickers depends on the demand for tomatoes.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal physical product (MPP)"}
The extra output produced by employing one more unit of the factor (one more worker), holding other inputs constant: MPP = change in total product ÷ change in the number of workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal revenue product (MRP)"}
The extra revenue a firm earns from employing one more unit of a factor: MRP = MPP × marginal revenue (MR) from selling the extra output. For a firm that sells at a given price in a competitive product market, MR = price, so MRP = MPP × price. *(Cambridge: marginal revenue product; ZIMSEC: marginal revenue productivity.)*
:::

:::keyfact{title="The hiring rule"}
A profit-maximising firm hires workers until **MRP = marginal cost of labour (MCL)**. If the firm is a wage taker, MCL is the wage, so it hires up to the point where MRP = wage. It does not hire a worker whose MRP is below the wage, because the worker would cost more than they add.
:::

## From the production schedule to MRP

The table shows a tomato farm with a fixed amount of land. Each tomato crate sells for US\$4 (a price taker), and the farm pays US\$36 a day to each worker. Output is in crates a day. All figures are illustrative.

| Workers | Total product (crates) | MPP (crates) | MRP (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 16 | 16 | 64 |
| 2 | 30 | 14 | 56 |
| 3 | 42 | 12 | 48 |
| 4 | 52 | 10 | 40 |
| 5 | 60 | 8 | 32 |
| 6 | 66 | 6 | 24 |
| 7 | 70 | 4 | 16 |
| 8 | 72 | 2 | 8 |

:::worked-solution{title="How many workers will the farm hire?"}
1. MPP of each worker = change in total product: the second worker adds 30 − 16 = 14 crates.
2. MRP = MPP × price: the second worker's MRP is 14 × 4 = US\$56.
3. The wage is US\$36. The fourth worker's MRP of US\$40 exceeds the wage, so hiring that worker adds US\$4 to profit. The fifth worker's MRP of US\$32 is below the wage, so hiring would reduce profit by US\$4.
4. The farm hires **4 workers**. MPP falls from the first worker because of diminishing returns, so MRP falls too.
:::

MPP falls because of the **law of diminishing returns**: as more workers share a fixed amount of land and equipment, each adds less than the one before. (If MPP first rises, the firm would still only hire on the falling part of the MRP curve.)

## The MRP curve is the demand curve for labour

Plotted against the wage, the falling part of the MRP schedule shows how many workers the firm demands at each wage: it is the firm's **demand curve for labour**. A fall in the wage increases the number of workers hired because workers with a lower MRP become worth hiring.

:::worked-solution{title="A change in the wage"}
The wage rises from US\$36 to US\$44 a day.

1. The fourth worker's MRP of US\$40 is now below the wage; the third worker's MRP of US\$48 is above it.
2. The farm hires **3 workers**: a *movement along* the demand curve for labour, with employment falling from 4 to 3.
:::

## What shifts the demand for labour?

| Change | Effect on MRP | Direction of shift |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher price of the product (or a rise in demand for it) | MR rises, so MRP rises | Right |
| Higher productivity from training, better capital or technology | MPP rises | Right |
| Fall in the price of capital that complements labour | Workers more productive | Right |
| Fall in the price of capital that substitutes for labour (machines) | Firm switches to machines | Left |
| Fall in demand for the product | MR falls | Left |

:::worked-solution{title="A rise in the price of tomatoes"}
The price of a crate rises from US\$4 to US\$5, with the wage at US\$36.

1. New MRP = MPP × 5: US\$80, 70, 60, 50, 40, 30, 20, 10 for 1 to 8 workers.
2. The fifth worker's MRP of US\$40 now exceeds the wage; the sixth worker's MRP of US\$30 does not.
3. The farm hires **5 workers** instead of 4: the demand curve for labour has *shifted* right.
:::

## MRP when the firm is a monopolist

A firm with market power faces a downward-sloping demand curve, so selling the extra output lowers the price on all units: MR is less than price. MRP = MPP × MR is therefore lower than MPP × price.

:::worked-solution{title="A firm with falling marginal revenue"}
Suppose the farm has market power and MR is US\$3.50 for the extra crates, not US\$4.

1. MRP = MPP × 3.50 = US\$56, 49, 42, 35, … for 1, 2, 3, 4 workers.
2. With a wage of US\$36 the fourth worker's MRP of US\$35 is below the wage, so the farm hires **3 workers**, one fewer than a price-taking farm.
:::

## The elasticity of the demand for labour

The demand for labour is more inelastic when labour is a small part of total costs, when it is hard to replace with capital, when demand for the product is inelastic and in the short run. A higher wage then reduces employment by little. Over the long run firms substitute capital for labour, so demand becomes more elastic.

## Land and capital (ZIMSEC detail)

The same logic applies to the other factors. A firm rents land or hires capital until its MRP equals the marginal cost of the factor (the rent or the cost of capital). A rise in the price of the product raises the MRP of land, which raises the rent that farmers will pay.

:::example{title="Evaluating MRP theory"}
MRP theory explains why workers who add more to revenue are paid more, and why a rise in the price of an exported mineral raises demand for miners. Its limits are that it assumes the firm can measure each worker's contribution (hard in a team or a service job), that workers are identical and that the firm is a wage taker. It also says nothing about bargaining, discrimination or minimum wages, which affect actual pay. It is best seen as an account of the *demand* side, to be combined with supply and market power.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require MPP, MRP, the hiring rule, the MRP curve as the demand for labour, and what shifts it. The application of MRP theory to land and capital is ZIMSEC detail; Cambridge examines it for labour.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calculating MPP as total product ÷ workers"}
That is average product. MPP is the *change* in total product from one more worker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hiring until MRP is zero"}
A profit-maximising firm stops where MRP equals the wage (MCL), not where MRP is zero.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a movement with a shift"}
A change in the wage moves the firm along its demand curve for labour. A change in the price of the product, productivity or technology shifts the curve.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for MPP and MRP calculations; Paper 2 data questions give a production schedule and a wage and ask how many workers to hire; Paper 3 essays ask about the demand for labour and wages. Cambridge Paper 3 tests the schedule in multiple choice, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of MRP theory.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: work out MPP, then MRP, then compare with the wage. *Explain*: link the hiring rule to profit. *Analyse*: trace the effect of a change in price or productivity on MRP and employment. *Evaluate*: weigh the assumptions of the theory against its usefulness.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The demand for labour is a derived demand, based on its marginal revenue product.
- MPP is the extra output from one more worker; MRP = MPP × MR (price for a competitive seller).
- A firm hires until MRP = the wage (MCL); the falling part of the MRP curve is its demand for labour.
- A wage change moves along the curve; the product price, productivity and technology shift it.
- The theory has limits: measuring each worker's product, identical workers and wage-taking firms.
:::
