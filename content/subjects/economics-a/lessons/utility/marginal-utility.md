## Introduction

Why does the first slice of bread on an empty stomach feel so valuable and the sixth hardly matter? Why will a hungry traveller pay a high price for a plate of sadza but refuse a second plate at the same price? Economists explain such behaviour with the idea of **utility**: the satisfaction a consumer gets from a good. The theory of marginal utility links the *law of demand* to individual behaviour and explains why demand curves slope downward.

At the level of Cambridge A Level, this topic is taken at the second stage of the course; in ZIMSEC it belongs to Form 6. You should know the demand curve, the law of demand and how to use a table of figures. The next lesson treats the more advanced indifference-curve approach.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Utility"}
The satisfaction or benefit that a consumer obtains from consuming a good or service. Utility is a subjective idea, measured here in imaginary units (utils) to allow arithmetic.
:::

:::definition{term="Total utility (TU)"}
The total satisfaction obtained from consuming a given quantity of a good.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal utility (MU)"}
The additional satisfaction obtained from consuming one more unit of a good: $\text{MU} = \Delta \text{TU} \div \Delta Q$.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of diminishing marginal utility"}
As a consumer consumes more units of a good in a given period, the marginal utility of each additional unit eventually falls, other things being equal.
:::

## Total and marginal utility

The table shows a fictional student's satisfaction from slices of bread eaten at one meal (illustrative utils).

| Slices consumed | Total utility | Marginal utility |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 10 | 10 |
| 2 | 18 | 8 |
| 3 | 24 | 6 |
| 4 | 28 | 4 |
| 5 | 30 | 2 |
| 6 | 30 | 0 |
| 7 | 28 | −2 |

Marginal utility is the difference between successive totals: MU of the 3rd slice $= 24 - 18 = 6$. Observe three things.

1. Total utility rises as long as marginal utility is positive.
2. Marginal utility **diminishes** with each extra slice.
3. Total utility is at its **maximum** (30) when marginal utility is zero, at the 6th slice (and equals 30 at 5 as well). A 7th slice gives negative marginal utility (disutility), so total utility falls.

A rational consumer who could consume freely would stop when MU reaches zero. In practice goods cost money and the consumer has a limited budget, so the consumer stops earlier.

## The equi-marginal principle

Suppose a consumer buys two goods, X and Y, with a fixed income. Each unit of money should go where it yields the most extra utility. Compare **marginal utility per dollar**: $\text{MU} \div \text{price}$. If MU/P of X exceeds MU/P of Y, buying less Y and more X raises total utility. The consumer reaches equilibrium (maximises total utility) when:

:::keyfact{title="Consumer equilibrium"}
$$\frac{MU_X}{P_X} = \frac{MU_Y}{P_Y} = \dots$$

and the whole budget is spent. In discrete examples, the best bundle is built by buying, one unit at a time, the unit with the highest MU per dollar until the budget runs out.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Choosing the best bundle"}
A consumer has US\$10 to spend on X (price US\$2) and Y (price US\$1). The marginal utilities are:

| Unit | MU of X | MU of X ÷ price | MU of Y | MU of Y ÷ price |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 21 | 10.5 | 9 | 9.0 |
| 2 | 17 | 8.5 | 7 | 7.0 |
| 3 | 13 | 6.5 | 6 | 6.0 |
| 4 | 9 | 4.5 | 5 | 5.0 |
| 5 | 5 | 2.5 | 4 | 4.0 |
| 6 | – | – | 3 | 3.0 |

1. Rank the units by MU per dollar: X1 (10.5), Y1 (9.0), X2 (8.5), Y2 (7.0), X3 (6.5), Y3 (6.0), Y4 (5.0), then X4 (4.5).
2. Buy in that order while the budget lasts: X1 (US\$2), Y1 (US\$1), X2 (US\$2), Y2 (US\$1), X3 (US\$2), Y3 (US\$1): total US\$9.
3. US\$1 remains. X4 costs US\$2 and cannot be bought; Y4 costs US\$1 and has MU per dollar 5.0. Buy Y4. Total spent: US\$10.
4. Best bundle: 3 units of X and 4 units of Y. Total utility $= (21 + 17 + 13) + (9 + 7 + 6 + 5) = 51 + 27 = 78$.
5. Check: the last unit of X bought gives 6.5 per dollar and the last unit of Y gives 5.0, and the next unit of X would give only 4.5. Because units are discrete, MU/P cannot be exactly equal.
:::

## Deriving an individual demand curve

Start from the consumer equilibrium. If the price of X falls, MU/P of X rises above MU/P of the other goods, so the consumer buys more X until diminishing marginal utility brings MU/P back into line. Quantity demanded of X rises when its price falls. Plotting price against the quantity bought gives a downward-sloping demand curve. The *height* of an individual demand curve shows the marginal utility, in money terms, of each unit; this is also why consumer surplus arises, since the consumer pays the same price for every unit but values earlier units more.

The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of all individuals' demand curves.

:::example{title="Why a price fall raises purchases"}
The student above spends US\$10 on X (price US\$2) and Y (price US\$1). If the price of X falls to US\$1, the MU per dollar of X doubles: X1 now gives 21, X2 gives 17 and so on, which exceeds many of the earlier units of Y. The student switches spending towards X, buying more units until diminishing marginal utility lowers MU/P for X again. The demand curve for X is downward sloping.
:::

## Evaluation: limitations of the theory

1. **Utility cannot be measured.** Utils are not real units; satisfaction cannot be compared between people. The ordinal indifference-curve approach (next lesson) avoids measuring utility directly.
2. **Rationality.** The theory assumes consumers are well informed and always seek to maximise utility. In reality people use habit, copy others and may be misled by advertising. Behavioural economics studies such departures.
3. **Divisibility and one period.** The theory treats goods as divisible and consumption as taking place at one time; durable goods and addictive goods behave differently, and marginal utility does not always diminish (the second drink for an addict).
4. **Constant income and tastes.** The theory holds other things equal, but tastes and incomes change.
5. **Strengths.** Despite these limits, the theory explains the downward slope of demand, the importance of the budget and the logic of diversifying spending, and it supports the idea of consumer surplus.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards treat utility and the equi-marginal principle at this level (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge A Level only). ZIMSEC is likely to ask for calculations of MU and best bundle; Cambridge often asks you to *evaluate* the usefulness of the theory.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing total and marginal utility"}
When MU falls, TU can still be rising. Total utility falls only when MU is negative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equalising MU instead of MU per dollar"}
The condition uses MU divided by price. Equal marginal utilities are right only when prices are equal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing diminishing MU means each unit is worth less in total"}
Each extra unit adds less than the previous one, but it still adds something while MU is positive.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the budget"}
Consumer equilibrium needs the whole budget to be spent. Buying until MU is zero ignores prices and income.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for MU from TU tables and the equilibrium condition; Paper 3 essays may ask you to explain the downward-sloping demand curve with utility theory. Cambridge Paper 3 tests calculation in multiple choice and Paper 4 asks for explanation and evaluation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate MU*: subtract successive TU figures. *Explain consumer equilibrium*: state the condition, then show why a consumer who is not in equilibrium gains by reallocating. *Evaluate*: set the logic of the theory against its problems of measurement and rationality.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- MU is the extra utility from one more unit; TU is maximised where MU equals zero.
- The law of diminishing marginal utility explains why extra units are valued less.
- A consumer maximises utility when MU/P is equal for all goods and the budget is spent.
- A fall in price raises MU/P and increases purchases, giving the downward-sloping demand curve.
- Limits: utility cannot be measured, consumers may not be rational and tastes change.
:::
