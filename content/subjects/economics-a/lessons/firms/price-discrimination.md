## Introduction

A bus company in Gweru charges a lower fare to students than to other adults. A hotel in Victoria Falls charges visitors from abroad more than residents in the low season. Electricity companies charge a lower unit price for large users than small ones. In each case the same service is sold at different prices that do not reflect different costs. This is **price discrimination**, and economists ask when a firm can do it, how it sets the prices, and whether consumers gain or lose.

You should know monopoly pricing, MR and elasticity. The data are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price discrimination"}
Charging different prices to different consumers or groups for the same good or service, where the price differences do not reflect differences in cost.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Three degrees"}
**First degree (perfect):** each consumer is charged the maximum they are willing to pay.
**Second degree:** the price depends on the quantity bought (bulk or block prices, such as a lower unit price for each additional block of electricity).
**Third degree:** different groups in separate markets are charged different prices (students and adults, peak and off-peak, domestic and foreign visitors).
:::

## Conditions for price discrimination

1. **Market power:** the firm must be a price-maker, facing a downward-sloping demand curve. A price-taker cannot discriminate.
2. **Separable markets:** the firm must be able to keep the groups apart (by identity cards, time of day, location or age), so that buyers in the cheap market cannot resell to those in the dear market (**no seepage**).
3. **Different price elasticities of demand** in the markets: otherwise the same price would be best in both.

A firm charges a **higher** price in the market where demand is less elastic and a lower price where it is more elastic.

## Third-degree discrimination: a two-market calculation

A fictional bus company has a constant marginal cost of US\$10 per passenger. Demand from adults is $P = 50 - 2Q$ and from students $P = 34 - 2Q$ (Q in hundreds of passengers a week, P in US\$). The company maximises profit by setting MR = MC **in each market**.

:::worked-solution{title="Prices and outputs in the two markets"}
1. **Adults:** $\text{MR} = 50 - 4Q = 10$, so $Q = 10$ and $P = 50 - 20 =$ US\$30. Revenue $= 30 \times 10 = 300$.
2. **Students:** $\text{MR} = 34 - 4Q = 10$, so $Q = 6$ and $P = 34 - 12 =$ US\$22. Revenue $= 22 \times 6 = 132$.
3. Total output $= 16$ hundred passengers; total revenue $= 432$.
4. Profit before fixed costs $= (30 - 10) \times 10 + (22 - 10) \times 6 = 200 + 72 =$ US\$272 (hundred).
5. Price elasticity at these prices: adults $= -30 \div (50 - 30) = -1.50$; students $= -22 \div (34 - 22) = -1.83$. Demand is more elastic in the student market, which gets the lower price.
:::

Now compare with charging a single price to everyone.

:::worked-solution{title="Discrimination compared with one price"}
1. At prices below US\$34 the market demand is the sum of the two quantities: $Q = (50 - P)/2 + (34 - P)/2 = 42 - P$, so $P = 42 - Q$ and $\text{MR} = 42 - 2Q$.
2. MR $=$ MC: $42 - 2Q = 10$, so $Q = 16$ and the single price is $P =$ US\$26.
3. At US\$26 adults buy $(50 - 26)/2 = 12$ and students $(34 - 26)/2 = 4$.
4. Profit before fixed costs $= (26 - 10) \times 16 =$ US\$256 (hundred).
5. Discrimination raises profit from 256 to 272, a gain of 16 (6.25%).
:::

Notice who gains and who loses. Adults pay more (US\$30 against US\$26) and buy less (10 against 12); students pay less (US\$22 against US\$26) and buy more (6 against 4). With straight-line demand curves like these, **total output is the same (16)**. When discrimination allows a market to be served that would not be served at a single price, output rises, and that is the strongest case in its favour.

## First- and second-degree discrimination

**First degree:** if the bus company could charge each adult passenger exactly what they would pay, it would sell until the price falls to marginal cost. In the adult market that is $50 - 2Q = 10$, so $Q = 20$. Revenue is the area under the demand curve, $50 \times 20 - 20^2 = 600$, and profit before fixed cost is $600 - 10 \times 20 =$ US\$400, against US\$200 under a single price. All consumer surplus passes to the firm. Perfect discrimination is rare in practice, because the firm cannot know each person's willingness to pay, but online pricing, auctions and negotiated deals approximate it.

**Second degree:** the firm cannot identify customers, so it offers a price schedule and customers sort themselves, for example a unit price of US\$0.12 for the first 100 units of electricity and US\$0.09 for further units, or cheaper tickets for those who book early. The firm profits from consumers' different valuations without separating them by group.

## Effects of price discrimination

| Group | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Firm | Higher revenue and profit than at a single price; supernormal profit can be used for investment |
| Consumers in the high-price market | Pay more and may lose out |
| Consumers in the low-price market | Pay less; some who would be priced out can now buy |
| Efficiency | Output may rise, and price is closer to marginal cost in the cheap market; but allocative efficiency (P = MC) is achieved only under perfect discrimination, and consumer surplus is reduced |
| Cross-subsidy | Profits in one market can sustain a service in another (for example a rural route kept open) |

:::example{title="Evaluating price discrimination"}
Student fares let low-income groups travel who would otherwise stay at home, and the profit from adults may keep a route open. But adults pay more than a single price would require, and the firm captures consumer surplus. The effect is more favourable when the discriminating firm serves extra consumers, when the profits are used to provide services or lower costs, and when the groups charged more have the means to pay. It is less favourable when the dear market is a captive group of people with no alternatives, such as patients, or when the firm uses its power to take surplus without raising output.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the three degrees, the three conditions, the numerical example of two markets (MR = MC in each) and the assessment of effects on revenue, consumers and efficiency. Examples such as transport, electricity tariffs and student fares appear in both.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating any price difference as discrimination"}
Different prices that reflect different costs (a longer journey, a peak-time service that costs more to provide) are not price discrimination.
:::

:::mistake{title="Charging the higher price in the more elastic market"}
The higher price goes to the group with the less elastic demand. The more elastic group gets the lower price.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the conditions"}
Without market power, separated markets or different elasticities, discrimination cannot be profitable.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the meaning and conditions; Paper 2 can give two demand schedules; Paper 3 asks for discussion of whether it benefits consumers. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 add MR = MC in each market and evaluation of efficiency and welfare.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain two conditions*: name each and say why it is needed. *Calculate*: find price and output in each market and then the revenue or profit. *Evaluate*: weigh the gain to the firm and low-price consumers against the loss to the high-price group, and judge whether output rises.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Price discrimination is charging different prices for the same product, not explained by cost differences.
- First degree charges each buyer their maximum price; second degree uses quantity-based prices; third degree separates groups.
- It needs market power, separable markets and different elasticities of demand.
- The firm sets MR = MC in each market and charges more where demand is less elastic.
- Profit rises; some consumers gain and some lose; output rises if new consumers are served.
:::
