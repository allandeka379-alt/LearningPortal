## Introduction

Economists want more than a market that works: they want one that uses scarce resources *well*. A bakery in Masvingo that sells bread at the lowest possible cost is doing something different from a bakery that sells bread at a price equal to the cost of the last loaf, and different again from a bakery that invents a new, cheaper way to bake. The word "efficiency" covers all of these, and examiners expect you to say **which kind** you mean. This lesson defines five kinds, shows the conditions for each and uses them to compare the market structures you have met (perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly).

You should already know the cost curves (ATC, MC), the profit-maximising rule MC = MR and the features of each market structure. Market failure, the reason governments intervene when markets are inefficient, is the subject of a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Productive efficiency"}
Output is produced at the lowest possible average total cost: the firm operates at the lowest point of its ATC curve, where MC = ATC. No resources are wasted in making each unit.
:::

:::definition{term="Allocative efficiency"}
Resources are allocated so that the goods produced match what consumers want most. For a good with no externalities this holds where price equals marginal cost (P = MC): the value consumers place on the last unit equals the cost of the resources used to make it.
:::

:::definition{term="Technical efficiency"}
The maximum output is obtained from a given set of inputs, so no input is wasted. A firm that produces less than it could from the same labour, land and capital is technically inefficient. *(ZIMSEC detail)*
:::

:::definition{term="Pareto efficiency (optimality)"}
An allocation of resources in which it is impossible to make one person better off without making at least one other person worse off.
:::

:::definition{term="Dynamic efficiency"}
Improvement in efficiency over time through innovation, new products and processes, and investment that lowers costs or raises quality in the future.
:::

:::definition{term="X-inefficiency"}
Costs that are higher than the minimum possible because a firm faces too little competitive pressure to keep them down: managers and workers allow slack, so the firm operates above its lowest possible cost curve for any output.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Conditions to learn"}
- Productive efficiency: MC = ATC, at the minimum of ATC.
- Allocative efficiency: P = MC (more generally marginal social benefit = marginal social cost).
- Technical efficiency: maximum output from given inputs.
- Dynamic efficiency: investment and innovation over time.
- X-inefficiency: actual costs above the lowest costs achievable at that output.
:::

## Productive efficiency from a cost schedule

The table gives the costs of a fictional firm that makes school desks in Gweru (output in desks a week; fixed costs US\$60). All figures are illustrative.

| Output (desks) | Total cost (US\$) | ATC (US\$) | MC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 96 | 96.00 | 36 |
| 2 | 120 | 60.00 | 24 |
| 3 | 138 | 46.00 | 18 |
| 4 | 156 | 39.00 | 18 |
| 5 | 178 | 35.60 | 22 |
| 6 | 210 | 35.00 | 32 |
| 7 | 256 | 36.57 | 46 |
| 8 | 320 | 40.00 | 64 |

:::worked-solution{title="Finding the productively efficient output"}
1. Work out ATC at each output as total cost ÷ output (for example, 210 ÷ 6 = 35.00).
2. Look for the lowest ATC. It falls until 6 desks (US\$35.00) and rises after that (US\$36.57 at 7 desks).
3. The firm is productively efficient at **6 desks a week**, where ATC is at its minimum. At 4 desks ATC is US\$39.00, so each desk costs US\$4.00 more than necessary.
4. MC (US\$32) is below ATC at 6 desks and above it at 7, so MC crosses ATC at the bottom of the U: this is why MC = ATC marks productive efficiency.
:::

A firm can be productively efficient without being allocatively efficient. It may make desks very cheaply but sell them at a price far above their cost, or make desks that nobody wants.

## Allocative efficiency and the monopoly loss

In perfect competition each firm is a price taker, so it sets MC = MR = P and produces where P = MC. In the long run, free entry also drives price down to the minimum of ATC, so a perfectly competitive firm is **both** productively and allocatively efficient. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve and sets MC = MR, which gives a price above MC.

:::worked-solution{title="The welfare loss from monopoly"}
A fictional monopoly bottler in Bulawayo faces demand P = 80 − 2Q (P in US\$, Q in thousand bottles) and constant MC of US\$20.

1. MR = 80 − 4Q. Profit is maximised where MR = MC: 80 − 4Q = 20, so Q = 15 (thousand) and P = 80 − 2 × 15 = US\$50.
2. Under competition the price would equal MC: 80 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 30 (thousand) and P = US\$20.
3. The monopolist restricts output by 15 thousand bottles. The last bottles it does not make are valued by consumers at more than MC, so there is a deadweight (welfare) loss.
4. Welfare loss = ½ × (50 − 20) × (30 − 15) = **US\$225 thousand**. Price exceeds MC by US\$30, so the monopoly is **allocatively inefficient**.
:::

## Technical, dynamic and X-efficiency

:::example{title="Technical efficiency"}
Two fictional tomato farms near Mutare each use 10 workers, 5 hectares and the same equipment. Farm A produces 120 crates a week and Farm B produces 90. Farm B is technically inefficient: with the same inputs it produces 25% less than Farm A. Output per worker is 12 crates on Farm A and 9 on Farm B.
:::

**Dynamic efficiency** concerns the future. A monopolist or an oligopolist earning supernormal (abnormal) profit has the funds to pay for research and new equipment, and patents give a reward for innovation, so such firms may be more dynamically efficient than price-taking firms that earn only normal profit. The opposite view is that firms protected from competition feel no pressure to innovate. The evidence differs between industries.

**X-inefficiency** arises when a firm with market power does not bother to keep costs down.

:::worked-solution{title="Measuring X-inefficiency"}
A protected fictional monopoly produces 15 thousand units at an average cost of US\$26 per unit. A competing firm of the same size could produce at US\$22 per unit.

1. Excess cost per unit = 26 − 22 = US\$4.
2. Total waste = 4 × 15 thousand = **US\$60 thousand** a year.
3. This is an upward shift of the whole cost curve, not a movement along it, which is what separates X-inefficiency from simply operating at the wrong output.
:::

## Comparing the market structures

| Structure | Productive | Allocative | Dynamic | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition (long run) | Yes: P = minimum ATC | Yes: P = MC | Weak: only normal profit | Benchmark, rarely found in practice |
| Monopoly | Not necessarily; may gain economies of scale | No: P > MC, welfare loss | Possible: profit funds R&D | Risk of X-inefficiency |
| Monopolistic competition (long run) | No: excess capacity | No: P > MC | Some product innovation | Variety is a gain to consumers |
| Oligopoly | Varies | Usually not | Often strong: rivalry and funds | Depends on collusion and rivalry |

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the conditions for productive and allocative efficiency, dynamic efficiency and X-inefficiency, and the comparison of structures. Technical efficiency and the evaluation of the structures for a whole economy are ZIMSEC detail; Cambridge expects the evaluation within a question on a given market.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a large monopoly"}
A single firm supplies electricity to a region. It enjoys large economies of scale, so splitting it into ten firms would raise average cost. It may be productively efficient, yet it charges above MC and so is allocatively inefficient. Whether it is better than competition depends on the size of the scale economies, whether it is regulated, how much it invests and the time horizon. A sound conclusion weighs these rather than saying "monopoly is bad".
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that productive efficiency means producing the most"}
It means the lowest cost per unit, not the largest output. A firm can produce a large output at a high average cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using P = MC for every good"}
P = MC gives allocative efficiency only when there are no externalities; otherwise the condition is marginal social benefit = marginal social cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing X-inefficiency with diseconomies of scale"}
Diseconomies of scale are a movement along the long-run curve as output grows. X-inefficiency is a failure to reach the lowest cost curve at a given output.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and conditions; Paper 2 data questions give a cost or price schedule; Paper 3 essays ask you to compare structures. Cambridge Paper 3 multiple choice tests the conditions, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of efficiency in a given market.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the condition in words and symbols. *Explain*: link the condition to the firm's price and cost. *Calculate*: find ATC or the welfare loss, showing the working. *Evaluate*: set static efficiency (now) against dynamic efficiency (over time) and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Productive efficiency is production at the lowest ATC (MC = ATC); allocative efficiency is P = MC.
- Technical efficiency is maximum output from given inputs (ZIMSEC); Pareto efficiency leaves nobody able to gain without another losing.
- Dynamic efficiency is gains over time from innovation; X-inefficiency is cost above the minimum due to weak competition.
- Perfect competition in the long run is productively and allocatively efficient; monopoly has P above MC and a welfare loss.
- Monopoly and oligopoly may be dynamically efficient; evaluate by weighing scale economies, regulation and innovation.
:::
