## Introduction

A village shop owner in Chinhoyi may try to earn as much as possible. The manager of a large company, who does not own it, may prefer to see the company grow, because larger companies pay higher salaries and bring more prestige. A cooperative may exist mainly to serve its members, and a clinic run by a church may exist to heal. If firms have different aims, they will choose different prices and outputs from the same costs and demand. This lesson looks at what firms may be trying to achieve and what each objective means for price and output.

You should be able to find the profit-maximising output from a revenue and cost schedule, as in the earlier lessons. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Profit maximisation"}
Producing the output (and charging the price) at which total revenue exceeds total cost by the greatest amount, which occurs where MC = MR with MC rising.
:::

:::definition{term="Revenue (sales revenue) maximisation"}
Producing the output at which total revenue is the highest, which occurs where MR = 0.
:::

:::definition{term="Satisficing"}
Aiming for a satisfactory level of profit and other targets rather than the maximum possible, so that owners, managers, workers and customers are all reasonably content.
:::

## Main objectives

**Profit maximisation.** The standard assumption: it gives a clear rule (MC = MR) and is plausible when owners control the firm and competition is intense. Profit is also needed for survival and for investment.

**Survival.** New and small firms, or firms in a recession, may care mainly about staying in business. They may set low prices or cut costs, accepting low profits.

**Growth.** A growing firm gains economies of scale, market share and power over suppliers, and its managers gain higher pay and status. Growth may be pursued even if it sacrifices some current profit.

**Revenue or sales maximisation.** A manager's pay and status may depend on the size of sales. A firm may also seek a large market share to attract advertisers or to deter rivals. It may be limited by a minimum acceptable profit (needed to keep shareholders content).

**Satisficing.** When ownership and control are separated, managers cannot easily be monitored, and they may settle for adequate profit so as to keep shareholders content while they pursue other aims, such as an easy life or prestige.

**Social and ethical objectives.** Cooperatives, social enterprises, state-owned firms and charities may aim at serving members, providing jobs or reaching a social target. Private firms may adopt ethical or environmental policies, either from genuine concern or because customers favour them.

## What each objective implies

Take a fictional firm whose demand and cost schedule is shown below (fixed cost US\$10).

| Output (units) | Price (US\$) | TR (US\$) | TC (US\$) | MR (US\$) | MC (US\$) | Profit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 20 | 0 | 10 | – | – | −10 |
| 1 | 18 | 18 | 16 | 18.00 | 6.00 | 2 |
| 2 | 16 | 32 | 20 | 14.00 | 4.00 | 12 |
| 3 | 14 | 42 | 23 | 10.00 | 3.00 | 19 |
| 4 | 12 | 48 | 27 | 6.00 | 4.00 | 21 |
| 5 | 10 | 50 | 32 | 2.00 | 5.00 | 18 |
| 6 | 8 | 48 | 40 | −2.00 | 8.00 | 8 |
| 7 | 6 | 42 | 52 | −6.00 | 12.00 | −10 |
| 8 | 4 | 32 | 70 | −10.00 | 18.00 | −38 |

:::worked-solution{title="Three objectives, three outputs"}
1. **Profit maximisation.** MR (6) is above MC (4) at 4 units; MR (2) is below MC (5) at 5 units. Output is 4 units at a price of US\$12; profit is US\$21.
2. **Revenue maximisation.** TR is highest at 5 units (US\$50), where MR has fallen to US\$2 and is about to become negative. Price is US\$10 and profit is US\$18.
3. **Sales (output) maximisation with a minimum profit of zero.** The firm can increase sales while profit stays at or above zero. At 6 units profit is US\$8, but at 7 units it is −US\$10. Output is 6 units, price US\$8.
:::

Comparing the three: the move from profit maximisation to revenue maximisation raises output (4 to 5) and lowers price (US\$12 to US\$10); sales maximisation subject to a profit floor raises output further (to 6) and lowers price again (to US\$8). If the firm must earn at least US\$10 profit, the sales maximiser stops at 5 units, where profit is US\$18, since 6 units would give only US\$8. In general a firm that does not maximise profit produces **more and charges less** than a profit maximiser, which is not bad for consumers, but it earns less profit.

:::worked-solution{title="The cost of pursuing revenue"}
Use the schedule to measure the profit given up by a firm that maximises revenue instead of profit.

1. Maximum profit is US\$21 (4 units); profit at revenue maximisation is US\$18 (5 units).
2. Profit given up $= 21 - 18 = 3$, which is $3 \div 21 \times 100 = 14.3\%$ of maximum profit.
3. Extra revenue gained $= 50 - 48 =$ US\$2.
:::

## Separation of ownership and control: the principal-agent problem

In a large company the owners (shareholders, the **principals**) employ managers (the **agents**) to run it. The two groups may have different aims: shareholders want profit and a high share price, while managers may want high salaries, bonuses tied to sales, a large staff and expensive offices. Managers know more about the business than shareholders do (asymmetric information) and monitoring them is costly. This **principal-agent problem** can lead the firm to depart from profit maximisation.

Ways to reduce it include:

1. Linking managers' pay to profit or to the share price (bonuses, share options), although this can encourage short-term behaviour.
2. Independent directors and audited accounts to improve monitoring.
3. The threat of takeover if poor management reduces the share price.
4. Active shareholders who vote on pay and strategy.

This is Cambridge detail; ZIMSEC answers may simply mention that managers and shareholders can have different aims.

## Why objectives differ

Objectives depend on ownership (the owner-manager of a small firm is likely to maximise profit, while a state-owned company may follow social aims), on the amount of competition (a firm in a competitive market must make profit to survive; a firm with market power can afford to satisfice), on the size and stage of the firm (new firms want survival and then growth) and on the information available to decision-makers.

:::example{title="Evaluating profit maximisation as an assumption"}
The assumption of profit maximisation is useful because it yields clear predictions: a rise in marginal cost reduces output, and an increase in demand raises it. It is also realistic where owners run firms and where competition punishes inefficient firms. However, in large firms with separated ownership and control, in firms with social aims and in firms that lack the information to find MC = MR, profit may not be the sole aim. Judging the assumption therefore depends on the type of firm and the market in which it operates.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect profit maximisation, survival, growth, revenue or sales maximisation and satisficing, with the effects on price and output. Cambridge adds the principal-agent problem and its solutions. ZIMSEC (Form 5) examples often involve state-owned enterprises, cooperatives and small firms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying revenue maximisation means profit maximisation"}
Revenue is largest where MR = 0, which is at a higher output than profit-maximising output, because MC is positive.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming only large firms have other objectives"}
A small owner-managed firm may seek survival, a good lifestyle or social goals instead of maximum profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing satisficing with loss-making"}
A satisficing firm earns a satisfactory, positive profit; it merely does not seek the maximum.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the meaning of the objectives; Paper 2 can give a table to find outputs under profit and revenue maximisation; Paper 3 asks for discussion of the objectives. Cambridge Paper 3 tests the outputs and prices implied by each objective, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of profit maximisation and the principal-agent problem.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give the objective and state the rule it implies (MC = MR; MR = 0). *Analyse*: compare price and output with those of a profit maximiser. *Discuss or evaluate*: weigh the plausibility of profit maximisation against ownership, competition and information, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Profit maximisation: MC = MR with MC rising; revenue maximisation: MR = 0.
- Other objectives are survival, growth, sales maximisation (often with a profit floor), satisficing and social aims.
- A revenue or sales maximiser produces more and charges less than a profit maximiser, but earns less profit.
- Separation of ownership and control causes the principal-agent problem; pay linked to profit and monitoring reduce it.
- Objectives depend on ownership, competition, size and information.
:::
