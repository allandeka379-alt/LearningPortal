## Introduction

A farmer selling maize at a roadside in Masvingo has dozens of identical competitors and must accept the market price. The national electricity transmission network has no competitor at all. Between these extremes lie hairdressing salons in Harare (many sellers, each a little different) and mobile networks (a few big firms who watch each other closely). Economists classify markets by their **structure**: the number of firms, the type of product, how easily new firms can enter and how much each firm knows. The structure helps to predict the price, output and efficiency of the market.

You should already know cost and revenue curves and the idea of supernormal profit. All data are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market structure"}
The characteristics of a market that affect firms' behaviour and performance: the number and size of firms, the degree of product differentiation, barriers to entry and exit, and the availability of information.
:::

:::definition{term="Barriers to entry"}
Obstacles that make it difficult or impossible for new firms to enter a market and compete with existing firms.
:::

:::definition{term="Contestable market"}
A market in which entry is easy and exit is costless (there are no or very low sunk costs), so that the threat of entry disciplines the behaviour of existing firms even when there are few of them.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentration ratio"}
The combined market share of the largest $n$ firms in an industry, given as a percentage (for example a four-firm concentration ratio).
:::

## The spectrum of structures

| Feature | Perfect competition | Monopolistic competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Number of firms | Very many | Many | A few dominant firms | One |
| Product | Identical | Differentiated | Identical or differentiated | One product with no close substitute |
| Entry and exit | Free | Easy | Barriers significant | Very high barriers |
| Price control | None (price-taker) | Some | Considerable but interdependent | Considerable (price-maker) |
| Information | Perfect | Imperfect | Imperfect | Imperfect |
| Examples | Small-scale maize or tomato sellers at a market (approximately) | Hair salons, restaurants, small shops | Mobile networks, banks, brewing | A single water or rail network (natural monopoly) |

No real market fits exactly: perfect competition is a benchmark. A **natural monopoly** arises when economies of scale are so large that one firm can supply the whole market at lower average cost than two or more could, as in electricity transmission. A **pure monopoly** is a single seller; in practice, laws often define a firm as having monopoly power at a market share above a stated level, and different countries choose different thresholds, so avoid quoting one.

## Measuring concentration

:::worked-solution{title="Concentration ratios"}
The market shares of nine firms in a fictional soft-drinks market are 28%, 22%, 15%, 12%, 8%, 5%, 4%, 3% and 3%.

1. Three-firm concentration ratio $= 28 + 22 + 15 = 65\%$.
2. Four-firm concentration ratio $= 65 + 12 = 77\%$.
3. Check that the shares add to 100: $65 + 12 + 8 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 3 = 100$.
4. A high ratio indicates a market dominated by few firms (oligopoly), though it does not show how fiercely they compete.
:::

Concentration ratios are easy to calculate, but they hide the distribution among the leaders, they depend on how the market is defined (a national or a local market) and they say nothing about barriers to entry or about competition from imports.

## Barriers to entry and exit

1. **Legal barriers:** patents, licences, government-granted monopolies and the ownership of a scarce resource by law.
2. **Market (strategic) barriers:** established brands, advertising spending, loyalty schemes, and the threat of price cuts by existing firms (limit pricing and predatory pricing).
3. **Cost barriers:** large economies of scale. If the minimum efficient scale is a large share of the market, a new firm cannot start small and survive.
4. **Physical or resource barriers:** control of essential raw materials, networks or sites.
5. **Exit barriers:** the cost of leaving, such as redundancy payments, long contracts, and the sunk cost of specialised equipment that cannot be sold.

:::worked-solution{title="Economies of scale as a barrier"}
In a fictional cement market total demand is 1,000,000 tonnes a year. A plant reaches minimum average cost at 250,000 tonnes.

1. The number of firms that can each operate at the minimum efficient scale is $1{,}000{,}000 \div 250{,}000 = 4$.
2. If the minimum efficient scale were 1,200,000 tonnes, one firm would supply the whole market at lowest cost: a natural monopoly.
3. Barriers are therefore high in this market: a new firm planning to produce only 50,000 tonnes would have much higher average costs than the established firms.
:::

## Contestable markets

A market can have a single firm and still be competitive in its behaviour if rivals could enter easily. For a market to be contestable:

1. There are no or low barriers to entry, so potential entrants have access to the same technology and costs as existing firms.
2. Exit is costless: there are few **sunk costs**, which are costs that cannot be recovered when leaving the market (specialised equipment, advertising already spent).
3. Entrants can use "hit-and-run" tactics: enter, take profits and leave when the firm responds.

If a market is contestable, existing firms are disciplined by the threat of entry. They keep price close to average cost and avoid supernormal profit, to avoid inviting rivals. For example, an airline route between two cities may have only one operator, but if aircraft can be moved to the route easily the operator cannot charge a high fare for long. High sunk costs, such as a purpose-built plant, make a market less contestable.

:::example{title="Assessing a market's competitiveness"}
A fictional bus route is served by one firm. Competition looks absent, but the route can be entered by any operator with a licensed minibus, the vehicles can be sold or moved to another route at little loss, and rivals have entered in the past. The market is highly contestable, and the incumbent keeps fares near cost. By contrast, a firm that owns a unique port facility has high sunk costs for any entrant and can charge higher prices. Counting firms is therefore not enough; the barriers matter as much as the number of firms.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the features of each structure, barriers to entry and exit and contestable markets (including sunk costs and the effect on behaviour). Cambridge Paper 4 questions often ask candidates to assess the importance of barriers or to judge whether a market is contestable. ZIMSEC questions are more likely to ask for features and examples.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating one firm with no competition"}
A market with one firm can still be contestable. Always ask about barriers and sunk costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a market a monopoly because the firm is large"}
Size alone does not decide the structure. The question is the number of sellers and the barriers to entry in the relevant market.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting sunk costs as any cost"}
Sunk costs are those that cannot be recovered on exit. Rent that can be ended is not sunk.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for features and ratios; Paper 3 asks for comparisons of structures. Cambridge Paper 3 tests features, concentration ratios and contestability; Paper 4 essays assess whether the number of firms determines behaviour.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: give a feature of each and say how it differs. *Explain*: link the barrier to the reduced entry. *Assess*: weigh the number of firms against the contestability of the market and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly, defined by number of firms, product type, entry and information.
- An $n$-firm concentration ratio is the combined market share of the $n$ largest firms.
- Barriers to entry may be legal, market, cost or physical; exit barriers include sunk costs.
- Natural monopoly occurs when economies of scale are large relative to the market.
- A contestable market has free entry and costless exit, so the threat of entry restrains firms.
:::
