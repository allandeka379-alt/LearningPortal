## Introduction

When you switch on a light in Harare, there is only one grid that carries the electricity to your home. A railway between two towns, a water pipe network, and a patented medicine are other cases in which one seller supplies the whole market. A firm in that position does not have to accept a market price; it is a **price-maker**, and it can choose a combination of price and quantity along the demand curve. The question for economists is how it chooses, how the result compares with competition, and what governments should do about it.

You should know marginal revenue, the MC = MR rule and the features of market structures. In this lesson prices are in US\$ and quantity is in thousand units, with all money values in US\$ thousand. The data are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monopoly"}
A market structure with a single seller of a product that has no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry, so that the firm is a price-maker.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural monopoly"}
A market in which economies of scale are so large that a single firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than any larger number of firms, so that the long-run average cost curve is still falling at the output demanded.
:::

:::definition{term="Deadweight (welfare) loss"}
The loss of total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus) that arises when output is below the output at which price equals marginal cost.
:::

## Sources of monopoly power

1. **Legal barriers:** patents, copyrights, licences and statutory monopolies.
2. **Control of a key resource** such as a mine, a pipeline or an essential site.
3. **Economies of scale** that leave room for only one efficient firm (natural monopoly).
4. **Strategic behaviour:** price cuts or advertising by the incumbent that deter entry, and strong brand loyalty.
5. **Network effects:** a service that is more valuable the more people use it makes it hard for a newcomer to compete.

## Equilibrium of a monopolist

The monopolist faces the whole market demand curve, which slopes downward, so $\text{MR} < \text{AR}$. It maximises profit where MR = MC with MC rising and sets the price from the demand curve at that output.

Take a fictional quarry supplying a town. Demand is $P = 80 - 2Q$, so $\text{MR} = 80 - 4Q$, constant marginal cost is US\$20 and fixed cost is 250 (US\$ thousand).

| Output Q (thousand units) | Price (US\$) | TR (US\$ thousand) | MR (US\$) | MC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 5 | 70 | 350 | 60 | 20 |
| 10 | 60 | 600 | 40 | 20 |
| 15 | 50 | 750 | 20 | 20 |
| 20 | 40 | 800 | 0 | 20 |
| 25 | 30 | 750 | −20 | 20 |
| 30 | 20 | 600 | −40 | 20 |

:::worked-solution{title="Monopoly price, output and profit"}
1. MR $=$ MC: $80 - 4Q = 20$, so $Q = 15$ thousand units.
2. Price from the demand curve: $P = 80 - 2 \times 15 =$ US\$50.
3. Profit before fixed cost $= (50 - 20) \times 15 = 450$. After the fixed cost of 250, supernormal profit is US\$200 thousand.
4. Check by average cost: $\text{ATC} = 20 + 250 \div 15 = 36.67$; profit $= (50 - 36.67) \times 15 = 200$.
:::

Price (US\$50) is **above** marginal cost (US\$20). Because entry is blocked, supernormal profit can last into the long run.

## Comparison with perfect competition

If the same industry were perfectly competitive, with many firms having the same marginal cost, output would be where price equals MC.

:::worked-solution{title="Welfare loss"}
1. Competitive output: $80 - 2Q = 20$, so $Q = 30$ thousand units at a price of US\$20.
2. The monopolist produces 15 thousand units at US\$50: **half the output and 2.5 times the price**.
3. Consumer surplus under monopoly $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times (80 - 50) \times 15 = 225$; under competition $\tfrac{1}{2} \times (80 - 20) \times 30 = 900$.
4. Deadweight loss $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times (30 - 15) \times (50 - 20) = 225$ (the part of the surplus that nobody receives).
5. The rest of the lost consumer surplus is transferred to the monopolist as profit: $450$ (the rectangle between the price and the MC).
:::

The monopoly is allocatively inefficient (P > MC) and, if it faces no pressure, may be productively inefficient (not producing at minimum average cost). **X-inefficiency** may arise: without competition managers have little incentive to control costs, so actual costs lie above the lowest possible. Monopoly also redistributes income from consumers to shareholders.

## In defence of monopoly

1. **Economies of scale** may make costs lower than those of many small firms; the cost saving could outweigh the higher mark-up.
2. **Supernormal profit** can fund research, development and investment (dynamic efficiency), and patents reward innovation.
3. **Price discrimination** may allow some low-income consumers to be served (see the lesson on price discrimination).
4. A monopolist in a contestable market may keep prices close to average cost for fear of entry.
5. International competition may limit a local monopoly's power.

## Natural monopoly and regulation

In a natural monopoly the LRAC curve is still falling at the output at which the demand curve cuts it, so duplicating the network (two railway lines, two sets of pipes) would waste resources. Competition is not practical, so the choice is between an unregulated monopolist and regulation.

Suppose the fictional quarry is a natural monopoly: its long-run average cost is $20 + 250 \div Q$ (marginal cost US\$20, fixed cost 250), which keeps falling as Q rises, and demand is $P = 80 - 2Q$.

:::worked-solution{title="Regulating a natural monopoly"}
1. **Unregulated:** $Q = 15$, $P = 50$, supernormal profit US\$200 thousand.
2. **Marginal-cost pricing (P = MC):** $Q = 30$, $P = 20$. This is allocatively efficient, but the price covers only marginal cost, so the firm loses the fixed cost of US\$250 thousand and needs a subsidy to survive.
3. **Average-cost pricing (P = ATC):** solve $20 + 250 \div Q = 80 - 2Q$, which gives $2Q^2 - 60Q + 250 = 0$, so $Q = 25$ (taking the larger root: the smaller root, $Q = 5$ at a price of US\$70, also gives P = ATC but with far less output and a much higher price). Then $P = 80 - 50 = 30$ and ATC $= 20 + 10 = 30$. The firm earns only normal profit and the output is close to the efficient level.
:::

Regulators therefore often choose between marginal-cost pricing with a subsidy and average-cost pricing, and they face problems of information: they know less about the firm's true costs than the firm does. Other tools are **price-cap regulation** (limits on price rises), rate-of-return regulation, performance targets, and franchising (competition for the right to run the monopoly), or public ownership.

:::example{title="Evaluating the case against monopoly"}
A fictional power utility is the only supplier of electricity in a region. It charges a price above marginal cost and earns supernormal profit, so consumers pay more and consume less than under competition. But splitting the utility into several firms would duplicate the network and raise cost, so a regulator might instead cap the price. The result depends on how large the economies of scale are and on how well the regulator knows the firm's costs. The case against monopoly is strongest where it is not a natural monopoly and where entry could be opened by removing legal barriers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect MC = MR equilibrium with supernormal profit, comparison with perfect competition, the welfare loss, X-inefficiency, benefits of monopoly and natural monopoly with regulation. Cambridge Paper 4 essays frequently ask whether monopoly is always bad; ZIMSEC questions often use state-owned utilities as examples.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Setting price where MC = MR"}
MC = MR gives the output only. The price is read from the demand curve (AR) at that output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the monopolist can charge any price"}
It can choose price or quantity, but not both: a higher price means lower sales along the demand curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming a monopolist always earns supernormal profit"}
If demand is weak or costs are high, a monopolist may earn only normal profit or make a loss.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for features and the price and output; Paper 2 can use a table of costs and revenue; Paper 3 asks to discuss the advantages and disadvantages. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 add welfare loss and regulation of natural monopoly.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: find MR = MC, read the price off demand, and show supernormal profit. *Compare*: state price, output and efficiency under both structures. *Evaluate*: weigh economies of scale and innovation against higher prices and X-inefficiency, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A monopolist is a price-maker facing the market demand curve, with MR below AR.
- It produces where MC = MR (MC rising) and sets price from the demand curve.
- Compared with perfect competition output is lower and price higher, with allocative inefficiency and a deadweight loss.
- Possible benefits are economies of scale, innovation and funds for investment.
- Natural monopoly arises when LRAC falls over the whole market; regulation uses price caps, average-cost pricing, subsidies or public ownership.
:::
