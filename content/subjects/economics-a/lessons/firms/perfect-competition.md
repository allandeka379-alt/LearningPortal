## Introduction

Walk through a produce market in Mutare on a Saturday and you will see dozens of sellers of tomatoes, onions and covo, with similar produce on offer at almost the same price. A seller who asks for much more loses her customers, and a seller who cuts the price much below the market price gives away income, because she can sell all she has at the going rate. Perfect competition is the model of a market like this carried to its logical limit. No real market meets every condition, but the model explains how competition drives prices to the level of cost, and it serves as the benchmark against which monopoly and other structures are judged.

You should know the cost curves, revenue curves for a price-taker and the shutdown rule. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Perfect competition"}
A market structure in which there are very many buyers and sellers of an identical product, each too small to influence the price, with perfect information and freedom of entry and exit, so that every firm is a price-taker.
:::

:::definition{term="Price-taker"}
A firm that must accept the market price because its own output is too small to influence it. Its demand curve is horizontal at the market price.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Assumptions of the model"}
1. Many buyers and sellers, each small relative to the market.
2. Identical (homogeneous) products, so buyers have no reason to prefer one seller.
3. Perfect knowledge of prices and technology.
4. Freedom of entry and exit: no barriers and no sunk costs.
5. Firms are profit maximisers and there are no externalities.
:::

## The firm's revenue curves

Because price is fixed by the market at, say, US\$36, the firm can sell as many units as it wishes at that price. Then $\text{AR} = \text{MR} = \text{price}$ at every output, and the firm's demand curve is a horizontal line. The firm's decision is only about quantity: it chooses the output at which marginal cost equals the price (= MR), with MC rising.

## Short-run equilibrium

A fictional maize farm has the following costs (the fixed cost is US\$40; output in tonnes).

| Output (units) | TFC (US\$) | TVC (US\$) | TC (US\$) | AFC (US\$) | AVC (US\$) | ATC (US\$) | MC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 40 | 0 | 40 | – | – | – | – |
| 1 | 40 | 30 | 70 | 40.00 | 30.00 | 70.00 | 30.00 |
| 2 | 40 | 52 | 92 | 20.00 | 26.00 | 46.00 | 22.00 |
| 3 | 40 | 66 | 106 | 13.33 | 22.00 | 35.33 | 14.00 |
| 4 | 40 | 81 | 121 | 10.00 | 20.25 | 30.25 | 15.00 |
| 5 | 40 | 100 | 140 | 8.00 | 20.00 | 28.00 | 19.00 |
| 6 | 40 | 130 | 170 | 6.67 | 21.67 | 28.33 | 30.00 |
| 7 | 40 | 172 | 212 | 5.71 | 24.57 | 30.29 | 42.00 |
| 8 | 40 | 226 | 266 | 5.00 | 28.25 | 33.25 | 54.00 |

:::worked-solution{title="Supernormal profit at a price of US\$36"}
1. Marginal cost is below US\$36 for the sixth unit (MC 30) and above it for the seventh (MC 42). The firm produces **6 units**.
2. $\text{TR} = 36 \times 6 = 216$; $\text{TC} = 170$; profit $=$ US\$46.
3. Check: $(\text{AR} - \text{ATC}) \times Q = (36 - 28.33) \times 6 =$ US\$46. This is supernormal profit.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Normal profit, loss and shutdown"}
1. **US\$28:** MC (19) is below 28 for the fifth unit, but the sixth costs 30. Output is 5 units. $\text{TR} = 140 = \text{TC}$, so profit is zero: normal profit. Price equals the minimum ATC.
2. **US\$24:** output is 5 units (MC 19 below 24; the sixth unit costs 30). $\text{TR} = 120$, $\text{TC} = 140$, loss US\$20. Price is above AVC (20.00), so the firm covers its variable cost and loses less than the fixed cost of 40: it **continues**.
3. **US\$18:** output is 4 units (MC 15 below 18; the fifth unit costs 19). $\text{TR} = 72$ and $\text{TC} = 121$, a loss of US\$49. Price is below AVC (20.25), so each unit adds to the loss. Shutting down limits the loss to the fixed cost, US\$40: the firm **shuts down**.
:::

**The firm's short-run supply curve** is the part of its MC curve above the minimum AVC. In the table, at prices of US\$20 to US\$54 the firm supplies the output where MC equals price, and below the minimum AVC (US\$20.00) it supplies nothing. The **industry** short-run supply curve is the sum of the supply curves of all firms in the market.

## Long-run equilibrium

In the long run firms can enter and leave. 

1. **Supernormal profit** (price above ATC) attracts new firms. Industry supply shifts to the right, the market price falls, and each firm's horizontal demand curve moves down.
2. **Losses** (price below ATC) cause firms to leave. Industry supply shifts to the left and the price rises.
3. **Long-run equilibrium** is reached when price equals the minimum of the long-run average cost curve: every firm earns normal profit, and no firm wants to enter or leave. At this point $\text{P} = \text{MR} = \text{MC} = \text{ATC}$ at the minimum point.

In the example, the long-run price is US\$28 (the minimum ATC) and each firm produces 5 units.

![The maize farm at three prices: at US\$28, the minimum of ATC, it produces 5 units and earns normal profit; at US\$24 it also produces 5 units and loses US\$20 but covers its variable cost (minimum AVC is US\$20); at US\$18, below minimum AVC, it shuts down.](media:diagrams/perfect-competition-shutdown.png "Figure: normal profit at P = minimum ATC (US\$28), a loss that is still worth bearing at US\$24, and shutdown below minimum AVC (US\$18)")

:::worked-solution{title="From supernormal profit to long-run equilibrium"}
Currently the price is US\$36 and each of the 100 firms in the industry makes US\$46 profit. At US\$28 the market would demand 600 units.

1. New firms enter because profit is supernormal: industry supply increases.
2. The price falls towards US\$28. At US\$28 each firm produces 5 units, with $\text{TR} = \text{TC} = 140$.
3. At US\$28 the market demands 600 units, so the number of firms in long-run equilibrium is $600 \div 5 = 120$: 20 new firms have entered, each producing 5 units.
:::

## Efficiency

Perfect competition in the long run leads to:

1. **Productive efficiency:** firms produce at the minimum of average total cost, so no resources are wasted.
2. **Allocative efficiency:** price equals marginal cost, so the value consumers place on the last unit equals the cost to society of producing it. Society cannot be made better off by changing the output.
3. **Normal profit only:** no supernormal profit in the long run.
4. **No dynamic efficiency guaranteed:** there is no supernormal profit to finance research and development, and all firms use the same technology, so innovation may be slow.

Real markets deviate from the model. Agricultural markets approximate it (many farmers, similar produce), but farmers often do not have perfect information, they can be affected by weather, and they may have to deal with a few large buyers. The model is nevertheless valuable as a benchmark for efficiency and for explaining how competition lowers price.

:::example{title="Evaluating perfect competition"}
Perfect competition delivers low prices and efficient use of resources, and it is useful in analysing farm markets and currency trading. But the assumptions are demanding: products are rarely identical, information is seldom perfect, and many industries have economies of scale that make a large number of small firms inefficient. Therefore its value is mainly as a standard against which to judge how far actual markets fall short and what government policy might do.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the assumptions, short-run and long-run equilibrium, the shutdown rule, the supply curve from MC above AVC and the efficiency of the long-run outcome. Cambridge requires clear use of terms such as productive and allocative efficiency; ZIMSEC questions often use agriculture as the example.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the firm has no supply decision"}
Although the firm cannot choose price, it chooses output. It produces where P = MC (rising) provided P is at least AVC.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that normal profit is included in cost"}
In long-run equilibrium the firm earns normal profit, not zero profit in the accounting sense.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing the firm with the industry"}
The firm's demand curve is horizontal; the industry's demand curve slopes downward. Entry shifts the industry's supply curve, and the price changes for all firms.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for assumptions and equilibrium; Paper 2 can use a cost table with a market price; Paper 3 often asks for the short-run and long-run position with efficiency. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 combine calculation with description of the diagrams and evaluation of efficiency.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe the diagram*: state the axes, the horizontal demand curve at the market price, MC cutting it from below, and the ATC curve to show profit or loss. *Explain*: use the entry or exit mechanism. *Evaluate*: say that the model is a benchmark and compare it with real markets.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Perfect competition: many small firms, identical products, perfect information, free entry and exit.
- The firm is a price-taker: P = AR = MR; it produces where MC = P with MC rising.
- Short run: profit, normal profit or loss; shut down if price is below AVC.
- The firm's supply curve is MC above minimum AVC.
- Long run: entry or exit leaves normal profit with P = minimum ATC = MC.
- The long-run outcome is productively and allocatively efficient.
:::
