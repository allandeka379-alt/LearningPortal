## Introduction

A roadside seller of airtime in Mutare, a regional bakery chain and a national bank are all firms, but they differ enormously in size. Some firms stay small for ever; others grow, and many of the largest firms you buy from today began as a single shop or workshop. A firm's growth changes its costs, its power in the market and the lives of its workers and customers, so governments watch large mergers closely. This lesson explains the reasons for different sizes, the two routes to growth and the three kinds of integration, and then asks who gains and who loses.

You should know the economies and diseconomies of scale, the market structures and the profit motive. Multinational firms and small and medium enterprises are treated in separate lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Organic (internal) growth"}
Growth of a firm from its own resources: reinvesting profit, selling more of existing products, opening new branches or developing new products and markets, without taking over another firm.
:::

:::definition{term="Merger"}
Two firms agree to combine into one new or enlarged business, usually by exchanging shares. A **takeover** (acquisition) occurs when one firm buys enough shares of another to control it, whether the target's managers agree (friendly) or not (hostile).
:::

:::definition{term="Integration"}
The combination of firms through merger or takeover. It may be **horizontal** (same industry, same stage of production), **vertical** (different stages of the same industry: *backward* towards suppliers, *forward* towards customers) or **conglomerate** (firms in unrelated industries).
:::

## Why firms differ in size

1. **Size of the market.** A hairdresser in a small town serves a small market; a mobile network serves a whole country.
2. **Economies of scale.** Where unit costs fall over a wide range of output, firms must be large to compete; where they do not, small firms survive.
3. **Access to finance.** Large firms can raise money from shares and loans more cheaply than small ones.
4. **Nature of the product.** Personal services and goods made to order suit small firms; standardised goods suit mass production.
5. **Owners' aims.** Some owners prefer independence and a quiet life to growth.
6. **Barriers and regulation.** Licences and competition rules can limit or encourage growth.

## Organic growth

Organic growth is slower and cheaper, avoids the clash of two business cultures and keeps control with the owners, but it depends on the firm's own profits and on demand for its products. **Diversification**, selling new products or entering new markets, spreads risk: a farm that sells maize, tomatoes and tourist accommodation is less exposed to one bad season. A firm's organic growth can be measured by the change in sales.

:::worked-solution{title="Measuring organic growth"}
A fictional printing firm in Bulawayo had sales of US\$2.5 million in Year 1 and US\$3.1 million in Year 2, after opening a second branch.

1. Increase = 3.1 − 2.5 = US\$0.6 million.
2. Growth = 0.6 ÷ 2.5 × 100 = **24%**.
:::

## Mergers and takeovers (external growth)

External growth is faster and can give immediate market share, but it is costly and risky: the acquiring firm often pays a **premium** over the market price of the target's shares.

:::worked-solution{title="The cost of a takeover"}
A fictional company wants to buy all 5 million shares of a rival, which trade at US\$2.40. It offers a 30% premium.

1. Offer price = 2.40 × 1.30 = US\$3.12 a share.
2. Total cost = 3.12 × 5 million = US\$15.6 million.
3. Market value before the bid = 2.40 × 5 million = US\$12 million, so the premium is **US\$3.6 million**. The acquirer must earn this back through lower costs or higher revenue.
:::

## Types of integration

| Type | What it combines | Example (fictional) | Main motive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal | Firms at the same stage in the same industry | Two bakery chains merge | Economies of scale, greater market share, less competition |
| Vertical backward | A firm and its supplier | A bakery buys a flour mill | Secure supplies, control quality and input costs |
| Vertical forward | A firm and its customer or retailer | A farm cooperative buys supermarkets | Secure outlets, capture the retailer's margin |
| Conglomerate | Firms in unrelated industries | A transport firm buys a hotel | Spread risk, enter new markets |

:::worked-solution{title="Market shares after a horizontal merger"}
The fictional cement market of Zandia has five firms with market shares of 32%, 24%, 18%, 14% and 12%.

1. Two-firm concentration ratio before = 32 + 24 = **56%**.
2. The firms with 24% and 18% merge to form a firm with 42%. The shares are now 42%, 32%, 14% and 12%.
3. Two-firm concentration ratio after = 42 + 32 = **74%**. The number of firms falls from five to four, and the merged firm becomes the market leader.
:::

## Reasons for integration

- **Economies of scale:** spreading fixed costs, bulk buying, a single head office.
- **Market power:** fewer rivals, so a greater ability to set price (horizontal).
- **Security of supply or outlets** (vertical), and avoiding a supplier's mark-up.
- **Risk spreading** (conglomerate): losses in one industry can be offset by profit in another.
- **Access to new markets, brands or technology**, and cost savings from combining activities (synergies).

:::worked-solution{title="Cost savings from a merger"}
Firm X makes 200,000 units a year at an average cost of US\$12; Firm Y makes 100,000 units at US\$14. Once merged and sharing a factory, the combined firm makes 300,000 units at US\$11 each.

1. Cost before = 200,000 × 12 + 100,000 × 14 = US\$2.4 million + US\$1.4 million = US\$3.8 million.
2. Cost after = 300,000 × 11 = US\$3.3 million.
3. Saving = **US\$0.5 million** a year, or 13.2% of the earlier cost.
:::

## Consequences of integration

| Group | Possible gains | Possible losses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Firms | Lower unit costs; larger market share; spread risk | Cost of the deal; culture clash; diseconomies of scale; debt |
| Consumers | Lower prices if savings are passed on; wider choice from one supplier | Higher prices and less choice if competition falls; lower quality |
| Workers | Job security and promotion in a larger firm | Redundancies where jobs are duplicated; changed conditions |

:::example{title="Evaluating a horizontal merger"}
Suppose the Zandian cement merger above cuts costs by 13%. Consumers gain only if the firm passes savings on in lower prices, which depends on how much competition remains and on whether a competition authority attaches conditions. With the concentration ratio at 74%, the merged firm may raise prices instead. Workers at the two head offices may lose their jobs. The overall judgement depends on the size of the savings against the loss of competition, and on whether imports can discipline the firm.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing vertical and horizontal"}
Horizontal means the *same* stage of production (two bakeries). A bakery buying a flour mill is vertical, because the mill is at an earlier stage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming every merger brings cost savings"}
Combined firms may suffer diseconomies of scale, conflict between managers and a poor fit of systems. Savings are possible, not certain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'merger' and 'takeover' as the same thing"}
A merger is agreed between the firms; a takeover is one firm buying control of another, sometimes against its wishes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 tests the types of integration; Paper 2 data questions use market-share or cost data; Paper 3 essays ask whether growth benefits consumers or workers. Cambridge Paper 3 tests integration and concentration, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of the effects on stakeholders.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: define both and state the difference. *Explain*: give the motive and link it to costs or market power. *Analyse*: develop the chain from integration to price and output. *Evaluate*: weigh scale economies against loss of competition and conclude, referring to the market's conditions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Firms differ in size because of the market, economies of scale, finance, product type and owners' aims.
- Organic growth uses the firm's own resources; external growth is by merger or takeover, often at a premium.
- Horizontal integration is at the same stage; vertical is backward or forward; conglomerate is in unrelated industries.
- Motives include scale economies, market power, security of supply or outlets and risk spreading.
- Effects on consumers depend on whether cost savings outweigh the loss of competition; workers may lose jobs.
:::
