## Introduction

There are hundreds of hair salons in Harare and Bulawayo. They all cut hair, yet each is slightly different: one offers braiding, another is close to a taxi rank, a third has a reputation for service. Because of these differences each salon faces a downward-sloping demand curve, and each has a small amount of control over its price. Yet if salons make high profits, new ones open nearby. **Monopolistic competition** describes this mix of competition and monopoly, and it fits restaurants, small shops, tailors and many other local businesses.

You should know the MC = MR rule, supernormal and normal profit, and the structure of perfect competition. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monopolistic competition"}
A market structure in which there are many firms selling differentiated products, with easy entry and exit and no barriers, so each firm has some price-making power in the short run but earns only normal profit in the long run.
:::

:::definition{term="Product differentiation"}
Making a product different from its competitors in real or perceived ways (quality, design, location, service or branding), so that buyers do not regard it as a perfect substitute.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-price competition"}
Competing for customers by means other than a lower price: advertising, branding, quality, location, service, loyalty rewards and delivery.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Features"}
Many sellers; differentiated products; easy entry and exit (low sunk costs); some control over price, with a demand curve that is downward sloping and fairly elastic because there are many close substitutes; firms are small, so each acts independently and does not worry about the reaction of any single rival.
:::

## Short-run equilibrium

In the short run the firm behaves like a monopolist. It faces a downward-sloping demand curve, produces where MC = MR and reads the price from the demand curve. Take a fictional salon with demand $P = 34 - 2Q$ (Q = customers served per day, price in US\$), so $\text{MR} = 34 - 4Q$, a constant marginal cost of US\$10 and a fixed cost of US\$50 a day.

:::worked-solution{title="Short-run supernormal profit"}
1. MR $=$ MC: $34 - 4Q = 10$, so $Q = 6$.
2. Price $= 34 - 2 \times 6 =$ US\$22.
3. $\text{TR} = 22 \times 6 = 132$; $\text{TC} = 50 + 10 \times 6 = 110$; supernormal profit $=$ US\$22 a day.
4. ATC at 6 customers $= 110 \div 6 = 18.33$, below the price of 22.
:::

The same equations can give a loss if demand is weak or costs are high; in that case the firm continues in the short run if price covers average variable cost.

## Long-run equilibrium: the tangency solution

Supernormal profit attracts entrants, who offer similar services nearby. Each existing salon loses customers, so its demand curve shifts to the left and becomes more elastic (more close substitutes). Entry continues until supernormal profit is competed away. In the long run the demand curve (AR) just touches (is tangent to) the ATC curve at the output where MC = MR, so that price equals average total cost and each firm makes **normal profit** only.

:::worked-solution{title="Long-run normal profit"}
Entry shifts the demand curve to $P = 30 - 2Q$, so $\text{MR} = 30 - 4Q$.

1. MR $=$ MC: $30 - 4Q = 10$, so $Q = 5$ and price $= 30 - 10 =$ US\$20.
2. $\text{TR} = 20 \times 5 = 100$; $\text{TC} = 50 + 10 \times 5 = 100$; profit is zero (normal profit).
3. $\text{ATC} = 100 \div 5 = 20$, which equals the price: the demand curve touches the ATC curve at that output. At any other output the ATC would be above the demand curve.
4. Entry stops because there is no supernormal profit; exit would begin if demand fell further, because price would be below ATC.
:::

(In this example the demand curve moves in parallel. In reality, entry also makes demand flatter, which does not change the logic: the final position is a tangency.)

## Non-price competition and advertising

Because the product is differentiated, firms try to make demand less elastic and shift it to the right. They advertise, build brand loyalty, improve quality and customer service, and choose good locations. This competition raises costs (advertising is a cost that raises ATC), but it may also provide information and variety, and may lead to innovation. In the long run the extra cost is a reason why the price is not lower. If the advertising shifts demand to the right, supernormal profit may reappear temporarily until rivals respond with their own spending.

## Efficiency

1. **Allocative efficiency is not achieved:** in the example price (US\$20) is above marginal cost (US\$10).
2. **Productive efficiency is not achieved:** the firm produces where ATC is still falling, below the output at which ATC would be lowest. In the example ATC at 5 customers is US\$20, but it would be only US\$18.33 at 6 customers, so the firm could use its fixed resources more fully at a higher output. This gap is called **excess capacity**: the firm could serve more customers at a lower cost per customer, but the demand for each salon is too small to fill it.
3. **Variety:** consumers get a range of choices, which has value. Compared with perfect competition, prices are higher but the choice is wider.
4. **Dynamic effects:** some supernormal profits in the short run reward innovation and new ideas.

:::worked-solution{title="Measuring excess capacity in the example"}
1. At the long-run position the salon serves 5 customers with ATC of US\$20.
2. At 6 customers (the output chosen in the short run) ATC would be $(50 + 60) \div 6 = 18.33$.
3. At a higher output the ATC would be lower still: at 10 customers ATC $= (50 + 100) \div 10 =$ US\$15.
4. The salon therefore has unused capacity: its fixed costs are spread over fewer customers than it could handle.
:::

(In this simplified model marginal cost is constant, so ATC falls at all outputs. With rising marginal cost the ATC curve is U-shaped, and the firm produces to the left of its minimum.)

:::example{title="Evaluating monopolistic competition"}
Monopolistic competition gives consumers variety and encourages firms to improve service, and the lack of barriers keeps profits close to normal in the long run. However, the prices are higher than marginal cost, firms operate with unused capacity, and advertising raises costs. Whether the outcome is good for society depends on how much people value variety and on whether advertising is informative or merely persuasive. On balance the inefficiency is small compared with the potential loss in a monopoly, and the market's responsiveness to consumers is a strength.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that firms make normal profit in the short run"}
In the short run a firm can make supernormal profit or a loss. Normal profit is the long-run result.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing monopolistic competition with monopoly"}
There are many firms and free entry. The word "monopolistic" refers only to the firm's control over its own differentiated product.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting why demand shifts after entry"}
New firms take away some of each existing firm's customers, so the demand curve for each existing firm shifts left.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for features and examples; Paper 3 asks for the long-run position and efficiency. Cambridge Paper 3 tests profit and efficiency; Paper 4 has essays on whether monopolistic competition is efficient and on the role of advertising.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe*: list the features and give an example. *Explain*: say why entry removes supernormal profit. *Evaluate*: weigh variety against excess capacity and the cost of advertising.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Many firms, differentiated products and easy entry: each firm faces a downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand curve.
- Short run: MC = MR, with supernormal profit or loss.
- Long run: entry or exit shifts demand until it is tangent to ATC and firms earn normal profit.
- Non-price competition (advertising, branding, quality) is common and adds to cost.
- Price exceeds marginal cost and there is excess capacity, but consumers gain variety.
:::
