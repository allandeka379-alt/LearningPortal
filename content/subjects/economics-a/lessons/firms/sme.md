## Introduction

Walk through Mbare Musika in Harare, a market in Gweru or a row of workshops in Kwekwe and you will see how much of Zimbabwe's economic activity is carried out by very small businesses: tailors, welders, vegetable sellers, hair salons, small transport operators. Together with firms of middle size they are called **small and medium enterprises (SMEs)**. They matter because they employ many people, supply local needs and are often the first step from informal to formal business. They also face real obstacles, which is why governments and development agencies pay them so much attention.

You should know the difference between a large and a small firm, the sources of business finance and the idea of economies of scale. Large firms are treated in the lesson on growth and integration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small and medium enterprises (SMEs)"}
Businesses that are small or medium in size by criteria such as the number of employees, annual turnover or value of assets. The criteria differ from country to country and from one sector to another, so in an examination use the figures the question gives you. Do not assume a national definition.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal and formal sector"}
The **formal sector** consists of businesses that are registered, keep records and pay tax. The **informal sector** consists of businesses that are not registered or whose activity is not fully recorded; many are micro-businesses run by one person or a family.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Criteria used to define SMEs"}
Number of employees; annual turnover; value of capital or assets; sometimes the legal form. A firm is classified by the criteria that the government or the question uses, not by a universal rule.
:::

## Why small firms survive

Large firms enjoy economies of scale, yet small firms survive in large numbers for several reasons.

1. **Niche markets.** A small firm can serve a market too small for a large firm, such as repairing a particular brand of phone.
2. **Personal service.** Customers value a tailor, hairdresser or mechanic who knows them.
3. **Flexibility.** Owners decide quickly, change products and respond to local demand without layers of management.
4. **Low barriers to entry.** Little capital is needed to start selling vegetables or repairing shoes.
5. **Small markets and perishable goods.** Local delivery of fresh produce does not suit national chains.
6. **Owners' aims.** Some owners prefer independence and a manageable workload to growth.
7. **Supplying large firms.** A small firm may specialise as a sub-contractor to a larger firm.

## Problems facing SMEs

| Problem | Why it arises |
| --- | --- |
| Finance | Little collateral and short records make banks reluctant to lend; loans cost more; savings are small |
| Markets | Small customer base, limited marketing, competition from imports or large firms |
| Infrastructure | Unreliable electricity, water, transport and internet raise costs |
| Regulation | Registration, licences and tax rules can be costly and confusing for small owners |
| Management skills | Owners may lack training in accounting, marketing and planning |
| Diseconomies of small scale | Higher unit costs from buying in small amounts and spreading fixed costs over little output |

:::worked-solution{title="A small business: profit and the cost of a loan"}
A fictional tailor in Gweru earns US\$2,400 a month from sales. Monthly costs are cloth and thread US\$900, rent US\$250, electricity US\$80 and a helper's wages US\$300.

1. Total costs = 900 + 250 + 80 + 300 = US\$1,530.
2. Monthly profit = 2,400 − 1,530 = **US\$870**.
3. The tailor wants a loan of US\$5,000 at 18% interest a year. Interest = 0.18 × 5,000 = **US\$900 a year**, or US\$75 a month, which is 8.6% of the monthly profit.
4. The loan is affordable if it raises output enough to earn more than the interest; if sales do not grow, the interest comes straight out of profit.
:::

## Government involvement and support

Governments may help SMEs because of their contribution to employment and because their problems are real. Typical measures include:

- **Finance:** credit guarantees or special loan schemes, training in record keeping so that banks will lend.
- **Training and advice:** business skills courses and mentoring.
- **Infrastructure:** workshop space, market stalls and reliable services for business areas.
- **Simplified rules:** easier registration and a simple tax for small firms, to help them move from informal to formal.
- **Markets:** rules that let small firms bid for public contracts.

Support has costs: it uses taxpayers' money, can benefit those who are already better off, and may keep firms small if help ends when they grow. Whether it works depends on how well it is targeted and administered.

## Benefits of SMEs to society

- **Employment:** they employ many people, often those with few qualifications.
- **Local supply and competition:** they provide goods and services near where people live and challenge larger firms.
- **Enterprise and innovation:** they are a training ground for entrepreneurs and test new ideas.
- **Incomes and poverty reduction:** they give households incomes, especially women and young people.
- **Moving from informal to formal:** a growing SME that registers can pay tax, offer more secure jobs and borrow more cheaply.

:::worked-solution{title="SMEs and employment in a fictional town"}
The table is a fictional survey of businesses in the town of Zandia. The size groups are the survey's own, not a legal definition.

| Size group | Firms | Workers |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Micro (owner and family) | 420 | 840 |
| Small | 90 | 1,350 |
| Medium | 12 | 1,500 |
| Large | 3 | 2,310 |
| **Total** | **525** | **6,000** |

1. Workers in micro, small and medium firms = 840 + 1,350 + 1,500 = 3,690.
2. Share of all workers = 3,690 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = **61.5%**.
3. Average workers per small firm = 1,350 ÷ 90 = **15**. The three large firms employ an average of 2,310 ÷ 3 = 770 each.
4. Micro firms are 420 ÷ 525 = 80% of the firms but employ only 14% of the workers (840 ÷ 6,000), so most of them are very small.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating the role of SMEs"}
SMEs create many jobs, but a large number are of low productivity and low pay, and many fail within a few years because of weak finance and markets. A fair evaluation says SMEs are important for employment and for enterprise, that the best help is aimed at the firms with the potential to grow, and that the quality of jobs matters as well as their number. It also notes that data on informal activity is hard to collect, so claims about the size of the sector should be treated with care.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Quoting a fixed definition"}
There is no single definition of an SME. Answer using the criteria in the question, or say that the criteria vary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating informal as illegal"}
An informal business is one that is unregistered or unrecorded. It is not necessarily criminal, and many informal firms sell perfectly legal goods.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing without explaining"}
"They need finance" scores little. Explain *why*: lack of collateral and records make lenders reluctant, so loans are scarce and costly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for features and problems of SMEs; Paper 2 data questions give a table of firms and employment and ask for shares and averages; Paper 3 essays ask you to discuss the contribution of SMEs to development or the case for government support.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe*: give the criteria and features. *Explain*: give the reason and link it to finance, markets or costs. *Calculate*: shares and averages from the table. *Discuss/Evaluate*: set employment and enterprise benefits against low productivity and failure, and judge which kinds of support work best.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SMEs are defined by employees, turnover or assets; the criteria vary, so use those given.
- Small firms survive through niches, personal service, flexibility, low barriers and specialising for larger firms.
- Their main problems are finance, markets, infrastructure, regulation and management skills.
- Governments support them with finance, training, infrastructure and simpler rules, at a cost to taxpayers.
- SMEs provide jobs, incomes and enterprise and can help move activity from the informal to the formal sector.
:::
