## Introduction

Mobile phone services in Zimbabwe, commercial banking, soft drinks and brewing are dominated by a few large firms. When one network cuts its data bundle prices, the others notice at once and respond. Each firm's best decision depends on what its rivals do, and so decisions cannot be made as if rivals did not exist. This **interdependence** is the key feature of **oligopoly**, and it makes the behaviour of the market harder to predict than that of perfect competition or monopoly.

You should know the features of market structures and the MC = MR rule. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oligopoly"}
A market structure dominated by a small number of large firms, protected by barriers to entry, in which each firm's decisions on price and output depend on the likely reactions of its rivals.
:::

:::definition{term="Interdependence"}
The situation in which the outcome of a firm's decision on price, output or advertising depends on how its rivals react, so each firm must take the reactions of the others into account.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentration ratio"}
The percentage of total market sales (or output) accounted for by the largest $n$ firms.
:::

## Features

1. A few firms hold most of the market, so the concentration ratio is high.
2. Products may be identical (cement) or differentiated (cars, phones, soft drinks).
3. Barriers to entry are significant: economies of scale, brands, control of distribution, high capital cost.
4. Firms are interdependent and often engage in non-price competition.
5. Firms may collude (see the next lesson), but they often compete fiercely.

:::worked-solution{title="Calculating concentration ratios"}
The shares of ten firms in a fictional mobile-data market are 34%, 26%, 18%, 8%, 5%, 3%, 2%, 2%, 1% and 1%.

1. Two-firm ratio $= 34 + 26 = 60\%$.
2. Three-firm ratio $= 60 + 18 = 78\%$.
3. Four-firm ratio $= 78 + 8 = 86\%$.
4. The market is highly concentrated. The ratio does not show whether the leaders compete or collude.
:::

## The kinked demand curve and price rigidity

Prices in oligopoly often stay unchanged for long periods even when costs change. The kinked demand curve model is one explanation. It rests on an assumption about how rivals react:

1. If the firm **raises** its price, rivals do **not** follow, so the firm loses many customers to them: demand above the current price is **elastic**.
2. If the firm **cuts** its price, rivals **do** follow to avoid losing customers, so the firm gains little: demand below the current price is **less elastic**.

The demand curve therefore has a kink at the current price. The marginal revenue curve has a **gap**, a vertical section, directly below the kink.

:::worked-solution{title="The kink and the marginal revenue gap"}
A fictional firm sells 100 units a week at US\$10. If it raises the price to US\$11, it sells only 60 units. If it cuts the price to US\$9, it sells 115 units.

1. Above the kink: price rises 10%, quantity falls 40%, so PED $= -40 \div 10 = -4.0$ (elastic).
2. Below the kink: price falls 10%, quantity rises 15%, so PED $= 15 \div (-10) = -1.5$ (less elastic).
3. The upper demand line is $P = 12.5 - Q/40$ (through 100 units at US\$10 and 60 units at US\$11), so its marginal revenue is $12.5 - Q/20$, which equals US\$7.50 at 100 units.
4. The lower demand line is $P = 50/3 - Q/15$ (through 100 units at US\$10 and 115 units at US\$9), so its marginal revenue is $50/3 - 2Q/15$, which equals US\$3.33 at 100 units.
5. At 100 units, MR therefore jumps from US\$7.50 to US\$3.33. The MR curve has a gap between US\$3.33 and US\$7.50.
:::

If marginal cost lies anywhere in the gap, the profit-maximising output (100 units) and price (US\$10) do not change.

:::worked-solution{title="A rise in costs that does not change the price"}
Marginal cost is US\$4 at 100 units. It then rises to US\$7 because of higher fuel prices.

1. Both US\$4 and US\$7 lie between the MR values of US\$3.33 and US\$7.50.
2. MC still cuts the MR curve in the vertical gap, so the firm still chooses 100 units at US\$10.
3. If MC rose above US\$7.50, the price would have to rise.
:::

**Limitations.** The model does not explain how the price was set in the first place (the kink starts at the existing price), and evidence for the assumed reactions is mixed. Firms may also move prices together when all face the same cost increase, which the model does not explain.

## Competition and cooperation

**Price competition** can take the form of price wars or price cuts, which may reduce profit for all. Because it is dangerous, oligopolists often avoid it.

**Non-price competition** is more common: branding, advertising, loyalty programmes, product quality and features, after-sales service, and variety. It builds loyalty without starting a price war.

**Price leadership.** A dominant firm sets the price and the others follow, which avoids price competition without explicit agreement. The leader may be the largest or the most efficient firm.

**Limit pricing.** Firms set a price low enough to discourage new entrants, because entry would reduce the profit of all. The price is above the competitive level but below the monopoly level.

**Predatory pricing.** A firm temporarily sets a price below cost to drive out a rival or deter entry, and raises it later. It is costly to the firm using it, it is hard to prove and it may be illegal under competition law.

## Efficiency of oligopoly

Oligopoly can be inefficient: prices usually exceed marginal cost, supernormal profit can last, and collusion harms consumers. But oligopolists may enjoy economies of scale, and supernormal profit may fund innovation and research. Competition between rival brands can be intense, and a market that is contestable restrains behaviour. Whether oligopoly benefits society depends on whether firms compete or collude and on how easily entry is possible.

:::example{title="Evaluating non-price competition"}
Advertising by competing soft-drink firms increases costs and may not raise consumption of soft drinks overall: it moves customers between brands. Consumers may also gain from information, variety and product improvement. The answer depends on whether the advertising is informative or persuasive and on whether it deters entry by raising the spending that a new firm must match.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the firm sets price independently"}
In oligopoly firms must anticipate rivals' reactions. This interdependence is the defining feature.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the kinked demand elasticities"}
Demand is elastic above the kink (rivals do not follow a price rise) and less elastic below it (rivals follow a price cut).
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the concentration ratio as proof of collusion"}
A high ratio shows that the market is concentrated, not that firms agree to fix prices.
:::

*Explain price rigidity*: state the assumption about rivals' reactions, the elasticity on each side of the kink and the gap in MR. *Assess*: weigh the evidence for and against the model. *Discuss*: balance the benefits of competition between a few firms against the risk of higher prices.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Oligopoly is a market with few large firms, high barriers to entry and interdependence.
- An $n$-firm concentration ratio adds the shares of the largest $n$ firms.
- Kinked demand: elastic above the kink, less elastic below, with a gap in MR, so price is stable while MC lies in the gap.
- Firms use non-price competition, price leadership, limit pricing and sometimes predatory pricing.
- Efficiency is uncertain: higher prices and profit against economies of scale and innovation.
:::
