## Introduction

A bumper maize harvest should make farmers better off, yet a glut often lowers the income of the whole farming community. A transport operator who raises fares may find that its daily takings go up, or down, depending on how passengers respond. Both puzzles are solved by the link between **price elasticity of demand** and **total revenue**.

This lesson assumes you can calculate PED (see the previous lesson). Here we ask what PED implies for the money that consumers spend and firms receive, how PED changes as you move down a straight-line demand curve, and why the answer matters for decisions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Total revenue (TR)"}
The total amount a firm receives from selling its output: price multiplied by quantity sold. From the consumer's side the same figure is **total expenditure** on the good.
:::

:::keyfact{title="PED and total revenue"}
$$\text{TR} = P \times Q$$

| Demand is | A price **rise** makes TR | A price **fall** makes TR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastic (size of PED above 1) | **fall** | **rise** |
| Unitary (PED = −1) | stay the same | stay the same |
| Inelastic (size of PED below 1) | **rise** | **fall** |
:::

The reasoning is simple. Revenue is price times quantity. When price rises, the price effect raises revenue and the quantity effect lowers it. If demand is inelastic, quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rose, so the price effect wins and revenue rises. If demand is elastic, quantity falls by a larger percentage, so the quantity effect wins and revenue falls.

## Worked calculations

:::worked-solution{title="Inelastic demand: raising the price"}
A fictional shop in Masvingo sells 1,000 packets of salt a week at US\$2.00. PED is −0.6. The owner raises the price by 20% to US\$2.40.

1. % change in quantity $= -0.6 \times 20 = -12\%$, so the new quantity is $1000 \times 0.88 = 880$ packets.
2. Old revenue $= 2.00 \times 1000 = 2{,}000$.
3. New revenue $= 2.40 \times 880 = 2{,}112$.
4. Revenue rises by $112 \div 2000 \times 100 = 5.6\%$. Because demand is inelastic, the rise in price more than offset the fall in sales.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Elastic demand: cutting the price"}
A fictional taxi firm in Harare carries 300 passengers a day at US\$5.00. PED is −2.0. It cuts the fare by 20% to US\$4.00.

1. % change in quantity $= -2.0 \times -20 = +40\%$, so passengers become $300 \times 1.4 = 420$.
2. Old revenue $= 5.00 \times 300 = 1{,}500$.
3. New revenue $= 4.00 \times 420 = 1{,}680$.
4. Revenue rises by 12%. Because demand is elastic, the fall in price was more than offset by the rise in sales.
:::

Revenue is not profit. A firm must also consider the extra costs of producing more. A fare cut that raises revenue by US\$180 is only worthwhile if the cost of carrying 120 extra passengers is less than that.

## PED along a straight-line demand curve

On a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant, but PED is not. In percentage terms a US\$2 change is a huge change when the price is US\$4 and a tiny one when it is US\$18, so elasticity falls steadily as you move down the curve. The schedule below is for a fictional market with demand $P = 20 - 0.1Q$ (P in US\$, Q in units per week). PED at each point is $-P \div (20 - P)$.

| Price (US\$) | Quantity (units) | Total revenue (US\$) | PED at that price |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 18 | 20 | 360 | −9 |
| 16 | 40 | 640 | −4 |
| 14 | 60 | 840 | −2.33 |
| 12 | 80 | 960 | −1.50 |
| 10 | 100 | 1,000 | −1 |
| 8 | 120 | 960 | −0.67 |
| 6 | 140 | 840 | −0.43 |
| 4 | 160 | 640 | −0.25 |
| 2 | 180 | 360 | −0.11 |

Reading the schedule:

1. In the **upper half** (prices above US\$10) demand is elastic. A price cut raises revenue: from US\$16 to US\$14 revenue climbs from 640 to 840.
2. At the **mid-point** (US\$10) PED is exactly −1, and revenue is at its maximum, US\$1,000.
3. In the **lower half** (below US\$10) demand is inelastic. A price cut now lowers revenue: from US\$8 to US\$6 it falls from 960 to 840.

Two points follow. A firm facing this demand and no costs would charge the price at the mid-point to maximise revenue, but real firms maximise profit and so also look at costs (a topic for A Level cost theory). And the same demand curve is elastic in one range and inelastic in another, so we describe elasticity *at a price* or *over a range* and not for a whole straight line.

:::keyfact{title="Straight-line demand"}
At the price halfway up the price axis, PED = −1. Above it demand is elastic; below it demand is inelastic. A straight-line curve is therefore *not* a curve of constant elasticity.
:::

## Implications for firms, households and governments

**Firms.** A firm with inelastic demand (a sole supplier of a necessity) can raise revenue by raising its price, though it may attract regulators and new entrants. A firm with elastic demand (one among many competing bakeries) will find that a price rise loses customers and revenue; it may gain by cutting prices, discounts and special offers. Selling to different groups at different prices is possible when their elasticities differ, as with student and business fares.

**Households.** The expenditure rule shows how a price rise changes spending: on an inelastic good, households spend *more* on it and so have less left for other things.

**Farmers.** Demand for staple foods is inelastic. A good harvest shifts supply to the right and lowers the price, and because demand is inelastic the quantity sold rises by a smaller percentage than the price falls, so farm revenue *falls*. This is one reason governments and farmers' organisations favour storage schemes, which are examined in the lesson on buffer stocks.

**Governments.** Indirect taxes raise more revenue, and lower consumption less, on goods with inelastic demand such as fuel, tobacco and alcohol. This is why such goods are commonly taxed, though the tax falls heavily on poorer households when the good is a necessity. Subsidies on goods with inelastic demand mostly benefit consumers through a lower price rather than raising consumption much.

:::example{title="Evaluating a price cut"}
A fictional bus company in Mutare is thinking of cutting its fare by 10% to raise revenue. Evidence is needed on PED: if it is elastic (say −1.8) revenue should rise; if it is inelastic (say −0.5) revenue will fall. Even if revenue rises, profit may not, because carrying more passengers costs more, and rival operators may match the cut. The time period matters too, since long-run demand is more elastic than short-run. The decision therefore needs an estimate of PED, a view of costs and an assessment of the likely reaction of competitors.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a price cut always raises revenue"}
A price cut raises revenue only if demand is elastic. On an inelastic range it lowers revenue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating revenue as profit"}
Total revenue ignores costs. A higher revenue does not mean higher profit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a straight-line demand curve has the same PED everywhere"}
Its slope is constant but PED falls steadily from the top to the bottom. PED is −1 at the mid-point only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the farm income argument"}
It is not that a bumper harvest lowers the *price per tonne* only; the point is that on inelastic demand the fall in price outweighs the rise in quantity, so total revenue falls.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Papers and calculations"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 often ask for the change in total revenue after a price change, given a PED. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the rule in multiple choice and Paper 2 asks you to explain why revenue changes. Always compute both old and new revenue and quote the figures.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain* why revenue falls after a price rise: say demand is elastic and quantity falls by a larger percentage. *Analyse* the effect on a farmer: chain from a good harvest to supply, price, inelastic demand and revenue. *Evaluate* a pricing decision: weigh the revenue effect against costs, competitors' reactions and the time period.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- TR = P × Q; for a price rise, TR rises if demand is inelastic and falls if it is elastic; the reverse applies to a price fall.
- At unitary elasticity a price change leaves TR unchanged, and TR is at its maximum where PED = −1.
- Along a straight-line demand curve PED is elastic above the mid-point, −1 at it and inelastic below it.
- Firms cannot judge a price change from revenue alone: costs, competitors and time also matter.
- Inelastic demand explains falling farm incomes after a good harvest and why governments tax fuel and tobacco.
:::
