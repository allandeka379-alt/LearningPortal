## Introduction

As a country grows richer, households do not simply buy more of everything. They buy more cars, restaurant meals and mobile data, but they may buy less second-hand clothing and fewer of the cheapest foods. When a rival bus company raises its fares, your own company may see its passenger numbers rise, while a rise in the price of petrol may reduce the sales of large cars. These patterns are measured by two further elasticities: **income elasticity of demand (YED)**, which links demand to consumers' incomes, and **cross elasticity of demand (XED)**, which links the demand for one good to the price of *another*.

You need to know how to calculate a percentage change and the meaning of normal and inferior goods, substitutes and complements. PED was covered in the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Income elasticity of demand (YED)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in consumers' income, ceteris paribus.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross elasticity of demand (XED)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of one good (A) to a change in the price of another good (B), ceteris paribus.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The formulae"}
$$\text{YED} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in income}}$$

$$\text{XED} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded of good A}}{\%\ \text{change in price of good B}}$$

For both, the percentage change uses the original value as the base. Unlike PED, the **sign matters**: it carries the meaning.
:::

## Interpreting income elasticity

| YED | Type of good | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negative (below 0) | Inferior good | Demand falls as income rises |
| Between 0 and 1 | Normal good, income inelastic (a necessity) | Demand rises, but by a smaller percentage than income |
| Above 1 | Normal good, income elastic (a luxury) | Demand rises by a larger percentage than income |

Demand for a **necessity** such as maize meal rises slowly with income: a richer household does not eat many times more sadza. Demand for a **luxury** such as foreign holidays rises faster than income. An **inferior good** is one for which demand falls when incomes rise, because consumers switch to better alternatives; whether a good is inferior depends on the country and the period, not on the quality of the good. For example, a cheap staple food may be inferior in a middle-income economy yet normal for very poor households.

:::worked-solution{title="A luxury good"}
Average household income in a fictional town rises from US\$400 to US\$480 a month. Monthly purchases of meat per household rise from 50 to 65 portions.

1. % change in income $= (480 - 400) \div 400 \times 100 = +20\%$.
2. % change in quantity $= (65 - 50) \div 50 \times 100 = +30\%$.
3. $\text{YED} = 30 \div 20 = +1.5$.
4. YED is positive and above 1: meat is a normal good with income-elastic demand (a luxury in this town).
:::

:::worked-solution{title="An inferior good"}
Household income rises from US\$600 to US\$690. Purchases of second-hand clothing fall from 200 to 176 items per month.

1. % change in income $= (690 - 600) \div 600 \times 100 = +15\%$.
2. % change in quantity $= (176 - 200) \div 200 \times 100 = -12\%$.
3. $\text{YED} = -12 \div 15 = -0.8$.
4. The negative sign shows that second-hand clothing is an inferior good for these households.
:::

## Interpreting cross elasticity

| XED | Relationship | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive | Substitutes | A rise in the price of B increases demand for A |
| Negative | Complements | A rise in the price of B decreases demand for A |
| Zero or close to zero | Unrelated goods | The price of B has no effect on demand for A |

The *size* shows the closeness of the relationship: +3.0 means very close substitutes (two brands of the same cooking oil), while +0.2 means weak substitutes. A larger negative value means a stronger complementary link (cars and petrol).

:::worked-solution{title="Substitutes"}
A fictional minibus company raises its fare from US\$1.00 to US\$1.20. A taxi firm on the same route sees daily trips rise from 300 to 345.

1. % change in price of B (minibus) $= +20\%$.
2. % change in quantity of A (taxi) $= (345 - 300) \div 300 \times 100 = +15\%$.
3. $\text{XED} = 15 \div 20 = +0.75$.
4. The positive sign confirms that taxis and minibuses are substitutes; the value shows only a moderate link.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Complements"}
The price of sugar rises from US\$2.00 to US\$2.40 a bag. Sales of tea fall from 800 to 720 packets a week.

1. % change in price of sugar $= +20\%$.
2. % change in quantity of tea $= (720 - 800) \div 800 \times 100 = -10\%$.
3. $\text{XED} = -10 \div 20 = -0.5$.
4. The negative sign shows that tea and sugar are complements.
:::

## Factors affecting each elasticity

**YED** depends on whether the good is a necessity or a luxury, the level of income (a good can be a luxury at low incomes and a necessity at high incomes), how many alternatives are available and the stage of economic development. **XED** depends on how closely the goods are related: how similar they are for substitutes, how necessary they are to each other for complements, and how the market is defined (two brands against two types of goods).

## Why firms and governments use them

**Forecasting demand.** If YED for restaurant meals is +1.5 and incomes are expected to rise by 4% next year, demand should rise by about $1.5 \times 4 = 6\%$, other things being equal. A firm selling a good with a negative YED should expect shrinking sales as the economy grows and should diversify.

**Product mix.** Firms in a growing economy tend to expand into goods with high positive YED and reduce reliance on inferior goods. Retailers decide which lines to stock as incomes change.

**Pricing and competition.** A firm with a close substitute produced by a rival (high positive XED) knows that its price changes strongly affect rivals' sales, and it must expect a reaction. Firms producing complements, such as printers and ink cartridges, consider the pricing of both together. Competition authorities consider high cross elasticity when judging whether two firms are in the same market.

**Governments and development.** In a developing economy where YED for food is low, agriculture declines as a share of national income as incomes rise while manufacturing and services grow; this helps to explain structural change and why prices of primary products have tended to rise slowly. Governments forecast tax revenue from goods with high YED (receipts rise faster than incomes) and plan public transport and housing with an eye on XED.

:::example{title="Evaluating the use of YED"}
A fictional supermarket chain in Bulawayo plans to expand its range of imported cheeses because YED is estimated at +2.0 and the economy is expected to grow. An evaluation would point out that the estimate may be unreliable; that growth may be concentrated among a few richer households, so national averages mislead; that a fall in incomes would hit sales of luxuries hard; and that import costs and the exchange rate affect profit as much as demand does. YED is a guide to the direction and size of demand but should be combined with other evidence.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards define and calculate YED and XED, interpret sign and size, and expect applications for firms and governments. Cambridge examiners often combine them with PED in one data-response question, asking which elasticity best explains a change in sales.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the sign"}
For YED and XED the sign is the main information. A value of −0.8 and +0.8 have opposite meanings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling any low-quality good inferior"}
A good is inferior only if demand *falls* as income rises (negative YED). A cheap good with positive YED is a normal good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong price in XED"}
XED divides the change in quantity of good A by the change in the price of good **B**, not of A.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a necessity with an inferior good"}
A necessity has positive YED below 1; demand still rises with income. Only a negative YED shows an inferior good.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for calculations and classification; Paper 2 data-response parts ask for a YED or XED calculation followed by an interpretation. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test both in multiple choice and explain-and-apply questions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: show both percentage changes and state the sign. *Interpret*: name the type of good or relationship, with the sign and size as evidence. *Explain why a firm would want to know*: link to forecasting sales, planning output and choosing the product range. *Assess*: mention reliability of estimates and the effect of other influences.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in income; XED = % change in quantity of A ÷ % change in price of B.
- Negative YED: inferior good; between 0 and 1: necessity; above 1: luxury.
- Positive XED: substitutes; negative XED: complements; near zero: unrelated goods.
- The sign carries the meaning; the size shows how strong the relationship is.
- Firms use YED to forecast sales and plan their product range; XED guides pricing and competition decisions.
:::
