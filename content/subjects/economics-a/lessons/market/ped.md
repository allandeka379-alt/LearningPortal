## Introduction

The law of demand tells you that a higher price lowers the quantity demanded. It does not tell you *by how much*. A bakery in Gweru that raises the price of a loaf by 10% needs to know whether it will lose 2% of its customers or 20%. A government that taxes cigarettes needs to know whether smokers will cut back or simply pay more. **Price elasticity of demand (PED)** answers this: it measures how responsive the quantity demanded is to a change in price.

You should already know the demand curve, the difference between a movement along it and a shift of it, and how to work out a percentage change. The next lesson, on PED and total revenue, builds directly on this one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price elasticity of demand (PED)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity demanded of a good to a change in its own price, ceteris paribus. It is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The formula"}
$$\text{PED} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity demanded}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

Percentage change = (new value − original value) ÷ original value × 100. The base is always the **original** value.
:::

:::definition{term="Elastic and inelastic demand"}
Demand is **elastic** when the size of PED is greater than 1: quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price. It is **inelastic** when the size of PED is between 0 and 1: quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price.
:::

## Calculating PED

Because the demand curve slopes downward, price and quantity move in opposite directions, so PED is normally **negative**. Many textbooks and examiners ignore the minus sign when comparing sizes ("PED is 0.6, so demand is inelastic"), but it is safest to write the sign and to say which you are using. A question will usually tell you whether to include the sign.

:::worked-solution{title="Bread: a price rise"}
A fictional bakery in Gweru raises the price of a loaf from US\$1.00 to US\$1.25. Weekly sales fall from 800 to 680 loaves. Calculate PED.

1. % change in price $= (1.25 - 1.00) \div 1.00 \times 100 = +25\%$.
2. % change in quantity demanded $= (680 - 800) \div 800 \times 100 = -15\%$.
3. $\text{PED} = -15 \div 25 = -0.6$.
4. The size, 0.6, is less than 1, so demand for bread is **inelastic**: a 25% price rise lowered sales by only 15%.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Minibus fares: a price fall"}
A fictional minibus operator cuts its fare from US\$2.00 to US\$1.60. Daily passengers rise from 1,000 to 1,300. Calculate PED.

1. % change in price $= (1.60 - 2.00) \div 2.00 \times 100 = -20\%$.
2. % change in quantity $= (1300 - 1000) \div 1000 \times 100 = +30\%$.
3. $\text{PED} = +30 \div (-20) = -1.5$.
4. The size, 1.5, exceeds 1, so demand is **elastic**: the fare cut raised passenger numbers by a larger percentage than the price fell.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Predicting the effect of a price change"}
A fictional supermarket in Mutare knows that PED for cooking oil is −0.4. It plans to raise the price by 15%. By how much will quantity demanded change, and what happens to sales of 2,500 bottles a week?

1. Rearranging: % change in quantity $= \text{PED} \times$ % change in price $= -0.4 \times 15 = -6\%$.
2. New quantity $= 2500 \times (1 - 0.06) = 2350$ bottles.
:::

## The range of values

| Size of PED | Description | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Quantity demanded does not change at all when price changes (a vertical demand curve) |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price |
| Exactly 1 | Unitary elastic | Quantity changes by the same percentage as price |
| Between 1 and infinity | Elastic | Quantity changes by a larger percentage than price |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Any price rise reduces quantity demanded to zero (a horizontal demand curve) |

The extremes are limiting cases that help to describe curves, not realistic descriptions of whole markets. A horizontal demand curve is the demand facing one small farmer who sells at the going market price; a vertical one might describe a life-saving medicine for a patient with no alternative. Do not confuse the *slope* of a curve with its elasticity: on a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant but PED changes from point to point, as the next lesson shows.

## What determines PED?

1. **Availability and closeness of substitutes.** The more and the closer the substitutes, the more elastic demand. If the price of one brand of cooking oil rises, shoppers switch to another, so demand for a single brand is elastic; demand for cooking oil in general is much less so.
2. **Necessity or luxury.** Necessities such as salt, basic medicines and maize meal have inelastic demand; luxuries such as holidays at Victoria Falls have elastic demand.
3. **Proportion of income spent.** A 20% rise in the price of matches matters little to a household budget, so demand is inelastic; a 20% rise in rent matters a great deal, so households respond more.
4. **Time.** Demand is more elastic in the long run, because people have time to find alternatives. If fuel prices rise, commuters cannot change at once, but within a few years they may move closer to work, share transport or buy more efficient vehicles.
5. **Habit and addiction.** Cigarettes and alcohol have inelastic demand among regular users.
6. **Breadth of definition of the good.** Demand for "food" is inelastic; demand for "tomatoes from one stall" is elastic.

:::example{title="Applying the determinants"}
Compare demand for (a) a particular brand of bread sold in a shopping centre in Bulawayo and (b) bread in general. For the brand, close substitutes (other bakers' bread) are readily available and a price rise sends customers elsewhere: demand is likely to be elastic. For bread in general there is no close substitute that serves the same purpose, it takes a small part of the budget and it is a staple: demand is likely to be inelastic. This shows why *how a good is defined* matters.
:::

## Point and arc elasticity

The method above uses the original value as the base. A change from 400 to 340 gives −15%, but a change from 340 back to 400 gives +17.6%, so the answer depends on the direction of the change. The **arc** (mid-point) method avoids this by dividing each change by the average of the two values. **Point elasticity** measures PED at one point on a curve using the slope of the curve there. Both appear in the ZIMSEC checklist of terms; the examination at either board expects the simple percentage-change method unless the question says otherwise.

:::worked-solution{title="The same data by the arc method"}
Price rises from US\$5.00 to US\$6.00 and quantity falls from 400 to 340.

1. Simple method: $+20\%$ and $-15\%$ give $\text{PED} = -0.75$.
2. Arc method: % change in price $= 1 \div 5.5 = 18.18\%$; % change in quantity $= -60 \div 370 = -16.22\%$; $\text{PED} = -0.89$.
3. Both show inelastic demand; the values differ because the bases differ.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test PED by the simple percentage-change method, its interpretation and its determinants. ZIMSEC lists point and arc elasticity among its terms; Cambridge does not print formulae and expects you to know the percentage-change formula.
:::

## Why PED matters

Firms use PED to decide whether to raise or lower prices (the effect on revenue is the subject of the next lesson). Governments use it to forecast how much an indirect tax will reduce consumption or raise revenue. Farmers' associations use it to understand why a bumper harvest can reduce farm incomes. Economists use it to judge how large the price changes caused by shifts in supply will be.

:::example{title="Evaluating an estimate of PED"}
A report says that PED for sugar in a fictional town is −0.3. Is the figure reliable? A fair evaluation notes that it depends on the period studied (long-run PED is likely to be larger), on whether other influences such as incomes and the prices of substitutes stayed constant, on the size of the price change (an elasticity measured for small changes may not hold for large ones) and on the quality of the data. It is a guide to likely responses, not an exact law.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the new value in the denominator"}
Calculating the percentage change as change ÷ new value gives a smaller answer in a rise and a larger one in a fall. The base is the original value.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing the wrong way round"}
PED is percentage change in **quantity** divided by percentage change in **price**. Inverting it gives 1.67 instead of 0.6.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equating steep with inelastic"}
A steep demand curve drawn on a particular scale is not always inelastic, because PED depends on percentage changes. Only a vertical line is perfectly inelastic everywhere.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the sign or contradicting the description"}
−2 is elastic and −0.2 is inelastic. Saying "PED is −0.2, so demand is elastic" confuses the sign with the size.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for short calculations and definitions; Paper 2 data-response questions usually include a PED calculation worth 2-3 marks; Paper 3 essays ask you to discuss the usefulness of PED to firms or governments. Cambridge Paper 1 tests calculation and interpretation in multiple choice; Paper 2 tests the determinants and uses of PED in data-response and essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: percentage change in quantity demanded ÷ percentage change in price. *Calculate*: show both percentage changes, then divide, and state whether demand is elastic or inelastic. *Explain*: give the reason and link it to substitutes, time or share of income. *Assess*: weigh the determinants, noting that more than one applies and that PED changes over time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; the base is the original value.
- It is normally negative; the size shows elastic (above 1), unitary (1) or inelastic (below 1) demand.
- Perfectly inelastic demand is a vertical line (PED = 0); perfectly elastic is a horizontal line.
- Substitutes, necessity, share of income, time and habit determine PED; demand is more elastic in the long run.
- Arc and point methods exist (ZIMSEC checklist); the simple percentage-change method is what examiners expect.
:::
