## Introduction

No minister decides how many loaves Bulawayo bakeries bake each morning, or how many bus drivers Harare needs. Millions of choices are co-ordinated by something much simpler: prices. When wheat becomes scarcer its price rises and bakers use it more carefully; when a profession pays more, more people train for it. The **price mechanism** is the system by which prices, set by the interaction of demand and supply, allocate resources in a market economy.

You should know how equilibrium is reached and how shifts in demand and supply change price and quantity. This lesson shows what prices *do* and how a change in one market spreads to others.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price mechanism"}
The way in which changes in prices, resulting from the interaction of demand and supply, co-ordinate the decisions of consumers, producers and resource owners, so that resources are allocated without central direction.
:::

:::definition{term="Derived demand"}
Demand for a good or factor that arises from the demand for something else that it helps to produce: for example demand for bricks is derived from demand for houses.
:::

:::definition{term="Joint supply"}
A situation in which the production of one good necessarily produces another: beef and hides are in joint supply.
:::

## The three functions of prices

**1. Signalling (transmitting preferences).** Prices carry information. A rise in the price of a good tells producers that consumers want more of it and tells consumers that it has become scarcer. When more households want solar lights, rising prices signal to firms that profits are available. Falling prices signal the opposite. Prices summarise information that no individual could collect alone.

**2. Rationing.** When a good is scarce relative to demand, a higher price discourages some buyers, reducing the quantity demanded until it equals the quantity available. Those with the greatest willingness and ability to pay obtain the good. The price mechanism rations without queues, tickets or rules, though it favours those with more income.

**3. Incentives (rewarding).** Higher prices raise the profit of producers and therefore give them an incentive to supply more; higher wages give workers an incentive to supply more labour or move to a better-paid sector; lower prices give consumers an incentive to buy more. Prices thus reward efficiency and risk-taking.

:::example{title="The three functions at work"}
After a poor maize harvest in a fictional region, the price of maize meal rises. It **signals** that maize is scarce. It **rations** the limited supply, since poorer households cut their purchases. It **provides an incentive** for farmers to plant more maize next season and for traders to import. All three functions operate through the same price change.
:::

## Limitations

Prices are not perfect signals. Rationing by price may leave poor households unable to afford essentials. Prices may not reflect external costs such as pollution. Where firms hold market power, prices may signal the wrong things. Information may be poor and prices slow to adjust. These criticisms lead to government intervention, studied later; you should be able to state both the strengths and weaknesses of the mechanism.

## Linked markets

Markets do not operate in isolation. There are four common links.

**Joint demand (complements).** Goods demanded together: cars and fuel, printers and ink. If the price of one rises, the quantity demanded of the other falls. A fall in demand for cars lowers demand for tyres and fuel.

**Alternative demand (substitutes).** Goods that can replace each other: maize meal and sorghum meal, minibuses and taxis. A rise in the price of one raises demand for the other.

**Derived demand.** Demand for factors of production and intermediate goods depends on the demand for the final product. A boom in housing raises demand for bricklayers, cement and timber.

**Joint supply.** A rise in demand for beef raises its price and encourages farmers to rear and slaughter more cattle, which also increases the supply of hides, so the price of hides falls. A rise in the supply of one by-product follows from the demand for the other.

## A worked chain: maize meal and sorghum meal

A poor harvest reduces the supply of maize meal. Use the market from the previous lesson: equilibrium price rises from US\$6 to US\$7 (a rise of 16.7%) and quantity falls from 75 to 60 thousand bags. Consumers now look for substitutes. Sorghum meal has $Q_d = 300 - 25P$ and $Q_s = 25P - 50$ (thousand bags).

:::worked-solution{title="Spreading the shock to a related market"}
1. Before the maize shortage, sorghum equilibrium: $300 - 25P = 25P - 50$, so $350 = 50P$, $P = 7$ and $Q = 125$.
2. Households switch to sorghum, raising demand by 25 thousand bags at every price: $Q_d = 325 - 25P$.
3. New equilibrium: $325 - 25P = 25P - 50$, so $375 = 50P$, $P = 7.50$ and $Q = 137.5$.
4. The price of sorghum meal rises from US\$7.00 to US\$7.50 (by 7.1%) and the quantity traded rises from 125 to 137.5 thousand bags.
5. At the old price of US\$7, demand for sorghum rose from 125 to 150 thousand bags, an increase of 20%. With maize meal's price up by 16.7%, the cross elasticity is $20 \div 16.7 = +1.2$: strongly substitutable.
:::

The chain continues. Higher sorghum prices raise the profit of sorghum farmers (a signal and an incentive), more land is planted to sorghum, and demand for sorghum seed and labour (derived demand) rises. In the maize market, the higher price encourages more planting next season, which shifts supply back to the right and brings the price down again.

:::worked-solution{title="Joint supply: beef and hides"}
Demand for beef rises in a fictional country.
1. The price of beef rises and farmers supply more beef cattle.
2. More cattle slaughtered means more hides come to the market: the supply of hides shifts to the right.
3. With demand for hides unchanged, the price of hides falls and the quantity rises.
4. Leather goods makers face lower costs, so the supply of leather bags and shoes may rise.
:::

## Evaluation

The price mechanism is efficient at conveying information and allocating resources quickly, without requiring planners to know everyone's preferences. However, it can leave out those who cannot pay, ignore external costs, require time to adjust (farmers cannot respond in the same season) and be distorted by market power or controls. A balanced answer judges how well the mechanism works for the *particular market*: a market for mobile phones with many sellers works much better than a market for a life-saving drug with one supplier.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC names the functions as signalling, rationing and rewarding; Cambridge refers to transmitting preferences, rationing and incentives. They are the same ideas. Both boards test linked markets with chains of reasoning.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing functions without applying them"}
Naming 'signalling, rationing, incentives' earns little; each needs an application to a specific price change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing derived demand with joint demand"}
Joint demand is for complements consumed together; derived demand is for a factor because of demand for the final good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction wrong in joint supply"}
When demand for beef rises, the supply of hides rises (price of hides falls). It does not mean the price of hides rises.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stopping the chain too early"}
Analysis requires you to follow the effect: first market, then the linked market, then the response in the first.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the functions or a linked-market consequence; Paper 3 essays ask you to discuss how well the price mechanism allocates resources. Cambridge Paper 1 tests joint demand and supply; Paper 2 uses chains of reasoning in data-response and essay answers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain how the price mechanism allocates resources*: name signalling, rationing and incentives and apply each. *Analyse the effects on a related market*: identify the link (substitute, complement, derived or joint) and the shift it causes. *Evaluate*: judge the mechanism by its strengths and its limits for the market in question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Prices signal scarcity and preferences, ration scarce goods and provide incentives to produce and consume.
- A change in one market spreads through joint demand, alternative demand, derived demand and joint supply.
- Complements move in the opposite direction in price and demand; substitutes in the same direction.
- Joint supply: more demand for beef raises the supply of hides and lowers their price.
- The mechanism is fast and decentralised but ignores those who cannot pay and external costs.
:::
