## Introduction

When the price of tomatoes rises at a Mutare market, farmers with spare land plant more, traders bring in extra crates and many growers bring forward the picking. When a drought or a rise in the cost of fertiliser makes growing more expensive, they supply less at any price. **Supply** is the other half of the market story, and as with demand, the key skill is distinguishing a **movement along** the supply curve from a **shift** of the curve.

This lesson is for both ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You need to know the lesson on demand and the idea of opportunity cost. Elasticity of supply and cost curves have their own lessons, and equilibrium follows after them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Supply"}
The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at each possible price in a given period, other things being equal.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of supply"}
Other things being equal, as the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises, and as the price falls, the quantity supplied falls. The supply curve slopes upward.
:::

:::definition{term="Indirect tax and subsidy"}
An **indirect tax** is a tax on spending, such as a tax per unit sold; it raises firms' costs. A **subsidy** is a payment by the government to producers, which lowers their costs.
:::

## Why the supply curve slopes upward

1. **Profit motive.** A higher price makes production more profitable, so existing firms produce more and new firms are attracted to the market.
2. **Rising marginal cost.** In the short run, at least one factor is fixed, so extra output requires more costly inputs (overtime, less productive land). Firms are willing to produce more only if price covers the higher marginal cost.
3. **Time and entry of firms.** A higher price lets firms use less efficient methods or resources that were not worth using at low prices.

## The supply schedule and market supply

Market supply is the sum of the quantities that all firms supply at each price. Three fictional farms grow tomatoes.

| Price (US\$ per crate) | Farm A | Farm B | Farm C | Market supply |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 2 | 0 | 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 | 3 | 2 | 10 |
| 3 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 18 |
| 4 | 11 | 9 | 6 | 26 |
| 5 | 14 | 12 | 9 | 35 |

**Describing the diagram.** Price (US\$ per crate) is on the vertical axis and quantity supplied (crates per week) on the horizontal axis. The supply curve S slopes upward from bottom left to top right: at US\$1 the market supplies 3 crates and at US\$5 it supplies 35.

At US\$3 the market supply is $8 + 6 + 4 = 18$ crates.

## Movements along and shifts of the supply curve

- A change in the good's **own price** causes a **movement along** the supply curve: an *extension* (price rises, more supplied) or a *contraction* (price falls, less supplied).
- A change in any **other determinant** shifts the curve: an *increase in supply* (shift right) or a *decrease in supply* (shift left).

| Determinant | Change | Effect on supply |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Costs of production (wages, materials, energy) | Fall | Increase (shift right) |
| | Rise | Decrease (shift left) |
| Technology | Improves | Increase |
| Indirect tax | Introduced or raised | Decrease (shift left, costs rise) |
| Subsidy | Introduced or raised | Increase (shift right) |
| Prices of other goods | Price of an alternative rises | Decrease in supply of this good (resources move to the more profitable good) |
| Number of firms | Rises | Increase |
| Weather / natural events (farming) | Good rainfall / drought | Increase / decrease |
| Expectations | Producers expect higher prices later | May decrease supply now (withholding stocks) |

:::worked-solution{title="Movement or shift in the tomato market?"}
Use the schedule above, where S1 is market supply.

1. The price rises from US\$2 to US\$3. Quantity supplied rises from 10 to 18 crates: $(18 - 10) \div 10 \times 100 = 80\%$.
2. This is an **extension of supply**, a movement along S1.
3. Now suppose a cheaper fertiliser lowers costs so that farms supply 6 more crates at every price. At US\$3 supply rises from 18 to 24 crates: $(24 - 18) \div 18 \times 100 = 33.3\%$.
4. This is an **increase in supply**: the curve shifts right from S1 to S2.
:::

| Price (US\$ per crate) | S1: before | S2: costs fall | S3: costs rise |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 3 | 9 | 1 |
| 2 | 10 | 16 | 8 |
| 3 | 18 | 24 | 16 |
| 4 | 26 | 32 | 24 |
| 5 | 35 | 41 | 33 |

**Describing the diagram.** S2 lies to the right of S1: at each price producers supply 6 more crates. S3 lies to the left of S1, with 2 fewer crates supplied at each price.

### Taxes and subsidies as shifts

A tax of US\$1 per crate raises the cost of each crate by US\$1. Producers receive the price minus the tax, so at a market price of US\$3 they receive only US\$2, and will supply the quantity they would previously have supplied at US\$2, which is 10 crates. The curve shifts **vertically upward** by the amount of the tax (and to the left).

| Market price (US\$ per crate) | Before the tax | After a US\$1 per crate tax |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 10 | 3 |
| 3 | 18 | 10 |
| 4 | 26 | 18 |
| 5 | 35 | 26 |

A subsidy of US\$1 per crate works in the opposite direction: the supply curve shifts downward by the subsidy, and to the right.

:::example{title="A supply shock"}
A fictional tobacco-curing firm in Mashonaland faces a rise in the price of firewood, its main fuel, and a new law requires cleaner burners.

1. The first change raises its costs: supply decreases (shift left).
2. A cleaner burner may eventually reduce wasted fuel: costs fall and supply rises.
3. In the short run the firm supplies less at each price; if it finds cheaper fuel or better technology, the curve moves right.
4. The effect on price and quantity depends on demand, which is considered in the lesson on equilibrium.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a price rise shifts the supply curve"}
A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve. The curve shifts when another determinant changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shifting the supply curve the wrong way for a tax"}
A tax raises costs and decreases supply (shift left or up); a subsidy lowers costs and increases supply (shift right or down).
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing supply with the amount produced or stock"}
Supply is the amount producers are willing and able to sell at each price, not what is already in the warehouse.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the other goods"}
If the price of maize rises, farmers may switch land from sunflowers to maize: the supply of sunflowers decreases.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Paper 1 multiple-choice (ZIMSEC and Cambridge) tests shifts caused by costs, taxes and subsidies and reading a schedule. Papers 2 and 3 use supply shifts to explain price changes in agriculture, mining and energy markets.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: name the determinant, the direction of the shift (increase or decrease in supply) and why costs or profits change. *Analyse*: add the effect on equilibrium price and quantity. Use the words extension and contraction only for movements along the curve.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the same determinants. ZIMSEC may phrase them as "factors affecting supply"; Cambridge expects precise use of "increase in supply" and "extension of supply".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply is the quantity producers are willing and able to offer at each price, ceteris paribus.
- The supply curve slopes upward because of the profit motive and rising marginal costs.
- Market supply is the sum of individual supplies at each price.
- A change in own price causes a movement along the curve; other determinants shift it.
- Lower costs, better technology, subsidies and more firms increase supply; higher costs and indirect taxes decrease it.
:::
