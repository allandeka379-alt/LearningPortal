## Introduction

When the price of tomatoes doubles in Harare, farmers cannot produce more tomatoes the next morning: plants take weeks to grow. When the price of bread rises, a bakery with idle ovens can bake more the same day. A gold mine facing a higher gold price needs years to open a new shaft. In each case the law of supply says that a higher price encourages more production, but the *speed and size* of the response differ enormously. **Price elasticity of supply (PES)** measures that response, and it matters because it determines how much a shift in demand changes the price and how much it changes the quantity.

You should know the supply curve and the difference between a movement along it and a shift. The method of percentage change is the same as for PED.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Price elasticity of supply (PES)"}
A measure of the responsiveness of the quantity supplied of a good to a change in its own price, ceteris paribus.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The formula"}
$$\text{PES} = \frac{\%\ \text{change in quantity supplied}}{\%\ \text{change in price}}$$

Supply curves slope upward, so PES is normally **positive**. The base for each percentage change is the original value.
:::

## Calculating and interpreting PES

:::worked-solution{title="Supply that responds weakly"}
The price of tomatoes at a fictional market rises from US\$2.00 to US\$2.50 per crate. Farmers increase the quantity supplied from 400 to 440 crates a week.

1. % change in price $= (2.50 - 2.00) \div 2.00 \times 100 = +25\%$.
2. % change in quantity supplied $= (440 - 400) \div 400 \times 100 = +10\%$.
3. $\text{PES} = 10 \div 25 = 0.4$.
4. PES is below 1: supply is **inelastic**. Quantity supplied rose by a smaller percentage than price.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Supply that responds strongly"}
The price of a product rises from US\$10 to US\$11. A factory with spare capacity raises output from 1,000 to 1,300 units.

1. % change in price $= +10\%$; % change in quantity $= +30\%$.
2. $\text{PES} = 30 \div 10 = 3.0$.
3. PES is above 1: supply is **elastic**.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Predicting the response"}
A fictional dairy knows that PES for milk is 0.5. A 12% rise in the price of milk should raise the quantity supplied by $0.5 \times 12 = 6\%$. If the dairy supplies 5,000 litres a day, the new supply is $5000 \times 1.06 = 5300$ litres.
:::

| PES | Description | Supply curve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Perfectly inelastic | Vertical: the quantity supplied is fixed whatever the price |
| Between 0 and 1 | Inelastic | Steep; quantity responds by a smaller percentage than price |
| 1 | Unitary elastic | Quantity responds by the same percentage; a straight line from the origin has PES = 1 at every point |
| Above 1 | Elastic | Quantity responds by a larger percentage than price |
| Infinity | Perfectly elastic | Horizontal: firms supply any quantity at one price |

Do not rely on the look of a curve alone. A straight-line supply curve that cuts the price axis (a positive price intercept) has PES above 1 at every point, falling towards 1 as quantity grows, while one that cuts the quantity axis has PES below 1 at every point. A straight line through the origin has PES of exactly 1 at every point.

## Factors affecting PES

1. **Spare capacity.** A firm with idle machines or workers can raise output quickly, so supply is elastic; a firm at full capacity cannot, so supply is inelastic.
2. **Stocks of finished goods.** If firms hold stocks they can release them when price rises. Goods that cannot be stored, such as fresh milk, strawberries or electricity, have inelastic supply in the short run.
3. **Time period.** Supply is more elastic the longer the time allowed, because firms can hire workers, buy machines or build factories.
4. **Mobility of factors of production.** If workers, land and capital can switch easily from other uses to this one, supply is elastic. Specialised machinery or skilled labour that is hard to move makes supply inelastic.
5. **Ease of entry.** Where new firms can enter easily, long-run supply is elastic; barriers to entry such as licences or large capital costs make it inelastic.
6. **Production time and perishability.** Crops with a long growing season cannot respond within a season; goods made by a quick process respond fast.
7. **Availability of raw materials and unit costs.** If expanding output raises costs sharply, firms supply little extra at a given price rise.

## Momentary, short-run and long-run supply

Economists distinguish periods by what can change.

| Period | What can change | PES |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Momentary | Nothing can be produced; only stocks can be sold | Perfectly inelastic or very inelastic |
| Short run | Variable factors (labour, materials) can change; capital is fixed | Inelastic to moderately elastic |
| Long run | All factors can change; new firms can enter | More elastic |

Consider tobacco farming. On the day of an auction the supply is fixed by what has been harvested and cured (momentary). Within a growing season the farmer can apply more fertiliser and labour (short run). Over several years the farmer can plant more land, build curing barns and new growers can enter (long run).

:::example{title="Agriculture and mining"}
Agricultural supply is often inelastic in the short run, since crops take a season to grow, weather may spoil them and stocks of perishable produce cannot be held for long. A price rise for maize this month cannot bring more maize to market this month. Mining supply is also inelastic in the short run, since opening a mine involves exploration, heavy capital and permits that may take years, although an existing mine with spare capacity can raise output faster. The practical result is that prices of primary products swing widely when demand changes, because supply cannot respond quickly.
:::

## Why PES matters

**For price stability.** Suppose demand for a good rises. If supply is elastic, quantity expands by a lot and price rises by a little; if supply is inelastic, price rises sharply and quantity barely changes. Inelastic supply therefore leads to volatile prices, as with agricultural markets after a drought or a surge in demand.

**For tax incidence.** The burden of an indirect tax falls more on consumers when supply is elastic and demand inelastic, and more on producers when supply is inelastic. This is examined in the lesson on taxes and subsidies.

**For policy.** Governments wanting to increase output, for example by raising farm prices, must consider whether supply will respond. If supply is inelastic, the policy raises prices, not output, in the short run. Investment in storage, transport, training and inputs can make supply more elastic.

**For firms.** A firm that can raise output quickly can take advantage of demand surges; one that cannot loses sales to rivals. Flexibility in production is a competitive advantage.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards define and calculate PES, interpret it and list the determinants. Cambridge candidates should also be ready to compare short-run and long-run supply responses in extended answers, and ZIMSEC questions often use agricultural and mining examples.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the slope as elasticity"}
A flat curve is not always elastic. Elasticity is about percentage changes, not the slope on a particular scale.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting time"}
"Supply is inelastic" is incomplete without a time period. The same good can be inelastic in the short run and elastic in the long run.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing PES with a shift"}
A rise in price causes a movement along the supply curve (PES measures that); a lower cost of production shifts the supply curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the formula"}
PES is % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price, not the other way round.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 test the calculation and the factors; Paper 3 may ask for a discussion of why agricultural prices fluctuate. Cambridge Paper 1 tests calculation and the effects of inelastic supply; Paper 2 asks for explanations with examples.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain two determinants of PES*: name each and say how it makes supply more or less elastic (for example spare capacity: output can be raised quickly). *Analyse*: build a chain, for example demand rises, supply is inelastic, price rises sharply. *Assess*: compare short-run and long-run, and note that firms differ.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price; it is normally positive.
- Supply is elastic when PES exceeds 1 and inelastic when it is below 1; a straight line from the origin has PES = 1.
- Spare capacity, stocks, time, factor mobility, ease of entry and perishability determine PES.
- Supply is more elastic in the long run than in the short run; momentary supply is fixed.
- Inelastic supply makes prices volatile, as in agriculture and mining, and affects the incidence of taxes.
:::
