## Introduction

Why did the price of maize meal in Zimbabwean shops rise after a drought, while the price of airtime did not? Why did the price of a minibus ride in Harare change when fuel became dearer, but the number of passengers barely moved? Economists answer such questions with one idea: the price of a good settles where the plans of buyers and sellers match, and it moves when demand or supply shifts.

You should know the demand and supply curves, what makes each shift, and the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift. This lesson puts them together.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market equilibrium"}
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change. The quantity traded is the equilibrium quantity.
:::

:::definition{term="Excess demand (shortage)"}
A situation in which, at the current price, the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied. The price is below the equilibrium price.
:::

:::definition{term="Excess supply (surplus)"}
A situation in which, at the current price, the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded. The price is above the equilibrium price.
:::

## Finding equilibrium from a schedule

The table shows the market for 10 kg bags of maize meal in a fictional town (quantities in thousand bags per week, price in US\$ per bag).

| Price (US\$) | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Excess demand (+) or excess supply (−) | Pressure on price |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 3 | 120 | 30 | +90 | Up |
| 4 | 105 | 45 | +60 | Up |
| 5 | 90 | 60 | +30 | Up |
| **6** | **75** | **75** | **0** | **None: equilibrium** |
| 7 | 60 | 90 | −30 | Down |
| 8 | 45 | 105 | −60 | Down |
| 9 | 30 | 120 | −90 | Down |

At US\$6 the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied (75 thousand bags). At US\$4 there is a shortage of 60 thousand bags: buyers who cannot find maize meal offer more, and sellers see that they can raise prices and still sell everything, so price rises. At US\$8 there is a surplus of 60 thousand bags: sellers who cannot sell cut prices, and as price falls, buyers want more and sellers offer less until the surplus disappears. The price adjusts through the **price mechanism** without anyone directing it.

## Finding equilibrium from equations

The schedule above comes from $Q_d = 165 - 15P$ and $Q_s = 15P - 15$.

:::worked-solution{title="Solving for equilibrium"}
1. Set $Q_d = Q_s$: $165 - 15P = 15P - 15$.
2. Collect terms: $180 = 30P$, so $P = 6$.
3. Substitute: $Q = 165 - 15 \times 6 = 75$ (check: $15 \times 6 - 15 = 75$).
4. Equilibrium: price US\$6 per bag, quantity 75 thousand bags.
:::

## Shifts in demand

:::worked-solution{title="A rise in demand"}
Suppose incomes rise and demand increases by 30 thousand bags at every price: $Q_d = 195 - 15P$. Supply is unchanged.

1. $195 - 15P = 15P - 15$, so $210 = 30P$ and $P = 7$.
2. $Q = 15 \times 7 - 15 = 90$.
3. Price rises from US\$6 to US\$7 (a rise of 16.7%) and quantity from 75 to 90 thousand bags.
:::

The demand curve D shifts to the right, from D1 to D2, with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. At the old price of US\$6 there is now excess demand of 30 thousand bags, so price rises. The rise in price causes an **extension** of quantity supplied (a movement up along the supply curve) and a **contraction** of quantity demanded (a movement up along the new demand curve D2) until the two are equal again at US\$7.

The general pattern:

| Change | Equilibrium price | Equilibrium quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand increases (shifts right) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (shifts left) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (shifts right) | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (shifts left) | Rises | Falls |

## Shifts in supply

:::worked-solution{title="A fall in supply"}
A drought reduces supply by 30 thousand bags at every price: $Q_s = 15P - 45$. Demand is unchanged at $Q_d = 165 - 15P$.

1. $165 - 15P = 15P - 45$, so $210 = 30P$ and $P = 7$.
2. $Q = 165 - 15 \times 7 = 60$.
3. Price rises from US\$6 to US\$7 and quantity falls from 75 to 60 thousand bags.
:::

Notice that the same price change can arise from a demand increase or a supply decrease; the quantity change tells you which happened.

## Both curves shift

When both curves shift, the effect on one variable is clear and on the other depends on the *relative size* of the shifts.

:::worked-solution{title="Demand rises and supply falls together"}
Demand rises by 30 and supply falls by 30 at every price: $Q_d = 195 - 15P$, $Q_s = 15P - 45$.

1. $195 - 15P = 15P - 45$, so $240 = 30P$ and $P = 8$.
2. $Q = 195 - 15 \times 8 = 75$.
3. Price rises from US\$6 to US\$8, while quantity stays at 75. Both shifts push price up, so price definitely rises; the shifts push quantity in opposite directions and happen to cancel here. If the demand shift were larger, quantity would rise; if the supply shift were larger, it would fall.
:::

## Applications

**Primary products and foodstuffs.** Because supply of crops depends on weather and demand is inelastic, small shifts in supply cause large price changes. A drought shifts supply left and raises the price of maize sharply; a bumper harvest does the reverse.

**Transport.** A rise in the price of fuel raises operating costs and shifts the supply of taxi and bus rides left, raising fares and reducing journeys.

**Foreign currency.** The exchange rate can be seen as the price of foreign currency. If demand for US dollars by importers rises, or supply from exporters falls, the price of US dollars in local currency rises. Policy and exchange rate systems are studied later in the course.

**Labour.** The wage is the price of labour; a rise in the demand for skilled workers raises their pay.

:::example{title="Analysing a price change"}
Suppose the price of tobacco on auction floors falls and the quantity sold rises. Is this a rise in supply or a fall in demand? The price fall could come from either, but a rise in quantity points to a *rise in supply* (for example a good growing season): a fall in demand would reduce quantity. An examiner expects you to use the direction of both price and quantity to identify the shift, and to say what could have caused it.
:::

## Evaluating the model

The model assumes many buyers and sellers, identical goods, perfect information and that price adjusts freely. In practice prices can be slow to adjust (menu costs, contracts), governments set maximum and minimum prices, and some firms have power over price. The model remains a powerful first approach, especially for predicting the *direction* of changes, but the size of the change depends on the elasticities of demand and supply: the steeper the curves, the larger the price change for a given shift.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect diagrams (described in words in this course), equations or schedules and analysis of shifts. Cambridge also rewards applying the model to a range of markets and evaluating it; ZIMSEC includes foreign currency and primary-product examples.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a shift with a movement"}
A change in the price of the good causes a movement along the curves. A change in income, tastes, costs or technology shifts a curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the price rose because demand rose, with no mention of excess demand"}
Explain the process: demand shifts, there is excess demand at the old price, price rises, and quantity supplied extends until the market clears.
:::

:::mistake{title="Predicting both price and quantity when both curves shift"}
When both shift, only one variable is certain. Say which and explain that the other depends on the relative size of the shifts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading shortage and surplus the wrong way round"}
A shortage (excess demand) occurs when price is **below** equilibrium; a surplus occurs when price is **above** it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and 2 test equilibrium calculations and shifts; Paper 3 essays often ask you to analyse price changes in a primary product market. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 require diagrams with labelled axes, curves and shifts, and an explanation of the adjustment process.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: describe the shift, the disequilibrium and the adjustment to the new equilibrium. *Calculate*: set quantity demanded equal to quantity supplied and solve. *Analyse*: chain from the cause to the shift, to the price and quantity effects. *Evaluate*: consider the elasticities, the time period and whether the market is free.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Equilibrium is where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; price above it gives excess supply, below it excess demand.
- Price moves towards equilibrium through the price mechanism.
- A shift right in demand raises price and quantity; a shift left in supply raises price and lowers quantity.
- When both curves shift, one variable is certain and the other depends on the relative sizes.
- Solve linear examples by setting Qd = Qs; the model is a first approximation whose predictions depend on elasticities.
:::
