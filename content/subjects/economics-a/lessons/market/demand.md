## Introduction

When the price of tomatoes at a Harare market falls from US\$1.00 to US\$0.60 a kilogram, people buy more; when a heavy hailstorm destroys the crop and the price doubles, they buy fewer. In a market economy, **demand** is half of the explanation of price and quantity, with supply as the other half. Demand is one of the most examined ideas in A Level Economics, and the way to earn marks is to be precise: say whether a change in price causes a *movement along* the curve, or whether another cause shifts the whole curve.

This lesson is for both ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge AS Level. You should know scarcity and opportunity cost. Elasticity, equilibrium and utility theory have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Demand"}
The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at each possible price in a given period, other things being equal.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective demand"}
Demand backed by the ability to pay. A wish for a car without the money to buy one is not effective demand.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of demand"}
Other things being equal, as the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and as the price falls, the quantity demanded rises. The demand curve slopes downward.
:::

:::definition{term="Normal good and inferior good"}
A **normal good** is one for which demand rises when consumers' incomes rise. An **inferior good** is one for which demand falls when incomes rise, because consumers switch to better alternatives.
:::

## The demand schedule and curve

A **demand schedule** lists the quantity demanded at each price. Take the market for 10 kg bags of maize meal in a fictional town.

**Describing the diagram.** Price (US\$ per bag) is on the vertical axis and quantity demanded (bags per week) on the horizontal axis. The demand curve D is a downward-sloping line from top left to bottom right. At US\$5 consumers want 3 bags and at US\$1 they want 25 bags.

### Individual and market demand

Market demand is the sum of the quantities demanded by all consumers at each price (a *horizontal* addition).

| Price (US\$ per bag) | Tendai | Rudo | Farai | Market demand |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 5 | 2 | 1 | 0 | 3 |
| 4 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 7 |
| 3 | 6 | 4 | 2 | 12 |
| 2 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 18 |
| 1 | 10 | 9 | 6 | 25 |

At US\$3 the market demand is $6 + 4 + 2 = 12$ bags.

## Why does the demand curve slope downward?

1. **Income effect.** A fall in price raises the real purchasing power of income, so consumers can afford to buy more.
2. **Substitution effect.** When the price of a good falls, it becomes cheaper relative to substitutes, so consumers switch to it.
3. **Diminishing marginal utility.** As a consumer buys more of a good in a period, each extra unit gives less extra satisfaction, so the consumer will pay less for additional units; the price must fall to persuade them to buy more.

## Movements along and shifts of the demand curve

This is the most important idea in the lesson.

- A **change in the good's own price** causes a **movement along** the demand curve: an *extension* (price falls, quantity demanded rises) or a *contraction* (price rises, quantity demanded falls).
- A change in any **other determinant** shifts the **whole curve**: an *increase in demand* (shift right) or a *decrease in demand* (shift left).

| Determinant | Change | Effect on demand for the good |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income | Rise | Increase for a normal good; decrease for an inferior good |
| Price of a substitute | Rise | Increase (consumers switch to this good) |
| Price of a complement | Rise | Decrease (the pair is used together) |
| Tastes and fashions | More favourable | Increase |
| Population | Rises | Increase |
| Expectations | Prices expected to rise | Increase now (buy before the rise) |
| Distribution of income, advertising, season | Various | Shift according to the effect |

:::worked-solution{title="Movement along or shift?"}
Using the market demand schedule above, suppose the price of maize meal falls from US\$3 to US\$2.

1. Quantity demanded rises from 12 to 18 bags.
2. This is an **extension of demand**: a movement along the same curve, caused by the change in the good's own price.
3. Now suppose that, with the price at US\$3, incomes rise and demand increases by 4 bags at every price.
4. The new schedule shows 16 bags at US\$3 (12 + 4). The percentage increase at US\$3 is $(16 - 12) \div 12 \times 100 = 33.3\%$.
5. This is an **increase in demand**: the whole curve shifts to the right from D1 to D2.
:::

| Price (US\$ per bag) | D1: before (bags) | D2: after (bags) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 5 | 3 | 7 |
| 4 | 7 | 11 |
| 3 | 12 | 16 |
| 2 | 18 | 22 |
| 1 | 25 | 29 |

**Describing the diagram.** D1 and D2 are parallel downward-sloping lines, with D2 to the right of D1. At each price the quantity demanded is 4 bags higher on D2.

## Normal and inferior goods

If the maize meal in the example is a normal good, rising incomes shift its demand curve to the right. For an inferior good, such as second-hand clothing or low-quality cooking oil, a rise in incomes shifts the curve to the left because consumers move to better products. The same good can be normal at low incomes and inferior at high incomes.

:::example{title="Applying determinants to a Zimbabwean case"}
A bus company in Gweru notices that demand for its tickets falls over three months. Possible causes:

1. Fuel prices fall, so more people use shared taxis, a substitute (demand for the bus decreases).
2. Incomes fall and some people walk, so bus travel is a normal good (demand decreases).
3. The population of the town falls (demand decreases).
4. The company's own price rises: this does not shift the curve, it causes a contraction along it.

An examiner expects you to identify the cause, say which curve moves and in which direction, and link your answer to the effect on price and quantity.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a price fall shifts the demand curve"}
A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve. The curve shifts only for a change in another determinant.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing need with demand"}
Demand must be backed by the ability to pay. People may need medicine they cannot afford without it being effective demand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong direction for substitutes and complements"}
A rise in the price of a *substitute* increases demand for this good. A rise in the price of a *complement* decreases it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding prices instead of quantities"}
To find market demand add the quantities at each price (horizontal summation), not the prices.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Cambridge Paper 1 use many short questions on movements and shifts, and reading a schedule. Paper 2 data-response questions and essays use demand changes to explain price changes. Cambridge Paper 3 assumes this at AS level.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give the cause, the curve that shifts and the direction (increase or decrease in demand). *Analyse*: add the effect on equilibrium price and quantity. Always state *ceteris paribus*, label axes with units, and use "increase/decrease in demand" for shifts and "extension/contraction" for movements.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the determinants of demand, individual and market demand, normal and inferior goods, and diminishing marginal utility as a reason for the slope. Cambridge may also use Giffen goods in the context of elasticity and utility; they are not needed for this lesson.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Demand is the quantity consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, ceteris paribus.
- The law of demand: a higher price means a lower quantity demanded because of income and substitution effects and diminishing marginal utility.
- Market demand is the sum of individual quantities at each price.
- A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve; any other determinant shifts the curve.
- Income, related goods, tastes, population and expectations shift demand; normal goods rise with income, inferior goods fall.
:::
