## Introduction

When tobacco prices rise and farmers spend more, when the price of fuel jumps, or when a new power station comes on stream, the whole economy feels the change: output, prices and jobs all move. The **AD/AS model** brings aggregate demand and aggregate supply together to show how the **price level** and **real output** are determined, and how shocks change them. It is the main tool of macroeconomic analysis in the examination.

You should know the aggregate demand curve (and what shifts it), the short-run and long-run aggregate supply curves, and the difference between a movement along and a shift of a curve. This lesson sets out equilibrium, then walks through the four main shifts using one worked schedule. The gaps between actual and potential output, and the multiplier, are in later lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Macroeconomic equilibrium"}
The position at which aggregate demand equals short-run aggregate supply: the price level at which the quantity of real output demanded equals the quantity supplied. It gives the equilibrium **real output** and **price level**.
:::

:::definition{term="Demand-pull inflation"}
A rise in the price level caused by an increase in aggregate demand (a rightward shift of AD), especially when the economy is close to capacity.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-push inflation"}
A rise in the price level caused by an increase in firms' costs (a leftward shift of SRAS).
:::

:::definition{term="Stagflation"}
A situation in which a rise in the price level (inflation) occurs together with falling output and rising unemployment, typically after a leftward shift of SRAS.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply shock"}
A sudden, unexpected event that changes costs or productive capacity, such as a drought, an oil price change or a disruption to transport.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Reading the model"}
Equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS. If the price level is **above** equilibrium, firms supply more than is demanded and the price falls; if it is **below**, demand exceeds supply and the price rises. Employment follows output: a rise in real output usually means more jobs (if productivity is unchanged), a fall means fewer.
:::

## Finding equilibrium from a schedule

:::worked-solution{title="Equilibrium (illustrative data)"}
A fictional economy has the following AD and SRAS schedules (real output in US\$ million). Potential output (LRAS) is US\$12,000 million.

| Price level (index) | AD (US\$ million) | SRAS (US\$ million) | AD − SRAS |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 90 | 11,800 | 9,200 | 2,600 |
| 100 | 11,200 | 10,000 | 1,200 |
| 110 | 10,600 | 10,600 | 0 |
| 120 | 10,000 | 11,100 | −1,100 |
| 130 | 9,400 | 11,500 | −2,100 |

1. AD equals SRAS at a price level of **110**, where both are **10,600**. Equilibrium real output is US\$10,600 million and the price level is 110.
2. At a price level of 100, AD (11,200) exceeds SRAS (10,000) by 1,200: there is excess demand, which pushes the price level up.
3. At 120, SRAS (11,100) exceeds AD (10,000) by 1,100: excess supply, which pushes the price level down.
4. Equilibrium output is below potential output: the gap is 12,000 − 10,600 = 1,400, or 11.7% of potential (1,400 ÷ 12,000 × 100). There is spare capacity and probably unemployment.
5. **Employment.** If each worker produces US\$20,000 of output a year, employment is 10,600 million ÷ 20,000 = **530,000** workers.
:::

## Shifts in AD

:::worked-solution{title="An increase in AD: demand-pull (illustrative data)"}
AD increases by US\$1,100 million at every price level (for example because the government spends more on roads). The new AD column is 12,900; 12,300; 11,700; 11,100; 10,500 at price levels 90 to 130.

1. Compare the new AD with SRAS: at 110, AD (11,700) is above SRAS (10,600); at 120, AD (11,100) equals SRAS (11,100). The new equilibrium is at a price level of **120** and real output of **11,100**.
2. Change in real output = (11,100 − 10,600) ÷ 10,600 × 100 = **+4.7%**.
3. Change in the price level = (120 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = **+9.1%**.
4. Employment rises: 11,100 ÷ 20,000 = 555,000, an increase of **25,000** workers.
5. Both output and prices rise: a **demand-pull** effect. Because SRAS is upward-sloping, part of the AD increase goes into higher prices rather than output. The closer the economy is to potential output (steeper SRAS), the more of the increase falls on prices.
:::

![AD/AS with the worked schedule: AD1 and SRAS meet at a price level of 110 and real output 10,600, below potential output 12,000 (vertical LRAS); AD rises by 1,100 at every price to AD2, giving a new equilibrium at price level 120 and output 11,100, so both output and the price level rise (demand-pull).](media:diagrams/ad-as-demand-pull.png "Figure: an increase in AD raises both real output (10,600 to 11,100) and the price level (110 to 120); the economy stays below potential output of 12,000")

**A decrease in AD** (for example higher interest rates, lower confidence, cuts in government spending or a world recession) has the opposite effect: output, employment and the price level fall. In the schedule, a fall in AD of 1,200 at every price level moves equilibrium to a price level of 100 and output of 10,000: output falls by 5.7% and the price level by 9.1%.

## Shifts in AS

:::worked-solution{title="A fall in SRAS: cost-push and stagflation (illustrative data)"}
A rise in oil prices shifts SRAS to the left by US\$1,100 million at every price level. The new SRAS column is 8,100; 8,900; 9,500; 10,000; 10,400 at price levels 90 to 130.

1. Compare the new SRAS with the original AD: at 120, AD (10,000) equals the new SRAS (10,000). The new equilibrium is at a price level of **120** and output of **10,000**.
2. Change in output = (10,000 − 10,600) ÷ 10,600 × 100 = **−5.7%**.
3. Change in the price level = **+9.1%**.
4. Employment falls: 10,000 ÷ 20,000 = 500,000 workers, a fall of **30,000**.
5. Higher prices with lower output and employment is **stagflation**: the price rise is **cost-push** and arises from the supply side.
:::

![Stagflation in AD/AS: an oil price rise shifts SRAS left by 1,100 at every price level from SRAS1 to SRAS2; the equilibrium moves from price level 110 and output 10,600 to price level 120 and output 10,000, so the price level rises while real output and employment fall (cost-push).](media:diagrams/ad-as-cost-push.png "Figure: a fall in SRAS raises the price level (110 to 120) and lowers real output (10,600 to 10,000): stagflation")

:::worked-solution{title="A rise in SRAS: lower costs or better technology (illustrative data)"}
A new technology lowers energy costs and also raises potential output to US\$13,000 million. SRAS shifts to the right by US\$1,200 million at every price level. The new SRAS column is 10,400; 11,200; 11,800; 12,300; 12,700 at price levels 90 to 130.

1. Compare the new SRAS with AD: at 100, AD (11,200) equals the new SRAS (11,200). The new equilibrium is at a price level of **100** and output of **11,200**.
2. Change in output = (11,200 − 10,600) ÷ 10,600 × 100 = **+5.7%**.
3. Change in the price level = (100 − 110) ÷ 110 × 100 = **−9.1%**.
4. Output and employment rise while the price level falls: this is the most favourable outcome. Potential output has also risen, to 13,000, so there is still spare capacity (a gap of (13,000 − 11,200) ÷ 13,000 × 100 = 13.8%), but with more output and a lower price level.
:::

**Long-run growth.** A rightward shift of LRAS (more or better resources, technology, education, infrastructure) raises potential output. It allows the economy to grow without inflationary pressure, because SRAS also shifts to the right as capacity rises.

## Summary of the effects

| Shift | Output | Price level | Employment | Typical cause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AD right | Rises | Rises (more if near capacity) | Rises | Lower interest rates, higher G, confidence, foreign demand |
| AD left | Falls | Falls (or stays) | Falls | Higher taxes, recession abroad, lower confidence |
| SRAS left | Falls | Rises | Falls | Higher oil prices, wages, drought, strikes |
| SRAS right | Rises | Falls | Rises | Lower costs, technology, subsidy |
| LRAS right | Potential output rises | Eases | Rises over time | Investment in resources, skills, technology |

## Describing a diagram in words

In the examination you are asked to describe or draw the model. A careful description of the first case would be: "The vertical axis shows the price level and the horizontal axis real GDP. The downward-sloping AD curve crosses the upward-sloping SRAS curve at a price level of 110 and real output of 10,600. AD shifts to the right from AD1 to AD2, and the new equilibrium is at a price level of 120 and real output of 11,100. Real output has risen and the price level has risen." Always label the axes and curves, state the old and new equilibrium and say what happened to price, output and employment.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
An increase in government spending on infrastructure shifts AD to the right, raising output and employment in the short run. However, if the economy is already near its potential output, SRAS is steep and most of the rise in AD becomes higher prices (demand-pull inflation) instead of extra output. If the spending also improves the economy's productive capacity, such as roads and power, then LRAS will shift to the right over time, allowing growth without inflation. The effectiveness therefore depends on spare capacity, the time lag and whether the spending raises supply.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require equilibrium real output, the price level and employment in the AD/AS model, and the effects of shifts in AD and AS (demand-pull, cost-push, supply shocks, growth), using worked schedules and diagrams. Cambridge asks for explanation of the effects on real output, the price level and employment; ZIMSEC (Form 6) asks for the same with examples.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a shift and a movement as the same thing"}
After a shift of AD, the economy moves **along** SRAS to the new equilibrium. Describe the shift of the curve first, then the movement along the other curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting employment"}
The syllabus asks about real output, the price level **and** employment. State how employment changes as well (usually in the same direction as output).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that cost-push always lowers the price level"}
A leftward shift of SRAS raises the price level and lowers output. A rightward shift lowers the price level and raises output. Check the direction before writing.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the effects of a shift; Paper 2 gives AD and AS schedules and asks for equilibrium and changes; Paper 3 essays ask for the effects of policies or shocks on output, prices and jobs. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the effect of a shift (often with a described diagram); Paper 2 asks for an explanation with a diagram described in words and evaluation of the effects.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain/Analyse*: shift of curve, then new equilibrium, then output, price level and employment. *Calculate*: find where AD = SRAS, then the percentage changes. *Evaluate*: consider spare capacity, the size of the shift, time lags and whether the shift is temporary or permanent, and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Equilibrium is where AD equals SRAS; it determines real output, the price level and (through output) employment.
- A rightward shift of AD raises output, prices and employment (demand-pull); the nearer to potential output, the more prices rise.
- A leftward shift of SRAS raises prices and lowers output and employment (cost-push, stagflation); a rightward shift does the opposite.
- LRAS shifts to the right with growth in productive capacity, allowing higher output without inflation.
- Always state the shift, the new equilibrium and the change in output, the price level and employment.
:::
