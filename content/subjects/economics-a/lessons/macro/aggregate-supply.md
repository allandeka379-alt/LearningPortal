## Introduction

When the price of fuel rises, bakers, transport operators and farmers all face higher costs and supply less at the same prices. When a country builds better roads, trains more workers and adopts better technology, it can produce more over the years. In both cases the **aggregate supply** of the economy has changed, but the causes and the time scale are different: one is a short-run change in costs, the other a long-run change in productive capacity.

You should know the factors of production, productivity, the production possibility curve and the difference between a movement and a shift. This lesson defines aggregate supply, describes the short-run and long-run curves, and explains what moves them. Equilibrium with AD, and the effects of shifts, are in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Aggregate supply (AS)"}
The total quantity of goods and services (real output) that all the firms in an economy are willing and able to supply at different price levels in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)"}
Aggregate supply in the period during which the prices of some inputs, notably wages, do not change in response to a change in the price level. It shows how much output firms supply at each price level, with input prices and capacity given.
:::

:::definition{term="Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)"}
Aggregate supply in the period when all input prices have adjusted. It shows the economy's **productive capacity** (potential output), determined by the quantity and quality of factors of production and technology, and not by the price level.
:::

:::definition{term="Productive capacity (potential output)"}
The maximum sustainable level of real output an economy can produce with its resources and technology, at full employment of resources.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Movement or shift"}
A change in the **price level** causes a **movement along** the SRAS curve. A change in costs of production (input prices, taxes, subsidies) **shifts SRAS**. A change in the **quantity or quality of resources or in technology** shifts **LRAS** (and also SRAS).
:::

## The shape of SRAS

1. **Upward-sloping.** When the price level rises while the prices of inputs such as wages are fixed in the short run, profits per unit rise and firms supply more. As more output is produced, firms use more overtime, less efficient machines and scarcer inputs, and costs rise faster.
2. **Sweeping curve.** In some textbooks SRAS is **shallow** when there is much spare capacity (output can rise with little pressure on prices) and **steep** near full capacity (shortages of labour and capital raise costs sharply, and output can rise little).

SRAS is drawn with the price level on the vertical axis and real output (real GDP) on the horizontal axis.

## The shape of LRAS

Two views are used by the boards:

- **The vertical (classical) view.** In the long run output is determined by supply-side factors only; the price level does not affect real output. LRAS is a **vertical line** at the potential output (full employment level).
- **The three-section (Keynesian) view.** LRAS has three parts. When there is a lot of spare capacity (a depression), output can rise with no rise in the price level: the curve is **highly elastic** (nearly horizontal). As the economy approaches capacity, bottlenecks appear and the curve **slopes upward**. At full capacity, output cannot rise further, and the curve is **vertical**.

:::worked-solution{title="A short-run AS schedule and a shift (illustrative data)"}
A fictional economy has the following SRAS schedule (real output in US\$ million). Potential output is US\$12,000 million.

| Price level (index) | Real output supplied before (US\$ million) | Real output supplied after (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 80 | 9,000 | 9,600 |
| 90 | 9,800 | 10,400 |
| 100 | 10,500 | 11,100 |
| 110 | 11,000 | 11,600 |
| 120 | 11,300 | 11,900 |

1. **Before:** as the price level rises from 100 to 110 output supplied rises from 10,500 to 11,000, an increase of 500 (4.8%). This is a **movement along** SRAS.
2. Slope: from 80 to 90 output rises by 800; from 110 to 120 by only 300. The curve gets **steeper** as output approaches potential output (12,000): firms find it harder to raise output.
3. **After:** every figure is 600 higher. SRAS has **shifted to the right**: more is supplied at each price level, because costs have fallen (for example lower oil prices).
4. Real output of 11,900 at a price level of 120 is still below potential output of 12,000, so the economy has a small amount of spare capacity.
5. LRAS would be a vertical line at **12,000**, irrespective of the price level.
:::

## Causes of shifts in SRAS

SRAS shifts when the **costs of production** change:

- **Wages and labour costs**, including changes in taxes on employment.
- **Prices of raw materials and energy** (oil, electricity, imported inputs).
- **The exchange rate**: a fall in the exchange rate makes imported inputs more expensive.
- **Taxes and subsidies** on firms (corporation tax, indirect taxes, subsidies on inputs).
- **Productivity** in the short run, for example from training or better organisation.
- **Supply shocks**: droughts, floods, pandemics, strikes, wars or disruptions to transport and power.
- **Regulation** that raises or lowers costs.

A fall in costs shifts SRAS to the **right** (more supplied at each price level); a rise in costs shifts it to the **left**.

:::worked-solution{title="Weighted change in costs (illustrative data)"}
A firm's costs are 60% labour, 30% materials and 10% other. Wages rise by 10% and material prices fall by 5%; other costs are unchanged.

1. Contribution of labour = 60% × 10% = **+6.0** percentage points.
2. Contribution of materials = 30% × (−5%) = **−1.5** percentage points.
3. Other costs = 10% × 0% = 0.
4. Total change in costs = 6.0 − 1.5 = **+4.5%**.
5. Costs of production have risen by 4.5%, so SRAS shifts to the left (less output supplied at each price level).
:::

## Causes of shifts in LRAS

LRAS shifts to the right when the **productive capacity** of the economy grows:

- **More factors of production:** a larger labour force (population growth, migration, higher participation), more capital (investment), more land and natural resources discovered or made usable.
- **Higher productivity:** education and training, better health, new technology, better infrastructure (roads, power, communications).
- **Institutions and efficiency:** reforms that improve incentives, competition, property rights and the working of markets (supply-side policies).

A fall in capacity (war, destruction of capital, emigration of skilled workers, long-term damage from drought) shifts LRAS to the left.

:::worked-solution{title="Growth of potential output (illustrative data)"}
A fictional economy has a labour force of 4.0 million workers, each producing on average US\$12,500 of output a year.

1. Potential output = 4.0 million × US\$12,500 = **US\$50,000 million (US\$50 billion)**.
2. The labour force grows by 2% and output per worker (productivity) grows by 3%.
3. New potential output = 50 × 1.02 × 1.03 = **US\$52.53 billion**.
4. Growth of potential output = (52.53 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = **5.1%** (to one decimal place). LRAS shifts to the right by about 5.1%.
5. Notice that the growth rate is slightly more than 2 + 3 = 5%, because the two changes compound.
:::

## Determinants of AS: short run and long run

| Determinant | Short-run effect | Long-run effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wages and input prices | Shift SRAS | Adjust to the price level |
| Productivity | Shifts SRAS and LRAS if lasting | Raises potential output |
| Technology | Lowers costs (SRAS right) | Raises potential output (LRAS right) |
| Labour force and capital | Little effect quickly | Shifts LRAS |
| Taxes and subsidies | Shift SRAS | Affect incentives and capacity |
| Supply shocks | Large short-run shifts | Little long-run effect unless damage is permanent |

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A fall in oil prices shifts SRAS to the right, because costs fall, lowering the price level and raising output in the short run. It does not change potential output, so LRAS does not shift. In contrast, an investment programme in education and energy would shift LRAS to the right over several years, allowing the economy to grow without inflationary pressure. The two kinds of shift therefore matter in different time periods, and a policy-maker must distinguish between a temporary cost change and a lasting change in capacity.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the definition and determinants of AS, the shape of SRAS (an upward-sloping line or a sweeping curve) and of LRAS (vertical, or in three sections: highly elastic, upward-sloping, vertical), the causes of shifts, and the distinction between a movement and a shift. Be ready to use whichever LRAS shape the question's diagram or description shows.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting SRAS when the price level changes"}
A change in the price level moves the economy **along** SRAS. Only a change in costs, productivity or other determinants shifts the curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing SRAS and LRAS shifts"}
A temporary change in oil prices shifts SRAS but not LRAS. LRAS shifts only when productive capacity changes (more or better resources, technology).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher wage always shifts LRAS"}
Higher wages raise costs and shift SRAS left. Unless they come with higher productivity they do not change potential output.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks about the causes of shifts; Paper 2 gives cost or capacity data; Paper 3 essays ask about supply-side changes and growth. Cambridge Paper 1 tests shifts and the shape of LRAS in multiple choice, and Paper 2 asks for explanation of changes in AS and their effects.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: AS, SRAS, LRAS. *Explain*: how a cost or productivity change shifts a curve. *Distinguish*: SRAS from LRAS and movement from shift. *Calculate*: weighted cost changes and growth of potential output. *Analyse*: trace the chain from cause to shift to output and prices.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Aggregate supply is the total output firms will supply at each price level; SRAS slopes upward because input prices are fixed in the short run.
- LRAS shows potential output; it is vertical (classical) or has three sections (highly elastic, upward-sloping, vertical).
- SRAS shifts with costs of production (wages, materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, supply shocks).
- LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of resources, technology and institutions.
- A price level change is a movement along SRAS; other determinants shift the curve.
:::
