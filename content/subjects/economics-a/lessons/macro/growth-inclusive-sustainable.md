## Introduction

Two countries can post the same growth rate and yet leave their people in very different positions. In one, most households see their incomes rise and the forests, soils and water on which future production depends are still there in twenty years. In the other, the gains go mainly to a few households, and the output is bought by running down resources or damaging the climate. Policy-makers now speak of **inclusive** and **sustainable** growth to describe the first pattern and to warn against the second.

You should know how growth is measured, how inequality is described (the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient) and the idea of an externality. This lesson is a Cambridge A Level topic; the ideas also help with ZIMSEC essays on the costs and benefits of growth.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inclusive growth"}
Economic growth whose benefits are widely shared across income groups and regions, so that poorer households take part in the growth process and gain from it, through jobs, higher incomes and access to services.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable growth"}
Growth that meets the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs; it uses renewable resources at a rate below their regeneration, limits pollution, and does not run down the stock of natural capital.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural capital"}
The stock of natural resources (minerals, forests, soils, water, fisheries and the atmosphere's capacity to absorb waste) that provides inputs and services to the economy.
:::

## Growth and equity

Does growth reduce or increase inequality? There is no automatic answer. Some economists argue that growth raises the incomes of the poor along with the rest (the "rising tide" or trickle-down view), because it creates jobs and tax revenue. Others point out that growth can be concentrated in capital-intensive sectors such as mining, or in one region, so that the poor, who depend on labour or on small farms, gain little. **Which one occurs depends on the source of growth and on policy.**

The table gives illustrative data for a fictional economy over five years (average income per person per month, by fifth of households).

| Income group (fifth of households) | Year 1 (US\$) | Year 6 (US\$) | Change (%) | Share of total, year 1 (%) | Share, year 6 (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Poorest 20% | 40 | 44 | 10.0 | 5.7 | 5.4 |
| Second | 70 | 76 | 8.6 | 10.0 | 9.3 |
| Middle | 100 | 110 | 10.0 | 14.3 | 13.4 |
| Fourth | 150 | 168 | 12.0 | 21.4 | 20.5 |
| Richest 20% | 340 | 422 | 24.1 | 48.6 | 51.5 |

:::worked-solution{title="Was this growth inclusive?"}
1. Average income in year 1 = (40 + 70 + 100 + 150 + 340) ÷ 5 = US\$140; in year 6 = (44 + 76 + 110 + 168 + 422) ÷ 5 = US\$164. Average growth = (164 − 140) ÷ 140 × 100 = **17.1%**.
2. The poorest fifth gained 10.0%, below the average of 17.1%, while the richest fifth gained 24.1%.
3. The share of the poorest fifth fell from 5.7% to 5.4%; that of the richest rose from 48.6% to 51.5%.
4. The Gini coefficient, calculated from the five group shares, rose from about **0.39 to 0.41**.
5. Conclusion: everyone gained, so incomes rose at all levels, but the benefits were unequal. Growth reduced absolute poverty a little but was **not fully inclusive**, since inequality increased.
:::

**Policies for inclusive growth** include investment in education and health (so that the poor can take better jobs), rural roads, electricity and water, support for small businesses and smallholder farmers (access to credit, markets, extension services), progressive taxation and transfers (see the lesson on redistribution), fair access to land and finance, and policies that favour labour-intensive growth and job creation. These measures can cost money and reduce incentives if badly designed, and the benefits take time. There may also be a trade-off: a very high tax on the rich may reduce investment and growth.

## Growth and the environment

Growth raises the demand for resources and the output of waste. The **costs** are mainly external: pollution of air and water, loss of forests and soils, depletion of non-renewable resources, and greenhouse gas emissions which contribute to climate change. Costs fall partly on future generations and often on poorer people who live close to mines or depend on rain-fed farming, so growth can also harm equity. Some of the damage is not captured in GDP, which counts the output of a mine but not the loss of the mineral stock or the harm to the river.

:::worked-solution{title="Adjusting GDP for the environment (an illustration)"}
A fictional economy has GDP of US\$5,000 million in year 1 and US\$5,300 million in year 2. The value of resources used up (depletion) is US\$250 million in year 1 and US\$340 million in year 2, and the value of environmental damage is US\$120 million and US\$180 million.
1. Adjusted output = GDP − depletion − damage: year 1 = 5,000 − 250 − 120 = **US\$4,630 million**; year 2 = 5,300 − 340 − 180 = **US\$4,780 million**.
2. Measured growth = (5,300 − 5,000) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = **6.0%**. Adjusted growth = (4,780 − 4,630) ÷ 4,630 × 100 = **3.2%**.
3. Counting the loss of natural capital almost halves the growth rate: part of the growth was bought by using up resources.
:::

This type of adjustment is an idea for discussion, not a formula you must know; the numbers are difficult to estimate in practice.

**Policies for sustainable growth:**

- **Taxes and charges** on pollution or carbon, which make the polluter pay the external cost (they raise revenue, but raise costs and prices and may be regressive).
- **Regulation**: emission standards, bans on harmful practices and rules on mining and forest use (they can be effective but need monitoring).
- **Tradable permits** that cap total emissions.
- **Subsidies and public investment** in renewable energy (solar, hydro and wind), energy efficiency and clean technology, and the removal of subsidies for polluting fuels.
- **Property rights and community management** of forests, fisheries and water, and recycling and conservation.
- **International agreements** to reduce emissions, since climate change is a global externality and each country has an incentive to free-ride.

:::example{title="Zimbabwean examples (factual and general)"}
Mining and energy: minerals such as gold, platinum, lithium and coal are important export industries, but extraction removes a non-renewable resource, and mining and coal burning can pollute land, water and air. Hydroelectric power from Kariba is renewable, but output depends on rainfall and water levels, and a drought reduces supply, which shows why a diverse energy supply and investment in solar power matter. Land use: farming and the use of wood for fuel can cause deforestation and soil erosion where land is not managed well. Arguments differ on how to balance the income and jobs from these industries against their environmental costs, and on how much a poor country can afford to spend on environmental protection.
:::

## Are the two aims compatible?

Inclusive and sustainable growth can support each other, for example when investment in clean cooking energy raises health and reduces deforestation, but there are also **conflicts**. Cutting subsidies on fuel or taxing carbon raises costs for poor households. Mining may create jobs for the poor and also pollute. A country at a low level of income may place a higher priority on raising current incomes. The policies chosen therefore depend on the weight given to the present and the future and to the poor and the rich, which is why the issue is evaluative.

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge only"}
These two ideas are named for Cambridge A Level (Paper 3 and Paper 4). ZIMSEC students will meet the same arguments in discussions of the costs and benefits of growth and of development.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming that growth is inclusive because average income rises"}
Check how the gain is shared across income groups: use the change in each group's income, or its share in total income, or a rise or fall in the Gini coefficient.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating sustainable growth as no growth"}
Sustainable growth does not mean zero growth. It means growth that does not undermine future production or well-being, for example by switching to renewable energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing policies without evaluating them"}
For each policy give an effect, a cost or limitation (revenue, enforcement, effect on the poor, time lag) and a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 3 asks for the meaning of inclusive and sustainable growth and for readings of data on income shares or emissions. Paper 4 includes data on growth and inequality or the environment, and essays ask you to assess whether growth reduces inequality, or whether governments should place the environment before growth.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: link growth to its effect on income distribution or the environment through a clear chain. *Analyse*: take a policy such as a carbon tax from its effect on cost to the outcome. *Evaluate*: weigh effectiveness, cost, the effects on the poor, time scale and the possibility of conflict between the aims, and reach a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inclusive growth is growth whose benefits reach all income groups, judged by income shares and the Gini coefficient.
- Growth may raise or lower inequality, depending on its source (labour-intensive or capital-intensive) and on policy.
- Sustainable growth meets present needs without damaging future ability to meet theirs; GDP ignores depletion and pollution.
- Policies include taxes, regulation, permits, renewable-energy investment, education, health and support for small producers.
- Policies carry costs, lags and trade-offs, so an evaluation must weigh them and reach a judgement.
:::
