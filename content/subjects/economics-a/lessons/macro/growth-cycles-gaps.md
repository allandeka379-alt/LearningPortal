## Introduction

No economy grows at a perfectly steady pace. Output surges in some years and stalls or falls in others, factories run flat out and then stand half idle, and unemployment rises and falls. Governments and central banks watch two questions: how fast could the economy grow without trouble, and how far is it from that path now? The answers decide whether they cool demand, stimulate it or invest in capacity.

You should know how real GDP and its growth rate are measured, and the idea of aggregate demand and aggregate supply. This lesson builds on them for Cambridge A Level (it is not part of the ZIMSEC syllabus).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Actual growth"}
The percentage change in the economy's real GDP from one period to the next, a movement of the economy from one level of output to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Potential growth"}
The rate at which the economy's productive capacity grows, when its resources are used at a normal, sustainable intensity. It rises with more or better factors of production and is shown by an outward shift of the production possibility curve or of long-run aggregate supply.
:::

:::definition{term="Output gap"}
The difference between actual real GDP and potential real GDP, often as a percentage of potential: (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100. A **positive** output gap means actual output is above potential output; a **negative** output gap means it is below.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade (business) cycle"}
The recurring fluctuations of actual real GDP around its long-run trend, passing through periods of faster and slower or negative growth.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Output gap formula and meaning"}
Output gap (%) = (actual GDP − potential GDP) ÷ potential GDP × 100.
Positive gap: spare capacity is exhausted, labour and materials are scarce, inflationary pressure builds. Negative gap: spare capacity and unemployment, low inflationary pressure.
:::

## Actual and potential growth

**Actual growth** can be fast for a while without any increase in capacity: when the economy is recovering from a recession, idle workers and machines return to work and output rises quickly. This cannot go on for long, because once spare capacity is used up, actual growth can only match **potential growth**, which depends on the quantity and quality of resources, technology and institutions (see the lesson on growth).

The table gives illustrative data for a fictional economy (US\$ billion at constant prices).

| Year | Actual real GDP (US\$ billion) | Potential real GDP (US\$ billion) | Actual growth (%) | Output gap (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 990 | 1,000 | – | −1.0 |
| 2 | 1,045 | 1,030 | 5.6 | 1.5 |
| 3 | 1,089 | 1,061 | 4.2 | 2.6 |
| 4 | 1,050 | 1,093 | −3.6 | −3.9 |
| 5 | 1,070 | 1,126 | 1.9 | −5.0 |
| 6 | 1,128 | 1,160 | 5.4 | −2.8 |

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the table"}
1. Output gap in year 3 = (1,089 − 1,061) ÷ 1,061 × 100 = **+2.6%**: actual output is above potential, so the economy is overheating; wages and prices tend to rise.
2. Output gap in year 4 = (1,050 − 1,093) ÷ 1,093 × 100 = **−3.9%**: actual GDP fell by 3.6% while potential grew by about 3%, so a large negative gap appeared.
3. In year 6 actual growth is 5.4%, well above potential growth of about 3.0%, yet the output gap is still −2.8%. Growth above potential is how a negative gap closes.
4. Potential output grew by about 3% a year throughout (1,000 → 1,160 in five years), so the trend of capacity is steady while actual output fluctuates around it.
:::

## The trade cycle

The cycle is usually described in four phases.

1. **Boom (peak):** growth is fast, demand is strong, unemployment low, the output gap is positive and inflation tends to rise; firms invest and hire.
2. **Downturn and recession:** growth slows and then output falls. A common rule of thumb is two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. Firms cut investment and jobs, unemployment rises and inflation falls.
3. **Trough (slump):** output is at its lowest, with a large negative gap and heavy unemployment.
4. **Recovery:** growth turns positive again as confidence returns, spare capacity is taken up and unemployment falls.

**Causes of cycles.** Many factors interact:

- **Changes in confidence and expectations** (sometimes called animal spirits) alter consumption and investment.
- **The multiplier and accelerator.** A change in spending causes a larger change in income (the multiplier), and a change in output causes a larger change in investment (the accelerator), so fluctuations are magnified.
- **Credit and interest rates.** Easy credit fuels a boom; tight credit and falling asset prices deepen a slump.
- **Shocks** from outside: commodity prices, drought, a global recession, a pandemic, a financial crisis.
- **Policy:** mistimed policy can add to the cycle, because of time lags.
- **Stocks (inventories):** firms cut orders when sales fall and add to them when sales rise, which magnifies swings in output.

## Automatic stabilisers

:::definition{term="Automatic stabilisers"}
Features of the government budget which, without any new decision, reduce the size of fluctuations in demand: tax revenue and spending adjust automatically as national income changes.
:::

In a boom, incomes rise and households pay more income tax (more so under a **progressive** tax system, where higher incomes face higher rates) and spend more on taxed goods, while fewer people claim unemployment benefits. Withdrawals from the circular flow rise and government spending falls, which restrains demand. In a slump the effects reverse: tax receipts fall, benefits rise, and the budget moves towards deficit, supporting spending. Stabilisers are quick and need no decision, but they only reduce, never prevent, a cycle, and are weak where few people pay income tax or receive benefits, as in economies with a large informal sector.

## Policies to promote growth

- **To raise actual growth towards potential (a negative gap):** demand-side policy: lower interest rates, higher government spending or lower taxes, a lower exchange rate to raise exports.
- **To raise potential growth:** supply-side policy: spending on education and skills, health, roads, power and water; incentives for investment and research; competition policy and deregulation; improved property rights; encouraging labour participation and enterprise.

:::example{title="Effectiveness: an evaluation paragraph"}
Demand-side measures can close a negative gap quickly, but they are ineffective if the gap is small and the problem is a lack of capacity; then they raise inflation and imports instead of output. Supply-side policy raises potential output permanently but works slowly: schools and roads take years to pay off, they cost money now, and the benefit depends on how well the spending is used and on whether there is demand for the extra output. Governments also risk misjudging the output gap, which is an estimate, since potential output cannot be observed. The best policy therefore depends on the size and cause of the gap, the time available and the cost.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating actual and potential growth as the same thing"}
Actual growth is a movement of GDP; potential growth is the growth of capacity. Actual growth can exceed potential growth for a time during a recovery.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the sign of the output gap"}
Actual above potential is a positive gap (boom, inflation pressure); actual below potential is a negative gap (spare capacity, unemployment).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that a recession means that the economy is not growing in capacity"}
Potential output can continue to grow during a recession; it is actual output that falls.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 3 multiple-choice questions describe a diagram or a table of actual and potential GDP and ask you to identify the gap or the phase. Paper 4 data-response questions ask you to calculate an output gap and discuss policy, and essays ask you to assess whether governments can stabilise the cycle or raise potential growth.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: define each and give one difference. *Calculate*: use (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100 and state the sign. *Explain*: use a chain such as falling confidence, lower investment, lower AD, lower output. *Assess* or *evaluate*: weigh effectiveness (speed, cost, size of gap, time lags) and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Actual growth is a change in real GDP; potential growth is the growth in productive capacity.
- Output gap = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100; positive means overheating, negative means spare capacity.
- The trade cycle has four phases: boom, downturn or recession, trough and recovery; causes include confidence, the multiplier and accelerator, credit and shocks.
- Automatic stabilisers (progressive taxes, benefits) reduce but do not remove fluctuations.
- Demand-side policy closes negative gaps quickly; supply-side policy raises potential growth slowly.
:::
