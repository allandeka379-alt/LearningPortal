## Introduction

When prices rise, a given amount of money buys less. A worker whose pay is fixed finds that the same wage buys fewer groceries; a saver watches the value of a bank balance shrink; a business cannot be sure what its costs will be next year. Yet a small, steady rise in prices is usually seen as normal in a growing economy. The problems come when inflation is high, unpredictable, or when prices actually fall. Zimbabwe's history before 2009, when money lost value so fast that prices were changed several times a day and the economy turned to foreign currencies, shows what extreme inflation can do. This lesson explains the vocabulary, the causes, the costs and the policies.

You should know how a price index measures changes in the price level (the lesson on price indices covers how the index is built) and the idea of aggregate demand and aggregate supply.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inflation"}
A sustained rise in the general level of prices in an economy over time, so that the purchasing power of money falls. It is measured by the percentage change in a price index, usually the consumer price index (CPI), over a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Deflation"}
A sustained fall in the general price level, that is, a negative rate of inflation.
:::

:::definition{term="Disinflation"}
A fall in the rate of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly than before.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formula"}
Inflation rate (%) = (price index this year − price index last year) ÷ price index last year × 100.
Real value = nominal value ÷ price index × 100.
:::

The table shows a consumer price index for a fictional economy (year 1 = 100).

| Year | CPI | Inflation rate (%) | Description |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1 | 100.0 | – | Base year |
| 2 | 105.0 | 5.0 | Inflation |
| 3 | 112.5 | 7.1 | Higher inflation |
| 4 | 116.9 | 3.9 | Disinflation: prices still rise, but slower |
| 5 | 114.6 | −2.0 | Deflation: the price level falls |

:::worked-solution{title="Inflation and real wages"}
1. Inflation in year 3 = (112.5 − 105.0) ÷ 105.0 × 100 = **7.1%**. In year 4 = (116.9 − 112.5) ÷ 112.5 × 100 = **3.9%**: this is disinflation, since the rate fell although prices kept rising.
2. In year 2 a worker earns US\$600 a month. In year 3 her wage rises by 8% to US\$648, but prices rise by 7.1%.
3. Real wage, year 2 = 600 ÷ 105 × 100 = **US\$571.43** (in year 1 prices). Year 3 = 648 ÷ 112.5 × 100 = **US\$576.00**.
4. Real wage change = (576.00 − 571.43) ÷ 571.43 × 100 = **+0.8%**: most of the 8% rise was lost to higher prices.
:::

## Causes of inflation

### Demand-pull inflation

Demand-pull inflation occurs when aggregate demand (AD) rises faster than the economy's capacity to produce, so firms raise prices. In AD and AS terms, the AD curve shifts to the right along an upward-sloping short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve, which raises the price level and, until capacity is full, real output. At or beyond full capacity, the long-run supply curve is vertical, so extra demand raises only the price level. Causes of an increase in AD: lower interest rates and easy credit, tax cuts or increased government spending, a rise in exports (for example a boom in world demand for tobacco or minerals), rising confidence and wealth, and a rapid increase in the money supply. It is associated with a positive output gap and low unemployment.

### Cost-push inflation

Cost-push inflation occurs when firms' costs rise independently of demand, so the SRAS curve shifts to the left and the price level rises while output falls. Causes: rises in wages above productivity, increases in the cost of raw materials or fuel, higher indirect taxes, a **fall in the exchange rate** which raises the price of imported inputs (imported inflation), and supply shocks such as drought, which raises food prices. Cost-push inflation can occur with rising unemployment (stagflation). A wage–price spiral can develop if workers demand higher wages to keep up with prices, which raises costs further.

:::example{title="Distinguishing the two"}
Prices rise 8% and real output rises 4%, with unemployment falling: this suggests demand-pull. Prices rise 8% and real output falls with unemployment rising: this suggests cost-push (a leftward shift of SRAS). In practice both can act together, and expectations of future inflation, which workers and firms build into wage and price decisions, can keep inflation going.
:::

## Consequences of inflation

- **Redistribution.** Those with fixed nominal incomes (pensioners, people on fixed contracts) lose; borrowers gain at the expense of lenders, because the real value of debt falls; savers lose if the interest rate is below inflation. A saver with US\$5,000 earning 4% when inflation is 7% has a real return of about 4 − 7 = −3% (exactly (1.04 ÷ 1.07 − 1) × 100 = −2.8%).
- **Menu costs:** the cost of changing prices (reprinting price lists, repricing). **Shoe-leather costs:** time and effort spent moving money to avoid its loss of value, or shopping around.
- **Uncertainty:** firms find it harder to plan and invest, so growth may be lower.
- **Competitiveness:** if domestic inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so the current account may worsen (unless the exchange rate falls).
- **Wage–price spiral and loss of confidence** in the currency.
- **Hyperinflation** (extremely rapid inflation, often defined as above 50% a month) destroys the functions of money: it ceases to be a reliable store of value or a unit of account, people hold foreign currencies or goods instead, use barter, savings are wiped out, and output falls. It is usually associated with the creation of money to finance government spending. Zimbabwe's experience before 2009 is an example, and the economy moved to using foreign currencies from that year.

Moderate and stable inflation has costs that are smaller; some economists even argue that low inflation (around 2 to 3%) helps firms to adjust real wages and avoids deflation.

### Consequences of deflation

Deflation can be harmful: consumers delay purchases in the hope that prices will fall further, so demand falls; the real value of debts rises, which makes borrowers cut spending; firms' revenues and profits fall, so they cut investment and jobs, which can start a downward spiral. A fall in prices caused by lower costs from higher productivity (benign deflation) is less harmful.

## Policies to reduce inflation

| Policy | Method | Effectiveness and problems |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monetary | Raise interest rates; slow the growth of the money supply | Reduces borrowing and consumption and investment. Time lags; may raise unemployment; less effective if inflation is cost-push; exchange rate may rise |
| Fiscal | Raise taxes, cut government spending | Reduces AD. Politically difficult; time lags; may reduce growth |
| Supply-side | Improve productivity: education, infrastructure, competition | Shifts SRAS to the right and lowers costs. Slow and costly, but lasting |
| Wage and price controls (incomes policy) | Limit wage rises or fix prices | Can reduce inflation quickly, but cause shortages, black markets, distortions and may not last |
| Exchange rate | Allow or support a higher exchange rate | Lowers import prices; but harms exporters |

**Evaluation.** Demand-side policies work best on demand-pull inflation, but they raise unemployment and cut output. For cost-push inflation supply-side policy gets at the cause but is slow. Controls give temporary relief but do not cure the underlying pressure. The best choice depends on the cause, the speed required, and the damage tolerated, and credible policy that lowers expectations helps in every case.

Both boards expect the definitions, demand-pull and cost-push, the costs of inflation and policies. ZIMSEC (Form 6) refers to price and wage controls and the experience of an economy with very high inflation; Cambridge AS expects AD and AS analysis. The construction of the price index is in a separate lesson, and the quantity theory of money is covered under money.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that disinflation means falling prices"}
Disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation: prices still rise. A fall in prices is deflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every price rise inflation"}
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, not a single price rise.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming that cost-push inflation can be cured by reducing demand without cost"}
Reducing demand lowers prices but also output and jobs, so it is a costly cure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and causes; Paper 2 uses CPI data to calculate rates and real wages, and asks for policies; Paper 3 essays ask whether inflation or unemployment is the greater problem. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test the causes with AD and AS, calculations from the CPI, and the effectiveness of policy.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: use (new − old) ÷ old × 100 and give the unit. *Distinguish*: demand-pull is a rise in AD; cost-push is a fall in SRAS. *Explain*: link the cause to AD or AS and then to the price level. *Evaluate*: consider the cause, time lags, side effects and the alternatives, and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; deflation is a sustained fall; disinflation is a fall in the rate of inflation.
- Demand-pull inflation comes from a rise in AD; cost-push comes from a rise in costs (a fall in SRAS).
- Costs include redistribution, menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty, loss of competitiveness, and, in the extreme, hyperinflation.
- Deflation can reduce spending and raise the real burden of debt.
- Policies are monetary, fiscal, supply-side and controls; their effectiveness depends on the cause, time lags and side effects.
:::
