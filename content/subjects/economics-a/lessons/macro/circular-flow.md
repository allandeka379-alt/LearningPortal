## Introduction

When a tobacco farmer near Karoi is paid for a crop, she spends part of the money on food, school fees and tools, saves some in a bank and pays some in tax. The shopkeeper, the school and the bank in turn pay wages to their workers, who spend, save and pay tax again. Money moves round the economy in a continuous **circular flow**. The size of the flow is the level of national income, and the model shows what makes it grow, shrink or stay stable.

You should know the factors of production, the difference between a flow and a stock, and the components of national income (C, I, G, X, M). This lesson builds the circular flow model step by step, from a simple economy to one with a government and foreign trade, and explains equilibrium. The multiplier effect of changes in injections is in a later lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Circular flow of income"}
A model of the economy that shows how money, goods, services and factors of production move between households and firms (and the government, the financial sector and the rest of the world), in a continuous flow.
:::

:::definition{term="Injection"}
Any addition to the flow of income that does not come from households' spending out of income at home: **investment (I)**, **government spending (G)** and **exports (X)**. Total injections are written **J**.
:::

:::definition{term="Leakage (withdrawal)"}
Any part of income that is taken out of the circular flow and not spent on domestically produced goods: **saving (S)**, **taxes (T)** and **imports (M)**. Total leakages are written **W**.
:::

:::definition{term="Closed and open economy"}
A **closed economy** has no trade with the rest of the world. An **open economy** has an international sector: it exports and imports.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium national income"}
The level of national income at which planned injections equal planned leakages (J = W), so that the flow neither grows nor shrinks.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Core results"}
Income (Y), output (O) and expenditure (E) are equal in the flow: Y = O = E.
Equilibrium: **J = W**, that is, I + G + X = S + T + M.
If J > W, the flow of income rises (expansion); if J < W, it falls (contraction).
:::

## Step 1: two sectors (households and firms)

1. **Households** supply factors of production (labour, land, capital and enterprise) to firms.
2. **Firms** pay households **factor incomes**: wages, rent, interest and profit. This is the flow of **income**.
3. Households spend the income on goods and services produced by firms. This is **consumption expenditure (C)**, which is the flow of **expenditure** and gives firms revenue and **output**.

In this simple economy all income is spent, so the flow is constant. Output = income = expenditure. Nothing leaks and nothing is injected.

## Step 2: add saving and investment (the financial sector)

Households do not spend all their income; they **save (S)**, which is a leakage from the flow. Saving goes to banks and other financial institutions. Firms borrow from them to buy capital goods: **investment (I)**, an injection. In equilibrium (with only these two): **S = I**. If planned saving exceeds planned investment, spending on output falls short of the output produced and national income falls; if I exceeds S, income rises.

## Step 3: add the government

The government collects **taxes (T)** from households and firms: a leakage. It spends on public services, wages and infrastructure: **government spending (G)**, an injection. If G exceeds T there is a budget deficit; if T exceeds G there is a surplus.

## Step 4: add the international sector (the open economy)

Households and firms buy foreign goods and services: **imports (M)**, a leakage because the money goes to foreign producers. Foreigners buy the country's goods and services: **exports (X)**, an injection. Net exports are X − M.

In words, the flow diagram for the full open economy shows: households receive wages and other incomes from firms and the government; they spend on firms' output (C), pay taxes to the government, save in banks and buy imports; firms receive revenue from consumption, from investment funded by banks, from government purchases and from exports; the government receives taxes and spends on goods and services; the financial sector receives saving and lends to firms for investment; and the foreign sector receives import payments and pays for exports.

## Injections, leakages and equilibrium

:::worked-solution{title="Equilibrium and disequilibrium (illustrative data)"}
A fictional open economy has the following planned flows (US\$ million a year).

| Leakages (withdrawals) | US\$ million | Injections | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Saving (S) | 1,200 | Investment (I) | 1,500 |
| Taxes (T) | 1,800 | Government spending (G) | 1,700 |
| Imports (M) | 900 | Exports (X) | 800 |
| **Total W** | **3,900** | **Total J** | **4,000** |

1. Total leakages W = S + T + M = 1,200 + 1,800 + 900 = **3,900**.
2. Total injections J = I + G + X = 1,500 + 1,700 + 800 = **4,000**.
3. J exceeds W by 4,000 − 3,900 = **100**. The economy is **not** in equilibrium: more is being injected than is leaking out.
4. Because spending on domestic output exceeds the leakages, firms' sales rise and national income **expands**. As income rises, saving, taxes and imports rise too (they depend on income), so leakages grow until W = J at a higher level of income. The size of the rise depends on the multiplier, covered later.
5. If instead planned saving rose to 1,400 while everything else stayed the same, W would be 4,100 and exceed J (4,000) by 100; national income would **fall** until the two were equal again.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Closed economy with two sectors (illustrative data)"}
Households receive income of US\$8,500 million, consume US\$7,000 million and save the rest. Firms plan to invest US\$1,500 million.

1. Saving S = Y − C = 8,500 − 7,000 = **US\$1,500 million**.
2. Leakage W = S = 1,500 and injection J = I = 1,500, so J = W: the flow is in **equilibrium**.
3. If firms planned to invest only US\$1,200 million, J (1,200) would be less than W (1,500), unsold stock would build up, firms would cut output and national income would fall.
:::

## Relating the model to national income

- The flow of **income** is the sum of factor incomes. The flow of **output** is the value of goods and services produced. The flow of **expenditure** is C + I + G + (X − M). They are equal in the sense used in national income accounting.
- **Equilibrium** is a statement about **planned** injections and leakages. Actual (measured) saving always equals actual investment in the accounts, but planned amounts may differ, and the difference leads to changes in income.
- The model shows why governments try to influence injections (G, or I through interest rates) and leakages (T) to stabilise income.

## Limitations of the model

1. It is a simplification: it treats all households and firms alike and ignores differences in who saves and who invests.
2. It does not show **why** injections and leakages change (for example, expectations).
3. It focuses on money flows, not on real resources and time lags.
4. In practice, imports are also used in producing exports and investment, so the sectors overlap.
5. It does not show prices and inflation, which are dealt with in AD/AS analysis.

:::example{title="Applying the model"}
Suppose a government in a fictional economy raises its spending on roads (G rises) while taxes stay the same. Injections exceed leakages, so national income rises. Construction firms hire workers, who spend more, so the effects spread through the economy. If the extra income also raises imports (M rises), part of the effect leaks abroad. The model therefore shows why an economy with a large share of imports in spending gains less from a rise in government spending.
:::

Cambridge (AS Level) does not require the multiplier or marginal and average propensities here; ZIMSEC (Form 5) expects you to be able to read and describe a flow diagram. The multiplier is in the lesson on the multiplier.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating saving as lost from the economy"}
Saving is a leakage from the flow of spending, but it is not lost: banks lend it to firms for investment. The economy is in equilibrium when planned saving and other leakages equal injections.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting government spending as a leakage"}
Government spending (G) is an injection; taxes (T) are the leakage. Imports are a leakage; exports are an injection.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that equilibrium means that income cannot change"}
At equilibrium J = W, so the flow is stable. Income changes again if any injection or leakage changes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and which items are injections or leakages; Paper 2 gives data on S, T, M, I, G and X; Paper 3 essays ask about the effect of changes in injections or leakages. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the identification of injections and leakages and the description of a flow diagram; Paper 2 includes short explanations of equilibrium.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe*: follow the flow from households to firms and back, including government and overseas. *Explain*: why J = W is equilibrium, and what happens if J ≠ W. *Calculate*: total injections and total leakages and compare. *Analyse*: show how a change in one injection or leakage changes national income.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The circular flow shows income, output and expenditure moving between households, firms, the financial sector, the government and the rest of the world.
- Injections (I, G, X) add to the flow; leakages (S, T, M) take money out of it.
- Equilibrium national income occurs where J = W; if J > W income rises, if J < W it falls.
- Saving is a leakage that banks lend to firms for investment; taxes fund government spending; imports and exports link the economy to the rest of the world.
- The model is a simplification; it shows direction of change but not size (the multiplier) or prices.
:::
