## Introduction

A young graduate in Bulawayo sends applications for months without success. A farm worker in Mashonaland is idle between the harvest and the next planting. A tailor loses her job when a factory closes and spends a year retraining. All are without work, but for different reasons, and the remedies differ. In an economy where many people earn a living in the informal sector, even the question "how many people are unemployed?" is difficult to answer.

You should know what the labour force is, what GDP measures, and the idea of aggregate demand. The lesson on unemployment and the natural rate (Cambridge A Level) takes these ideas further, to equilibrium unemployment and policy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Unemployment"}
The state of being without work, available to start work and actively seeking it at the going wage rate. People who do not fit this description (students, retired people, full-time carers, and those who have given up looking) are not counted as unemployed.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour force (economically active population)"}
All those of working age who are in work or unemployed: the number employed plus the number unemployed.
:::

:::definition{term="Unemployment rate"}
The number of unemployed as a percentage of the labour force.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulae"}
Labour force = employed + unemployed.
Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
Participation (activity) rate = labour force ÷ working-age population × 100.
:::

## Measuring unemployment

Two main methods are used. A **labour force survey** asks a sample of households whether people are working, available and looking for work; it follows an international definition and allows comparison between countries. The **claimant count** counts people who register for unemployment benefit; it depends on the rules of the benefit scheme and is lower in countries where few people receive benefits, as in many developing economies.

The table gives illustrative figures for a fictional economy (thousands of people).

| Group | Number (thousand) |
| --- | ---: |
| Working-age population | 10,000 |
| Economically inactive (students, carers, retired and others) | 3,000 |
| Employed | 6,300 |
| Unemployed | 700 |

:::worked-solution{title="Labour force, unemployment rate and participation rate"}
1. Labour force = 6,300 + 700 = **7,000 thousand**. (Check: 10,000 − 3,000 inactive = 7,000.)
2. Unemployment rate = 700 ÷ 7,000 × 100 = **10.0%**.
3. Participation rate = 7,000 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **70.0%**.
4. Next year the labour force grows to 7,100 thousand and 640 thousand are unemployed. The rate is 640 ÷ 7,100 × 100 = **9.0%**, down from 10.0%.
:::

### Problems with the official figures

- **Hidden (disguised) unemployment and discouraged workers.** People who have given up looking leave the labour force, so the rate falls without any new job. If 150 thousand of the 700 thousand unemployed in the example become discouraged and stop searching, the labour force falls to 6,850 thousand and the unemployed to 550 thousand, so the rate falls to 550 ÷ 6,850 × 100 = **8.0%**, although no job has been created.
- **Underemployment.** People working part-time who want full-time work, or whose skills are not used, count as employed. In many developing economies underemployment is a larger problem than open unemployment.
- **The informal sector.** Millions of people may earn a living through own-account trading, small-scale farming or casual work. They are counted as employed even if their income is low and unstable, so low measured unemployment does not mean a healthy labour market.
- **Cheating and unrecorded work.** Some people claim benefits while working, and some who are working are not recorded.
- **Averages hide differences** between regions, age groups (youth unemployment is typically higher), gender and skill groups.
- **Definitions and survey methods differ** between countries, so comparisons are not exact.

## Types and causes of unemployment

:::definition{term="Frictional unemployment"}
Short-term unemployment of people who are between jobs or have just entered the labour market, because it takes time to search for and match with a job.
:::

:::definition{term="Structural unemployment"}
Unemployment caused by a mismatch between the skills or location of the workers and those that the jobs demand, because the structure of the economy has changed. It tends to be long-lasting.
:::

:::definition{term="Cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment"}
Unemployment caused by a fall in aggregate demand during a slowdown or recession, so that firms need fewer workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Seasonal unemployment"}
Unemployment caused by regular seasonal changes in demand for labour, in agriculture, tourism or construction.
:::

**Technological unemployment** is a form of structural unemployment in which machines or new methods replace workers in some tasks.

| Type | Cause | Example | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frictional | Time to search and match | A school-leaver looking for a first job | Better job information, shorter search |
| Structural | Skills or location mismatch; decline of industries | Textile workers after a factory closure | Retraining, mobility, regional policy |
| Cyclical | Low aggregate demand | Retrenchments in a recession | Raise aggregate demand |
| Seasonal | Seasonal demand | Farm workers after harvest | Diversify activity; off-season work |
| Technological | Machines replace labour | Automated sorting at a depot | Retraining, new industries |

Other causes include rising wages above the market level (real wage unemployment), the immobility of labour, and weak growth with a rapidly growing labour force.

## Consequences of unemployment

:::example{title="Costs of unemployment"}
**For the unemployed:** loss of income and poverty, loss of skills and confidence, worse health and stress, and, if long-term, difficulty finding work again.

**For firms:** weaker demand for goods and services, since unemployed people have less to spend; but also a greater supply of labour that may keep wage costs low.

**For the government:** lower income tax and VAT revenue, higher spending on benefits or social support (where they exist), and possibly more crime and demands on health services.

**For the economy:** a waste of resources, since output is lost (the economy is inside its production possibility curve and has a negative output gap); lower growth; and wider inequality. Long-term unemployment can reduce the skills of workers permanently and so lower the potential output of the economy.
:::

Unemployment can also bring benefits to some groups: lower pressure on wages for employers, and time for people to search for better jobs. But these are small compared with the costs.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the definition, measurement and its problems, the types and causes, and the consequences. ZIMSEC (Form 5) pays particular attention to the informal sector and underemployment in a developing economy. Cambridge AS requires the unemployment rate calculation and the distinction between the types. Policies to reduce unemployment and the idea of the natural rate are in a separate lesson.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting everyone without a job as unemployed"}
Students, retired people, full-time carers and discouraged workers who are not searching are economically inactive, not unemployed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the population as the base for the rate"}
The unemployment rate is the unemployed as a percentage of the **labour force**, not of the whole population or of the working-age population.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing structural and frictional unemployment"}
Frictional unemployment is temporary and arises from searching; structural unemployment is long-lasting and arises from a mismatch of skills or location.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and types and Paper 2 gives labour force data to calculate the rate and discuss the consequences; Paper 3 essays ask about causes and policies. Cambridge Paper 1 asks for the rate and for matching causes to types; Paper 2 asks for the measurement problems and consequences in data response and essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: find the labour force first, then use the formula. *Distinguish*: define each type and give an example with a distinguishing feature (duration, cause). *Explain*: give a cause and a chain of effects. *Discuss* or *assess*: weigh costs for different groups, and consider how reliable the data are.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Unemployment means being without work, available and actively seeking it; the rate is the unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
- Measures such as the survey and the claimant count miss hidden unemployment, underemployment and the informal sector.
- The main types are frictional, structural, cyclical (demand-deficient), seasonal and technological.
- Consequences include lost output, lower tax revenue, higher benefit costs, poverty and inequality.
- The remedy depends on the type: more demand, better skills and mobility, or better job information.
:::
