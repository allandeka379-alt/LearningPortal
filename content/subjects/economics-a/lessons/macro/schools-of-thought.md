## Introduction

Economists agree on many facts but disagree about how a modern economy behaves when it is out of balance. If unemployment is high, should the government spend more, or leave markets to adjust? If inflation is high, is the cause too much money, or too much spending, or rising costs? Two schools have dominated macroeconomic debate: the **Keynesian** school, based on the ideas of John Maynard Keynes, who wrote in the 1930s during the Great Depression, and the **monetarist** school, associated with Milton Friedman and, in an older form, the **classical** economists. Policy-makers use ideas from both, and examination answers must set out each view fairly and then judge them.

You should know aggregate demand and supply, the multiplier, the quantity theory of money (MV = PT) and the natural rate of unemployment from earlier lessons. This lesson is a ZIMSEC (Form 6) topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Keynesian school"}
The view that the level of output and employment in the short and medium run is determined mainly by aggregate demand, that wages and prices adjust slowly, so the economy can remain below full employment for a long time, and that government demand management (fiscal policy) can and should stabilise the economy.
:::

:::definition{term="Monetarist (classical) school"}
The view that free markets, with flexible wages and prices, tend to restore full employment, that the main influence on inflation in the long run is the growth of the money supply, and that government should control the money supply steadily, limit its own role and improve the working of markets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Quantity theory"}
MV = PT, where M is the money supply, V velocity of circulation, P the price level and T the volume of transactions. Monetarists assume that V is stable and that T (real output) tends to full employment in the long run, so a rise in M leads to a proportional rise in P.
:::

## The main differences

| Issue | Keynesian view | Monetarist (classical) view |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cause of fluctuations** | Instability of aggregate demand, especially investment and confidence | Changes in the money supply and government interference with markets |
| **Wages and prices** | Sticky, especially downwards, so markets do not clear quickly | Flexible, so markets clear and return to full employment |
| **Aggregate supply** | Upward-sloping in the short run: horizontal at low levels of output with spare capacity, steep near full employment | Vertical at the full employment level of output in the long run |
| **Unemployment** | Can be involuntary and persistent, caused by deficient demand | Equals the natural rate in the long run; caused by rigidities such as union power, benefits and minimum wages |
| **Money** | Money affects output through interest rates, but velocity is unstable and monetary policy may be weak in a slump (the demand for money may become very responsive to the interest rate) | Money supply growth determines inflation in the long run; velocity is stable; money matters most |
| **Inflation** | Demand-pull and cost-push; can be reduced by cutting demand | "Always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon" in the long run: too much money chasing goods |
| **Role of government** | Active: manage aggregate demand with fiscal policy (and monetary) to stabilise the economy | Limited: control growth of the money supply by a rule; supply-side policies; keep markets competitive |
| **Fiscal policy** | Effective: the multiplier raises income | Weak: crowding out of private spending, and it only causes inflation at full employment |

## Illustrations with numbers

:::worked-solution{title="A Keynesian view: spending raises output when there is spare capacity"}
An economy has high unemployment and a multiplier of 2.5. The government raises its spending by US\$100 million.
1. Rise in national income = 2.5 × 100 = **US\$250 million**.
2. Keynesians expect most of this to be extra real output and employment, because there is unused capacity and wages and prices are slow to rise.
3. Monetarists reply that the borrowing needed to finance the spending raises interest rates and crowds out private spending, and that unemployment will fall by itself if wages adjust.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="A monetarist view: money and the price level"}
Using MV = PT, let M = 200, V = 4 and T = 400.
1. P = MV ÷ T = 200 × 4 ÷ 400 = **2.0**.
2. The money supply grows by 10% to 220, with V and T constant: P = 220 × 4 ÷ 400 = **2.2**, a rise of **10%**.
3. Monetarists conclude that inflation can be controlled by controlling the growth of the money supply. Keynesians question the assumption that V and T are constant: if V falls, or output rises, the effect on prices is smaller.
:::

Describe the aggregate supply curves in words. For the **classical** view, long-run aggregate supply is a vertical line at full employment output, so a rise in AD raises only prices. For the **Keynesian** view, the curve is flat at low levels of output (unemployed resources can be put to work without a price rise), then rises, and becomes vertical at full capacity.

## Policy implications

- **Recession and unemployment.** Keynesian: raise government spending or cut taxes (and lower interest rates). Monetarist: be patient while wages adjust; remove obstacles to employment (reduce union power or the minimum wage, simplify rules); keep the money supply steady.
- **Inflation.** Keynesian: reduce aggregate demand, and in cost-push cases use incomes policy. Monetarist: cut the growth of the money supply; reduce budget deficits financed by money creation.
- **Role of the government.** Keynesians accept deficits in a slump; monetarists favour balanced budgets and a smaller state.

## Evaluation

:::example{title="Assessing the two schools"}
**Keynesian strengths:** explains the persistence of unemployment in the 1930s and in later recessions; provides tools (multiplier, stabilisers) to deal with slumps; recognises that wages are sticky. **Weaknesses:** fiscal policy has time lags, may crowd out private spending, can be misjudged and politically biased towards spending; persistent deficits raise debt; may cause inflation if used when the economy is near capacity.

**Monetarist strengths:** emphasises that excessive money growth causes inflation, supported by episodes of hyperinflation in which governments financed spending by creating money; highlights the importance of expectations and of supply-side flexibility. **Weaknesses:** velocity is often unstable, and the money supply is hard to define and control; adjustment to full employment can be slow and painful; a policy of waiting through a slump may be socially costly.

**Judgement.** Most modern policy-makers use ideas from both: demand management in a deep recession, a commitment to low inflation through monetary policy, and supply-side measures to improve market flexibility. Which view fits best depends on the circumstances: Keynesian ideas fit a deep slump with spare capacity, monetarist ideas fit an economy near full capacity facing inflation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that monetarists want no government"}
Monetarists want a limited role: controlling the money supply and providing the framework in which markets work. They do not deny a role for the state.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that Keynesians ignore inflation and money"}
Keynesians accept that demand beyond capacity causes inflation and that interest rates matter. They simply believe that in a slump demand management can raise output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting one school as always correct"}
Evaluation requires both views and a judgement based on the circumstances (spare capacity, inflation, the reliability of velocity).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the main features of each school; Paper 2 may use a short passage about a government's response to recession or inflation; Paper 3 essays ask you to compare the views on unemployment and inflation and to evaluate which better explains a given situation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: state the contrast on one issue. *Explain*: give the reasoning of the school (for example sticky wages). *Discuss* or *evaluate*: give both views, apply them to the situation in the question and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Keynesians stress aggregate demand, sticky wages and prices, involuntary unemployment and active fiscal policy.
- Monetarists stress flexible markets, a vertical long-run aggregate supply curve, the natural rate and control of the money supply.
- Monetarists see inflation as caused by excess money growth (MV = PT with V and T stable).
- Keynesians favour demand management; monetarists favour rules for money, limited government and supply-side reform.
- Neither is always right: the choice depends on spare capacity, the reliability of velocity and the speed of adjustment.
:::
