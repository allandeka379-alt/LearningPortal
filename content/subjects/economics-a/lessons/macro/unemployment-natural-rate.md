## Introduction

If the economy had enough jobs for everyone who wanted one, would unemployment be zero? Not quite. Some people will always be between jobs, some skills will always be in less demand than others, and some people will choose not to accept the wages that are on offer. Economists therefore separate unemployment that remains when the labour market is working normally from unemployment that arises because wages or demand are out of line. The distinction decides which policy works: raising demand cures one kind and may only cause inflation for the other.

You should know the types of unemployment (frictional, structural, cyclical, seasonal) and how the labour market determines the wage. This lesson is for Cambridge A Level.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Full employment"}
A situation in which everyone who is willing and able to work at the going wage can find a job, apart from those who are temporarily between jobs or who lack the skills or location the jobs need. It does not mean zero unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Equilibrium unemployment"}
Unemployment that exists when the labour market is in equilibrium: the frictional, structural and voluntary unemployment that remains even when the number of vacancies equals the number of people seeking work.
:::

:::definition{term="Disequilibrium unemployment"}
Unemployment that arises when the wage is above the equilibrium wage, or aggregate demand is too low, so that the supply of labour exceeds the demand for labour. It includes real-wage and demand-deficient unemployment.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural rate of unemployment"}
The rate of unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium (when there is no cyclical unemployment). It is also the rate at which inflation is stable, which is why it is also called the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment (NAIRU).
:::

:::definition{term="Voluntary and involuntary unemployment"}
**Voluntary** unemployment: people who choose not to work at the going wage (they are able but unwilling to accept the wage, or are searching for a better job). **Involuntary** unemployment: people who are willing and able to work at the going wage but cannot find jobs.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key relationship"}
Actual unemployment = natural rate (equilibrium unemployment) + disequilibrium (cyclical or real-wage) unemployment.
:::

## Equilibrium and disequilibrium in the labour market

Describe the labour market for a type of labour in words. The wage in US\$ per hour is on the vertical axis and the number of workers on the horizontal axis. The demand curve D, from the marginal revenue product of labour, slopes downward; the supply curve S slopes upward. They cross at the equilibrium wage and employment.

| Hourly wage (US\$) | Workers demanded | Workers supplied |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 10 | 1,000 | 600 |
| 12 | 800 | 800 |
| 14 | 600 | 1,000 |
| 16 | 400 | 1,200 |

:::worked-solution{title="Real-wage (disequilibrium) unemployment"}
1. Equilibrium: demand equals supply at a wage of **US\$12** with **800** workers employed.
2. Suppose that a union agreement or a legal minimum sets the wage at **US\$14**, above equilibrium.
3. At US\$14 firms demand only 600 workers, but 1,000 want to work: **400 workers are involuntarily unemployed**, an excess supply of labour.
4. Employment has fallen by 200 (a movement up the demand curve), while 200 more people have entered the market, attracted by the higher wage. All 400 are willing to work at US\$14 and cannot find a job, so their unemployment is involuntary.
5. This is **disequilibrium** unemployment, caused by the wage being held above its market level (also called classical or real-wage unemployment). Demand-deficient unemployment is another cause: a fall in demand shifts D to the left.
:::

Even at the equilibrium wage (US\$12), some of the 800 are in the middle of searching or have the wrong skills; that is **equilibrium unemployment**.

:::worked-solution{title="Splitting actual unemployment"}
A fictional economy has a labour force of 12,000 thousand, an unemployment rate of 8.5% and a natural rate of 6.0%.
1. Actual unemployment = 8.5% × 12,000 = **1,020 thousand**.
2. Natural (equilibrium) unemployment = 6.0% × 12,000 = **720 thousand**.
3. Disequilibrium (cyclical) unemployment = 1,020 − 720 = **300 thousand**, equal to 8.5% − 6.0% = **2.5 percentage points** of the labour force.
4. Policy to raise demand could bring back up to 300 thousand jobs; further expansion beyond that would create inflationary pressure rather than jobs, because the economy is at its natural rate.
:::

## Determinants of the natural rate

- **Labour-market flexibility and information:** easier matching of workers and vacancies lowers frictional unemployment.
- **Skills and mobility:** mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs raises structural unemployment.
- **Benefits:** generous and long-lasting benefits may lengthen search time (the incentive to accept a job falls), although they also help people find a better match.
- **Wage-setting:** strong trade unions, minimum wages and efficiency wages keep wages above the market level for some groups.
- **Employers' costs of hiring and firing:** high costs of dismissal and taxes on labour discourage hiring.
- **Past unemployment (hysteresis).**

:::definition{term="Hysteresis"}
The idea that the natural rate itself depends on the history of actual unemployment: after a long period of high cyclical unemployment, workers lose skills and contacts and become discouraged, and employers regard them as less employable, so the natural rate rises.
:::

For example, if a deep recession raises actual unemployment for years, the natural rate may rise from 5% to 7% in the recovery, and the economy reaches stable inflation at a higher level of unemployment than before. This is why a long slump can have permanent costs.

## Patterns and trends

Unemployment varies over time with the trade cycle, rising in recessions and falling in booms. It also varies between groups: youth unemployment is usually higher than adult unemployment, and unemployment is usually higher among people with lower skills, in some regions, and among people from minority groups. A rising share of the unemployed who have been out of work for a long time is a sign of structural problems and hysteresis.

## Mobility of labour

- **Geographical mobility:** the ability of workers to move to where jobs exist. Barriers: the cost and shortage of housing, family and community ties, lack of information about jobs elsewhere, the cost of travel and uncertainty.
- **Occupational mobility:** the ability to move between types of job. Barriers: specific skills, the cost and time of training, licensing and qualifications, age, and discrimination.

Low mobility raises structural unemployment and makes wage differentials last.

## Policies to reduce unemployment

| Policy | How it works | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand-side (lower interest rates, higher government spending, lower taxes) | Raises aggregate demand and so the demand for labour | Works for cyclical unemployment only; risks inflation and debt; time lags |
| Education and training | Improves skills, so reduces structural unemployment and raises productivity | Slow; costly; training may not match jobs |
| Better labour-market information and employment services | Cuts search time and frictional unemployment | Small effect on its own |
| Lower unemployment benefits (or limits on duration) | Raises the incentive to accept a job | Raises hardship and inequality; may not create jobs |
| Reform of minimum wages and union power | Lowers the wage floor, raising the demand for labour | Lower pay for the poorest; fairness issues |
| Help with moving and regional policy (subsidies, infrastructure, enterprise zones) | Raises geographical mobility or brings jobs to areas of high unemployment | Costly; tends to be slow |
| Lower employer costs (payroll taxes) | Encourages hiring | Cost to the government |

:::example{title="Evaluation: which policy is most effective?"}
The most suitable policy depends on the type of unemployment. If unemployment is mainly cyclical, raising aggregate demand is quickest. If it is structural or the natural rate has risen because of hysteresis, more spending on demand only raises inflation, so retraining and mobility policies are needed, even though they are slow and expensive. Reform of benefits and wages may reduce unemployment but at a cost to fairness. Because the types are hard to identify, and the benefits of each policy take time, governments often combine policies and should monitor the results.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that full employment means zero unemployment"}
Full employment allows for frictional and structural unemployment: it is reached when there is no cyclical unemployment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using demand-side policy to cure structural unemployment"}
Raising demand does not create the right skills in the right place. Beyond the natural rate it increases inflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing voluntary with involuntary unemployment"}
A person who is willing to work at the going wage and cannot find a job is involuntarily unemployed. A person who refuses jobs at the going wage is voluntarily unemployed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 3 multiple-choice items ask you to identify the type of unemployment from a labour-market description or table and to read the effects of a minimum wage. Paper 4 data-response questions use unemployment data with policies, and essays ask whether supply-side or demand-side policy is better for reducing unemployment.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: define both and give the cause of each. *Calculate*: use actual − natural = cyclical. *Analyse*: describe the labour-market diagram in words: the wage, the quantity demanded and supplied, the gap. *Evaluate*: match the policy to the type, weigh cost, time and side effects, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Full employment does not mean zero unemployment; it means there is no disequilibrium (cyclical) unemployment.
- Equilibrium unemployment (the natural rate) is frictional, structural and voluntary; disequilibrium unemployment arises from wages above equilibrium or low demand.
- The natural rate is the rate consistent with stable inflation (NAIRU) and depends on flexibility, skills, benefits, wage-setting and past unemployment (hysteresis).
- Geographical and occupational immobility raise structural unemployment.
- Demand-side policy suits cyclical unemployment; supply-side policy suits structural unemployment, but it is slow and costly.
:::
