## Introduction

Every government wants its economy to grow, and every household feels the result: more jobs, more goods in the shops, more tax revenue for schools and clinics. Yet growth figures are often misread. A headline that "GDP rose by 10%" tells you nothing until you know whether prices rose too, whether the population grew faster than output, and who received the extra income. This lesson shows how growth is defined and measured, what causes it, and what it costs as well as what it brings.

You should already know the circular flow, the three methods of measuring national income, and how a price index or GDP deflator converts nominal values into real values. If these are rusty, revisit the lessons on national income and on price indices first.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic growth"}
An increase in the volume of goods and services an economy produces over time, measured by the percentage rise in **real** GDP (GDP adjusted for price changes).
:::

:::definition{term="Real GDP per head"}
Real GDP divided by the population. It shows average output per person and is the usual measure of changes in average living standards.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic development"}
A wider, qualitative improvement in people's lives: income, health, education, freedom from poverty and access to opportunities. Growth is usually necessary for development but does not guarantee it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulae"}
Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator × 100.
Growth rate (%) = (real GDP in the new year − real GDP in the old year) ÷ real GDP in the old year × 100.
Real GDP per head = real GDP ÷ population.
Average annual (compound) growth over n years = (final value ÷ starting value)^(1/n) − 1.
:::

## Measuring growth

**Nominal GDP** is measured at the prices of the year in which the output is produced, so it rises when prices rise even if output is unchanged. **Real GDP** removes the effect of price changes by valuing output at the prices of a base year, using the GDP deflator. Only real GDP measures the change in the volume of output, so economic growth is always measured with real figures.

The table gives illustrative data for a fictional economy, Zandia.

| Year | Nominal GDP (US\$ million) | GDP deflator (2021 = 100) | Population (million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2021 | 8,000 | 100 | 12.0 |
| 2022 | 8,820 | 105 | 12.3 |
| 2023 | 9,702 | 110 | 12.6 |

:::worked-solution{title="Nominal growth, real growth and growth per head"}
1. Real GDP 2022 = 8,820 ÷ 105 × 100 = **US\$8,400 million**. Real GDP 2023 = 9,702 ÷ 110 × 100 = **US\$8,820 million**.
2. Nominal growth in 2023 = (9,702 − 8,820) ÷ 8,820 × 100 = **10%**. Real growth in 2023 = (8,820 − 8,400) ÷ 8,400 × 100 = **5%**. Half of the nominal rise was only higher prices.
3. Real GDP per head 2022 = 8,400 ÷ 12.3 = US\$682.93 million per million people, that is **US\$682.93** per person. In 2023 it is 8,820 ÷ 12.6 = **US\$700.00**.
4. Growth of real GDP per head in 2023 = (700.00 − 682.93) ÷ 682.93 × 100 = **2.5%**, only half of real GDP growth, because the population rose by 2.4% as well.
5. Average annual real growth from 2021 to 2023 = (8,820 ÷ 8,000)^(1/2) − 1 = **5.0%**.
:::

A useful approximation: growth of real GDP per head ≈ growth of real GDP − growth of the population. With real GDP growth of 6% and population growth of 2.5% the exact answer is (1.06 ÷ 1.025) − 1 = 3.4%, close to 6 − 2.5 = 3.5%.

:::example{title="Reading a growth headline"}
A report says that output in a fictional country grew by 12% while the GDP deflator rose by 9%. Real growth is roughly 12 − 9 = 3% if the 12% is nominal; if the 12% is already real, there is no need to adjust. Always ask whether a figure is nominal or real, and whether it is per head.
:::

### Why actual growth is not the same as better living standards

GDP per head is an average, so it can rise while many people become no better off. It excludes unpaid work and much of the informal sector (although estimates are often added), ignores leisure and quality of life, ignores the damage done to the environment, and says nothing about how income is shared. Comparing countries also depends on exchange rates or on purchasing power adjustments. For these reasons growth is only one indicator of well-being, which is why development indicators exist.

## Causes of growth

Growth comes from an increase in the **quantity** or **quality** of factors of production, or from using them more productively.

1. **More factors:** a larger labour force (population growth, migration, a higher participation rate, for example more women in paid work); more capital through investment; new land or natural resources (a mineral discovery); more enterprise.
2. **Better quality:** education and training (human capital) and better health raise labour productivity; new machinery embodies better technology.
3. **Higher productivity:** technology and innovation, better management, specialisation and economies of scale, and more efficient allocation as resources move from low- to high-productivity uses (for example from subsistence farming to higher-value horticulture or manufacturing).
4. **Institutions and conditions:** secure property rights, the rule of law, stable money, working infrastructure (roads, power, water), openness to trade and investment, and political stability make investment and enterprise worthwhile.

Investment links several of these. A higher share of national income used for investment raises the capital stock and, if the capital is productive, output. The funds come from domestic saving, foreign direct investment, borrowing or aid. Growth through more inputs eventually meets diminishing returns, whereas growth through technology and better skills can continue.

## Consequences of growth

:::example{title="Benefits and costs of growth"}
**Benefits.** Higher output per head raises average incomes and consumption. Higher incomes raise tax revenue, so the government can spend more on health, education and infrastructure without raising tax rates. Growth creates jobs and reduces unemployment and poverty. It can improve the balance of payments if it rests on exports.

**Costs.** Fast growth can raise inflation if demand grows faster than capacity, and may increase imports and a current account deficit. It can use up non-renewable resources and damage the environment through pollution, deforestation or climate-changing emissions, imposing external costs on future generations. Inequality can widen if gains go mainly to owners of capital or to one region, so average incomes rise while the poor gain little. Rapid change can also raise structural unemployment as old industries decline.
:::

Whether growth is on balance beneficial depends on its source and distribution: growth based on investment in skills and clean technology, shared widely, brings more benefit and fewer costs than growth based on exhausting a resource.

## Worked example: comparing two economies

Country A's real GDP grows at 4% and its population at 1%; Country B's real GDP grows at 6% and its population at 3.5%.

:::worked-solution{title="Which grows faster per head?"}
1. Country A: real GDP per head grows by about 1.04 ÷ 1.01 − 1 = **2.97%**.
2. Country B: 1.06 ÷ 1.035 − 1 = **2.42%**.
3. Although B has the faster growth of total output, A's average income per person rises faster. A statement about living standards must use per head figures.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the definition, measurement with real GDP and real GDP per head, the causes and the consequences. ZIMSEC (Form 6) also expects the distinction between growth and development to be explained in the same answer; Cambridge treats development in a separate topic and, at AS level, asks for growth in terms of real GDP and the benefits and costs. Output gaps, the cycle and potential growth are a separate Cambridge A Level lesson.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using nominal GDP to measure growth"}
A rise in nominal GDP may be all inflation. Divide by the deflator first, then calculate the percentage change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing growth with development"}
Growth is a quantitative rise in output. Development includes health, education and distribution. A country can grow without developing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using total GDP to talk about living standards"}
If the population grows faster than output, average income falls. Use real GDP per head.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and simple calculations; Paper 2 gives a table of nominal GDP and a deflator and then asks for real growth and a short discussion; Paper 3 essays ask whether growth is always desirable. For Cambridge, Paper 1 contains short calculations and Paper 2 uses growth data in the data response and in AS essays.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: deflate first and show the working. *Explain*: link each cause to output through factor quantity, quality or productivity. *Assess* or *discuss*: give benefits and costs, say who gains, and reach a judgement that depends on the source and the sharing of growth.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economic growth is the rise in real GDP; real GDP per head is the better guide to average living standards.
- Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ deflator × 100; growth rates are always calculated from real figures.
- Growth comes from more or better factors, higher productivity (technology, skills) and supportive institutions.
- Benefits include higher incomes, jobs and tax revenue; costs include inflation, environmental damage and unequal gains.
- Growth is not development: health, education and distribution also matter.
:::
