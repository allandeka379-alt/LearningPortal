## Introduction

When we say that an economy grew by 4% last year, or that one country is richer than another, we are using **national income statistics**. They are the main measure of the total output of an economy and the basis for comparing living standards, planning government policy and judging whether an economy is growing. They are collected by a national statistics agency (in Zimbabwe, ZIMSTAT, together with the Ministry of Finance and the Reserve Bank) using surveys, tax records and business returns.

You should know the circular flow of income, the difference between a flow and a stock, and the meaning of nominal and real values. This lesson explains what is counted, the three ways of measuring it, and how to move between the different measures. Real values and price indices are in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="National income"}
The total value of the goods and services produced by an economy in a period of time (usually a year), which is also the total income earned by its factors of production and the total spending on its output.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross domestic product (GDP)"}
The total value of all final goods and services produced **within a country's borders** in a year, whoever owns the factors of production.
:::

:::definition{term="Gross national income (GNI)"}
GDP plus **net primary income from abroad** (income earned by the country's residents from abroad minus income sent abroad by foreign residents). ZIMSEC calls this **gross national product (GNP)**.
:::

:::definition{term="Net national income (NNI)"}
GNI minus **depreciation** (capital consumption), the value of capital used up during the year. ZIMSEC calls this **net national product (NNP)**.
:::

:::definition{term="Value added"}
The value of a firm's output minus the value of the intermediate goods and services it bought from other firms.
:::

:::definition{term="Basic prices and market prices"}
**Market prices** are the prices paid by buyers, including taxes on products (such as sales tax) and excluding subsidies. **Basic prices** are the prices received by producers: market prices minus taxes on products plus subsidies on products.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The adjustments"}
GDP at basic prices = GDP at market prices − taxes on products + subsidies on products.
GNI = GDP + net primary income from abroad.
NNI = GNI − depreciation.
National income per head = national income ÷ population.
:::

## Three approaches to measurement

The same total can be measured in three ways, because the value of output is paid out as incomes and spent by someone.

1. **Output (production) approach.** Add up the **value added** at each stage of production in all sectors (agriculture, mining, manufacturing, services). Only **final** goods are counted, or equivalently only value added, so that intermediate goods are not counted twice (double counting).
2. **Income approach.** Add up all factor incomes earned in the production of output: wages and salaries, rent, interest and profits (and mixed income of the self-employed), then adjust for depreciation, taxes and subsidies.
3. **Expenditure approach.** Add up all spending on final goods and services: **GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)**, where C is household consumption, I is investment, G is government spending, X is exports and M is imports.

:::worked-solution{title="Value added and double counting (illustrative data)"}
Wheat is grown, milled and baked into bread.

| Stage | Value of sales (US\$) | Cost of inputs bought (US\$) | Value added (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Farmer (grain) | 40 | 0 | 40 |
| Miller (flour) | 70 | 40 | 30 |
| Baker (bread sold to consumers) | 120 | 70 | 50 |

1. Value added = value of sales − cost of inputs. Farmer 40 − 0 = 40; miller 70 − 40 = 30; baker 120 − 70 = 50.
2. Total value added = 40 + 30 + 50 = **US\$120**, which equals the value of the **final** bread (US\$120).
3. If we added all sales (40 + 70 + 120 = US\$230) we would count the grain and flour more than once. That is **double counting**, and the output approach avoids it.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="National accounts calculation (illustrative data)"}
Figures for a fictional "Country X" in US\$ million, with a population of 5 million.

| Item | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Consumption (C) | 6,400 |
| Investment (I) | 1,900 |
| Government spending (G) | 2,100 |
| Exports (X) | 2,600 |
| Imports (M) | 2,900 |
| **GDP at market prices** | **10,100** |

Taxes on products are US\$1,100 million, subsidies on products US\$200 million, net primary income from abroad is −US\$350 million (more income is sent abroad than received) and depreciation is US\$900 million.

1. GDP at market prices = C + I + G + (X − M). Domestic spending: 6,400 + 1,900 + 2,100 = 10,400. Net exports = 2,600 − 2,900 = −300. GDP = 10,400 − 300 = **10,100**.
2. GDP at basic prices = 10,100 − 1,100 + 200 = **9,200**.
3. GNI (at market prices) = 10,100 + (−350) = **9,750**.
4. NNI = 9,750 − 900 = **8,850**.
5. GDP per head = 10,100 million ÷ 5 million = **US\$2,020**. NNI per head = 8,850 ÷ 5 = **US\$1,770**.
6. GNI is lower than GDP because the country pays out more income to foreign owners of capital and labour than it receives. Where large foreign-owned mines or farms exist, GNI may be well below GDP.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require GDP and the three approaches to measuring national income (ZIMSEC detail: the output, income and expenditure approaches in full). ZIMSEC uses **GNP** and **NNP**; Cambridge uses **GNI** and **NNI** and expects the adjustment from **market prices to basic prices** and from **gross to net** values. Both state the limitations of the statistics.
:::

## Uses of national income data

- Measuring **economic growth** (the change in real GDP; see the lesson on real values).
- **Comparing living standards** across countries and over time, using per capita figures.
- Showing the **structure** of the economy by sector.
- Planning **government policy** and budgets, and comparing countries' ability to pay taxes or service debt.

## Limitations

National income statistics are an imperfect guide to welfare. Points to remember:

1. **The informal economy.** Much activity in small trading, farming and services is not recorded or taxed, so GDP is understated, especially in developing economies.
2. **Non-marketed output.** Subsistence farming, housework and unpaid care are mostly not counted (or only estimated). The more such output, the more GDP understates production.
3. **Illegal activity** (for example smuggling) is not recorded, although it generates income.
4. **Quality and variety.** A new product or better quality adds to welfare but may not show in the figures.
5. **Distribution.** The average (per capita) hides inequality: GDP per head may rise while most people gain nothing.
6. **Externalities and sustainability.** Pollution, congestion and depletion of resources are not subtracted, while spending to repair damage adds to GDP.
7. **Leisure and working conditions** are ignored, as are health, education and happiness.
8. **Price comparison across countries.** Exchange rates may not reflect purchasing power, so international comparisons need adjustments (purchasing power parity).
9. **Accuracy and revisions.** Data may be collected from incomplete surveys and are revised later.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
GDP per head is a useful starting point for comparing living standards, because it measures the average value of output per person. However, it understates production in an economy with a large informal sector, ignores the distribution of income and takes no account of environmental damage. A country with a lower GDP per head may therefore have a higher standard of living than one with a higher figure if its income is more equally shared and its services are better. For a fuller comparison, economists also use other indicators, such as life expectancy and education, in addition to GDP.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding up all sales to find GDP"}
Counting the value of intermediate goods as well as final goods leads to double counting. Use final goods only, or value added.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing GDP and GNI"}
GDP counts output **within** the country's borders; GNI counts income **of the country's residents** wherever it is earned. Add net primary income from abroad to GDP to get GNI.
:::

:::mistake{title="Subtracting depreciation to get GDP"}
Depreciation is subtracted from GNI to give NNI. GDP is a gross measure and includes it. Taxes on products are subtracted, and subsidies added, to move from market prices to basic prices.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and simple calculations; Paper 2 gives national accounts data to calculate GDP, GNP and NNP, and to comment on limitations; Paper 3 essays ask whether GDP measures welfare. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the definitions and the adjustments in multiple choice; Paper 2 includes a calculation from national income data and an evaluation of GDP as a measure of living standards.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: GDP, GNI, NNI. *Calculate*: use C + I + G + (X − M) and the adjustments in order. *Explain*: why the three approaches give the same total; why GNI differs from GDP. *Assess/Evaluate*: limitations of the data and whether per capita GDP shows living standards, with a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- National income is the value of output, income and spending of an economy in a year; GDP = output within the borders.
- GNI (ZIMSEC: GNP) = GDP + net primary income from abroad; NNI (NNP) = GNI − depreciation; basic prices = market prices − taxes on products + subsidies.
- The output (value added), income and expenditure (C + I + G + (X − M)) approaches measure the same total.
- Per capita figures divide by population; real values are needed to measure growth.
- Limitations include the informal sector, non-marketed output, distribution, externalities and data quality.
:::
