## Introduction

When the Zimbabwe tobacco season ends and farmers receive their payments, shops in Karoi and Harare see more customers. When the government cuts its spending, contractors lay off workers. When foreign demand for minerals falls, mines and the businesses that supply them suffer. These are all changes in **aggregate demand**: the total demand for the goods and services produced in the economy. Aggregate demand is one half of the model (the other is aggregate supply) that economists use to analyse output, prices and employment for the whole economy.

You should know the components of national income, the circular flow, the difference between a movement along a curve and a shift, and the difference between nominal and real values. This lesson defines aggregate demand, examines the components and their determinants, explains the shape of the AD curve and sets out the causes of shifts. Consumption and investment functions are covered in a later lesson, and aggregate supply and equilibrium in the next two.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Aggregate demand (AD)"}
The total planned spending on an economy's output at different price levels, in a given period: **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)**.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumption (C)"}
Household spending on goods and services (the largest component of AD in most economies).
:::

:::definition{term="Investment (I)"}
Spending by firms on capital goods (machinery, buildings, equipment) and on additions to stocks, together with new housing. It does not include the purchase of financial assets.
:::

:::definition{term="Government spending (G)"}
Spending by central and local government on goods and services (public services, infrastructure and wages of government employees), but not transfer payments such as pensions.
:::

:::definition{term="Net exports (X − M)"}
Spending on exports by foreigners minus spending by residents on imports.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The AD curve"}
The AD curve shows the relationship between the **general price level** (vertical axis) and **real output demanded** (horizontal axis, real GDP). It slopes downward: a lower price level means a higher level of real aggregate demand. A change in the price level is a **movement along** the AD curve; a change in any other determinant **shifts** the curve.
:::

## The components of AD and their determinants

| Component | Main determinants |
| --- | --- |
| **Consumption (C)** | Disposable income (income minus tax); interest rates (borrowing cost, reward to saving); wealth; confidence and expectations; availability of credit; distribution of income; age structure of the population |
| **Investment (I)** | Interest rates (cost of borrowing); expected profits and business confidence; changes in demand for output; technology; corporation tax and incentives; availability of finance |
| **Government spending (G)** | Government's policy aims, tax revenue and borrowing, the economic cycle, political priorities |
| **Net exports (X − M)** | Incomes abroad (for exports) and at home (for imports); the exchange rate; relative prices at home and abroad; trade barriers; quality and non-price competitiveness |

## Why the AD curve slopes downward

AD is not a simple sum of the individual demand curves for goods. Its downward slope has three standard explanations:

1. **Wealth (real balances) effect.** A lower price level raises the real value of money and other fixed-value assets that people hold, so households feel wealthier and spend more.
2. **Interest rate effect.** A lower price level reduces the demand for money, which tends to lower interest rates; lower interest rates raise investment and consumption.
3. **International trade (exchange rate) effect.** A lower domestic price level makes the country's goods cheaper relative to foreign ones, so exports rise and imports fall, which raises net exports.

:::worked-solution{title="Movement along the AD curve (illustrative data)"}
A fictional economy has the following AD schedule (US\$ million, real).

| Price level (index) | Real AD before (US\$ million) | Real AD after (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 80 | 12,400 | 13,400 |
| 90 | 11,700 | 12,700 |
| 100 | 11,000 | 12,000 |
| 110 | 10,400 | 11,400 |
| 120 | 9,900 | 10,900 |

1. On the "before" curve, when the price level rises from 100 to 110, real AD falls from 11,000 to 10,400: a **movement along** the curve (up and to the left). The change was caused by the price level.
2. The "after" column shows 1,000 more real AD at every price level: the whole curve has **shifted to the right**. This was caused by something other than the price level, such as higher consumer confidence or higher government spending.
3. At the price level of 100, real AD rises from 11,000 to 12,000: an increase of 1,000, or 9.1% (1,000 ÷ 11,000 × 100).
:::

## Calculating AD and its components

:::worked-solution{title="Components and a change in AD (illustrative data)"}
In Year 1 a fictional economy has (US\$ million) C = 7,200, I = 1,800, G = 2,400, X = 2,500 and M = 2,900. In Year 2 investment rises by 10% and exports fall by 8%; other components are unchanged.

| Component (US\$ million) | Year 1 | Year 2 | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Consumption (C) | 7,200 | 7,200 | 0 |
| Investment (I) | 1,800 | 1,980 | 180 |
| Government spending (G) | 2,400 | 2,400 | 0 |
| Exports (X) | 2,500 | 2,300 | −200 |
| Imports (M) | 2,900 | 2,900 | 0 |
| **AD = C + I + G + (X − M)** | **11,000** | **10,980** | −20 |

1. Year 1: AD = 7,200 + 1,800 + 2,400 + (2,500 − 2,900) = 11,400 − 400 = **11,000**.
2. Investment: 1,800 × 1.10 = **1,980** (a rise of 180). Exports: 2,500 × 0.92 = **2,300** (a fall of 200).
3. Year 2: AD = 7,200 + 1,980 + 2,400 + (2,300 − 2,900) = 11,580 − 600 = **10,980**.
4. Change in AD = 10,980 − 11,000 = **−20** (−0.2%). The rise in investment was slightly outweighed by the fall in exports.
5. Shares of AD in Year 1: C = 7,200 ÷ 11,000 = **65.5%**; I = **16.4%**; G = **21.8%**; net exports = **−3.6%**. Consumption is the largest component.
:::

## Causes of a shift in the AD curve

An **increase** in AD (a shift to the right) is caused by, for example:

- Higher consumer confidence or wealth, a fall in taxes on households, a rise in real incomes, cheaper or easier credit.
- Lower interest rates (raising consumption and investment).
- Higher business confidence or expected profits.
- A rise in government spending.
- A rise in foreign incomes, or a fall in the exchange rate, raising exports and reducing imports.

A **decrease** in AD is caused by the opposite: higher taxes, higher interest rates, lower confidence, cuts in government spending, a recession in trading partners, or a rise in the exchange rate.

:::example{title="Applying the AD model"}
Suppose the central bank of a fictional country raises interest rates. Borrowing becomes more expensive, so firms postpone investment and households cut spending on goods bought on credit, while saving becomes more attractive. C and I fall at every price level and the AD curve shifts to the left. Real output demanded falls at the current price level, with consequences for output, employment and prices that are analysed with aggregate supply.
:::

## Evaluation points

- **Size of the effect** depends on how much each component changes and on its share in AD (a change in consumption matters more because it is the largest component).
- **Time lags**: investment responds slowly to interest rates; consumption responds faster.
- **Imports** reduce the effect: if extra income is spent on imports it does not raise domestic AD.
- **Expectations and confidence** may amplify or dampen a change.
- **The multiplier** may magnify the initial change (covered later).

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the definition of AD, its components, determinants, the reasons for the downward-sloping curve (Cambridge: wealth, interest rate and exchange-rate effects), shifts and movements. Cambridge says that detailed knowledge of the components is not required at AS; ZIMSEC (Form 6) expects the explanation of the determinants of each component.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Explaining the downward slope like a demand curve for a single good"}
The AD curve slopes down because of changes in wealth, interest rates and net exports when the **general price level** changes, not because of substitution of one good for another.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a price change as a shift of AD"}
A change in the price level moves the economy along AD. Only changes in other determinants (confidence, taxes, interest rates, foreign income) shift the curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including transfer payments in G"}
Government spending in AD covers purchases of goods and services. Pensions and benefits become part of consumption only when households spend them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks about the components and shifts; Paper 2 gives data on C, I, G, X and M to calculate AD and its changes; Paper 3 essays ask about the effect of policy on AD. Cambridge Paper 1 tests shifts and movements in multiple choice; Paper 2 asks for explanation of the AD curve's shape and of shifts using a diagram described in words.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: say how the determinant changes a component of AD. *Calculate*: add C + I + G + (X − M) and compare years. *Analyse*: trace the chain from a cause (for example an interest rate rise) to AD. *Distinguish*: movement along against shift.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- AD = C + I + G + (X − M): total planned spending on the economy's output at each price level.
- Each component has its own determinants (income, interest rates, confidence, tax, the exchange rate, foreign income).
- The AD curve slopes downward because of the wealth effect, the interest rate effect and the international trade effect.
- A change in the price level is a movement along the AD curve; a change in any other determinant shifts it.
- Increases in AD come from higher confidence, lower taxes or interest rates, higher government spending and exports; decreases from the opposite.
:::
