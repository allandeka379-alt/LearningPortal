## Introduction

A farmer in Mashonaland sells tobacco that is auctioned in Harare and shipped to buyers on other continents; her child uses a phone assembled from parts made in several countries; a young graduate in Bulawayo works online for a client abroad; a clothing shop sells imported second-hand and new garments. All of these show **globalisation**: the growing integration of economies through trade, finance, investment, technology and the movement of people and ideas. Globalisation has lifted incomes in many places and caused painful change in others. This lesson explains why it has happened and how to weigh its gains and losses.

You should know the case for free trade, what foreign direct investment is and what a multinational company does.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Globalisation"}
The process by which national economies become increasingly integrated and interdependent through growing flows of goods, services, capital, technology, information and people across borders.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade openness"}
A measure of how integrated a country is in world trade: openness = (exports + imports) ÷ GDP × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Offshoring"}
The relocation of production or services by a firm to another country, usually to lower costs.
:::

## Causes of globalisation

1. **Technology and communications.** The internet, mobile phones and mobile money allow firms to coordinate production worldwide, advertise and sell across borders, and let workers sell services remotely.
2. **Lower transport costs.** Containerisation, larger ships and cheaper air freight reduce the cost of moving goods, so it becomes profitable to produce far from the final consumer.
3. **Trade liberalisation.** Falling tariffs and quotas through agreements under the GATT and the WTO, regional trading blocs and bilateral agreements make imports cheaper and exports easier.
4. **Capital mobility.** The removal of controls on capital flows and the growth of international banking allow money to flow quickly to where returns are highest.
5. **Multinational companies (MNCs).** Firms organise production across several countries, locate each stage where it is cheapest and spread technology and management methods.
6. **The growth of emerging economies** and the opening of former closed economies increased the number of countries taking part in world markets.

## Measuring openness

:::worked-solution{title="Trade openness"}
A fictional country has exports of US\$3,200 million, imports of US\$3,600 million and GDP of US\$8,000 million.
1. Openness = (3,200 + 3,600) ÷ 8,000 × 100 = **85%**.
2. Ten years earlier, trade was US\$4,030 million and GDP US\$6,500 million: openness = 4,030 ÷ 6,500 × 100 = **62%**.
3. Openness rose by 23 percentage points, showing greater integration into world trade. It also means that the economy is more exposed to changes in world demand and prices.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of lower transport costs"}
A manufactured good costs US\$80 at the factory. Shipping and insurance add US\$20 to reach the buyer. A fall in transport costs of 40% cuts the extra cost to 0.60 × 20 = **US\$12**.
1. Delivered price falls from US\$100 to **US\$92**, a fall of 8%.
2. A lower delivered price raises the quantity bought, so trade grows.
:::

## Consequences

| Group | Possible gains | Possible losses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Economies** | Larger markets and scale economies; specialisation by comparative advantage; competition and lower prices; access to technology and capital; higher growth | Exposure to shocks (financial crises, falling commodity prices); structural unemployment in industries that lose out; tax competition; loss of policy independence |
| **Workers** | New jobs in export industries and MNCs; higher wages in expanding sectors; cheaper goods | Job losses and wage pressure for low-skilled workers in industries facing import competition or offshoring; insecure work; migration of skilled workers (brain drain) |
| **Consumers** | Lower prices, wider choice | Dependence on imports; standards of quality or safety may vary |
| **Environment** | Spread of cleaner technology; efficient use of resources; funds for environmental policy | Rising emissions from transport and production; pressure on resources; firms may locate where rules are weakest (the "pollution haven" argument, whose evidence is mixed) |
| **Developing countries** | Export markets, FDI, technology transfer, remittances, jobs in manufacturing and services | Dependence on a few commodities or on MNCs; unequal bargaining power; benefits concentrated among the better-off or in some regions; local firms may not compete; terms of trade may worsen |

:::example{title="Winners and losers"}
A textile factory in a high-income country closes when production moves abroad. Consumers gain cheaper clothing, and workers in the new location gain jobs and incomes, but the dismissed workers lose income and may be unemployed for a long time if they cannot retrain. National income may rise overall, but the gains and losses are not shared evenly, so governments must decide whether to compensate losers through retraining and support. The balance depends on how well institutions help workers adjust.
:::

## Assessing globalisation

Whether globalisation is "good" depends on who is asked, over what period and under what policies:

- It raises average incomes and has been linked to rapid growth and falling poverty in several countries that opened to trade and investment, though the policies they followed differed and economists disagree on how much of the growth came from openness itself.
- It can widen inequality within countries, and gains between countries have been uneven, with some countries (particularly those exporting only a few primary products) benefiting little.
- The risks (shocks, dependence) can be reduced by diversification, good institutions, investment in skills, social protection and regulation of capital flows and the environment.

A balanced assessment states both gains and losses, specifies for whom and judges using the circumstances described.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating globalisation as the same as free trade"}
Globalisation includes trade but also capital flows, technology, migration and the spread of ideas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming that everyone gains or that everyone loses"}
Gains and losses are unevenly shared. Say who gains, who loses, and why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explanation"}
For each cause, show the mechanism: for example, lower transport costs reduce delivered prices, which raises trade.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 3 contains multiple-choice questions on causes and measures of globalisation, and Paper 4 essays ask you to assess its effects on developing countries or on workers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: link each cause to a change in costs, prices or flows. *Assess*: set out gains and losses for different groups, then reach a judgement that depends on the circumstances (policies, institutions, the structure of the economy).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Globalisation is the growing integration of economies through trade, capital, technology, information and migration.
- Causes: technology and communications, lower transport costs, trade liberalisation, capital mobility, MNCs and the rise of emerging economies.
- Openness = (exports + imports) ÷ GDP × 100.
- Gains: scale, competition, lower prices, technology, growth; losses: exposure to shocks, job losses, inequality, environmental pressure.
- Effects differ between groups and countries, so judgements must be supported by circumstances.
:::
