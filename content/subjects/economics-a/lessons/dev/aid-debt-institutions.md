## Introduction

A new clinic funded by a foreign government, a road built with a loan from an international bank, a payment to relieve the effects of a flood: these are all ways in which resources move from richer to poorer countries outside normal trade. But money given or lent has effects, positive and negative. Loans must be repaid with interest, and a country that borrows too much may find that debt payments crowd out spending on schools and clinics. This lesson explains aid, external debt and the institutions that lend, and trains you to assess their effects without taking sides.

You should know the components of the balance of payments and the meaning of GDP and exports.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign aid"}
Resources transferred to a country on concessional terms (as grants, or loans with lower interest rates and longer repayment periods than market loans) with the aim of promoting development or relieving suffering.
:::

:::definition{term="External debt and debt service"}
**External debt** is the total owed by a country's government and residents to foreign lenders. **Debt service** is the payment of interest plus repayments of principal in a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Debt service ratio"}
Debt service payments as a percentage of export earnings in a year: debt service ratio = debt service ÷ export earnings × 100.
:::

## Forms of aid

| Form | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Grants** | Gifts that need not be repaid |
| **Concessional loans** | Loans with low interest and long repayment periods (they include a grant element) |
| **Bilateral aid** | From one government directly to another |
| **Multilateral aid** | Through an international organisation (for example the World Bank, UN agencies) |
| **Tied aid** | Conditional on spending the money on goods or services from the donor country |
| **Technical assistance** | Experts, training and know-how rather than money |
| **Emergency (humanitarian) aid** | Food, shelter and medicine after disasters, droughts or conflict |
| **Non-governmental organisation (NGO) aid** | From charities and private organisations |

## Why aid is given, and what it does

**Reasons.** Humanitarian concern; to fill the savings gap and the foreign exchange gap that limit growth; to build infrastructure, health and education; and also political, strategic and commercial motives of donors.

**Possible benefits.** Higher investment and growth; better health and education; relief in emergencies; technology transfer.

**Possible problems.** Dependence on donors and uncertain future flows; tied aid may be spent on more expensive goods from the donor; poorly designed projects or corruption may waste funds; aid may reduce incentives to raise taxes and may strengthen the exchange rate in a way that harms exporters; donors' priorities may differ from the recipient's.

**Evidence and balance.** Studies of the effect of aid on growth give mixed results. Supporters argue that well-targeted aid for health and infrastructure has saved lives and raised growth. Critics argue that large flows can weaken accountability to citizens and have not raised growth in all cases. Both views can be supported by examples, so use the characteristics of the particular programme in an answer.

:::worked-solution{title="Value of tied aid"}
A grant of US\$50 million must be spent on equipment from the donor's suppliers, whose prices are 25% higher than world prices for the same equipment.
1. At world prices the grant would buy US\$50 million of equipment.
2. Tied to the donor's suppliers it buys only 50 ÷ 1.25 = **US\$40 million** worth of equipment (measured at world prices).
3. The real value of the aid is reduced by US\$10 million, or **20%**.
:::

## Trade and investment links

Developing countries also receive resources through trade (exports earn foreign exchange), **foreign direct investment** and remittances. Many economists argue that access to markets and a stable environment for investment matter at least as much as aid, because trade and investment are larger and more reliable flows and are not tied to donor conditions.

## External debt

**Causes.** Borrowing to finance deficits on the current account or government budget; falls in export prices that reduce earnings; rises in world interest rates on loans with variable rates; a fall in the exchange rate, which raises the home-currency value of foreign-currency debt; poor use of borrowed funds; and a tendency to borrow when prices are high and be left with the repayments when they fall.

**Consequences.** Debt service takes a large share of export earnings and government revenue, leaving less for health, education and investment ("debt overhang"); less foreign exchange for imports; pressure on the exchange rate; reduced creditworthiness and higher borrowing costs; and possible need for debt rescheduling or relief.

:::worked-solution{title="Debt service ratio"}
A fictional country pays US\$420 million of interest and principal in a year and earns US\$2,100 million from exports.
1. Debt service ratio = 420 ÷ 2,100 × 100 = **20%**.
2. One fifth of export earnings goes to foreign creditors before any other imports are bought.
3. If export earnings fall 25% to US\$1,575 million and debt service is unchanged, the ratio rises to 420 ÷ 1,575 × 100 = **26.7%**.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Debt and interest rates"}
External debt is US\$6,000 million, and the GDP is US\$10,000 million, so debt is 60% of GDP. A loan of US\$5,000 million has a variable interest rate that rises by 2 percentage points. The extra annual interest = 0.02 × 5,000 = **US\$100 million**.
:::

**Remedies.** Debt rescheduling (longer repayment), debt relief (cancelling part of the debt), improving export earnings, using loans for productive investment, and better debt management.

## The IMF and the World Bank

:::keyfact{title="Roles (described neutrally)"}
**The International Monetary Fund (IMF)** promotes international monetary cooperation and stability; monitors economies (surveillance); and lends, mainly for **balance of payments and short- to medium-term macroeconomic problems**. Loans usually come with an agreed programme of policies (conditionality), such as budget measures, monetary policy and reforms.
**The World Bank** provides loans, grants and advice for **long-term development**: infrastructure, health, education and poverty reduction. Its lending is generally for specific projects or sectors.
:::

**Arguments in favour of their programmes:** they provide finance when markets will not lend; they encourage policies that stabilise inflation and the exchange rate; their approval can attract other lenders and investors; they provide advice and technical assistance.

**Arguments against:** conditions may impose cuts in public spending that fall on the poor and reduce growth in the short run; the same policy package may not suit every country; governments may feel that the conditions limit their independence; and borrowing from the institutions adds to debt. Views differ and the evidence for particular programmes is mixed.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
Whether an IMF-supported programme helps a country depends on the cause of the crisis, how well the policies fit the country's circumstances, how strongly the government supports them and how the costs and benefits are shared. A programme that restores low inflation and a stable exchange rate can help growth in the medium term, but if it requires deep cuts in health and education spending without protecting the poor, the short-run cost may be large.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cambridge only"}
This concept is Cambridge A Level only. Describe the institutions factually, state arguments on both sides and avoid claims about particular countries that you cannot support from the data in the question.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all aid as a gift"}
Much aid is in the form of loans that must be repaid; tied aid also reduces its real value.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the IMF and the World Bank"}
The IMF focuses on balance of payments and macroeconomic stability; the World Bank focuses on long-term development projects.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using debt as a share of GDP and the debt service ratio interchangeably"}
Debt/GDP compares the stock of debt with output; the debt service ratio compares a year's payments with export earnings.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 3 contains multiple-choice questions on forms of aid and the debt service ratio, and Paper 4 asks you to evaluate the effects of aid or debt or the policies of the IMF and the World Bank.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: give the feature that separates each form of aid. *Calculate*: divide debt service by export earnings and multiply by 100. *Evaluate*: set benefits against dependence, conditions and debt, and conclude on the circumstances in which aid or lending is effective.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Aid comes as grants, concessional loans, bilateral or multilateral flows, tied aid, technical help and emergency relief.
- It can raise investment and welfare, but may create dependence, waste or reduced value when tied.
- External debt arises from borrowing and is a burden when debt service takes a large share of export earnings.
- Debt service ratio = debt service ÷ export earnings × 100.
- The IMF lends mainly for balance of payments problems, the World Bank for long-term development; both attach conditions that are debated.
:::
