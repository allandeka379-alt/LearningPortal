## Introduction

People are the most important resource of an economy: they are its workers, its consumers and its entrepreneurs. But a rapidly growing population can strain schools, clinics and jobs, while a shrinking and ageing one can leave too few workers to support the old. The study of population in economics therefore asks three questions: how fast is the population changing, what is its age structure, and is its size right for the resources available? In Zimbabwe, as in many countries, health, migration and the return of money sent home by relatives abroad all shape the answers.

You should be able to calculate percentages and rates and know what GDP per head measures.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Birth rate and death rate"}
The **birth rate** is the number of live births per 1,000 of the population in a year. The **death rate** is the number of deaths per 1,000 of the population in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Infant mortality rate"}
The number of deaths of children under one year of age per 1,000 live births in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural increase and net migration"}
**Natural increase** = birth rate − death rate (per 1,000, or divided by 10 for a percentage). **Net migration** = immigrants − emigrants. Population change = natural increase + net migration.
:::

:::definition{term="Dependency ratio"}
The number of dependants (usually people under 15 and over 64) per 100 people of working age (15 to 64): dependency ratio = (young + old) ÷ working-age population × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Optimum population"}
The population size that, with existing resources, technology and capital, produces the highest output per head (the highest average standard of living). A population above the optimum is **over-populated**; below it, **under-populated**.
:::

## Calculating population change

A fictional country has a population of 8 million. In a year there are 240,000 births and 96,000 deaths; 10,000 people immigrate and 50,000 emigrate.

:::worked-solution{title="Rates and change"}
1. Birth rate = 240,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **30 per 1,000**.
2. Death rate = 96,000 ÷ 8,000,000 × 1,000 = **12 per 1,000**.
3. Natural increase = 30 − 12 = **18 per 1,000**, which is **1.8%** a year.
4. Net migration = 10,000 − 50,000 = **−40,000**, or −5 per 1,000.
5. Population change = 144,000 + (−40,000) = **+104,000**, or **1.3%** a year.
6. Net emigration reduces the growth that natural increase would produce.
:::

## Causes of changes

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Falling death rate (better health care, nutrition, clean water, vaccination) | Raises natural increase, especially if the birth rate stays high |
| Falling infant mortality | Raises life expectancy and may later reduce the birth rate as families expect children to survive |
| Rising incomes, education of women, access to family planning, urbanisation | Reduce birth rates |
| HIV/AIDS and other disease | Raises death rates, especially among working-age adults, reducing the labour force and leaving more orphans; effective treatment reverses this |
| Migration | Emigration of skilled workers (for example nurses and teachers) reduces the working population; remittances sent home raise household incomes |
| Government policy | Family planning, child benefits or immigration controls change birth rates and migration |

## Age structure and dependency

The age structure is shown by a population pyramid or a table of age groups. A country with a **young population** has a wide base: many children, a high dependency ratio and strong pressure on schools and, later, on jobs. An **ageing population** has a high proportion of old people: pressure on pensions and health care, and fewer workers to pay taxes.

| Age group | Population (millions) |
| --- | ---: |
| 0–14 | 3.2 |
| 15–64 | 4.4 |
| 65 and over | 0.4 |

:::worked-solution{title="Dependency ratio"}
1. Dependants = 3.2 + 0.4 = 3.6 million.
2. Dependency ratio = 3.6 ÷ 4.4 × 100 = **81.8** dependants per 100 people of working age.
3. A high ratio means that each worker supports many dependants, leaving less income for saving and investment. Much of the burden here is children, who will become workers, so the **demographic dividend** (when the working-age share rises and the dependency ratio falls) may come later if there are jobs for them.
:::

## Optimum population

If a country has too few people to use its land, minerals and machines fully, output per head can rise as the population grows (more specialisation and a bigger market). At some point extra workers add less than the average, and output per head starts to fall (diminishing returns). The optimum is the population where output per head is highest. It changes: new technology, discoveries of resources and investment in capital raise the optimum; depletion of resources or climate damage lowers it. Because it depends on all of these, the optimum cannot be measured exactly, and a country may be over-populated in one period and under-populated in another.

## Urbanisation

Urbanisation is the growth in the proportion of people who live in towns and cities. It is driven by migration from rural areas, caused by **push factors** (low farm incomes, drought, shortage of land) and **pull factors** (jobs, schools, services). Benefits: access to markets, specialisation and scale in production. Costs: unemployment and a large informal sector, pressure on housing, water, transport and sanitation, and the loss of workers from farming.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
A rapid fall in the death rate with a high birth rate is likely to create a young population and fast growth. This could provide labour for growth if jobs are created, or high unemployment and strain on services if not. So whether a growing population helps or hinders development depends on the resources, capital and institutions available, and on whether investment in education and jobs keeps pace.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the definitions and calculations, causes of changes in births, deaths and migration, age structure, dependency and optimum population (ZIMSEC Form 5; Cambridge A Level only). Use only general statements about Zimbabwe (health, migration, remittances) and no invented statistics.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing rates with totals"}
A birth rate is per 1,000 of the population, not the number of births. Check whether a question asks for a rate or a number.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting migration"}
Population change = natural increase **plus** net migration. A country can have a high natural increase and a falling population if emigration is large.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating optimum population as a fixed number"}
The optimum changes with technology, capital and resources, and is the point of highest output per head, not the largest population.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 often include population calculations and dependency ratios; Paper 3 may ask about the problems of a rapidly growing population. Cambridge Paper 3 tests rates and ratios and Paper 4 asks about the effects of population change on development.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: state the formula, convert totals to rates, and give units (per 1,000). *Explain*: link the cause to births, deaths or migration. *Discuss*: consider both the benefits (labour, market) and the costs (services, jobs, resources) of the change.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Birth and death rates are per 1,000; natural increase is their difference; net migration is immigrants minus emigrants.
- Population change = natural increase + net migration.
- Dependency ratio = (young + old) ÷ working-age population × 100.
- Optimum population gives the highest output per head and changes with technology and resources.
- Urbanisation is driven by push and pull factors and brings both opportunities and strains.
:::
