## Introduction

If you compare the workforce of a farming district with that of a bank or a car factory, you are comparing the **structure** of economic activity. Economies differ in the share of their workers and output in farming and mining, in manufacturing, and in services, in what they sell abroad, and in the quality of their roads, power and technology. These differences help to explain why incomes differ and why moving from one pattern to another is at the heart of development. But the labels "developing" and "developed" hide wide variety, so use them with care.

You should know the three sectors of the economy and how to calculate percentage shares.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors"}
The **primary sector** extracts or grows natural resources (agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining). The **secondary sector** manufactures, processes and builds. The **tertiary sector** provides services (trade, transport, finance, education, health, tourism).
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Economic activity carried out by small, unregistered or unregulated firms and workers, such as street traders and small workshops, which is mostly outside the tax system and does not usually give workers contracts or social protection.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary commodity dependence"}
A situation in which a country earns a large share of its export income from a small number of unprocessed agricultural or mineral products.
:::

## Structure by sector

As an economy develops, the pattern typically changes: first the share of **employment** and output in the primary sector falls as productivity in farming rises and workers move to manufacturing, and later the **tertiary sector** grows fastest. The employment share often changes more slowly than the output share, which is why many developing economies have a large share of workers in low-productivity farming.

The table shows two fictional economies (employment and output as % of the total).

| Sector | Employment (%) | Output (%) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Primary | 60 | 20 |
| Secondary | 12 | 25 |
| Tertiary | 28 | 55 |

:::worked-solution{title="Relative productivity by sector"}
For the economy in the table, relative productivity = output share ÷ employment share.
1. Primary: 20 ÷ 60 = **0.33**. Each primary worker produces a third of the national average.
2. Secondary: 25 ÷ 12 = **2.08**.
3. Tertiary: 55 ÷ 28 = **1.96**.
4. Moving workers from the primary sector to the other sectors would raise national output per head, provided jobs and skills are available there. This is a major reason why development is linked to structural change.
5. A pie chart of employment would show angles of 0.60 × 360° = **216°** (primary), 43.2° (secondary) and 100.8° (tertiary).
:::

In a typical **developed economy**, the primary sector may employ only a few per cent of workers, and the tertiary sector the majority. In a typical **developing economy**, the primary sector employs a large share, often a majority, producing much less than its share of the workforce.

## Pattern of trade

Many developing countries export mainly **primary products** (tobacco, cotton, minerals, coffee) and import manufactured goods, machinery and fuel. Developed countries export mostly manufactured goods and services, and also trade heavily with other developed economies.

Dependence on a few exports has costs: world prices of primary products are volatile, so export earnings, tax revenue and the terms of trade fluctuate; demand for raw materials grows slowly in the long run as incomes rise; and the country gains less of the value added in processing.

:::worked-solution{title="Export concentration"}
Exports of a fictional country: tobacco 38%, gold 27%, cotton 9%, other goods and services 26%.
1. The two-product concentration ratio = 38 + 27 = **65%**.
2. The three-product ratio = 38 + 27 + 9 = **74%**.
3. A fall of 20% in the price of tobacco would, with the quantity unchanged, reduce total export earnings by 0.20 × 38 = **7.6%**.
:::

## Other characteristics

| Characteristic | Typical developing economy | Typical developed economy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sector structure | Large primary sector and low productivity | Small primary sector, large tertiary sector |
| Exports | A few primary commodities | Diverse: manufactures and services |
| Infrastructure | Gaps in roads, power, water, communications | Dense and reliable networks |
| Informal sector | Large | Smaller |
| Saving and investment | Low incomes limit saving; investment is low or depends on foreign funds | Higher saving; large investment in capital and research |
| Technology and skills | Lower average skill levels, limited access to technology | High skills, much spending on research |
| Population | Younger, faster growth (often) | Older, slower growth |
| Health and education | Generally lower life expectancy and schooling | Generally higher |
| Inequality and poverty | Higher poverty, wider inequality in many cases | Generally lower poverty, though inequality exists |

:::example{title="Careful comparison"}
It is a mistake to treat all developing countries as alike. Some, such as mineral exporters, have high average incomes but depend on a few exports; others have diversified into manufacturing and services and grow rapidly. Some developed countries also have large informal or low-wage sectors. Therefore general statements should be backed by the particular characteristics of the countries you are asked about.
:::

## Why does structure matter?

1. **Productivity:** moving workers from low-productivity farming to higher-productivity industry and services raises output per head.
2. **Stability:** a diversified economy is less exposed to price falls in one product.
3. **Value added:** processing raw materials (for example turning tobacco leaf into cigarettes or cotton into clothing) keeps more income in the country.
4. **Constraints:** poor roads and power, weak finance and low skills limit the growth of industry. An informal sector provides jobs and income, but its low productivity and lack of tax payments limit government revenue and growth.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC (Form 5) expects a comparison of the characteristics of developing and developed countries; Cambridge (A Level only) expects the structure of employment and output, the pattern of trade and the effects of dependence on a few commodities. In both, avoid stigmatising language and support claims with the characteristics described.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming a large primary sector always means a poor country"}
Some high-income economies have large primary sectors because of minerals or oil. What matters is productivity and diversification.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing output share and employment share"}
The primary sector's share of output is often much lower than its share of employment; this gap shows low productivity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the informal sector as only a problem"}
It provides employment and income when formal jobs are scarce, although it limits tax revenue and productivity.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 3 test the characteristics of developing countries, often through a comparison of two economies; Cambridge Paper 3 asks multiple-choice questions on sector shares and trade patterns, and Paper 4 may ask about reducing dependence on a few commodities.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Compare*: state similarities and differences with figures. *Explain*: link a characteristic to its effect on growth or stability. *Discuss*: weigh advantages of specialisation against risks of dependence.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Economies are described by the shares of employment and output in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors.
- Development usually involves a shift from primary to secondary and tertiary activity and higher productivity.
- Dependence on a few primary exports exposes a country to price swings and gives little value added.
- Developing countries typically have gaps in infrastructure, larger informal sectors and lower saving and investment, but they differ widely.
- Diversification and processing raise stability and income.
:::
