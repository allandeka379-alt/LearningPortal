## Introduction

A mobile network, a soft-drinks bottler, a mining company with a mine near Hwange: many of the firms that employ workers and sell goods in Zimbabwe and the region are owned or part-owned from abroad, and many Zimbabwean products are sold by foreign-owned firms in other countries. These firms are **multinational companies (MNCs)**, and the money they invest across borders is **foreign direct investment (FDI)**. Governments compete to attract FDI because it can bring capital, technology and jobs, yet it can also cost the host country control, profits and environmental quality. This lesson explains the concepts, the reasons for FDI and a balanced assessment of its effects.

You should know how firms grow, the difference between exports and investment, and the idea of the balance of payments. Aid, debt and globalisation are separate lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Multinational company (MNC)"}
A firm that owns or controls production or service operations in more than one country, usually with its headquarters in one home country and subsidiaries or branches in the host countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign direct investment (FDI)"}
Investment by a firm or person in one country in a business in another country that gives them a lasting interest and a degree of control: building a new factory or mine (*greenfield* investment), buying an existing firm (*merger or acquisition*) or expanding an existing subsidiary. Statistical agencies usually treat ownership of about 10% or more of the voting shares as direct investment.
:::

:::definition{term="Portfolio investment"}
The purchase of shares or bonds in a foreign firm without a controlling interest. It is more easily reversed than FDI.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit repatriation"}
The sending back of profits earned in the host country to the MNC's home country.
:::

## Why firms invest abroad

1. **Access to markets:** producing inside a market avoids transport costs and import duties and brings the firm close to customers.
2. **Avoiding trade barriers:** a tariff or quota on imports makes it cheaper to produce locally.
3. **Lower costs:** cheaper labour, land or energy, or nearness to raw materials such as minerals.
4. **Resources and skills:** access to ore, farmland or skilled workers that are not available at home.
5. **Economies of scale and spreading risk:** selling in many countries reduces dependence on one market.
6. **Incentives:** tax holidays, special economic zones or a stable, predictable policy environment.

## Benefits for host economies

- **Capital inflow:** FDI adds to investment and appears as an inflow in the financial account of the balance of payments (ZIMSEC wording: a long-term capital inflow). It is not a loan, so it need not be repaid.
- **Technology and skills:** workers learn new methods, and local suppliers may adopt higher standards.
- **Employment:** direct jobs in the plant and indirect jobs in suppliers and services.
- **Tax revenue and royalties:** taxes on profit, income and, for mining, royalties.
- **Exports and foreign exchange:** an MNC with export markets earns foreign currency.
- **Competition and choice:** local firms must raise efficiency.

:::worked-solution{title="How much of an MNC's sales stays in the host economy?"}
A fictional foreign-owned copper mine in Zandia sells output worth US\$150 million a year abroad. The table (illustrative data) shows where the money goes.

| Use of revenue | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Wages paid to local workers | 12 |
| Purchases from local suppliers | 25 |
| Taxes and royalties to the government | 22 |
| Imported inputs and services | 43 |
| Profit sent abroad (repatriated) | 48 |
| **Total** | **150** |

1. Value that stays in the host economy = 12 + 25 + 22 = US\$59 million.
2. Share of revenue = 59 ÷ 150 × 100 = **39.3%**.
3. Profit repatriated as a share of revenue = 48 ÷ 150 × 100 = **32%**.
4. With 1,500 local workers, the average annual wage is 12 million ÷ 1,500 = **US\$8,000**.
:::

## Costs and risks for host economies

- **Profit repatriation:** a large share of the gains leaves the country.
- **Dependence:** the economy relies on decisions made at a distant head office, which can close a plant if conditions change.
- **Transfer pricing:** an MNC may price trade between its own branches so that profit appears in a low-tax country, reducing tax paid in the host country.
- **Environmental and social costs:** pollution, use of land and water, resettlement of communities.
- **Crowding out:** large firms may drive out local producers, and the best local workers may be drawn away from local firms.
- **Limited technology transfer:** if skilled jobs and research remain at head office, local gains are small.

:::worked-solution{title="Repatriation against the initial investment"}
The same mine cost US\$120 million to build, financed entirely by foreign investors. It sends US\$48 million of profit abroad each year.

1. Years for repatriated profit to equal the initial investment = 120 ÷ 48 = **2.5 years**.
2. After that, the foreign investors have recovered their money, and the host country's net gain depends on jobs, taxes and local purchases.
3. FDI inflow compared with the size of the economy: a fictional inflow of US\$0.6 billion into an economy with GDP of US\$12 billion is 0.6 ÷ 12 × 100 = **5% of GDP**.
:::

## Local ownership policies

Some governments require foreign investors to share ownership with local people or firms, to employ local managers or to buy local inputs. This is a factual policy choice with arguments on both sides. **Supporters** argue that it keeps more profit and decision-making in the country, spreads benefits to local owners and encourages skills transfer. **Critics** argue that it can discourage investment, reduce the capital and expertise brought in, and cost jobs if investors choose other countries. The outcome depends on how the rules are designed, how stable and clear they are and what alternatives investors have.

:::example{title="Evaluating the effects of an MNC"}
Whether the mine above is good for Zandia depends on several things: the share of revenue that stays in the country (39.3%), whether workers learn skills that can be used elsewhere, the size of the environmental damage, whether the tax rules prevent profit-shifting, and what would happen otherwise (without the mine, the minerals might stay underground for lack of capital). A balanced answer weighs the jobs, taxes and exports against repatriation and dependence, and notes that the outcome depends on the bargaining strength of the government and on the contract terms.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the definitions, reasons for FDI, and benefits and costs to host economies. ZIMSEC (Form 6) treats MNCs as a kind of large firm and expects the local ownership policies to be described factually; Cambridge (A Level) uses MNCs and FDI in its work on development and globalisation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating FDI as a loan"}
FDI is an ownership stake in a business. It need not be repaid, but the investors take profits for as long as they own the business.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing FDI with portfolio investment or aid"}
Buying a few shares on a stock exchange without control is portfolio investment; a grant or loan from a government is aid.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
MNCs bring benefits and costs. State both and reach a judgement that depends on conditions such as tax rules, local linkages and the size of the repatriation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and examples; Paper 2 data questions give flows of revenue or investment; Paper 3 essays ask you to discuss the benefits and costs of MNCs to a developing country. Cambridge Paper 3 tests definitions and effects in multiple choice, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: say who invests, where and with what degree of control. *Explain*: link a reason to costs or markets. *Calculate*: shares and years of payback. *Assess/discuss/evaluate*: give both sides, use the data and judge on conditions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An MNC operates in more than one country; FDI is investment that gives lasting control, unlike portfolio investment.
- Firms invest abroad for markets, lower costs, resources, to avoid trade barriers and for incentives.
- Hosts gain capital, technology, jobs, tax revenue and exports.
- Hosts risk profit repatriation, dependence, transfer pricing, environmental damage and the crowding out of local firms.
- Local ownership rules aim to keep more benefits at home but may deter investment; judge by the evidence and conditions.
:::
