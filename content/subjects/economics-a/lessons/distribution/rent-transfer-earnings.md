## Introduction

A striker who plays for a top club may earn US\$60,000 a month. If football disappeared, the best job she could find might pay US\$12,000. The difference, US\$48,000, is a surplus: she would play for much less. A farmer's land in a fertile valley earns more than poorer land, though the supply of land is fixed. These are examples of **economic rent**, a surplus paid to a factor above what is needed to keep it in its present use. The idea explains why some people earn so much more than they "need", why taxing some incomes may not reduce supply and why a fixed supply of a factor is paid whatever its price.

You should know opportunity cost, the demand and supply of a factor and the elasticity of supply. This lesson builds on the introduction to factor rewards.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transfer earnings"}
The minimum payment needed to keep a factor of production in its present use. It equals what the factor could earn in its next best use: its **opportunity cost**.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic rent"}
The surplus a factor earns over its transfer earnings: the actual payment minus the minimum needed to keep it in its present use. (It is different from the rent paid on a house or on land.)
:::

:::definition{term="Quasi-rent"}
A temporary surplus earned by a factor, such as a machine, whose supply is fixed in the short run. It is the revenue the factor earns minus the variable costs of using it, and it disappears in the long run as the supply adjusts. *(ZIMSEC detail)*
:::

:::keyfact{title="The key relationship"}
Payment to a factor = transfer earnings + economic rent.

Economic rent = actual payment − transfer earnings.
:::

## Individuals: rent as a surplus

:::worked-solution{title="A footballer's earnings"}
A fictional footballer earns US\$60,000 a month. Her best alternative job pays US\$12,000 a month.

1. Transfer earnings = US\$12,000 (her opportunity cost).
2. Economic rent = 60,000 − 12,000 = **US\$48,000**.
3. Share of her earnings that is rent = 48,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **80%**.
4. If a tax cut her earnings to US\$30,000 she would still play, because 30,000 exceeds 12,000. A tax on rent need not reduce the supply of players.
:::

## Markets: the supply curve

For a whole occupation with an upward-sloping supply curve, the market wage is paid to every worker, but most workers would have accepted less. Workers at the bottom of the supply curve are close to indifferent; those further along would have worked for less than the going wage. Their surplus is rent.

- The area **under the supply curve** up to the quantity employed is the total transfer earnings.
- The area **above the supply curve and below the price** is the total economic rent.

:::worked-solution{title="Rent in a labour market"}
The supply of skilled welders in a fictional town is W = 4 + 0.05L, where W is the wage in US\$ an hour and L is the number of welders. The market wage is US\$10.

1. Number employed: 10 = 4 + 0.05L, so L = 120.
2. Total payment = 10 × 120 = US\$1,200 an hour.
3. Economic rent = ½ × (10 − 4) × 120 = **US\$360** an hour (a triangle with base 120 and height 6).
4. Transfer earnings = 1,200 − 360 = **US\$840** an hour.
5. A rise in demand lifts the wage to US\$12: L = (12 − 4) ÷ 0.05 = 160, rent = ½ × 8 × 160 = US\$640 and transfer earnings = 1,920 − 640 = US\$1,280.
6. Rent rises from 30% to 33.3% of the payment (360 ÷ 1,200 and 640 ÷ 1,920).
:::

## Elasticity of supply decides the split

| Supply of the factor | Shape of the supply curve | Transfer earnings | Economic rent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Perfectly inelastic (fixed) | Vertical | Zero (it has no alternative use) | **All** of the payment |
| Inelastic | Steep | Small share | Large share |
| Elastic | Flat | Large share | Small share |
| Perfectly elastic | Horizontal | **All** of the payment | Zero |

So the less responsive supply is to the price, the more of the payment is rent. This is why highly talented individuals with no close substitutes receive large rents, while a factor in perfectly elastic supply, such as unskilled workers who can easily switch between jobs, receives only transfer earnings.

:::worked-solution{title="Land in fixed supply"}
A fictional island has 1,000 hectares of farmland with no other use. It is rented at US\$40 a hectare.

1. Transfer earnings = 0, because the land has no alternative use.
2. Economic rent = 40 × 1,000 = **US\$40,000** a year, the entire payment.
3. If demand falls and the rent drops to US\$25, the land is still farmed: all the payment is still rent, now US\$25,000.
:::

In practice land has alternative uses (farming, housing, tourism), so only the supply of land to the economy as a whole is fixed. The supply of land to a particular use is more elastic, and then part of the payment is transfer earnings.

## Quasi-rent (ZIMSEC detail)

In the short run the supply of a machine or a building is fixed, so its owner receives a surplus over the cost of running it. This surplus is quasi-rent because it is rent-like but temporary.

:::worked-solution{title="Quasi-rent on a machine"}
A fictional maize mill earns revenue of US\$90,000 a year from one machine. Variable costs (labour, fuel, maintenance) are US\$62,000. Fixed costs, including the machine's depreciation, are US\$20,000.

1. Quasi-rent = revenue − variable costs = 90,000 − 62,000 = **US\$28,000**.
2. It covers the fixed costs of US\$20,000 and leaves **US\$8,000** of profit above all costs.
3. In the long run, supernormal profit attracts new mills and the extra supply lowers the price until the machine earns only enough to cover all its costs. The quasi-rent then falls to the level of the fixed costs, and the US\$8,000 surplus above them is competed away.
:::

## Why the idea matters

- **Taxation:** a tax on economic rent does not reduce the supply of the factor, because the factor stays in its use as long as it earns its transfer earnings.
- **Pay and fairness:** high earnings of stars may be rent, which raises the question whether they are earned or simply the result of scarcity.
- **Pricing of land:** land prices are largely rent capitalised.

:::example{title="Evaluating a tax on top earnings"}
A government proposes a higher rate of tax on the highest earnings. If most of those earnings are economic rent, supply will barely fall, so the tax raises revenue at little cost to output. But the amount that is rent is hard to measure, because transfer earnings depend on alternatives that may include working abroad. If top earners can move to other countries, the supply of talent is more elastic than it seems and less of the income is rent. The judgement depends on how mobile the factor is.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the definitions, the split of earnings, the effect of the elasticity of supply, and the effect on rent of changes in demand. Quasi-rent is ZIMSEC detail.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing economic rent with the rent paid on a house"}
Economic rent is a surplus over transfer earnings, and it can be earned by any factor, including labour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying transfer earnings are the wage"}
Transfer earnings are only the minimum needed to keep the factor in its use. The wage is transfer earnings plus any economic rent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting rent below the supply curve"}
Rent is the area *above* the supply curve and below the price; transfer earnings are the area *below* the supply curve.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions; Paper 2 data questions give an earnings table or supply schedule and ask you to compute rent; Paper 3 essays ask about taxation of rent and wage differences. Cambridge Paper 3 multiple choice tests the area on a supply diagram and the elasticity link.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: payment above the minimum to keep the factor in use. *Calculate*: actual payment minus transfer earnings, or the triangle area. *Explain*: link the size of rent to the elasticity of supply. *Evaluate*: weigh the lack of supply response against mobility and the difficulty of measuring rent.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transfer earnings are a factor's opportunity cost: the minimum payment needed to keep it in its present use.
- Economic rent is the surplus above transfer earnings: payment minus transfer earnings.
- With a supply curve, rent is the area above the curve and below the price; transfer earnings are the area under the curve.
- The less elastic the supply, the greater the share of rent: all rent if supply is fixed, none if perfectly elastic.
- Quasi-rent is a temporary short-run surplus on a fixed asset (revenue minus variable costs) that disappears in the long run (ZIMSEC).
:::
