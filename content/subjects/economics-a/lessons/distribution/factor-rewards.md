## Introduction

When a farm near Marondera sells its tobacco, the money received is shared out. Some pays the farm workers, some pays the landowner or the lease, some repays the bank that lent for the barn, and what remains is the owner's profit. These four payments are the **rewards to the factors of production**, and together they make up the income of a nation. How the income is shared is one of the oldest questions in economics, and it connects to inequality, wages policy and taxation.

You should already know the four factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) and the idea of demand and supply. The next lesson on economic rent and transfer earnings refines the idea of rent, and a later lesson explains how the demand for labour is derived (marginal revenue product theory).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Factor reward (factor income)"}
The payment made to the owner of a factor of production for its use: **rent** to land, **wages** to labour, **interest** to capital and **profit** to enterprise.
:::

:::definition{term="Functional distribution of income"}
The division of national income between the factors of production: how much goes to wages, rent, interest and profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Personal distribution of income"}
The division of national income between individuals or households, regardless of which factors they own: how much goes to the richest and poorest households.
:::

## The four rewards

| Factor | Reward | What it pays for | Main determinants |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Land | Rent | The use of land and natural resources | Demand for the land's products and its (often fixed) supply |
| Labour | Wages (and salaries) | Physical and mental effort | Demand for labour (productivity, price of output) and supply (skills, qualifications, working conditions) |
| Capital | Interest | The use of money or capital goods | The demand for and supply of loanable funds; risk and the length of the loan |
| Enterprise | Profit | Organising production and bearing risk | Success of the business, risk, competition and the demand for the product |

Wages are the largest share in most economies. Profit is a **residual**: what remains after the other factors have been paid, and it can be negative. *Normal profit* is the minimum reward that keeps the entrepreneur in the business; anything above it is *supernormal (abnormal) profit*.

## How each reward is determined

Every factor has a market with a demand and a supply. The demand for a factor is a **derived demand**: it depends on the demand for what the factor produces. A rise in demand for maize raises the demand for farmland and farm workers.

:::worked-solution{title="Rent when the supply of land is fixed"}
The table gives the demand for and supply of farmland in a fictional district of Zandia (hectares). The total supply of land is fixed at 100 hectares whatever the rent.

| Rent (US\$ per hectare) | Demand (hectares) | Supply (hectares) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 20 | 140 | 100 |
| 30 | 120 | 100 |
| 40 | 100 | 100 |
| 50 | 80 | 100 |
| 60 | 60 | 100 |

1. At US\$40 demand equals supply (100 hectares), so the equilibrium rent is **US\$40** per hectare.
2. A new road and a higher price for maize raise demand by 20 hectares at every rent: demand becomes 160, 140, 120, 100, 80.
3. The new equilibrium is at US\$50, where demand is 100. The rent rises by (50 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = **25%**, with no change in the quantity of land.
4. Because supply is fixed, the whole change in demand shows up in the rent.
:::

**Wages** are explained by the demand for and supply of labour in each occupation: occupations with scarce skills and high productivity pay more. **Interest** is the price of borrowing: a rise in the supply of savings lowers it and a rise in the demand for loans raises it. **Profit** depends on the success of the entrepreneur and the competition faced.

## Functional distribution

The table shows the national income of a fictional country, Zandia, by factor reward (illustrative figures).

| Reward | National income (US\$ million) | Share (%) | Angle on a pie chart (degrees) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Wages | 11,000 | 55.0 | 198.0 |
| Rent | 1,400 | 7.0 | 25.2 |
| Interest | 2,200 | 11.0 | 39.6 |
| Profit | 5,400 | 27.0 | 97.2 |
| **Total** | **20,000** | **100.0** | **360.0** |

:::worked-solution{title="Labour's share over time"}
Five years later Zandia's national income is US\$24,000 million and the wage bill is US\$12,000 million.

1. Labour's share now = 12,000 ÷ 24,000 × 100 = **50%**.
2. Wages rose by (12,000 − 11,000) ÷ 11,000 × 100 = 9.1%, but national income rose by 20%, so labour's share fell from 55% to 50%.
3. A rising wage bill can therefore go with a falling share of income.
:::

## Personal distribution

The functional distribution does not tell you who is rich. A household may earn wages, own a rented house and hold bank deposits, so it receives several rewards. The **personal distribution** is usually shown by ranking households and giving each fifth (quintile) its share of total income.

:::worked-solution{title="Comparing the richest and poorest fifths"}
In a fictional economy the poorest fifth of households receive 6% of income and the richest fifth receive 47%.

1. The richest fifth receive 47 ÷ 6 = **7.8 times** the share of the poorest fifth.
2. If all households earned equally, each fifth would receive 20%. The richest fifth's share is 27 percentage points above equality.
:::

## Why rewards differ

- **Differences in ownership:** some people own land, shares or houses, so they receive rent, interest and profit as well as wages.
- **Skills and productivity:** workers with scarce skills receive higher wages.
- **Risk and responsibility:** entrepreneurs who bear risk can earn very high profits or make losses.
- **Market power:** monopolies and trade unions can raise rewards above competitive levels.
- **Discrimination and access:** unequal access to education, credit or land causes differences that are not based on productivity.

:::example{title="Evaluating the usefulness of the functional distribution"}
Economists use it to study how growth is shared between workers and owners. Its weakness is that it hides inequality within each group (a chief executive and a cleaner are both paid "wages") and that the borders between factors blur: a self-employed taxi owner earns a mix of wages, interest and profit. For judging inequality, the personal distribution is more informative.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the four rewards, introductory determination by demand and supply, the functional versus personal distinction and reasons for differences. Marginal revenue product theory and economic rent are covered in later lessons; at this level do not use them to explain individual rewards.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling interest 'profit on capital'"}
Interest is the reward to capital; profit is the reward to enterprise. They are different payments.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the functional with the personal distribution"}
The functional distribution is between factors; the personal distribution is between people or households. A single household can receive several factor rewards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying rent is the price of renting a house"}
In factor-reward theory, rent is the payment for the use of land and natural resources, whatever the arrangements. Do not mix it up with a tenant's weekly rent.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the four rewards; Paper 2 data questions give a table of factor incomes or quintile shares and ask for percentages and ratios; Paper 3 essays ask why incomes differ. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the distinctions in multiple choice and Paper 2 uses them in essays on income distribution.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify*: name the reward for each factor. *Calculate*: shares and ratios from the table. *Explain*: link the reward to demand and supply of the factor. *Compare*: functional with personal distribution, giving a difference. *Evaluate*: weigh market forces against power, ownership and discrimination.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land earns rent, labour wages, capital interest and enterprise profit.
- Each reward is determined, at this level, by the demand for and supply of the factor; the demand is derived.
- Profit is a residual and can be negative; normal profit keeps the entrepreneur in business.
- The functional distribution divides income between factors; the personal distribution divides it between households.
- Rewards differ because of ownership, skills, risk, market power and unequal access.
:::
