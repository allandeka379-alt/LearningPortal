## Introduction

Wages and the use of land are among the most important prices and resources in any economy. Workers depend on wages for their livelihood, and in a mostly agricultural country the ownership and use of land affect incomes, food supply and exports. Because these are so important, governments in almost every country intervene in **factor markets**: they set minimum wages, regulate bargaining between unions and employers, limit wage growth, regulate unions and redistribute land.

These policies are matters of economic analysis and also of strong public debate. The skill you need in the examination is to set out the arguments of different groups fairly, to use the economic models correctly, and to reach a reasoned judgement based on efficiency, equity and output. This lesson uses a neutral approach: where views differ it says who holds them (supporters, critics) and avoids claims that cannot be supported.

You should know how wages are determined by labour demand and supply (the lesson on wage determination) and the basic ideas of efficiency and equity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Minimum wage"}
A legal minimum price that employers must pay for labour, either one rate for the whole economy or different rates for sectors or occupations.
:::

:::definition{term="Collective bargaining"}
Negotiation of wages and working conditions between employers (or employers' associations) and workers' representatives (trade unions).
:::

:::definition{term="Incomes policy"}
Government action to limit increases in wages and other incomes, by guidelines, agreements or controls, usually to reduce inflation.
:::

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
A deliberate change in the ownership, distribution or conditions of use of agricultural land, for example by redistributing land from large holdings to many smaller ones, or by changing the rules of tenure.
:::

:::definition{term="Factor market"}
A market in which the factors of production (labour, land, capital) are bought and sold, and in which factor rewards (wages, rent, interest) are determined.
:::

## Minimum wage legislation

A minimum wage aims to protect low-paid workers from exploitation and to raise their incomes.

**In a competitive labour market** a minimum wage set above the equilibrium wage raises the wage of those who keep their jobs but reduces the quantity of labour demanded and increases the number who want to work, so unemployment results.

:::worked-solution{title="A minimum wage in a competitive labour market (illustrative data)"}
Labour demand is W = 12 − 0.01L and labour supply is W = 2 + 0.01L (W in US\$ an hour, L in workers).

1. Equilibrium: 12 − 0.01L = 2 + 0.01L, so 10 = 0.02L and L = **500** workers at W = **US\$7**.
2. A minimum wage of US\$8 is above the equilibrium wage, so it is binding. Demand: 8 = 12 − 0.01L gives L = **400**. Supply: 8 = 2 + 0.01L gives L = **600**.
3. Employment falls to 400. Unemployment (excess supply) = 600 − 400 = **200** workers.
4. Wage bill before = 7 × 500 = US\$3,500 an hour; after = 8 × 400 = US\$3,200 an hour, a **fall of US\$300**.
5. Elasticity of demand for labour between the two wages: %ΔL = (400 − 500) ÷ 500 = −20%; %Δwage = (8 − 7) ÷ 7 = +14.3%. Elasticity = 20 ÷ 14.3 = **1.4** (elastic), so the total wage bill falls. If demand had been inelastic the wage bill would have risen.
:::

**In a monopsonistic labour market** (one dominant employer, such as one large employer in a mining town), the employer pays less than the competitive wage and employs fewer workers. A minimum wage can then raise both wages and employment, provided it is not set too high.

:::worked-solution{title="A minimum wage under monopsony (illustrative data)"}
Labour demand (MRP) is W = 12 − 0.01L and labour supply is W = 3 + 0.005L. The employer is the only buyer of labour, so its marginal cost of labour is W = 3 + 0.01L.

1. Competitive outcome: 12 − 0.01L = 3 + 0.005L, so L = **600**, W = **US\$6**.
2. Monopsony: the employer sets MRP = marginal cost: 12 − 0.01L = 3 + 0.01L, so L = **450**, and reads the wage off the supply curve: W = 3 + 0.005 × 450 = **US\$5.25**.
3. A minimum wage of US\$5.50: supply = (5.50 − 3) ÷ 0.005 = 500 workers; demand = (12 − 5.50) ÷ 0.01 = 650. The employer hires up to the supply: **500** workers, at US\$5.50. Employment rises by 50 and the wage by US\$0.25.
4. A minimum wage of US\$7: demand = (12 − 7) ÷ 0.01 = **500**; supply = (7 − 3) ÷ 0.005 = 800. Employment is 500 (still above 450) but 300 workers would like a job and cannot find one.
5. The result depends on the **level** of the minimum wage and the **market structure**: a moderate minimum can raise employment under monopsony, but one set well above the competitive wage reduces it.
:::

**Other points.** Many workers in a country such as Zimbabwe work in the informal sector, where minimum wage laws are hard to enforce; employers may respond by reducing hours or hiring informally. Supporters of minimum wages argue that they raise living standards and reduce poverty, may raise productivity (an "efficiency wage") and reduce turnover; critics argue that they raise costs, cut jobs and encourage informality. Evidence depends on the level of the wage relative to the average wage and on the structure of the market.

## Collective bargaining and the role of trade unions

Where unions and employers bargain together, wages can be set above the level that an individual worker could obtain. In Zimbabwe, wages in many sectors are set through sectoral bargaining, in bodies in which employers' and workers' representatives negotiate, and a tripartite forum brings together government, employers and labour to discuss labour and economic policy.

**Arguments for collective bargaining.** It balances the bargaining power of workers against employers, may reduce strikes through agreed procedures, and can raise productivity through better working conditions. **Arguments against.** It may raise wages above productivity and cause unemployment among outsiders (people who are not union members), it may protect insiders at the expense of the unemployed, and sector-wide agreements may not suit small or struggling firms.

## Incomes policy

An incomes policy tries to control **cost-push inflation** by limiting wage rises (a wage freeze, or guidelines that wage rises should not exceed a figure), sometimes together with price controls.

:::worked-solution{title="Real wage under an incomes policy (illustrative data)"}
A guideline limits wage increases to 6% while inflation is 12%.

1. Real wage change = (1.06 ÷ 1.12 − 1) × 100 = **−5.4%** (to one decimal place). Workers' purchasing power falls.
2. Roughly, real wage change ≈ 6 − 12 = −6 percentage points.
3. If inflation falls because wage costs rise less, the policy has worked; but if inflation stays at 12% the policy transfers income from workers to firms and is likely to cause disputes.
:::

**Strengths.** It can reduce inflationary pressure without raising unemployment. **Weaknesses.** It cannot work if the cause of inflation is not wage costs; it is difficult to enforce in the informal sector and in negotiations; it can reduce real incomes and cause disputes; and it distorts wage differentials, so workers in key skills may leave.

## Controls on trade unions

Governments may regulate unions through registration rules, rules on strikes and rules on union activities. Supporters of such regulation argue that it prevents disruption of essential services, limits the monopoly power of unions over labour supply, and protects the public and investors. Critics argue that it weakens workers' bargaining power, holds down wages, and conflicts with the freedom of association recognised in international labour standards. The economic effects depend on how much the rules change bargaining power: where unions are weakened, wages and strikes may fall, but so may workers' share of income and their morale.

## Land reform

Land reform involves changing who owns, uses or has rights to agricultural land. Zimbabwe's land has been a central economic and political question since before independence: at independence in 1980 a large share of the best agricultural land was held in large-scale commercial farms, while most of the population lived on much less land. Land redistribution has followed in several forms, including a resettlement programme in the 1980s and 1990s and, from 2000, the Fast Track Land Reform Programme, which redistributed a large area of land to many smallholder and smaller commercial farmers. This lesson presents the economic arguments only.

Economists assess land reform on three criteria:

1. **Efficiency.** Are the factors used productively? Supporters argue that small farms can use labour intensively and that many previously under-used areas were brought into use. Critics argue that loss of the economies of scale, of established managers and of tenure that can be used as collateral can lower yields in the short run.
2. **Equity.** Land ownership and income become more evenly spread, and more households gain access to a livelihood; but the process may be disputed and some groups bear costs.
3. **Output.** Total output depends on yields, the area farmed and the availability of inputs, credit, irrigation and markets. Output of some crops fell after 2000, while that of others, such as tobacco, recovered substantially later, partly through contract farming; analysts differ on how far the changes resulted from the reform and how far from drought, macroeconomic instability, and shortages of finance and inputs.

:::worked-solution{title="Efficiency, equity and output (illustrative data)"}
A fictional district has 5,000 hectares in 10 large farms. The land is redistributed into 250 holdings of 20 hectares.

| Stage (fictional district) | Holdings | Land (hectares) | Yield (tonnes per hectare) | Output (tonnes) | Output per holding (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Before reform | 10 | 5,000 | 6 | 30,000 | 3,000 |
| Year 2 after reform | 250 | 5,000 | 4 | 20,000 | 80 |
| Year 8 after reform | 250 | 5,000 | 5.5 | 27,500 | 110 |

1. Output = land × yield. Before: 5,000 × 6 = **30,000** tonnes. Year 2: 5,000 × 4 = **20,000**. Year 8: 5,000 × 5.5 = **27,500**.
2. Change in total output in year 2 = (20,000 − 30,000) ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **−33.3%**; in year 8 = (27,500 − 30,000) ÷ 30,000 × 100 = **−8.3%**.
3. **Equity:** the number of holdings (households with land) rises from 10 to 250, a 25-fold increase.
4. **Efficiency and output:** the transition costs (new farmers without credit, machinery or experience) lower yield and output at first; support (credit, inputs, extension advice, secure tenure) allows yields to recover. In this illustration output remains below its pre-reform level in year 8, but is spread across 250 households. Whether the reform was "successful" depends on how much weight is given to equity against total output, and on whether yields continue to rise.
5. These figures are invented to show the method; real results depend on the crops, rainfall and support.
:::

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A minimum wage can raise the incomes of the lowest paid, which supports equity and may reduce poverty. However, if it is set well above the equilibrium wage it can reduce employment, especially where demand for labour is elastic, and in an economy with a large informal sector it may be avoided by employers. The effect is therefore likely to be favourable if the wage is moderate, enforced and accompanied by measures to raise productivity, and unfavourable if it is high relative to productivity. A judgement requires knowledge of the structure of the particular labour market.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a minimum wage always causes unemployment"}
In a competitive market a binding minimum wage reduces employment, but under monopsony a moderate minimum wage can raise it. The result depends on the level of the wage and the market structure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking sides in an essay on land reform"}
Economic analysis weighs efficiency, equity and output with evidence. Attribute views to groups, give both sides, and reach a judgement based on criteria, not on political preference.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing nominal and real wage changes"}
A wage rise of 6% when inflation is 12% is a fall in the real wage. Always compare wage growth with inflation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: link the intervention to its effect using the model. *Calculate*: employment, unemployment, the wage bill, elasticity or output. *Assess/Discuss*: give the arguments for and against, using efficiency, equity and output, and a justified judgement that depends on the case.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Minimum wages raise the pay of those employed but, in a competitive market, reduce employment; under monopsony a moderate minimum can raise it.
- Collective bargaining can raise wages and balance bargaining power, but may cause unemployment among outsiders.
- Incomes policy limits wage rises to reduce cost-push inflation, but cuts real wages if inflation is not reduced and is hard to enforce.
- Controls on trade unions change bargaining power: supporters stress stability and essential services, critics stress workers' rights and incomes.
- Land reform is judged on efficiency, equity and output; effects depend on support, tenure, inputs and credit, and views are presented fairly.
:::
