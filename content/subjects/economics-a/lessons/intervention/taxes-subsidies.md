## Introduction

A packet of cigarettes, a litre of fuel and a bottle of beer all carry taxes that the government collects from the seller, yet the buyer and the seller both feel the effect. Fertiliser and some basic foods have been subsidised in many countries to keep farming affordable. How much of a tax do consumers actually bear? Who gains from a subsidy? The answers depend on the elasticities of demand and supply, which is why this topic builds on the previous lessons.

You should know supply and demand curves, shifts, elasticities and consumer and producer surplus. The taxes of fiscal policy (income tax, VAT as a source of revenue) are treated in a later lesson; here we focus on their effect on a single market.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indirect tax"}
A tax on spending on goods and services, collected by the seller on behalf of the government. It raises the seller's cost of supplying the good.
:::

:::definition{term="Specific tax and ad valorem tax"}
A **specific tax** is a fixed amount per unit (US\$4 per litre). An **ad valorem tax** is a percentage of the price (15% of the selling price).
:::

:::definition{term="Subsidy"}
A payment by the government to producers (or consumers) that lowers the cost of production or purchase, so that a larger quantity is supplied or demanded at each price.
:::

:::definition{term="Incidence of a tax"}
The way the burden of a tax is shared between consumers (through a higher price) and producers (through a lower net price).
:::

## The effect of a specific tax

A specific tax raises the cost of each unit by the amount of the tax, so supply shifts **upward (to the left) by the amount of the tax**: at every quantity sellers must receive the old price plus the tax. A specific tax gives a parallel shift; an ad valorem tax makes the supply curve pivot, since the tax per unit rises with the price.

Take a fictional market where demand is $P = 24 - 0.02Q$ and supply is $P = 4 + 0.02Q$ (P in US\$, Q in units).

:::worked-solution{title="A specific tax of four dollars per unit"}
1. **Before the tax.** $24 - 0.02Q = 4 + 0.02Q$, so $20 = 0.04Q$, giving $Q = 500$ and $P = 14$.
2. **After the tax.** Supply becomes $P = 8 + 0.02Q$. Then $24 - 0.02Q = 8 + 0.02Q$, so $16 = 0.04Q$, $Q = 400$ and $P = 16$.
3. **Consumers** pay US\$2 more per unit (16 − 14). **Producers** receive $16 - 4 = 12$ per unit after paying the tax, which is US\$2 less than before (14).
4. **Tax revenue** $= 4 \times 400 = 1{,}600$, shared as consumer burden $2 \times 400 = 800$ and producer burden $2 \times 400 = 800$.
5. **Deadweight loss** $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (500 - 400) = 200$: the value of trades that no longer occur.
:::

The tax reduces quantity from 500 to 400: consumer surplus and producer surplus both fall by more than the revenue raised, and the difference is the deadweight loss.

## Incidence and elasticity

The incidence depends on the relative elasticities of demand and supply. The more inelastic side of the market bears the greater share, because it is less able to avoid the tax by changing behaviour.

:::worked-solution{title="A steeper (more inelastic) demand curve"}
Now demand is $P = 34 - 0.04Q$ with the same supply and the same tax of US\$4. The pre-tax equilibrium is still US\$14 and 500 units.

1. After the tax: $34 - 0.04Q = 8 + 0.02Q$, so $26 = 0.06Q$ and $Q = 433.3$; $P = 34 - 0.04 \times 433.3 = 16.67$.
2. Consumers pay US\$2.67 more per unit (about two-thirds of the tax); producers bear US\$1.33.
3. Revenue $= 4 \times 433.3 = 1{,}733$ (higher than in the first market because quantity falls less); deadweight loss $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (500 - 433.3) = 133$ (lower than in the first market).
:::

| Demand | Supply | Who bears more of the tax | Effect on quantity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inelastic | Elastic | Consumers | Small fall |
| Elastic | Inelastic | Producers | Large fall |
| Perfectly inelastic | Any | Consumers (all) | No fall |
| Perfectly elastic | Any | Producers (all) | Falls sharply |

This explains why governments tax goods with inelastic demand such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco: revenue is high and quantity falls little. It also shows the tax is **regressive** when poorer households spend a larger share of income on the good.

### An ad valorem tax

If the tax is 15% of the pre-tax price and a good sells at US\$20 before tax, the tax is US\$3 and the consumer pays US\$23. The supply curve pivots upward: the vertical gap between the old and new curves widens as price rises. The analysis of incidence follows the same logic as for a specific tax.

## The effect of a subsidy

A subsidy lowers costs, so supply shifts **downward (to the right) by the amount of the subsidy**.

:::worked-solution{title="A subsidy of three dollars per unit (original market)"}
1. After the subsidy, supply becomes $P = 1 + 0.02Q$. Then $24 - 0.02Q = 1 + 0.02Q$, so $23 = 0.04Q$, $Q = 575$ and $P = 24 - 0.02 \times 575 = 12.50$.
2. Consumers pay US\$1.50 less per unit (14 − 12.50). Producers receive $12.50 + 3 = 15.50$, which is US\$1.50 more than before.
3. **Cost to the government** $= 3 \times 575 = 1{,}725$.
4. **Who receives the payment.** Consumers receive $1.50 \times 575 = 862.50$ of it through the lower price and producers receive $1.50 \times 575 = 862.50$ through the higher net price; the two shares add up to the cost of 1,725.
5. **Welfare loss** (excess burden) $= \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3 \times (575 - 500) = 112.50$. Consumer plus producer surplus rise by only 1,612.50 (from 5,000 to 6,612.50), which is 112.50 less than the cost to the government.
:::

A subsidy on a good with inelastic demand mostly lowers price and raises quantity only a little; on an elastic good quantity expands more. The share of the subsidy benefit follows the elasticities: the more inelastic side gains more.

## Uses and effectiveness

**Correcting externalities.** A tax on a good with negative externalities (for example tobacco or fuel) is meant to raise its price to cover the social cost; a subsidy on a good with positive externalities (such as vaccination or education materials) lowers its price to raise consumption towards the socially efficient level. The details belong to the lesson on externalities.

**Other aims.** Taxes raise revenue; subsidies support farmers, protect infant industries and make essentials affordable.

**Effectiveness depends on:**

1. **Elasticities.** A tax on inelastic goods raises revenue but changes behaviour little; taxing a good to discourage consumption works poorly if demand is inelastic.
2. **Size of the tax or subsidy** relative to the externality, which is hard to measure.
3. **Avoidance and black markets.** High taxes encourage smuggling and under-reporting.
4. **Distribution.** Taxes on necessities hit poor households hard; subsidies may benefit richer consumers who buy more.
5. **Cost and administration.** A subsidy requires government finance, which may mean higher taxes elsewhere or borrowing, and may cause dependence and inefficiency among producers.
6. **Time.** Responses are larger in the long run when elasticities are higher.

:::example{title="Evaluating a fertiliser subsidy"}
A fictional government subsidises fertiliser to raise maize output. Supply shifts right, the price of maize falls and quantity rises. This helps consumers and farmers, and may improve food security. However, with inelastic demand for maize the price falls sharply and farm revenue may fall; the subsidy is costly; richer farmers with more land gain most; and it may encourage over-use of fertiliser. Whether it is worthwhile depends on the size of the benefit compared with the cost and on whether alternatives, such as improving roads or seed supply, would raise output more cheaply.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect specific and ad valorem taxes, incidence with elasticity, and the effect of subsidies. Cambridge Paper 2 often asks you to explain the incidence on a described diagram and assess the effectiveness of a tax or subsidy; ZIMSEC data-response questions may ask for tax revenue and the burden calculated from a table.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Shifting the demand curve for an indirect tax"}
The tax is collected from the seller, so it is the **supply** curve that shifts, by the amount of the tax.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the whole tax is passed to consumers"}
Consumers bear the whole tax only if demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic. Usually the burden is shared.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting the whole tax per unit on the old quantity"}
Revenue is the tax per unit times the **new** (lower) quantity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the deadweight loss"}
Both taxes and subsidies distort quantity from the equilibrium and reduce total surplus, unless they correct a market failure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 test calculations of revenue and burden and descriptions of effects. Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 test the shift of supply, the incidence and effectiveness of taxes and subsidies.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain the incidence*: show the new price paid by consumers, the net price received by producers and link the shares to elasticity. *Calculate*: tax revenue = tax per unit × new quantity. *Assess effectiveness*: weigh elasticity, administration and distribution, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A specific tax shifts supply upward by the tax per unit; an ad valorem tax pivots supply.
- Consumers' share of the tax rises when demand is more inelastic than supply; producers' share rises in the opposite case.
- Revenue = tax per unit × new quantity; deadweight loss arises from the lost trades.
- A subsidy shifts supply down by the subsidy, lowers price, raises quantity, costs the government and creates a welfare loss unless it corrects a failure.
- Effectiveness depends on elasticity, size, avoidance, distribution, cost and time.
:::
