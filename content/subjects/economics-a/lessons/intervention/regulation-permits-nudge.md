## Introduction

When a firm dumps waste into a river, when a vendor sells unsafe medicine or when a trawler empties a lake of fish, the market price does not stop the harm. Governments can respond with taxes and subsidies (treated in their own lesson), but they can also act directly on **quantities and behaviour**: they can set rules, limit output, ban a product, issue licences or permits, define who owns a resource, or redesign the way choices are presented to people.

You should know what an externality is, how a free market can produce too much or too little, and the idea of the social optimum. This lesson describes each of these tools, shows how to work out a quota and a permit scheme with figures, and explains how to weigh their strengths and weaknesses. Nationalisation and privatisation are in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regulation"}
Rules made by government that firms or consumers must obey, backed by penalties, such as emission standards, safety rules, minimum quality standards and price rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Deregulation"}
The removal or relaxation of rules so as to make markets more open and competitive, for example by removing licensing barriers to entry.
:::

:::definition{term="Production quota"}
A legal limit on the quantity that a firm, industry or country may produce or sell in a given period.
:::

:::definition{term="Prohibition and licence"}
A **prohibition** bans a good or activity outright; a **licence** permits it only for those who meet conditions (and often pay a fee).
:::

:::definition{term="Tradable pollution permit"}
A permit that allows its holder to emit a stated quantity of a pollutant. The total number of permits is capped by government, and permits can be bought and sold between firms.
:::

:::definition{term="Property rights"}
Legally recognised rights to own, use and sell a resource and to exclude others from it.
:::

:::definition{term="Nudge"}
A feature of the way choices are presented (a default option, framing, a reminder, a social comparison) that predictably changes behaviour without banning any option or changing prices much.
:::

## Regulation and deregulation

Regulation sets minimum standards: a maximum quantity of a pollutant a mine may release, a rule that buildings meet fire standards, or a requirement that drivers hold licences. It is **command and control**: the rule and the penalty replace price signals.

**Strengths.** It acts directly and can stop serious harm quickly; it gives certainty about the result (a limit is a limit); it is the only sensible approach for dangerous activities where no amount of harm is acceptable.

**Weaknesses.** Rules are the same for every firm, so low-cost and high-cost firms must both comply, which is inefficient; monitoring and enforcement cost money and can fail where inspectors are few or underpaid; firms have no reward for doing better than the standard; the regulator may lack information about the firm's true costs; and rules can raise costs for small firms more than for large ones.

**Deregulation** removes rules that act as barriers to entry (for example a licence system that protects existing operators) so that competition grows, prices fall and efficiency rises. Its risk is that, where the rules also protected consumers or the environment, harm may rise. Whether deregulation helps depends on whether the rule corrected a real market failure or merely protected producers.

## Production quotas, prohibitions and licences

A **quota** limits supply. It can protect a resource (a maximum fish catch in a lake) or support price (producers limited so that the price stays up). A **prohibition** removes the market entirely and a **licence** restricts it to approved suppliers.

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of a production quota (illustrative data)"}
A fictional market has demand P = 90 − 0.5Q and supply P = 10 + 0.5Q (P in US\$ per tonne, Q in tonnes a week). The regulator sets a quota of 60 tonnes a week.

1. Free market: 90 − 0.5Q = 10 + 0.5Q, so Q = **80** tonnes and P = **US\$50**.
2. With the quota only 60 tonnes are sold. Consumers will pay 90 − 0.5 × 60 = **US\$60** a tonne for the 60th tonne.
3. Producers would supply the 60th tonne for 10 + 0.5 × 60 = **US\$40**. The gap of US\$20 a tonne is a premium that goes to whoever holds the right to sell (or to black-market dealers if sales are above the legal price).
4. Consumer surplus falls from ½ × (90 − 50) × 80 = US\$1,600 to ½ × (90 − 60) × 60 = **US\$900** (a fall of US\$700); some of this is transferred to producers as a higher price, and some is lost.
5. Welfare loss = ½ × 20 × (80 − 60) = **US\$200**: the 20 tonnes that consumers value above their cost but are not produced.
:::

The result shows why quotas are criticised: they raise prices for consumers, create a premium that encourages cheating and illegal sales, and cannot adjust easily when demand changes. Their strength is certainty about the quantity, which matters when a resource is endangered.

## Tradable pollution permits

Under a **cap-and-trade** scheme the government sets a cap on total emissions, issues permits that add up to the cap, and lets firms trade them. A firm that can cut pollution cheaply cuts more and sells spare permits; a firm for which cutting is costly buys permits instead. The cap is met at the **lowest total cost**.

:::worked-solution{title="Why trading saves cost (illustrative data)"}
Three fictional firms each emit 100 tonnes of waste a year (300 in total). The government wants emissions cut to 180 tonnes, so it issues 60 permits to each firm. Each firm can cut up to 100 tonnes at a constant cost per tonne.

| Firm | Emissions now (tonnes) | Permits issued (tonnes) | Cost of cutting 1 tonne (US\$) | Cost of cutting 40 tonnes (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| A | 100 | 60 | 10 | 400 |
| B | 100 | 60 | 20 | 800 |
| C | 100 | 60 | 40 | 1,600 |

1. **Without trading** each firm must cut 40 tonnes. Total cost = 400 + 800 + 1,600 = **US\$2,800**.
2. **With trading** the 120-tonne reduction is made by the cheapest firms first. Firm A cuts all 100 tonnes (cost 100 × 10 = US\$1,000); firm B cuts the remaining 20 tonnes (cost 20 × 20 = US\$400). Firm C cuts nothing and buys permits. Total cost = **US\$1,400**.
3. The permit price settles at **US\$20**, the cost of cutting the last tonne (firm B's cost). At that price firm A gains by cutting everything and selling its 60 permits (it earns 60 × 20 = US\$1,200 against a cutting cost of US\$1,000), firm B is indifferent, and firm C prefers to buy permits.
4. Firm C buys 40 permits for 40 × 20 = US\$800, half of the US\$1,600 it would spend on cutting its own emissions. Firm B emits 80 tonnes, so it buys 20 permits (US\$400) and its net cost, 400 for cutting plus 400 for permits, is US\$800, the same as without trading.
5. Saving from trading = 2,800 − 1,400 = **US\$1,400**, for exactly the same total emissions.
:::

Check the permit flows: firm A holds 60 permits and emits 0, so it sells 60; firm B emits 80 and holds 60, so it buys 20; firm C emits 100 and holds 60, so it buys 40. Sales (60) equal purchases (20 + 40), and emissions are 0 + 80 + 100 = 180 tonnes, the cap. Each firm's net cost is: A 1,000 − 1,200 = −200 (a gain); B 800; C 800; total US\$1,400.

**Strengths.** The cap guarantees the environmental result; the cost of reaching it is the lowest; firms have a continuing incentive to find cheaper ways to cut pollution; the government may raise revenue if it sells the permits. **Weaknesses.** Setting the cap needs good information on the damage; monitoring is costly; permits given free to existing firms favour them and may be a barrier to new entrants; the permit price can swing; and pollution may become concentrated in one area ("hot spots").

## Property rights

Many externalities arise because nobody owns the resource: an open-access fishery, a river, a grazing commons. If the resource is not owned, no one has an incentive to protect it, and each user ignores the cost to others. Assigning **property rights** (to a community, a family or a firm) gives the owner an incentive to protect the resource's future value and a right to bargain with those who harm it. Community wildlife schemes that give local people a share in the income from wildlife are an example of this idea.

**Strengths.** It can solve the problem without continuing government spending; it uses market incentives. **Weaknesses.** Rights can be hard to define and enforce (fish and air move); bargaining is costly when many people are involved; the owners may be poor and unable to enforce their rights; and the original distribution of rights is itself a question of fairness.

## Nudge theory

Behavioural economics notes that people do not always decide as the textbook "rational consumer" would: they follow defaults, are influenced by how information is framed, and are affected by what other people do. A **nudge** uses these habits:

- **Default choice.** Automatic enrolment in a pension scheme with the option to opt out raises membership; organ donation is higher where consent is presumed unless a person opts out.
- **Framing.** "Ninety per cent of customers pay on time" is more effective than "ten per cent are late".
- **Reminders and social norms.** A text message reminding a patient of an appointment, or a letter showing how a household's water use compares with its neighbours'.
- **Making the good choice easy.** Placing healthy food at eye level in a canteen.

**Strengths.** Nudges are cheap, keep freedom of choice and can be tested on a small scale. **Weaknesses.** The effects may be small or short-lived; they may not work for addictive goods or for large external costs; they depend on knowing how people really behave; and some people regard them as manipulation.

:::keyfact{title="Comparing the tools"}
Rules and quotas give **certainty about quantity** but not about cost. Permits give certainty about quantity and **lower cost**. Taxes give certainty about price but not about quantity. Nudges and information are cheap but uncertain in effect. Choose by asking: how serious is the harm, how well is it measured, and how costly is enforcement?
:::

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
For a toxic pollutant that can cause serious illness, a legal limit is more appropriate than a permit scheme, because the harm from exceeding the limit is large and a certain result matters more than saving cost. For a pollutant whose damage is moderate and firms differ greatly in the cost of cutting it, tradable permits would reach the same target at lower cost. The right tool depends on the type of harm and on the ability of the regulator to monitor.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying permits and quotas are the same"}
A quota limits the output of a good; a permit limits the emission of a pollutant and can be traded. The ability to trade is what lowers the total cost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that regulation has to be enforced"}
A rule with no inspections and no penalties changes nothing. Mention monitoring and enforcement cost when evaluating.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a nudge as a ban or a tax"}
A nudge leaves all choices open and does not change prices much; it works through the way choices are presented.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and examples; Papers 2 and 3 ask how a government can control pollution or protect a resource and to assess the methods. Cambridge Paper 3 tests the tools in multiple choice, including simple permit or quota calculations, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of the choice between tools.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: say how the tool changes the behaviour that causes the market failure. *Calculate*: use the schedule, the permits, the cap and the cost of cutting. *Analyse*: follow the chain from the rule or permit to costs, output and the social optimum. *Evaluate*: compare tools by certainty, cost, enforcement and fairness, and reach a judgement for the case.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Regulation, quotas, prohibitions and licences act on quantities and behaviour; they give certainty but need monitoring and enforcement, and treat all firms alike.
- Deregulation removes barriers to entry to raise competition, but can remove protection that was needed.
- Tradable permits cap total pollution and let firms with low abatement costs cut most, so the cap is met at the lowest cost.
- Property rights give owners an incentive to protect a resource and to bargain, but rights are hard to define and enforce.
- Nudges (defaults, framing, reminders) change behaviour cheaply without removing choice, but effects are uncertain.
:::
