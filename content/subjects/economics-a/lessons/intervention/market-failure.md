## Introduction

Why does a farmer in Masvingo get a good price one year and a poor one the next, even though he works just as hard? Why does nobody in a neighbourhood pay for a street light that would help everyone? Why do factories discharge waste into a river that others rely on? These are all examples of **market failure**: situations in which the free market, left alone, does not allocate resources in the way that gives the greatest benefit to society.

Market failure is the starting point for every question about government intervention, so it is worth learning well. You should already know how the price mechanism allocates resources through demand and supply, and the meanings of the types of good (public, merit, demerit) from the lesson on classifying goods. The detailed externality model, and the policies governments use, have their own lessons; here we focus on the **sources** of market failure and the **case** for intervention.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Market failure"}
A situation in which the free market, left to itself, leads to a misallocation of resources: output is not at the level that maximises the welfare of society, so the market is not allocatively efficient.
:::

:::definition{term="Allocative efficiency"}
Resources are allocated so that the price paid for the last unit equals the marginal cost of producing it (P = MC); in a market with externalities the test is social benefit equal to social cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Free-rider problem"}
When a good is non-excludable, people can enjoy its benefits without paying, so private firms cannot charge for it and it is under-provided or not provided at all.
:::

:::definition{term="Asymmetric information"}
A situation in which one party to a transaction (usually the seller) has more or better information than the other, so that the less-informed party may make a poor decision.
:::

:::definition{term="Moral hazard"}
The tendency of a person who is protected from the cost of a risk (for example by insurance) to take more risk, because someone else bears the cost.
:::

## Sources of market failure

**1. Public goods.** A public good is non-excludable and non-rivalrous (street lighting, national defence, flood warning systems). Because people can free-ride, no one has an incentive to pay, so private firms do not supply it, although the benefit to society exceeds the cost. This is a complete failure: the market provides none.

**2. Externalities.** An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction. If a factory ignores the pollution cost it imposes on others, the market produces too much (**negative externality**); if people ignore the benefit that vaccination gives to others, the market produces too little (**positive externality**). The private price does not reflect the full social cost or benefit. (The model is developed in the lesson on externalities.)

**3. Merit and demerit goods.** Merit goods (education, vaccination) are under-consumed because consumers undervalue the benefit, often through imperfect information, and because they create positive externalities. Demerit goods (tobacco, hard drugs) are over-consumed because consumers underestimate the harm, and they create negative externalities.

**4. Imperfect and asymmetric information.** Consumers and producers do not have full information. A seller of a used vehicle knows more about it than the buyer; a patient cannot judge the quality of medical advice. With asymmetric information, a market can fail because good products are driven out or buyers pay too much.

**5. Moral hazard.** If insurance pays for losses, the insured person has less incentive to take care. This raises the cost of claims and can lead to higher premiums for all.

**6. Monopoly power.** A firm with market power restricts output to raise price above marginal cost. Allocative efficiency (P = MC) is lost and consumers pay more. Competition is the market's own protection, but where barriers to entry are high the market may not provide it.

**7. Immobility of factors of production.** Labour that cannot easily change occupation or location, and capital that is tied to one use, cannot move to where it is most valuable. The result may be structural unemployment and idle resources.

**8. Common property (ZIMSEC detail).** Resources that nobody owns (open-access fishing grounds, grazing land, forests) tend to be over-used because each user gains the whole benefit of taking more while the cost of depletion is shared. This is sometimes called the tragedy of the commons.

**9. Inequality.** The market allocates goods according to ability to pay, not need. A market outcome can be efficient yet leave some people unable to afford food, health care or education. Whether this is a "failure" is partly a value judgement.

**10. Volatile prices of primary products.** The prices of agricultural and mineral products fluctuate widely because demand and supply are both inelastic in the short run, and supply depends on weather and harvests. This makes the incomes of producers unstable and makes planning difficult, which is a reason for some forms of intervention.

## Worked examples

All figures below are illustrative.

:::worked-solution{title="A public good and the free-rider problem"}
A street light would cost US\$100. Four households each value it at US\$30.
1. Total benefit to society = 4 × 30 = **US\$120**.
2. Net benefit = 120 − 100 = **US\$20**, so providing the light is worth doing.
3. But no single household values it at more than US\$30, which is below the cost of US\$100, and each hopes the others will pay.
4. A private firm cannot charge, because anyone can use the light for free. The market provides **none**, although society would gain US\$20. Government provision, paid for by taxation, solves the problem.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Monopoly power and the welfare loss"}
Demand is P = 90 − 0.05Q and the marginal cost is a constant US\$30 (P in US\$, Q in units).
1. Competitive outcome: P = MC, so 90 − 0.05Q = 30 and Q = **1,200** at a price of US\$30.
2. Monopoly: set MR = MC. MR = 90 − 0.1Q, so 90 − 0.1Q = 30 and Q = **600**; the price is 90 − 0.05 × 600 = **US\$60**.
3. Price is US\$30 above marginal cost, and output is 600 units below the efficient level.
4. Welfare loss = ½ × 600 × 30 = **US\$9,000** (the triangle of lost surplus).
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Volatile primary-product prices"}
Maize supply in the short run is fixed by the harvest. Demand is P = 160 − 0.02Q (P in US\$ per tonne, Q in tonnes).
1. Good harvest, Q = 6,000: P = 160 − 120 = **US\$40**. Farmers' revenue = 40 × 6,000 = US\$240,000.
2. Poor harvest, Q = 4,000: P = 160 − 80 = **US\$80**. Revenue = 80 × 4,000 = US\$320,000.
3. Quantity falls by 33.3% and price rises by 100%, so the price elasticity of demand between the two points is −33.3 ÷ 100 = **−0.33** (inelastic).
4. A one-third fall in output doubles the price. Because demand is inelastic, prices are very unstable, and farmers' incomes swing from year to year; a good harvest brings a **lower** income than a poor one.
:::

:::example{title="Information failure: used cars"}
In a fictional market half the used cars are good (worth US\$8,000) and half are poor (worth US\$4,000). Buyers cannot tell them apart, so they offer the average, US\$6,000. Owners of good cars are unwilling to sell at that price, so more of the cars offered are poor. Buyers learn this and offer even less. The market shrinks, even though buyers and sellers of good cars would both gain from trade. Guarantees, inspections and certificates are ways of reducing the information gap.
:::

## The case for intervention, and its limits

Governments intervene to correct these failures: to provide public goods, to tax or regulate negative externalities, to subsidise or provide merit goods, to inform consumers, to promote competition, to retrain labour, to protect common resources and to stabilise the prices of primary products. The aim is to move the market towards a better outcome for society.

But intervention has costs. Governments also lack full information, can be influenced by interest groups, and can create **government failure** (the lesson on government failure). A sensible evaluation asks whether the failure is large, whether the policy can correct it at a reasonable cost, and whether there are unintended effects.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the sources of market failure and the reasons for intervention. Common property resources are ZIMSEC detail; Cambridge uses imperfect competition, asymmetric information and moral hazard. The externality model with calculations is a separate lesson (Cambridge A Level).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying market failure means the market has collapsed"}
Market failure means resources are **misallocated**: the market works, but the outcome is not the best for society.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a public good with a good provided by the government"}
A public good is defined by non-excludability and non-rivalry. Education is often provided by governments, but it is a merit good, not a public good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing asymmetric information with moral hazard"}
Asymmetric information is about unequal knowledge **before** a transaction. Moral hazard is a change in behaviour **after** the risk is covered.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for the causes of market failure; Paper 2 data questions use pollution, public goods or monopoly; Paper 3 essays ask for explanations and discussions. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the sources and types of failure in multiple choice, and Paper 2 asks for explanation and assessment of intervention.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the precise meaning of market failure. *Explain*: name the source and say why the market misallocates resources. *Calculate*: use P = MC, surplus triangles or revenue changes. *Evaluate*: weigh the size of the failure against the cost and effects of the policy, and say what government failure might arise.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Market failure is a misallocation of resources: the free market does not give the best outcome for society.
- Its sources are public goods, externalities, merit and demerit goods, information problems, monopoly power, immobility, common property and inequality.
- Because of inelastic demand and supply, the prices of primary products are volatile, which makes incomes unstable.
- Intervention can correct failures but carries costs and the risk of government failure.
- Always name the failure, say why the market misallocates, and compare the market outcome with the efficient one.
:::
