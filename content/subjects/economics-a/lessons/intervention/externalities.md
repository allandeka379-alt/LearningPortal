## Introduction

When a gold panner upstream in a fictional river washes mud into the water, the people who fish and drink downstream pay a cost that the panner never counts. When a farmer vaccinates her cattle, her neighbours' herds are safer too, but she receives no payment for that benefit. These are **externalities**: effects of production or consumption on people who are not part of the transaction. Because the price in the market reflects only the costs and benefits to the buyer and seller, the market quantity is wrong from society's point of view.

You should know how price is set by demand and supply, the meaning of marginal cost and marginal benefit, and the idea of market failure. This lesson builds the model of externalities and shows how to measure the welfare loss. Policies to correct externalities (taxes, subsidies, regulation, tradable permits) are treated in their own lessons, and the method for weighing the total costs and benefits of a project is in the lesson on cost-benefit analysis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Externality"}
A cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on a third party and is not reflected in the market price.
:::

:::definition{term="Private cost and private benefit"}
The cost to the producer of making a good (private cost) and the benefit to the consumer of using it (private benefit).
:::

:::definition{term="External cost and external benefit"}
The cost (or benefit) that falls on third parties: for example the harm of pollution, or the benefit of a neighbour's vaccination.
:::

:::definition{term="Social cost and social benefit"}
The total cost (or benefit) to society: **social cost = private cost + external cost**, and **social benefit = private benefit + external benefit**.
:::

:::definition{term="Social optimum"}
The output at which marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost (MSB = MSC), the output that maximises the welfare of society.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulae"}
MSC = MPC + MEC (marginal private cost plus marginal external cost).
MSB = MPB + MEB (marginal private benefit plus marginal external benefit).
Free market output: MPB = MPC. Social optimum: MSB = MSC.
:::

## The four types

| | Externality of production | Externality of consumption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Negative** (external cost) | Pollution by a factory or a mine | Loud music, smoking in public places |
| **Positive** (external benefit) | A firm that trains workers who later move to other firms | Vaccination, education |

**Negative externality.** MSC lies above MPC. The market produces or consumes where MPB = MPC, which is **more** than the social optimum, so there is over-provision.

**Positive externality.** MSB lies above MPB. The market produces or consumes where MPB = MPC, which is **less** than the social optimum, so there is under-provision.

The vertical gap between the curves is the marginal external cost (or benefit). The loss of welfare from producing at the wrong output is the **welfare loss (deadweight loss)**: the triangle between MSC and MSB over the units that should not have been produced (or the extra units that should have been).

## A schedule example (negative externality of production)

A firm's output has a marginal external cost of US\$4 a unit (illustrative data).

| Output (units) | MPB = price (US\$) | MPC (US\$) | MEC (US\$) | MSC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 40 | 8 | 4 | 12 |
| 2 | 36 | 12 | 4 | 16 |
| 3 | 32 | 16 | 4 | 20 |
| 4 | 28 | 20 | 4 | 24 |
| 5 | 24 | 24 | 4 | 28 |
| 6 | 20 | 28 | 4 | 32 |
| 7 | 16 | 32 | 4 | 36 |
| 8 | 12 | 36 | 4 | 40 |

:::worked-solution{title="Market output, social optimum and welfare loss"}
1. Free market: output where MPB = MPC. At 5 units both are US\$24, so the market produces **5** units.
2. Social optimum: MSB (= MPB, as there is no external benefit) equals MSC. At 4 units MSB is US\$28 and MSC is US\$24, so the fourth unit is worth producing. At 5 units MSB is US\$24 and MSC is US\$28, so the fifth unit costs society more than it is worth. The optimum is **4** units.
3. Over-production = 5 − 4 = **1 unit**.
4. Welfare loss = MSC − MSB on the extra unit = 28 − 24 = **US\$4**. (With more units the loss is the sum of the gaps for each unit beyond the optimum.)
:::

## A linear example (negative externality of production)

Marginal private benefit (demand) is P = 100 − 0.5Q, marginal private cost is P = 10 + 0.25Q, and the marginal external cost is a constant US\$15 per unit (P in US\$, Q in units).

:::worked-solution{title="Welfare loss from linear curves"}
1. Free market: 100 − 0.5Q = 10 + 0.25Q, so 90 = 0.75Q and Q = **120** at a price of 100 − 60 = **US\$40**.
2. Marginal social cost: MSC = MPC + 15 = 25 + 0.25Q.
3. Social optimum: 100 − 0.5Q = 25 + 0.25Q, so 75 = 0.75Q and Q = **100**, at a price of US\$50.
4. At Q = 120 the MSC is 25 + 30 = US\$55, which is US\$15 more than the MSB of US\$40.
5. Welfare loss = ½ × (120 − 100) × 15 = **US\$150**, the triangle between MSC and MSB over the 20 units of over-production.
6. The total external cost on the 120 units produced is 120 × 15 = US\$1,800, but the net loss to society is only the US\$150: the first 100 units are worth producing, and the extra 20 still give consumers some benefit, so only the excess of cost over benefit on them is lost.
:::

## A linear example (positive externality of consumption)

Demand (MPB) is P = 94 − 0.5Q, supply (MPC = MSC) is P = 10 + 0.25Q, and the marginal external benefit is a constant US\$12 a unit.

:::worked-solution{title="Under-provision of a positive externality"}
1. Free market: 94 − 0.5Q = 10 + 0.25Q, so Q = **112** at a price of US\$38.
2. MSB = MPB + 12 = 106 − 0.5Q.
3. Social optimum: 106 − 0.5Q = 10 + 0.25Q, so Q = **128**, at a price of US\$42.
4. Under-provision = 128 − 112 = **16 units**.
5. Welfare loss = ½ × 12 × 16 = **US\$96**.
:::

## Interpreting the results

:::example{title="Reading an externality question"}
In a fictional town a brickworks emits smoke that raises the health costs of residents by US\$3 for every tonne of bricks produced. If the question gives MPB and MPC schedules, first find the market output (MPB = MPC), then add US\$3 to MPC to get MSC, then find the optimum (MSB = MSC). With a negative externality the market output is always higher than the optimum. The size of the welfare loss depends on how large the external cost is and on how far output exceeds the optimum: a larger external cost makes the gap in output and the loss bigger.
:::

## Evaluation points

- **Measurement.** External costs and benefits are hard to measure: how do you put a value on a polluted river or on better health? Estimates are uncertain.
- **Size and distribution.** An externality may be small or large; it may fall on a few people or many, and on the poor or the wealthy.
- **Time.** Some externalities, such as climate change or soil erosion, appear over a long period.
- **Property rights.** Some externalities arise because nobody owns the resource. Giving people rights to the river, for example, may allow them to negotiate, but this is costly when many people are involved.
- **Government action.** Taxes, subsidies and regulations can correct the externality but need accurate information; the policy lessons cover them.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the four types and the social-cost and social-benefit relationships. Cambridge (A Level) expects marginal analysis and diagrams with welfare loss; ZIMSEC (Form 5) asks for private, external and social costs, with examples from pollution and from the benefits of education and health.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong sign for a positive externality"}
For a positive externality MSB = MPB **plus** MEB (above MPB). MSC is not changed. For a negative one MSC = MPC **plus** MEC.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking the welfare loss as the whole external cost"}
The welfare loss is only the triangle for the units beyond the social optimum. It is not the external cost on every unit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the free market produces where MSB = MSC"}
The free market produces where MPB = MPC. The social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 tests the meaning of the terms; Paper 2 gives costs and benefits to calculate; Paper 3 essays ask for the effect of pollution and the benefit of education. Cambridge Paper 3 tests the calculation or diagram reading in multiple choice, and Paper 4 asks for analysis and evaluation of externalities, including welfare loss.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: private, external, social. *Calculate*: add the external cost to MPC (or MEB to MPB), find the equilibria and the triangle. *Explain*: say why the price does not include the external effect. *Analyse*: show the over- or under-provision with figures. *Evaluate*: consider measurement problems and the size of the externality.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An externality is a cost or benefit to a third party that is not included in the market price.
- Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
- A negative externality means over-provision (MSC above MPC); a positive externality means under-provision (MSB above MPB).
- The free market output is at MPB = MPC; the social optimum is at MSB = MSC.
- The welfare loss is the triangle between MSB and MSC over the units that are over- or under-provided.
:::
