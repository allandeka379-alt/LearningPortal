## Introduction

Every economy must decide what to produce, how to produce it and for whom. In a market economy these decisions are made mainly by buyers and sellers responding to prices. In a **centrally planned** economy a state authority makes most of them. Few economies are at either extreme. Zimbabwe, like most countries, has a **mixed economy**: private firms, households, the state and state-owned enterprises all take decisions, and the government guides the economy through national plans, the annual budget and laws and regulations.

You should already know the features of market, planned and mixed economies (the lesson on economic systems) and why governments intervene in markets. This lesson explains how planners allocate resources, weighs the strengths and weaknesses of central planning, and describes in general terms how economic decisions are planned in Zimbabwe. The description is factual and neutral and is simplified: institutions and plan names change, so always check the details in your syllabus and class notes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Central planning"}
A way of organising an economy in which a state planning authority, instead of the price mechanism, decides what is produced, by whom and how it is distributed, using targets, quotas and fixed prices.
:::

:::definition{term="Command economy"}
An economic system in which the state owns most resources and decides the allocation of resources through a central plan.
:::

:::definition{term="Indicative planning"}
Planning in a mixed economy in which the government sets targets and priorities and tries to influence private decisions with incentives and information, without compelling firms to meet the targets.
:::

:::definition{term="National development plan"}
A medium-term document setting out a government's economic objectives, priority sectors, targets and how public resources will be used to reach them.
:::

:::definition{term="Budget"}
The government's annual statement of planned revenue and expenditure, which allocates public resources among competing uses.
:::

## How central planners allocate resources

1. **Setting objectives.** The authority decides national aims: for example faster growth, food security, more industry, or access to basic services.
2. **Collecting information.** It gathers information on resources (labour, land, capital, minerals), existing output and needs.
3. **Setting targets.** Output targets are set for sectors, regions and enterprises, often in physical terms (tonnes of maize, megawatts of electricity).
4. **Balancing the plan.** Planners try to ensure that the inputs required for each target are available: steel for construction, power for factories. Targets for different sectors must be consistent (a "material balance").
5. **Allocating inputs and setting prices.** The authority allocates materials, labour and finance to enterprises, and fixes prices or uses rationing.
6. **Monitoring.** Actual output is compared with targets and the plan is revised.

:::worked-solution{title="Plan targets and outcomes (illustrative data)"}
The planning authority of a fictional "Country X" sets annual targets and compares them with results.

| Item (Country X plan) | Target | Actual | Actual as % of target | Difference |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Maize (thousand tonnes) | 1,800 | 1,530 | 85.0 | −270 |
| Cement (thousand tonnes) | 900 | 1,050 | 116.7 | 150 |
| Electricity (GWh) | 9,000 | 8,100 | 90.0 | −900 |
| Cotton (thousand tonnes) | 200 | 150 | 75.0 | −50 |

1. Actual as a percentage of target = actual ÷ target × 100. For maize 1,530 ÷ 1,800 × 100 = **85.0%**.
2. Cement exceeded its target by 150 thousand tonnes (**116.7%**), while the other three items fell short.
3. Cotton is furthest below target (**75.0%**).
4. In a planned system, shortfalls in one item can disrupt others: if electricity falls 10% short, factories that need it may be unable to meet their own targets, even if the targets for those factories were correct. Over-fulfilling one target (cement) may also waste resources if there is no demand for it.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Reading a budget allocation (illustrative data)"}
The budget of "Country X" allocates a total of US\$20.0 billion.

| Spending area | Allocation (US\$ billion) | Share (%) | Pie angle (degrees) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Education | 5.0 | 25.0 | 90 |
| Health | 2.5 | 12.5 | 45 |
| Roads and energy | 3.0 | 15.0 | 54 |
| Agriculture | 1.5 | 7.5 | 27 |
| Debt service | 2.0 | 10.0 | 36 |
| Other | 6.0 | 30.0 | 108 |

1. Share = allocation ÷ total × 100; for education 5.0 ÷ 20.0 × 100 = **25.0%**.
2. Pie angle = share × 360° ÷ 100; for education 25% × 3.6 = **90°**. The angles add up to 360°.
3. The budget shows the priorities of a government: here, education has the largest named allocation. Because the money is limited, spending more on one area means less for another: this is the **opportunity cost** of budget choices.
:::

## Strengths of central planning

- **Mobilising resources for national goals.** Resources can be directed to priorities such as building infrastructure, expanding schools and clinics, or raising output of a staple food.
- **Equity and basic needs.** Basic goods and services can be provided to all, regardless of ability to pay.
- **Co-ordination of large projects** that private firms would not undertake because of cost or long payback (dams, railways).
- **Avoiding wasteful duplication and competition** in some activities, and exploiting economies of scale.
- **Stability of prices and employment targets** can be set as policy aims.
- **Long-term view.** Planners can look beyond short-run profit.

## Weaknesses of central planning

- **The information problem.** An economy involves millions of products and decisions. A central authority cannot collect and process all the information on costs and preferences that prices signal automatically, so targets may be wrong.
- **No price signals.** Without prices that reflect scarcity, shortages and surpluses persist and resources are misallocated.
- **Weak incentives.** If workers and managers do not gain from effort and innovation, productivity and quality may be low.
- **Rigidity and delay.** Changing the plan takes time, so the economy responds slowly to shocks, new technology or changes in taste.
- **Bureaucracy and the cost of administration**, and the risk of corruption in the allocation of scarce inputs.
- **Limited choice for consumers** and the growth of black markets where prices are held below equilibrium.
- **Wrong targets.** Quantity targets may be met by cutting quality, or by producing goods that nobody wants.

:::keyfact{title="Plan, price or both?"}
Most economies mix the two. Planning is strongest for public goods and infrastructure, where markets fail; the price mechanism is strongest for ordinary goods and services, where information is dispersed and consumer choice matters. The important question is not "plan or market?" but "which decisions are better made by which?"
:::

## Economic decision-making in Zimbabwe: a general description

The following is a simplified description. A reviewer should check institutional detail, since names of plans and offices change.

**Long-term and medium-term plans.** Zimbabwe has a long-term national vision and uses medium-term national development plans to set priorities, targets and the role of different sectors. In the 1980s the government used national development plans (for example the Transitional National Development Plan and a five-year plan) in a mixed economy. Later medium-term programmes have included economic reform and stabilisation programmes and national development strategies, such as National Development Strategy 1 (2021-2025), which is linked to the national goal (Vision 2030) of becoming an upper-middle-income economy. These documents are statements of priorities and targets, typically grouped by sector or "pillar", and they guide public spending, but most economic activity is carried out by private households, firms and farmers.

**The annual budget cycle.** The government's main tool for turning plans into resources is the annual budget. In outline:

1. **Preparation.** The Ministry of Finance issues guidance to ministries on spending ceilings and priorities. Ministries submit their spending requests and negotiate with the Treasury.
2. **Consultation.** Business, labour, civil society and Parliamentary committees are consulted and give their views.
3. **Cabinet approval.** The Minister of Finance presents the proposals to Cabinet.
4. **Presentation to Parliament.** The Minister presents the Budget Statement to Parliament, typically in the last months of the year, setting out expected revenue, planned spending, the budget deficit and how it will be financed. The financial year follows the calendar year.
5. **Approval.** Parliament debates and passes the necessary legislation, including an appropriation bill that authorises spending and a finance bill that sets tax measures.
6. **Implementation.** The Treasury releases funds to ministries during the year, and ministries and state enterprises carry out the programmes.
7. **Audit and review.** The Auditor-General examines the accounts and Parliament reviews them.

**Other decision-makers.** The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe makes monetary policy decisions; local authorities take decisions on local services; state-owned enterprises operate in sectors such as energy, rail and telecommunications; and private firms and farmers take most production decisions in response to prices.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A national development plan can give direction to public spending and help co-ordinate investment in roads, energy and schools, and an annual budget that is debated in Parliament gives some public accountability. But a plan can succeed only if it is realistic and financed: targets that do not match the budget, or budgets that are changed in-year by shocks to revenue, weaken it. Because most production is carried out by private households and firms, results also depend on whether incentives, prices and the business environment encourage them to act in line with the plan. A plan is therefore best seen as a framework for priorities and public spending, not as a substitute for markets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC only"}
This concept is ZIMSEC (Form 6). It is not a Cambridge 9708 concept. In an essay on planning, use the general analysis (information, incentives, co-ordination) and apply it to Zimbabwe's mixed economy with care and balance.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating Zimbabwe's planning as a command economy"}
Zimbabwe has a mixed economy. Plans and budgets guide public decisions but most output is produced by private decision-makers. Do not describe the whole economy as centrally planned.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing weaknesses with no balance"}
Central planning has real strengths, such as co-ordinating large projects and providing basic services. A good answer gives both sides and a judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the budget and the plan"}
A plan sets medium-term objectives and targets; the budget allocates actual money for one year. The budget is the main way a plan is made real.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for features and definitions; Paper 2 gives a plan or budget table to read (shares, percentage of target, pie angles); Paper 3 essays ask for the advantages and disadvantages of central planning and for the process of economic decision-making in Zimbabwe. It is not examined in Cambridge.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe*: the steps (objectives, targets, allocation, monitoring; or the budget stages). *Explain*: why planners lack information, or why incentives matter. *Calculate*: percentage of target and budget shares. *Discuss*: strengths and weaknesses, then a judgement about the extent to which a plan or the market should decide.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Central planning replaces the price mechanism with targets, quotas and fixed prices set by a state authority.
- Strengths include mobilising resources for national goals, providing basic needs and co-ordinating large projects.
- Weaknesses include the information problem, no price signals, weak incentives, rigidity and black markets.
- Zimbabwe has a mixed economy; national development plans set priorities and the annual budget allocates public resources through preparation, Cabinet, Parliament, implementation and audit.
- A plan is a framework for public decisions, not a substitute for markets; success depends on realistic targets, finance and incentives.
:::
