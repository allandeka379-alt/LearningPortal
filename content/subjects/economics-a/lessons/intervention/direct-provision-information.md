## Introduction

A child in Gweru walks into a government primary school and a patient in Mutare queues at a council clinic. In both cases the service is provided by the state and is free or very cheap at the point of use. Elsewhere, a packet of cigarettes carries a health warning and a radio campaign tells listeners how to prevent cholera. These are two of the ways in which a government can respond to market failure: it can **provide** the good or service itself, or it can **inform** people so that they make better decisions.

You should already know what public goods and merit goods are, why a free market may under-provide them, and what an externality is. This lesson looks at the two policies, how they are paid for, and how to judge them. Regulation, permits and nudges, taxes and subsidies, and privatisation are in their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Direct provision"}
The supply of goods and services by the government (central or local), paid for out of public funds, either free or at a price below the cost of supply.
:::

:::definition{term="Public good"}
A good that is **non-excludable** (people cannot be prevented from using it) and **non-rival** (one person's use does not reduce what is left for others), such as street lighting or national defence. Because of the free-rider problem, private firms will not supply it.
:::

:::definition{term="Merit good"}
A good that is under-consumed in a free market, because consumers do not fully recognise its benefits or because it generates external benefits. Education and basic health care are standard examples.
:::

:::definition{term="Provision of information"}
Action by the government to give consumers and producers accurate information, for example health warnings, product labelling, public campaigns and compulsory disclosure, so that they can decide with fewer errors.
:::

:::definition{term="Imperfect (asymmetric) information"}
A situation in which one party to a transaction knows more than the other, or in which people lack the knowledge needed to judge the true costs and benefits of a choice.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Provide, produce and pay are different things"}
The government may **finance** a service (pay for it from taxation), **provide** it (make it available to citizens) and **produce** it (run the clinics and employ the staff). It can do the first without the last, for example by paying private or church-run schools and clinics to supply the service.
:::

## Why governments provide goods directly

1. **Public goods.** A road lit at night or a flood-control embankment benefits everyone nearby whether or not they pay. Each person has an incentive to be a **free rider**, so no firm can collect enough revenue to cover its costs. The market supplies none, so government provision (or government payment for it) is needed.
2. **Merit goods.** Education and primary health care give large benefits to the user and to others (a healthier, more skilled workforce, less spread of disease, higher productivity). People on low incomes may not be able to pay, and some do not see the long-term value, so too little is bought. State provision aims at a level closer to the **social optimum**.
3. **Equity.** If the service is free or cheap, access does not depend on income. This is an equity argument as well as an efficiency one.
4. **Natural monopoly and scale.** Some networks (main roads, water mains) are so costly to duplicate that one supplier is efficient. The state may own it so that prices are not set at monopoly levels.

### How is it financed?

| Source | How it works | Main problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taxation | Income, sales and other taxes pay for the service | Taxes have their own distortions; the money has an opportunity cost |
| Borrowing | The government borrows now and repays later | Interest and repayment burden on future taxpayers |
| User charges (fees) | Users pay part of the cost | May exclude low-income users, which defeats the aim |
| Donor or aid funds | Grants or concessional loans | May be unreliable and short-term |

## Evaluating direct provision

**Strengths.** The good reaches people who could not pay; the quantity can be chosen to match the social benefit; there is no need for the provider to make a profit; public goods that no firm would supply become available.

**Weaknesses.**

- **Opportunity cost.** Money spent on one service cannot be spent on another. If a government spends US\$1.5 million on a road, that money cannot build clinics.
- **Cost and efficiency.** Without competition or a profit motive there may be less pressure to control costs, so unit costs can be high (a form of X-inefficiency), and the service may not respond to users' needs.
- **Rationing by queue.** If the price is zero and supply is limited, demand exceeds supply and the service is rationed by waiting, by distance or by connections instead of by price.
- **Information problem.** The government does not know people's true preferences or the true social benefit, so it may provide too much or too little, or the wrong kind.
- **Financing.** The scheme depends on tax revenue and on a political decision each year, so quality can fall when budgets are cut.
- **Quality and opting out.** If the public service is poor, richer households pay privately for better, which can leave the state service for the poor with fewer influential users demanding improvement.

:::worked-solution{title="Cost of provision (illustrative data)"}
A fictional district health authority spends US\$4,200,000 a year on clinics for a population of 600,000, and the clinics record 210,000 patient visits.

1. Spending per head of population = 4,200,000 ÷ 600,000 = **US\$7**.
2. Cost per visit = 4,200,000 ÷ 210,000 = **US\$20**.
3. A new road scheme costs US\$1,500,000. As a share of the clinic budget this is 1,500,000 ÷ 4,200,000 × 100 = **35.7%** (to one decimal place), which indicates the scale of the opportunity cost: the same money could fund about a third of the clinic service for a year.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Comparing providers by cost per visit (illustrative data)"}
Three clinics report their annual running cost and patient visits.

| Clinic | Annual cost (US\$) | Patient visits | Cost per visit (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Public clinic A | 900,000 | 30,000 | 30.00 |
| Public clinic B | 1,200,000 | 32,000 | 37.50 |
| Contracted clinic C | 1,000,000 | 40,000 | 25.00 |

1. Cost per visit = annual cost ÷ visits, as in the last column.
2. Clinic C, run by a private operator paid by the government, has the lowest cost per visit; clinic B has the highest.
3. The comparison is incomplete: the clinics may treat different patients (a remote clinic has fewer, sicker patients), and quality and outcomes are not shown. Cost alone does not decide which is better, but it suggests that **paying a private producer** may be a way of keeping the benefits of public finance with greater efficiency.
:::

## Provision of information

Many markets fail because buyers do not know enough. A smoker may underestimate the health costs of smoking; a patient cannot judge a medicine; a farmer may not know the best seed variety. The government can respond by:

- **Labelling and health warnings** on tobacco, alcohol, food and medicines.
- **Public campaigns** on radio, in clinics and schools (hand-washing, HIV prevention, immunisation).
- **Compulsory disclosure** by firms (ingredients, interest rates on loans, safety standards).
- **Market information services** such as price information for farmers by mobile phone.

The aim is to move demand for demerit goods **left** (consumers value them less once they know the harm) and for merit goods **right** (they value them more), so that the market quantity moves closer to the social optimum.

:::worked-solution{title="Cost-effectiveness of an information campaign (illustrative data)"}
A fictional town of 50,000 adults runs a campaign costing US\$60,000. The share of adults who smoke falls from 18% to 15.5%.

1. Fall in the share of smokers = 18 − 15.5 = **2.5 percentage points**.
2. Fewer smokers = 2.5% of 50,000 = **1,250** adults.
3. Cost per adult who stopped = 60,000 ÷ 1,250 = **US\$48**.
4. This assumes the campaign caused all of the fall. Other causes (a rise in the cigarette price, a ban in public places) may have contributed, so the true cost per adult is probably higher.
:::

### Strengths and weaknesses of information

**Strengths.** It is cheap compared with provision or a ban; it respects freedom of choice; it can change long-term attitudes; it can be targeted.

**Weaknesses.**

- It may be **ignored**: people who are addicted or short-sighted may not respond.
- It takes **time** to show results, and effects are hard to measure.
- **Literacy and access**: written warnings and web campaigns do not reach everyone; radio and local languages matter.
- **Competing messages**: producers spend heavily on advertising which may outweigh the government's message.
- The information may itself be **wrong, biased or confusing**, or the public may distrust the source.
- It does not help where the problem is not ignorance, for example a public good or a person who cannot afford the product.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
Information campaigns can be a sensible first step in reducing the use of a demerit good, because they are cheaper than a ban and keep choice. However, a campaign alone is unlikely to cut consumption much if the product is addictive and heavily advertised, so a government may need to combine it with taxes or regulation. The best mix depends on how addictive the good is, how many people can be reached, and how large the external costs are.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require direct provision (public goods and merit goods, financing and efficiency problems) and the provision of information. ZIMSEC (Form 5) often asks about the provision of education and health by the state and its financing; Cambridge (AS Level) asks you to *explain* direct provision and the provision of information and to apply them to merit, demerit and public goods.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that a public good is one provided by the government"}
A public good is defined by its characteristics (non-excludable and non-rival), not by who supplies it. A government school is a merit good that the state happens to provide; a street lamp is a public good.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the opportunity cost"}
"Free" provision is never free: someone pays through taxes, and the money could have been used elsewhere. Always mention the opportunity cost when evaluating provision.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating information as a complete solution"}
Information works only if people lack knowledge and respond to it. For addictive goods, public goods and low incomes it is not enough on its own.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and examples; Papers 2 and 3 ask why the government provides health, education or roads and how it should pay. Cambridge Paper 1 tests the meaning of the terms in multiple choice, and Paper 2 asks for explanation and evaluation of direct provision or information in a data response or short essay.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the characteristics (non-excludable, non-rival; merit). *Explain*: link the market failure (free rider, external benefit, imperfect information) to the policy. *Calculate*: cost per head or per user, and percentage shares. *Analyse*: develop a chain from the policy to consumption, to the social optimum. *Evaluate*: weigh the benefits against the opportunity cost, efficiency problems and the limits of information, and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Direct provision means the government supplies and pays for goods such as public goods and merit goods, because a free market under-provides them.
- Financing is by taxation, borrowing, user charges or aid, and every method has an opportunity cost.
- Problems include high unit costs without competition, rationing by queue, poor information about preferences and dependence on budgets.
- The government can finance a service without producing it, for example by paying a private operator.
- Information (labels, campaigns, disclosure) corrects imperfect information and shifts demand towards the social optimum, but can be ignored, slow and costly to measure.
:::
