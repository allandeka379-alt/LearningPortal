## Introduction

Electricity, railways, telephones, water and air transport are often at the centre of debates about who should own the means of production. In many countries, including Zimbabwe, the state owns enterprises (often called **parastatals** or state-owned enterprises) in sectors such as energy, rail, telecommunications and air transport. Over the years governments and citizens have discussed whether such enterprises should stay in the state's hands, be run more like businesses, or be sold in whole or part to private owners. Economists do not agree on one answer, so the questions require balanced reasoning, not a slogan.

You should know the difference between public and private sector, the meaning of efficiency, and how monopoly can harm consumers. This lesson defines the terms, sets out the arguments on both sides, shows how to analyse a before-and-after case with figures, and explains why regulation matters after a sale.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nationalisation"}
The transfer of an enterprise or industry from private ownership to the state (the public sector).
:::

:::definition{term="Privatisation"}
The transfer of ownership of an enterprise or asset from the state to private owners, usually by selling shares or the business. Partial privatisation sells only part of the ownership.
:::

:::definition{term="Commercialisation (ZIMSEC detail)"}
Changing a state-owned enterprise so that it is run on business lines, with commercial targets (profit or cost recovery), managerial independence and cuts in subsidies, while it stays in public ownership.
:::

:::definition{term="Deregulation and liberalisation"}
The removal of legal barriers to entry so that private firms can compete with the state enterprise. This is different from privatisation: ownership may not change.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Three different decisions"}
**Ownership** (state or private), **management** (commercial or not) and **market structure** (monopoly or competition) are separate. A state enterprise can be commercialised without being sold, and a privatised monopoly can still be a monopoly. Much of the effect of privatisation depends on whether competition and regulation follow.
:::

## Why governments nationalise

1. **Natural monopoly.** In industries such as electricity grids, where one network is cheapest, the state can prevent a private monopolist from charging high prices.
2. **Strategic or essential services.** Defence-related industries, water or a rail network may be seen as too important to leave to profit decisions.
3. **Externalities and public interest.** A service with large external benefits (rural transport, electrification) may not be profitable but is socially valuable.
4. **Rescue of a failing firm** that provides many jobs.
5. **Equity and control of the commanding heights**, as a matter of principle, when the government believes key assets should be owned for the public.

## Why governments privatise or commercialise

1. **Efficiency.** Private owners have a profit motive and are exposed to the discipline of shareholders and, if there is competition, of rivals. Managers may cut costs and improve service.
2. **Revenue and the budget.** Sale proceeds raise money, and subsidies to loss-making enterprises stop, easing pressure on the budget.
3. **Competition.** Opening a market can lead to lower prices and wider choice.
4. **Less political interference.** State firms may be told to hire staff, keep prices low or serve uneconomic routes, which raises costs.
5. **Wider ownership.** Selling shares to many people spreads ownership.

## Effects of privatisation

| Area | Possible gains | Possible costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Efficiency | Lower costs, higher productivity, better service | Little change if the firm stays a monopoly; neglect of unprofitable services |
| Prices | Fall if competition grows | Rise if a private monopoly is not regulated |
| Revenue | One-off sale proceeds; end of subsidies | Loss of future profits (dividends) that the state would have received; sale may be below true value |
| Employment | Jobs may grow if the firm expands | Job losses as the firm cuts staff to reduce costs |
| Equity | Wider share ownership | Unprofitable rural or low-income services may be dropped; ownership may end up with a few wealthy buyers or foreign firms |

:::worked-solution{title="Before-and-after comparison (illustrative data)"}
A fictional telecommunications firm is sold by the state. The table compares the year before the sale with three years later.

| Measure | State-owned (year before sale) | Privately owned (three years later) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Employees | 4,800 | 3,600 |
| Calls handled (million a year) | 288 | 324 |
| Operating cost (US\$ million a year) | 96 | 81 |
| Average price per call (US\$) | 0.50 | 0.45 |

1. Calls per employee: before = 288,000,000 ÷ 4,800 = **60,000**; after = 324,000,000 ÷ 3,600 = **90,000**. Productivity rose by (90,000 − 60,000) ÷ 60,000 × 100 = **50%**.
2. Operating cost per call: before = 96 ÷ 288 = US\$0.333; after = 81 ÷ 324 = **US\$0.25**. The unit cost fell by 25%.
3. Jobs: (3,600 − 4,800) ÷ 4,800 × 100 = **−25%**, a loss of 1,200 jobs.
4. Price per call: (0.45 − 0.50) ÷ 0.50 × 100 = **−10%**.
5. Interpretation: efficiency and prices improved, but at a cost of 1,200 jobs. To judge whether privatisation was a success we need to know what happened to the displaced workers, whether quality and rural coverage were kept, and whether competition (not just ownership) caused the gains.
:::

## Regulation after privatisation

A privatised monopoly, such as a water or electricity network, has the power to raise prices and reduce quality, so most countries create a **regulator**. Typical tools:

- **Price caps.** The regulator allows prices to rise by no more than inflation minus an efficiency target, **RPI − X** (or CPI − X): the firm keeps any extra profit it earns by cutting costs faster than X.
- **Quality and coverage standards**, for example that rural customers must be served.
- **Access rules** that force the owner of a network to let rivals use it at a fair price, so competition is possible.
- **Licence conditions** and penalties.

:::worked-solution{title="A price cap (illustrative data)"}
Inflation is 6.5% and the regulator sets X = 2.5% for a water company. The current price is US\$40 for a standard monthly bill.

1. Maximum average price rise = inflation − X = 6.5 − 2.5 = **4%**.
2. New maximum price = 40 × 1.04 = **US\$41.60**.
3. The firm cannot raise prices more than this, but if it cuts costs by more than 2.5% a year it keeps the extra profit, so it has an incentive to be efficient.
4. A weakness: the regulator has less information about costs than the firm, so it may set X too low (the firm loses money and cuts quality) or too high (the firm makes excessive profit).
:::

## Nationalisation: strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths.** Control of natural monopolies in the public interest; investment guided by social aims, not profit; protection of essential services; equity of access.

**Weaknesses.** Without competition or profit pressure, managers may have weak incentives to cut costs (X-inefficiency); political interference may distort decisions (jobs, prices); losses are paid out of taxes; the state may lack the funds for investment, so quality falls; and the compensation to previous owners costs money.

## Commercialisation (ZIMSEC detail)

Commercialisation is a middle path. The enterprise remains state-owned but is run on commercial lines: managers have targets and independence, prices cover costs, subsidies are reduced and performance is monitored. The gains depend on whether the government really leaves managers to manage. Where it continues to direct prices and staffing, commercialisation may change little.

## Evaluation

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
Privatising a state electricity company may reduce costs and relieve the budget of subsidies, and the sale raises revenue. However, electricity transmission is a natural monopoly, so if the new owner is not regulated it may raise prices; low-income and rural consumers may be dropped if they are unprofitable; and the state loses the future profits of the company. Privatisation is therefore more likely to succeed if it is accompanied by competition in generation and supply, by a strong regulator and by protection for poor consumers. The success cannot be judged from ownership alone, but from the market structure, regulation and the quality of the sale.
:::

Points to include: the **type of industry** (a natural monopoly differs from a market that can support competition); the **price obtained** (selling too cheaply transfers value to buyers); the **regulatory capacity** of the country; the **speed** and **sequencing** (regulation before sale); and the **time period** (efficiency gains take time, but job losses are immediate).

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require nationalisation and privatisation, reasons, and effects on efficiency, equity, revenue and employment. ZIMSEC (Form 5) also names commercialisation of state enterprises and expects a factual, balanced account of state enterprise reform. Cambridge (A Level) asks for assessment of nationalisation and privatisation as a measure to tackle market failure, and for the role of regulation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating privatisation with competition"}
Selling a monopoly to a private owner does not create competition. Efficiency gains are strongest where competition follows, and a private monopoly may need more regulation, not less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating sale proceeds as pure gain"}
The sale gives a one-off sum, but the state loses the future profits (and the end of subsidies must be compared with that). Always weigh both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing commercialisation with privatisation"}
Commercialisation changes how a state enterprise is run; ownership stays public. Privatisation changes ownership.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions; Paper 2 gives data on a state enterprise or a sale and asks for calculations and discussion; Paper 3 essays ask whether the state should sell its enterprises. Cambridge Paper 3 tests the concepts in multiple choice and Paper 4 asks for an evaluation of privatisation or nationalisation against a market failure.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Distinguish*: ownership against management. *Explain*: link the change of ownership to incentives, costs and prices. *Calculate*: productivity, unit cost and percentage changes. *Evaluate*: weigh efficiency and revenue against employment, equity and monopoly power, take account of regulation and competition, and give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nationalisation moves an enterprise to state ownership; privatisation moves it to private owners; commercialisation keeps state ownership but runs the enterprise on business lines.
- Reasons for privatisation: efficiency, sale revenue and the end of subsidies, competition and less political interference; reasons for nationalisation: natural monopoly, essential services, social aims.
- Effects include changes in costs, prices, revenue, jobs and access, which must be judged with data, not assumed.
- A privatised monopoly needs regulation, for example RPI − X price caps, quality standards and access rules.
- Success depends on competition, the regulator, the price at which the sale is made and what happens to affected workers and poor consumers.
:::
