## Introduction

When a market fails, the natural response is to ask the government to put it right. But governments are made of people and institutions that also have limited information, face pressure from interest groups, and work with limited funds. A policy that looks good on a diagram may make society worse off once it meets the real world: a price control that creates shortages, a subsidy that mostly helps those who did not need it, or a licence system that protects existing firms. This is **government failure**.

You should know how market failure arises (public goods, externalities, imperfect information, monopoly) and the main policies used to correct it. This lesson explains why intervention can fail, summarises the strengths and weaknesses of each policy, and shows how to reach a reasoned judgement about intervention in a particular economy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Government failure"}
A situation in which government intervention in a market leads to a net welfare loss compared with no intervention, or to a worse allocation of resources than the one it was intended to improve.
:::

:::definition{term="Regulatory capture"}
A situation in which a regulator comes to act in the interests of the industry it is supposed to control (not the public), for example because of lobbying, shared staff or reliance on the industry for information.
:::

:::definition{term="Unintended consequences"}
Effects of a policy that were not planned or foreseen, often because people change their behaviour in response to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Administrative costs"}
The costs of designing, running, monitoring and enforcing a policy, including collecting taxes and paying inspectors.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The test of a policy"}
A policy is justified only if its benefits (the welfare gain from correcting the market failure) exceed its costs (including administration, compliance and the distortions it creates). Government failure occurs when the costs exceed the benefits.
:::

## Causes of government failure

1. **Poor information.** The government cannot know the true size of an externality, the value of a merit good, or what people would pay for a service. A tax or subsidy set on a wrong estimate misses the optimum.
2. **Regulatory capture.** The regulator depends on the industry for data and staff and may become sympathetic to it, so rules protect producers instead of consumers.
3. **Unintended consequences.** A maximum price on a basic food may create shortages, queues and a black market. A ban may create an illegal market.
4. **Administrative and compliance costs.** The cost of collecting a tax or inspecting firms can be a large share of the revenue or benefit. In an economy with a large informal sector, tax and rules are harder to enforce.
5. **Short-term political horizons.** Governments and politicians face elections and may choose policies that yield visible gains soon (such as holding down prices) even if the long-run costs are higher. Spending decisions may favour groups with political influence.
6. **Interest-group pressure (lobbying)** and **corruption**, which distort policy towards special interests.
7. **Lack of competition and incentives** in state production, leading to waste (X-inefficiency).

## Consequences of government failure

- **Misallocation of resources:** output moves away from the social optimum (too much or too little of the good).
- **Welfare loss:** the cost of the policy may exceed the benefit.
- **Distortions and shortages:** black markets, queues, hoarding and illegal trade.
- **Reduced incentives:** high taxes or controls discourage production, investment and work.
- **Waste of public funds** and a larger budget deficit.
- **Loss of confidence:** if policies change often, firms and households cannot plan.

:::worked-solution{title="A tax set at the wrong level (illustrative data)"}
Demand (marginal private benefit) is P = 104 − 0.5Q and supply (marginal private cost) is P = 8 + 0.25Q, with P in US\$ and Q in units. Each unit causes a marginal external cost of US\$12.

1. Free market: 104 − 0.5Q = 8 + 0.25Q, so Q = **128** and P = US\$40.
2. Social optimum: MSC = 20 + 0.25Q. Setting 104 − 0.5Q = 20 + 0.25Q gives Q = **112**, at P = US\$48.
3. Welfare loss with no policy = ½ × 12 × (128 − 112) = **US\$96**.
4. A tax of US\$12 a unit shifts supply to 20 + 0.25Q and gives the optimum (Q = 112). Welfare loss = **0**.
5. Suppose the government has poor information and sets the tax at **US\$24** (twice the true external cost). Supply becomes 32 + 0.25Q, so Q = **96** and P = US\$56. Output is now **below** the optimum. The gap between MSB (104 − 48 = 56) and MSC (20 + 24 = 44) at 96 units is US\$12.
6. Welfare loss = ½ × 12 × (112 − 96) = **US\$96**, the **same as having no tax at all**. The overcorrection has wasted the whole benefit of the policy.
7. A tax of US\$18 would give Q = 104 and a loss of ½ × 6 × 8 = US\$24, so even an imperfect tax helps, but a tax that is too high can be as bad as doing nothing.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Administrative cost (illustrative data)"}
A tax is expected to raise US\$1,200,000. The revenue authority's cost of collecting it is US\$150,000.

1. Net revenue = 1,200,000 − 150,000 = **US\$1,050,000**.
2. Collection cost as a share of revenue = 150,000 ÷ 1,200,000 × 100 = **12.5%**.
3. A tax with a high collection cost relative to revenue (as can happen where most trade is informal and hard to record) may be an example of government failure, however sound the theory.
:::

## Strengths and weaknesses of each policy

| Policy | Main strength | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Taxes (e.g. on a demerit good or pollution) | Raise revenue and internalise the externality | Need accurate estimate of the external cost; may be regressive; collection costs |
| Subsidies | Increase output of merit goods; support poor consumers | Cost to the budget; may go to those who need them least; hard to remove |
| Regulation and bans | Certain, direct result | Monitoring and enforcement costs; same rule for all firms; black markets |
| Tradable permits | Meet a cap at the lowest cost | Cap hard to set; monitoring; permits may favour existing firms |
| Price controls (maximum or minimum prices) | Protect consumers or producers in the short run | Shortages or surpluses; black markets; costs of storage and enforcement |
| Direct provision | Reaches everyone; produces public goods | Opportunity cost; possible waste; rationing by queue |
| Information | Cheap; keeps choice | May be ignored; slow; hard to measure |
| Nudges | Cheap; keeps choice | Small or uncertain effects; limited for addictive goods |
| Property rights | Give owners an incentive to protect resources | Hard to define and enforce; bargaining is costly |
| Nationalisation | Control natural monopolies | X-inefficiency; political interference; losses fall on taxpayers |
| Privatisation | Efficiency; revenue; competition | Private monopoly; job losses; neglect of unprofitable services |

## Evaluating intervention in an economy such as Zimbabwe

An answer that evaluates intervention should consider the specific conditions of the economy:

- **Institutional capacity.** Does the revenue authority, regulator or ministry have the staff, equipment and information needed? Weak capacity raises the risk of failure for complex policies (tradable permits, fine-tuned taxes) and favours simpler ones.
- **The informal sector.** A large informal sector, including many small traders and farmers, makes taxation, licensing and regulation hard to enforce and limits the base for taxes.
- **Fiscal space.** Subsidies and state provision need funds. If the budget is under strain or the government relies on borrowing, expensive schemes may crowd out other spending or lead to inflation.
- **Credibility and consistency.** Policies that change often (for example repeated changes to price controls or exchange-rate rules) make it hard for firms to plan, a point economists of all views accept even when they disagree on the policies themselves.
- **Corruption and rent seeking**, where licences and permits can become a source of private gain.
- **Alternatives.** Compare intervention not with a perfect market but with the real alternative: living with the market failure, or using another policy.

Different groups hold different views. Supporters of active intervention argue that market failures such as poverty, poor health and lack of infrastructure are so large that doing nothing is worse. Critics argue that weak institutions mean intervention is often ineffective or captured, and that simple, predictable rules do better. Both views contain truth, and the weight depends on the policy, its design and the institutions available.

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph"}
A government that sets a maximum price on maize meal to protect poor consumers may achieve this in the short run, but if the price is below the cost of production, millers may cut output, shortages may appear and a black market may develop where the poor pay more than the controlled price. The policy is therefore more likely to succeed if combined with measures to increase supply, such as support for farmers, or replaced by direct payments to poor households. Whether the control is justified depends on the size of the gap between the controlled and the market price and on the government's ability to enforce it.
:::

ZIMSEC (Form 6) expects the strengths and weaknesses of each solution to market failure and an evaluation of intervention in a developing economy such as Zimbabwe. Cambridge (A Level) asks you to define government failure and explain its causes and consequences, and to use it when assessing the measures to tackle market failure.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that intervention always fails"}
Government failure means that a particular intervention costs more than it gains. Many policies, such as vaccination and well-designed taxes, produce net gains. Evaluate the policy in its context.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing market failure with government failure"}
Market failure is the free market's failure to allocate resources efficiently; government failure is the failure of the policy intended to correct it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without applying them"}
"Poor information" should be linked to the policy: for example, an inaccurate estimate of an external cost leads to a tax that is too high or too low and so to a welfare loss.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 tests the meaning of government failure and examples; Papers 2 and 3 ask whether the government should intervene in markets such as housing, fuel or farming. Cambridge Paper 3 tests causes in multiple choice, and Paper 4 asks for evaluation of intervention using government failure as the counter-argument.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: net welfare loss from intervention. *Explain*: link a cause (poor information, capture) to a specific effect. *Analyse*: follow the chain from the policy to behaviour, to outcomes. *Evaluate*: weigh market failure against government failure, consider the economy's capacity and give a justified judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Government failure occurs when intervention causes a net welfare loss or a worse allocation of resources than before.
- Causes include poor information, regulatory capture, unintended consequences, administrative costs, short-term political horizons and lobbying.
- A tax set at the wrong level can leave welfare no better than with no tax at all.
- Every policy for market failure has strengths and weaknesses; the choice depends on the type of market failure and on the economy's capacity.
- Evaluate by comparing the real policy with real alternatives and give a judgement based on institutions, the informal sector, fiscal space and credibility.
:::
