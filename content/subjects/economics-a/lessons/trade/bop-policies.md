## Introduction

When a country's current account is in deficit year after year, the government usually wants to reduce it. But the cures are not painless. Cutting imports by reducing spending can cause unemployment; raising tariffs can start a trade war; lowering the exchange rate raises the price of fuel and food. This lesson sets out the main policies, shows how each works, and trains you to evaluate them.

You should know the components of the current account and the effects of a deficit (previous lessons), the aggregate demand and supply model, and the multiplier.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Current account stability"}
A policy objective: that over time the current account is close to balance, or that any deficit is small and can be financed by long-term inflows without putting pressure on the exchange rate.
:::

:::definition{term="Expenditure-switching policy"}
A policy that **switches** spending from imports to domestically produced goods without necessarily reducing total spending, by changing relative prices (tariffs, quotas, subsidies, a lower exchange rate).
:::

:::definition{term="Expenditure-reducing policy"}
A policy that **reduces** total aggregate demand (higher taxes, lower government spending, higher interest rates), which reduces spending on imports as well as on home goods.
:::

## Expenditure-reducing policies

1. **Contractionary fiscal policy** (higher taxes, lower government spending) lowers disposable income and aggregate demand, so households buy fewer imports. It may also reduce domestic inflation, making exports more competitive.
2. **Contractionary monetary policy** (higher interest rates) reduces borrowing and spending and may attract capital inflows, which can strengthen the currency.

:::worked-solution{title="What an expenditure-reducing policy achieves"}
The government cuts spending by US\$120 million. The multiplier is 2.0 and the marginal propensity to import is 0.15.
1. Fall in national income = 2.0 × 120 = **US\$240 million**.
2. Fall in imports = 0.15 × 240 = **US\$36 million**.
3. The deficit improves by only US\$36 million, but national income falls by US\$240 million, so each US\$1 of imports saved costs about 240 ÷ 36 = **US\$6.67 of lost income** (and jobs).
4. The policy works but at a high price in lost output and employment.
:::

## Expenditure-switching policies

| Policy | How it works | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Depreciation or devaluation** | Exports become cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home, switching demand to home goods | Needs elastic enough demand (Marshall-Lerner); raises import prices and may cause inflation; time lags (J-curve) |
| **Tariffs** (tax on imports) | Raise the domestic price of imports | Raise costs of inputs; retaliation; consumers pay more; may breach trade agreements |
| **Quotas** (limits on quantity) | Directly limit the volume of imports | Create scarcity, higher prices, black markets; retaliation |
| **Subsidies to exporters and import-competing industries** | Lower costs so that firms can sell more abroad and compete with imports | Opportunity cost for the government; may breach trade rules; may protect inefficient firms |
| **Export promotion** (trade fairs, credit, tax relief) | Helps firms enter foreign markets | Slow; costly; effective only if goods are competitive |

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of a tariff on import spending"}
A country spends US\$1,000 million a year on imports (at world prices). It imposes a 20% tariff. The price elasticity of demand for imports is −0.5.
1. Domestic price of imports rises by 20%.
2. Quantity demanded falls by 0.5 × 20 = **10%**.
3. Payments abroad fall from US\$1,000 million to 1,000 × 0.90 = **US\$900 million**, an improvement of **US\$100 million** in the current account (before any retaliation).
4. Tariff revenue = 20% × US\$900 million = US\$180 million, which goes to the government and is not part of the current account.
5. Because demand is inelastic, the policy has a modest effect and consumers pay more.
:::

## Supply-side policies

Policies that raise productivity and competitiveness work more slowly but do not reduce demand. Examples are education and training, investment in roads, power and rail, support for research, competition policy and reducing the costs of doing business. They shift the aggregate supply curve to the right and make exports more competitive in price and quality. They suit a deficit caused by **structural** weakness, but results may take many years.

## ZIMSEC trade policies

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC detail"}
- **Exchange rate policy:** deliberate management of the exchange rate to influence competitiveness and the current account.
- **Import substitution:** encouraging domestic production of goods previously imported, usually through protection and subsidies; it saves foreign exchange but may protect inefficient producers and raise consumer prices.
- **Protectionism:** tariffs, quotas and other barriers.
- **Export promotion:** incentives for exporters, such as export credit or tax relief, and help with finding markets.
- **The "Look East" policy:** Zimbabwe's policy of developing trade and investment links with Asian economies, notably China, introduced in the early 2000s, aimed at widening markets and sources of finance and equipment. Supporters argue it diversifies partners; critics argue that it can add to dependence on a few partners and on commodity exports. Judge it by the evidence for specific agreements.
:::

## Choosing between policies

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
A country with a large deficit caused by strong demand for imported goods might use expenditure-reducing policies, which work quickly but cost jobs and growth. If the deficit arises because exporters are uncompetitive, expenditure-switching policies such as a lower exchange rate address the cause, but they raise import prices and work only if export and import demands are elastic enough. If the cause is a structural weakness in production, supply-side policies are the only lasting solution, but they take years. The best policy therefore depends on the cause of the deficit, the elasticities, how quickly the correction is needed and the cost to employment and living standards; a package of policies is often needed.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge (AS Level) asks for the effects of fiscal, monetary, supply-side, protectionist and exchange rate policies on the current account and the distinction between switching and reducing policies. ZIMSEC (Form 6) names exchange rate policy, import substitution, protectionism, export promotion and the Look East policy; describe the Look East policy factually and give arguments for and against.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the cost of expenditure-reducing policies"}
They reduce the deficit by reducing national income. Always mention the effect on growth and unemployment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that a depreciation always improves the current account"}
It depends on the price elasticities (Marshall-Lerner) and on time (the J-curve). Imported inputs get dearer too.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring retaliation"}
Tariffs and quotas may be met with similar measures by trading partners, cancelling the gain.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 3 test the list of policies, with Paper 3 essays on whether protection can cure a deficit. Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 test the distinction between switching and reducing policies and require evaluation of one or two policies.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: show the chain from the policy to imports or exports. *Evaluate*: consider the cause of the deficit, elasticities, time lags, retaliation and costs to living standards, then recommend a policy or package.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Expenditure-reducing policies cut aggregate demand and imports; expenditure-switching policies change relative prices.
- Tariffs, quotas, subsidies and a lower exchange rate switch spending to home goods but invite retaliation or cost increases.
- Supply-side policies improve competitiveness slowly without reducing demand.
- ZIMSEC also covers import substitution, export promotion and the Look East policy.
- The best policy depends on the cause of the deficit, elasticities, time lags and costs.
:::
