## Introduction

A country that sells mainly raw tobacco, cotton or minerals and buys fuel, vehicles and machinery depends on the **prices** of those goods as much as on the amount it sells. If the world price of its exports falls while the price of its imports stays the same, the country can buy fewer imports for the same effort, even if it exports as much as before. The **terms of trade** measure this relationship between export prices and import prices.

You should know what a price index is (a base year of 100), how the exchange rate affects the price of traded goods, and the basic structure of the current account.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Terms of trade"}
An index of the relationship between the average price of a country's exports and the average price of its imports, calculated as (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formula"}
**Terms of trade = (index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100**
Both indices must have the same base year. In the base year the terms of trade are 100. A figure above 100 means export prices have risen by more than import prices since the base year, and one below 100 means the opposite.
:::

:::definition{term="Improvement and deterioration"}
The terms of trade **improve** (favourable change) when the index rises: a given quantity of exports buys more imports. They **deteriorate** (unfavourable change) when the index falls: more exports must be sold to buy the same quantity of imports.
:::

## Calculating the terms of trade

The table shows illustrative price indices for a fictional exporter of tobacco and cotton (year 1 = 100).

| Year | Export price index | Import price index | Terms of trade |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 100.0 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| 2 | 110.0 | 105.0 | 104.8 |
| 3 | 120.0 | 110.0 | 109.1 |
| 4 | 108.0 | 120.0 | 90.0 |
| 5 | 120.0 | 125.0 | 96.0 |

:::worked-solution{title="Terms of trade in year 4 and their change"}
1. Year 4: terms of trade = 108.0 ÷ 120.0 × 100 = **90.0**.
2. Year 3: 120.0 ÷ 110.0 × 100 = **109.1** (to one decimal place).
3. Percentage change from year 3 to year 4 = (90.0 − 109.09) ÷ 109.09 × 100 = **−17.5%**.
4. The terms of trade **deteriorated**. A unit of exports now buys about 17.5% fewer imports than in year 3.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the change in prices"}
Export prices rise by 8% and import prices rise by 5% (base year = 100).
1. Export index = 108 and import index = 105.
2. Terms of trade = 108 ÷ 105 × 100 = **102.9**: an improvement of about 2.9%.
3. Even though import prices rose, the terms of trade improved, because export prices rose faster.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="What a fall means in physical terms"}
A tractor costs US\$40,000. When copper sells for US\$8,000 a tonne, a country must export 40,000 ÷ 8,000 = **5 tonnes** of copper to buy one tractor. If the copper price falls to US\$5,000 a tonne, it needs 40,000 ÷ 5,000 = **8 tonnes**. The terms of trade have deteriorated and the country must give up 3 more tonnes of copper for the same tractor.
:::

## Causes of changes in the terms of trade

1. **Changes in world demand and supply of the goods traded.** Commodity prices change strongly with weather, harvests and world incomes: a drought in a producing region or strong demand from growing economies raises export prices.
2. **The exchange rate.** When export prices are set in the home currency, a depreciation makes exports cheaper in foreign currency while imports become dearer at home, so the terms of trade (measured in foreign currency) fall; an appreciation does the opposite. If a country sells commodities priced in US dollars, a depreciation changes the home-currency prices of both exports and imports and leaves the terms of trade little changed. State how the indices are measured.
3. **Relative inflation.** If prices at home rise faster than prices abroad, home export prices rise relative to import prices and the index rises, but exports become less competitive and the volume sold may fall.
4. **Productivity and technology.** Productivity gains in the export industry lower costs and, over time, prices; synthetic substitutes can lower the demand for a commodity.
5. **Changes in the structure of trade.** A move from raw materials to processed goods tends to raise average export prices.

## Effects of a change

:::keyfact{title="Terms of trade and the balance of payments"}
A change in the terms of trade does not by itself tell you what happens to the **current account**. The effect depends on the elasticities of demand for exports and imports. If demand is price inelastic, as for fuel and many primary products, a fall in export prices cuts export revenue, while import spending falls little, so the current account moves towards deficit. If an improvement raises export prices, revenue rises when export demand is inelastic.
:::

| | Improvement in the terms of trade | Deterioration in the terms of trade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Real income and living standards | Each unit of exports buys more imports, so real income and consumption can rise | Real income falls because more exports are needed to pay for the same imports |
| Current account | Export revenue rises (if demand is inelastic); import spending may rise too, so effect depends on elasticities | Export revenue falls for commodity exporters; deficit tends to widen |
| Output and jobs | Exporters earn more and expand | Producers may cut output and jobs |
| Inflation | Imports are relatively cheaper, which lowers cost-push pressure | Imported inputs are dearer, adding to cost-push inflation |

:::example{title="Primary-commodity exporters"}
Many developing countries earn most of their foreign exchange from a few primary products, whose prices are volatile because supply depends on weather and demand is price inelastic. Their export prices therefore swing widely while the prices of imported machinery and fuel move more slowly. Some economists argue that over long periods primary products have fallen in price relative to manufactures (the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis), so exporters of raw materials suffer a long-run deterioration. Critics reply that the evidence is mixed, that some commodity prices have risen for long periods, and that the right comparison depends on the products and the period chosen. A sensible conclusion is that **price volatility** and dependence on a narrow range of exports are the clearer risks, which is why diversification and processing are widely recommended.
:::

An **improvement** in the terms of trade is not always beneficial. If it is caused by a rise in the exchange rate, the volume of exports may fall as exports become less competitive. A **deterioration** is not always harmful: a fall in export prices caused by higher productivity can raise the volume of sales and employment.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the formula, the calculation from indices and the effects on the balance of payments and living standards. ZIMSEC (Form 6) sets numerical questions on the formula; Cambridge (AS Level) expects the calculation and the evaluation of the effects, with attention to price elasticity, and the position of primary-product exporters.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Inverting the formula"}
It is exports over imports, not imports over exports. Remember that a higher figure is favourable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the terms of trade with the trade balance"}
The terms of trade describe **prices**; the balance of trade describes **values** (price × quantity). The terms of trade can improve while the trade balance worsens, if the volume of exports falls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a change in the index as a percentage"}
A fall in the index from 109.1 to 90.0 is a fall of 19.1 index points, which is **−17.5%**. State which you are asked for.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 usually includes a calculation of the terms of trade from two indices, and Paper 3 may ask about the effects of changes. Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 set calculations and ask you to analyse effects on the current account, usually using elasticity.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: show export index ÷ import index × 100. *Explain*: say whether more or fewer imports can be bought. *Assess*: weigh the effects on living standards, the current account and output, and note that elasticity and the cause of the change matter.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100.
- A rise in the index is an improvement: exports buy more imports.
- Causes: world demand and supply, exchange rate, relative inflation, productivity and the structure of trade.
- Effects on the current account depend on price elasticities, not only on the direction of the change.
- Primary-commodity exporters face volatile terms of trade, which makes diversification attractive.
:::
