## Introduction

Zimbabwe is a landlocked economy that sells tobacco, minerals and horticultural products to the world and buys fuel, machinery and medicines in return. Why should a country export some goods and import others, instead of making everything at home? The answer given by the economists Adam Smith and David Ricardo is that countries gain by specialising in what they do best and trading. The idea of **comparative advantage** is one of the most important in economics, and it applies equally to a farmer, a firm or a country.

You should be able to calculate opportunity costs from the production possibility curve (earlier lesson) and know the meaning of specialisation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Absolute advantage"}
The ability of a country (or firm or person) to produce a good using fewer resources than another, or to produce more of it with the same resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Comparative advantage"}
The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country. A country has a comparative advantage in the good in which its opportunity cost is lower.
:::

:::definition{term="Terms of trade (in the theory)"}
The rate at which one good is exchanged for another in international trade, for example 3 tonnes of maize for 1 tonne of cotton.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Opportunity cost from an output table"}
Opportunity cost of one unit of good X = (output of Y given up) ÷ (output of X gained) = $\dfrac{\text{max. output of Y}}{\text{max. output of X}}$ when the same resources are used for either good.

The country with the **lower opportunity cost** of a good has the comparative advantage in it.
:::

## Calculating opportunity costs

Suppose that Zandia and Mbira use the same amount of resources and can produce the following maximum output of maize or cotton (illustrative figures).

| Country | Maize (tonnes) | Cotton (tonnes) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Zandia (A) | 60 | 30 |
| Mbira (B) | 40 | 10 |

Zandia has an **absolute advantage** in both goods because it can produce more of each. But the opportunity costs differ.

| Country | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of maize | Opportunity cost of 1 tonne of cotton |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Zandia (A) | 0.5 tonne of cotton | 2 tonnes of maize |
| Mbira (B) | 0.25 tonne of cotton | 4 tonnes of maize |

:::worked-solution{title="Finding comparative advantage"}
1. Zandia: if all resources go to maize, 60 tonnes of maize are made, or 30 tonnes of cotton. So 1 tonne of maize costs 30 ÷ 60 = **0.5 tonne of cotton**, and 1 tonne of cotton costs 60 ÷ 30 = **2 tonnes of maize**.
2. Mbira: 1 tonne of maize costs 10 ÷ 40 = **0.25 tonne of cotton**, and 1 tonne of cotton costs 40 ÷ 10 = **4 tonnes of maize**.
3. Maize: Mbira's opportunity cost (0.25) is lower than Zandia's (0.5), so **Mbira has the comparative advantage in maize**.
4. Cotton: Zandia's opportunity cost (2 tonnes of maize) is lower than Mbira's (4), so **Zandia has the comparative advantage in cotton**.
5. The opportunity costs of the two goods are reciprocals within each country, so a country that has a comparative advantage in one good must have a comparative *disadvantage* in the other, unless the opportunity costs are equal.
:::

## The gains from specialisation and trade

If each country specialises in the good in which it has a comparative advantage and they trade, total output rises. Mbira will sell maize and Zandia will sell cotton. Trade is attractive to both only if the terms of trade lie **between the two countries' opportunity costs**: for 1 tonne of cotton Zandia would accept any amount of maize greater than 2 tonnes (its own opportunity cost), and Mbira would pay up to 4 tonnes (its own opportunity cost). The terms of trade therefore lie between **2 and 4 tonnes of maize per tonne of cotton**. Suppose they agree on 3 tonnes of maize for 1 tonne of cotton.

|  | Zandia: maize | Zandia: cotton | Mbira: maize | Mbira: cotton |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Before specialisation (tonnes) | 12 | 24 | 24 | 4 |
| After specialisation, before trade | 0 | 30 | 40 | 0 |
| After trading 5 cotton for 15 maize | 15 | 25 | 25 | 5 |

Before specialisation the two countries together produce 12 + 24 = 36 tonnes of maize and 24 + 4 = 28 tonnes of cotton. After specialisation, total output is 40 tonnes of maize and 30 tonnes of cotton, both more than before. After trading 5 tonnes of cotton for 15 tonnes of maize, Zandia has more of both goods (15 > 12 maize and 25 > 24 cotton) and so does Mbira (25 > 24 maize and 5 > 4 cotton).

:::worked-solution{title="The trading possibility curve"}
Zandia's production possibility curve runs from 60 tonnes of maize on one axis to 30 tonnes of cotton on the other. If it specialises in cotton and trades at 3 tonnes of maize per tonne of cotton:
1. 30 tonnes of cotton can be exchanged for 30 × 3 = **90 tonnes of maize**.
2. The **trading possibility curve** (a straight line with slope given by the terms of trade) joins 30 tonnes of cotton on the cotton axis to 90 tonnes of maize on the maize axis.
3. This line lies **outside** the production possibility curve (which reaches only 60 tonnes of maize), so trade lets Zandia consume combinations that it could not produce alone.
:::

## Assumptions and limitations

The theory makes simplifying assumptions, and the real gains from trade are smaller and less certain for the following reasons.

1. **Transport costs and other costs of trade** reduce or remove the gains, especially for a landlocked country.
2. **Costs are not constant.** The theory assumes constant opportunity costs; in practice, costs may rise as a country specialises (increasing opportunity cost) or fall because of economies of scale.
3. **Resources are not perfectly mobile.** Workers displaced from the declining industry may be unemployed for a long time.
4. **Trade barriers** such as tariffs and quotas prevent the full gains.
5. **Comparative advantage changes over time**, as skills, technology and the exchange rate change (dynamic comparative advantage). A country that specialises in primary products may be locked into a low-value pattern of production.
6. **Overspecialisation** makes a country vulnerable to price falls or poor harvests in its main exports.
7. **Perfect information, two countries, two goods and no externalities** are simplifications.

Zimbabwe is often said to have advantages in sectors such as farming (tobacco, horticulture), mining and tourism, which rely on climate, land and mineral resources. Whether any of these is a comparative advantage depends on the opportunity cost of using resources there rather than elsewhere and on trade costs and policies.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
The theory of comparative advantage shows that trade can raise the output and consumption of both countries even when one of them is more efficient at everything. It provides a strong argument for specialisation and open trade. However, its assumptions of constant costs, no transport costs and perfectly mobile resources are unrealistic, and the gains are not shared equally; workers in declining industries may lose. A developing country that specialises in a few primary products may be exposed to price swings and may find it hard to build new industries. The theory is therefore a useful guide to the potential gains from trade but must be tempered by attention to the costs of adjustment and the long-term pattern of development.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing absolute with comparative advantage"}
Absolute advantage compares **outputs** (or resources used). Comparative advantage compares **opportunity costs**. A country can have an absolute advantage in everything and still gain from trade.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing the wrong way"}
For the opportunity cost of X, divide the output of Y given up by the output of X gained. Reversing the ratio gives the cost of Y.
:::

:::mistake{title="Choosing terms of trade outside the range"}
The terms of trade must lie between the two opportunity costs. Outside this range one country has no incentive to trade.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Absolute advantage is producing more with the same resources; comparative advantage is producing at a lower opportunity cost.
- Opportunity cost of a unit of X = output of Y given up ÷ output of X gained; the lower cost gives the comparative advantage.
- Both countries gain from specialisation and trade if the terms of trade lie between the two opportunity costs.
- The trading possibility curve lies outside the production possibility curve.
- Limitations include transport costs, changing and increasing costs, immobile resources, trade barriers and overspecialisation.
:::
