## Introduction

If you travel to Johannesburg, a bank will quote you the number of South African rand you get for a US dollar. That number changes from day to day, and it matters: it decides how much a tourist pays at the Victoria Falls, how much a Zimbabwean importer pays for a truck from abroad and whether a tobacco exporter earns more or less. An **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another, and in a **floating** system this price is determined by market forces. This lesson shows how it is determined and how to calculate with it.

You should know the demand and supply model and be able to calculate percentage changes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Exchange rate"}
The price of one currency expressed in terms of another, for example US\$1 = R18.00.
:::

:::definition{term="Floating exchange rate"}
An exchange rate determined by the demand for and supply of the currency in the foreign exchange market, with no official target or intervention by the central bank.
:::

:::definition{term="Appreciation and depreciation"}
An **appreciation** is a rise in the value of a currency relative to another currency under a floating system; a **depreciation** is a fall. (**Revaluation** and **devaluation** are the equivalent deliberate changes under a fixed or managed system.)
:::

## Demand and supply of a currency

Take the South African rand (R) in terms of US cents. The price of R1 is on the vertical axis and the quantity of rand traded (R million per day) on the horizontal axis.

- **Demand for rand** comes from foreigners who want to buy South African goods, services, shares or property, and from tourists and investors. It slopes **downward**: a higher price of rand makes South African goods dearer for foreigners, so they buy fewer.
- **Supply of rand** comes from South African residents who sell rand to buy foreign currency for imports, investments abroad and holidays. It slopes **upward**: at a higher price of rand, imports are cheaper, so more rand are supplied.

The equilibrium rate is where demand equals supply (illustrative data):

| Price of R1 (US cents) | Demand for rand before (R million) | Supply of rand (R million) | Demand for rand after (R million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 5.0 | 500 | 250 | 625 |
| 5.5 | 450 | 325 | 575 |
| 6.0 | 400 | 400 | 525 |
| 6.5 | 350 | 475 | 475 |
| 7.0 | 300 | 550 | 425 |

:::worked-solution{title="An appreciation"}
1. Before the change demand equals supply at 6.0 cents, with R400 million traded.
2. Foreign demand for South African goods and assets rises, so the demand curve shifts to the right (the last column): demand is 125 higher at every price.
3. At 6.0 cents demand (525) exceeds supply (400): there is excess demand for rand, so the price of the rand rises.
4. The new equilibrium is at **6.5 cents**, with R475 million traded.
5. The percentage appreciation = (6.5 − 6.0) ÷ 6.0 × 100 = **+8.3%** (to one decimal place).
:::

## Causes of changes in a floating rate

| Cause | Effect on the currency |
| --- | --- |
| Higher interest rates than abroad | Foreign investors place money in the country to earn the higher return, raising demand: appreciation |
| Higher inflation than competitors | Exports become dearer and imports cheaper, so demand for the currency falls and supply rises: depreciation (the long-run link is purchasing power parity) |
| A rise in the demand for exports | More demand for the currency: appreciation |
| A rise in imports (strong income growth at home) | More supply of the currency: depreciation |
| Foreign direct investment inflow | Demand for the currency rises: appreciation |
| Speculation | If traders expect a fall they sell the currency, causing the fall they expect |
| Falling confidence (political or economic) | Capital flows out: depreciation |
| Central bank intervention | Buying the currency with reserves supports it (a managed float) |

## Conversions and percentage changes

:::keyfact{title="Converting currencies"}
If US\$1 = R18, then US\$ × 18 = rand, and rand ÷ 18 = US\$. The currency quoted *per unit* is the one you multiply by. A change in a rate is a percentage change from the original rate, and the percentage change in one currency's value is **not** the same as the other's.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Conversion"}
At US\$1 = R18: (a) US\$250 buys 250 × 18 = **R4,500**; (b) R1,800 buys 1,800 ÷ 18 = **US\$100**.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Appreciation and depreciation from a rate change"}
The rate moves from US\$1 = R18.00 to US\$1 = R16.20.
1. The dollar buys fewer rand: change = (16.20 − 18.00) ÷ 18.00 × 100 = **−10.0%**, so the dollar has **depreciated by 10%** against the rand.
2. In the other direction R1 = US\$0.0556 rises to R1 = US\$0.0617, so the rand has **appreciated by (0.0617 − 0.0556) ÷ 0.0556 × 100 = 11.1%**.
3. Effect on a hotel room priced at R1,350: it cost US\$75 (1,350 ÷ 18) and now costs 1,350 ÷ 16.2 = **US\$83.33**, a rise of 11.1% for dollar-paying tourists.
4. The rand's appreciation makes South African exports and tourism dearer for foreigners and imports cheaper for South Africans.
:::

:::example{title="Effect on the economy"}
After an appreciation, exporters earn fewer rand from each dollar of sales and may lose competitiveness; importers pay fewer rand per dollar of goods and may gain; imported inflation falls. A depreciation does the reverse. The size of the effects depends on price elasticities, which the lesson on exchange rate effects covers.
:::

## Strengths and limits of the floating system

- **Strengths:** the rate adjusts automatically to correct imbalances (a current account deficit weakens the currency, which makes exports cheaper), so the central bank does not need large reserves, and the government keeps interest rate policy for domestic goals.
- **Limits:** the rate may be volatile, creating uncertainty for trade and investment; speculation can drive the rate away from fundamentals; depreciation raises import prices and may cause inflation; the automatic correction may be weak if elasticities are low.

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the direction of a rate"}
A fall in the rand price of a dollar (R18 to R16.20 per US\$1) is a **depreciation of the dollar** and an **appreciation of the rand**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the wrong price on the axis"}
For the market for rand, the vertical axis is the price of the rand (in dollars), not the price of the dollar.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that a high interest rate lowers the currency"}
Higher interest rates relative to other countries usually attract capital and raise demand for the currency, so the currency appreciates.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 3 test conversions, percentage changes and causes; Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 test the shifts of demand and supply curves for a currency and interpret exchange rate data.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: say which currency is in the denominator and compute from the original rate. *Explain*: say whether demand or supply of the currency shifts and show the new equilibrium. *Assess*: weigh the advantages and disadvantages for exporters, importers and inflation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another; a floating rate is set by demand and supply.
- Demand for a currency slopes downward and supply upward; equilibrium sets the rate.
- Interest rates, inflation, trade and investment flows, speculation and confidence shift the curves.
- A rise in the currency is an appreciation; if one currency appreciates, the other depreciates.
- Percentage changes are measured from the original rate, and are not symmetrical.
:::
