## Introduction

When the currency of an exporting country falls, tobacco and tourism become cheaper for foreigners, while imported fuel and machinery become dearer at home. Does the trade balance improve? Does output rise? Do prices rise? The answers depend on how strongly buyers respond to price changes and on how long we wait. This lesson links the exchange rate to aggregate demand and supply, and introduces the two classic ideas: the Marshall-Lerner condition and the J-curve.

You should know how an exchange rate is determined, price elasticity of demand, and the aggregate demand and supply model.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marshall-Lerner condition"}
A depreciation (or devaluation) improves the trade balance, after quantities have fully adjusted, only if the sum of the absolute values of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports is **greater than 1**: |PEDx| + |PEDm| > 1.
:::

:::definition{term="J-curve effect"}
The tendency for the trade balance to **worsen first** after a depreciation and only improve later, because quantities of exports and imports adjust slowly to the new prices (short-run demand is inelastic), so the balance traces a "J" over time.
:::

:::definition{term="Competitiveness"}
The ability of a country's firms to sell their goods and services against foreign rivals, affected by price (including the exchange rate), quality, delivery and non-price factors.
:::

## Effects of a depreciation

1. **Prices.** The foreign-currency price of exports falls; the home-currency price of imports rises.
2. **Aggregate demand.** If quantities respond enough, export volumes rise and import volumes fall, so net exports (X − M) increase and AD shifts **right**.
3. **Short-run aggregate supply.** Imported raw materials, fuel and components become dearer, so costs rise and SRAS shifts **left**.
4. **Output, prices and employment.** The price level rises (imported inflation and extra demand). Output usually rises if the AD effect is larger, with employment growing in export and import-competing industries, but the output gain is smaller than the AD effect alone suggests. If the economy is close to full capacity, the result is mostly inflation.
5. **Other effects.** Debts denominated in foreign currency become dearer to repay; foreign tourists' spending buys more, helping tourism; the terms of trade may deteriorate.

An **appreciation** has the opposite effects: export prices (in foreign currency) rise, imports become cheaper, net exports fall, AD shifts left, SRAS shifts right (cheaper imported inputs), the price level falls and output may fall, but living standards from cheaper imports can rise.

:::keyfact{title="Summary of directions"}
**Depreciation:** exports cheaper abroad, imports dearer at home, AD right, SRAS left, price level up, output and jobs up (if elastic enough).
**Appreciation:** exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper at home, AD left, SRAS right, price level down, output and jobs down in export industries.
:::

## The Marshall-Lerner condition

Spending on a good depends on both price and quantity. After a depreciation, the foreign price of exports falls and the home price of imports rises. If demand is price inelastic, quantities change only a little, so what matters for the balance is the price effect, which worsens it.

:::worked-solution{title="A case where the condition is met"}
Initially exports and imports are each worth US\$1,000 million, so the balance is zero. The currency depreciates by 10%. Assume that the foreign-currency price of exports falls by 10% and the home-currency price of imports rises by 10%. PED for exports = −1.2 and PED for imports = −0.6.
1. |PEDx| + |PEDm| = 1.2 + 0.6 = **1.8 > 1**, so the condition is met.
2. Export volume rises by 1.2 × 10 = **12%**; export revenue in home currency rises to 1,000 × 1.12 = **US\$1,120 million**.
3. Import volume falls by 0.6 × 10 = **6%**; import spending in home currency = 1,000 × 1.10 (price) × 0.94 (volume) = **US\$1,034 million**.
4. The trade balance moves from zero to 1,120 − 1,034 = **+US\$86 million**, an improvement.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="A case where the condition fails"}
If PED for exports is −0.3 and for imports −0.5, the sum is **0.8 < 1**. Quantities change too little to offset the price effects, so the trade balance gets worse after the depreciation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Why the elasticities are low in the short run"}
Exporters and importers have contracts, and buyers take time to find new suppliers. Over a longer period (one to two years) elasticities become larger, and the Marshall-Lerner condition is more likely to hold.
:::

## The J-curve

The table shows an illustrative trade balance for a country that depreciated its currency at the start of quarter 1.

| Quarter | Trade balance (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: |
| 0 (before) | −100 |
| 1 | −140 |
| 2 | −120 |
| 3 | −60 |
| 4 | 10 |
| 5 | 40 |

:::worked-solution{title="Reading the J-curve"}
1. The deficit **widens** from US\$100 million to US\$140 million in quarter 1, because import prices rise at once while volumes have not yet changed.
2. From quarter 2 volumes adjust and the deficit narrows.
3. The balance moves into surplus by quarter 4 (+US\$10 million) and improves further: the pattern forms a "J".
4. The improvement after the dip shows that the Marshall-Lerner condition holds in the long run.
:::

## Exchange rate policy and competitiveness

Governments may try to manage the exchange rate to improve competitiveness: letting the currency depreciate, or buying foreign currency to prevent it from rising. **Arguments for:** cheaper exports, more output and jobs, a better trade balance. **Arguments against:** imported inflation, higher costs of imported inputs, higher foreign debt burdens, retaliation by other countries (competitive devaluations), and no change in productivity, so any gain may be temporary as inflation erodes it. A depreciation is therefore not a substitute for supply-side improvements.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
Depreciation is more likely to improve the trade balance if demands for exports and imports are elastic, there is spare capacity so that output can respond, and inflation is kept under control. It is less effective for a country that exports a few primary commodities priced in dollars, imports essential goods with inelastic demand, and depends on imported inputs. The effect also takes time, with the trade balance possibly worsening first.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge (AS Level) expects the effects on AD, SRAS, output, prices and employment, and the policy of managing the exchange rate; the Marshall-Lerner condition and J-curve belong to the A Level extension (Cambridge) and are named in the ZIMSEC checklist. ZIMSEC (Form 6) also expects exchange rate policy and competitiveness.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that a depreciation always improves the trade balance"}
It depends on the elasticities (Marshall-Lerner) and on time (J-curve).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the SRAS effect"}
A depreciation raises the price of imported inputs, shifting SRAS to the left, so the output gain is smaller than the AD effect alone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding the elasticities without absolute values"}
Use absolute values: PEDs are negative, so −1.2 and −0.6 sum to 1.8 in absolute terms.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 3 test the effects of exchange rate changes on exports, imports, prices and employment; Cambridge Paper 2 and Paper 3 test the AD/AS analysis, and Paper 4 can ask you to evaluate a depreciation using the Marshall-Lerner condition and the J-curve.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give the chain from the exchange rate to prices to quantities to AD/AS. *Calculate*: apply %ΔQ = PED × %ΔP. *Evaluate*: weigh elasticities, time lags, imported inflation, spare capacity and other policies.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A depreciation lowers export prices abroad and raises import prices at home; an appreciation does the opposite.
- A depreciation shifts AD right (net exports) and SRAS left (imported inputs): prices rise, output and jobs may rise.
- Marshall-Lerner: the trade balance improves if |PEDx| + |PEDm| > 1.
- The J-curve: the balance worsens first because elasticities are low in the short run.
- Exchange rate policy cannot replace productivity improvements.
:::
