## Introduction

A country, like a household, can spend more than it earns for a while only if somebody lends to it. The **balance of payments** is the record of all the transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period, usually a year. Its most watched part is the **current account**, which records payments for goods and services, income from investment and work abroad, and transfers such as remittances and aid. A persistent current account deficit can lead to borrowing, falling reserves and pressure on the exchange rate, so governments and the media follow it closely.

You should know the difference between a visible and an invisible export, what a price index is, and what exports and imports do to aggregate demand.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Balance of payments"}
A record of all financial transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world during a period. Every transaction appears once as a credit (a receipt from abroad) and once as a debit (a payment abroad) in the full accounts, so the whole balance of payments always balances.
:::

:::definition{term="Current account"}
The part of the balance of payments that records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income. A **surplus** means receipts exceed payments; a **deficit** means payments exceed receipts.
:::

## The four components

1. **Trade in goods (visible trade):** exports and imports of physical goods such as tobacco, minerals, maize, fuel and vehicles. Exports are credits; imports are debits. The balance is the **balance of trade in goods**.
2. **Trade in services (invisible trade):** tourism, transport, insurance, banking and communications. A tourist spending in Victoria Falls is a services *export* for Zimbabwe, because it brings in foreign currency. Balance: **balance of trade in services**.
3. **Primary income:** income from the ownership of factors of production abroad: wages earned by a country's residents working abroad (for a short time), and profits, interest and dividends. Profits sent home by a foreign-owned mine to its parent are a **debit**; interest received on foreign assets is a **credit**.
4. **Secondary income (current transfers):** transfers with no good or service in return: remittances sent home by relatives abroad, grants and foreign aid, and contributions to international organisations.

:::keyfact{title="Balances"}
Balance of trade in goods = exports of goods − imports of goods.
Balance of trade in services = exports of services − imports of services.
Balance of trade in goods and services = the two added together.
**Current account balance = goods + services + primary income + secondary income.**
A negative figure is a deficit; a positive figure is a surplus. Many countries have a deficit in goods but a surplus in services, or the reverse.
:::

## Worked example

The table shows the current account of a fictional country, Zandia (illustrative data, US\$ million).

| Section | Credits (US\$ million) | Debits (US\$ million) | Balance (US\$ million) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Trade in goods | 5,200 | 6,100 | −900 (deficit) |
| Trade in services | 900 | 1,250 | −350 (deficit) |
| Primary income | 300 | 640 | −340 (deficit) |
| Secondary income | 1,100 | 260 | 840 (surplus) |
| **Current account balance** | | | **−750** |

:::worked-solution{title="Balances for Zandia"}
1. Goods: 5,200 − 6,100 = **−US\$900 million** (a deficit).
2. Services: 900 − 1,250 = **−US\$350 million**.
3. Goods and services: −900 + (−350) = **−US\$1,250 million**.
4. Primary income: 300 − 640 = **−US\$340 million**.
5. Secondary income: 1,100 − 260 = **+US\$840 million**, mainly remittances and aid.
6. Current account: −1,250 − 340 + 840 = **−US\$750 million**, a deficit.
7. If Zandia's GDP is US\$12,500 million, the deficit is 750 ÷ 12,500 × 100 = **6.0% of GDP**.
:::

Notice that the secondary-income surplus reduced the deficit: without transfers the deficit would have been US\$1,590 million.

:::worked-solution{title="A surplus country"}
A second fictional country has goods exports of 4,300 and imports of 3,900; services exports of 700 and imports of 800; primary income credits of 250 and debits of 310; secondary income credits of 400 and debits of 90 (US\$ million).
1. Goods +400; services −100; goods and services = **+300**.
2. Primary −60; secondary +310.
3. Current account = 300 − 60 + 310 = **+US\$550 million** (a surplus).
4. The country has a surplus overall even though it has a deficit in services and in primary income.
:::

## Causes of current account imbalances

| Cause | Why it leads to a deficit (or a surplus) |
| --- | --- |
| Fast growth of income | Imports have a positive income elasticity, so imports grow faster than exports when the economy booms |
| A high exchange rate | Exports are dearer abroad and imports cheaper at home, so a deficit appears |
| Low competitiveness and low productivity | Home goods are overpriced or of poor quality compared with foreign goods |
| Faster inflation than trading partners | Home export prices rise relative to foreign prices, reducing competitiveness |
| A fall in the world price of a main export | Export revenue falls (terms of trade) |
| Dependence on a few commodities | Export earnings are volatile |
| Large foreign debts or foreign-owned firms | Interest, profits and dividends flow abroad (a primary-income deficit) |
| Weak demand abroad | Fewer exports are sold |

A **surplus** may be caused by the reverse: a competitive economy with a low exchange rate, strong exports of minerals or commodities when their prices are high, large remittances, or low domestic spending.

## Consequences

**A deficit**

- *External economy:* it must be financed by borrowing from abroad, selling assets or using reserves. Debt rises; reserves may fall; interest payments abroad increase (a primary-income debit). If lenders lose confidence, the exchange rate may fall.
- *Domestic economy:* net exports are negative, so aggregate demand is lower than it would be, which may reduce growth and employment. A weaker currency raises import prices and cost-push inflation. However, imports of capital goods can raise future capacity, so a deficit is not always harmful.
- *When it matters most:* a deficit is more of a problem if it is large, persistent, and financed by short-term borrowing rather than by long-term investment.

**A surplus**

- Net exports add to aggregate demand and reserves grow. But the surplus means other countries have deficits, which may lead to trade disputes; it could also mean that the country is consuming and investing too little at home, and a rising currency can then reduce competitiveness.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
A current account deficit of 6% of GDP is often described as a danger, but its seriousness depends on the cause and the financing. If the deficit arises because firms import machinery that will raise future output and is financed by long-term foreign investment, it may be sustainable. If it is due to rising consumption of imported goods and is financed by short-term borrowing or by using up reserves, it is more likely to force a policy change. Therefore the size, cause, duration and financing of a deficit matter more than the figure alone.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the balance of trade with the current account"}
The balance of trade covers only goods (and, in Cambridge usage, goods and services). The current account also includes primary and secondary income.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating remittances as a trade in services"}
Remittances are **secondary income** (a transfer). A tourist's spending is a services export.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the balance of payments is in deficit"}
The whole balance of payments always balances; it is the **current account** (or another part) that is in deficit or surplus, and the other accounts offset it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 usually asks you to calculate balances from a table and to explain a deficit; Paper 3 essays ask about causes and cures. Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 give a table of credits and debits (AS Level), ask you to identify the effects of a deficit, and ask you to assess its significance.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: subtract debits from credits for each part, keeping signs. *Explain*: link the cause to exports or imports. *Assess*: consider size, cause, financing and how long the deficit lasts.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The current account records trade in goods, trade in services, primary income and secondary income.
- Balance = credits − debits; negative is a deficit.
- Deficits are caused by fast growth, an overvalued currency, low competitiveness, inflation, falling export prices and income paid abroad.
- A deficit must be financed by borrowing, asset sales or reserves; a persistent deficit can put pressure on the exchange rate.
- The seriousness of a deficit depends on its size, cause and financing, not just its existence.
:::
