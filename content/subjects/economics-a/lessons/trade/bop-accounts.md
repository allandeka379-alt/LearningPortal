## Introduction

The previous lesson showed that a country with a current account deficit pays abroad more than it receives. But money cannot simply vanish: somebody must supply the foreign currency to pay for the extra imports. The full **balance of payments** records both sides of every international transaction, so it also records *how the deficit is financed*. This lesson shows the three accounts, the role of the reserves and why the whole record balances, and why economists still talk about a country being in "disequilibrium".

You should know the four parts of the current account, what an exchange rate is and what foreign direct investment is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Capital account"}
The part of the balance of payments that records capital transfers (such as debt forgiveness and the transfers of migrants) and the purchase and sale of non-produced, non-financial assets such as patents and land rights. Its size is usually small.
:::

:::definition{term="Financial account"}
The part of the balance of payments that records cross-border investment and lending: **direct investment**, **portfolio investment**, **other investment** (mainly loans and bank deposits) and changes in **reserve assets**.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign direct investment and portfolio investment"}
**Direct investment** is the setting up or purchase of a controlling interest in a business abroad (a lasting interest). **Portfolio investment** is the purchase of shares or bonds without control, which can be sold quickly and so is more volatile ("hot money").
:::

:::definition{term="Reserves and official financing"}
**Reserve assets** are the foreign currencies and other assets held by the central bank for international payments. **Official financing** is the use of reserves, or of borrowing from other governments and institutions such as the IMF, to cover any gap left after private flows.
:::

## The structure of the accounts

| Account | What it records | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Current account** | Trade in goods and services; primary and secondary income | Exports of tobacco, tourism, dividends paid abroad, remittances |
| **Capital account** | Capital transfers and non-produced non-financial assets | Debt forgiveness, migrants' transfers, sale of patent rights |
| **Financial account** | Investment and lending flows | A foreign firm builds a plant (direct); a foreign fund buys shares (portfolio); a bank loan (other); the central bank sells foreign currency from its reserves (reserve assets) |
| **Net errors and omissions** | The balancing item | Because data are collected from different sources, the totals never match exactly |

:::keyfact{title="The accounting identity"}
**Current account + capital account + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.**
In the layout used here, flows into the country are positive and flows out are negative; the financial account is shown **before** any change in reserves, and the change in reserves (official financing) is the balance that makes the total zero.
**Balance for official financing = current + capital + financial (private) + net errors and omissions.** A negative figure is met by running down reserves (or official borrowing); a positive figure adds to reserves.
:::

## Worked example

The table shows the balance of payments of a fictional country (illustrative data, US\$ million; inflows positive).

| Item | US\$ million |
| --- | ---: |
| Current account balance | −750 |
| Capital account balance | +40 |
| Direct investment (net inflow) | +300 |
| Portfolio investment (net inflow) | +180 |
| Other investment, mainly loans (net inflow) | +200 |
| Net errors and omissions | −20 |

:::worked-solution{title="Balance for official financing"}
1. Financial account (before reserves) = 300 + 180 + 200 = **+US\$680 million**.
2. Balance for official financing = −750 + 40 + 680 − 20 = **−US\$50 million**.
3. The country is short of US\$50 million, which is met by running down its reserves, so reserves **fall by US\$50 million** (the official financing is +50 in the reserves line).
4. Including reserves, the total is −50 + 50 = **0**: the balance of payments balances.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="A surplus case"}
A country has a current account surplus of US\$300 million, capital account of 0, and a net outflow of US\$120 million on direct investment (firms investing abroad), an inflow of US\$30 million of portfolio investment and an outflow of US\$40 million of other investment; errors and omissions are +10.
1. Financial account = −120 + 30 − 40 = **−US\$130 million**.
2. Balance for official financing = 300 + 0 − 130 + 10 = **+US\$180 million**.
3. Reserves **rise by US\$180 million**.
:::

## Why the balance of payments always balances, and what "disequilibrium" means

Every transaction has two sides. If a Zimbabwean firm imports a machine (a current account debit), the payment either leaves the country through a bank, is financed by a loan from abroad (a financial account credit) or by selling foreign currency held in reserves. So in the accounting sense the **total always balances**.

Economists nevertheless speak of **disequilibrium** when one part is persistently in deficit or surplus, in particular the current account. A current account deficit is *financed* by a financial account surplus, but the financing may not be sustainable:

- **Sustainable financing:** long-term foreign direct investment that builds capacity and exports (it also creates future profit outflows, which are a primary-income debit).
- **Unsustainable financing:** short-term portfolio flows or borrowing, which may reverse suddenly when confidence falls and force a sharp fall in the currency; or rapid use of reserves, which are finite.

:::example{title="Reading an account"}
A country has a current account deficit of 8% of GDP, financed mainly by direct investment in a new mine and in factories. Supporters argue that the investment brings capital, jobs and skills, so the deficit is a sign of confidence and will be repaid from higher exports. Critics argue that profits will flow abroad (a larger primary income deficit later), and that the deficit could reverse if investors lose confidence. The conclusion depends on whether the investment raises export capacity.
:::

## Direct, portfolio and other investment

| Type | Features | Risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct investment | Long-term, controlling interest, brings capital, technology and skills | Profits sent abroad; possible dependence |
| Portfolio investment | Shares and bonds; easily bought and sold | Volatile; sudden outflows ("hot money") can cause a currency crisis |
| Other investment | Bank loans and deposits, trade credit | Must be repaid with interest, adding to external debt |
| Reserve assets | Foreign currency and gold held by the central bank | Finite; used to support the currency or cover a gap |

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge uses the modern layout of current, capital and financial accounts (with reserve assets in the financial account). ZIMSEC (Form 6) describes the older layout, in which long-term and short-term capital flows sit in the "capital account", and uses the **balance for official financing** (the change in reserves and official borrowing). Both layouts record the same flows and both end with an identity that sums to zero; know the term used in your syllabus and use the other as an alternative if the question's table uses it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that a deficit means the accounts do not balance"}
The accounts always balance in total. A current account deficit is offset by a financial account surplus or by a fall in reserves.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up signs"}
A **fall** in reserves is the financing item that offsets a deficit; it is not itself a deficit. Check whether the table shows inflows as positive.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all capital inflows as equally good"}
Direct investment is long-term and stable, but portfolio flows can reverse quickly. A deficit financed by "hot money" is more vulnerable.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 3 test the accounts and the balance for official financing, usually with a table of figures. Cambridge Paper 3 asks multiple-choice questions on the accounts and reserves, and Paper 4 may ask you to assess whether a deficit is sustainable.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: add the items with their signs, then state whether reserves rise or fall. *Explain*: link each financial inflow to the deficit it finances. *Assess*: judge sustainability by the type of finance, the size of the deficit and the level of reserves.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The balance of payments has a current account, a capital account (small transfers) and a financial account (direct, portfolio, other investment and reserves), plus a balancing item.
- Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions sums to zero, so the accounts always balance.
- The balance for official financing is met by changing reserves or official borrowing.
- Direct investment is long-term; portfolio flows can reverse suddenly.
- Disequilibrium means a persistent imbalance, especially a current account deficit financed in an unsustainable way.
:::
