## Introduction

When a shop in Harare sells shirts made in another country, shoppers gain from lower prices and more choice, but the clothing factory in Bulawayo may lose customers and workers. This tension lies at the heart of the debate on **free trade**. Almost all economists agree that trade creates gains overall; the question is how the gains and losses are shared and what a government should do about those who lose.

This lesson reviews what determines the flows of trade, the benefits and costs of free trade, and how to evaluate them. The lesson on absolute and comparative advantage explains why countries trade; the next lesson on protectionism looks at the tools that restrict trade. You should know supply and demand, consumer and producer surplus, and the idea of comparative advantage.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Free trade"}
International trade between countries without tariffs, quotas, subsidies or other barriers that restrict or favour particular imports or exports.
:::

:::definition{term="Specialisation"}
Concentrating on producing the goods or services in which a country, firm or worker is most efficient (has a comparative advantage), and trading for the rest.
:::

:::definition{term="Dumping"}
Selling a good in a foreign market at a price below its cost of production or below its price in the home market.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade openness"}
A measure of the importance of trade to an economy: (exports + imports) ÷ GDP × 100.
:::

## What determines the pattern and flow of trade?

1. **Comparative advantage.** Countries export the goods in which they have a lower opportunity cost, based on their land, minerals, climate, labour skills and technology.
2. **Exchange rates.** A fall in a country's exchange rate makes its exports cheaper abroad and imports more expensive at home, which raises exports and reduces imports (other things equal).
3. **Incomes.** Higher incomes at home raise imports (especially of luxury goods with a high income elasticity of demand); higher incomes abroad raise demand for exports.
4. **Trade barriers and agreements.** Tariffs, quotas and regulations reduce trade, whereas free trade areas such as those in the SADC and COMESA regions encourage it among members.
5. **Quality, brand and non-price factors**, and the cost of transport and the quality of infrastructure.
6. **Political factors.** Political stability, relations between countries, sanctions and the security of property affect willingness to trade and invest.

## The benefits of free trade

1. **Efficiency from specialisation.** Resources move to the industries in which the country has a comparative advantage, raising total output.
2. **Lower prices and greater choice.** Imports give consumers a wider range of goods, often at lower prices.
3. **Competition.** Domestic firms facing foreign rivals have an incentive to cut costs and innovate.
4. **Economies of scale.** Firms selling to a world market can produce on a larger scale and lower their average costs.
5. **Access to inputs and technology.** Firms can import machinery, fuel and raw materials that are not available at home.
6. **Economic growth.** Higher efficiency and investment can raise output and incomes, and export earnings pay for needed imports.

:::worked-solution{title="Gains from free trade in a market"}
In a (fictional, illustrative) country the demand for shirts is P = 100 − Q and home supply is P = 20 + Q (P in US\$, Q in units). Without trade, the market clears where 100 − Q = 20 + Q, giving **Q = 40** and **P = US\$60**. The world price of shirts is US\$40, and the country is small, so it can import any quantity at that price.
1. At US\$40, quantity demanded = 100 − 40 = **60**; home firms supply 40 − 20 = **20**; imports = 60 − 20 = **40**.
2. Consumer surplus without trade = ½ × (100 − 60) × 40 = **US\$800**; with free trade = ½ × (100 − 40) × 60 = **US\$1,800**. Consumers gain US\$1,000.
3. Producer surplus without trade = ½ × (60 − 20) × 40 = **US\$800**; with free trade = ½ × (40 − 20) × 20 = **US\$200**. Home producers lose US\$600.
4. Net gain = 1,000 − 600 = **US\$400**: the gains to consumers are larger than the losses to producers.
:::

|  | No trade (autarky) | Free trade at the world price |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Price (US\$) | 60 | 40 |
| Quantity demanded (units) | 40 | 60 |
| Quantity supplied by home firms (units) | 40 | 20 |
| Imports (units) | 0 | 40 |
| Consumer surplus (US\$) | 800 | 1,800 |
| Producer surplus (US\$) | 800 | 200 |
| **Total surplus (US\$)** | **1,600** | **2,000** |

The net gain of US\$400 is larger than zero, so the winners could compensate the losers and still be better off, but in practice compensation seldom happens.

:::worked-solution{title="Trade openness"}
An economy has exports of US\$3,000 million, imports of US\$3,600 million and GDP of US\$10,000 million.
1. Exports plus imports = 3,000 + 3,600 = US\$6,600 million.
2. Trade openness = 6,600 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = **66%**.
3. A high ratio shows that the economy depends on trade, so it benefits from open markets but is exposed to events abroad.
:::

## The costs and risks of free trade

1. **Adjustment costs.** Industries that cannot compete shrink or close, and workers may suffer structural unemployment because their skills do not match the jobs available. Regions that depend on one industry are especially hurt.
2. **Dependence and vulnerability.** A country that specialises in a few exports is exposed to price falls and to demand shocks abroad; dependence on imported food or fuel is risky.
3. **Dumping.** Foreign firms may sell below cost to drive out local firms, and then raise prices.
4. **Loss of infant industries.** New industries may be unable to grow when exposed at once to powerful foreign rivals.
5. **Structural change and inequality.** The gains may go to consumers and exporters while the costs fall on particular workers and areas.
6. **Environmental and other external costs**, such as transport emissions and pressure on resources.
7. **Loss of control** over policy, and the risk that rich countries' firms dominate.

## Justifying free trade

Supporters argue that the gains from specialisation, competition, choice and growth outweigh the costs, which can be reduced by policies such as retraining, regional support and safety nets. Critics argue that developing countries need to protect new industries, that the gains are shared unequally and that unrestricted trade can harm the environment.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
Free trade allows countries to specialise in what they produce at lowest opportunity cost, which raises output and gives consumers lower prices and more choice. Competition from imports pushes domestic firms to become more efficient. However, the gains are not shared equally. Workers in declining industries may become unemployed for long periods, and a developing country that opens its markets too quickly may see new industries fail before they can compete. Free trade is therefore most beneficial when it is accompanied by policies for retraining, investment in skills and infrastructure and, where justified, temporary support for new industries. The size of the net benefit depends on how easily resources can move to new uses.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that everyone gains from free trade"}
Free trade gives net gains to the economy, but there are winners and losers. Consumers and efficient exporters gain; producers who compete with imports may lose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing dumping with ordinary low prices"}
Dumping means selling below cost (or below the price in the home market), usually to damage competitors. A low price caused by lower costs is not dumping.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the time dimension"}
Gains appear gradually, while the costs of adjustment (job losses) often appear first.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 tests the determinants, benefits and costs; Paper 2 data responses use trade data; Paper 3 essays ask whether a country should adopt free trade. Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 test the benefits, costs and an evaluation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: give a benefit or cost and link it to its mechanism (for example, competition leads to lower costs). *Calculate*: use the surplus formulae or the openness ratio. *Discuss* or *evaluate*: weigh the benefits against the costs, say who gains and who loses, and reach a conclusion that depends on the circumstances.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The pattern of trade depends on comparative advantage, exchange rates, incomes, trade barriers, quality and transport, and political factors.
- Free trade allows specialisation, lower prices, more choice, competition, economies of scale and growth.
- Costs include structural unemployment, dependence, dumping, damage to infant industries and unequal gains.
- A small country that opens its market gains net surplus (consumers gain more than producers lose).
- The case for free trade is stronger where resources can move easily and adjustment is supported.
:::
