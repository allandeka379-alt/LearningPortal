## Introduction

Governments do not all leave the exchange rate to the market. Some peg their currency to the US dollar or the euro, some let it float but step in when it moves too far, and a few abandon their own currency and use another country's. Each system has costs and benefits for inflation, trade, investment and the freedom to set policy. This lesson explains how each system works and how to judge between them.

You should know how a floating exchange rate is determined by demand and supply, what reserves are, and the effect of interest rates on capital flows.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fixed (pegged) exchange rate"}
A system in which the government or central bank commits to keep the exchange rate at, or within a narrow band around, a stated value against another currency or a basket of currencies, by buying and selling currency and by adjusting interest rates.
:::

:::definition{term="Managed floating exchange rate"}
A system in which the rate is mainly determined by the market, but the central bank intervenes from time to time to limit large fluctuations or to steer the rate towards a desired level.
:::

:::definition{term="Revaluation and devaluation"}
A **revaluation** is a deliberate rise in the official value of a currency under a fixed system; a **devaluation** is a deliberate fall.
:::

:::definition{term="Nominal, real and trade-weighted exchange rates"}
The **nominal** exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. The **real** exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels between countries and so measures competitiveness. A **trade-weighted** exchange rate index measures a currency against a basket of currencies weighted by each partner's share in the country's trade.
:::

## Holding a rate: fixed and managed systems

Take the market for a currency with a floating equilibrium of 28 US cents (illustrative schedule from the previous lesson). Suppose the government fixes the rate at **32 cents**, above the equilibrium (an overvalued currency).

| Price of 1 unit of currency (US cents) | Demand (millions) | Supply (millions) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 28 | 700 | 700 |
| 32 | 600 | 850 |

:::worked-solution{title="Defending an overvalued peg"}
1. At the peg of 32 cents, supply (850 million units) exceeds demand (600 million): excess supply = **250 million units**.
2. To stop the price falling, the central bank must buy the excess supply with foreign currency from its reserves.
3. Cost per period = 250 million × US\$0.32 = **US\$80 million of reserves**.
4. If this continues, reserves run out, and the peg must be abandoned, a devaluation made, or the excess demand removed by raising the interest rate (to attract funds and reduce spending on imports).
:::

The central bank can defend a peg by:

1. **Using reserves** to buy its own currency (above) or selling it to stop a rise (below).
2. **Changing interest rates**: a higher rate attracts capital and raises demand; a lower rate does the reverse.
3. **Controls on capital or on imports** (which have costs).
4. **Changing domestic policy**: reducing spending to cut imports.

If a peg cannot be defended, the authorities **devalue**. If the rate is below the market equilibrium (undervalued), reserves rise as the central bank buys foreign currency, and a **revaluation** may follow.

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of a devaluation"}
A currency is devalued from US\$1 = M10 to US\$1 = M12.
1. The value of M falls from US\$0.1000 to US\$0.0833: a fall of (0.0833 − 0.1) ÷ 0.1 × 100 = **−16.7%**.
2. A machine priced at US\$50 now costs 50 × 12 = **M600** instead of M500: imports are 20% dearer in the home currency.
3. An export priced at M600 costs US\$50 abroad instead of US\$60: exports are cheaper for foreigners.
:::

## Real and trade-weighted exchange rates

:::keyfact{title="Indices"}
**Trade-weighted index = Σ (weight × currency index) ÷ Σ weights**, where the weights are each partner's share of trade.
**Real exchange rate index = nominal index × (home price index ÷ foreign price index)**, with the nominal index showing foreign currency per unit of home currency (a rise is an appreciation). A rise in the real index is a real appreciation (loss of competitiveness).
:::

| Trading partner | Share of trade (%) | Currency index (base year = 100) | Weight × index |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Partner A | 50 | 110 | 55.0 |
| Partner B | 30 | 95 | 28.5 |
| Partner C | 20 | 80 | 16.0 |

:::worked-solution{title="Trade-weighted and real indices"}
1. Trade-weighted index = (55.0 + 28.5 + 16.0) ÷ 100 = **99.5**. The currency has, on average, fallen slightly (0.5%) against its trading partners, though it rose against Partner A.
2. A nominal index of 105, with home prices at 120 and foreign prices at 110 (base year 100), gives a real index of 105 × (120 ÷ 110) = **114.5**.
3. The currency has appreciated in real terms by 14.5%: even if the nominal rate rose only 5%, faster inflation at home has reduced competitiveness further.
:::

## Comparing the systems

| | Fixed (pegged) | Managed float | Free float |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Advantages** | Certainty for traders and investors; discipline on inflation; credibility | Some stability, with flexibility to adjust | Automatic adjustment; no need for large reserves; monetary policy is free for domestic aims |
| **Disadvantages** | Needs large reserves; interest rates must defend the peg, so domestic policy is limited; risk of speculative attack; overvaluation harms exporters | Intervention can be costly and fail; uncertainty about the policy; possible market suspicion | Volatility; speculation; imported inflation after a depreciation |
| **Examples of type** | Currency boards, pegs to the dollar or euro | Many emerging economies | Currencies of large, open financial markets |

## Using foreign currencies

Some countries replace their own currency with a foreign one (**dollarisation**) or allow foreign currencies to circulate beside the local one (a **multi-currency system**). After the severe inflation of 2007 to 2008, Zimbabwe used foreign currencies, mainly the US dollar and the South African rand, from 2009, and later reintroduced local currencies.

:::example{title="Using a foreign currency: arguments on both sides"}
Supporters argue that it can quickly end high inflation, because the central bank cannot print the currency, and it removes the risk of exchange rate fluctuations against the dollar. Critics argue that the country loses its own monetary policy and exchange rate as tools, cannot create money to act as a lender of last resort, depends on foreign currency inflows from exports and remittances, and faces a shortage of notes and liquidity if those inflows fall. The effect therefore depends on the strength of the export sector and the discipline of the government.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge (A Level only) expects the three systems, the use of reserves and interest rates to hold a rate, trade-weighted and real exchange rates, and an evaluation. ZIMSEC (Form 6) expects fixed, managed and floating systems and their use in policy, and describes devaluation and revaluation. The use of foreign currencies alongside a local currency should be described neutrally with both sides stated.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up appreciation and revaluation"}
Appreciation and depreciation happen under a floating system (market forces); revaluation and devaluation are deliberate official changes of a pegged rate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the cost of defending a peg"}
Defending an overvalued peg uses up reserves and may require high interest rates that reduce growth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the nominal rate to judge competitiveness"}
Competitiveness depends on the real exchange rate, which includes price levels, and on the trade-weighted rate against the main partners.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 2 and 3 ask about systems and their advantages; Cambridge Paper 3 asks multiple-choice questions on the use of reserves and index calculations, and Paper 4 on evaluating a policy choice (a peg, a float or a managed float).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: describe what the central bank must do to hold the rate. *Calculate*: use the formulae for weighted averages and the real index. *Evaluate*: weigh stability against policy freedom and the size of the reserves.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fixed systems hold the rate by using reserves and interest rates; floating systems leave it to the market; managed floats combine the two.
- An overvalued peg needs the central bank to buy its own currency, using up reserves.
- Devaluation and revaluation are deliberate; appreciation and depreciation are market movements.
- Trade-weighted and real exchange rate indices measure average and competitive positions.
- Each system trades stability against policy freedom; foreign currency use brings credibility but costs control.
:::
