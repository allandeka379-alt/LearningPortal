## Introduction

Zimbabwe belongs to more than one regional trading arrangement. It is a member of the Southern African Development Community (SADC) and of the Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA), and the continent as a whole has agreed an African Continental Free Trade Area. Why do governments sign such agreements, and who gains and who loses when neighbours trade more freely with each other than with the rest of the world? This lesson gives you the vocabulary of integration, the model of **trade creation** and **trade diversion**, and the arguments you need to evaluate a trading bloc.

You should already know the case for free trade and the effects of a tariff (the trade lessons so far in this course).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Economic integration"}
The process by which countries reduce or remove barriers to trade and, in deeper forms, to the movement of capital and labour and to differences in policy, so that their economies become more closely linked.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove tariffs and quotas on trade between themselves, while each member keeps its own tariffs on imports from non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Customs union"}
A free trade area whose members also adopt a **common external tariff** on imports from non-members.
:::

:::definition{term="Monetary union"}
A group of countries that share a single currency and a common central bank and monetary policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade creation and trade diversion"}
**Trade creation** occurs when a trading bloc replaces higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a partner. **Trade diversion** occurs when it replaces lower-cost imports from outside the bloc with higher-cost imports from a partner, because the partner's goods have become cheaper than the outsider's after the external tariff.
:::

## The stages of integration

Each step goes further than the one before it:

1. **Preferential trade area:** members give each other lower tariffs on some goods.
2. **Free trade area:** no tariffs or quotas between members; separate external tariffs.
3. **Customs union:** free trade inside, plus a common external tariff.
4. **Common market** (a step beyond a customs union): free movement of labour and capital as well as goods and services, with common rules.
5. **Monetary union:** a single currency and a common monetary policy (the euro area is the usual example).
6. **Full economic union:** a monetary union with common fiscal and other economic policies and shared institutions.

:::keyfact{title="Customs union against free trade area"}
In a free trade area each member keeps its own external tariff, so goods can enter through the member with the lowest tariff and cross to a partner (so rules of origin are needed). In a customs union all members charge the same external tariff, so no such checks are needed, but members lose control of their own tariffs.
:::

## Trade creation and trade diversion: a worked example

Two economies, Zandia and its neighbour Partnia, form a customs union. Both used to apply a 50% tariff to imports from the rest of the world (ROW) and from each other. The table shows the cost of two goods in each source (illustrative data, US\$ per unit; the tariff is added to the ROW price and, before the union, to the partner's price).

| Good | Home cost | Partner cost | World price | World + 50% tariff | Partner + 50% tariff | Cheapest source before union | Cheapest source after union |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Good P (clothing) | 14 | 11 | 20 | 30.00 | 16.50 | 14.00 (home) | 11.00 (partner) |
| Good Q (fertiliser) | 22 | 11 | 9 | 13.50 | 16.50 | 13.50 (rest of world) | 11.00 (partner) |

:::worked-solution{title="Good P: trade creation"}
1. Before the union Zandian consumers buy clothing from home producers at US\$14, because imports cost US\$16.50 (partner) or US\$30 (world) after tariff.
2. After the union, partner clothing enters free of tariff at US\$11.
3. Zandia now imports from Partnia. The price falls by 14 − 11 = **US\$3 per unit**, consumers gain, and resources move out of the high-cost Zandian clothing industry into other uses. This is **trade creation**.
4. Cost: Zandian clothing producers lose output and jobs, so adjustment costs arise.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Good Q: trade diversion"}
1. Before the union the cheapest source is the rest of the world: it costs US\$9 and US\$13.50 after the 50% tariff, which is below the partner's price of US\$16.50 and the home cost of US\$22.
2. After the union, partner fertiliser costs US\$11 tariff-free, which is below the US\$13.50 tax-inclusive price of the world's fertiliser. Imports switch to the partner.
3. The **consumer price falls** from US\$13.50 to US\$11.00, but the real resource cost of each unit rises from US\$9 to US\$11, an extra **US\$2 per unit**, because the world's more efficient producer is displaced. The government also loses the tariff revenue of 13.50 − 9 = **US\$4.50 per unit**.
4. This is **trade diversion**. Whether the union is worth it depends on the balance of creation and diversion, and on the size of the gains from larger markets.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Judging a customs union"}
A customs union is more likely to raise welfare if (a) its members' costs are close to the lowest world costs, so diversion is small, (b) the external tariff is low, (c) the members were already large trading partners, and (d) members' economies are competitive rather than complementary (creation is bigger where the partners make similar goods at different costs).
:::

## Merits and demerits of integration

| Merits | Demerits |
| --- | --- |
| Lower prices and wider choice for consumers (trade creation) | Trade diversion if the external tariff is high |
| Larger market allows economies of scale and attracts foreign direct investment | Loss of national control of tariffs, and in a monetary union of interest rates and exchange rate |
| More competition raises efficiency | Adjustment costs: job losses in industries that cannot compete |
| Stronger bargaining power in world trade talks | Benefits may flow mainly to the more developed members, so inequality between members can widen |
| Specialisation by comparative advantage; regional cooperation and peace | Members with weak infrastructure and few exports may gain little; overlapping memberships create complex rules |

## Regional examples and trade agreements

:::example{title="SADC and COMESA"}
Supporters of regional integration in southern Africa argue that small domestic markets limit specialisation, so larger regional markets and common rules help firms to grow and attract investment. Critics argue that many members export similar primary products, so there is limited scope for trade between them, that poor roads and border delays raise costs, and that overlapping memberships make rules complicated. Both sets of arguments are about **trade creation, trade diversion and the costs of adjustment**.
:::

**Commodity agreements** (ZIMSEC detail). Producers and sometimes consumers agree to stabilise the price of a primary product by regulating supply, for example through quotas or buffer stocks. They aim to protect export earnings, but they are hard to keep going: members cheat on quotas, new producers enter, and a price held above equilibrium creates a surplus.

**GATT and the WTO** (ZIMSEC detail). The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (1947) was a series of rounds of negotiated tariff cuts. In 1995 the World Trade Organization (WTO) replaced it, adding a dispute-settlement system and covering services as well as goods. Its main principle is non-discrimination: a member must normally give every other member the same terms (most-favoured-nation treatment). Customs unions and free trade areas are permitted exceptions, provided they cover substantially all trade. **Bilateral agreements** are deals between two countries; they are quicker to agree than multilateral ones but add to a tangle of different rules.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge (A Level only) expects the forms of integration, trade creation and diversion, and an assessment of their effects. ZIMSEC (Form 5) also names commodity agreements, GATT/WTO and bilateral agreements, and the common market as a step. Both expect regional examples to be described cautiously and accurately.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a free trade area with a customs union"}
The difference is the **common external tariff**. Free trade inside is common to both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that any trade diversion makes a union harmful"}
Diversion has a cost, but a union can still raise welfare if the gains from creation, scale and competition exceed it. Judge the balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up consumer price and resource cost"}
In diversion the consumer price may fall (as with Good Q) while the real cost of supplying the good rises, and the government loses tariff revenue.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 to 3 test the definitions, the stages of integration, agreements such as the WTO and commodity agreements, and Paper 3 may ask for a discussion of the benefits and costs of regional integration. Cambridge A Level Paper 3 asks multiple-choice questions on the forms of integration and on trade creation against diversion, and Paper 4 may ask you to evaluate membership of a bloc.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: use the precise feature (for example the common external tariff). *Explain*: show how a price or source of supply changes. *Assess* or *evaluate*: weigh creation against diversion, then judge using the size of the external tariff, the members' costs and the size of the market.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Integration deepens from a preferential trade area to a free trade area, customs union, common market, monetary union and full economic union.
- A customs union adds a common external tariff to free internal trade.
- Trade creation replaces higher-cost home output with lower-cost partner imports; trade diversion replaces lower-cost outside imports with higher-cost partner imports.
- Benefits include lower prices, scale, competition and investment; costs include diversion, lost policy control and adjustment.
- ZIMSEC also covers commodity agreements, GATT/WTO and bilateral agreements.
:::
