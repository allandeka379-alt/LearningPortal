## Introduction

Tendai runs a small welding workshop in Bulawayo. At the end of the year his accounts show US\$15,000 left after paying all bills. He could have earned US\$12,000 as an employee at a large engineering firm. Is the workshop doing well? An economist says that US\$12,000 of the US\$15,000 is simply what Tendai needs to keep him in the business, and only US\$3,000 is extra. The lesson explains this idea of normal profit, the other profit categories and the decision on when a loss-making firm should close.

You should know average and marginal cost and the MC = MR rule. All numbers are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Normal profit"}
The minimum profit needed to keep the entrepreneur in the current line of production in the long run. It is the opportunity cost of enterprise (and of the owner's own capital) and is counted as a cost, so it is included in the firm's average total cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Supernormal (abnormal) profit"}
Profit above normal profit: total revenue exceeds total economic cost, including normal profit. The two names mean the same; Cambridge uses supernormal and ZIMSEC often uses abnormal.
:::

:::definition{term="Subnormal profit"}
Profit below normal profit: total revenue is less than total economic cost (which includes normal profit). The firm may still have a positive accounting profit that is smaller than normal profit, or it may make an accounting loss.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Profit formula"}
$\text{Profit} = \text{TR} - \text{TC} = (\text{AR} - \text{ATC}) \times Q$. Because ATC includes normal profit, a result of zero means **normal profit**; a positive result is supernormal profit; a negative result is subnormal profit (when measured against economic cost, which includes normal profit).
:::

## Types of profit

| Condition | Meaning | Long-run effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AR > ATC | Supernormal profit | Attracts new firms where entry is possible |
| AR = ATC | Normal profit (break-even in the economic sense) | Firms stay: no entry or exit |
| AR < ATC | Subnormal profit or loss | Firms leave in the long run unless things improve |

:::worked-solution{title="Accounting profit and economic profit"}
Tendai's workshop has revenue of US\$80,000 and pays explicit costs (steel, rent, electricity, helper wages) of US\$65,000. Tendai could earn US\$12,000 a year working for someone else.

1. Accounting profit $= 80{,}000 - 65{,}000 = 15{,}000$.
2. Normal profit (the opportunity cost of his own labour and enterprise) $= 12{,}000$.
3. Economic (supernormal) profit $= 15{,}000 - 12{,}000 = 3{,}000$.
:::

## Profit from a cost schedule

The bakery in the cost lesson has total fixed cost of US\$60. Its costs are repeated here (the final column shows marginal cost).

| Output (units) | AVC (US\$) | ATC (US\$) | MC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3 | 30.00 | 50.00 | 20.00 |
| 4 | 28.00 | 43.00 | 22.00 |
| 5 | 28.20 | 40.20 | 29.00 |
| 6 | 30.33 | 40.33 | 41.00 |
| 7 | 34.00 | 42.57 | 56.00 |

As a price-taker the bakery produces where price (= MR) equals rising MC. Consider three prices.

:::worked-solution{title="Supernormal profit"}
The market price is US\$50.

1. The bakery expands while MC is below 50: the sixth unit costs US\$41, the seventh US\$56. Output is 6 units.
2. $\text{TR} = 50 \times 6 = 300$ and $\text{TC} = 242$, so profit $= 300 - 242 =$ US\$58.
3. Check using the formula: $(\text{AR} - \text{ATC}) \times Q = (50 - 40.33) \times 6 =$ US\$58. This is supernormal profit.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="A loss, but keep producing"}
The market price falls to US\$36.

1. Output where MC is below the price: 5 units (MC 29; the sixth unit costs 41).
2. $\text{TR} = 180$, $\text{TC} = 201$, so profit $= -21$: a loss of US\$21.
3. Price (36) exceeds AVC (28.20), so each unit covers its variable cost and contributes $36 - 28.20 = 7.80$ towards fixed cost. The total contribution is $7.80 \times 5 = 39$, which pays part of the fixed cost of 60.
4. If it closed, the loss would be the full fixed cost of US\$60. It is better to produce and lose US\$21.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Shut down"}
The market price falls to US\$25.

1. Output where MC is below the price and rising: 4 units (MC 22; the fifth costs 29).
2. $\text{TR} = 100$ and $\text{TC} = 172$, so producing gives a loss of US\$72.
3. Price (25) is below AVC (28.00). Each unit fails to cover its variable cost, so producing makes the loss larger than the fixed cost of US\$60.
4. Shutting down costs only the fixed cost: a loss of US\$60. The firm should shut down.
:::

## The shutdown conditions

:::keyfact{title="Shutdown rules"}
**Short run:** a firm should stop producing if the price (average revenue) is below average variable cost, because TR then fails to cover TVC. If AVC < price < ATC, it makes a loss but should carry on, since it covers variable cost and part of fixed cost.
**Long run:** all costs are variable, so a firm should leave the industry if price is below ATC; it must cover normal profit in full.
:::

A firm may also carry on at a loss for a short time if it expects the price to recover, or if shutting down would lose skilled workers or customers. These are practical considerations around the rule.

## Profit maximisation and cost minimisation

Profit maximisation finds the output that makes TR − TC largest. **Cost minimisation** asks a different question: for a given output, which combination of inputs gives the lowest total cost? A firm that maximises profit must also minimise the cost of that output; otherwise it could make more profit by cutting costs. A firm may, however, aim at lowest cost of producing a chosen output (for example a contract to deliver a fixed quantity) without choosing the output to maximise profit. This distinction is ZIMSEC detail.

:::example{title="Applying the shutdown rule"}
During a sharp fall in the price of maize meal, a small fictional mill in Gweru sells at US\$24 a bag while its variable cost is US\$20 and its average total cost is US\$27. It loses US\$3 on each bag but covers its variable cost, so it should carry on producing in the short run: closing would make it lose all of its fixed cost. If the price stays below US\$27 permanently, the owner will eventually leave the industry because the mill earns less than normal profit.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect normal, supernormal (abnormal) and subnormal profit, the calculation from ATC and the shutdown rule. ZIMSEC (Form 6) adds the cost-minimisation point. Cambridge may ask for the answer to be expressed as a diagram described in words with price on the vertical axis, and asks to apply the rules to market structures.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Believing that normal profit is zero profit"}
Normal profit is the return the entrepreneur needs; it is a cost. A firm earning only normal profit is not making a loss in the economic sense.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shutting down whenever there is a loss"}
In the short run the firm should shut down only when price is below AVC. A loss that is smaller than fixed cost is better than closing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using ATC to decide the short-run shutdown"}
The short-run condition uses AVC; the long-run condition uses ATC.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for definitions and the shutdown rule; Paper 2 gives a cost and revenue table to calculate profit; Paper 3 asks for discussion of when firms leave the industry. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 test (AR − ATC) × Q on a diagram described in words.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate profit*: use TR − TC or (AR − ATC) × Q. *Explain*: link the shutdown rule to covering variable cost. *Evaluate*: consider whether short-run losses justify continuing, giving the time horizon.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Normal profit is the opportunity cost of enterprise and is included in costs.
- Supernormal profit means AR > ATC; subnormal profit means AR < ATC.
- Profit = (AR − ATC) × Q = TR − TC.
- Short run: shut down if price is below AVC; keep going if price covers AVC but not ATC.
- Long run: leave the industry if price is below ATC.
- Cost minimisation (lowest cost for a given output) is part of, but not the same as, profit maximisation.
:::
