## Introduction

A carpenter in Gweru who takes on a second worker can usually make far more than twice as much furniture in a day, because the two can share tasks. Add a sixth and a seventh worker to the same small workshop and the benefit shrinks: people wait for the one saw, and bump into each other. This pattern, in which each extra worker adds less than the one before, lies behind the shape of every firm's cost curves. In this lesson you study production; the next lesson turns it into costs.

You should already know the factors of production (land, labour, capital and enterprise) and the idea of opportunity cost. All figures in this lesson are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Short run"}
The period of time in which at least one factor of production is fixed in quantity, so that output can be changed only by varying the other (variable) factors.
:::

:::definition{term="Long run"}
The period of time in which all factors of production can be varied, although the state of technology is still given.
:::

:::definition{term="Law of diminishing marginal returns"}
If successive units of a variable factor are added to a given quantity of fixed factors, with technology unchanged, the marginal product of the variable factor will eventually fall.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Product formulae"}
Total product (TP) is the total output of the firm. Average product $\text{AP} = \dfrac{\text{TP}}{\text{number of workers}}$. Marginal product $\text{MP} = \dfrac{\text{change in TP}}{\text{change in the number of workers}}$, which is the extra output from one more worker when workers are added one at a time.
:::

The short run and the long run are not fixed lengths of time. For a street vendor the long run may be a week, since a new stall can be built quickly; for an electricity generator it may be ten years.

## Reading a production schedule

A fictional furniture workshop has one building and a fixed set of tools. It hires more workers and records the daily output of chairs.

| Workers | Total product (units) | Average product | Marginal product |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 0 | – | – |
| 1 | 12 | 12 | 12 |
| 2 | 30 | 15 | 18 |
| 3 | 54 | 18 | 24 |
| 4 | 74 | 18.50 | 20 |
| 5 | 87 | 17.40 | 13 |
| 6 | 95 | 15.83 | 8 |
| 7 | 99 | 14.14 | 4 |
| 8 | 98 | 12.25 | −1 |

:::worked-solution{title="Average and marginal product"}
Find AP and MP for the fourth and fifth workers.

1. Fourth worker: MP $= (74 - 54) \div 1 = 20$ chairs. AP with four workers $= 74 \div 4 = 18.5$ chairs.
2. Fifth worker: MP $= 87 - 74 = 13$ chairs. AP with five workers $= 87 \div 5 = 17.4$ chairs.
3. The marginal product of the fourth worker (20) is below that of the third (24), so diminishing marginal returns set in with the fourth worker.
:::

## Why returns diminish

In the first stage each worker adds more than the last, because the fixed tools are under-used and workers can specialise. This is sometimes called increasing marginal returns. Eventually the fixed factor becomes the constraint: the workshop has one building, and workers share the same machines, so each additional worker has less capital to work with. The law is a statement about the short run and about adding units of **one** variable factor to **fixed** others. It is not a claim that workers become lazy or less skilled: every unit of labour is assumed to be identical. It also does not apply to the long run, where all inputs rise together.

In the table, total product keeps rising until the seventh worker, then falls: the eighth worker has a negative marginal product (−1), because the workshop is overcrowded.

## The relationship between average and marginal product

:::keyfact{title="MP and AP rule"}
When MP is above AP, AP rises. When MP is below AP, AP falls. MP cuts AP at the maximum point of AP.
:::

This is simply the arithmetic of averages: a new value above the current average pulls it up; a value below pulls it down. In the table, AP rises from 12 to 18.5 while each MP (12, 18, 24, 20) is above the previous AP, and falls from the fifth worker, whose MP (13) is below AP (18.5). MP reaches its maximum (24) before AP does (at four workers), because MP starts to fall while it is still above AP.

:::worked-solution{title="Where average product peaks"}
Use the schedule to find the number of workers at which average product is highest.

1. AP after each addition is 12, 15, 18, 18.5, 17.4, … so AP is highest with four workers.
2. The marginal product of the fourth worker (20) is above the AP of three workers (18), so AP rises. The fifth worker's MP (13) is below the AP of four workers (18.5), so AP falls.
3. AP therefore peaks at four workers, and MP crosses AP at that point.
:::

## Productivity

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
Output per unit of a factor of production in a given time, for example output per worker per day or per hour.
:::

Productivity differs from total output: a firm can raise output simply by hiring more workers, while productivity rises only if each worker produces more. Productivity grows through training, better machines and tools, better organisation and better health.

:::worked-solution{title="Labour productivity"}
A fictional clothing factory in Bulawayo employs 40 workers who make 1,200 shirts a day. After training and new sewing machines it makes 1,380 shirts a day with the same 40 workers.

1. Productivity before $= 1200 \div 40 = 30$ shirts per worker per day.
2. Productivity after $= 1380 \div 40 = 34.5$ shirts per worker per day.
3. Change $= (34.5 - 30) \div 30 \times 100 = +15\%$.
:::

:::example{title="Why productivity matters for costs"}
If wages are US\$10 a day, a worker who makes 30 shirts adds US\$0.33 of labour cost to each shirt, while a worker who makes 34.5 shirts adds about US\$0.29. Higher productivity means a lower labour cost per unit, which lets the firm lower its price or raise its profit. This is why economies compete on productivity as well as on wage rates: low wages do not help if output per worker is also low.
:::

## Linking to costs

Diminishing returns mean that each extra worker raises output by less, but the firm must still pay the same wage. The cost of the extra output (marginal cost) therefore rises. That is the central link taught in the next lesson: marginal product falls, so marginal cost rises.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC (Form 5) expects the short run and the long run, total, average and marginal product and the law of diminishing returns, with productivity. Cambridge examines the same ideas at A Level only, where the relation between AP and MP and a clear statement of the law are often tested in Paper 3 and Paper 4.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Diminishing returns versus decreasing returns to scale"}
Diminishing returns happen in the short run when one factor is increased and others are fixed. Decreasing returns to scale happen in the long run when all factors are increased together. They are different ideas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying total product falls"}
Diminishing returns mean that *marginal* product falls. Total product still rises until the marginal product becomes negative.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing average with marginal"}
Marginal product is the extra output of one more worker; average product is output divided by all workers. MP is read as a difference in TP, not a division.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2 test calculation of AP and MP from a table and the law of diminishing returns; Paper 3 may ask for an explanation of why costs rise. Cambridge Paper 3 asks multiple-choice questions on the AP and MP relationship, and Paper 4 uses production in data response.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: give the full wording, including "variable factor added to fixed factors" and "eventually". *Calculate*: show TP differences and the division, and give units. *Explain*: link rising MP to specialisation and falling MP to the fixed factor. *Analyse*: carry the chain through to marginal cost.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The short run has at least one fixed factor; in the long run all factors can be changed.
- AP = TP ÷ workers and MP = change in TP ÷ change in workers.
- The law of diminishing marginal returns: adding a variable factor to fixed factors, MP eventually falls.
- MP above AP raises AP; MP below AP lowers it; MP cuts AP at its maximum.
- Productivity is output per unit of input; higher productivity lowers the cost of each unit.
- Falling MP is the reason marginal cost rises in the short run.
:::
