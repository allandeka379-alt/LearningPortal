## Introduction

A baker in Mutare pays rent on the shop whether she bakes 10 loaves or 500. Flour, however, is bought only for the loaves she bakes. When she plans how much to bake and what price to charge, she needs to know three things: what the whole output costs, what each loaf costs on average, and what one more loaf costs to make. These are total, average and marginal costs, and they are the cost side of every profit decision in the rest of this topic.

You should already know the short run (at least one fixed factor) and the law of diminishing marginal returns. Costs here are **economic costs**: they include the opportunity cost of every resource used. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fixed costs"}
Costs that do not change with the level of output in the short run, such as rent, insurance and the salaries of permanent staff. Total fixed cost (TFC) is the same at every output, even zero.
:::

:::definition{term="Variable costs"}
Costs that change with the level of output, such as raw materials, fuel and piece-rate wages. Total variable cost (TVC) is zero when nothing is produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Marginal cost (MC)"}
The addition to total cost of producing one more unit of output.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Cost formulae"}
$\text{TC} = \text{TFC} + \text{TVC}$. $\text{AFC} = \text{TFC} \div Q$. $\text{AVC} = \text{TVC} \div Q$. $\text{ATC} = \text{TC} \div Q = \text{AFC} + \text{AVC}$. $\text{MC} = \dfrac{\Delta \text{TC}}{\Delta Q}$, which equals the change in TVC for the same change in output, since fixed cost does not change.
:::

## A cost schedule

A fictional bakery has total fixed costs of US\$60 a day. The table shows its costs at each level of output (units of bread, in hundreds of loaves).

| Output (units) | TFC (US\$) | TVC (US\$) | TC (US\$) | AFC (US\$) | AVC (US\$) | ATC (US\$) | MC (US\$) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | 60 | 0 | 60 | – | – | – | – |
| 1 | 60 | 40 | 100 | 60.00 | 40.00 | 100.00 | 40.00 |
| 2 | 60 | 70 | 130 | 30.00 | 35.00 | 65.00 | 30.00 |
| 3 | 60 | 90 | 150 | 20.00 | 30.00 | 50.00 | 20.00 |
| 4 | 60 | 112 | 172 | 15.00 | 28.00 | 43.00 | 22.00 |
| 5 | 60 | 141 | 201 | 12.00 | 28.20 | 40.20 | 29.00 |
| 6 | 60 | 182 | 242 | 10.00 | 30.33 | 40.33 | 41.00 |
| 7 | 60 | 238 | 298 | 8.57 | 34.00 | 42.57 | 56.00 |
| 8 | 60 | 320 | 380 | 7.50 | 40.00 | 47.50 | 82.00 |

:::worked-solution{title="Calculating the costs at 5 units"}
1. $\text{TC} = 60 + 141 = 201$.
2. $\text{AFC} = 60 \div 5 = 12.00$, $\text{AVC} = 141 \div 5 = 28.20$, and $\text{ATC} = 201 \div 5 = 40.20$ (check: $12.00 + 28.20 = 40.20$).
3. $\text{MC}$ of the fifth unit $= 201 - 172 = 29$.
:::

## The shapes of the curves

**AFC** falls continuously as output rises, because the same fixed cost is spread over more units. It never reaches zero.

**AVC** first falls and then rises. At low output, specialisation and the better use of equipment raise marginal product, so each unit needs less variable input. After diminishing returns set in, each additional worker or batch of inputs adds less to output, so more variable input is needed per unit, and AVC rises. In the table AVC is lowest at 4 units (US\$28.00).

**ATC** is the sum of AFC and AVC, so it falls steeply at first (both are falling), reaches its lowest point later than AVC, and then rises once the rise in AVC outweighs the fall in AFC. ATC is lowest at 5 units (US\$40.20), where output is higher than at AVC's minimum. The gap between ATC and AVC is AFC, which narrows as output grows.

**MC** falls briefly and then rises, because it is the mirror image of marginal product: when MP is rising, MC is falling, and when diminishing returns appear MC rises. The same wage buys less extra output, so each extra unit costs more.

:::keyfact{title="Why MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points"}
Marginal cost is the cost of the next unit. When it is below the average, it pulls the average down; when it is above the average, it pulls the average up. Therefore the MC curve crosses AVC and ATC from below at the lowest point of each. This is the same arithmetic as average and marginal product.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Locating the minimum points"}
1. AVC is 40, 35, 30, 28, 28.2: it falls while MC (40, 30, 20, 22) is below it, and rises once MC (29 for the fifth unit) is above AVC of the previous unit (28). The minimum is at 4 units.
2. ATC is 100, 65, 50, 43, 40.2, 40.33: MC of the fifth unit (29) is below ATC (43), so ATC falls to 40.2. MC of the sixth unit (41) is above ATC (40.2), so ATC rises to 40.33. The minimum ATC is at 5 units.
3. In this discrete table MC passes AVC between the fourth and fifth units and ATC between the fifth and sixth units, each time from below.
:::

## From product curves to cost curves

The cost curves are the mirror image of the product curves. When marginal product rises, marginal cost falls; at the output where MP is highest, MC is lowest; when diminishing returns begin, MC rises. Likewise, AP is highest where AVC is lowest. If a firm's workers all earn the same wage, then $\text{MC} = \text{wage} \div \text{MP}$. For example, with a wage of US\$40 and an MP of 20 units, the extra unit costs US\$2.

:::example{title="Why a rise in a fixed cost or a variable cost has different effects"}
If the rent on the shop rises, TFC and AFC rise and so does ATC at every output, but MC and AVC are unchanged: the best output (found where MC meets marginal revenue) does not change. If the price of flour rises, TVC, AVC and MC all rise, so the best output falls. Telling the two apart is a common application question.
:::

## Using the cost curves

Cost curves are the basis of the firm's supply decision. A firm compares the revenue from an extra unit with its marginal cost. The ATC curve shows whether the firm earns a profit at its chosen output, and the AVC curve shows whether it should stay open in the short run. Both are developed in the next lessons.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC (Form 5) and Cambridge (A Level) both expect all seven cost concepts, the curves and the link to diminishing returns. ZIMSEC questions often ask for the schedule to be completed; Cambridge multiple-choice items often describe a curve and ask what happens to another when a cost changes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all costs as variable in the short run"}
Fixed costs do not disappear when output is zero in the short run. The firm still pays them, which is why a loss-making firm may continue to operate if it covers its variable costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying MC cuts the averages at the lowest point of MC"}
MC meets AVC and ATC at the lowest points of AVC and ATC, not at the lowest point of MC.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing average and marginal"}
Marginal cost is the cost of one more unit: the change in TC, not TC divided by output.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting fixed costs in MC"}
Fixed costs do not change with output, so they do not enter marginal cost.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks for a cost from a table; Paper 2 gives a cost schedule and asks for AVC, ATC and MC. Paper 3 asks why average cost curves are U-shaped. Cambridge Paper 3 uses calculation and curve questions; Paper 4 uses cost data in a data-response question.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: show the formula and the numbers. *Explain why ATC is U-shaped*: AFC falls while AVC first falls, then diminishing returns raise AVC. *Analyse*: use the cost curves to trace the effect of a rent or wage change. Describe diagrams in words, giving the axes and the point where the curves cross.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- TC = TFC + TVC; ATC = AFC + AVC; MC is the change in TC from one more unit.
- AFC falls throughout as fixed cost is spread; AVC and ATC are U-shaped.
- Diminishing returns explain why AVC, ATC and MC eventually rise.
- MC cuts AVC and ATC from below at their minimum points.
- MC falls when MP rises; MC rises once diminishing returns begin.
- A rise in a fixed cost raises ATC but leaves MC unchanged.
:::
