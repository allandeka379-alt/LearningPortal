## Introduction

A small bakery in Masvingo with one oven cannot easily double its output by hiring more workers, because the oven is fixed. In the long run, however, the owner can build a bigger bakery, buy more ovens and change everything at once. A big industrial bakery may then produce each loaf at a lower cost than the small one, thanks to specialised machines and bulk buying. Yet a very large organisation can also become hard to manage, and its costs per unit may rise again. This lesson explains why average cost can fall and then rise as a firm changes its whole scale of production.

You should already know the short run and the long run, and the U-shaped short-run average cost curve. All figures are illustrative.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Returns to scale"}
The change in output when all factors of production are changed in the same proportion in the long run. Output may rise by a larger proportion (increasing returns), the same proportion (constant returns) or a smaller proportion (decreasing returns) than the inputs.
:::

:::definition{term="Economies of scale"}
The fall in long-run average cost that results when a firm increases the scale of its output.
:::

:::definition{term="Long-run average cost (LRAC)"}
The lowest average cost at which each level of output can be produced when all factors are variable.
:::

:::definition{term="Minimum efficient scale (MES)"}
The lowest level of output at which long-run average cost is at its minimum.
:::

## Returns to scale and costs

Suppose a fictional bakery can expand its scale in steps, each step costing US\$5,000 a month for all inputs (labour, ovens, space) at constant input prices.

| Scale (index) | Total cost (US\$) | Output (units) | LRAC (US\$ per unit) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 5,000 | 100 | 50.00 |
| 2 | 10,000 | 260 | 38.46 |
| 3 | 15,000 | 450 | 33.33 |
| 4 | 20,000 | 620 | 32.26 |
| 5 | 25,000 | 700 | 35.71 |
| 6 | 30,000 | 720 | 41.67 |

:::worked-solution{title="Returns to scale and LRAC"}
1. From scale 1 to scale 2, inputs double (+100%) and output rises from 100 to 260 (+160%). Output rises faster than inputs: **increasing returns to scale**. LRAC falls from 50.00 to 38.46.
2. From scale 4 to scale 5, inputs rise by 25% (20,000 to 25,000) and output rises from 620 to 700 (+12.9%). Output rises more slowly than inputs: **decreasing returns to scale**. LRAC rises from 32.26 to 35.71.
3. Whenever output rises by a larger percentage than total cost, LRAC falls. The minimum LRAC (US\$32.26) is at scale 4, so the minimum efficient scale is 620 units.
:::

![Long-run average cost for the bakery's six scales: LRAC falls from US\$50.00 at 100 units to a minimum of US\$32.26 at 620 units (the minimum efficient scale), then rises to US\$35.71 at 700 and US\$41.67 at 720 units because of diseconomies of scale.](media:diagrams/lrac-bakery-scales.png "Figure: the bakery's long-run average cost falls (economies of scale) to 620 units and rises after (diseconomies)")

When input prices are constant, increasing returns to scale mean falling LRAC, constant returns mean unchanged LRAC, and decreasing returns mean rising LRAC. That link is the reason the three terms are often used together; but do not forget that economies of scale can also come from cheaper inputs, such as discounts on bulk purchases, even when physical returns are constant.

## The shape of the LRAC curve

The LRAC curve is usually drawn as a smooth, U-shaped (or L-shaped) curve that is the envelope of many short-run average cost curves: for each scale of plant there is a short-run ATC curve, and the LRAC curve touches the lowest cost available for each output after the firm has chosen the best scale. It slopes down because of economies of scale, is flat around the minimum efficient scale, and may rise because of diseconomies of scale. Some industries show an L-shape: costs fall and then stay level, because there is no strong force raising costs at large scale.

The position of the minimum efficient scale relative to the size of the market decides how many firms an industry can support. If MES is a small share of the market, many firms can reach the lowest cost; if MES is a large share, only a few can, and the industry tends to be concentrated.

## Internal economies of scale

These arise inside the firm as it grows.

1. **Technical.** Larger firms can use large, specialised machines and production lines, and the cost of a big machine does not rise in proportion to its output (the principle of increased dimensions: doubling the size of a tank more than doubles its volume).
2. **Managerial.** Large firms employ specialist managers (finance, marketing, production), who work more efficiently than one owner doing everything.
3. **Financial.** Large firms are less risky to lenders, can borrow at lower interest rates and can raise funds by issuing shares.
4. **Marketing.** The cost of an advertising campaign or a sales team is spread over more units.
5. **Purchasing (bulk buying).** Large orders give discounts from suppliers.
6. **Risk-bearing.** A firm that makes many products or sells in many markets is less hurt when one fails.

## External economies of scale

These arise outside the firm when the whole industry in an area grows. Examples are a skilled labour force in a town known for an industry, specialist suppliers and repair services that cluster nearby, better transport links and shared research. The cost reduction reaches every firm in the industry, not only the largest. Note that external economies shift the LRAC curve **downwards**, while internal economies are movements **along** it.

## Diseconomies of scale

**Internal diseconomies** arise when a firm grows too large:

1. Communication problems: long chains of command slow decisions and distort messages.
2. Coordination and control: it becomes harder to monitor effort and avoid waste.
3. Poor motivation and industrial relations: workers may feel remote from owners and work less carefully.

**External diseconomies** arise when the whole industry grows in one area: congestion on roads, higher rents and wages as firms compete for land and workers, and pollution that raises costs for all.

:::worked-solution{title="Economies of scale as a percentage"}
In the table above, by what percentage does LRAC fall when the firm expands from scale 1 to scale 3?

1. LRAC at scale 1 $= 50.00$ and at scale 3 $= 33.33$.
2. Fall $= (50.00 - 33.33) \div 50.00 \times 100 = 33.3\%$.
:::

:::example{title="An evaluation paragraph on growth"}
A manager at a fictional mining company argues that the company should double its capacity to cut costs per tonne. Economies of scale (a larger shaft, a larger crushing plant and bulk purchases of fuel) can lower average cost. However, the saving depends on the firm's current position: if output is already near the minimum efficient scale, additional growth will bring few savings, and management problems may even raise costs. The conclusion is that expansion cuts costs only if the firm is below its minimum efficient scale and has the managers to control the larger organisation.
:::

## Why scale matters

Economies of scale give larger firms a cost advantage and are a barrier to entry for small firms, which can lead to oligopoly or natural monopoly. They also lower prices for consumers if the cost saving is passed on. Governments must therefore weigh the benefits of large-scale production against the loss of competition, a theme developed in the lessons on market structures.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards list the same economies and diseconomies of scale. ZIMSEC questions often name the economies and ask for examples in industries such as mining, brewing and retail. Cambridge Paper 3 and Paper 4 also test the LRAC curve, minimum efficient scale and the link between economies of scale and market structure.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing diminishing returns with decreasing returns to scale"}
Diminishing returns apply to one variable factor in the short run. Decreasing returns to scale apply to all factors in the long run.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every cost fall an economy of scale"}
Economies of scale arise from growth in output, not from a new technology, a fall in wages or a better product, which shift the cost curves.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a list without application"}
Naming "technical economies" earns little. Explain how a larger firm lowers cost per unit, and use an example such as bulk buying of flour.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 3 ask for economies and diseconomies of scale and examples; Paper 2 may give a table of output and cost. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 ask for LRAC diagrams described in words, minimum efficient scale and the effect on market structure.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: say how the economy lowers cost per unit. *Distinguish internal from external*: internal come from the firm's own size; external from the size of the industry. *Assess*: say that gains are not automatic and that diseconomies may set in.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Returns to scale compare the change in output with an equal-proportion change in all inputs.
- Economies of scale are falling LRAC; diseconomies are rising LRAC.
- Minimum efficient scale is the lowest output at which LRAC is at its minimum.
- Internal economies: technical, managerial, financial, marketing, purchasing, risk-bearing.
- External economies shift LRAC down for every firm in an industry; external diseconomies shift it up.
- A high MES relative to the market leads to few firms.
:::
