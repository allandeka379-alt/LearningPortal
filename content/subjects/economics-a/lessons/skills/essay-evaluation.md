## Introduction

Most students who know the economics still lose marks in essays, for two reasons. They describe where they should analyse, and they stop at analysis where the question asks them to judge. In A Level Economics the top marks go to answers that apply the right model, develop a chain of cause and effect, weigh up the other side and reach a conclusion that follows from the argument. This lesson explains what each command word asks for, shows how to build analysis and evaluation, and shows how an essay is planned and timed.

You should have studied some economic models already, because essay skills are practised on content. Use this lesson together with the content lessons; the worked examples here use a minimum wage, a rise in the interest rate and a tax on a good that are covered elsewhere.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chain of reasoning"}
A sequence of linked steps showing how one economic change leads to another ("because … this causes … which leads to …"), each step justified.
:::

:::definition{term="Evaluation"}
Weighing up arguments, evidence or policies to reach a supported judgement, by considering strengths and weaknesses, the size and likelihood of effects, the time period and the assumptions made.
:::

:::definition{term="Levels-based marking"}
Marking an extended answer by the quality of the whole response against level descriptors (for example Level 3: developed, two-sided and applied, with a justified conclusion), and then placing the mark within the level.
:::

## Command words

| Command word | What it asks you to do | Typical marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Define / state / identify** | Give a precise meaning or name; no explanation needed | 1-3 |
| **Calculate** | Show the working and the answer with units | 2-4 |
| **Explain** | Give a reason and link it to the economic mechanism (why) | 3-8 |
| **Analyse** | Break the problem down and develop a chain of cause and effect, often with a model or diagram | 4-10 |
| **Assess / discuss** | Weigh arguments for and against, and say which is stronger | 6-15 |
| **Evaluate** | Make a judgement about the importance, size or likelihood of effects and justify it | 6-20 |

Reading the command word first tells you how much of the answer should be **knowledge**, **application**, **analysis** and **evaluation**. A "state" answer that runs for a page wastes time; an "evaluate" answer that only explains loses most of its marks.

## Building a chain of reasoning

Weak analysis is a list ("a higher wage raises costs and so employment falls"). Strong analysis develops each link:

1. **Point**: a higher minimum wage raises the cost of labour to employers.
2. **Mechanism**: with demand for labour downward sloping (MRP), the quantity of labour demanded falls along the curve.
3. **Link to the data or context**: in a labour-intensive industry such as farming, where the wage is a large share of costs, the fall will be larger.
4. **Consequence**: unemployment rises, but those who keep jobs earn more.
5. **So what**: the effect on workers as a group depends on the elasticity of demand for labour.

Every link answers "why?" and uses economic terms precisely. A number or a short reference to the case makes the answer applied.

## Evaluation: ways to weigh an argument

| Question to ask | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Short run or long run?** | A fall in the exchange rate may raise export demand only after contracts are renegotiated |
| **How big?** | A tax of 2% on a good with inelastic demand reduces the quantity only slightly |
| **Elasticity** | The effect of a price change depends on PED, YED, PES or labour demand elasticity |
| **Assumptions** | The model assumes ceteris paribus and perfect information; are they realistic? |
| **Context and evidence** | The effect in a small open economy may differ from a large one |
| **Who gains and loses?** | Winners and losers (consumers, firms, government, the poor) may differ |
| **Priorities and value judgements** | Efficiency or equity? Growth or the environment? |
| **Alternatives** | Is another policy cheaper or less damaging? |

An evaluative point has a claim, a reason and a consequence for the conclusion ("this matters because …, so the policy is likely to be …"). Saying "it depends" is not evaluation unless you say **on what** and **how**.

## Planning and structure

A reliable structure for an essay (ZIMSEC Paper 3, Cambridge Paper 2 Sections B and C, Cambridge Paper 4 Sections B and C):

1. **Introduction**: define the key term(s) and state, in a sentence, how you will answer. Do not copy the question.
2. **Analysis paragraphs**: two or three, each with a point, a developed chain and, where useful, a diagram described in words or a number.
3. **Evaluation**: a paragraph (or a sentence in each analysis paragraph) weighing the other side and the factors above.
4. **Conclusion**: a short, direct judgement that answers the question and says why, not a repeat of the introduction.

:::worked-solution{title="Timing an essay"}
Marks give the clearest guide to time. ZIMSEC Paper 3 is 180 minutes for 100 marks, so about 180 ÷ 100 = **1.8 minutes a mark**; each 25-mark essay deserves about 25 × 1.8 = **45 minutes**, including a few minutes of planning. Cambridge Papers 2 and 4 are 120 minutes for 60 marks, so **2 minutes a mark**; a 20-mark essay deserves **40 minutes** and a 12-mark part (b) about 24 minutes.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Planning a 20-mark essay question in five minutes"}
Question: "A government raises the national minimum wage. Discuss the likely effects." A plan might be: (1) define the minimum wage; (2) analysis one: competitive market, wage above equilibrium, quantity demanded falls, unemployment; (3) analysis two: monopsony, a moderate minimum raises employment; (4) analysis three: effects on firms' prices and the informal sector; (5) evaluation: elasticity of labour demand, size of the minimum, enforcement, short and long run; (6) conclusion: a judgement on when it helps or harms.
:::

## A model evaluation paragraph

:::example{title="Evaluating a minimum wage"}
The effect of a minimum wage depends first on how far it is above the equilibrium wage: a small rise has little effect on employment, but a large one risks unemployment, especially among young and unskilled workers. It also depends on the elasticity of demand for labour. If demand for labour is inelastic, the fall in employment is small and the total pay of workers rises. In a labour market with a dominant employer, a minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can raise employment. Over time firms may substitute machines for workers or raise prices, which weakens the gain. On balance, a **moderate** minimum wage is more likely to help low-paid workers than harm them, but a high one, set without knowledge of local labour markets, is likely to reduce employment among the very workers it is meant to help.
:::

This paragraph makes a claim, gives a reason, uses the model, mentions time and elasticity and ends in a judgement that is qualified, not vague.

## Levels-based marking

Extended responses are marked by level. The typical pattern (our summary; the boards' own wording differs) is:

| Level | What the answer shows |
| --- | --- |
| **Level 3** (top) | Developed, two-sided analysis applied to the question, with evaluation and a justified judgement that answers the question |
| **Level 2** | Some analysis and application; evaluation present but limited, or a judgement that is not supported |
| **Level 1** | Simple, undeveloped points, little application or evaluation |

Within a level the mark depends on how fully the descriptors are met. In a 12-mark question, for example, Level 3 may be 9-12 marks, Level 2 5-8 and Level 1 1-4. Knowing the levels helps you to see that a very accurate description of a model still earns a low level if there is no development or judgement.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test the same skills. ZIMSEC Paper 3 asks for four essays from twelve, typically an explanation part and a discussion or evaluation part; Cambridge Papers 2 and 4 require essays in two sections, with Paper 2 essays in two parts and Paper 4 essays unstructured. ZIMSEC data response (Paper 2) rewards application to the data; Cambridge data response also rewards evaluation in the last part.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing instead of analysing"}
A list of effects with no "because" earns Level 1. Develop each link in the chain.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation or an unsupported conclusion"}
Giving only the benefits (or only the costs) of a policy, or ending with "overall it depends", does not reach Level 3. Weigh both sides and justify a judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wasting time on the introduction or on a long model description"}
Define only what the question needs, then go straight to the analysis. Spread your time by marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 3 (essays) and the last parts of Paper 2 data questions; Cambridge Paper 2 Sections B and C, the final parts of the Paper 2 and Paper 4 data questions and the Paper 4 essays. Multiple-choice papers do not test essay technique, but they test precise command-word skills such as reading data.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain*: reason and mechanism. *Analyse*: developed chain. *Assess* and *discuss*: both sides and a view. *Evaluate*: a judgement that is justified by size, time, elasticity or context. Always end an assess, discuss or evaluate answer with a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read the command word first; it tells you how much knowledge, analysis and evaluation to give.
- Analysis is a developed chain of cause and effect with a justified link at every step.
- Evaluation weighs the argument by time, size, elasticity, assumptions, context, who gains and loses, and priorities.
- Plan, structure (introduction, analysis, evaluation, conclusion) and spend time in proportion to marks.
- Levels-based marking rewards development, application and a justified judgement, not length.
:::
