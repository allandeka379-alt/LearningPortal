## Introduction

Examiners in economics reward two things that are not found in the textbook definitions: the ability to use a diagram to *show* an argument and the ability to turn a table of numbers into evidence. A candidate who writes "supply increases so the price falls" earns less than one who shows the supply curve shifting to the right, the new equilibrium and the figures that quantify the change. This lesson covers the skills you need across both ZIMSEC (Papers 1 to 3, with data response in Paper 2) and Cambridge (Section A of Papers 2 and 4, as well as essays).

You should know the basic demand and supply model and how to calculate a percentage. The lesson on essay evaluation (a separate lesson) deals with extended writing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Percentage change"}
The change in a value as a percentage of the original value: (new value − original value) ÷ original value × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Percentage point"}
The unit used to describe the difference between two percentages. A rise in the unemployment rate from 8% to 10% is a rise of 2 percentage points, but a 25% rise in the rate.
:::

:::definition{term="Index number"}
A figure that expresses a value as a percentage of its value in a base year, which is set at 100. An index of 132 means a value 32% above the base.
:::

## Diagrams in economics answers

Every diagram, whether drawn in an examination or described in words, needs the same parts.

1. **Labelled axes with units**: price (US\$) on the vertical axis and quantity (units per week) on the horizontal axis, or, for the macroeconomy, the price level against real output.
2. **Labelled curves**: D for demand, S for supply, with a subscript for each position (D1, D2).
3. **Equilibrium marked** with the price and quantity read to their axes (P1, Q1).
4. **Shifts shown with arrows** and a new label, and the new equilibrium (P2, Q2).
5. **Annotation**: areas such as tax revenue, consumer surplus or deadweight loss shaded and labelled.

Where you cannot draw, or a question asks you to *describe* a diagram, state the same things in words, using the vocabulary precisely: a **movement along** a curve is caused by a change in the price of the good; a **shift** of a curve is caused by a change in a condition such as income, tastes or costs; a curve shifts **to the right** when it increases and **to the left** when it decreases.

:::example{title="Describing a diagram in words"}
"A downward-sloping demand curve D1 and an upward-sloping supply curve S1 cross at the equilibrium price P1 and quantity Q1. An indirect tax of US\$4 per unit shifts the supply curve upward (to the left) from S1 to S2 by a vertical distance equal to the tax. The new curve crosses D1 at a higher price P2 and a lower quantity Q2. The tax paid by consumers is the area from P1 to P2 up to Q2; the tax paid by producers is the area between P1 and the price they receive, up to Q2."
:::

A diagram must be *used*. Refer to it in the text ("As shown in Figure 1, the shift of supply raises the price from P1 to P2") and explain what causes the shift. A diagram that is drawn but not explained gains little credit, and a diagram that contradicts the text loses marks.

## Reading tables and graphs

Follow a routine with every table or graph.

1. **Read the title, headings and units.** Is it US\$ million or US\$ billion? Is it a percentage, an index or an absolute figure? Is it real or nominal?
2. **Note the time period** and the intervals.
3. **Look for trends and turning points**, the biggest and smallest values and any sudden changes.
4. **Compare like with like**: percentage changes against percentage changes; real with real.
5. **Quote figures** with units in your answer and, where relevant, calculate a change or a share.
6. **Be careful with inference**: a table shows what happened, not necessarily why. Say "the data suggests", and name other factors that might explain it.

## Calculations

:::worked-solution{title="Percentage change and index numbers"}
A fictional firm's sales in US\$ thousand are 250 in 2022, 275 in 2023 and 330 in 2024.

1. Percentage change 2022 to 2023 $= (275 - 250) \div 250 \times 100 = +10\%$.
2. Percentage change 2023 to 2024 $= (330 - 275) \div 275 \times 100 = +20\%$.
3. Index with 2022 = 100: 2022 is 100; 2023 is $275 \div 250 \times 100 = 110$; 2024 is $330 \div 250 \times 100 = 132$.
4. The increase from 2022 to 2024 is 32%, **not** 10% + 20% = 30%. Percentage changes cannot be added across years because each has a different base.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Percentage points against percentage change"}
The unemployment rate in a fictional country rises from 8% to 10%.

1. The change is $10 - 8 = 2$ **percentage points**.
2. The percentage change in the rate is $(10 - 8) \div 8 \times 100 = 25\%$.
3. Use percentage points for differences between rates; use percentage change for the change in a rate treated as a number. Say which you mean.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Real and nominal values"}
Nominal sales in 2024 are US\$330 thousand and the price index (2022 = 100) is 120.

1. Real value in 2022 prices $= 330 \div 120 \times 100 = 275$ thousand.
2. Real sales in 2024 are therefore 275 compared with 250 in 2022: a rise of 10%, not the 32% shown by the nominal figures. Inflation accounts for the difference.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Shares of a total"}
A fictional country's exports are worth US\$200 million, of which tobacco is US\$50 million.

1. Share of tobacco $= 50 \div 200 \times 100 = 25\%$.
2. In a pie chart the angle is $25\% \times 360° = 90°$.
3. Use the share to comment on dependence: one product supplying a quarter of export earnings leaves the country exposed to a fall in its price.
:::

### Units, accuracy and presentation

1. Write the **unit** with every answer (US\$, %, percentage points, units, index points).
2. Follow the **rounding instructions** in the question; if none, give a sensible precision and do not round intermediate steps.
3. Show **working**. A wrong final answer can still earn most of the marks for the method, and a correct answer with no working may earn fewer in a multi-mark question.
4. Show the **sign** where it matters (a fall is negative) and say if the answer is a rise or fall.
5. Check that the answer is **reasonable**: a percentage change of 2,000% in a price after a small tax suggests a slip.

## Data-response questions

A data-response question gives a passage and one or more tables or graphs, followed by parts that become progressively demanding. ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Section A both follow this pattern. A typical sequence is:

| Part | Command word | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early | State, identify, define | Take the information from the source or give a definition; be brief |
| Middle | Calculate | Show the formula, substitute data, give the answer with units |
| Middle | Explain, analyse | Give a reason and a developed chain of cause and effect, using the data |
| Final | Discuss, assess, evaluate | Weigh two sides, use the evidence and reach a conclusion |

Techniques that gain marks:

1. **Use the source.** Quote or paraphrase figures and phrases; answers that could be written without reading the extract score poorly.
2. **Match the depth to the marks.** A 2-mark part needs two points; a 6-mark part needs developed analysis and a conclusion.
3. **Apply theory to the context** (the country, the product, the period) instead of reciting general theory.
4. **Use the command word.** *Define* is not *explain*; *evaluate* needs a judgement.
5. **Manage time.** A 20-mark question gives roughly one mark per minute plus reading time.

:::example{title="A short extract answered well"}
Extract: "The price of a bag of maize meal in Zandia rose from US\$8 to US\$10 after a drought. Sales fell from 400 to 380 bags a day." Question (4 marks): *Calculate PED and explain what the value shows.* The answer: price change $= +25\%$; quantity change $= -5\%$; PED $= -5 \div 25 = -0.2$. Demand is inelastic because maize meal is a staple with few substitutes in the short run. The calculation earns the first two marks and the interpretation, linked to the context, the others.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards use data-response questions and expect diagrams. ZIMSEC marks calculations and knowledge heavily in the early parts of Paper 2, with a discussion at the end; Cambridge Section A has about six parts ending with an evaluative question worth about six marks. Practise with both formats.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding percentage changes across years"}
10% followed by 20% is a rise of 32%, not 30%. Chain by multiplying (1.10 × 1.20 = 1.32).
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing percentage points with percentages"}
A fall in inflation from 10% to 8% is 2 percentage points (a 20% fall in the rate).
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing without labelling or explaining"}
Missing axis labels, units or curve names cost marks, and an unexplained diagram does not score as analysis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Copying the data without interpreting it"}
Listing figures is not analysis. Say what they show, calculate a change and link it to theory.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Using this skill across papers"}
Practise calculations until they are routine, since they are the quickest marks in data-response and multiple choice. Keep a standard list of diagram parts and check each one. In the final part of a data-response question, use at least one figure from the source to support a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A good diagram has labelled axes with units, labelled curves, marked equilibria, arrows for shifts and clear annotation, and is explained in the text.
- Read titles, units, periods and base years before using any table or graph; quote figures with units.
- Percentage change uses the original value; percentage points describe differences between rates; changes across years combine by multiplication.
- An index sets the base year at 100; real values divide the nominal value by the price index and multiply by 100.
- In data-response answers, use the source, match depth to marks, follow the command word and show working.
:::
