## Introduction

Why do prices rise? One of the oldest answers in economics is that prices rise because there is too much money chasing too few goods. The **quantity theory of money** turns this idea into an equation and a policy: if inflation is caused by the money supply, then controlling the growth of the money supply controls inflation. Zimbabwe's experience of extremely rapid inflation in 2007 and 2008, which coincided with extremely rapid growth of the money supply, is often discussed in this context. This lesson explains the theory, its calculations and its limits.

You should know the money supply and the meaning of inflation, and be able to work with percentage changes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Equation of exchange"}
The identity MV = PT, where M is the money supply, V is the velocity of circulation (the average number of times a unit of money is spent in a period), P is the average price level and T is the volume of transactions (the quantity of goods and services traded) in the period. Total spending (MV) is always equal to the total value of transactions (PT).
:::

:::definition{term="Velocity of circulation"}
The average number of times each unit of money is used to make a payment in a given period: V = PT ÷ M.
:::

:::definition{term="Quantity theory of money"}
The theory that, if the velocity of circulation (V) and the volume of transactions (T) are constant, a change in the money supply causes a proportional change in the price level.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Formulae"}
MV = PT, so P = MV ÷ T, M = PT ÷ V and V = PT ÷ M.

In percentage changes: %ΔM + %ΔV ≈ %ΔP + %ΔT. If V and T are constant, %ΔP ≈ %ΔM.

PT is close in meaning to nominal GDP (the value of output at current prices), so V = nominal GDP ÷ M is often used as the velocity of circulation.
:::

## Using the equation

:::worked-solution{title="Finding the price level"}
The money supply is US\$300 million, each dollar is spent 4 times a year and there are 600 million transactions.
1. MV = 300 × 4 = 1,200.
2. P = MV ÷ T = 1,200 ÷ 600 = **2.0** (the average price of a transaction, in US\$).
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Finding the velocity"}
The money supply is US\$240 million, the average price is US\$1.50 and the volume of transactions is 800 million.
1. PT = 1.5 × 800 = 1,200.
2. V = PT ÷ M = 1,200 ÷ 240 = **5 times a year**.
:::

## The quantity theory and inflation

The classical and monetarist argument depends on two assumptions:

1. **V is constant (or changes predictably) in the short run**, because it depends on habits and the way payments are made.
2. **T is constant at the full-employment level** in the long run, because output is determined by the real factors of production (labour, capital, technology), not by the amount of money.

If both are true, the equation shows that P changes in proportion to M. Doubling the money supply doubles prices. Money is "neutral" in the long run: it affects the price level but not real output.

|  | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Money supply, M (US\$ million) | 200 | 240 |
| Velocity of circulation, V (times a year) | 4 | 4 |
| Volume of transactions, T (million) | 400 | 416 |
| Price level, P = MV ÷ T | 2.00 | 2.31 |

:::worked-solution{title="Effect of a rise in the money supply"}
Using the table, M rises by 20% (200 to 240), V is unchanged at 4 and T rises by 4% (400 to 416).
1. Year 1: P = 200 × 4 ÷ 400 = **2.00**.
2. Year 2: P = 240 × 4 ÷ 416 = **2.31** (to two decimal places).
3. Rise in the price level = (2.3077 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = **15.4%**.
4. Approximation: %ΔP ≈ %ΔM + %ΔV − %ΔT = 20 + 0 − 4 = 16%. The two answers are close; the exact answer uses the full equation.
5. Faster growth of the money supply than of output causes inflation.
:::

## The monetarist conclusions

Monetarists, whose best-known leader was Milton Friedman, argued that:

- Inflation is caused in the long run by growth in the money supply that is faster than growth of real output.
- The central bank can control inflation by controlling the growth rate of the money supply.
- Governments cause inflation when they finance deficits by creating money.
- Expansionary policy to reduce unemployment has only short-run effects on output.

In hyperinflation, the link between rapid money growth and rising prices is clear. When governments cover their spending by printing money, the money supply and the price level may rise together by very large amounts.

## Criticisms

1. **Velocity is not constant.** It changes with interest rates, expectations, technology (mobile money) and confidence. In a recession or in hyperinflation velocity changes sharply, so the link from M to P is not stable.
2. **T is not fixed.** When there is spare capacity, a rise in the money supply can raise output and employment rather than prices (the Keynesian view).
3. **Causation may run the other way.** If prices rise because of higher costs, the central bank may increase the money supply to accommodate it, so money follows prices.
4. **Money is difficult to measure**, especially with new payment methods and foreign currencies.
5. **Inflation also has non-monetary causes**, such as cost-push shocks, which the theory does not explain.

:::example{title="Evaluation paragraph"}
The quantity theory provides a clear explanation of high inflation and shows why governments should not finance deficits by printing money. It is most convincing over the long run and in cases of very rapid money growth. However, its assumptions of a constant velocity and a fixed volume of transactions are unrealistic in the short run, and in a recession a rise in the money supply may raise output with little effect on prices. Cost-push inflation is not explained by the theory. The theory is therefore useful as a long-run guide but should not be used on its own to explain short-run inflation.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect MV = PT, the assumptions, calculations and the monetarist conclusion that inflation is caused by money supply growth. ZIMSEC (Form 6) emphasises the link between the money supply and the price level; Cambridge (A Level) expects the assumptions to be criticised and the theory to be applied to policy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the equation without the assumptions"}
MV = PT is an identity and is always true. The *theory* adds that V and T are constant, so that M determines P. Do not say that the equation by itself proves the cause of inflation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up M, V, P and T"}
M is the money supply, V is velocity, P is the price level, T is the number of transactions. Do not treat T as time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming that prices rise by exactly the percentage rise in M"}
This is true only when V and T are constant. Where T rises, prices rise by less than M; use the full equation.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 1 test calculations with MV = PT, and Paper 3 essays ask whether inflation is caused by the money supply. Cambridge Paper 3 asks for calculations and a discussion of the assumptions, and Paper 4 essays ask whether the money supply can control inflation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Calculate*: write the equation, rearrange to find the missing term and give units. *Explain*: link the money supply to prices through spending. *Evaluate*: set the long-run evidence against the unrealistic assumptions of constant V and T, and add cost-push and Keynesian views.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The equation of exchange MV = PT is an identity; the quantity theory adds that V and T are constant, so changes in M cause proportional changes in P.
- Calculations: P = MV ÷ T, M = PT ÷ V, V = PT ÷ M; in percentages %ΔP ≈ %ΔM + %ΔV − %ΔT.
- Monetarists conclude that inflation is a monetary phenomenon and that controlling money supply growth controls inflation.
- Criticisms: velocity and transactions are not constant, causation may be reversed, cost-push inflation and spare capacity.
- The theory is a useful long-run guide, especially for hyperinflation, but not a complete explanation.
:::
