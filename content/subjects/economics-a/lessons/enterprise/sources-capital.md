## Introduction

Starting a poultry project in Gweru, opening a hardware shop in Mutare or buying a second minibus all need money before any sales are made. A business needs finance for **start-up costs** (equipment, premises, licences), **working capital** (stock and wages until customers pay) and **expansion**. This lesson, set for ZIMSEC Form 5, compares the main sources and shows how to pick the one that fits a particular business.

The word *capital* here means finance, which is different from the capital goods you met in the lesson on factors of production. You should know what an entrepreneur does. How banks create credit is a later topic in money and banking.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Start-up capital and working capital"}
Start-up capital is the finance needed to set up a business. Working capital is the finance needed to pay day-to-day costs, such as materials and wages, while waiting for customers to pay.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal and external finance"}
Internal finance comes from within the business or its owners (savings, retained profit). External finance comes from outside (loans, shares, grants, trade credit).
:::

:::definition{term="Short-term and long-term finance"}
Short-term finance is repaid within about a year (trade credit, overdraft). Long-term finance is repaid over many years or never repaid (shares, long loans).
:::

## The main sources

| Source | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal savings | Owner invests own money | No interest; full control | Limited amount; owner bears all risk |
| Retained profit | Profit kept in the business instead of paid out | No interest; no outside control | Only available once profitable; owners get less income |
| Bank loan | Fixed sum borrowed and repaid with interest | Large amounts; owner keeps control | Interest must be paid; collateral needed; risk of losing assets |
| Microfinance loan | Small loans from microfinance institutions, often to small traders | Easier to obtain for small firms; no large collateral | High interest rates; small amounts |
| Overdraft | Bank allows account to go below zero | Flexible; interest only on amount used | Expensive; can be withdrawn |
| Shares (equity) | Sold to investors in exchange for part ownership | No repayment or interest; large sums | Ownership and control shared; dividends are paid from profit |
| Leasing | Business rents equipment for regular payments | No big purchase; maintenance often included | Never owns the asset; total cost may be higher |
| Hire purchase | Pay a deposit then instalments; ownership passes at the end | Spread cost; use asset at once | Total cost higher than cash price |
| Trade credit | Supplier allows payment after delivery | Free for a short time | Short-term; may lose discounts or supply if late |
| Grants | Money given by government or agencies for specified purposes | Not repaid | Competitive; conditions; time to apply |

## Calculations: comparing the cost of finance

:::worked-solution{title="Cost of a loan with simple interest"}
A fictional trader in Masvingo borrows US\$10,000 for 3 years at 12% a year simple interest and repays equally each year.

1. Interest each year: $10000 \times 0.12 = 1{,}200$.
2. Total interest over 3 years: $1200 \times 3 = 3{,}600$.
3. Total repaid: $10000 + 3600 = 13{,}600$.
4. Yearly repayment: $13600 \div 3 = 4{,}533.33$, so about US\$4,533 each year (rounded).
5. The extra cost of borrowing is 3,600, or $3600 \div 10000 \times 100 = 36\%$ of the amount borrowed.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Hire purchase against paying cash"}
A fictional bakery can buy an oven for a cash price of US\$1,200 or on hire purchase with a 20% deposit and 12 monthly instalments of US\$95.

1. Deposit: $1200 \times 0.20 = 240$.
2. Instalments: $12 \times 95 = 1{,}140$.
3. Total hire purchase price: $240 + 1140 = 1{,}380$.
4. Extra cost over cash: $1380 - 1200 = 180$, which is $180 \div 1200 \times 100 = 15\%$ of the cash price.
5. The bakery pays US\$180 more, but it does not need US\$1,200 at once and gets the oven immediately.
:::

:::example{title="Share issue"}
A fictional company needs US\$50,000 and issues 25,000 shares at US\$2 each. It raises $25000 \times 2$ = US\$50,000. Buyers become part-owners. If the company later pays a dividend of US\$0.10 per share, it pays $25000 \times 0.10$ = US\$2,500 in dividends. Existing owners now share control with the new shareholders.
:::

## How to obtain finance

1. **Prepare a business plan** that explains the product, the market, costs, expected sales and profit, and what finance is needed and for how long.
2. **Identify suitable sources** and compare costs and conditions.
3. **Offer security (collateral)** where required: property or equipment that the lender can claim if the loan is not repaid.
4. **Apply** to the bank, microfinance institution or other source, with identity documents, business registration, and financial records.
5. **Negotiate and sign** an agreement stating the amount, interest, repayment dates and penalties.

Lenders judge the risk of non-payment, so a clear plan, a record of savings and some collateral improve the chance of success.

## Choosing a source

Four questions help to decide.

- **Cost:** what interest, dividends or fees must be paid?
- **Risk:** what happens if the business cannot pay? Loans must be repaid whatever profit is made; shares need no repayment.
- **Control:** does the source give outsiders a say in the business?
- **Term:** is the money needed for a short time (stock, wages) or a long time (a new factory)? A rule: match the term of finance to the term of the need. Long-term assets need long-term finance, short-term needs short-term finance.

For example, a street trader buying stock for a week suits trade credit or personal savings; a new maize mill expected to last 15 years suits a long-term loan or share capital.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing capital in this lesson with capital goods"}
Here capital means finance (money). Capital goods are machines and buildings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying shares must be repaid"}
Share capital is not repaid in the ordinary course; investors may receive dividends if there is profit and sell their shares to others.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying leasing and hire purchase are the same"}
With leasing the business never becomes the owner. With hire purchase ownership passes after the last payment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending a source without reasons"}
A recommendation must be linked to the business: its size, risk, need and ability to offer collateral.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
This is a ZIMSEC-only topic, tested in Paper 1 (identify the source), Paper 2 (calculate interest or compare costs) and in essays on small businesses. Cambridge 9708 does not examine it as a separate topic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State/identify*: name the source. *Explain*: give an advantage or disadvantage linked to the business. *Compare*: strengths and weaknesses of both. *Advise/recommend*: choose a source, give reasons using cost, risk, control and term, and note what you reject.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Businesses need finance for start-up, working capital and expansion.
- Internal sources (savings, retained profit) cost no interest; external sources include loans, shares, leasing, hire purchase, trade credit and grants.
- Loans and hire purchase must be repaid with interest; shares give investors ownership and a share of profit.
- Choose a source by cost, risk, control and term, matching the term of finance to the need.
- A clear business plan, a record of savings and collateral help a business obtain a loan.
:::
