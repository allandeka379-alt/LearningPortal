## Introduction

A hairdresser who works alone at a stall in Bulawayo, a group of farmers who sell their tomatoes together, a company that owns a chain of supermarkets and a state-owned electricity supplier are all businesses, but they are organised in different ways. The form chosen decides who owns the business, who makes decisions, how much of their own wealth the owners can lose and how easily the business can raise money. Once it exists, a business also has to decide how to behave towards its workers, customers, community and environment.

This ZIMSEC (Form 5) lesson follows the lesson on sources of finance and needs the idea of risk and the entrepreneur. Growth by merger is a later topic.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Limited liability"}
The owners of a company can lose only the amount they invested in it. Their personal property cannot be claimed to pay the company's debts.
:::

:::definition{term="Unlimited liability"}
The owners are personally responsible for all the debts of the business, so their personal assets can be used to pay them.
:::

:::definition{term="Business ethics"}
The moral principles that guide how a business behaves towards its workers, customers, suppliers, the community and the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Unhu / Ubuntu / Vumunhu"}
An African philosophy of humanity, expressed in the idea that a person is a person through other people. In business it means honesty, respect, fairness, care for the community and sharing.
:::

## Forms of business organisation

| Form | Ownership | Liability | Control | Finance | Continuity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sole trader | One person | Unlimited | The owner decides alone | Own savings, retained profit, loans | Ends with the owner (death or retirement) |
| Partnership | Two or more people | Unlimited (jointly) | Partners share decisions | Partners' contributions and loans | May end if a partner leaves, unless a new agreement is made |
| Cooperative | Members (producers or consumers) | Usually limited | One member, one vote | Members' contributions, loans, retained surplus | Continues as members change |
| Private limited company | Shareholders (shares are not offered to the public) | Limited | Directors elected by shareholders | Shares sold privately, loans | Separate legal person; continues |
| Public limited company | Shareholders (shares can be bought by the public, often through a stock exchange) | Limited | Directors accountable to many shareholders | Large share issues, loans | Separate legal person; continues |
| State-owned enterprise | The government | The state | Government-appointed board | Government funds, loans, revenue | Continues until the state decides otherwise |

A **separate legal personality** means a company can own assets, sue and be sued in its own name.

### Advantages and disadvantages

- **Sole trader:** simple to start, keeps all profit, quick decisions, personal service. But unlimited liability, limited finance, long hours and no continuity.
- **Partnership:** more finance, shared skills and workload. But unlimited liability (a partner can be responsible for another's debts), disagreements, profits shared.
- **Cooperative:** members share benefits, bargaining power with suppliers or buyers, democratic control. But small capital, decision-making may be slow, and members may lack management skills.
- **Private limited company:** limited liability, continuity, easier to raise finance than a small firm. But legal requirements and costs of registration, share transfer restrictions, and profits shared with shareholders.
- **Public limited company:** can raise very large sums of money by selling shares to the public and has strong credibility. But strict disclosure rules, high costs, risk of takeover and a separation between owners and managers.
- **State-owned enterprise:** can provide essential services where private firms would not and pursue social goals. But may be inefficient, depend on subsidies and be affected by political pressures.

## Calculations

:::worked-solution{title="Sharing partnership profit"}
Three partners in a fictional garage, Tendai, Rudo and Farai, invested US\$20,000, US\$10,000 and US\$10,000. They agreed to share profit in the ratio of their investment. The profit for the year is US\$36,000.

1. Total invested: $20000 + 10000 + 10000 = 40{,}000$.
2. Ratio: $20 : 10 : 10 = 2 : 1 : 1$ (4 parts).
3. One part: $36000 \div 4 = 9{,}000$.
4. Tendai: $2 \times 9000 = 18{,}000$. Rudo: $9{,}000$. Farai: $9{,}000$.
5. Check: $18000 + 9000 + 9000 = 36{,}000$.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Limited and unlimited liability"}
A fictional shop fails with debts of US\$50,000. The business's assets are worth US\$30,000, so US\$20,000 of debts remain.

1. **Sole trader:** the owner has unlimited liability. The remaining debts of US\$20,000 can be paid from personal property such as a car or a house.
2. **Private limited company with shareholders who invested US\$15,000 each:** shareholders have limited liability. They lose their US\$15,000 each, but creditors cannot claim their personal property for the remaining US\$20,000. Creditors bear the loss.
3. Limited liability reduces the risk to investors, so more people are willing to invest, but lenders may then ask for security from the owners.
:::

## Choosing a form

Matching the form to the situation means considering the size of the business, finance needed, the risk, how much control the owners want and the legal requirements.

- A small tailoring service run by one person with little capital: **sole trader**.
- Two doctors starting a clinic and sharing equipment: **partnership** (or a private company if the risk is high).
- Smallholder farmers needing a better price and a shared tractor: **cooperative**.
- A family firm that needs limited liability but wants to keep ownership private: **private limited company**.
- A mining business needing very large capital: **public limited company**.
- Services considered essential, such as national electricity or railways, may be organised as **state-owned enterprises**.

## Business ethics

Businesses are expected to do more than make a profit.

- **Unhu/Ubuntu/Vumunhu:** treat workers, customers and neighbours with respect, honesty and fairness; share benefits with the community; do not cheat or exploit people.
- **Accountability:** being answerable for decisions and actions to owners, workers, customers and the state, for example by keeping honest accounts and paying taxes.
- **Social responsibility:** taking account of the effect of business activities on society, for example paying fair wages, providing safe working conditions, supporting local schools and clinics, and not selling harmful products.
- **Environmental consciousness:** reducing pollution and waste, using resources sustainably (for example replanting trees used for tobacco curing) and obeying environmental laws.

:::example{title="Costs and benefits of ethical behaviour"}
A fictional mine in Hwange installs equipment that cleans the water before it returns to a river, at a cost of US\$40,000 a year.

1. Costs: lower profits in the short run, perhaps higher prices.
2. Benefits: healthier communities and farms downstream, a better reputation, fewer fines and legal disputes, and more willing workers and customers.
3. Judgement: many firms find that ethical behaviour protects long-term profit, though some costs fall on the firm first. Where laws are weak, ethics matter even more.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a partnership has limited liability"}
In an ordinary partnership liability is unlimited. Limited liability belongs to companies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing private limited and public limited"}
Shares in a public limited company can be bought by the public, often on a stock exchange; shares in a private limited company cannot.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a cooperative as a partnership"}
A cooperative is owned and controlled by its members on a democratic basis, usually one member, one vote.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming ethics always cost profit"}
Some ethical behaviour reduces profit in the short run but protects a firm's reputation and survival in the long run.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
This is a ZIMSEC-only topic: Paper 1 asks for features and differences; Paper 2 may use a case study to recommend a form; essays ask about the advantages of limited liability or social responsibility. Cambridge 9708 does not examine it as a topic.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State*: name the form or ethic. *Distinguish*: define both with a contrast. *Advise*: choose a form for a setting, with reasons on liability, finance, control and continuity, and mention what you reject. *Discuss social responsibility*: costs, benefits and a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Business forms differ in ownership, liability, control, finance and continuity.
- Sole traders and partners have unlimited liability; shareholders in companies have limited liability.
- Private companies sell shares privately; public companies sell shares to the public; cooperatives are member-owned with one vote each.
- State-owned enterprises are owned by the government and often provide essential services.
- Ethics include Unhu/Ubuntu, accountability, social responsibility and care for the environment.
:::
