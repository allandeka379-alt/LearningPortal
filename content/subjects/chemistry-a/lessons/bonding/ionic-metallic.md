## Introduction

Why is table salt a brittle, high-melting crystal while copper wire bends without breaking, and why does one conduct electricity only when molten while the other conducts as a solid? The answers lie in how the electrons are held: transferred completely, shared, or spread through a whole lattice.

Zimbabwe's mineral wealth is a good place to start. Chromite from the Great Dyke is an ionic oxide; the chromium extracted from it is a metal. Copper, nickel and the platinum group metals mined in the country are valued precisely for the properties that metallic bonding gives them: conductivity, ductility and strength.

From O-Level you know that metals and non-metals form ions, and that metals contain "a sea of electrons". This lesson adds the idea that decides which kind of bond forms, **electronegativity**, and sharpens the descriptions to A-Level standard.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Electronegativity"}
The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself.
:::

:::definition{term="Ionic bond"}
The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
:::

:::definition{term="Metallic bond"}
The electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a sea of delocalised electrons.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Pauling electronegativities (Cambridge data booklet values)"}
| H | C | N | O | F | Na | Mg | Al | Si | S | Cl | K | Ca |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 0.9 | 1.3 | 1.5 | 1.9 | 2.6 | 3.0 | 0.8 | 1.0 |

Fluorine is the most electronegative element. ZIMSEC questions give the values you need.
:::

## What controls electronegativity

Three factors decide how strongly an atom attracts a bonding pair:

1. **Nuclear charge**: more protons attract the bonding electrons more strongly.
2. **Atomic radius**: the closer the bonding pair is to the nucleus, the stronger the attraction.
3. **Shielding**: more inner shells between the nucleus and the bonding pair weaken the attraction.

**Across a period** electronegativity **increases** (Na 0.9 to Cl 3.0): nuclear charge rises, the radius falls and shielding is about the same.

**Down a group** electronegativity **decreases** (F 4.0, Cl 3.0): the atoms are larger and have more shells of shielding, which outweighs the increase in nuclear charge.

## Predicting the type of bond

The **difference** in electronegativity between two bonded atoms tells you how unevenly the electrons are shared.

| Difference | Type of bond | Examples (difference) |
|---|---|---|
| zero | non-polar covalent | Cl–Cl (0), H–H (0) |
| small to moderate | polar covalent | C–Cl (0.5), Si–Cl (1.1), Al–Cl (1.5) |
| large (about 2 or more, between a metal and a non-metal) | mainly ionic | Al–O (2.0), Na–Cl (2.1), Ca–O (2.5), K–F (3.2) |

Bonding is a continuum, not two separate boxes. As a rough guide, a large difference (about 2 or more) between a metal and a non-metal gives a mainly ionic compound; a difference near 1.5 or less gives a polar covalent compound. This is why aluminium oxide behaves as an ionic solid with a very high melting point, while aluminium chloride, with a smaller difference, is a covalent compound that sublimes at a low temperature.

:::example{title="Using electronegativity to predict bonding"}
Predict the bonding in magnesium oxide and in silicon(IV) chloride.

- $\ce{MgO}$: $3.5 - 1.3 = 2.2$. Large difference, metal with non-metal: **ionic**. Each Mg atom transfers two electrons to an O atom.
- $\ce{SiCl4}$: $3.0 - 1.9 = 1.1$. Moderate difference: **polar covalent**. The Si–Cl bonds are polar (Cl δ−), but the electrons are shared, not transferred.
:::

## Ionic bonding

In an ionic compound, the metal atom loses its outer electrons and the non-metal atom gains them, so both ions usually have a noble gas configuration. The oppositely charged ions attract each other **in all directions**, forming a giant ionic lattice; there are no molecules.

Dot-and-cross diagrams show only the outer electrons. Electrons from one atom are drawn as dots, from the other as crosses; each ion is drawn in square brackets with its charge outside. Described in words:

| Compound | What the diagram shows |
|---|---|
| sodium chloride, $\ce{NaCl}$ | $\ce{[Na]+}$ with no outer electrons shown (or a full second shell of eight crosses); $\ce{[Cl]-}$ with eight outer electrons: seven dots and one cross |
| magnesium oxide, $\ce{MgO}$ | $\ce{[Mg]^2+}$ and $\ce{[O]^2-}$; the oxide ion has eight outer electrons: six dots and two crosses |
| calcium fluoride, $\ce{CaF2}$ | one $\ce{[Ca]^2+}$ and two separate $\ce{[F]-}$ ions, each with seven dots and one cross |
| aluminium oxide, $\ce{Al2O3}$ (ZIMSEC detail) | two $\ce{[Al]^3+}$ ions and three $\ce{[O]^2-}$ ions, each oxide ion with six dots and two crosses: six electrons transferred in total |

The strength of ionic bonding increases with the **charge** on the ions and decreases with their **size**: $\ce{MgO}$ ($\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{O^2-}$) has a much higher melting point than $\ce{NaCl}$ ($\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$). The comparison of charge density and lattice energies is taken further in the energetics lessons.

## Metallic bonding

In a metal, each atom releases its outer electrons into a **sea of delocalised electrons** that move freely through a lattice of positive ions. The bond is the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons. It is non-directional, so it holds the whole lattice together.

This model explains the typical properties:

- **Electrical conductivity**: delocalised electrons move through the lattice when a potential difference is applied, in the solid and the liquid.
- **Thermal conductivity**: the mobile electrons (and vibrating ions) transfer energy quickly.
- **Malleability and ductility**: layers of positive ions can slide over each other without breaking the bonding, because the delocalised electrons move with them and keep holding them together.
- **High melting and boiling points**: much energy is needed to overcome the strong attraction between the ions and the electrons. The bond is stronger when there are more delocalised electrons per atom and the ions are smaller and more highly charged, which is why magnesium (two outer electrons, $\ce{Mg^2+}$) melts at a higher temperature than sodium (one, $\ce{Na+}$), and aluminium higher still.

:::example{title="Why copper is used for electrical wiring"}
Copper has a giant metallic lattice with delocalised electrons that carry charge, so it is a very good conductor. It is also ductile: the layers of positive copper ions can slide to draw the metal into thin wires without the lattice breaking, because the sea of electrons keeps attracting the ions in their new positions.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Defining electronegativity as attracting 'electrons'"}
The definition is about the **pair of electrons in a covalent bond**. "Attracts electrons" alone does not score.
:::

:::mistake{title="Talking about molecules of sodium chloride"}
Ionic compounds form giant lattices of ions, not molecules. Use "formula unit" or "lattice".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying metals conduct because ions move"}
In a solid metal the positive ions are fixed; the **delocalised electrons** carry the current.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying an ionic bond is the transfer of electrons"}
Transfer forms the ions; the **bond** is the electrostatic attraction between them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 on both boards often asks you to predict bonding from electronegativity values or to choose a correct description of metallic bonding. Learn the three definitions word for word in your own precise phrasing.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **draw** (on paper) a dot-and-cross diagram, show only outer electrons, put each ion in square brackets with its charge, and make the numbers of each ion match the formula.
:::

:::exam-tip
To **explain** a property of a metal, link the property to the structure: "delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge" or "layers of ions can slide while the delocalised electrons hold them together".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract the bonding pair in a covalent bond; it rises across a period and falls down a group.
- Electronegativity difference: zero = non-polar covalent; moderate = polar covalent; large (metal with non-metal) = mainly ionic.
- Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice.
- Metallic bonding is the attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons.
- Delocalised electrons explain conductivity; sliding layers explain malleability; strong attraction explains high melting points.
:::
