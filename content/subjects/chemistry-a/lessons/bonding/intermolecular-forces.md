## Introduction

Methane and water have similar molecular masses (16 and 18), yet methane boils at −162 °C and water at 100 °C. Lake Kariba is liquid, not gas, because of forces **between** water molecules that methane lacks. The same kinds of forces explain why petrol evaporates faster than diesel, why ice floats, and why liquid paraffin feels oily while ethanol evaporates quickly from your skin.

These forces are much weaker than covalent, ionic or metallic bonds, but they decide melting points, boiling points, volatility, viscosity and solubility of every simple molecular substance. You should already know electronegativity and the shapes of molecules.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Polar bond"}
A covalent bond between atoms of different electronegativity, in which the bonding pair is drawn towards the more electronegative atom, giving it a partial negative charge (δ−) and the other atom a partial positive charge (δ+).
:::

:::definition{term="Dipole moment (polar molecule)"}
A molecule has a permanent dipole when its bond dipoles do not cancel, so one end is slightly negative and the other slightly positive. Symmetrical molecules with polar bonds, such as $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$, have no overall dipole.
:::

:::definition{term="van der Waals' forces"}
The general name for the weak attractive forces between molecules: instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces (also called London or dispersion forces) and permanent dipole-permanent dipole forces.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrogen bond"}
A strong type of intermolecular attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to N, O or F (a δ+ hydrogen) and a **lone pair** on an N, O or F atom of another molecule.
:::

## Instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces

Electrons are always moving. At any instant, the electron cloud of a molecule may be slightly uneven, creating a temporary (**instantaneous**) dipole. This dipole distorts the electron cloud of a neighbouring molecule, **inducing** a dipole in it, and the two attract. The dipoles constantly appear and disappear, but the net effect is an attraction between all molecules, polar or not.

These forces are **stronger** when molecules have:

- **more electrons** (larger molecules, higher $M_\mathrm{r}$): the electron cloud is more easily distorted;
- **larger surface area of contact**: long, unbranched chains can lie close together along their length.

They are the only intermolecular forces between non-polar molecules such as the halogens, noble gases and alkanes.

## Permanent dipole-permanent dipole forces

Polar molecules such as hydrogen chloride or propanone have permanent dipoles. The δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of the next. These forces act **in addition to** instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces, so a polar molecule usually boils higher than a non-polar one with the same number of electrons.

:::example{title="Butane and propanone"}
Butane ($M_\mathrm{r}$ 58) and propanone, $\ce{CH3COCH3}$ ($M_\mathrm{r}$ 58), have the same number of electrons. Data: butane boils at about −1 °C, propanone at 56 °C. Propanone's polar C=O group adds permanent dipole-permanent dipole attractions, so more energy is needed to separate its molecules.
:::

## Hydrogen bonding

When hydrogen is bonded to a very electronegative, small atom (N, O or F), the bond is so polar that the hydrogen is strongly δ+ and almost bare of electrons. It is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of a neighbouring molecule. The arrangement N–H···N, O–H···O or F–H···F is close to linear (about 180° at the hydrogen).

Hydrogen bonds are the strongest intermolecular forces, roughly a tenth of the strength of a covalent bond.

:::keyfact{title="Boiling points of hydrides (data, °C)"}

| $\ce{CH4}$ | −162 | $\ce{NH3}$ | −33 | $\ce{HF}$ | 20 |

## The anomalous properties of water

Each water molecule has two δ+ hydrogen atoms and two lone pairs on oxygen, so it can form up to **four** hydrogen bonds, more than $\ce{NH3}$ or $\ce{HF}$ can on average. This explains:

![Hydrogen bonding in water: each water molecule has two partially positive hydrogen atoms and two lone pairs on oxygen; dashed hydrogen bonds join a hydrogen atom of one molecule to a lone pair on the oxygen of a neighbour, so one molecule can form up to four hydrogen bonds.](media:diagrams/hydrogen-bonds-water.png "Figure: hydrogen bonds (dashed) between water molecules")

- **High melting and boiling points** for so small a molecule: many hydrogen bonds must be overcome.
- **High surface tension**: molecules at the surface are pulled inwards by hydrogen bonds, which lets some insects walk on water.
- **Ice is less dense than liquid water**: in ice every molecule forms four hydrogen bonds in an open, tetrahedral lattice that holds the molecules further apart than in the liquid. When ice melts, some hydrogen bonds break and the molecules move closer together. So ice floats, insulating the water beneath it in cold climates.

## Comparing boiling points

Use a three-step check: (1) which forces act between the molecules; (2) for similar forces, compare the number of electrons; (3) for isomers, compare the shape (surface contact).

:::example{title="Isomers of pentane"}
Pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane are all $\ce{C5H12}$ (same number of electrons). Data: boiling points 36 °C, 28 °C and 10 °C. The more branched the molecule, the more compact and nearly spherical it is, so the area of contact between molecules is smaller, the instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces are weaker, and the boiling point is lower.
:::

## Intermolecular forces versus bonds

When a simple molecular substance melts or boils, only the **intermolecular forces** are overcome; the covalent bonds inside the molecules are unaffected. Boiling water produces $\ce{H2O}$ molecules in the gas, not hydrogen and oxygen. Typical strengths: instantaneous dipole-induced dipole and permanent dipole forces up to a few tens of kJ mol⁻¹, hydrogen bonds roughly 10-40 kJ mol⁻¹, covalent bonds hundreds of kJ mol⁻¹.

## Demonstrating polarity (ZIMSEC suggested experiment)

Let a thin stream of water run from a burette and bring a charged rod (a plastic ruler rubbed with a cloth, or a charged polythene rod) close to it. The stream bends towards the rod, whichever sign of charge the rod carries: the polar water molecules turn so that their oppositely charged ends face the rod and are attracted. A stream of a non-polar liquid such as hexane is not deflected. (Hexane is flammable: no naked flames.)

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying covalent bonds break when a molecular substance boils"}
Boiling separates molecules; only intermolecular forces are overcome.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hydrogen bonding with any hydrogen atom"}
Hydrogen bonds need H attached to N, O or F **and** a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule. The C–H hydrogens in methane cannot form hydrogen bonds.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling instantaneous dipoles 'permanent'"}
They are temporary and constantly changing; they exist in all molecules, including non-polar ones.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing boiling points by Mr alone"}
Check for hydrogen bonding and permanent dipoles first: propan-1-ol (Mr 60, 97 °C) boils far above butane (Mr 58, about −1 °C, data).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Explain** the difference in boiling points" (Paper 2 on both boards, ZIMSEC Paper 3) needs: the type of intermolecular force in each substance, which is stronger and why, and the link "more energy needed to overcome the forces".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **draw** (on paper) a hydrogen bond, show the δ+ H and δ− atom, the lone pair on the acceptor atom and a dashed line from H to the lone pair, roughly in a straight line.
:::

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 often lists four substances and asks which has hydrogen bonding or the highest boiling point. Look for O–H, N–H or H–F first.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bond polarity comes from electronegativity differences; symmetrical molecules can have polar bonds but no overall dipole.
- Instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces act between all molecules and increase with the number of electrons and surface contact.
- Permanent dipole forces add to these in polar molecules; hydrogen bonds (H on N, O or F to a lone pair on N, O or F) are the strongest.
- Hydrogen bonding explains water's high boiling point, surface tension and the low density of ice.
- Melting and boiling of simple molecular substances overcome only intermolecular forces, which are much weaker than bonds.
:::
