## Introduction

Nitrogen makes up about four-fifths of the air, yet plants cannot use it directly, and fertiliser plants need high temperatures, high pressures and an iron catalyst to turn it into ammonia. Why is nitrogen so unreactive when chlorine, another diatomic gas, attacks almost everything? The answer is in the bond: the triple bond in $\ce{N2}$ is one of the strongest in chemistry.

This lesson builds the A-Level picture of covalent bonding: what holds a shared pair in place, how to represent molecules (including those that break the octet rule), how a coordinate bond differs from an ordinary one, and how bond energy, bond length and polarity explain reactivity. You should already be able to draw simple dot-and-cross diagrams from O-Level and know what electronegativity is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Covalent bond"}
The electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Coordinate (dative covalent) bond"}
A covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom. Once formed, it is identical to any other covalent bond between the same atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Bond energy"}
The energy needed to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in gaseous molecules, forming gaseous atoms (or radicals). For bonds that occur in many molecules, such as C–H, an average value is quoted.
:::

:::definition{term="Bond length"}
The distance between the nuclei of two atoms joined by a covalent bond.
:::

## Single, double and triple bonds

Each shared pair is one covalent bond. Atoms usually share enough electrons to reach eight in their outer shell (two for hydrogen). In a dot-and-cross diagram, electrons from one atom are dots and from the other crosses; lone pairs are shown too.

| Molecule | Bonds | Lone pairs (on which atoms) |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{H2}$ | one H–H single bond | none |
| $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{HCl}$ | one single bond | three on each Cl |
| $\ce{O2}$ | one O=O double bond (two shared pairs) | two on each O |
| $\ce{N2}$ | one N≡N triple bond (three shared pairs) | one on each N |
| $\ce{CO2}$ | two C=O double bonds | two on each O |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | three N–H bonds | one on N |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | four C–H bonds | none |
| $\ce{C2H6}$ | one C–C and six C–H bonds | none |
| $\ce{C2H4}$ | one C=C and four C–H bonds | none |

## Breaking the octet rule

Elements in Period 3 and beyond can have **more than eight** electrons around the central atom (an *expanded octet*), because they have vacant d orbitals available in their outer shell.

- $\ce{PCl5}$: five P–Cl bonds, ten electrons around phosphorus.
- $\ce{SF6}$: six S–F bonds, twelve electrons around sulfur.
- $\ce{SO2}$: often drawn with two S=O double bonds and one lone pair on sulfur, giving ten electrons around sulfur.

Some molecules have an **odd number of electrons**, so one atom cannot have an octet. Nitrogen monoxide, NO, has 11 outer electrons (5 + 6) and an unpaired electron; nitrogen dioxide, $\ce{NO2}$, has 17. These are free radicals, which helps to explain their reactivity in polluted air.

Boron trifluoride, $\ce{BF3}$, has only **six** electrons around boron. This electron-deficient boron can accept a lone pair, as below.

## Coordinate (dative covalent) bonding

A coordinate bond forms when an atom with a **lone pair** donates it into an empty orbital of an atom or ion that is **electron-deficient**. In diagrams it is drawn as an arrow pointing from the donor to the acceptor.

:::example{title="The ammonium ion"}
Ammonia has a lone pair on nitrogen; a hydrogen ion has an empty 1s orbital. When ammonia reacts with an acid, the lone pair forms a coordinate bond to $\ce{H+}$:

$$
\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}
$$

In the dot-and-cross diagram of $\ce{NH4+}$, nitrogen has four shared pairs; one of them contains two electrons that both came from nitrogen. The ion is placed in square brackets with a + charge. Once formed, all four N–H bonds are identical in length and strength.
:::

:::example{title="Aluminium chloride dimer, Al₂Cl₆"}
In aluminium chloride vapour at lower temperatures, two $\ce{AlCl3}$ units join to form $\ce{Al2Cl6}$. Each aluminium atom in $\ce{AlCl3}$ has only six outer electrons. A chlorine atom of one unit donates a lone pair to the aluminium of the other unit, and vice versa: **two coordinate bonds**, each from a bridging Cl to an Al. Each aluminium then has eight outer electrons and is surrounded tetrahedrally by four chlorine atoms.
:::

Other examples you will meet later: the oxonium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$ (water donating a lone pair to $\ce{H+}$), and the bonds between ligands and metal ions in complexes.

## Bond energy and bond length

More shared pairs between the same two atoms mean more electron density between the nuclei, a stronger attraction, a **shorter** bond and a **larger** bond energy.

| Bond | Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet) | Bond length / nm (data) |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| C–C | 350 | 0.154 |
| C=C | 610 | 0.134 |
| C≡C | 840 | 0.120 |
| N≡N | 944 | 0.110 |

Down a group, bonds to the larger atom are longer and weaker, because the shared pair is further from the nucleus and more shielded. For the carbon-halogen bonds: C–Cl 340, C–Br 280, C–I 240 kJ mol⁻¹.

## Using bond data to explain reactivity

:::example{title="Why nitrogen is unreactive"}
To react, $\ce{N2}$ must usually have its triple bond broken (or at least weakened). Its bond energy, 944 kJ mol⁻¹, is very large, so the activation energy of its reactions is high and nitrogen reacts only under extreme conditions: lightning, the Haber process, or enzymes in the root nodules of legumes such as groundnuts. The triple bond is also non-polar, so the molecule does not attract nucleophiles or electrophiles.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing the halogenoalkanes"}
The rate of hydrolysis of halogenoalkanes increases in the order chloro- < bromo- < iodo-. Two factors could matter:

- **Bond polarity**: C–Cl is the most polar (chlorine is the most electronegative), which on its own would make the chloroalkane carbon the most attractive to nucleophiles.
- **Bond energy**: C–I (240) is much weaker than C–Cl (340 kJ mol⁻¹), so it is the easiest to break.

The observed order follows bond energy, so **bond strength** is the deciding factor. This shows why both the energy and the polarity of a bond must be considered.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a covalent bond as 'sharing electrons' only"}
The bond is the **attraction** between the nuclei and the shared pair. Mention both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a dative bond is different once formed"}
All four N–H bonds in $\ce{NH4+}$ are identical. The word *dative* describes only where the electrons came from.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting lone pairs"}
Dot-and-cross diagrams of $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ must show the non-bonding pairs; they also decide molecular shapes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining halogenoalkane reactivity by polarity alone"}
Polarity predicts the wrong order. The C–I bond reacts fastest because it is the weakest.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **draw** a dot-and-cross diagram (paper exams), show outer electrons only, distinguish the atoms' electrons, and include every lone pair. For a dative bond, both electrons of that pair come from the donor atom.
:::

:::exam-tip
"**Explain** why nitrogen is unreactive" needs the triple bond, its high bond energy (state 944 kJ mol⁻¹ if given), and the high activation energy that results. Non-polarity is a useful extra point.
:::

:::exam-tip
Both boards may give you bond energies in a table and ask you to **deduce** which bond breaks most easily or to compare reactivity. ZIMSEC questions sometimes ask about bond polarity: discuss both factors and say which one wins.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A covalent bond is the attraction between two nuclei and a shared pair of electrons; double and triple bonds have two and three shared pairs.
- Period 3 atoms can expand their octet ($\ce{PCl5}$, $\ce{SF6}$, $\ce{SO2}$); NO and $\ce{NO2}$ have odd numbers of electrons.
- In a coordinate bond both electrons come from one atom: $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{H3O+}$, $\ce{Al2Cl6}$.
- More shared pairs mean shorter, stronger bonds; bonds to larger atoms are longer and weaker.
- Very strong N≡N makes nitrogen unreactive; weak C–I makes iodoalkanes the most reactive halogenoalkanes.
:::
