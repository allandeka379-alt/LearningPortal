## Introduction

Dot-and-cross diagrams tell you how many electrons are shared, but not **where** they are. Orbital overlap answers that question, and it explains facts that dot-and-cross diagrams cannot: why ethene is flat, why a C=C bond is less than twice as strong as a C–C bond, why but-2-ene exists as two different (cis and trans) isomers, and why alkenes react with bromine while alkanes do not.

This is the bonding model behind organic chemistry, from the ethanol made from sugar-cane molasses in the Lowveld to the polymers in plastic water pipes. You should already know the shapes of s and p orbitals and how to draw dot-and-cross diagrams with single, double and triple bonds.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sigma (σ) bond"}
A covalent bond formed by the direct, end-on (head-on) overlap of two orbitals, with the electron density concentrated **on the line between the two nuclei**.
:::

:::definition{term="Pi (π) bond"}
A covalent bond formed by the sideways overlap of two parallel p orbitals, with the electron density **above and below** the line between the two nuclei.
:::

:::definition{term="Hybridisation"}
The mixing of atomic orbitals (s and p) on the same atom to form a set of equivalent hybrid orbitals, which then overlap with orbitals of other atoms to form σ bonds.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Counting σ and π bonds"}
| Bond | σ bonds | π bonds |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| single | 1 | 0 |
| double | 1 | 1 |
| triple | 1 | 2 |
:::

## σ bonds: end-on overlap

Any two orbitals that point at each other can overlap end-on to form a σ bond:

- s with s: the H–H bond in $\ce{H2}$ (two 1s orbitals).
- s with p: the H–Cl bond in $\ce{HCl}$ (H 1s with Cl 3p).
- p with p end-on: the Cl–Cl bond in $\ce{Cl2}$ (two 3p orbitals along the bond axis).
- hybrid orbitals with s or with each other: the C–H and C–C bonds in organic molecules.

Because the electron density lies on the axis, rotating one end of a σ bond does not reduce the overlap. **Free rotation** is possible about a single bond.

## π bonds: sideways overlap

When two atoms are already joined by a σ bond, any p orbitals left over that are parallel to each other (perpendicular to the bond axis) can overlap sideways. The π bond has two lobes, one above and one below the σ bond, and together they hold **one** pair of electrons.

Sideways overlap is less effective than end-on overlap, so a π bond is **weaker** than a σ bond. This is why C=C (610 kJ mol⁻¹) has less than twice the bond energy of C–C (350 kJ mol⁻¹). The exposed π electrons are also the reason alkenes attract electrophiles.

**Restricted rotation**: twisting one end of a π bond would move the p orbitals out of line and break the π overlap. So there is no free rotation about a C=C bond, which is why cis-trans (geometric) isomers exist.

| Molecule | σ bonds | π bonds | Notes |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| $\ce{H2}$ | 1 | 0 | 1s–1s overlap |
| $\ce{Cl2}$ | 1 | 0 | 3p–3p end-on |
| $\ce{O2}$ | 1 | 1 | O=O |
| $\ce{N2}$ | 1 | 2 | N≡N: two π bonds at right angles |
| $\ce{C2H6}$ | 7 | 0 | six C–H, one C–C |
| $\ce{C2H4}$ | 5 | 1 | four C–H, C=C (σ + π) |
| $\ce{HCN}$ | 2 | 2 | H–C σ; C≡N is σ + 2π |

## Hybridisation in carbon compounds

A carbon atom in its ground state is 1s² 2s² 2p², with only two unpaired electrons, yet carbon forms four bonds. In the bonding model, one 2s electron is promoted to 2p and then the 2s and some 2p orbitals mix (hybridise).

### sp³: four single bonds

The 2s and all three 2p orbitals mix to give **four sp³ hybrid orbitals**, each with one electron. They repel to point to the corners of a tetrahedron, **109.5°** apart. Each overlaps end-on with a hydrogen 1s orbital or another carbon's hybrid orbital: four σ bonds. Examples: methane, ethane, every carbon in an alkane, whether straight-chain, branched or in a ring such as cyclohexane.

### sp²: a double bond

The 2s and **two** 2p orbitals mix to give **three sp² hybrids** in a plane at **120°**, leaving one unhybridised 2p orbital at right angles to the plane. In ethene each carbon uses its sp² hybrids to form three σ bonds (two C–H and one C–C), all in one plane. The two leftover p orbitals, parallel to each other, overlap sideways to form the π bond. Result: ethene is **planar**, with bond angles of about 120°.

### sp: a triple bond

The 2s and **one** 2p orbital mix to give **two sp hybrids** pointing in opposite directions, **180°** apart, with two unhybridised p orbitals left at right angles to each other. In hydrogen cyanide, H–C≡N, carbon uses its sp hybrids for the σ bonds to H and N, and its two remaining p orbitals overlap with two p orbitals of nitrogen to form two π bonds. The molecule is **linear**.

:::example{title="Hybridisation in propene, CH₃CH=CH₂"}
- The CH₃ carbon forms four single bonds: **sp³**, bond angles about 109.5°.
- The two carbons of the C=C bond each form three σ bonds and one π bond: **sp²**, bond angles about 120°.
- Total bonds: six C–H σ, two C–C σ (one single, one within the double bond) and one π. So 8 σ and 1 π.
- The three carbons and the three hydrogens on the C=C carbons lie in one plane, but the CH₃ hydrogens do not all lie in it.
:::

:::example{title="Counting σ and π bonds in buta-1,3-diene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂"}
Six C–H bonds (σ), three C–C bonds in the chain (each contains one σ) and two C=C double bonds (each contributes one π). Total: **9 σ bonds and 2 π bonds**. All four carbons are sp² hybridised.
:::

## Benzene in outline

In benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, each carbon is sp² hybridised and forms three σ bonds in a flat hexagonal ring. Each carbon has one p orbital left over. Instead of forming three separate π bonds, the six p orbitals overlap all round the ring to give a **delocalised π system** above and below the plane. The full treatment, with the evidence for it, is in the lesson on the structure of benzene.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting a double bond as two σ bonds"}
A double bond is one σ bond and one π bond; a triple bond is one σ and two π.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a π bond is two bonds because it has two lobes"}
The two lobes above and below the axis together form one π bond holding one pair of electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing π bonds between hybrid orbitals"}
π bonds form from **unhybridised** p orbitals. Hybrid orbitals form σ bonds only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming every carbon in an alkene is sp²"}
Only the carbons of the C=C bond are sp²; any saturated carbon (such as CH₃) is sp³.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Describe** how a π bond forms" (Paper 2 on both boards): sideways overlap of two p orbitals, electron density above and below the plane or bond axis. One mark is usually for each idea.
:::

:::exam-tip
To **state** the hybridisation of an atom, count its σ bonds (and, for N or O, lone pairs): four regions → sp³, three → sp², two → sp. Give the bond angle with it.
:::

:::exam-tip
Counting σ and π bonds is a favourite Paper 1 question. Draw out the full structural formula first, then count each bond.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- σ bonds come from end-on overlap (density on the bond axis); π bonds from sideways overlap of parallel p orbitals (density above and below).
- Single = σ; double = σ + π; triple = σ + 2π; a π bond is weaker than a σ bond.
- sp³: four σ bonds, tetrahedral, 109.5°; sp²: three σ bonds, planar, 120°, one p left for π; sp: two σ bonds, linear, 180°.
- Rotation about a π bond is restricted, which allows cis-trans isomers.
- Benzene: sp² carbons with a ring of delocalised π electrons.
:::
