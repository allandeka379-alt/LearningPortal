## Introduction

Water is a bent molecule; carbon dioxide is a straight one. That difference decides a great deal: water's bent shape makes it polar, so it dissolves salts and sugars, forms hydrogen bonds and has an unusually high boiling point for so small a molecule. Carbon dioxide's linear shape makes it non-polar and a gas.

Shape also matters in medicine and agriculture. Drugs and pesticides work because their molecules fit the shapes of particular protein sites, and enzymes in a maize plant act only on molecules of the right shape. Being able to predict shape from a formula is therefore one of the most useful skills in chemistry.

You should already be able to draw dot-and-cross diagrams, including lone pairs, expanded octets and coordinate bonds.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="VSEPR theory"}
Valence shell electron pair repulsion theory: the pairs of electrons in the outer shell of the central atom repel each other and arrange themselves as far apart as possible, which decides the shape of the molecule or ion.
:::

:::definition{term="Bonding pair"}
A pair of electrons shared between the central atom and another atom. A double or triple bond counts as **one** region of electron density for working out the shape.
:::

:::definition{term="Lone pair"}
A pair of outer-shell electrons on the central atom that is not involved in bonding.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The order of repulsion"}
lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair

A lone pair is held only by the central nucleus, so it is closer to it and spreads out more. Each lone pair squeezes the bonding pairs closer together, reducing the bond angle by about 2.5°.
:::

## The method

1. Find the central atom and its number of outer electrons.
2. Add one electron for each atom bonded by a single bond (H, F, Cl and so on). For a negative ion add electrons equal to the charge; for a positive ion subtract them. (Oxygen atoms joined by double bonds add no electrons in this count; treat each double bond as one region.)
3. Divide by two to get the number of electron pairs (regions of electron density).
4. Subtract the bonding regions to get the lone pairs.
5. Choose the basic arrangement from the total number of regions, then name the shape from the positions of the **atoms** only.

## The shapes to learn

| Example | Bonding regions | Lone pairs | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|---|
| $\ce{CO2}$ | 2 | 0 | linear | 180° |
| $\ce{BF3}$ | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° |
| $\ce{CH4}$ | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |
| $\ce{NH3}$ | 3 | 1 | (trigonal) pyramidal | 107° |
| $\ce{H2O}$ | 2 | 2 | non-linear (bent, V-shaped) | 104.5° |
| $\ce{PF5}$ | 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 120° and 90° |
| $\ce{SF6}$ | 6 | 0 | octahedral | 90° |

Notice how methane, ammonia and water all have four pairs around the central atom, arranged roughly tetrahedrally. The angle falls from 109.5° to 107° to 104.5° as bonding pairs are replaced by lone pairs, which repel more strongly.

## Applying the method to new species

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$]
1. Central N has 5 outer electrons; four H atoms add 4; the + charge removes 1: $5 + 4 - 1 = 8$ electrons.
2. 8 ÷ 2 = 4 pairs, all bonding (four N–H bonds), no lone pairs.
3. Shape: **tetrahedral**, bond angle **109.5°**. (Ammonia's lone pair has become a bonding pair, so the angle opens out from 107°.)
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the oxonium ion, $\ce{H3O+}$]
1. Central O has 6 outer electrons; three H add 3; the + charge removes 1: 8 electrons, 4 pairs.
2. Three bonding pairs and one lone pair, just like ammonia.
3. Shape: **pyramidal**, bond angle about **107°**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: sulfur dioxide, $\ce{SO2}$]
1. S has 6 outer electrons. Each oxygen is joined by a double bond, so it adds no electrons in the count, and each S=O counts as one bonding region.
2. Six electrons on sulfur: four are used in the two double bonds, leaving **one lone pair**.
3. Three regions (two bonding, one lone pair): the arrangement is trigonal planar, so the molecule is **non-linear (bent)**, with an angle a little less than 120° (about 119°).
:::

Other analogous cases follow directly: $\ce{PCl3}$ and $\ce{PH3}$ are pyramidal like ammonia; $\ce{H2S}$ is bent like water; $\ce{SiCl4}$ and $\ce{CCl4}$ are tetrahedral like methane; $\ce{PCl5}$ is trigonal bipyramidal like $\ce{PF5}$; $\ce{AlCl4-}$ is tetrahedral. Molecules with five regions including lone pairs, such as $\ce{ClF3}$ (three bonding pairs and two lone pairs, a T-shape), are beyond what you need to predict in detail; know only that lone pairs again reduce the angles.

## Shape and polarity (outline)

A molecule with polar bonds is polar overall only if the bond dipoles do not cancel. Shape decides this:

- $\ce{CO2}$ (linear), $\ce{BF3}$ (trigonal planar) and $\ce{CCl4}$ (tetrahedral) have polar bonds arranged symmetrically: the dipoles cancel and the molecules are **non-polar**.
- $\ce{H2O}$ (bent) and $\ce{NH3}$ (pyramidal) are not symmetrical, so the dipoles add up: they are **polar**.

Intermolecular forces and dipole moments are developed further in the next bonding lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling ammonia 'trigonal planar'"}
Ammonia has a lone pair as well as three bonding pairs. Its arrangement of pairs is tetrahedral, and its shape (atoms only) is pyramidal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting a double bond as two regions"}
For shape, a double bond counts as one region of electron density. $\ce{CO2}$ has two regions and is linear.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the charge on an ion"}
Add electrons for a negative charge and subtract for a positive charge before dividing by two.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming the shape from all the pairs"}
The name describes the positions of atoms. Water has four pairs around oxygen, but its shape is bent, not tetrahedral.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 on both boards often gives a formula and asks for the shape or bond angle. Show (in your head or on scrap paper) the count: electrons, pairs, bonding, lone pairs.
:::

:::exam-tip
To **explain** a shape (Paper 2, ZIMSEC Paper 3), state the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs, that electron pairs repel to be as far apart as possible, that lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs, and then the shape and angle. Each of these is a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **predict** the angle in an analogous species, compare it with one you know (for example $\ce{PCl3}$ with $\ce{NH3}$): VSEPR gives "about 107°", or "less than 109.5°". Measured angles for heavier analogues are often smaller (about 94° in $\ce{PH3}$), which VSEPR alone does not predict.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Electron pairs around the central atom repel and get as far apart as possible.
- Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, closing bond angles by about 2.5° each.
- 2 regions: linear 180°; 3: trigonal planar 120°; 4: tetrahedral 109.5°; 5: trigonal bipyramidal 120° and 90°; 6: octahedral 90°.
- With lone pairs: NH₃ pyramidal 107°, H₂O bent 104.5°; NH₄⁺ tetrahedral 109.5°.
- Symmetrical shapes cancel bond dipoles (non-polar); unsymmetrical shapes give polar molecules.
:::
