## Introduction

Magnesium ribbon burning with a blinding white light, a lump of sodium racing across a trough of water, white phosphorus smoking in air: some of the most memorable demonstrations in a school laboratory come from Period 3. Behind the drama is a clear pattern. Across the period the elements change from reactive metals that lose electrons to non-metals that share them, and the formulae of their oxides and chlorides change in a regular way.

This lesson covers the reactions of the elements themselves; the behaviour of the oxides and chlorides with water is in the next lesson. You should already know the physical trends across Period 3, how to work out oxidation numbers, and how to balance equations and calculate reacting masses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oxidation number (in a binary compound)"}
The charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic, with the shared electrons given to the more electronegative atom. In oxides and chlorides of Period 3 elements, oxygen is −2 and chlorine is −1.
:::

:::definition{term="Chemical periodicity"}
The regular, repeating change in the chemical behaviour of the elements, and in the formulae and properties of their compounds, across each period.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Highest oxidation number across Period 3"}
Na +1, Mg +2, Al +3, Si +4, P +5, S +6. This equals the number of outer-shell electrons, because in the highest oxide or chloride every outer electron is used in bonding to the more electronegative oxygen or chlorine.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: reactions with oxygen (Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃, P₄O₁₀, SO₂), with chlorine (NaCl, MgCl₂, AlCl₃, SiCl₄, PCl₅) and with water (Na and Mg only), the oxidation numbers, and predicting the properties of elements. Cambridge 9.3 also asks you to deduce the identity and position of an unknown element from data.
:::

## Reactions with oxygen

| Element | Conditions | Observation | Equation | Product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sodium | heat, then it burns in air | bright yellow-orange flame | $\ce{4Na + O2 -> 2Na2O}$ | white solid |
| magnesium | heat, then it burns | brilliant white flame | $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$ | white solid |
| aluminium | powder heated strongly | burns with a white flame | $\ce{4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3}$ | white solid |
| silicon | heated strongly in oxygen | slow reaction | $\ce{Si + O2 -> SiO2}$ | white solid |
| phosphorus (white) | ignites spontaneously in air | yellow-white flame, dense white smoke | $\ce{P4 + 5O2 -> P4O10}$ | white solid |
| sulfur | heat, then it burns | blue flame | $\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}$ | colourless, choking gas |

Some useful details:

- Sodium also forms some sodium peroxide, Na₂O₂, in excess oxygen; the syllabuses expect Na₂O.
- Aluminium objects do not burn because a thin, tough layer of Al₂O₃ protects the metal; only powder or foil reacts readily.
- With a limited supply of oxygen phosphorus forms P₄O₆; in excess it forms P₄O₁₀.
- Sulfur gives SO₂. Turning SO₂ into SO₃ needs a catalyst (vanadium(V) oxide, about 400-450 °C, as in the Contact process): $\ce{2SO2 + O2 <=> 2SO3}$.
- Chlorine and argon do not react directly with oxygen.

## Reactions with chlorine

All of these need heating to start, then usually continue on their own.

| Element | Observation | Equation | Product at room temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| sodium | burns with a yellow flame | $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$ | white solid |
| magnesium | burns with a white flame | $\ce{Mg + Cl2 -> MgCl2}$ | white solid |
| aluminium | glows; product sublimes | $\ce{2Al + 3Cl2 -> 2AlCl3}$ (vapour $\ce{Al2Cl6}$) | white or pale yellow solid |
| silicon | slow, needs strong heating | $\ce{Si + 2Cl2 -> SiCl4}$ | colourless liquid |
| phosphorus | reacts readily | $\ce{P4 + 10Cl2 -> 4PCl5}$ (excess chlorine) | pale yellow (off-white) solid |

With limited chlorine, phosphorus gives phosphorus trichloride, PCl₃, a colourless liquid. Sulfur does form chlorides, but they are not in either syllabus.

## Reactions with water

**Sodium** reacts vigorously with cold water. The metal floats, melts into a silvery ball because the reaction is very exothermic, fizzes and moves around; sometimes the hydrogen ignites. The solution becomes strongly alkaline (pH about 13-14):

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

**Magnesium** reacts only very slowly with cold water, giving a few bubbles over days and a weakly alkaline solution (pH about 9-10) because magnesium hydroxide is only slightly soluble:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)}
$$

Heated magnesium reacts rapidly with **steam**, glowing brightly and leaving white magnesium oxide:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}
$$

The difference reflects reactivity: sodium loses one electron easily (low first ionisation energy), while magnesium must lose two and is protected by a layer of oxide.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: hydrogen from sodium and water]
0.46 g of sodium reacts completely with water. Calculate the volume of hydrogen formed at room conditions. (Ar Na = 23.0; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

1. $n(\ce{Na}) = 0.46 \div 23.0 = 0.0200$ mol.
2. From $\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$, $n(\ce{H2}) = 0.0200 \div 2 = 0.0100$ mol.
3. $V = 0.0100 \times 24.0 = 0.240$ dm³ $= 240$ cm³.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of oxide from burning magnesium]
1.20 g of magnesium ribbon is burned completely in air. Calculate the mass of magnesium oxide formed. (Ar Mg = 24.3; Mr MgO = 40.3)

1. $n(\ce{Mg}) = 1.20 \div 24.3 = 0.04938$ mol.
2. From $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$, $n(\ce{MgO}) = n(\ce{Mg}) = 0.04938$ mol.
3. Mass $= 0.04938 \times 40.3 = 1.99$ g. The mass increases because oxygen from the air has combined with the metal.
:::

## Oxidation numbers of the oxides and chlorides

| Compound | Na₂O | MgO | Al₂O₃ | SiO₂ | P₄O₁₀ | SO₂ | SO₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| oxidation number of the Period 3 element | +1 | +2 | +3 | +4 | +5 | +4 | +6 |

| Compound | NaCl | MgCl₂ | AlCl₃ | SiCl₄ | PCl₅ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| oxidation number of the Period 3 element | +1 | +2 | +3 | +4 | +5 |

The oxidation number rises by one for each step across the period because each element has one more outer-shell (valence) electron, and in these compounds all of them are used in bonding to the more electronegative oxygen or chlorine. Sulfur shows +4 in SO₂ (four of its six outer electrons used) and +6 in SO₃ (all six). There is no stable chloride with sulfur in the +6 state: six chlorine atoms do not fit around a sulfur atom (although six smaller fluorine atoms do, in SF₆).

## Predicting and deducing

Periodicity lets you predict properties of elements you have not studied, and identify unknowns.

:::example{title="Example 3: identifying an unknown element"}
Element **Z** is in Period 3. It burns in oxygen to form a white solid with a very high melting point. The oxide does not dissolve in water but dissolves in both hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide. **Z** reacts with chlorine to form a chloride that sublimes at about 180 °C. Identify **Z** and give the formula of each compound.

1. A white solid oxide with a very high melting point is a giant (ionic) lattice, so **Z** is a metal or near-metal.
2. An oxide that reacts with acids and alkalis is amphoteric: in Period 3 that is aluminium oxide.
3. A chloride that sublimes at a low temperature is covalent: aluminium chloride (dimer Al₂Cl₆ in the vapour).
4. **Z** is aluminium; the oxide is Al₂O₃ and the chloride AlCl₃. Its highest oxidation number, +3, equals its three outer electrons, placing it in Group 13 (Group III).
:::

To predict the behaviour of, say, potassium or selenium, use its position: potassium is below sodium in Group 1, so it reacts even more vigorously with water and forms K₂O; selenium is below sulfur in Group 16, so its highest oxide should be SeO₃ and its common oxide SeO₂.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing MgO for magnesium with cold water"}
Cold water gives magnesium **hydroxide**; steam gives magnesium **oxide**. Check the state of the water in the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the formula P₄O₁₀"}
Phosphorus(V) oxide is P₄O₁₀ (sometimes simplified to P₂O₅). Balance as P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving SO₃ as the product of burning sulfur"}
Burning sulfur gives SO₂. SO₃ forms only with a catalyst, as in the Contact process.
:::

:::mistake{title="Linking oxidation number to the charge of an ion in a covalent compound"}
SiCl₄ and PCl₅ are covalent. The oxidation numbers +4 and +5 are a bookkeeping device, not real ionic charges.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. Equations with observations are common 2-4 mark parts; data-based "identify the element" questions appear in both multiple-choice and structured papers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* a reaction: give the observations (flame colour, product colour and state) and a balanced equation. *Explain* the oxidation numbers: link them to the number of outer-shell electrons used in bonding. *Predict*: name the trend you are using. *Deduce*: state each piece of evidence and what it tells you, then give the identity.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- With oxygen: Na₂O, MgO, Al₂O₃ (white solids), SiO₂, P₄O₁₀ (white smoke) and SO₂ (gas, blue flame).
- With chlorine: NaCl, MgCl₂, AlCl₃ (sublimes, Al₂Cl₆ vapour), SiCl₄ (liquid) and PCl₅ (solid, excess chlorine).
- Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water to give NaOH and H₂; magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water but rapidly with steam to give MgO and H₂.
- The highest oxidation number rises from +1 (Na) to +6 (S in SO₃): all the outer-shell electrons are used in bonding.
- Use the trends to predict properties of other elements and to identify unknowns from data.
:::
