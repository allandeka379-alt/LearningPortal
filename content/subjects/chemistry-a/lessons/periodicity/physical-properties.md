## Introduction

Silicon chips in every phone sold in Harare, aluminium roofing sheets, sulfur for the sulfuric acid used in fertiliser factories, chlorine at the Morton Jaffray water works: the third row of the Periodic Table, sodium to argon, is full of familiar elements. They sit side by side in one period, yet they range from a soft metal that melts below the boiling point of water (sodium) to a hard semiconductor that melts above 1400 °C (silicon) and a gas that hardly reacts with anything (argon).

The word **periodicity** means that such patterns repeat across each period. Period 3 is the model period for both boards because every main type of structure appears in it. This lesson explains the physical trends; the reactions of the elements and their compounds come in the next two lessons. You should already know electron configurations, the factors that affect ionisation energy, and the main types of structure (giant metallic, giant covalent, simple molecular).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Periodicity"}
The repeating pattern of physical and chemical properties of the elements across successive periods of the Periodic Table.
:::

:::definition{term="Atomic radius"}
Half the distance between the nuclei of two neighbouring atoms of the same element: the metallic radius for metals, the covalent radius for non-metals that form covalent bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="First ionisation energy"}
The energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions: $\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Shielding"}
The reduction in the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons caused by repulsion from electrons in inner shells.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: atomic and ionic radius, melting point and electrical conductivity across Period 3, explained by structure and bonding. ZIMSEC lists first ionisation energy in this topic; Cambridge covers it in atomic structure (topic 2) and expects you to use it here too.
:::

## Atomic radius

Atomic radius **decreases** from sodium to chlorine.

1. Each step adds one proton to the nucleus, so the nuclear charge increases.
2. The extra electron goes into the same (third) shell, so shielding by the inner electrons stays almost the same.
3. The outer electrons therefore feel a stronger attraction and are pulled closer to the nucleus.

## Ionic radius

The ionic radii fall into two groups.

![Atomic and ionic radii across Period 3: atomic radius falls steadily from 0.186 nm for Na to 0.099 nm for Cl; the cations Na+ to Si4+ are much smaller (0.095 down to 0.041 nm) while the anions P3-, S2- and Cl- are larger than their atoms (0.212, 0.184, 0.181 nm), so ionic radius jumps up between Si4+ and P3-.](media:diagrams/period-3-atomic-ionic-radii.png "Figure: atomic and ionic radii across Period 3 (data from the table above)")


- **Positive ions** Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ (and the hypothetical Si⁴⁺) have lost the whole third shell, so they are much smaller than their atoms. All four have the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (isoelectronic), and the radius falls as the nuclear charge rises from 11 to 14.
- **Negative ions** P³⁻, S²⁻ and Cl⁻ have gained electrons into the third shell, so they are larger than their atoms. They are isoelectronic with argon (1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶), and again the radius falls as the nuclear charge rises from 15 to 17.

So there is a sharp jump in ionic radius between Si⁴⁺ and P³⁻: the anions have one more occupied shell than the cations.

## First ionisation energy

The general trend is an **increase** across the period, from 494 kJ mol⁻¹ for sodium to 1520 kJ mol⁻¹ for argon (data booklet values), for the same reasons that the radius decreases: greater nuclear charge, similar shielding, smaller atoms. Two dips break the pattern.

- **Mg to Al.** Aluminium's outer electron is in a 3p orbital, higher in energy than magnesium's 3s orbital and partly shielded by the 3s electrons, so it is easier to remove.
- **P to S.** In sulfur, the electron removed comes from a 3p orbital that holds a pair of electrons. Repulsion between the paired electrons makes it easier to remove than an electron from phosphorus, whose three 3p electrons are unpaired.

## Melting point

The melting point depends on the **structure and bonding**, not on the size of the atoms alone.

![Bar chart of melting points across Period 3 in kelvin: Na 371, Mg 922, Al 933 (giant metallic, rising), Si 1687 (giant covalent, highest), then P4 317, S8 388, Cl2 172 and Ar 84 (simple molecules or atoms, low).](media:diagrams/period-3-melting-points.png "Figure: melting points across Period 3; metals rise to Al, Si (giant covalent) is highest, molecular elements are low")


| Elements | Structure | What must be overcome on melting | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | giant metallic lattice of cations in a sea of delocalised electrons | metallic bonding | high; rises Na → Al |
| Si | giant covalent lattice (each Si bonded to four others, like diamond) | many strong Si–Si covalent bonds | highest in the period |
| P₄, S₈, Cl₂ | simple molecules | weak instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces between molecules | low; S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ |
| Ar | single atoms | very weak forces between atoms | lowest |

From sodium to aluminium the metallic bonding gets stronger: each atom donates one, two and then three electrons to the delocalised sea, and the cation charge rises while its radius falls, so the attraction between the cations and the delocalised electrons increases.

For the molecular elements, the size of the molecule decides the strength of the instantaneous dipole-induced dipole (van der Waals) forces. S₈ has the most electrons, so the strongest forces; then P₄; then Cl₂; and single argon atoms have the fewest electrons of all.

## Electrical conductivity

Sodium, magnesium and aluminium conduct because their delocalised electrons move through the lattice when a potential difference is applied. Conductivity increases from Na to Al as the number of delocalised electrons per atom rises. Silicon is a **semiconductor**: its electrons are held in covalent bonds, but a few gain enough energy to move, and the conductivity rises with temperature. Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon have no mobile charge carriers and are insulators.

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining a melting-point graph"}
A graph of melting point against proton number for Period 3 rises from Na to a peak at Si, then falls steeply to P, rises slightly to S and falls again to Cl and Ar. Explain the shape.

1. Na → Al: giant metallic lattices; more delocalised electrons and smaller, more highly charged cations give stronger metallic bonding.
2. Si: giant covalent lattice; melting needs many strong covalent bonds to be broken, so it is the peak.
3. Si → P: a fall to a simple molecular solid; only weak intermolecular forces are overcome, and the covalent bonds inside P₄ stay intact.
4. P → S → Cl → Ar: the size of the instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces follows the number of electrons per particle: S₈ (128) > P₄ (60) > Cl₂ (34) > Ar (18).
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: identifying elements from data"}
Element **E** has a melting point of about 1700 K and conducts electricity slightly, better when warm. Element **G** melts at 317 K and does not conduct. Both are in Period 3. Identify them.

1. **E**: a very high melting point means a giant structure; poor conduction that rises with temperature means a semiconductor, not a metal. **E** is silicon.
2. **G**: a low melting point means a simple molecular solid; no conduction fits a non-metal. Of P₄ and S₈, the lower melting point matches P₄ (fewer electrons, weaker intermolecular forces). **G** is phosphorus.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying atoms get smaller because they have fewer electrons"}
Across the period the number of electrons increases. The atoms shrink because the nuclear charge increases while shielding stays almost constant.
:::

:::mistake{title="Breaking covalent bonds when sulfur or phosphorus melts"}
Melting a simple molecular solid overcomes forces **between** molecules. The covalent bonds within S₈ or P₄ are not broken.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing every ion with every other"}
Cations and anions are two separate isoelectronic series. Compare Na⁺ with Al³⁺, or S²⁻ with Cl⁻, not Al³⁺ with P³⁻ except to point out the extra shell.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling silicon a metal because it conducts"}
Silicon is a metalloid with a giant covalent structure. It is a semiconductor, and its conductivity increases with temperature, unlike a metal's.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 (Paper 2 and Paper 3 Section B often start an inorganic question with this); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. Expect a table of data or a described graph to interpret.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* the trend: say what happens, quoting the elements at either end and any exceptions. *Explain*: link to nuclear charge, shielding and distance for radius and ionisation energy, and to the type of structure and the forces broken for melting point. *Sketch* (described in words): mark the peak at silicon, the fall at phosphorus and the small rise at sulfur. *Deduce* an element: use melting point for the type of structure and conductivity for metallic or not.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Atomic radius decreases from Na to Cl: nuclear charge rises, shielding stays almost the same.
- Cations (Na⁺ to Si⁴⁺) are small and get smaller; anions (P³⁻ to Cl⁻) are larger by one shell and also get smaller.
- First ionisation energy rises across the period, with dips at Al (3p electron) and S (paired 3p electron).
- Melting point peaks at silicon (giant covalent); metals rise Na → Al; simple molecules are low, in the order S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar.
- Na, Mg and Al conduct (delocalised electrons), silicon is a semiconductor, the rest are insulators.
:::
