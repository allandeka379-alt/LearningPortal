## Introduction

Farmers on acidic sandveld soils spread lime because a basic oxide neutralises acid. Rain falling downwind of a coal-fired power station can be acidic because sulfur dioxide, a non-metal oxide, dissolves in it. Aluminium oxide sits in the middle and reacts with both acids and alkalis, which is why the Bayer process can dissolve bauxite in hot sodium hydroxide. These everyday facts are one Period 3 trend: the oxides change from basic to amphoteric to acidic from left to right.

The chlorides show a parallel change, from sodium chloride, which simply dissolves, to silicon tetrachloride, which fumes violently in moist air. This lesson describes both sets of reactions and explains them through bonding. You need the reactions of the elements from the previous lesson, the idea of electronegativity, and pH as a measure of acidity.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Basic oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with acids to form a salt and water, but not with alkalis; typically an ionic metal oxide such as Na₂O or MgO.
:::

:::definition{term="Acidic oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with bases or alkalis to form a salt and water, and often dissolves in water to give an acidic solution; typically a covalent non-metal oxide such as SO₂.
:::

:::definition{term="Amphoteric oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to form salts, for example aluminium oxide.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrolysis"}
A reaction in which water breaks a chemical bond in a compound, for example the reaction of SiCl₄ with water to form SiO₂ and HCl.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: oxides Na₂O to SO₃ and chlorides NaCl to PCl₅ with water and the pH of the solutions, acid-base behaviour including amphoteric Al₂O₃ and Al(OH)₃, explanations through bonding and electronegativity, and suggesting the bonding from properties. Cambridge lists SiO₂ only for its reaction with water; ZIMSEC covers the acid-base behaviour of all the oxides.
:::

## The oxides

| Oxide | Na₂O | MgO | Al₂O₃ | SiO₂ | P₄O₁₀ | SO₂ | SO₃ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bonding and structure | giant ionic | giant ionic | giant ionic (some covalent character) | giant covalent | simple molecular | simple molecular | simple molecular |
| with water | NaOH | Mg(OH)₂, slightly soluble | none | none | H₃PO₄ | H₂SO₃ | H₂SO₄ |
| pH of the mixture | 13-14 | about 9-10 | 7 | 7 | about 1-2 | about 2-3 | about 0-1 |
| acid-base nature | basic | basic | amphoteric | acidic | acidic | acidic | acidic |

### Reactions with water

$$
\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{MgO(s) + H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{P4O10(s) + 6H2O(l) -> 4H3PO4(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{SO2(g) + H2O(l) <=> H2SO3(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{SO3(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO4(aq)}
$$

Magnesium hydroxide is only slightly soluble, so the pH is only about 9-10. Aluminium oxide and silicon dioxide do not react with water (their lattices are too strong), so the water stays at pH 7.

### Reactions with acids and bases

| Oxide or hydroxide | With dilute hydrochloric acid | With aqueous sodium hydroxide |
|---|---|---|
| Na₂O | $\ce{Na2O + 2HCl -> 2NaCl + H2O}$ | no reaction |
| MgO | $\ce{MgO + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2O}$ | no reaction |
| Al₂O₃ | $\ce{Al2O3 + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2O}$ | $\ce{Al2O3 + 2NaOH + 3H2O -> 2NaAl(OH)4}$ |
| SiO₂ | no reaction | $\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}$ (hot, concentrated) |
| P₄O₁₀ | no reaction | $\ce{P4O10 + 12NaOH -> 4Na3PO4 + 6H2O}$ |
| SO₂ | no reaction | $\ce{SO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO3 + H2O}$ |
| SO₃ | no reaction | $\ce{SO3 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + H2O}$ |

The hydroxides follow the same pattern. NaOH and Mg(OH)₂ are bases ($\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + 2H2O}$). Aluminium hydroxide is amphoteric:

$$
\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + 3HCl(aq) -> AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}
$$

$$
\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaAl(OH)4(aq)}
$$

The product with sodium hydroxide is sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate, containing the ion $\ce{[Al(OH)4]-}$.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: pH of a sodium oxide solution]
0.310 g of sodium oxide is dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. Calculate the pH of the solution. (Mr Na₂O = 62.0; Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶)

1. $n(\ce{Na2O}) = 0.310 \div 62.0 = 0.00500$ mol.
2. $\ce{Na2O + H2O -> 2NaOH}$, so $n(\ce{OH-}) = 2 \times 0.00500 = 0.0100$ mol.
3. $[\ce{OH-}] = 0.0100 \div 0.250 = 0.0400$ mol dm⁻³.
4. $[\ce{H+}] = K_\text{w} \div [\ce{OH-}] = 1.00 \times 10^{-14} \div 0.0400 = 2.50 \times 10^{-13}$ mol dm⁻³.
5. pH $= -\log(2.50 \times 10^{-13}) = 12.60$. A strongly alkaline solution, as expected for a basic ionic oxide.
:::

## The chlorides

| Chloride | NaCl | MgCl₂ | AlCl₃ | SiCl₄ | PCl₅ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| bonding and structure | giant ionic | giant ionic | covalent (Al₂Cl₆ dimers); ionic lattice with covalent character in the solid | simple molecular | simple molecular (ionic in the solid) |
| with water | dissolves | dissolves; very slight hydrolysis | hydrolysed | hydrolysed violently | hydrolysed violently |
| pH of the solution | 7 | about 6.5 | about 3 | about 1-2 | about 1-2 |

**Sodium chloride** simply dissolves; the hydrated ions do not react with water: $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$.

**Magnesium chloride** dissolves to give a very slightly acidic solution, because the small Mg²⁺ ion polarises its water ligands a little.

**Aluminium chloride** gives an acidic solution. In plenty of water the small, highly charged Al³⁺ ion is hydrated as $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}$ and it draws electron density from the O–H bonds of its water ligands so strongly that a proton is released:

$$
\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+(aq) <=> [Al(H2O)5(OH)]^2+(aq) + H+(aq)}
$$

With only a little water (or in moist air), steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride are given off: $\ce{AlCl3 + 3H2O -> Al(OH)3 + 3HCl}$.

**Silicon tetrachloride** reacts violently, giving steamy fumes of HCl and a white solid (hydrated silicon dioxide):

$$
\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}
$$

**Phosphorus pentachloride** reacts vigorously, giving a strongly acidic solution of two acids:

$$
\ce{PCl5(s) + 4H2O(l) -> H3PO4(aq) + 5HCl(aq)}
$$

With a limited amount of water the first step gives phosphorus oxychloride: $\ce{PCl5 + H2O -> POCl3 + 2HCl}$.

Why are the covalent chlorides hydrolysed? A water molecule's lone pair attacks the δ+ central atom. Silicon and phosphorus have empty, low-energy orbitals in the third shell that can accept the lone pair, and the polar Si–Cl and P–Cl bonds then break. (Carbon in CCl₄ has no such orbitals available and is shielded by four large chlorine atoms, so CCl₄ is not hydrolysed; this comparison is part of the Group IV lesson.)

:::worked-solution[Example 2: acid from the hydrolysis of phosphorus pentachloride]
0.417 g of PCl₅ is added to excess water. Calculate the volume of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide that would neutralise all the acid formed. (Mr PCl₅ = 208.5)

1. $n(\ce{PCl5}) = 0.417 \div 208.5 = 0.00200$ mol.
2. $\ce{PCl5 + 4H2O -> H3PO4 + 5HCl}$: each mole gives 1 mol H₃PO₄ (3 mol H⁺ when fully neutralised) and 5 mol HCl (5 mol H⁺), so 8 mol of NaOH are needed per mole of PCl₅.
3. $n(\ce{NaOH}) = 8 \times 0.00200 = 0.0160$ mol.
4. $V = 0.0160 \div 0.400 = 0.0400$ dm³ $= 40.0$ cm³.
:::

## Explaining the trends: bonding and electronegativity

Across Period 3 the electronegativity of the element increases (Pauling values from the data booklet: Na 0.9, Mg 1.3, Al 1.5, Si 1.9, P 2.2, S 2.6), so the difference in electronegativity between the element and oxygen (3.5) or chlorine (3.0) gets smaller.

1. **Large difference (Na, Mg)**: electrons are transferred, giving ionic compounds with giant lattices. The oxide ion is a strong base, $\ce{O^2- + H2O -> 2OH-}$, so these oxides are basic; the chlorides just dissolve.
2. **Intermediate (Al)**: Al³⁺ is so small and highly charged that it polarises the anion, so the bonding is ionic with considerable covalent character. The oxide is amphoteric and the chloride is partly covalent and hydrolysed.
3. **Small difference (Si, P, S)**: electrons are shared, giving covalent compounds. The oxides are acidic, and the chlorides are hydrolysed to acidic solutions.

:::example{title="Example 3: suggesting the bonding from properties"}
An oxide **Q** of a Period 3 element melts at 2072 °C, does not dissolve in water, and dissolves in both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide. Suggest its bonding and identity.

1. A very high melting point indicates a giant lattice (ionic or covalent).
2. Reaction with both acid and alkali shows it is amphoteric.
3. The only amphoteric Period 3 oxide is aluminium oxide, so **Q** is Al₂O₃: giant ionic, with some covalent character because Al³⁺ polarises the oxide ion.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling silicon dioxide basic because it is a solid"}
SiO₂ is a giant covalent, **acidic** oxide: it reacts with hot concentrated NaOH, not with acids. It does not dissolve in water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing Al₂O₃ + H₂O → ..."}
Aluminium oxide does not react with water. Its amphoteric nature is shown only with acids and alkalis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying MgCl₂ solution is alkaline"}
MgCl₂ is a salt of a strong acid; its solution is neutral to very slightly acidic (about pH 6.5). Only the oxide and hydroxide are basic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the steamy fumes"}
The observation for SiCl₄ or PCl₅ with water is "steamy (white) fumes of HCl" and, for SiCl₄, a white solid. Mentioning only "it dissolves" scores nothing.
:::

:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* the reaction with water: equation, observation and approximate pH. *Explain* the trend: link the type of bonding to the difference in electronegativity and say what that means for the reaction with water. *Suggest* the bonding from properties: melting point for giant or simple structure; behaviour with water, acid and alkali for ionic or covalent character. *Write equations* to show amphoteric behaviour: one with an acid and one with an alkali.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Oxides: Na₂O and MgO basic (pH 13-14, about 9-10); Al₂O₃ amphoteric and insoluble; SiO₂ acidic and insoluble; P₄O₁₀, SO₂ and SO₃ acidic (pH about 0-3).
- Al₂O₃ and Al(OH)₃ react with acids and with NaOH(aq), forming [Al(OH)₄]⁻.
- Chlorides: NaCl pH 7; MgCl₂ about 6.5; AlCl₃ about 3 (hydrated Al³⁺ releases H⁺); SiCl₄ and PCl₅ hydrolysed violently to HCl, pH about 1-2.
- Across the period, falling electronegativity difference changes the bonding from ionic to covalent, and the compounds from basic or neutral to acidic.
- Use melting points and behaviour with water, acids and alkalis to suggest the bonding in an unknown oxide or chloride.
:::
