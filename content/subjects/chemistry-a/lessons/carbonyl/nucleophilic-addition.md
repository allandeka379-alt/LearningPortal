## Introduction

Alkenes and carbonyl compounds both contain a double bond, yet they react with completely different reagents. Bromine adds across C=C, but it has no effect on the C=O of propanone; cyanide ions add to C=O but ignore C=C. The difference is polarity. The C=C bond is non-polar and attracts electrophiles; the C=O bond is strongly polar, so its carbon attracts **nucleophiles**. Many fruit stones, including those of peaches and apricots, contain natural compounds built on the hydroxynitrile structure you meet in this lesson, which is why they can release cyanide.

This lesson explains nucleophilic addition, the characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones, using hydrogen cyanide as the main example. You should know the naming of aldehydes and ketones, curly-arrow conventions, and the idea of a chiral centre.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nucleophile"}
A species with a lone pair of electrons that it donates to an electron-deficient atom, forming a new covalent bond.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleophilic addition"}
A reaction in which a nucleophile attacks the δ+ carbon of a C=O (or similar) double bond and the π bond breaks, so that two groups add across the bond to form a single product.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydroxynitrile"}
A compound containing both an –OH group and a –C≡N group on the carbon chain; from aldehydes and ketones the two groups are on the same carbon (for example 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Reaction to learn"}
Aldehyde or ketone + HCN, with a little KCN as catalyst, room temperature → hydroxynitrile.

$\ce{CH3CHO + HCN -> CH3CH(OH)CN}$ (2-hydroxypropanenitrile)

$\ce{CH3COCH3 + HCN -> (CH3)2C(OH)CN}$ (2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile)
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the HCN/KCN reaction and its mechanism, the racemic product, and reduction by hydride in outline. Cambridge examines this at AS Level (Papers 1 and 2); ZIMSEC in Form 6 (Papers 1-3).
:::

## The polar carbonyl bond

Oxygen is much more electronegative than carbon, so the electrons of the C=O bond, especially the more exposed π electrons, are drawn towards oxygen: $\ce{C^{\delta+}=O^{\delta-}}$. The carbonyl carbon is trigonal planar (sp², bond angles about 120°), so a nucleophile can approach it from above or below the plane.

## Adding hydrogen cyanide

HCN is a very weak acid and is barely ionised, so on its own it supplies too few cyanide ions to react at a useful rate. A little potassium cyanide is added to provide $\ce{CN-}$ ions, the actual nucleophile; it is regenerated in the last step, so it acts as a catalyst. Conditions are room temperature; the mixture is kept slightly acidic or neutral so that HCN is available to protonate the intermediate. In schools, HCN is never handled: it is extremely toxic, and work with cyanides is done only in supervised laboratories with a fume cupboard.

## The mechanism, step by step

| Step | Species | Curly arrows | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\ce{CN-}$ and the carbonyl compound | from the lone pair on the carbon of $\ce{CN-}$ to the δ+ carbonyl carbon; from the C=O π bond to the oxygen atom | a new C–C bond forms; an **alkoxide intermediate** with a negative charge on oxygen |
| 2 | the alkoxide intermediate and HCN (or $\ce{H+}$/water) | from a lone pair on the negative oxygen to the hydrogen of H–CN; from the H–CN bond to the carbon of the cyanide | the oxygen is protonated, giving the hydroxynitrile; $\ce{CN-}$ is regenerated |

![Nucleophilic addition of HCN to ethanal: the carbon lone pair of CN minus attacks the delta-plus carbonyl carbon while the C=O pi bond moves onto oxygen, giving an alkoxide intermediate CH3CH(O minus)CN; the O minus then takes the H of H-CN, giving 2-hydroxypropanenitrile and regenerating CN minus.](media:diagrams/hcn-ethanal-mechanism.png "Figure: nucleophilic addition of HCN to ethanal, in two steps, with cyanide as the catalyst")

:::example{title="Example 1: the mechanism for ethanal"}
Describe the mechanism of the reaction between ethanal and HCN in the presence of KCN.

1. The C=O bond in ethanal is polar: carbon δ+, oxygen δ−.
2. A cyanide ion uses the lone pair on its **carbon** atom to attack the δ+ carbonyl carbon (curly arrow from the lone pair to that carbon).
3. At the same time the π electrons of C=O move onto the oxygen (curly arrow from the C=O double bond to O).
4. This gives a tetrahedral intermediate, $\ce{CH3CH(O^-)CN}$, with the negative charge on oxygen.
5. A lone pair on the O⁻ attacks the hydrogen of an HCN molecule (arrow from O⁻ to H) and the H–C bond breaks heterolytically (arrow from the H–CN bond to C), releasing a new $\ce{CN-}$.
6. Product: 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$. The cyanide ion used in step 2 is replaced, so KCN is a catalyst.
:::

## Why a racemic mixture forms

The product from ethanal, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$, has a chiral carbon: it carries four different groups (H, $\ce{CH3}$, OH and CN). Because the carbonyl carbon is planar, the cyanide ion is equally likely to attack from either face. Attack from above gives one enantiomer, attack from below gives the other, in equal amounts. The product is a **racemic mixture** (racemate), which does not rotate plane-polarised light because the rotations of the two enantiomers cancel.

Propanone gives 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CN}$, which has two identical methyl groups on the central carbon, so it is not chiral and no optical isomers form. Any aldehyde other than methanal, and any ketone with two different groups, gives a racemate.

## Lengthening the carbon chain

The new C–C bond adds one carbon to the chain, and the nitrile group can be converted into other groups:

| Step | Reagent and conditions | Product from ethanal | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | HCN with KCN catalyst, room temperature | 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$ | nucleophilic addition |
| 2a | heat under reflux with dilute HCl (or $\ce{H2SO4}$) | 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), $\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}$ | hydrolysis |
| 2b | $\ce{LiAlH4}$ in dry ether, or $\ce{H2}$ with Ni | 1-aminopropan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2NH2}$ | reduction |

Hydrolysis: $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH(OH)COOH + NH4+}$

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a two-step synthesis]
5.80 g of propanone is converted into 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile in 80.0% yield, and this is hydrolysed to 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanoic acid, $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)COOH}$, in 70.0% yield. Calculate the mass of acid obtained. $M_\text{r}$: propanone 58, acid 104.

1. n(propanone) = 5.80 ÷ 58 = 0.100 mol.
2. Each step is 1 : 1, so the overall yield is 0.800 × 0.700 = 0.560 (56.0%).
3. n(acid) = 0.100 × 0.560 = 0.0560 mol.
4. Mass = 0.0560 × 104 = 5.82 g.
:::

## Hydride reduction is nucleophilic addition too

$\ce{NaBH4}$ and $\ce{LiAlH4}$ act as sources of the hydride ion, $\ce{H-}$, which is a nucleophile. In outline: the hydride ion attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon while the π electrons move to oxygen, giving an alkoxide intermediate; protonation (from water or dilute acid added afterwards) gives the alcohol. Aldehydes give primary alcohols and ketones secondary alcohols. Reduction of an unsymmetrical ketone such as butanone also gives a racemic product, butan-2-ol.

## Aldehydes react faster than ketones

Two effects make ketones less reactive towards nucleophiles:

1. **Electronic:** alkyl groups donate electron density (positive inductive effect). A ketone has two alkyl groups on the carbonyl carbon, an aldehyde only one, so the ketone's carbon is less δ+ and attracts nucleophiles less strongly.
2. **Steric:** two alkyl groups crowd the carbonyl carbon and hinder the approach of the nucleophile; an aldehyde has a small hydrogen on one side.

Methanal, with two hydrogens, is the most reactive of all.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Electrophilic, not nucleophilic"}
C=O undergoes nucleophilic addition; C=C undergoes electrophilic addition. Do not describe cyanide as an electrophile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Where the arrow starts"}
The first arrow starts from the lone pair on the **carbon** of $\ce{CN-}$, not from the nitrogen and not from the negative charge in general. It ends at the carbonyl carbon, not at the oxygen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting carbons in names"}
The nitrile carbon is counted in the name: $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$ has three carbons, so it is 2-hydroxy**propane**nitrile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Racemate from propanone"}
Propanone's product has two identical methyl groups, so it is not chiral and no racemate forms.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS) and ZIMSEC Papers 1-3. *Describe the mechanism*: name the nucleophile, show (in words) both arrows of step 1 with dipoles and the intermediate with its charge, then the protonation step. *Explain* the racemic mixture: planar C=O, equal attack from both sides, equal amounts of two enantiomers.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Synthesis questions"}
When a chain must grow by one carbon from an aldehyde or ketone, think of HCN/KCN; then hydrolyse (acid) or reduce (amine). From a halogenoalkane, use KCN in ethanol instead.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The C=O bond is polar (C δ+), so aldehydes and ketones undergo nucleophilic addition.
- HCN with a KCN catalyst at room temperature gives hydroxynitriles; CN⁻ is the nucleophile.
- Mechanism: CN⁻ lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon, π electrons move to O (alkoxide intermediate); O⁻ is protonated by HCN, regenerating CN⁻.
- Planar C=O is attacked equally from both faces, so a chiral product forms as a racemic mixture.
- Hydroxynitriles lengthen chains (hydrolysis to hydroxy acids, reduction to amines); NaBH₄/LiAlH₄ reduce by nucleophilic H⁻ addition; aldehydes react faster than ketones.
:::
