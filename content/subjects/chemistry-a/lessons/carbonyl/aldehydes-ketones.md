## Introduction

The smell of a freshly cut lemon, the flavour of vanilla and cinnamon, and the solvent in nail-varnish remover all come from carbonyl compounds. Aldehydes and ketones both contain the carbonyl group, C=O, but they differ in where it sits: at the end of a carbon chain in an aldehyde, inside the chain in a ketone. That one difference decides how easily they are oxidised, and the classic laboratory tests in this lesson exploit it. Diabetes clinics once used Benedict's solution to detect glucose (which has an aldehyde group) in urine for the same reason.

This lesson covers naming, preparation, reduction and the tests used to detect and identify carbonyl compounds. The mechanism by which nucleophiles add to C=O is in the next lesson. You should already know the oxidation of alcohols and the idea of functional-group isomerism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Aldehyde"}
A compound containing the –CHO group: a carbonyl carbon bonded to at least one hydrogen atom (RCHO, or HCHO for methanal).
:::

:::definition{term="Ketone"}
A compound in which the carbonyl carbon is bonded to two carbon atoms (RCOR′).
:::

:::definition{term="Brady's reagent"}
A solution of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH) in methanol and sulfuric acid; it gives a yellow, orange or red precipitate with aldehydes and ketones.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Tests at a glance"}
2,4-DNPH: orange precipitate with **any** C=O (aldehyde or ketone). Tollens', Fehling's, Benedict's and acidified dichromate(VI) react with **aldehydes only**. Alkaline iodine gives pale yellow $\ce{CHI3}$ with $\ce{CH3CO-}$ compounds (ethanal and methyl ketones).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: naming, preparation, reduction and all the tests here. Cambridge names Fehling's and Tollens'; ZIMSEC questions may also use Benedict's solution, which behaves like Fehling's.
:::

## Naming and isomerism

Aldehydes take the ending **-al**; the CHO carbon is always carbon 1, so no number is needed. Ketones take **-one**, with a number when the position is not fixed.

| Formula | Name | Type |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{HCHO}$ | methanal | aldehyde |
| $\ce{CH3CHO}$ | ethanal | aldehyde |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$ | propanal | aldehyde |
| $\ce{CH3COCH3}$ | propanone | ketone |
| $\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}$ | butanone | ketone |
| $\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CH3}$ | pentan-2-one | ketone |
| $\ce{CH3CH2COCH2CH3}$ | pentan-3-one | ketone |
| $\ce{C6H5CHO}$ | benzaldehyde | aromatic aldehyde |

Aldehydes and ketones with the same number of carbons are **functional-group isomers**: propanal and propanone are both $\ce{C3H6O}$. Ketones from five carbons upwards also show positional isomerism (pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one), and branched chains give chain isomers such as 3-methylbutan-2-one.

## Preparation by oxidation

| Starting alcohol | Reagent and conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|
| primary, e.g. propan-1-ol | $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ and dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$, warm; **distil off** the aldehyde as it forms | propanal |
| secondary, e.g. butan-2-ol | $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ and dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$, heat under reflux | butanone |

$\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CH2CHO + H2O}$ and $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH2CH3 + H2O}$

Aldehydes must be distilled out because they are easily oxidised further to carboxylic acids; ketones are not, so reflux is safe.

## Reduction

| Reagent | Conditions | Aldehyde gives | Ketone gives |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{NaBH4}$ | in water or methanol, warm | primary alcohol | secondary alcohol |
| $\ce{LiAlH4}$ | in dry ether, room temperature, then dilute acid | primary alcohol | secondary alcohol |

$\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}$ and $\ce{CH3COCH3 + 2[H] -> CH3CH(OH)CH3}$

$\ce{NaBH4}$ is milder and safer: it does not reduce C=C bonds or carboxylic acids. $\ce{LiAlH4}$ reacts violently with water, so it is used in dry ether.

## Tests for carbonyl compounds

| Test | How it is done | Aldehyde | Ketone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2,4-DNPH (Brady's reagent) | add a few drops of the compound | orange (yellow-orange) precipitate | orange precipitate |
| Fehling's or Benedict's solution | warm in a water bath | blue solution → **red-brown precipitate** ($\ce{Cu2O}$) | stays blue |
| Tollens' reagent, $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$ | warm in a water bath | **silver mirror** (or grey-black precipitate) | no change |
| acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ | warm | orange → green | stays orange |
| alkaline iodine ($\ce{I2}$, NaOH(aq)) | warm | pale yellow $\ce{CHI3}$ only with ethanal | pale yellow $\ce{CHI3}$ with methyl ketones ($\ce{CH3CO-}$) |

In each oxidation test the aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid (or its anion in alkaline reagents) and the metal ion is reduced:

$\ce{RCHO + 2Ag+ + H2O -> RCOOH + 2Ag + 2H+}$ (shown simply; in Tollens' the silver is present as $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$)

$\ce{RCHO + 2Cu^2+ + 2H2O -> RCOOH + Cu2O + 4H+}$ (copper(II) reduced to copper(I) oxide)

Tollens' reagent is made fresh (silver nitrate, a drop of NaOH, then ammonia until the precipitate just dissolves) and the tubes are washed out afterwards, because stored reagent can form explosive residues. Heat with a water bath, never a naked flame, because many carbonyl compounds are flammable.

**The tri-iodomethane test.** Warm with iodine and sodium hydroxide. A compound containing $\ce{CH3CO-}$ gives a pale yellow precipitate of $\ce{CHI3}$ and the sodium salt of an acid with one carbon fewer:

$\ce{CH3COCH2CH3 + 3I2 + 4NaOH -> CHI3 + CH3CH2COONa + 3NaI + 3H2O}$

Ethanal is the only aldehyde that gives the test; propanone, butanone and pentan-2-one do, but pentan-3-one does not.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: silver from Tollens' reagent]
0.880 g of ethanal is warmed with excess Tollens' reagent. Calculate the maximum mass of silver deposited.

1. $M_\text{r}(\ce{CH3CHO}) = 44$; n(ethanal) = 0.880 ÷ 44 = 0.0200 mol.
2. Each aldehyde group reduces two $\ce{Ag+}$ ions: n(Ag) = 2 × 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol.
3. Mass of silver = 0.0400 × 107.9 = 4.32 g.
:::

## Identifying an unknown carbonyl compound

The 2,4-DNPH precipitate (the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone) is a crystalline solid. To identify the original compound in outline:

1. Filter off the precipitate.
2. Recrystallise it from a minimum volume of hot solvent (ethanol), cool, filter and dry it.
3. Measure its melting point and compare with a table of melting points of derivatives; a sharp melting point shows the solid is pure.

Different carbonyl compounds with very similar boiling points usually give derivatives with clearly different melting points, which is why the derivative is used rather than the liquid itself.

:::example{title="Example 2: deducing a structure from tests"}
Compound W, $\ce{C5H10O}$, gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH, does not react with Fehling's solution, and gives a pale yellow precipitate with alkaline iodine. Suggest the structure of W.

1. Orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH: W contains C=O.
2. No reaction with Fehling's: W is a ketone, not an aldehyde.
3. Yellow precipitate with alkaline iodine: W contains $\ce{CH3CO-}$.
4. A methyl ketone with five carbons: pentan-2-one, $\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CH3}$, or 3-methylbutan-2-one, $\ce{CH3COCH(CH3)2}$. The melting point of the 2,4-DNPH derivative distinguishes the two; pentan-3-one is ruled out because it lacks $\ce{CH3CO-}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="2,4-DNPH does not distinguish"}
2,4-DNPH shows that C=O is present but gives an orange precipitate with both aldehydes and ketones. Use Tollens' or Fehling's to tell them apart.
:::

:::mistake{title="Colour of Fehling's result"}
The positive result is a **red-brown (brick-red) precipitate**, not a red solution. Ketones leave the solution blue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Which product forms from reduction"}
Reduction of a ketone gives a **secondary** alcohol, never a carboxylic acid; reduction removes the C=O, oxidation keeps it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Iodoform needs CH₃CO–"}
Butanal does not give the iodoform test; the carbonyl must carry a methyl group directly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-4 and Cambridge Papers 1-3. *Describe* a test with reagent, conditions (warm in a water bath) and the result for both possibilities. *Deduce* a structure by using each test result in turn, as in Example 2, and state what each result shows.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Equations"}
Use [O] for oxidations by dichromate(VI) and [H] for reductions by NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄; balance hydrogens carefully: RCHO + 2[H] → RCH₂OH.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Aldehydes (-al, RCHO) have C=O at the chain end; ketones (-one, RCOR′) inside the chain; same-carbon aldehydes and ketones are functional-group isomers.
- Primary alcohols give aldehydes (distil); secondary alcohols give ketones; NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ reduce them back to primary and secondary alcohols.
- 2,4-DNPH gives an orange precipitate with any carbonyl compound.
- Only aldehydes give a silver mirror with Tollens', a red-brown precipitate with Fehling's or Benedict's and turn dichromate(VI) green.
- CH₃CO– compounds give pale yellow CHI₃ with alkaline iodine; derivative melting points identify a particular compound.
:::
