## Introduction

Is the orange colour in a soft drink a permitted food dye? Does a tablet sold at a market stall really contain the drug on its label? Has a reaction in the laboratory finished? Chromatography answers all of these questions cheaply, with tiny samples, and is one of the most used techniques in analytical chemistry. You probably first met it as ink spreading up a strip of filter paper.

This lesson explains how paper, thin-layer and column chromatography separate substances, how Rf values are calculated and used, and why different substances travel different distances. You should know intermolecular forces, polarity and hydrogen bonding, and the idea of partition between two solvents.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stationary phase"}
The phase that does not move: water held in the cellulose fibres of chromatography paper, or a thin layer of silica (silicon dioxide) or aluminium oxide on a glass, plastic or metal plate.
:::

:::definition{term="Mobile phase"}
The solvent (polar or non-polar) that moves through or over the stationary phase, carrying the components of the mixture with it.
:::

:::definition{term="Partition"}
The distribution of a solute between two immiscible phases (for example water in the paper and the moving solvent) according to its relative solubility in each.
:::

:::definition{term="Adsorption"}
The attraction of solute molecules to the surface of a solid stationary phase, such as silica, by intermolecular forces.
:::

:::definition{term="Retardation factor, Rf"}
$R_\text{f} = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by the spot from the baseline}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front from the baseline}}$; it lies between 0 and 1 and has no units.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: principles, Rf values and interpretation (ZIMSEC Form 6, 6.4.2.3; Cambridge A Level, 37.1, Paper 4 and Paper 5). ZIMSEC also names column and ion-exchange chromatography and asks for applications in industry and medicine. Gas-liquid chromatography and HPLC are in the next lesson.
:::

## How chromatography separates

Every form of chromatography has a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**. As the mobile phase moves, each component of the mixture is continually shared between the two phases. A component that spends more time in the mobile phase moves faster and further; one held more strongly by the stationary phase moves slowly.

- In **paper chromatography** the main process is **partition**: the stationary phase is water bound to the cellulose, and components distribute between this water and the moving solvent.
- In **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)** the main process is **adsorption**: components are attracted to the polar surface of silica or alumina (which carries –OH groups) and are carried along by the solvent.

## Running a chromatogram

1. Draw a **baseline** in **pencil** about 1-2 cm from the bottom (ink would itself separate and run).
2. Spot small, concentrated samples of the mixture and of known reference substances on the baseline, spaced apart. Let each spot dry.
3. Stand the paper or plate in a beaker or tank with solvent **below** the baseline (otherwise the spots dissolve into the solvent), and cover it so that the air is saturated with solvent vapour.
4. Remove it before the solvent reaches the top; immediately mark the **solvent front** in pencil.
5. Dry it and **locate** colourless spots: spray with **ninhydrin** and warm (amino acids give purple spots), view under **ultraviolet light** (TLC plates containing a fluorescent indicator show dark spots), or expose to iodine vapour (brown spots).
6. Measure from the baseline to the **centre** of each spot and to the solvent front; calculate Rf.

Safety: many solvents are flammable and harmful; work with no naked flames and in a fume cupboard; ninhydrin is toxic and should be sprayed with gloves in a fume cupboard.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating Rf values]
A TLC plate is developed until the solvent front is 8.0 cm above the baseline. Two spots from a mixture have travelled 3.6 cm and 5.6 cm.

1. Rf of the first spot = 3.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.45.
2. Rf of the second spot = 5.6 ÷ 8.0 = 0.70.
3. The second component interacts less strongly with the stationary phase (or is more soluble in the mobile phase).
:::

## Using Rf values

- **Identification.** Under the same conditions (same stationary phase, solvent and temperature) a substance always has the same Rf. Compare the unknown with reference spots run **on the same plate**; equal Rf values suggest the same substance. Two different substances can have similar Rf values in one solvent, so confirm with a second solvent.
- **Purity.** A pure substance gives **one** spot. Extra spots show impurities (or that a reaction has not finished).
- **Following a reaction.** Spot the starting material, the reaction mixture and a co-spot of both; the starting-material spot fades as the product spot grows.

:::example{title="Example 2: which dyes are in a sweet colouring?"}
A food colouring, S, and three permitted reference dyes P, Q and R were run on the same paper. Solvent front: 7.5 cm.

| Substance | Distance moved / cm | Rf |
|---|---|---|
| P | 1.8 | 0.24 |
| Q | 4.2 | 0.56 |
| R | 6.0 | 0.80 |
| S, spot 1 | 1.8 | 0.24 |
| S, spot 2 | 6.0 | 0.80 |

1. S gives two spots, so it is a mixture of two dyes.
2. Their Rf values match P and R, so S probably contains P and R and not Q.
3. To be more confident, repeat with a different solvent and check that the spots still match.
:::

## Explaining different Rf values

The Rf of a substance depends on the balance between its attraction to the stationary phase and its solubility in the mobile phase.

- Silica and alumina are **polar** (surface –OH groups). Polar molecules, especially those that can hydrogen bond (–OH, –NH₂, –COOH), are **adsorbed strongly** and move slowly: **low Rf**.
- Non-polar molecules are adsorbed weakly and travel with the solvent: **high Rf**.
- Changing to a **more polar solvent** competes more strongly for the silica surface and dissolves polar compounds better, so all Rf values tend to increase.

For example, on silica with a non-polar solvent, benzoic acid (hydrogen bonding –COOH) has a lower Rf than methyl benzoate (an ester, which cannot donate a hydrogen bond).

## Two-way chromatography

Some mixtures, such as the amino acids from hydrolysing a protein, contain components that do not separate well in any single solvent. In **two-way chromatography** the sample is spotted in one corner of a square sheet and run in one solvent; the sheet is dried, turned through 90°, and run in a second solvent. Spots that overlapped after the first run are separated in the second, because their Rf values differ in the second solvent. Ninhydrin then locates the amino acids.

## Column chromatography

To **separate** (not just analyse) larger amounts, the stationary phase (silica or alumina) is packed into a vertical glass column. The mixture is added at the top and solvent (the **eluent**) is run through. Components move down at different speeds and are collected separately as **fractions** at the bottom; TLC of each fraction shows which contain which component. It is used to purify products in organic synthesis and natural products from plant extracts.

**Ion-exchange chromatography** (ZIMSEC detail) uses a resin with charged groups as the stationary phase. Ions in the mixture are attracted to the oppositely charged groups and held with different strengths, so they are released at different times as the eluent passes. It is used, for example, to separate amino acids by their charge and to purify water.

## Applications

In general terms: checking food colourings and flavourings; testing tablets and body fluids for drugs (often TLC followed by more precise methods); identifying amino acids from hydrolysed proteins; monitoring reactions and purifying products in the pharmaceutical industry; forensic comparison of inks and dyes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Baseline in ink or below the solvent"}
Use pencil, and keep the solvent level below the baseline.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring to the top of the paper"}
Rf uses the distance to the **solvent front**, which must be marked as soon as the plate is removed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rf greater than 1"}
A spot cannot move further than the solvent. If you get Rf > 1, the ratio is upside down.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing Rf values from different conditions"}
Rf depends on the stationary phase, solvent and temperature; compare only spots run together.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Separation depends on how strongly each component is held by the stationary phase compared with the mobile phase (partition in paper, adsorption in TLC).
- Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front (0 to 1, no units).
- Identify by matching Rf with references on the same plate; one spot suggests a pure substance.
- On polar silica, polar and hydrogen-bonding compounds have low Rf values.
- Two-way chromatography separates overlapping spots; column and ion-exchange chromatography separate larger amounts.
:::
