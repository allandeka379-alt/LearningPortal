## Introduction

How do chemists know that electrons are arranged in shells and sub-shells? Nobody can see an orbital. The strongest evidence comes from measuring how much energy it takes to pull electrons off atoms one at a time. The pattern of those **ionisation energies** reveals the shells, the sub-shells and even the pairing of electrons.

Ionisation energies also explain reactivity. Potassium reacts violently with water, while magnesium needs steam; caesium, at the bottom of Group 1, is more reactive still. Each trend follows the energy needed to remove the outer electron.

You should already know the filling order of sub-shells, how to write configurations such as [Ne] 3s² 3p¹, and the meaning of shielding.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="First ionisation energy"}
The energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of **gaseous** atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
:::

:::definition{term="Second ionisation energy"}
The energy required to remove one electron from each ion in one mole of gaseous 1+ ions to form one mole of gaseous 2+ ions.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Equations to learn (sodium as the example)"}
First: $\ce{Na(g) -> Na+(g) + e-}$

Second: $\ce{Na+(g) -> Na^2+(g) + e-}$

Third: $\ce{Na^2+(g) -> Na^3+(g) + e-}$

Ionisation energies are always **endothermic** (positive, in kJ mol⁻¹) and always need **gaseous** species. The $n$th ionisation energy removes one electron from a gaseous $(n-1)+$ ion.
:::

## The four factors

Every explanation in this topic uses some of these four ideas. Name them explicitly.

1. **Nuclear charge.** More protons attract the electrons more strongly, so the ionisation energy rises.
2. **Distance (atomic radius).** The further the outer electron is from the nucleus, the weaker the attraction, so the ionisation energy falls.
3. **Shielding.** Inner electrons repel the outer electron and reduce the attraction it feels. More inner shells mean more shielding and a lower ionisation energy. Electrons in the same shell shield each other only slightly; a filled inner s sub-shell shields the p electrons of the same shell a little.
4. **Spin-pair repulsion.** An electron that shares an orbital with another is repelled by it, so it is easier to remove than an unpaired electron in an otherwise similar position.

## Trend down a group

Down Group 1 the first ionisation energy falls: Li 519, Na 494, K 418, Rb 403, Cs 376 kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet values). Although the nuclear charge increases, each element has an extra shell, so the outer electron is **further away** and **better shielded**. These two effects outweigh the increased nuclear charge. The same explains why reactivity of Group 1 and Group 2 metals increases down the group.

## Trend across a period

Across a period the first ionisation energy generally **increases**: the nuclear charge rises, the outer electrons are in the same shell (similar distance) and shielding is almost unchanged. But there are two dips, best seen in Period 3:

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 494 | 736 | 577 | 786 | 1060 | 1000 | 1260 | 1520 |
| Outer configuration | 3s¹ | 3s² | 3s² 3p¹ | 3s² 3p² | 3s² 3p³ | 3s² 3p⁴ | 3s² 3p⁵ | 3s² 3p⁶ |

![First ionisation energy across Period 3 in kJ per mole: Na 494, Mg 736, Al 577, Si 786, P 1060, S 1000, Cl 1260, Ar 1520. The general rise is interrupted by a dip from Mg to Al (the 3p electron is higher in energy than 3s) and a dip from P to S (the first paired 3p electron is repelled).](media:diagrams/ionisation-energy-period-3.png "Figure: first ionisation energies across Period 3, with the two dips explained")

**Dip 1, Mg to Al (Group 2 to Group 13).** Aluminium's outer electron is in a 3p orbital, which is of higher energy than 3s and slightly further from the nucleus; it is also partly shielded by the 3s² electrons. So, despite the extra proton, less energy is needed.

**Dip 2, P to S (Group 15 to Group 16).** In phosphorus the three 3p electrons are unpaired, one per orbital. In sulfur the fourth 3p electron must pair up. The **spin-pair repulsion** in that orbital makes one electron easier to remove.

Period 2 shows exactly the same pattern: boron is lower than beryllium, and oxygen is lower than nitrogen.

After the noble gas (argon) there is a big fall to potassium, which starts a new shell.

## Successive ionisation energies

Removing electrons one by one, each ionisation energy is larger than the one before, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion: the same nuclear charge holds fewer electrons, there is less repulsion between them, and the ion is smaller.

The key evidence for shells is the **big jump** that appears when the next electron has to come from a shell nearer the nucleus. For sodium (data booklet values for the first four; the rest are standard data):

| Electron removed | 1st | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | … | 9th | 10th | 11th |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IE / kJ mol⁻¹ | 494 | 4560 | 6940 | 9540 | 13 400 | … | 28 900 | 141 000 | 159 000 |

There is one huge jump after the 1st (3s → 2p electrons) and another after the 9th (shell 2 → shell 1). The pattern 1, 8, 2 matches sodium's 2,8,1 arrangement read from the outside inwards. A graph of log₁₀(IE) against the number of electrons removed shows the jumps clearly as steps.

![Graph of log10 of the successive ionisation energies of sodium against the number of electrons removed: one electron is removed easily (494 kJ per mole), then eight electrons with slowly rising values from 4560 to 28 900, then a huge jump to 141 000 and 159 000 for the last two, showing the 2,8,1 shell structure.](media:diagrams/ionisation-energy-sodium-successive.png "Figure: successive ionisation energies of sodium; each step up marks a new shell closer to the nucleus")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: deducing the group from successive ionisation energies]
The first four ionisation energies of element Q are 590, 1150, 4940 and 6480 kJ mol⁻¹. Deduce the group of Q.

1. Compare each value with the one before: 1150 ÷ 590 ≈ 1.95; 4940 ÷ 1150 ≈ 4.30; 6480 ÷ 4940 ≈ 1.31.
2. The largest jump is between the **2nd and 3rd** ionisation energies.
3. So two electrons are in the outer shell, and the third comes from an inner shell, closer to the nucleus and less shielded.
4. Q is in **Group 2** (it is calcium, [Ar] 4s²).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: energy to form a gaseous ion]
Use the data booklet values for aluminium (577, 1820 and 2740 kJ mol⁻¹) to find the energy needed for $\ce{Al(g) -> Al^3+(g) + 3e-}$.

1. Forming $\ce{Al^3+}$ from the atom needs the first, second and third ionisation energies.
2. Sum: $577 + 1820 + 2740 = 5137\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
3. The fourth ionisation energy (11 600 kJ mol⁻¹) is not included: it would remove a 2p electron from the inner shell, which is why Al never forms $\ce{Al^4+}$.
:::

## Reading a configuration from the data

The size of the jumps tells you the group; finer detail tells you the sub-shells. In the first ionisation energies across a period, the dip at Group 13 shows the start of the p sub-shell, and the dip at Group 16 shows pairing in the p orbitals. For successive values within one shell, a smaller jump (for example after the second electron of aluminium, 3p to 3s) is evidence for sub-shells.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the state symbols"}
$\ce{Mg -> Mg+ + e-}$ scores nothing. Write $\ce{Mg(g) -> Mg+(g) + e-}$: ionisation energies refer to gaseous species.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing the second ionisation energy from the atom"}
$\ce{Ca(g) -> Ca^2+(g) + 2e-}$ is the **sum** of the first and second ionisation energies. The second is $\ce{Ca+(g) -> Ca^2+(g) + e-}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining the Group 13 dip by pairing"}
The dip from Mg to Al is about the 3p sub-shell (higher energy, extra shielding by 3s). Pairing explains the Group 16 dip.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'more shells, so more nuclear charge'"}
Down a group the nuclear charge does increase, but the extra distance and shielding win. Give all three factors.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 often gives four or five successive values and asks for the group: find the biggest **ratio**, not just the biggest difference, and count the electrons before it.
:::

:::exam-tip
"**Explain** the trend" questions on Papers 2 (and ZIMSEC Paper 3) need the factor and the direction: "increased nuclear charge; same shell so similar shielding; outer electrons more strongly attracted; more energy needed".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **write an equation** for the third ionisation energy of an element, the reactant is the gaseous 2+ ion and only one electron is shown.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- First ionisation energy: energy to remove one electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms, $\ce{X(g) -> X+(g) + e-}$.
- Ionisation energy depends on nuclear charge, distance, shielding and spin-pair repulsion.
- It decreases down a group (distance and shielding win) and generally increases across a period.
- Dips at Group 13 (new p sub-shell) and Group 16 (first paired p electron).
- Successive ionisation energies rise steadily, with big jumps when an inner shell is entered: these jumps reveal the group.
:::
