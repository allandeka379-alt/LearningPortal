## Introduction

At O-Level you wrote sodium as 2,8,1. That simple picture explains a lot, but it cannot explain why chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, why iron forms both $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, or why the first ionisation energy dips at oxygen. For that you need the A-Level model: each shell is divided into **sub-shells**, and each sub-shell into **orbitals**.

This matters well beyond the exam. The chromium plated onto car parts and the nickel and platinum group metals mined on the Great Dyke owe their colours, magnetism and catalytic behaviour to partly filled d sub-shells. Electron configurations are the language in which all of that chemistry is explained.

You should already know the 2,8,8 arrangement for the first twenty elements and how to count electrons in atoms and ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Shell (principal quantum number, n)"}
A main energy level for electrons, numbered $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots$ outwards from the nucleus. Shell $n$ can hold a maximum of $2n^2$ electrons.
:::

:::definition{term="Sub-shell"}
A group of orbitals of equal energy within a shell, labelled s, p, d (and f). Shell 1 has only 1s; shell 2 has 2s and 2p; shell 3 has 3s, 3p and 3d.
:::

:::definition{term="Orbital"}
A region of space around the nucleus in which there is a high probability of finding an electron. Each orbital holds a maximum of **two** electrons, which must have **opposite spins**.
:::

:::definition{term="Ground state"}
The lowest-energy electron arrangement of an atom or ion, with the electrons in the lowest available orbitals.
:::

:::definition{term="Free radical"}
A species with one or more unpaired electrons, such as a chlorine atom, Cl•, or a methyl radical, CH₃•. The dot stands for the unpaired electron.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Sub-shell capacities"}
| Sub-shell | Number of orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |
| f | 7 | 14 |

Filling order up to krypton: **1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p**.

## Shapes of s and p orbitals

An **s orbital** is spherical, centred on the nucleus. The 2s orbital is larger than 1s, and 3s larger still, because the electrons are on average further out.

A **p orbital** is dumbbell-shaped: two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus. There are three p orbitals in each p sub-shell, at right angles to each other along the x, y and z axes: $p_x$, $p_y$ and $p_z$. They have equal energy.

The shapes of d orbitals are met in the lesson on transition elements.

## Filling the orbitals

Three rules decide the ground-state arrangement:

1. **Lowest energy first.** Electrons enter the lowest-energy sub-shell available. The 4s sub-shell is slightly lower in energy than 3d in potassium and calcium, so 4s fills **before** 3d.
2. **Two per orbital, opposite spins.** In electrons-in-boxes diagrams, each box is an orbital and the electrons are drawn as ↑ and ↓.
3. **Singly first in equal-energy orbitals.** In a p or d sub-shell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before any pairing occurs. Two electrons in the same orbital repel each other (spin-pair repulsion), so spreading out gives a lower energy.

:::example{title="Electrons in boxes for nitrogen and oxygen"}
Nitrogen, 7 electrons: 1s [↑↓] 2s [↑↓] 2p [↑][↑][↑]. The three 2p electrons are unpaired, one in each orbital, with parallel spins.

Oxygen, 8 electrons: 1s [↑↓] 2s [↑↓] 2p [↑↓][↑][↑]. The fourth 2p electron must pair up, so oxygen has one paired 2p orbital and two unpaired electrons. The extra repulsion in that pair is why oxygen's first ionisation energy is lower than nitrogen's.
:::

## Writing configurations

The **full** configuration lists every sub-shell with a superscript for its electrons. The **shorthand** form replaces the inner electrons with the noble gas symbol in square brackets.

| Element | Z | Full configuration | Shorthand |
|---|:---:|---|---|
| sodium | 11 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ | [Ne] 3s¹ |
| phosphorus | 15 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³ | [Ne] 3s² 3p³ |
| calcium | 20 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² | [Ar] 4s² |
| vanadium | 23 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d³ 4s² | [Ar] 3d³ 4s² |
| bromine | 35 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵ | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵ |

Once the 3d sub-shell holds electrons it is usual to write 3d before 4s, grouping sub-shells by shell, as above. Both orders are accepted if the numbers are right.

### Two exceptions: chromium and copper

| Element | Expected | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| chromium (24) | [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² | **[Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹** |
| copper (29) | [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s² | **[Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹** |

In chromium one 4s electron moves into 3d to give six unpaired electrons, five of them in a half-filled 3d sub-shell; in copper the 3d sub-shell becomes completely filled. The 3d and 4s energies are so close that these arrangements, with less spin-pair repulsion in the case of chromium, are lower in energy overall. You need to learn the two exceptions; you do not need to justify them in detail.

## Configurations of ions

For main-group ions, add or remove electrons from the highest-energy sub-shell: $\ce{Cl-}$ is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁶, and $\ce{Al^3+}$ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.

For **transition-metal ions, the 4s electrons are removed before the 3d electrons**. Once the 3d orbitals are occupied, 4s is the outer sub-shell of the atom, so it is lost first even though it filled first.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: configurations of $\ce{Fe}$, $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$]
1. Iron has $Z = 26$: [Ar] has 18 electrons, leaving 8 to place: **[Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²**.
2. $\ce{Fe^2+}$ has lost two electrons, both from 4s: **[Ar] 3d⁶**.
3. $\ce{Fe^3+}$ loses one more, now from 3d: **[Ar] 3d⁵**.
4. In boxes, 3d⁵ is [↑][↑][↑][↑][↑]: five unpaired electrons in a half-filled sub-shell.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: identifying an ion from its configuration]
An ion $\ce{M^2+}$ has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁷. Identify M.

1. Count the electrons in the ion: $2 + 2 + 6 + 2 + 6 + 7 = 25$.
2. The atom has two more electrons than the 2+ ion: $25 + 2 = 27$.
3. The element with proton number 27 is **cobalt**: Co is [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s², and $\ce{Co^2+}$ is [Ar] 3d⁷ because the two 4s electrons are lost first.
:::

## Unpaired electrons and free radicals

Electrons-in-boxes diagrams show how many electrons are unpaired. A chlorine atom, [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵, has one unpaired 3p electron, so a chlorine atom is a free radical. When ultraviolet light splits a $\ce{Cl2}$ molecule, each atom keeps one electron of the shared pair: Cl₂ → 2Cl•. Radicals are reactive because the unpaired electron readily pairs with an electron from another species; you will meet this again in free-radical substitution of alkanes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Removing 3d electrons before 4s"}
$\ce{Fe^2+}$ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². Transition-metal atoms lose their 4s electrons first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Pairing electrons too early"}
Carbon's 2p electrons go into two separate orbitals with parallel spins: [↑][↑][ ], not [↑↓][ ][ ].
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting chromium and copper"}
Cr is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. These are standard exam questions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing an orbital with a sub-shell"}
A p sub-shell holds six electrons; a single p orbital holds only two.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 (ZIMSEC and Cambridge) regularly tests configurations of transition-metal ions and counts of unpaired electrons. Write the atom first, then remove 4s electrons, then 3d.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **draw** or **complete** electrons in boxes, show arrows with opposite spins in a full orbital and parallel spins in singly occupied orbitals, and label each sub-shell.
:::

:::exam-tip
To **deduce** an element from a configuration, add up the superscripts, then adjust for the charge before going to the Periodic Table.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Shells contain sub-shells (s, p, d) made of orbitals; each orbital holds two electrons of opposite spin.
- s holds 2, p holds 6 and d holds 10 electrons; s orbitals are spherical and p orbitals dumbbell-shaped.
- Filling order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; equal-energy orbitals fill singly before pairing.
- Cr is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
- Transition-metal atoms lose 4s electrons before 3d electrons when they form ions.
- A free radical has an unpaired electron.
:::
