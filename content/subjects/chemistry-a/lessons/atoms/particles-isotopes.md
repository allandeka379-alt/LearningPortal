## Introduction

Every chemical idea in A-Level Chemistry, from bonding to reaction mechanisms, rests on one picture: a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by electrons. Chemistry is about what the electrons do; the nucleus decides how many electrons there are and how strongly they are held.

Isotopes are part of everyday science in Zimbabwe. Hospitals use radioactive isotopes such as iodine-131 to diagnose and treat thyroid conditions, and agricultural researchers use isotopes of nitrogen and phosphorus to follow how a maize crop takes up fertiliser. In each case the isotope behaves chemically like the ordinary element, which is exactly why it can be used as a tracer.

From O-Level you should already know that atoms contain protons, neutrons and electrons, and the meaning of proton number and nucleon number. This lesson takes those ideas to A-Level depth: the relative masses and charges, the behaviour of particle beams in an electric field, counting particles in ions, and the trends in the sizes of atoms and ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Proton number (atomic number), Z"}
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It identifies the element: every atom of carbon has $Z = 6$.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleon number (mass number), A"}
The total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus of an atom.
:::

:::definition{term="Isotopes"}
Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so they have the same proton number but different nucleon numbers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The three sub-atomic particles"}
| Particle | Relative mass | Relative charge | Location |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| proton | 1 | +1 | nucleus |
| neutron | 1 | 0 | nucleus |
| electron | 1/1836 (about 0.0005) | −1 | shells (orbitals) around the nucleus |

Number of neutrons $= A - Z$.
:::

## The nuclear atom

The nucleus contains all the protons and neutrons, so it contains almost all of the mass of the atom and all of its positive charge. Yet it is extremely small: the nucleus is roughly 10⁻¹⁵ m across while the whole atom is about 10⁻¹⁰ m, so the atom is mostly empty space.

The electrons move around the nucleus in shells (energy levels). They carry all the negative charge but only a tiny fraction of the mass. In a neutral atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges cancel.

Summary of the distribution:

- **Mass**: concentrated in the nucleus (protons and neutrons).
- **Positive charge**: concentrated in the nucleus (protons).
- **Negative charge**: spread through the much larger volume occupied by the electrons.

## Writing and reading isotope notation

A nuclide is written with the nucleon number at the top left and the proton number at the bottom left: $\ce{^{37}_{17}Cl}$ is a chlorine atom with 17 protons and $37 - 17 = 20$ neutrons. A charge goes at the top right.

For an ion, the number of electrons changes but the nucleus does not:

- a positive ion has **lost** electrons: electrons $= Z - \text{charge}$;
- a negative ion has **gained** electrons: electrons $= Z + \text{size of charge}$.

| Species | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $\ce{^{23}_{11}Na}$ | 11 | 12 | 11 |
| $\ce{^{23}_{11}Na+}$ | 11 | 12 | 10 |
| $\ce{^{37}_{17}Cl-}$ | 17 | 20 | 18 |
| $\ce{^{56}_{26}Fe^3+}$ | 26 | 30 | 23 |
| $\ce{^{16}_{8}O^2-}$ | 8 | 8 | 10 |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: counting particles in $\ce{^{81}_{35}Br-}$ and $\ce{^{52}_{24}Cr^3+}$]
1. Bromide: the proton number is 35, so there are **35 protons**.
2. Neutrons $= A - Z = 81 - 35 =$ **46 neutrons**.
3. The ion has a 1− charge, so it has gained one electron: $35 + 1 =$ **36 electrons**.
4. Chromium(III): $Z = 24$, so **24 protons**; neutrons $= 52 - 24 =$ **28 neutrons**.
5. A 3+ charge means three electrons have been lost: $24 - 3 =$ **21 electrons**.
:::

## Isotopes: same chemistry, different physics

Chlorine has two common isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. Both have 17 protons and 17 electrons in the neutral atom, arranged in the same way. Chemical reactions involve electrons only, and the electron arrangement is decided by the number of protons, so **isotopes have the same chemical properties**: $\ce{^{35}Cl2}$ and $\ce{^{37}Cl2}$ both bleach damp litmus and both react with sodium to give sodium chloride.

Physical properties that depend on **mass** are different. Molecules made of heavier isotopes have a greater density, diffuse a little more slowly and, for light elements, have slightly different melting and boiling points. "Heavy water", $\ce{D2O}$ (deuterium is hydrogen-2), is denser than ordinary water and boils at a slightly higher temperature, yet it takes part in the same reactions.

:::note{title="Where Ar values come from"}
Relative atomic masses such as 35.5 for chlorine are weighted averages of the isotopic masses. That calculation belongs to the lesson on relative masses and the mole.
:::

## Beams of particles in an electric field

When beams of protons, neutrons and electrons, all travelling at the same speed, pass between two charged plates, they behave differently.

| Particle | Direction of deflection | Size of deflection |
|---|---|---|
| proton (+1) | towards the **negative** plate | small (large mass) |
| electron (−1) | towards the **positive** plate | large: about 1836 times more than a proton at the same speed (tiny mass) |
| neutron (0) | not deflected | none (no charge) |

Two ideas explain the table. The **direction** depends on the sign of the charge: opposite charges attract. The **size** of the deflection is proportional to the charge-to-mass ratio: a larger charge means a larger force, and a larger mass means a smaller acceleration for that force.

:::example{title="Predicting the deflection of ions"}
A beam of $\ce{^1H+}$ ions and a beam of $\ce{^4He^2+}$ ions travel at the same speed between charged plates. Both bend towards the negative plate. Charge-to-mass ratios: $\ce{H+}$ is $1/1 = 1$; $\ce{He^2+}$ is $2/4 = 0.5$. So the $\ce{He^2+}$ beam is deflected **half as much** as the $\ce{H+}$ beam. A beam of $\ce{^2H+}$ (deuterium) ions, ratio $1/2$, would be deflected by the same angle as the $\ce{He^2+}$ beam.
:::

## Atomic and ionic radius: the trends

**Across a period** (for example sodium to chlorine), atomic radius **decreases**. The nuclear charge increases by one proton each step, but the extra electrons go into the same shell, so shielding hardly changes. The outer electrons are pulled closer to the nucleus.

**Down a group**, atomic radius **increases**: each element has one more occupied shell, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and better shielded by the inner shells. This outweighs the increase in nuclear charge.

**Ions compared with their atoms:**

- Positive ions are **smaller** than their atoms. $\ce{Na+}$ has lost its only 3s electron, so it has one shell fewer, and the remaining electrons are pulled in by a nuclear charge that now outnumbers them.
- Negative ions are **larger** than their atoms. In $\ce{Cl-}$ the extra electron increases repulsion between electrons in the outer shell while the nuclear charge is unchanged, so the electron cloud expands.

**Isoelectronic ions** (same number of electrons) shrink as the nuclear charge rises: $\ce{N^3-} > \ce{O^2-} > \ce{F-} > \ce{Na+} > \ce{Mg^2+} > \ce{Al^3+}$. All have 10 electrons in the arrangement 2,8, but aluminium has 13 protons pulling on them while nitrogen has only 7.

:::example{title="Explaining why Mg²⁺ is smaller than Na⁺"}
Both ions have 10 electrons in two shells. Magnesium has 12 protons and sodium 11, so in $\ce{Mg^2+}$ the same number of electrons, with similar shielding, is attracted by a greater nuclear charge and held closer to the nucleus. Hence $\ce{Mg^2+}$ has the smaller radius.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding electrons for a positive ion"}
A 2+ charge means two electrons have been **lost**, not gained. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has $20 - 2 = 18$ electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying isotopes have different chemical properties"}
Isotopes have the same number and arrangement of electrons, so their chemical reactions are the same. Only mass-dependent physical properties (density, rate of diffusion) differ.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying electrons are deflected less because they are small"}
Electrons are deflected **more**, because their mass is tiny compared with a proton's while the size of the charge is the same. Mention the charge-to-mass ratio.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining the trend across a period with extra shells"}
Across a period no new shell is added. The radius falls because nuclear charge rises while shielding stays about the same.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 (both boards) often tests particle counting and deflection; ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 2 ask you to **explain** deflection or radius trends. Use the words *charge*, *mass* (or *charge-to-mass ratio*), *nuclear charge*, *shielding* and *distance* explicitly.
:::

:::exam-tip
**State** the relative mass and charge as numbers (1, 1/1836, +1, −1, 0). To **explain** why isotopes react in the same way, link "same number of electrons / same electron configuration" to "chemical reactions involve electrons".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **deduce** the identity of a species from particle counts, use the proton number to find the element first, then the electrons for the charge, then the neutrons for the isotope.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds almost all the mass and all the positive charge; the atom is mostly empty space.
- Proton: mass 1, charge +1; neutron: mass 1, charge 0; electron: mass 1/1836, charge −1.
- Neutrons $= A - Z$; ions have gained or lost electrons but never protons.
- Isotopes have the same proton number and different neutron numbers: same chemistry, different mass-dependent physical properties.
- In an electric field, deflection direction depends on the sign of the charge and its size on charge ÷ mass; neutrons are not deflected.
- Atomic radius falls across a period and rises down a group; cations are smaller and anions larger than their atoms.
:::
