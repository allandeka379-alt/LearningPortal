## Introduction

Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon, yet one is the hardest natural substance and the other is soft enough to write with and conducts electricity. Sand (silicon dioxide) melts at well over a thousand degrees while iodine turns to a purple vapour with gentle warming. The difference in every case is the **structure**: how the particles are arranged and what holds them together.

Structure decides how a material can be used. Copper is drawn into the wires of the national grid, graphite makes the electrodes of the dry cells in a torch, quartz sand is fused to make glass, and salt dissolves in water while candle wax does not. This lesson brings together the bonding you have met (ionic, covalent, metallic and intermolecular) to explain the properties of solids.

You should already know the four bonding types and the main intermolecular forces.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lattice"}
A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles (atoms, ions or molecules) in a crystalline solid.
:::

:::definition{term="Giant structure"}
A lattice in which strong bonds (ionic, covalent or metallic) extend throughout the whole crystal, with no separate molecules.
:::

:::definition{term="Simple molecular structure"}
A lattice of separate small molecules held in place by weak intermolecular forces; the covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong.
:::

## Liquids and solids: the kinetic-molecular picture

In a **solid**, particles are closely packed in fixed positions and vibrate about those positions. In a **liquid**, particles are still close together (so liquids are hardly compressible) but they move past one another, so a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. Only in a **gas** are the particles far apart.

Changes of state (the interconversion of states) involve energy:

- **Melting** and **boiling** need energy to overcome forces between particles; the temperature stays constant during the change because the energy is used to separate particles, not to speed them up.
- **Freezing** and **condensation** release energy as forces re-form.
- **Evaporation** occurs from the surface of a liquid below its boiling point: the most energetic particles escape. **Vapour pressure** rises with temperature, and a liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. (This is why water boils below 100 °C at high altitude: in the Eastern Highlands, where air pressure is lower, water boils at a slightly lower temperature.)
- **Sublimation** is the change directly from solid to gas, as with iodine and solid carbon dioxide.

## The five lattice types

| Type | Particles | Forces holding the lattice | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| giant ionic | positive and negative ions | strong ionic bonds in all directions | $\ce{NaCl}$, $\ce{MgO}$ |
| simple molecular | small molecules | weak intermolecular forces | iodine $\ce{I2}$, buckminsterfullerene $\ce{C60}$ |
| hydrogen-bonded (a molecular lattice) | molecules | hydrogen bonds | ice |
| giant molecular (giant covalent) | atoms | strong covalent bonds throughout | diamond, graphite, silicon(IV) oxide $\ce{SiO2}$, silicon (ZIMSEC detail) |
| giant metallic | positive ions in delocalised electrons | metallic bonding | copper |

![Four lattice types: a giant ionic layer with alternating Na+ and Cl- ions; a giant metallic lattice of positive ions with delocalised electrons between them; graphite as stacked layers with strong covalent bonds inside each layer and weak forces between layers; and diamond with each carbon bonded tetrahedrally to four others.](media:diagrams/lattice-types.png "Figure: ionic, metallic, graphite and diamond lattices (schematic)")

### Giant ionic lattices

In sodium chloride each $\ce{Na+}$ is surrounded by six $\ce{Cl-}$ ions and each $\ce{Cl-}$ by six $\ce{Na+}$ ions in a cubic arrangement. Magnesium oxide has the same arrangement but with doubly charged ions, so its forces are much stronger.

### Simple molecular lattices

Iodine crystals contain $\ce{I2}$ molecules in a regular lattice held by instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces, which are weak, so iodine sublimes on gentle warming. **Buckminsterfullerene**, $\ce{C60}$, is a carbon molecule shaped like a football: 60 atoms joined in hexagons and pentagons. Because the molecules are separate, $\ce{C60}$ is much softer and lower-melting than diamond or graphite.

### Ice: a hydrogen-bonded lattice

In ice each water molecule is hydrogen-bonded to four others in a tetrahedral, open arrangement. This explains why ice is less dense than water (see the intermolecular forces lesson).

### Giant molecular (covalent) lattices

- **Diamond**: each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a tetrahedral arrangement extending throughout the crystal. All electrons are in strong, localised bonds: very hard, very high melting point, no electrical conductivity.
- **Graphite**: each carbon is bonded to three others in flat hexagonal layers; the fourth outer electron of each atom is delocalised along the layer. The layers are held together by weak intermolecular forces. So graphite conducts electricity along the layers, has a very high melting point (the strong covalent bonds within layers must break), and is soft and slippery because the layers slide.
- **Silicon(IV) oxide** (quartz, sand): each silicon atom is bonded to four oxygen atoms and each oxygen to two silicon atoms, in a tetrahedral network. Hard, very high melting point, insulator.
- **Silicon** has a diamond-like structure (ZIMSEC detail); it is a semiconductor.

### Giant metallic lattices

Copper consists of positive ions packed closely in a regular lattice, surrounded by delocalised electrons: good conductor, malleable and ductile, high melting point.

## How structure explains properties

:::keyfact{title="Properties of the lattice types"}
| Property | Giant ionic | Simple molecular | Giant molecular | Giant metallic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| melting point | high | low | very high | usually high |
| conductivity of solid | no | no | no (graphite yes) | yes |
| conductivity of liquid | yes | no | no | yes |
| solubility in water | often soluble | usually insoluble (unless polar or hydrogen-bonding) | insoluble | insoluble |
:::

**Melting point** depends on what must be overcome: strong bonds throughout a giant structure, or only weak intermolecular forces in a simple molecular one.

**Electrical conductivity** needs mobile charged particles: delocalised electrons (metals, graphite) or ions free to move (molten or dissolved ionic compounds).

**Solubility in water**: ionic solids often dissolve because the polar water molecules are attracted to the ions and surround them (hydration). Simple molecular substances dissolve in water only if they can form hydrogen bonds or interact strongly with it (ethanol, sugar); non-polar ones such as iodine dissolve better in non-polar solvents. Giant covalent and metallic substances do not dissolve.

:::example{title="Deducing a structure from data"}
Substance Q melts at about 1710 °C (data), does not conduct electricity when solid or molten, and is insoluble in water and in hexane.

1. Very high melting point: a giant structure with strong bonds throughout.
2. No conduction even when molten: not ionic and not metallic, so no mobile ions or electrons.
3. Insoluble in both solvents: consistent with a giant covalent network.
4. Q is a **giant molecular (covalent)** substance, such as silicon(IV) oxide.
:::

:::example{title="Why graphite conducts but diamond does not"}
In diamond all four outer electrons of each carbon are used in localised C–C σ bonds, so there are no mobile charge carriers. In graphite each carbon uses only three electrons in σ bonds; the fourth is delocalised over the layer and can move when a potential difference is applied.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying covalent bonds break when iodine sublimes"}
Only the weak intermolecular forces between $\ce{I2}$ molecules are overcome; I–I bonds remain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling silicon dioxide a molecule"}
$\ce{SiO2}$ is a giant covalent lattice. The formula shows the ratio of atoms, not a molecule.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying graphite's layers are held by covalent bonds"}
Within a layer the bonds are covalent; **between** layers there are only weak intermolecular forces, which is why graphite is soft.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying solid ionic compounds conduct because they contain ions"}
The ions must be **free to move**, so the compound must be molten or dissolved.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Explain** the high melting point" questions (Paper 2 on both boards, ZIMSEC Paper 3) need three things: the structure (giant ionic / giant covalent), the type of bond, and "much energy needed to break many strong bonds".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **deduce** a structure, use each piece of data in turn (melting point, conductivity as solid and liquid, solubility) and say what each one rules in or out.
:::

:::exam-tip
Diamond, graphite and $\ce{C60}$ are favourite comparison questions in Paper 1: know the bonding around each carbon and whether the structure is giant or molecular.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Solids have particles fixed and vibrating; liquids have close particles that move past each other; changes of state overcome or form forces between particles.
- Giant ionic (NaCl, MgO), simple molecular (I₂, C₆₀), hydrogen-bonded (ice), giant molecular (diamond, graphite, SiO₂, Si) and giant metallic (Cu) lattices.
- Giant structures have high melting points; simple molecular ones are low-melting because only intermolecular forces are overcome.
- Conduction needs mobile ions or delocalised electrons: metals, graphite, molten or aqueous ionic compounds.
- Data on melting point, conductivity and solubility can be used to deduce structure and bonding.
:::
