## Introduction

Beans, groundnuts, kapenta, eggs and meat are valued in Zimbabwean diets as sources of protein. Digestion breaks the proteins down into their building blocks, the amino acids, which the body reassembles into its own proteins: enzymes, muscle fibres, hormones such as insulin, and antibodies. Twenty different amino acids, joined in different orders, give the enormous variety of proteins in living things.

This lesson looks at amino acids as chemical compounds: why they are crystalline solids that dissolve in water, how they behave as both acids and bases, how they join into peptides, and how mixtures of them are separated. You should know the reactions of carboxylic acids, amines and amides, and the idea of a chiral centre.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="2-amino acid (α-amino acid)"}
A compound $\ce{H2NCH(R)COOH}$ in which the amino group and the carboxylic acid group are attached to the same carbon atom (carbon 2); R is the side chain.
:::

:::definition{term="Zwitterion"}
An ion with both a positive and a negative charge on the same molecule but no overall charge, such as $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COO-}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Isoelectric point"}
The pH at which an amino acid exists mainly as its zwitterion and has no overall charge, so it does not move in an electric field.
:::

:::definition{term="Peptide bond"}
The amide link, –CO–NH–, formed by a condensation reaction between the –COOH group of one amino acid and the –NH₂ group of another, with loss of water.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Forms of an amino acid"}
Low pH (acid): $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COOH}$, a cation. Isoelectric point: $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COO-}$, the zwitterion. High pH (alkali): $\ce{H2NCH(R)COO-}$, an anion.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: zwitterions, the isoelectric point, the forms at different pH, peptides, hydrolysis and electrophoresis. Titration curves of amino acids are a ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge examines this at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC in Form 6. Genetic fingerprinting is in the electrophoresis lesson.
:::

## Structure

| Amino acid | Side chain R | Formula | $M_\text{r}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| glycine (Gly) | H | $\ce{H2NCH2COOH}$ | 75 |
| alanine (Ala) | $\ce{CH3}$ | $\ce{H2NCH(CH3)COOH}$ | 89 |
| serine (Ser) | $\ce{CH2OH}$ | $\ce{H2NCH(CH2OH)COOH}$ | 105 |
| aspartic acid (Asp) | $\ce{CH2COOH}$ | $\ce{H2NCH(CH2COOH)COOH}$ | 133 |
| lysine (Lys) | $\ce{(CH2)4NH2}$ | $\ce{H2NCH((CH2)4NH2)COOH}$ | 146 |

Every 2-amino acid except glycine has four different groups on carbon 2 (H, NH₂, COOH and R), so it is chiral and has two optical isomers; natural proteins use only one of them.

## The zwitterion

An amino acid contains an acidic group (–COOH) and a basic group (–NH₂) in the same molecule. An internal proton transfer from –COOH to –NH₂ gives the zwitterion:

$\ce{H2NCH(R)COOH -> H3N+CH(R)COO-}$

In the solid, zwitterions are held together by strong ionic attractions between the –NH₃⁺ of one ion and the –COO⁻ of another. This explains why amino acids are crystalline solids with high melting points (glycine decomposes at about 230 °C, data) and why they dissolve in water but not in non-polar solvents.

## Behaviour at different pH

| pH | Main form | Charge | Moves in electrophoresis towards |
|---|---|---|---|
| low (added acid) | $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COOH}$ | + | negative electrode (cathode) |
| isoelectric point | $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COO-}$ | 0 | does not move |
| high (added alkali) | $\ce{H2NCH(R)COO-}$ | − | positive electrode (anode) |

Adding acid protonates the –COO⁻; adding alkali removes H⁺ from –NH₃⁺. Because the zwitterion can neutralise both $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$, amino acid solutions act as buffers. Amino acids with an extra –COOH in the side chain (aspartic acid) have a low isoelectric point (about 2.8); those with an extra –NH₂ (lysine) have a high one (about 9.7); glycine's is about 6.0 (data).

## Titration curves of amino acids (ZIMSEC detail)

When the fully protonated form of glycine, $\ce{H3N+CH2COOH}$ (glycine hydrochloride), is titrated with NaOH, the curve has **two** buffer regions and two equivalence points, because two protons are removed in turn: first from –COOH, then from –NH₃⁺.

![Titration curve of 20.0 cm3 of 0.100 mol per dm3 glycine hydrochloride with NaOH: the pH starts near 1.7, has a first buffer region around pH 2.34, rises steeply at the first equivalence point (20.0 cm3, pH 5.97, the isoelectric point), has a second buffer region around pH 9.60, and rises again to about pH 11 at the second equivalence point (40.0 cm3).](media:diagrams/glycine-titration-curve.png "Figure: glycine titration curve with two buffer regions and two equivalence points (pKa 2.34 and 9.60)")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: reading a glycine titration curve]
20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ glycine hydrochloride, $\ce{H3N+CH2COOH}$, is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Data: pKa(–COOH) = 2.34; pKa(–NH₃⁺) = 9.60. Find the volumes at the two equivalence points, the pH at the isoelectric point and the pH after 10.0 cm³ of NaOH.

1. n(glycine hydrochloride) = 0.0200 × 0.100 = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. First equivalence (–COOH neutralised, zwitterion formed): n(NaOH) = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol → V = 2.00 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.100 = 0.0200 dm³ = 20.0 cm³.
3. Second equivalence (–NH₃⁺ neutralised): 40.0 cm³ in total.
4. The first equivalence point is the isoelectric point: pH = (2.34 + 9.60) ÷ 2 = 5.97.
5. At 10.0 cm³ half of the –COOH groups are neutralised, so [acid form] = [zwitterion] and pH = pKa = 2.34 (the middle of the first buffer region).
:::

| Volume of NaOH / cm³ | Approximate pH | Main species |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | about 1.7 | $\ce{H3N+CH2COOH}$ |
| 10.0 | 2.34 | equal amounts of $\ce{H3N+CH2COOH}$ and zwitterion |
| 20.0 | 5.97 (steep rise) | zwitterion $\ce{H3N+CH2COO-}$ |
| 30.0 | 9.60 | equal amounts of zwitterion and $\ce{H2NCH2COO-}$ |
| 40.0 | about 11 (rise) | $\ce{H2NCH2COO-}$ |

## Peptides and proteins

The –COOH of one amino acid condenses with the –NH₂ of another, forming a peptide (amide) bond and eliminating water:

$\ce{H2NCH2COOH + H2NCH(CH3)COOH -> H2NCH2CONHCH(CH3)COOH + H2O}$ (Gly-Ala)

The dipeptide still has a free –NH₂ (the N-terminus) and a free –COOH (the C-terminus), so more amino acids can be added to make tripeptides, polypeptides and proteins. $M_\text{r}$ of a peptide = sum of the amino acids − 18 for each peptide bond: Gly-Ala = 75 + 89 − 18 = 146.

**How many sequences?** Two different amino acids can form two dipeptides, Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly, which are different compounds (each named from the free –NH₂ end). Three different amino acids, each used once, give 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 tripeptides. If any amino acid may be used any number of times, n amino acids give $n^k$ peptides of length k (for example $3^3 = 27$ tripeptides).

**Hydrolysis.** Peptide bonds are broken by heating with 6 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid under reflux for many hours (the products are the amino acids as their cations), by boiling with aqueous alkali (giving the carboxylate anions), or, at body temperature, by protease enzymes during digestion.

:::example{title="Example 2: products of hydrolysis"}
The tripeptide Ala-Gly-Ser is heated with excess hydrochloric acid. Give the formulae of the products.

1. Two peptide bonds are hydrolysed, so two molecules of water are used.
2. The products are the three amino acids, each in the protonated (cationic) form because the solution is strongly acidic.
3. $\ce{H3N+CH(CH3)COOH}$, $\ce{H3N+CH2COOH}$ and $\ce{H3N+CH(CH2OH)COOH}$ (as chlorides).
4. With NaOH(aq) instead, the products would be the anions, $\ce{H2NCH(R)COO-}$, as sodium salts.
:::

## Electrophoresis

In electrophoresis a mixture is placed in a buffer of known pH on paper or gel, and a voltage is applied. Each species moves according to its charge at that pH:

- positive ions move to the negative electrode; negative ions to the positive electrode; species at their isoelectric point stay at the start;
- the larger the charge and the smaller the ion, the further it moves in a given time.

For example, at pH 6.0 glycine (pI about 6.0) stays near the start, lysine (pI about 9.7, positive at pH 6.0) moves towards the negative electrode, and aspartic acid (pI about 2.8, negative at pH 6.0) moves towards the positive electrode. A dipeptide moves less far than a single amino acid with the same charge because it is larger. The colourless spots are located by spraying with ninhydrin and warming, which gives purple spots.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Wrong form at low pH"}
In acid the amino group is protonated **and** the carboxyl group is not ionised: $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COOH}$. Do not write $\ce{H3N+CH(R)COO-}$ for acidic solution.
:::

:::mistake{title="Direction of movement"}
Positive species move to the negative electrode (cathode). An amino acid at its isoelectric point does not move at all.
:::

:::mistake{title="Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly"}
These are different dipeptides; the sequence is read from the free –NH₂ end.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mass of a peptide"}
Subtract 18 for each peptide bond formed, not for each amino acid.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- 2-amino acids, H₂NCH(R)COOH, exist as zwitterions H₃N⁺CH(R)COO⁻: ionic solids with high melting points, soluble in water.
- Low pH: H₃N⁺CH(R)COOH (cation); isoelectric point: zwitterion (no net charge); high pH: H₂NCH(R)COO⁻ (anion).
- Amino acids join by condensation into peptides through –CO–NH– bonds; n different amino acids used once each give n! sequences.
- Proteins and peptides are hydrolysed by hot acid, alkali or enzymes to their amino acids (as cations or anions).
- In electrophoresis, species move according to charge (direction) and charge and size (distance); glycine's titration curve has two buffer regions and an isoelectric point at about pH 6.
:::
