## Introduction

Before the 1850s almost every dye came from plants, insects or shellfish, and bright, colour-fast dyes were expensive. The discovery that coal-tar chemicals such as phenylamine (aniline) could be turned into intensely coloured compounds started the synthetic dye industry and, from it, much of the modern chemical and pharmaceutical industry. Today azo dyes, made from phenylamine and related amines, colour a large share of the cotton fabrics, inks and food colourings in use.

This lesson follows the chemistry from benzene to phenylamine, then to a diazonium salt, and finally to an azo dye. You should know the nitration of benzene, electrophilic substitution in arenes, the basicity of amines and the reactions of phenol.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Phenylamine"}
$\ce{C6H5NH2}$, an aromatic amine in which an –NH₂ group is bonded directly to a benzene ring (also called aniline).
:::

:::definition{term="Diazotisation"}
The reaction of a primary aromatic amine with nitrous acid (from sodium nitrite and dilute acid) below 10 °C to form a diazonium salt containing the –N⁺≡N group.
:::

:::definition{term="Azo compound"}
A compound containing the azo group, –N=N–, linking two carbon groups; when both are aromatic rings the compound is usually coloured and may be used as a dye.
:::

:::definition{term="Coupling reaction"}
The reaction of a diazonium ion with an activated arene (a phenol or an aromatic amine) to join the two rings through an –N=N– group.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The route to an azo dye"}
benzene → (conc. HNO₃/conc. H₂SO₄, 25-60 °C) nitrobenzene → (Sn + conc. HCl, heat under reflux; then NaOH(aq)) phenylamine → (NaNO₂ + dilute HCl, below 10 °C) benzenediazonium chloride → (phenol in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C) azo dye.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: every step here. Cambridge examines it at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC in Form 6 and also expects students to apply the route to synthesising other dyestuffs.
:::

## Making phenylamine

**Step 1: nitration.** Benzene is warmed with a mixture of concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids at 25-60 °C to give nitrobenzene: $\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 -> C6H5NO2 + H2O}$. (Above about 60 °C, dinitration becomes significant.)

**Step 2: reduction.** Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. The tin and acid act as the reducing agent:

$\ce{C6H5NO2 + 6[H] -> C6H5NH2 + 2H2O}$

In the acid the product is present as the phenylammonium ion, $\ce{C6H5NH3+}$ (as a salt with tin chloride). Excess sodium hydroxide solution is added to release the free amine: $\ce{C6H5NH3+ + OH- -> C6H5NH2 + H2O}$. The phenylamine is then separated by steam distillation.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: yield of phenylamine]
12.3 g of nitrobenzene is reduced and 6.51 g of pure phenylamine is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield. $M_\text{r}$: nitrobenzene 123, phenylamine 93.

1. n(nitrobenzene) = 12.3 ÷ 123 = 0.100 mol.
2. The reduction is 1 : 1, so the maximum mass of phenylamine = 0.100 × 93 = 9.30 g.
3. Percentage yield = 6.51 ÷ 9.30 × 100 = 70.0%.
:::

## Bromination of phenylamine

Like phenol, phenylamine has a lone pair (on nitrogen) that is partly delocalised into the ring, so the ring is strongly activated at the 2-, 4- and 6-positions. With bromine water at room temperature it immediately gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine and decolorises the bromine:

$\ce{C6H5NH2 + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3NH2 + 3HBr}$

Benzene, by contrast, needs liquid bromine and a halogen carrier such as $\ce{FeBr3}$.

## Diazotisation

Phenylamine is dissolved in dilute hydrochloric acid and cooled in ice. A cold solution of sodium nitrite is added slowly, keeping the temperature **below 10 °C** (usually 0-5 °C). Nitrous acid forms in the mixture, $\ce{NaNO2 + HCl -> HNO2 + NaCl}$, and reacts with the amine:

$\ce{C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl -> C6H5N2+Cl- + 2H2O}$

The product, benzenediazonium chloride, is kept in cold solution and used at once.

**Why keep it cold?** Above about 10 °C the diazonium ion decomposes, losing nitrogen gas and reacting with water to give phenol. Nitrous acid itself is also unstable. Diazonium salts are hazardous: solid diazonium salts can explode, so they are never isolated dry.

**Hydrolysis on warming.** If the diazonium solution is warmed, nitrogen bubbles off and phenol forms. This is a useful route to phenol:

$\ce{C6H5N2+Cl- + H2O -> C6H5OH + N2 + HCl}$

## Coupling: making an azo dye

When the cold diazonium solution is added to a cold solution of phenol in sodium hydroxide, a yellow-orange precipitate forms immediately:

$\ce{C6H5N2+ + C6H5OH -> C6H5N=NC6H4OH + H+}$ (the H⁺ is removed by the alkali)

The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile. It substitutes into the very electron-rich phenoxide ring, at the 4-position (opposite the –O⁻ group) if that is free. The product is 4-hydroxyazobenzene (4-(phenylazo)phenol). Other activated arenes couple in the same way: 2-naphthol in alkali gives an orange-red dye, and phenylamine or N,N-dimethylphenylamine give yellow to orange dyes in weakly acidic solution.

:::example{title="Example 2: designing a dye synthesis (ZIMSEC dyestuff activity)"}
Outline how an orange-red azo dye can be made from phenylamine and 2-naphthol, with the conditions and the reason for each.

1. Dissolve phenylamine in dilute hydrochloric acid and cool to 0-5 °C in an ice bath.
2. Add cold aqueous sodium nitrite slowly, with stirring, keeping the temperature below 10 °C: benzenediazonium chloride forms. The low temperature stops it decomposing to phenol and nitrogen.
3. Dissolve 2-naphthol in sodium hydroxide solution and cool it in ice. The alkali converts it to the naphthoxide ion, which is more reactive (more electron-rich) than the neutral naphthol.
4. Add the cold diazonium solution to the naphthoxide solution with stirring: an orange-red precipitate of the azo dye forms.
5. Filter, wash with cold water and dry; the dye can be tested on a strip of cotton cloth. Wear gloves and eye protection: phenylamine is toxic through the skin, and the reagents are irritants.
:::

## Why azo compounds are coloured

In an azo dye, the π electrons of the two benzene rings and the –N=N– group form one large delocalised system. The bigger the delocalised system, the smaller the energy gap between the electron energy levels, so the molecule absorbs light in the visible region (rather than in the ultraviolet, as benzene does). The colour we see is the complementary colour of the light absorbed. Changing the groups attached to the rings (for example –OH, –NH₂, –SO₃⁻Na⁺ to make the dye water-soluble) changes the energy gap and so the colour, which is how a range of dyes is made from one reaction.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Temperature for diazotisation"}
Below 10 °C, not "room temperature" or "heat". Warming converts the diazonium salt into phenol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the NaOH after reduction"}
Tin and HCl give the phenylammonium salt. Add NaOH(aq) to release free phenylamine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Coupling conditions"}
Phenol is coupled in **alkaline** solution (as phenoxide); give "NaOH(aq), below 10 °C".
:::

:::mistake{title="Formula of the azo product"}
Only one hydrogen is lost from the phenol ring: $\ce{C6H5N=NC6H4OH}$ (not $\ce{C6H5N=NC6H5OH}$).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Papers 1-3. *State* the reagents and conditions for each step (Sn/conc. HCl then NaOH; NaNO₂/HCl below 10 °C; phenol in NaOH); *explain* why the temperature must be low; *suggest* the structure of the azo product from a given amine and coupling agent.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Colour"}
To *explain* colour, link extended delocalisation across the –N=N– bridge to absorption of visible light; mention that the colour seen is complementary to the light absorbed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Phenylamine is made by nitrating benzene (conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄, 25-60 °C), reducing with Sn/conc. HCl under reflux, then adding NaOH(aq).
- Phenylamine decolorises bromine water, giving white 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine (activated ring).
- NaNO₂ + dilute HCl below 10 °C converts phenylamine into benzenediazonium chloride; warming gives phenol + N₂.
- In alkali, diazonium ions couple with phenols (at the 4-position) to form azo compounds, –N=N–.
- Azo dyes are coloured because extended delocalisation lowers the energy gap so visible light is absorbed.
:::
