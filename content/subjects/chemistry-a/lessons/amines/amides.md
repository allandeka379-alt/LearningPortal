## Introduction

The amide link, –CONH–, holds together every protein in your body, the nylon in a fishing net and the Kevlar in protective vests. Medicines such as paracetamol contain it too. Amides are remarkably stable: proteins survive for years in living cells, and nylon ropes last in sun and rain. Yet amides can be broken down by boiling with acid or alkali, which is how chemists find out what a protein or a polyamide is made of.

This lesson covers simple amides: how they are named and made, how they are hydrolysed and reduced, and why, unlike amines, they are hardly basic at all. Peptides and proteins are in the amino acids lesson; polyamides are in the polymers lessons. You should know the reactions of acyl chlorides and the basicity of amines.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Amide"}
A compound containing the group –CONH₂, –CONHR or –CONR₂, in which nitrogen is bonded directly to the carbon of a C=O group.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary, secondary and tertiary amide"}
An amide in which the nitrogen is bonded to no, one or two carbon groups (other than the C=O carbon): $\ce{RCONH2}$, $\ce{RCONHR'}$ and $\ce{RCONR'R''}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Amide (peptide) link"}
The –CO–NH– group formed when the –COOH (or –COCl) of one molecule condenses with the –NH₂ of another.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Reactions to learn"}
Acid hydrolysis: $\ce{RCONH2 + H2O + HCl -> RCOOH + NH4Cl}$. Alkaline hydrolysis: $\ce{RCONH2 + NaOH -> RCOONa + NH3}$. Reduction: $\ce{RCONH2 + 4[H] -> RCH2NH2 + H2O}$ (LiAlH₄ in dry ether).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: naming (up to six plus six carbons), formation, hydrolysis, reduction and the weak basicity of amides. Cambridge examines amides at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC in Form 6.
:::

## Naming amides

The name comes from the parent carboxylic acid, replacing -oic acid by **-amide**. Groups on the nitrogen are shown with the locant **N-**.

| Formula | Name | Type |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH3CONH2}$ | ethanamide | primary |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CONH2}$ | propanamide | primary |
| $\ce{CH3CONHCH3}$ | N-methylethanamide | secondary |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CONHCH2CH3}$ | N-ethylpropanamide | secondary |
| $\ce{CH3CON(CH3)2}$ | N,N-dimethylethanamide | tertiary |
| $\ce{C6H5CONH2}$ | benzamide | primary |

Working from a name: in N-ethylpropanamide, "propanamide" ($\ce{CH3CH2CO-}$) is the acyl part and the ethyl group is on nitrogen. Note that "primary, secondary, tertiary" for amides counts the carbon groups on N, just as for amines.

## Making amides

Amides are made at room temperature from an acyl chloride and ammonia or an amine (concentrated ammonia or the amine in excess, which also removes the HCl):

| Reagents | Product | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| ethanoyl chloride + ammonia | ethanamide | $\ce{CH3COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl}$ |
| ethanoyl chloride + methylamine | N-methylethanamide | $\ce{CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3+Cl-}$ |
| propanoyl chloride + dimethylamine | N,N-dimethylpropanamide | $\ce{CH3CH2COCl + 2(CH3)2NH -> CH3CH2CON(CH3)2 + (CH3)2NH2+Cl-}$ |

The mechanism is addition-elimination, described in the acyl chlorides lesson. A tertiary amine has no H on nitrogen, so it cannot form an amide.

## Hydrolysis of amides

Amides are hydrolysed only slowly, by heating under reflux with aqueous acid or alkali:

| Conditions | Products from N-methylethanamide |
|---|---|
| dilute HCl, heat under reflux | ethanoic acid and methylammonium chloride: $\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + H2O + HCl -> CH3COOH + CH3NH3+Cl-}$ |
| NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | sodium ethanoate and methylamine: $\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + NaOH -> CH3COONa + CH3NH2}$ |

To deduce the products, break the C–N bond of the amide link and add water: the C=O side becomes the carboxylic acid; the N side becomes ammonia or the amine. Then adjust for the conditions: in **acid**, the nitrogen product is protonated (ammonium or alkylammonium ion); in **alkali**, the acid is deprotonated (carboxylate ion) and ammonia or the amine is released (it turns damp red litmus blue).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: purity of an amide by back titration]
A 0.900 g sample of impure ethanamide is boiled with excess NaOH(aq). All the ammonia released is absorbed in 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The excess HCl needs 25.00 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Calculate the percentage purity of the ethanamide. $M_\text{r}$(ethanamide) = 59.

1. n(HCl) at the start = 0.0500 × 0.500 = 0.0250 mol.
2. n(HCl) left over = n(NaOH) = 0.02500 × 0.400 = 0.0100 mol.
3. n(HCl) that reacted with $\ce{NH3}$ = 0.0250 − 0.0100 = 0.0150 mol = n($\ce{NH3}$).
4. $\ce{CH3CONH2 + NaOH -> CH3COONa + NH3}$ is 1 : 1, so n(ethanamide) = 0.0150 mol; mass = 0.0150 × 59 = 0.885 g.
5. Percentage purity = 0.885 ÷ 0.900 × 100 = 98.3%.
:::

## Reduction to amines

Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate in dry ether reduces the C=O of an amide to CH₂, giving an amine with the same number of carbons:

$\ce{CH3CONH2 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2NH2 + H2O}$ (ethanamide → ethylamine)

$\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2NHCH3 + H2O}$ (a secondary amine)

## Why amides are such weak bases

In an amine, the lone pair on nitrogen is available to accept a proton. In an amide, the nitrogen lone pair is **delocalised** over the N–C=O group: it overlaps with the π bond of the carbonyl group, and the electronegative oxygen draws electron density towards itself. The lone pair is therefore much less available, so amides are almost neutral: ethanamide does not turn red litmus blue and does not form stable salts with dilute acids, unlike ethylamine. The same delocalisation makes the C–N bond in amides shorter and stronger than a normal C–N single bond, which is one reason the amide link is so stable.

:::example{title="Example 2: deducing hydrolysis products"}
A compound has the structure $\ce{CH3CH2CONHC6H5}$. Give the products of heating it under reflux with (a) dilute hydrochloric acid and (b) sodium hydroxide solution.

1. Break the C–N bond: the acyl side is $\ce{CH3CH2CO-}$ (propanoic acid), the nitrogen side is $\ce{C6H5NH-}$ (phenylamine).
2. (a) In acid: propanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$, and phenylammonium chloride, $\ce{C6H5NH3+Cl-}$.
3. (b) In alkali: sodium propanoate, $\ce{CH3CH2COONa}$, and phenylamine, $\ce{C6H5NH2}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Amides are not amines"}
An amide has N bonded to C=O; an amine does not. Ethanamide ($\ce{CH3CONH2}$) is neutral; ethylamine ($\ce{CH3CH2NH2}$) is basic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Products depend on the conditions"}
Acid hydrolysis gives the free carboxylic acid and an ammonium salt; alkaline hydrolysis gives the carboxylate salt and free ammonia or amine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reducing agent"}
NaBH₄ will not reduce amides; use LiAlH₄ in dry ether.
:::

:::mistake{title="N- locants"}
$\ce{CH3CONHCH2CH3}$ is N-ethylethanamide, not "ethylethanamide" or "butanamide".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Papers 1-3. *Name* amides with N- locants; *deduce* hydrolysis products under acid or alkaline conditions (state the form of each product); *explain* the weak basicity using delocalisation of the N lone pair over C=O.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Tests"}
Primary amides release ammonia (damp red litmus turns blue) only on prolonged boiling with NaOH(aq), whereas ammonium salts release it on gentle warming. Mention the heating time when using this as a test.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Amides contain N bonded to C=O; names end in -amide with N- locants for groups on nitrogen.
- They are made at room temperature from acyl chlorides with ammonia (primary amides) or amines (secondary and tertiary amides).
- Acid hydrolysis gives a carboxylic acid and an ammonium (or alkylammonium) salt; alkaline hydrolysis gives a carboxylate salt and ammonia (or an amine).
- LiAlH₄ in dry ether reduces amides to amines with the same carbon count.
- Amides are very weak bases because the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised over the C=O group.
:::
