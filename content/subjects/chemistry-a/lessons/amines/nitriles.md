## Introduction

Organic chemists often need to make a molecule one carbon longer than the material they can buy. Most reactions of halogenoalkanes, alcohols and carbonyl compounds keep the carbon skeleton unchanged, so a reaction that makes a new carbon-carbon bond is valuable. The nitrile group, –C≡N, provides one: cyanide ions attach a new carbon atom, and the C≡N can then be turned into a carboxylic acid or an amine. Acrylic fibres used in blankets and jerseys are made from a nitrile, propenenitrile, showing that nitriles are industrial materials as well as synthetic intermediates.

This lesson collects the chemistry of nitriles. You should already know nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes, nucleophilic addition to carbonyl compounds, and the idea of hydrolysis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nitrile"}
An organic compound containing the –C≡N group; named by adding **-nitrile** to the name of the alkane with the same total number of carbon atoms, counting the nitrile carbon (for example $\ce{CH3CH2CN}$ is propanenitrile).
:::

:::definition{term="Hydroxynitrile"}
A nitrile that also contains an –OH group; from aldehydes and ketones both groups are on the same carbon.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key reactions"}
RX + KCN (ethanol, heat under reflux) → RCN + KX. RCN + 2H₂O + H⁺ → RCOOH + NH₄⁺ (dilute acid, reflux). RCN + 4[H] → RCH₂NH₂ (LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or H₂/Ni).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: naming straight-chain nitriles, both routes to nitriles, hydrolysis and reduction, and their use in synthesis. Cambridge examines nitriles at AS Level; ZIMSEC in Form 6.
:::

## Naming nitriles

| Formula | Name | Made from |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH3CN}$ | ethanenitrile | bromomethane + KCN |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CN}$ | propanenitrile | bromoethane + KCN |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}$ | butanenitrile | 1-bromopropane + KCN |
| $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$ | 2-hydroxypropanenitrile | ethanal + HCN/KCN |
| $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CN}$ | 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile | propanone + HCN/KCN |

The nitrile carbon is carbon 1, so the name always has one more carbon than the halogenoalkane it came from.

## Making nitriles

**From halogenoalkanes.** Heat the halogenoalkane under reflux with potassium (or sodium) cyanide dissolved in ethanol. The cyanide ion is the nucleophile and replaces the halogen:

$\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CH2CN + Br-}$

Ethanol is used as the solvent because the halogenoalkane dissolves in it and, without water, little alcohol forms by competing substitution with hydroxide.

**From aldehydes and ketones.** Hydrogen cyanide adds across C=O in the presence of a little KCN, at room temperature, by nucleophilic addition, to give a hydroxynitrile. The mechanism is in the nucleophilic addition lesson.

Cyanides are extremely toxic. KCN must never come into contact with acid (which releases HCN gas), and work is done only under supervision, with gloves, in a fume cupboard.

## Hydrolysis of nitriles

| Conditions | Products | Equation (propanenitrile) |
|---|---|---|
| dilute HCl (or $\ce{H2SO4}$), heat under reflux | carboxylic acid + ammonium salt | $\ce{CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + HCl -> CH3CH2COOH + NH4Cl}$ |
| NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | carboxylate salt + ammonia gas | $\ce{CH3CH2CN + NaOH + H2O -> CH3CH2COONa + NH3}$ |
| then add dilute HCl | carboxylic acid | $\ce{CH3CH2COO- + H+ -> CH3CH2COOH}$ |

Ammonia released during alkaline hydrolysis turns damp red litmus paper blue, a sign that the reaction is happening. Hydroxynitriles are hydrolysed in the same way to 2-hydroxy carboxylic acids: 2-hydroxypropanenitrile gives 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).

## Reduction of nitriles

Reduction converts –C≡N into –CH₂NH₂, a primary amine, using either lithium tetrahydridoaluminate in dry ether or hydrogen with a nickel catalyst:

$\ce{CH3CH2CN + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2NH2}$ (propylamine) or $\ce{CH3CH2CN + 2H2 -> CH3CH2CH2NH2}$

Sodium tetrahydridoborate is not strong enough. Unlike the halogenoalkane-plus-ammonia route, this gives the primary amine without secondary or tertiary amines.

## Nitriles in synthesis

:::example{title="Example 1: lengthening a chain"}
Plan a synthesis of butanoic acid from propan-1-ol.

1. Count carbons: propan-1-ol has three, butanoic acid has four, so one carbon must be added; a nitrile is needed.
2. Step 1: convert the alcohol into a halogenoalkane, for example heat with KBr and concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ (HBr) → 1-bromopropane.
3. Step 2: heat 1-bromopropane under reflux with KCN in ethanol → butanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}$ (nucleophilic substitution).
4. Step 3: heat butanenitrile under reflux with dilute HCl → butanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}$ (hydrolysis).
5. Using LiAlH₄ in step 3 instead would give butylamine, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: yield of a nitrile]
6.85 g of 1-bromobutane is heated with excess KCN in ethanol. Calculate the maximum mass of pentanenitrile, and the mass obtained at 65.0% yield. $M_\text{r}$: 1-bromobutane 136.9, pentanenitrile 83.

1. n(1-bromobutane) = 6.85 ÷ 136.9 = 0.0500 mol.
2. $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CH2CH2CN + Br-}$: 1 : 1, so n(pentanenitrile) = 0.0500 mol.
3. Maximum mass = 0.0500 × 83 = 4.15 g.
4. At 65.0% yield: 0.650 × 4.15 = 2.70 g.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a route from a ketone"}
Suggest how propanone can be converted into 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanoic acid, $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)COOH}$.

1. The product has four carbons and propanone has three, so a cyanide step is needed; the –OH on the same carbon as the new group points to a hydroxynitrile.
2. Step 1: HCN with a little KCN at room temperature → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CN}$ (nucleophilic addition).
3. Step 2: heat under reflux with dilute HCl → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanoic acid and ammonium chloride (hydrolysis).
4. If NaOH(aq) is used in step 2, the sodium salt forms and ammonia is given off; dilute acid must then be added to obtain the free acid.
:::

## Comparing the two cyanide reactions

| Feature | Halogenoalkane + KCN | Carbonyl + HCN/KCN |
|---|---|---|
| mechanism | nucleophilic substitution | nucleophilic addition |
| conditions | KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux | HCN with KCN catalyst, room temperature |
| product | nitrile (no –OH) | hydroxynitrile |
| carbon count | +1 | +1 |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Miscounting carbons"}
Bromoethane gives **propane**nitrile. The CN carbon counts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Aqueous cyanide"}
Use KCN in **ethanol**. In water, hydroxide ions compete and some alcohol forms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hydrolysis versus reduction"}
Hydrolysis (water, acid or alkali) gives a carboxylic acid; reduction (LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni) gives an amine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to acidify"}
Alkaline hydrolysis gives the carboxylate salt; the free acid needs a final acidification.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS) and ZIMSEC Papers 1-3. *State* reagents and conditions for each step of a route; *name* the nitrile correctly; *suggest* a synthesis that adds one carbon. In multi-step routes, give every intermediate's name or formula.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Spotting the nitrile step"}
If the target has one more carbon than the starting material and ends in –COOH, –CH₂NH₂ or –CH(OH)COOH, a nitrile step is almost certainly needed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nitriles contain –C≡N and are named -nitrile, counting the CN carbon.
- Halogenoalkanes + KCN in ethanol (reflux) give nitriles; aldehydes and ketones + HCN/KCN give hydroxynitriles.
- Hydrolysis with dilute acid (reflux) gives the carboxylic acid and NH₄⁺; with NaOH(aq) it gives the carboxylate and NH₃, then acidify.
- Reduction with LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni gives a primary amine, RCH₂NH₂.
- The cyanide reactions add one carbon to a chain, a key tool in synthesis.
:::
