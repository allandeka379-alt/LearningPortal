## Introduction

Polymers were chosen for water pipes, cable insulation, fishing nets and drink bottles because they are cheap, light, tough and resistant to water and chemicals. Those same properties mean that a carrier bag dropped in a storm drain in Mutare may still be there many years later. Chemistry explains both sides: why each polymer suits its job, and which polymers can be broken down again.

This lesson matches uses to properties, explains why some polymers degrade and others do not, and compares ways of dealing with plastic waste. You should know addition and condensation polymerisation, ester and amide hydrolysis, and intermolecular forces.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biodegradable polymer"}
A polymer that microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) can break down into small molecules, such as carbon dioxide and water, in the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Photodegradable polymer"}
A polymer whose chains are broken when it absorbs light, usually ultraviolet radiation from sunlight.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrolysis of a polymer"}
Breaking the ester or amide links of a condensation polymer by reaction with water, catalysed by acid or alkali (or by enzymes), giving back the monomers or their salts.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: uses linked to properties, inertness of poly(alkene)s, photodegradable polymers and hydrolysis of polyesters and polyamides (ZIMSEC Form 6, "uses of polymers"; Cambridge A Level, Paper 4). ZIMSEC suggests a visit to a plastics or recycling works; any such visit is described in general terms here.
:::

## Uses linked to properties

| Polymer | Key properties | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| LDPE | flexible, light, waterproof, cheap | carrier bags, cling film, squeeze bottles |
| HDPE | stiffer, stronger, chemically inert | water tanks, bottles, crates, pipes |
| poly(propene) | tough, strong fibres, higher softening point than poly(ethene) | woven sacks, ropes, carpets, crates, microwaveable containers |
| PVC (rigid) | hard, durable, does not rot, electrical insulator | water and drain pipes, window frames, gutters |
| PVC (with plasticiser) | flexible, insulating | electrical cable insulation, flooring, raincoats |
| PTFE | extremely inert, very low friction, stable to high temperature | non-stick coatings, plumbing tape, bearings |
| polystyrene (expanded) | very light, thermal insulator, shock absorbing | packaging, insulation, disposable food containers |
| nylon | strong, hard-wearing fibres (hydrogen bonds between chains) | ropes, fishing nets, clothing, gears and bearings |
| PET | strong, transparent, low permeability to gases | drink bottles, polyester fibres for clothing |
| Kevlar | very high strength for its mass, heat resistant | protective vests and helmets, ropes, reinforcing tyres |

When asked to "relate use to property", give the property that the use **needs**: a water pipe needs a material that does not corrode or rot (PVC), a fishing net needs fibres of high tensile strength that do not absorb much water (nylon).

## Why poly(alkene)s persist

A poly(alkene) chain has only C–C and C–H bonds. These bonds are strong and almost non-polar, and there is no functional group that water, acids, alkalis or enzymes can attack. The polymers are therefore **chemically inert** and **non-biodegradable**. Inertness is a strength in use (a chemical-storage bottle should not react with its contents) and a weakness in disposal.

## Photodegradable polymers

A poly(alkene) can be made photodegradable by including a small proportion of carbonyl groups (C=O) in its chain, for example by copolymerising ethene with a little carbon monoxide. The C=O groups absorb ultraviolet radiation, and the energy absorbed breaks nearby bonds in the chain. The polymer becomes brittle and falls apart into small pieces.

Limits of photodegradation:

- It needs light. Plastic buried in a landfill site, or covered by soil, is not exposed to UV and does not break down.
- The small fragments can still persist; breaking a bag into tiny pieces is not the same as converting it to CO₂ and water.

## Hydrolysis of polyesters and polyamides

Condensation polymers contain **polar** ester or amide links, which can be hydrolysed, just like simple esters and amides. This is why they are more degradable than poly(alkene)s (though some, like PET and nylon, still break down slowly in the environment).

| Polymer type | Hydrolysed by hot aqueous acid | Hydrolysed by hot aqueous alkali |
|---|---|---|
| polyester (diol + diacid) | the diol and the dicarboxylic acid | the diol and the salt of the dicarboxylic acid (dicarboxylate) |
| polyamide (diamine + diacid) | the dicarboxylic acid and the ammonium salt of the diamine | the diamine and the dicarboxylate salt |
| protein (polypeptide) | amino acids (as their cations in strong acid) | amino acids (as their carboxylate anions) |

The acid or alkali changes the products' form:

- In acid, amine groups are protonated: –NH₂ → –NH₃⁺.
- In alkali, carboxylic acid groups lose H⁺: –COOH → –COO⁻.

For example, hydrolysis of nylon-6,6 with hot hydrochloric acid gives hexanedioic acid and $\ce{ClH3N(CH2)6NH3Cl}$ (hexane-1,6-diammonium chloride); hydrolysis with hot sodium hydroxide gives hexane-1,6-diamine and sodium hexanedioate.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: alkali needed to hydrolyse PET]
A 3.84 g sample of PET (repeat unit –OCH₂CH₂O–CO–C₆H₄–CO–, Mr 192) is completely hydrolysed by boiling with 2.00 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide.

1. Amount of repeat units = 3.84 ÷ 192 = 0.0200 mol.
2. Each repeat unit has two ester links, and each link uses one OH⁻: amount of NaOH = 2 × 0.0200 = 0.0400 mol.
3. Minimum volume of NaOH(aq) = 0.0400 ÷ 2.00 = 0.0200 dm³ = 20.0 cm³.
4. Products: 0.0200 mol of ethane-1,2-diol (0.0200 × 62.0 = 1.24 g) and 0.0200 mol of disodium benzene-1,4-dicarboxylate.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: predicting hydrolysis products"}
A polyamide has the repeat unit –NH(CH₂)₆NH–CO(CH₂)₈CO– (nylon-6,10). What forms when it is boiled with aqueous sodium hydroxide?

1. Break each amide link –CO–NH–, adding OH to the C=O carbon and H to the N.
2. This gives H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ (hexane-1,6-diamine) and HOOC(CH₂)₈COOH (decanedioic acid).
3. In alkali the acid is present as its salt: sodium decanedioate, NaOOC(CH₂)₈COONa. The diamine is released as the free amine.
:::

Some polyesters, such as poly(lactic acid), break down much more quickly in industrial composting conditions (warm, moist and full of microorganisms) than poly(alkene)s do. Absorbable surgical stitches are polyesters hydrolysed slowly by water in the body.

## Dealing with plastic waste

| Option | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| landfill | simple, low cost | poly(alkene)s persist for a very long time; uses land; no resource recovered |
| incineration | reduces volume; heat can generate electricity | CO₂ released; PVC gives HCl and possibly toxic chlorinated compounds; gases must be cleaned |
| mechanical recycling | saves crude oil and energy; less waste | plastics must be sorted and cleaned; quality falls each cycle |
| chemical recycling (e.g. hydrolysis of PET to monomers) | monomers can make new, high-quality polymer | energy and chemicals needed |
| reducing use and reusing | avoids waste altogether | needs changes in habits and design |

Plastic litter blocks drains (contributing to flooding), harms livestock and wildlife that swallow it, and spoils the landscape. A balanced answer gives both the benefits of plastics (cheap, light, safe food and water storage) and the problems of their disposal, and does not claim that any one method solves everything.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all polymers are non-biodegradable"}
Poly(alkene)s are; polyesters and polyamides have hydrolysable links and some degrade.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong product form in hydrolysis"}
In acid, amines appear as ammonium salts; in alkali, acids appear as carboxylate salts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Photodegradation underground"}
Photodegradable plastics need UV light. In a landfill they behave like ordinary plastics.
:::

:::mistake{title="One link per repeat unit"}
A diol + diacid or diamine + diacid repeat unit has two links: it needs two H₂O or two OH⁻ to hydrolyse.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1, Paper 3 Section C or D; Cambridge Paper 4. Questions ask you to give a use and the property it depends on, to predict hydrolysis products, or to discuss disposal.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Suggest* a use: name a specific product and the property it needs. *Explain* why a polyester is biodegradable but poly(ethene) is not: name the polar ester link that can be hydrolysed and the non-polar C–C and C–H bonds of the poly(alkene). *Discuss* disposal: give advantages and disadvantages of at least two methods with a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Each polymer's uses follow from its properties: inertness, strength, flexibility, low friction or transparency.
- Poly(alkene)s are inert and non-biodegradable: only strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds.
- Photodegradable polymers contain C=O groups that absorb UV and break the chain, but need light.
- Polyesters and polyamides are hydrolysed by hot acid or alkali (acid gives ammonium salts of amines; alkali gives carboxylate salts).
- Landfill, incineration and recycling each have advantages and drawbacks; a good answer weighs them.
:::
