## Introduction

Look around any market in Harare or Bulawayo: water tanks, buckets, carrier bags, maize-meal sacks woven from plastic tape, electrical cable insulation and drainpipes. Almost all of these are **addition polymers**, made by joining thousands of small alkene molecules into long chains. Their usefulness and their main drawback (they last for a very long time after they are thrown away) both come from the same chemistry: a long, saturated chain of carbon atoms.

This lesson shows how addition polymerisation works, how to move between a monomer and its polymer on paper, how the two main types of poly(ethene) differ, and why poly(alkene)s are a disposal problem. You should already know the structure of the C=C double bond, alkene naming, and that alkenes undergo addition reactions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monomer"}
A small molecule that can join repeatedly to other molecules of the same kind (or of a partner) to form a polymer.
:::

:::definition{term="Polymer"}
A very large molecule (macromolecule) made of many monomer-derived units joined by covalent bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="Addition polymerisation"}
The joining of many unsaturated monomer molecules, by opening their C=C double bonds, to form a single long-chain polymer and no other product.
:::

:::definition{term="Repeat unit"}
The smallest section of a polymer chain that, repeated over and over, gives the whole chain; for an addition polymer it contains the two carbon atoms that formed the C=C bond in the monomer.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Learn these"}
- $n\ce{CH2=CH2 -> (-CH2-CH2-)_n}$ — poly(ethene)
- $n\ce{CH2=CHCH3 -> (-CH2-CH(CH3)-)_n}$ — poly(propene)
- $n\ce{CH2=CHCl -> (-CH2-CHCl-)_n}$ — poly(chloroethene), PVC
- An addition polymer has the **same empirical formula** as its monomer, and the reaction has no by-product.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the reaction, repeat units, identifying monomers and the disposal problem (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge AS Level, Papers 1 and 2). Low- and high-density poly(ethene) are needed in outline. Condensation polymers and the wider uses of polymers have their own lessons.
:::

## How addition polymerisation happens

In each monomer the π bond of the C=C breaks. The two carbon atoms that were joined by the double bond each use one electron to form a new σ bond to a neighbouring monomer. The process repeats thousands of times, so the chain grows. The result is:

1. A **saturated** backbone of C–C single bonds: the polymer no longer has C=C bonds, so it does not decolorise bromine water.
2. **No by-product**: every atom of the monomer ends up in the polymer (100% atom economy).
3. A **mixture of chain lengths**: polymerisation stops at random, so a sample has an average relative molecular mass, not a single value.

The substituent groups on the C=C carbons (H, CH₃, Cl and so on) do not take part. They end up hanging off alternate carbon atoms of the backbone.

The industrial reaction usually runs through free radicals or through a metal-based catalyst; the mechanism is not required by either syllabus.

## From monomer to repeat unit, and back

**Monomer to repeat unit.** Draw the C=C with its four groups. Change the double bond to a single bond and add a bond on each side that "continues" the chain. Write the result in brackets with n.

| Monomer | Formula | Repeat unit | Polymer |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethene | $\ce{CH2=CH2}$ | –CH₂–CH₂– | poly(ethene) |
| propene | $\ce{CH2=CHCH3}$ | –CH₂–CH(CH₃)– | poly(propene) |
| chloroethene | $\ce{CH2=CHCl}$ | –CH₂–CHCl– | poly(chloroethene), PVC |
| tetrafluoroethene | $\ce{CF2=CF2}$ | –CF₂–CF₂– | poly(tetrafluoroethene), PTFE |
| phenylethene | $\ce{CH2=CHC6H5}$ | –CH₂–CH(C₆H₅)– | poly(phenylethene), polystyrene |
| propenenitrile | $\ce{CH2=CHCN}$ | –CH₂–CH(CN)– | poly(propenenitrile) |

**Polymer section to monomer.** In an addition polymer the main chain is all carbon. Split the chain into **two-carbon** pieces so that each piece carries the same pattern of side groups, then put a C=C between those two carbons.

Notice that the methyl group in poly(propene) is a **side group**. A common slip is to write the repeat unit of poly(propene) as –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–, a three-carbon chain; that would be poly(ethene) written differently.

![Two addition polymer chains with an all-carbon backbone: poly(propene) has a methyl side group on every second backbone carbon, repeat unit -CH2-CH(CH3)-; poly(but-2-ene) has a methyl group on every backbone carbon, repeat unit -CH(CH3)-CH(CH3)-, so the two are different polymers although both come from alkenes.](media:diagrams/addition-polymer-repeat-units.png "Figure: repeat units of poly(propene) and poly(but-2-ene); the methyl groups are side groups, not part of the backbone")

:::example{title="Example 1: finding the monomer from a polymer section"}
A section of a polymer chain is –CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–.

1. The backbone is all carbon, so this is an addition polymer.
2. The pattern repeats every two carbons: –CH₂–C(CH₃)(COOCH₃)–. That is the repeat unit.
3. Put a double bond between those two carbons: the monomer is CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃, methyl 2-methylpropenoate.
4. Check: three repeat units in the section, so three monomer molecules were joined.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: a polymer from but-2-ene"}
But-2-ene is CH₃CH=CHCH₃. Each C=C carbon carries one H and one CH₃.

1. Open the double bond: –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–.
2. Repeat unit: –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–, with a methyl group on **every** backbone carbon.
3. Compare poly(propene), with a methyl group on every **second** carbon: the two polymers are different even though both monomers are alkenes.
:::

## Quantities: a polymer chain in numbers

Because no atoms are lost, the relative molecular mass of a chain is simply n × Mr(monomer).

:::worked-solution[Example 3: counting monomer units and finding chlorine content]
A sample of PVC has an average relative molecular mass of 1.50 × 10⁵. The monomer, chloroethene, has Mr = 62.5, and chlorine makes up 35.5 of this.

1. Mr(CH₂=CHCl) = 62.5 (from two C, three H and one Cl).
2. Average number of monomer units = 1.50 × 10⁵ ÷ 62.5 = 2400.
3. Percentage of chlorine by mass = 35.5 ÷ 62.5 × 100 = 56.8% (the same for the polymer as for the monomer, because the empirical formula is unchanged).
:::

## Low-density and high-density poly(ethene)

Two forms of poly(ethene) are made under different conditions, and their properties differ because of how well the chains pack.

| Feature | Low-density poly(ethene), LDPE | High-density poly(ethene), HDPE |
|---|---|---|
| typical conditions | very high pressure (about 1000-2000 atm), about 200 °C, trace of oxygen or a peroxide initiator (free radicals) | low pressure (a few atm), about 60 °C, Ziegler-Natta catalyst |
| chain structure | many side branches | few branches, nearly straight chains |
| packing | chains cannot pack closely | chains pack closely; more crystalline regions |
| properties | lower density, softer, more flexible, lower melting point | higher density, stiffer, stronger, higher softening temperature |
| typical uses | carrier bags, cling film, squeeze bottles | water tanks, crates, bottles for milk and chemicals, pipes |

Better packing means the chains are closer together, so the instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces between them are stronger overall. That raises the density, stiffness and softening temperature.

## Why poly(alkene)s are hard to dispose of

- **Non-biodegradable.** The chain contains only strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, with no polar groups such as ester or amide links for water, acids, alkalis or enzymes to attack. Microorganisms cannot break them down, so poly(alkene) litter persists in soil, drains and rivers for a very long time.
- **Landfill** therefore keeps them indefinitely and uses up land.
- **Burning** releases energy but produces carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas) and, with too little air, carbon monoxide and soot. Burning **PVC** also releases hydrogen chloride, an acidic, toxic gas, and incomplete combustion of chlorinated plastics can form other toxic chlorinated compounds. Incinerators must burn at high temperature and remove HCl from the flue gases (for example by neutralising with a base).
- **Recycling** of sorted plastics (melting and remoulding) reduces both waste and the use of crude oil, but mixed and dirty plastics are hard to sort.

Burning plastic waste in the open, as sometimes happens at dumpsites, is the worst option: low temperatures and no gas cleaning.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Leaving the double bond in the polymer"}
The repeat unit has single bonds only. Writing –CH₂=CH₂– inside the brackets is wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting side groups into the backbone"}
For propene the CH₃ hangs off the chain: –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–, never –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the continuation bonds"}
A repeat unit must show a bond leaving each end, so that it links to the next unit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying addition polymers give off water"}
That is condensation polymerisation. Addition polymerisation has a single product.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 (multiple choice and multiple-completion items), Paper 2 and Paper 3 Section C; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. Expect to draw or write a repeat unit, identify a monomer from a polymer section, or explain a disposal problem.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* the type of polymerisation: "addition". *Deduce* the monomer: split the backbone into two-carbon units and restore C=C. *Explain* why poly(alkene)s are not biodegradable: name the strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds **and** the absence of polar groups that could be hydrolysed. *Suggest* a problem of burning PVC: HCl gas (acidic, toxic) as well as CO₂.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Addition polymerisation opens the C=C of many alkene monomers to form one saturated polymer and nothing else.
- Repeat unit: the two former C=C carbons with their side groups, single-bonded, with continuation bonds.
- To find the monomer, split the all-carbon backbone into two-carbon units and put back the C=C.
- LDPE is branched (soft, flexible); HDPE has nearly straight chains that pack closely (dense, rigid).
- Poly(alkene)s are inert and non-biodegradable; burning gives CO₂, and PVC also gives HCl.
:::
