## Introduction

A school uniform shirt labelled "polyester-cotton", a fishing net on Lake Kariba, a fizzy-drink bottle and the protein in a plate of beans have something in common: their long chains are held together by **ester** or **amide** links formed by condensation reactions. Each time two monomers join, a small molecule such as water or hydrogen chloride is released.

This lesson explains how polyesters and polyamides form, how to write their repeat units, and how to work backwards from a polymer section to its monomers. You should know the reactions of alcohols, carboxylic acids, acyl chlorides and amines (esterification and amide formation), and addition polymerisation for comparison.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Condensation polymerisation"}
The joining of monomers that each have two reactive functional groups, with the elimination of a small molecule (such as $\ce{H2O}$ or $\ce{HCl}$) every time a new link forms.
:::

:::definition{term="Polyester"}
A condensation polymer whose monomer residues are joined by ester links, –CO–O–.
:::

:::definition{term="Polyamide"}
A condensation polymer whose monomer residues are joined by amide links, –CO–NH– (called peptide links in proteins).
:::

:::keyfact{title="The rule of two"}
A monomer for condensation polymerisation needs **two** reactive groups, either the same group at each end (a diol, a diamine, a dicarboxylic acid, a dioyl dichloride) used with a partner, or two different groups in one molecule (a hydroxycarboxylic acid, an aminocarboxylic acid, an amino acid).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: formation of polyesters and polyamides, the molecule eliminated, repeat units and monomers, predicting the type of polymerisation (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge A Level, Paper 4). Hydrolysis and degradability are in the lesson on uses of polymers.
:::

## Polyesters

**From a diol and a dicarboxylic acid.** Ethane-1,2-diol and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid react, with heat and a catalyst, to form PET (poly(ethylene terephthalate), "Terylene"):

$$
n\,\ce{HOCH2CH2OH} + n\,\ce{HOOC-C6H4-COOH -> (-O-CH2CH2-O-CO-C6H4-CO-)_n} + 2n\,\ce{H2O}
$$

Each repeat unit contains two ester links, so two water molecules are lost per repeat unit. (Strictly, a chain of n units loses 2n − 1 molecules of water, because the two ends are not linked; for long chains "2n" is used.)

**From a diol and a dioyl dichloride.** Replacing the acid with its dichloride, ClOC–C₆H₄–COCl, gives the same polyester but eliminates **HCl** instead of water. Acyl chlorides are much more reactive, so the reaction is fast at room temperature without a catalyst.

**From a hydroxycarboxylic acid.** One molecule carries both groups. 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), $\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}$, gives poly(lactic acid), with repeat unit –O–CH(CH₃)–CO–. Poly(lactic acid) can be made from sugars obtained from crops such as maize or sugar cane.

## Polyamides

**From a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid (or dioyl dichloride).** Nylon-6,6 forms from hexane-1,6-diamine and hexanedioic acid:

$$
n\,\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2} + n\,\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH -> (-NH(CH2)6NH-CO(CH2)4CO-)_n} + 2n\,\ce{H2O}
$$

With hexanedioyl dichloride, ClOC(CH₂)₄COCl, HCl is eliminated instead. The "6,6" counts the carbon atoms in the diamine and in the diacid.

**From an aminocarboxylic acid.** 6-aminohexanoic acid, H₂N(CH₂)₅COOH, polymerises to nylon-6, repeat unit –NH(CH₂)₅CO–.

**From amino acids.** Proteins are polyamides made from 2-amino acids, $\ce{H2NCH(R)COOH}$. Each peptide link –CO–NH– forms with loss of water. Because the R groups differ, a protein chain is not a simple repeating pattern; the backbone is –NH–CH(R)–CO– repeated.

**Kevlar** is an aromatic polyamide made from benzene-1,4-diamine and benzene-1,4-dicarbonyl dichloride. Its chains are straight and rigid and lie side by side, held by many hydrogen bonds between the N–H of one chain and the C=O of the next. This makes Kevlar extremely strong for its weight (used in protective vests, helmets and ropes) and heat resistant.

### Hydrogen bonding between polyamide chains

In nylon, the δ+ hydrogen of an N–H group on one chain is attracted to a lone pair on the δ− oxygen of a C=O group on a neighbouring chain. These hydrogen bonds, repeated all along the chains, make polyamides stronger and give them higher melting points than poly(alkene)s of similar chain length, which have only instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces between chains.

## Writing repeat units and finding monomers

| Monomer(s) | Link formed | Small molecule lost | Repeat unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| HOCH₂CH₂OH + HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH | ester | H₂O | –O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO– |
| HOCH₂CH₂OH + ClOC–C₆H₄–COCl | ester | HCl | –O–CH₂CH₂–O–CO–C₆H₄–CO– |
| CH₃CH(OH)COOH | ester | H₂O | –O–CH(CH₃)–CO– |
| H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH | amide | H₂O | –NH(CH₂)₆NH–CO(CH₂)₄CO– |
| H₂N(CH₂)₅COOH | amide | H₂O | –NH(CH₂)₅CO– |
| H₂NCH(R)COOH (amino acids) | amide (peptide) | H₂O | –NH–CH(R)–CO– |

To find the monomers from a polymer section:

1. Find each ester (–CO–O–) or amide (–CO–NH–) link in the **main chain**.
2. Break the C–O or C–N bond of the link.
3. Add –OH to the carbonyl carbon (giving –COOH; or –Cl for a dioyl dichloride) and –H to the O or N (giving –OH or –NH₂).

:::example{title="Example 1: monomers of a polyester section"}
Section: –O–(CH₂)₄–O–CO–(CH₂)₂–CO–O–(CH₂)₄–O–CO–(CH₂)₂–CO–

1. The links are –CO–O–, so it is a polyester.
2. Break each C–O bond of the ester links.
3. The –O–(CH₂)₄–O– pieces become HO(CH₂)₄OH, butane-1,4-diol.
4. The –CO–(CH₂)₂–CO– pieces become HOOC(CH₂)₂COOH, butanedioic acid.
5. Repeat unit: –O(CH₂)₄O–CO(CH₂)₂CO–; two molecules of water are lost per repeat unit.
:::

## Predicting the type of polymerisation

| Clue | Type |
|---|---|
| one monomer with a C=C and no other reactive groups | addition |
| two reactive groups per monomer (OH, COOH, COCl, NH₂) | condensation |
| polymer backbone all carbon atoms | addition |
| ester or amide links in the backbone | condensation |
| a small molecule is formed as well as the polymer | condensation |

A molecule such as $\ce{CH2=CHCOOH}$ (propenoic acid) has a C=C and a COOH: under radical conditions it forms an addition polymer through the C=C, with COOH side groups. The backbone test still works.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of a repeat unit and water released]
Nylon-6,6 is made from hexane-1,6-diamine (Mr 116) and hexanedioic acid (Mr 146).

1. Each repeat unit forms from one of each monomer with the loss of two water molecules (Mr 18.0 each).
2. Mr(repeat unit) = 116 + 146 − 2 × 18.0 = 226.
3. A chain of average Mr 1.13 × 10⁴ therefore contains about 1.13 × 10⁴ ÷ 226 = 50 repeat units.
4. Making 226 g (one mole of repeat units) releases 2 × 18.0 = 36.0 g of water.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Wrong number of small molecules"}
A diol + diacid repeat unit has **two** links, so two H₂O (or two HCl) are lost per repeat unit, not one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving OH or H on the repeat unit"}
The repeat unit shows the residues with the link already formed and a continuation bond at each end; it has no –OH or –H end groups.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every polymer with oxygen a polyester"}
Check the main chain. Ester groups in side chains (as in poly(ethenyl ethanoate)) belong to an addition polymer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the amide and ester link"}
Amide: –CO–NH–. Ester: –CO–O–. Write the carbonyl carbon next to the N or O.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1, Paper 2 and Paper 3 Section C; Cambridge Paper 4. Typical tasks: draw a repeat unit from two monomers, identify monomers from a section, name the small molecule, explain the strength of nylon or Kevlar.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Deduce* the monomers: break the links and add H and OH (or Cl). *Explain* why nylon is stronger than poly(ethene): hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O of neighbouring chains, stronger than instantaneous dipole forces. *Predict* the type of polymerisation: name the groups that react and say whether a small molecule is lost.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Condensation polymers form from monomers with two reactive groups, losing a small molecule (H₂O or HCl) at every link.
- Polyesters (ester links): diol + diacid or dioyl dichloride, or a hydroxy acid (PET, poly(lactic acid)).
- Polyamides (amide links): diamine + diacid or dioyl dichloride, an amino acid or aminocarboxylic acid (nylon-6,6, nylon-6, proteins, Kevlar).
- To find the monomers, break the links in the main chain and add H and OH (or Cl).
- Hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O on neighbouring chains make polyamides such as Kevlar strong.
:::
