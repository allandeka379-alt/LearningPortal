## Introduction

Zimbabwe generates most of its electricity from two very different sources: coal burned at thermal power stations such as Hwange, and falling water at Kariba. Both keep the lights on, and both have environmental costs. Coal smoke carries sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, fine particles and carbon dioxide; a hydroelectric scheme needs a huge lake and enough rain to fill it.

This lesson is about the chemistry behind those costs and the chemistry used to reduce them: which pollutants come from where, how sulfur dioxide is trapped before it leaves the chimney, how cars are made cleaner, and how different ways of generating power compare. You should already know acid-base reactions, the oxides of nitrogen and sulfur, combustion, and catalytic converters in outline.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Primary pollutant"}
A harmful substance released directly into the air from a source, for example sulfur dioxide from a power-station chimney or carbon monoxide from an exhaust pipe.
:::

:::definition{term="Flue-gas desulfurisation (FGD)"}
The removal of sulfur dioxide from the waste gases (flue gases) of a furnace or power station, usually by reaction with a base such as limestone or lime, before the gases leave the chimney.
:::

:::definition{term="Lean-burn engine"}
A petrol engine that runs on a mixture with more air than is needed for complete combustion of the fuel.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable energy source"}
A source that is replaced naturally as fast as it is used, such as sunlight, wind, flowing water or crops grown for fuel.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This lesson is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 covers the oxides of nitrogen, acid rain and catalytic converters within its nitrogen and sulfur topic, but not FGD, power generation or radon.
:::

## The main industrial air pollutants

| Pollutant | Main sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| sulfur dioxide, SO₂ | burning coal and heavy fuel oil containing sulfur; smelting sulfide ores | acid rain (damages plants, buildings, lakes); irritates lungs and worsens asthma |
| oxides of nitrogen, NO and NO₂ (NOₓ) | any very hot combustion (engines, furnaces): $\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}$, then $\ce{2NO + O2 -> 2NO2}$ | acid rain (nitric acid); photochemical smog with hydrocarbons in sunlight; lung irritation |
| particulates (soot, fly ash, fine dust) | coal burning, diesel engines, open burning, quarrying and mining | breathing and heart disease; dirty buildings; reduced visibility |
| carbon monoxide, CO | incomplete combustion in engines, stoves and furnaces | toxic: binds strongly to haemoglobin and reduces oxygen transport |

Acid rain forms when these oxides react with water and oxygen in clouds: $\ce{2SO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 2H2SO4}$ and $\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}$.

## Controlling sulfur dioxide

**1. Wet limestone scrubbing (FGD).** Flue gas is sprayed with a slurry of powdered limestone in water. Sulfur dioxide is an acidic oxide and reacts with the base:

$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) + SO2(g) -> CaSO3(s) + CO2(g)}
$$

Air blown through the slurry oxidises the calcium sulfite to calcium sulfate, which crystallises as **gypsum**, CaSO₄·2H₂O, a useful by-product for plasterboard and cement:

$$
\ce{2CaSO3 + O2 -> 2CaSO4}
$$

A slurry of **lime** (calcium hydroxide) works in the same way: $\ce{Ca(OH)2 + SO2 -> CaSO3 + H2O}$.

**2. Alkaline scrubbing.** Smaller plants and smelters may pass the gas through sodium hydroxide solution: $\ce{2NaOH + SO2 -> Na2SO3 + H2O}$. The sulfite can be oxidised and sold or treated. Many metal smelters instead convert concentrated SO₂ into sulfuric acid by the Contact process.

**3. Fluidised-bed combustion.** Powdered coal is mixed with powdered limestone and burned in a bed that is kept "fluid" by air blown up through it. The limestone decomposes and the calcium oxide traps the sulfur dioxide inside the furnace:

$$
\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}
$$

$$
\ce{2CaO + 2SO2 + O2 -> 2CaSO4}
$$

Because the burning is efficient at a relatively low temperature (below about 900 °C), less nitrogen monoxide forms as well.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sulfur dioxide from coal, and the limestone to remove it]
A power station burns 1.00 tonne (1.00 × 10⁶ g) of coal containing 1.80% sulfur by mass. Calculate (a) the mass of SO₂ formed and (b) the minimum mass of limestone (CaCO₃) needed to remove it. (Ar: C 12.0, O 16.0, S 32.1, Ca 40.1)

1. Mass of sulfur = 0.0180 × 1.00 × 10⁶ g = 1.80 × 10⁴ g; amount = 1.80 × 10⁴ ÷ 32.1 = 560.7 mol.
2. S + O₂ → SO₂, so 560.7 mol of SO₂ forms; mass = 560.7 × 64.1 = 3.59 × 10⁴ g = 35.9 kg.
3. CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂ is 1 : 1, so 560.7 mol of CaCO₃ is needed: 560.7 × 100.1 = 5.61 × 10⁴ g = 56.1 kg.
4. Note that the scrubbing releases carbon dioxide; removing one pollutant adds a little to another.
:::

## Cleaner petrol engines

**Lean-burn engines** use extra air, so the fuel burns more completely: less carbon monoxide and fewer unburnt hydrocarbons are emitted and fuel economy improves. The lower combustion temperature also reduces NOₓ, although the excess oxygen makes it harder for a normal catalytic converter to reduce the NOₓ that does form.

**Catalytic converters** in the exhaust of petrol cars contain platinum, palladium and rhodium on a ceramic honeycomb:

$$
\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}
$$

$$
\ce{2CO + O2 -> 2CO2}
$$

Unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water. The converter works only once it is hot and is poisoned by lead, so unleaded fuel must be used.

## Generating power: environmental considerations

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| coal (thermal stations) | reliable on demand; local coal at Hwange; established technology | CO₂ (climate change); SO₂, NOₓ, particulates; large amounts of ash; mining damages land |
| nuclear (uranium fission) | no CO₂ while operating; tiny mass of fuel; reliable | radioactive waste that stays hazardous for very long times; risk of accidents; very high cost; uranium mining hazards |
| hydroelectric (Kariba) | renewable; no emissions in operation; quick to start | large areas flooded and people moved when built; output falls in droughts when lake levels drop |
| solar | abundant sunshine in Zimbabwe; no emissions in use; suits rural homes and clinics | output only in daylight and lower when cloudy; needs batteries or backup |
| wind | renewable; no emissions in use | variable; few sites with strong steady winds |
| biogas | methane from anaerobic digestion of manure and food waste; slurry left is a fertiliser | small scale; burning still makes CO₂ (but from recently grown material) |
| biofuels (for example ethanol from sugar cane) | renewable; can be blended with petrol | uses farmland and water that could grow food; energy is used in growing and processing |

**Carbon dioxide and climate change.** Burning fossil fuels returns carbon that was stored underground for millions of years to the atmosphere as CO₂, a greenhouse gas that absorbs infrared radiation emitted by the Earth. Rising CO₂ concentration is the main cause of the warming observed in recent decades. Biofuels and biogas are described as close to carbon-neutral because the CO₂ they release was recently absorbed by photosynthesis, but energy used in farming and processing means they are not completely neutral.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: carbon dioxide from coal]
Coal containing 80.0% carbon by mass is burned completely. Calculate the mass of CO₂ produced from 1.00 tonne of coal.

1. Mass of carbon = 0.800 × 1.00 t = 0.800 t.
2. C + O₂ → CO₂: 12.0 t of carbon gives 44.0 t of CO₂.
3. Mass of CO₂ = 0.800 × 44.0 ÷ 12.0 = 2.93 t (3 s.f.): burning coal produces nearly three times its own mass of CO₂.
:::

## Radon and nuclear hazards

Uranium in some rocks (granites, and uranium-bearing ores) decays slowly through a series of radioactive elements. One of them is **radon**, an unreactive gas that seeps out of the ground and can collect in poorly ventilated buildings, underground mines and caves. Radon and its decay products emit alpha particles; when breathed in, they irradiate the lining of the lungs and raise the risk of lung cancer. Good ventilation reduces the exposure.

Nuclear power stations release very little radiation in normal operation, but serious accidents (Chernobyl, Ukraine, 1986; Fukushima, Japan, 2011) released radioactive isotopes such as iodine-131 and caesium-137, which contaminated land and food over wide areas and led to evacuations. These events are why nuclear plants need multiple safety systems and strict regulation, and why the long-term storage of nuclear waste is debated.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying catalytic converters remove carbon dioxide"}
They turn CO, NOₓ and hydrocarbons into CO₂, N₂ and H₂O. Carbon dioxide is a product.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that limestone scrubbing removes all pollution"}
FGD removes SO₂ only. It does not remove CO₂ (it adds a little) or NOₓ.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling nuclear power 'pollution-free'"}
It produces no CO₂ while operating, but it produces radioactive waste and carries a risk of accidents. Give both sides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SO₂ (coal, smelting) and NOₓ (hot combustion) cause acid rain; particulates harm lungs; CO binds to haemoglobin.
- FGD: CaCO₃ + SO₂ → CaSO₃ + CO₂, then 2CaSO₃ + O₂ → 2CaSO₄ (gypsum); lime or NaOH scrubbing works similarly.
- Fluidised beds trap SO₂ with limestone inside the furnace and, at lower temperature, form less NOₓ.
- Lean-burn engines burn fuel more completely; catalytic converters (Pt, Pd, Rh) convert CO, NOₓ and hydrocarbons to CO₂, N₂ and H₂O.
- Coal gives CO₂ and other pollutants; nuclear gives waste and accident risk; hydro, solar, wind, biogas and biofuels each have their own trade-offs; radon from uranium-bearing rocks raises lung-cancer risk.
:::
