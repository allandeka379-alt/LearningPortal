## Introduction

Every town produces solid waste, sewage and industrial effluent, and every mine leaves behind waste rock and tailings. Where this waste goes, and what chemistry happens to it there, decides whether rivers stay fit to drink, whether fish survive in lakes such as those that supply Harare and Bulawayo, and whether land near old mines can be farmed.

This lesson treats waste as chemistry: which reactions make a landfill dangerous, why sewage removes oxygen from a river, why a few milligrams of mercury matter, how ion exchange cleans metal-laden water, and why rock containing pyrite turns streams acidic. You should already know redox, acid-base reactions, pH, precipitation of hydroxides and the meaning of half-life.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Leachate"}
The liquid that drains through a landfill, carrying dissolved and suspended substances from the waste, which can pollute groundwater and streams.
:::

:::definition{term="Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)"}
The mass of dissolved oxygen used by microorganisms to break down the organic matter in a sample of water (usually in mg dm⁻³ over five days at 20 °C); a high BOD means heavy organic pollution.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
The over-enrichment of a lake or river with nutrients, mainly nitrates and phosphates, which causes rapid growth of algae and water plants and then oxygen depletion when they die and decompose.
:::

:::definition{term="Bioaccumulation"}
The build-up of a persistent substance, such as mercury, in an organism over time because it is taken in faster than it is removed; it increases further up a food chain (biomagnification).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This lesson is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 does not examine waste management.
:::

## Ion exchange in treating industrial waste water

Waste water from electroplating, battery or tanning works may contain toxic metal ions. An **ion-exchange resin** is a polymer bead covered in charged groups whose counter-ions (H⁺ or Na⁺ on a cation-exchange resin) can be swapped for ions in the water:

$$
\ce{2R-SO3H + Pb^2+ -> (R-SO3)2Pb + 2H+}
$$

The toxic ions are held on the resin and harmless H⁺ (later neutralised) passes out. When the resin is full, it is **regenerated** by washing with concentrated acid, which reverses the reaction and gives a small volume of concentrated metal solution from which the metal can be recovered. Anion-exchange resins (with OH⁻ or Cl⁻ counter-ions) remove anions such as chromate(VI).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sizing an ion-exchange column]
500 dm³ of waste water contains 0.0200 g dm⁻³ of Pb²⁺. A cation-exchange resin can take up 2.00 mol of H⁺-equivalent per kg. Calculate the minimum mass of resin needed. (Ar Pb = 207.2)

1. Mass of Pb²⁺ = 500 dm³ × 0.0200 g dm⁻³ = 10.0 g; amount = 10.0 ÷ 207.2 = 4.83 × 10⁻² mol.
2. Each Pb²⁺ replaces two H⁺, so 9.65 × 10⁻² mol of exchange sites are needed.
3. Mass of resin = 9.65 × 10⁻² mol ÷ 2.00 mol kg⁻¹ = 4.83 × 10⁻² kg = 48.3 g.
:::

## Solid waste: landfill, incineration and recycling

**Landfill.** Waste is buried in layers in a lined pit and covered with soil.

- Rainwater percolating through forms **leachate**, which may carry heavy metals, acids and organic compounds into groundwater. Modern sites use clay or plastic liners and collect and treat the leachate.
- Buried organic matter decomposes **anaerobically**, forming **methane** (and carbon dioxide). Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas and can explode if it collects in buildings; it can be piped off and burned as a fuel.

**Incineration.** Burning waste reduces its volume greatly and can generate electricity. But plastics such as PVC contain chlorine, and burning them at too low a temperature, or cooling the flue gas slowly, can form **dioxins**, very toxic chlorinated organic compounds. Incinerators therefore burn waste at a high, controlled temperature (above about 850 °C) with plenty of air, cool the gases quickly, and scrub out acidic gases such as hydrogen chloride. The ash may contain heavy metals and has to go to a controlled landfill.

**Recycling** of metals, glass, paper and plastics saves raw materials and energy (aluminium in particular, because its extraction by electrolysis uses so much electricity), and reduces the volume going to landfill. Its costs are collecting and sorting, and some materials (mixed plastics) are hard to recycle.

## Dumping waste in rivers and the sea

Dumping is cheap and dilution reduces concentrations, but toxic and persistent substances are not destroyed; they spread, settle in sediments and build up in food chains.

**Sewage** contains organic matter that bacteria break down using dissolved oxygen. A high BOD lowers the oxygen concentration, so fish and other animals suffocate. Untreated sewage also spreads diseases such as cholera and typhoid.

**Eutrophication.** Treated sewage and fertiliser run-off still carry nitrates and phosphates:

1. nutrients enter the water and algae and water plants grow rapidly (an algal bloom; water hyacinth may cover the surface);
2. light is blocked and plants below die; the algae themselves soon die;
3. aerobic bacteria decompose the dead material, using up the dissolved oxygen;
4. fish and other animals die from lack of oxygen.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: oxygen demand of dissolved sugar]
Waste water from a food factory contains 12.0 mg dm⁻³ of glucose, which bacteria oxidise: $\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}$. Calculate the mass of oxygen needed per dm³ (Mr glucose = 180.0; O₂ = 32.0).

1. Amount of glucose = 12.0 × 10⁻³ g ÷ 180.0 g mol⁻¹ = 6.67 × 10⁻⁵ mol per dm³.
2. Amount of O₂ = 6 × 6.67 × 10⁻⁵ = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
3. Mass of O₂ = 4.00 × 10⁻⁴ × 32.0 = 1.28 × 10⁻² g = 12.8 mg per dm³. This is more than water can hold at 20 °C (data: about 9 mg dm⁻³ when saturated), so the river would be stripped of oxygen.
:::

**Oil spills** float as a layer that stops oxygen dissolving, coats birds' feathers and animals, and poisons shoreline life. Booms and skimmers collect it; dispersants break it into droplets but are themselves harmful; bacteria slowly break down what remains.

## Heavy metals

Heavy metals cannot be broken down; they are toxic in small amounts and bioaccumulate.

| Metal | Typical sources | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| lead | old lead paint, lead-acid battery recycling, old water pipes, leaded fuel in the past | damages the nervous system and brain development, especially in children; anaemia |
| mercury | small-scale gold processing (gold is amalgamated with mercury, which is then evaporated by heating), some industries | vapour is toxic when inhaled; in water bacteria convert it to methylmercury, which builds up in fish and damages the nervous system |
| chromium | electroplating, leather tanning, chromite processing | chromium(VI) is toxic and carcinogenic; chromium(III) is much less harmful |

Treatments include precipitating metals as insoluble hydroxides or sulfides by adding lime or sodium sulfide, ion exchange, and, for chromium(VI), reducing it to chromium(III) (for example with iron(II) or sulfur dioxide) before precipitating Cr(OH)₃. In small-scale gold processing, using retorts that capture and condense the mercury vapour greatly reduces exposure.

## Radioactive waste and mine waste

**Radioactive waste** from hospitals, research and nuclear power varies widely. Low-level waste (gloves, clothing) is packed and buried in shallow engineered sites. High-level waste (spent fuel) is intensely radioactive and hot, and some isotopes have very long half-lives: it is cooled under water for years, can be **vitrified** (sealed into glass), and is stored in steel containers; deep geological disposal in stable rock is the long-term plan in several countries. The problem is that it must be isolated for thousands of years.

**Mine waste and acid mine drainage.** Waste rock and tailings that contain pyrite (FeS₂) react with air and water:

$$
\ce{2FeS2 + 7O2 + 2H2O -> 2FeSO4 + 2H2SO4}
$$

The sulfuric acid lowers the pH of streams (often below 3), dissolves heavy metals from the rock and kills aquatic life; iron(III) hydroxide forms orange deposits on stream beds. It can continue for decades after a mine closes. Treatment includes covering the waste to keep out air and water, neutralising with lime or limestone, and passing the water through constructed wetlands.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying sewage kills fish because it is poisonous"}
The main effect is oxygen depletion: bacteria use dissolved oxygen to break down the organic matter.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing eutrophication as algae using up oxygen"}
Living algae release oxygen in daylight. Oxygen is used up when aerobic bacteria decompose the dead algae and plants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking dilution removes heavy metals"}
Heavy metals are not broken down; they settle in sediments and accumulate in organisms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ion exchange swaps toxic metal ions for H⁺ or Na⁺ on a resin; regeneration with acid gives a concentrated solution for recovery.
- Landfill produces leachate and methane; incineration must be hot (above about 850 °C), well aired and quickly cooled to avoid dioxins; recycling saves resources and energy.
- Sewage raises BOD and removes dissolved oxygen; nitrates and phosphates cause eutrophication; oil spills block oxygen and coat wildlife.
- Lead, mercury (small-scale gold processing) and chromium(VI) are persistent, toxic and bioaccumulate.
- High-level radioactive waste needs long-term isolation; pyrite in mine waste forms sulfuric acid (acid mine drainage), treated with lime.
:::
