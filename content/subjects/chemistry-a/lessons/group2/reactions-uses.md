## Introduction

Walk past a farm supply shop in Chinhoyi and you will see bags of agricultural lime. Visit a building site and you will find cement and plaster. In a hospital, a patient may drink a white "barium meal" before an X-ray, and a pharmacy shelf holds antacid tablets of magnesium hydroxide. All of these are compounds of the Group 2 elements, the **alkaline earth metals**: magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium (beryllium and radium are not studied here).

Group 2 is the best place to see how a group trend works: the same type of reaction, with the same type of equation, becoming faster or slower in a regular way as you go down. This lesson describes the reactions and the trends and the uses of the compounds; the explanations of the thermal stability and solubility trends are in the next lesson. You should already know electron configurations, ionisation energy, and how to write equations for acids with metals, bases and carbonates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkaline earth metals"}
The Group 2 elements; each atom has two electrons in its outer s sub-shell (ns²) and forms an M²⁺ ion in its compounds.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal decomposition"}
The breaking down of a compound into simpler substances by heat alone, for example $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal stability"}
The resistance of a compound to thermal decomposition; a more stable compound needs a higher temperature to decompose.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: physical trends from data, reactions of the elements with oxygen, water and dilute acids, reactions of the oxides, hydroxides and carbonates, thermal decomposition of carbonates and nitrates, and the solubility trends of the hydroxides and sulfates. ZIMSEC only: the uses of Group 2 compounds (MgO, CaO, Ca(OH)₂, CaCO₃, MgSO₄, CaSO₄, BaSO₄, Mg(OH)₂ and magnesium trisilicate).
:::

## Physical properties

| Element | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| electron configuration | [Ne] 3s² | [Ar] 4s² | [Kr] 5s² | [Xe] 6s² |
| first IE / kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet) | 736 | 590 | 548 | 502 |
| metallic radius / nm (textbook data) | 0.160 | 0.197 | 0.215 | 0.217 |

Down the group the atomic radius increases because each element has one more occupied shell. Although the nuclear charge increases, the extra shells increase shielding and distance, so the outer electrons are held less strongly and the ionisation energies fall. The **reactivity increases down the group**, because the two outer electrons are lost more easily. Melting points generally decrease from calcium to barium as the metallic bonding weakens (larger cations, same charge); magnesium does not fit this pattern because it has a different crystal structure.

## Reactions of the elements

### With oxygen

All four burn in air or oxygen to form a white ionic oxide, $\ce{2M(s) + O2(g) -> 2MO(s)}$, with a characteristic flame: magnesium brilliant white, calcium brick-red (orange-red), strontium crimson, barium apple-green. Barium also forms some barium peroxide, BaO₂, in excess oxygen.

### With water

| Metal | With cold water | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Mg | very slow; a few bubbles | $\ce{Mg + 2H2O -> Mg(OH)2 + H2}$ |
| Mg (heated) | with steam: bright glow, white solid | $\ce{Mg + H2O(g) -> MgO + H2}$ |
| Ca | steady fizzing; cloudy white suspension | $\ce{Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$ |
| Sr | faster | $\ce{Sr + 2H2O -> Sr(OH)2 + H2}$ |
| Ba | vigorous | $\ce{Ba + 2H2O -> Ba(OH)2 + H2}$ |

The solutions become more alkaline down the group because the hydroxides become more soluble. Calcium's mixture turns cloudy because some calcium hydroxide does not dissolve.

### With dilute acids

With dilute hydrochloric acid every metal fizzes and forms a soluble chloride: $\ce{M + 2HCl -> MCl2 + H2}$, faster down the group. With dilute sulfuric acid, magnesium reacts completely ($\ce{Mg + H2SO4 -> MgSO4 + H2}$), but for calcium, strontium and barium the reaction soon slows or stops: an insoluble sulfate coats the metal and keeps the acid away.

## Reactions of the compounds

| Compound | With water | With dilute HCl |
|---|---|---|
| oxide MO | $\ce{MO + H2O -> M(OH)2}$; pH rises down the group (Mg about 9-10, Ca about 12, Ba about 13-14) | $\ce{MO + 2HCl -> MCl2 + H2O}$ |
| hydroxide M(OH)₂ | dissolves more down the group | $\ce{M(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MCl2 + 2H2O}$ |
| carbonate MCO₃ | insoluble | $\ce{MCO3 + 2HCl -> MCl2 + H2O + CO2}$ (effervescence) |

With dilute sulfuric acid the products are sulfates: $\ce{MgO + H2SO4 -> MgSO4 + H2O}$; for barium the white barium sulfate precipitates, and a lump of a calcium, strontium or barium carbonate soon stops fizzing as it is coated with insoluble sulfate. Calcium oxide reacts with water very exothermically ("slaking" quicklime), and steam can be seen.

## Thermal decomposition

**Carbonates** decompose to the oxide and carbon dioxide:

$$
\ce{MCO3(s) -> MO(s) + CO2(g)}
$$

**Nitrates** decompose to the oxide, brown nitrogen dioxide and oxygen:

$$
\ce{2M(NO3)2(s) -> 2MO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)}
$$

The **thermal stability increases down the group**: magnesium carbonate decomposes easily on heating, calcium carbonate needs strong heating (in industry, a lime kiln at about 900 °C or more), and barium carbonate needs much higher temperatures. The same order applies to the nitrates. Heat them in a fume cupboard: NO₂ is toxic.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: gas from heating calcium nitrate]
3.28 g of anhydrous calcium nitrate is heated until decomposition is complete. Calculate the total volume of gas formed at room conditions. (Mr Ca(NO₃)₂ = 164.1; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

1. $n(\ce{Ca(NO3)2}) = 3.28 \div 164.1 = 0.0200$ mol.
2. $\ce{2Ca(NO3)2 -> 2CaO + 4NO2 + O2}$: 2 mol of nitrate give 5 mol of gas, so $n(\text{gas}) = 0.0200 \times \dfrac{5}{2} = 0.0500$ mol.
3. $V = 0.0500 \times 24.0 = 1.20$ dm³ (0.960 dm³ of NO₂ and 0.240 dm³ of O₂).
:::

## Solubility trends

| Compound | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba | Trend down the group |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| hydroxide | sparingly soluble | slightly soluble | more soluble | soluble | increases |
| sulfate | soluble | sparingly soluble | almost insoluble | insoluble | decreases |

These two opposite trends are used in tests: acidified barium chloride detects sulfate ions because barium sulfate is so insoluble, and limewater is a dilute solution of calcium hydroxide.

## Uses of Group 2 compounds (ZIMSEC detail)

| Compound | Use | Property it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| magnesium oxide, MgO | refractory lining of furnaces | very high melting point (strong ionic lattice) |
| calcium oxide (quicklime) and calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) | neutralising acidic soils; water treatment | basic |
| calcium carbonate (limestone) | agricultural lime; making cement, glass and quicklime; extraction of iron | basic; decomposes to CaO |
| calcium sulfate (gypsum, CaSO₄·2H₂O) | plaster for buildings and plaster of Paris for casts | sets as it rehydrates |
| magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) | used in medicine and as a fertiliser supplement for magnesium | soluble source of Mg²⁺ |
| barium sulfate | "barium meal" for X-ray imaging of the gut | insoluble, so barium ions are not absorbed; dense, absorbs X-rays |
| magnesium hydroxide (milk of magnesia) and magnesium trisilicate | antacids for indigestion | weak bases that neutralise excess stomach acid |

Soluble barium compounds are toxic; barium sulfate is safe to swallow only because it is so insoluble.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: an antacid tablet]
An antacid tablet contains 0.500 g of magnesium hydroxide. Calculate the amount of hydrochloric acid it neutralises and the volume of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl that this represents. (Mr Mg(OH)₂ = 58.3)

1. $n(\ce{Mg(OH)2}) = 0.500 \div 58.3 = 0.00858$ mol.
2. $\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + 2H2O}$, so $n(\ce{HCl}) = 2 \times 0.00858 = 0.0172$ mol.
3. $V = 0.0172 \div 0.100 = 0.172$ dm³ $= 172$ cm³.
:::

## Predictions

Radium is below barium, so it should react even more vigorously with water, have an even more soluble hydroxide and an even less soluble sulfate, and its carbonate should be the most thermally stable. Beryllium, at the top, is untypical (its compounds are largely covalent), which is why the syllabuses start at magnesium.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the two solubility trends"}
Hydroxides become **more** soluble down the group; sulfates become **less** soluble. Learn the two together: Mg(OH)₂ and BaSO₄ are the insoluble ends.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying thermal stability decreases down the group"}
Barium carbonate is the hardest to decompose. Stability **increases** down the group.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong products for the nitrates"}
Group 2 nitrates give the oxide, NO₂ and O₂ (unlike sodium nitrate, which gives the nitrite and O₂). Balance as 2M(NO₃)₂ → 2MO + 4NO₂ + O₂.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling barium sulfate poisonous"}
Soluble barium salts are toxic; barium sulfate is used in medicine because it is insoluble.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 (uses of compounds appear in Paper 1 and Paper 3 Section B); Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 for reactions and trends. The explanations of the trends are tested at Cambridge A Level (Paper 4) and in ZIMSEC Form 5.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* a reaction: observations (effervescence, flame colour, cloudiness, brown gas) and a balanced equation. *State* a trend: say which way it goes down the group. *Predict* for radium or strontium: name the trend you are using. *Suggest* a use: link it to the property in the table (basic, insoluble, high melting point).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Down Group 2 the radius increases, the ionisation energies fall and the reactivity increases.
- The metals form MO with oxygen, M(OH)₂ and H₂ with water (Mg needs steam to react quickly), and salts and H₂ with dilute acids; insoluble sulfates stop the reaction with sulfuric acid for Ca, Sr and Ba.
- Oxides and hydroxides are basic; carbonates fizz with acids. Carbonates decompose to MO + CO₂, nitrates to MO + NO₂ + O₂, and thermal stability increases down the group.
- Hydroxides become more soluble and sulfates less soluble down the group.
- Uses: MgO furnace linings; CaO, Ca(OH)₂ and CaCO₃ for soil and building; CaSO₄ plaster; BaSO₄ barium meal; Mg(OH)₂ and magnesium trisilicate antacids.
:::
