## Introduction

A lime kiln must be run far hotter than a laboratory Bunsen burner because calcium carbonate is reluctant to give up its carbon dioxide; magnesium carbonate, by contrast, breaks down readily. Barium sulfate is so insoluble that a patient can swallow it safely before an X-ray, while magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) dissolves easily. The previous lesson described these Group 2 trends. This lesson explains them.

Two ideas do all the work: the **charge density** of the Group 2 cation, which decides how strongly it distorts (polarises) a neighbouring anion, and the **energy balance** when an ionic solid dissolves, set by lattice energy and hydration enthalpies. You should already know the trends themselves, the definitions of lattice energy and enthalpy change of hydration, and how to use an energy cycle.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Charge density"}
The charge of an ion divided by its size (radius or volume). A small, highly charged cation has a high charge density.
:::

:::definition{term="Polarisation (of an anion)"}
The distortion of the electron cloud of an anion by a neighbouring cation, which pulls electron density towards itself. Large anions are the most easily polarised.
:::

:::definition{term="Lattice energy"}
The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions under standard conditions, for example $\ce{Mg^2+(g) + SO4^2-(g) -> MgSO4(s)}$; it is always exothermic.
:::

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change of hydration"}
The enthalpy change when one mole of a specified gaseous ion dissolves in enough water to give an infinitely dilute solution, for example $\ce{Ca^2+(g) -> Ca^2+(aq)}$; it is always exothermic.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards explain these trends. Cambridge examines the explanations at A Level (Paper 4: topic 27); ZIMSEC teaches them in Form 5 with the Group II topic. ZIMSEC uses the term **charge density**; Cambridge talks about the effect of ionic charge and radius. Both mean the same idea.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Enthalpy change of solution from a cycle"}
$\Delta H^\ominus_\text{sol} = -\Delta H^\ominus_\text{latt} + \sum \Delta H^\ominus_\text{hyd}$ (lattice energy as the exothermic formation of the lattice from gaseous ions).
:::

## Charge density of the cations

Data (ionic radius, textbook values):

| Ion | Mg²⁺ | Ca²⁺ | Sr²⁺ | Ba²⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ionic radius / nm | 0.065 | 0.099 | 0.113 | 0.135 |
| charge ÷ radius / nm⁻¹ | 30.8 | 20.2 | 17.7 | 14.8 |

All four ions carry a 2+ charge, but the radius increases down the group because each ion has one more occupied shell. So the charge density **decreases** from Mg²⁺ to Ba²⁺.

## Thermal stability of the carbonates and nitrates

When a carbonate decomposes, $\ce{MCO3 -> MO + CO2}$, one C–O bond in the carbonate ion breaks and an oxide ion is left with the metal ion.

1. The carbonate ion is large and its electron cloud is easily distorted.
2. A small cation with a high charge density (Mg²⁺) attracts the electron cloud of the carbonate ion towards itself: it **polarises** the anion.
3. This draws electron density away from the C–O bonds nearest the cation, **weakening** one of them.
4. Less heat energy is then needed to break that bond and release CO₂, so the carbonate decomposes at a lower temperature.
5. Down the group the cation's charge density falls, it polarises the carbonate ion less, the C–O bonds are weakened less, and a higher temperature is needed.

The nitrates follow exactly the same argument: the large nitrate ion is polarised less by larger cations, so $\ce{2M(NO3)2 -> 2MO + 4NO2 + O2}$ needs more heat further down the group.

A second, supporting point: the oxide formed is more stabilised (larger lattice energy) when the cation is small. The gain in lattice energy on going from MCO₃ to MO is greatest for magnesium, which also favours decomposition at the top of the group. Exams usually want the polarisation explanation.

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining an order of decomposition"}
Explain why magnesium nitrate decomposes at a lower temperature than barium nitrate.

1. Mg²⁺ and Ba²⁺ have the same charge, but Mg²⁺ is much smaller, so it has the higher charge density.
2. Mg²⁺ polarises the large nitrate ion more, distorting its electron cloud.
3. The N–O bonds in the nitrate ion are weakened more, so they break more easily on heating.
4. Therefore magnesium nitrate decomposes at a lower temperature; Ba²⁺ polarises the anion least and barium nitrate is the most stable.
:::

## Solubility: the energy balance

When an ionic solid dissolves we can imagine two steps:

1. Break up the lattice into gaseous ions: this needs energy equal to $-\Delta H_\text{latt}$ (endothermic).
2. Hydrate the gaseous ions: this releases the hydration enthalpies of the cation and the anions (exothermic).

$$
\Delta H_\text{sol} = -\Delta H_\text{latt} + \Delta H_\text{hyd}(\ce{M^2+}) + \Delta H_\text{hyd}(\text{anions})
$$

The more exothermic (or less endothermic) ΔH_sol is, the more soluble the compound tends to be. (Entropy changes also matter, so ΔH_sol is a guide to the trend rather than a complete explanation.)

Both terms get **less negative** down the group, because both depend on the size of the cation:

- **Hydration enthalpy** of M²⁺ becomes less exothermic as the ion gets larger and attracts water molecules less strongly. Data (textbook values): Mg²⁺ −1920, Ca²⁺ −1650, Sr²⁺ −1480, Ba²⁺ −1360 kJ mol⁻¹.
- **Lattice energy** becomes less exothermic as the cation gets larger, because the ions in the lattice are further apart. How much it changes depends on the anion.

### Sulfates: solubility decreases

The sulfate ion is **large**. In the lattice the distance between ion centres is (radius of cation + radius of anion), and with a large anion this sum changes only a little as the cation grows. So the lattice energy of the sulfates falls only slightly down the group, while the hydration enthalpy of the cation falls a lot. The energy released on hydration falls faster than the energy needed to break the lattice, so ΔH_sol becomes **more endothermic** (less exothermic) and the sulfates become **less soluble**.

### Hydroxides: solubility increases

The hydroxide ion is **small**. Now the cation's size makes up a large fraction of the distance between ions, so the lattice energy falls **steeply** down the group, faster than the hydration enthalpy of the cation. Less energy is needed to break the lattice compared with the energy released on hydration, so ΔH_sol becomes more exothermic (less endothermic) and the hydroxides become **more soluble**.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two sulfates (values given in the question)]
Use these values (kJ mol⁻¹) to find ΔH_sol for MgSO₄ and BaSO₄ and explain the difference in solubility: lattice energy MgSO₄ −2870, BaSO₄ −2470; hydration enthalpy Mg²⁺ −1920, Ba²⁺ −1360, SO₄²⁻ −1100.

1. $\Delta H_\text{sol}(\ce{MgSO4}) = -(-2870) + (-1920) + (-1100) = -150$ kJ mol⁻¹.
2. $\Delta H_\text{sol}(\ce{BaSO4}) = -(-2470) + (-1360) + (-1100) = +10$ kJ mol⁻¹.
3. From Mg to Ba the lattice energy becomes less exothermic by 400 kJ mol⁻¹, but the cation's hydration enthalpy becomes less exothermic by 560 kJ mol⁻¹.
4. The fall in hydration enthalpy outweighs the fall in lattice energy, so ΔH_sol changes from exothermic to endothermic: magnesium sulfate is soluble, barium sulfate is not.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: the hydroxides (values given in the question)]
Lattice energy Mg(OH)₂ −2995, Ba(OH)₂ −2320; hydration enthalpy OH⁻ −460; cation values as above (kJ mol⁻¹). Find ΔH_sol for each.

1. $\Delta H_\text{sol}(\ce{Mg(OH)2}) = 2995 - 1920 - 2 \times 460 = +155$ kJ mol⁻¹.
2. $\Delta H_\text{sol}(\ce{Ba(OH)2}) = 2320 - 1360 - 2 \times 460 = +40$ kJ mol⁻¹.
3. Here the lattice energy falls by 675 kJ mol⁻¹ from Mg to Ba, more than the 560 kJ mol⁻¹ fall in hydration enthalpy, so ΔH_sol becomes much less endothermic and Ba(OH)₂ is far more soluble.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the carbonate ion polarises the cation"}
It is the small **cation** that polarises the large **anion**. Write "Mg²⁺ has a higher charge density and polarises the carbonate ion more".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that both energy terms change"}
Lattice energy and hydration enthalpy both become less exothermic down the group. The explanation must compare **how fast** each changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using 'stronger bonds' to explain stability"}
The extra stability down the group is not about a stronger M–O bond. It is that the C–O (or N–O) bonds in the anion are weakened less by a cation of lower charge density.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sign errors in the cycle"}
Breaking the lattice is the reverse of the lattice energy, so it enters with a change of sign: ΔH_sol = −ΔH_latt + ΣΔH_hyd.
:::

*Explain* the thermal stability trend: three linked points: same charge but larger cation; lower charge density so less polarisation of the anion; anion's bonds weakened less, so more heat needed. *Explain* the solubility trend: say that both lattice energy and hydration enthalpy decrease in magnitude, then which one decreases more for that anion and what that does to ΔH_sol. *Calculate* ΔH_sol: show the cycle and every sign.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Down Group 2 the cation radius increases at constant 2+ charge, so the charge density decreases.
- Smaller cations polarise large carbonate and nitrate ions more, weakening C–O or N–O bonds, so decomposition is easier at the top: thermal stability increases down the group.
- ΔH_sol = −ΔH_latt + ΣΔH_hyd; both lattice energy and hydration enthalpy become less exothermic down the group.
- Sulfates (large anion): lattice energy changes little, hydration enthalpy falls a lot, ΔH_sol becomes more endothermic, solubility decreases.
- Hydroxides (small anion): lattice energy falls faster than hydration enthalpy, ΔH_sol becomes less endothermic, solubility increases.
:::
