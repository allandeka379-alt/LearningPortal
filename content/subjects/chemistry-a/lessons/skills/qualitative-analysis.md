## Introduction

A water-treatment chemist in Bulawayo checking a borehole sample for iron, a technician at a fertiliser plant confirming that a batch contains ammonium and sulfate ions, a student in a Paper 4 practical holding a test tube of an unknown green solution: all of them rely on the same small set of reliable tests. Qualitative analysis answers the question *what is present?* rather than *how much?*

Both boards test this in the practical papers (ZIMSEC Paper 4, Cambridge Paper 3), and the reactions behind the tests appear in theory papers too. The exam always provides a set of qualitative analysis notes, so the skill being tested is not memory alone: it is choosing the right reagent, describing exactly what you see, and drawing a conclusion that follows from it. You should already know how to write ionic equations, the colours of common transition-metal ions, and the idea of an amphoteric hydroxide.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Observation"}
What is actually seen, heard or smelt during a test, described fully: the colour and state of any product (for example "pale blue precipitate"), whether it dissolves in excess reagent, effervescence, or a colour change of a solution.
:::

:::definition{term="Deduction (inference)"}
A conclusion about the identity of a substance or ion that follows from one or more observations, for example "Cu²⁺ present" or "an aldehyde or ketone present".
:::

:::definition{term="Precipitate"}
An insoluble solid formed when two solutions are mixed; it makes the mixture cloudy or opaque and settles on standing.
:::

:::definition{term="Amphoteric hydroxide"}
A hydroxide that reacts with both acids and bases; in qualitative analysis it is a precipitate that dissolves in excess aqueous sodium hydroxide, as with Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, Cr³⁺ and Pb²⁺.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the cations NH₄⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, Cr³⁺, Mn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺, the common anions and the gas tests. ZIMSEC only: Pb²⁺, the chromate(VI) ion, lead(II) ions as a reagent for halides and sulfate, the SO₂ test with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) and the nitrite test with dilute acid. Cambridge: the nitrite and sulfite tests with acidified potassium manganate(VII), and thiosulfate with acid.
:::

## Cations: aqueous sodium hydroxide and aqueous ammonia

Add the reagent a few drops at a time, then in excess, and record what happens at each stage. Both reagents supply hydroxide ions, so most metal ions give a hydroxide precipitate first. The differences come from what happens in excess.

| Cation | NaOH(aq), a few drops | NaOH(aq), in excess | NH₃(aq), a few drops | NH₃(aq), in excess |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NH₄⁺ | no precipitate; NH₃ given off on warming | — | no precipitate | — |
| Mg²⁺ | white ppt | insoluble | white ppt | insoluble |
| Al³⁺ | white ppt | dissolves, colourless solution | white ppt | insoluble |
| Ca²⁺ | white ppt (unless very dilute) | insoluble | no precipitate | — |
| Ba²⁺ | no ppt, or a faint white ppt | — | no precipitate | — |
| Cr³⁺ | grey-green ppt | dissolves, dark green solution | grey-green ppt | insoluble |
| Mn²⁺ | off-white ppt, quickly turns brown in air | insoluble | off-white ppt, turns brown | insoluble |
| Fe²⁺ | green ppt, turns brown on standing in air | insoluble | green ppt, turns brown | insoluble |
| Fe³⁺ | red-brown ppt | insoluble | red-brown ppt | insoluble |
| Cu²⁺ | pale blue ppt | insoluble | pale blue ppt | dissolves, dark blue solution |
| Zn²⁺ | white ppt | dissolves, colourless solution | white ppt | dissolves, colourless solution |
| Pb²⁺ (ZIMSEC) | white ppt | dissolves, colourless solution | white ppt | insoluble |

Three ideas explain most of this table.

1. **Precipitation.** $\ce{M^{n+}(aq) + nOH-(aq) -> M(OH)_n(s)}$, for example $\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + 3OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}$.
2. **Amphoteric hydroxides** dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide because they form soluble hydroxo complexes: $\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + OH-(aq) -> [Al(OH)4]-(aq)}$. Aqueous ammonia is too weak a base (its $[\ce{OH-}]$ is too low) to do this.
3. **Ammine complexes.** Copper(II) and zinc hydroxides dissolve in excess ammonia because ammonia molecules replace water as ligands, for example the dark blue $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ ion.

The darkening of the Fe²⁺ and Mn²⁺ precipitates is oxidation by air: green iron(II) hydroxide becomes red-brown iron(III) hydroxide at the surface first.

:::example{title="Example 1: telling the white precipitates apart"}
A colourless solution gives a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH(aq).

1. The ion could be Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺ or (ZIMSEC) Pb²⁺.
2. Add excess NaOH(aq). If the precipitate dissolves: Al³⁺, Zn²⁺ or Pb²⁺. If it stays: Mg²⁺ or Ca²⁺.
3. Use aqueous ammonia on a fresh sample. Zn²⁺ dissolves in excess; Al³⁺ and Pb²⁺ do not; Ca²⁺ gives no precipitate at all, while Mg²⁺ gives a white precipitate.
4. To separate Al³⁺ from Pb²⁺ (ZIMSEC), add dilute hydrochloric acid or potassium iodide: lead(II) chloride is a white precipitate and lead(II) iodide a bright yellow one; aluminium ions give neither.
:::

## Anions

| Anion | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | add dilute acid | effervescence; the gas (CO₂) turns limewater milky |
| sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | acidify, add Ba²⁺(aq) (barium chloride or nitrate) | white ppt of BaSO₄, insoluble in excess dilute strong acid |
| sulfite, SO₃²⁻ | add Ba²⁺(aq), then dilute strong acid | white ppt that dissolves in the acid; SO₂ is released by dilute acid |
| chloride, Cl⁻ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, add Ag⁺(aq) | white ppt of AgCl, soluble in dilute NH₃(aq) |
| bromide, Br⁻ | as for chloride | cream ppt of AgBr, partially soluble in dilute NH₃(aq), soluble in concentrated |
| iodide, I⁻ | as for chloride | pale yellow ppt of AgI, insoluble in NH₃(aq) |
| nitrate, NO₃⁻ | warm with NaOH(aq) and aluminium foil | NH₃ given off (damp red litmus turns blue) |
| nitrite, NO₂⁻ | as for nitrate; or add dilute acid (ZIMSEC); or acidified KMnO₄ (Cambridge) | NH₃ given off; with acid, colourless NO turning pale brown NO₂ in air; decolourises purple acidified manganate(VII) |
| chromate(VI), CrO₄²⁻ (ZIMSEC) | add H⁺(aq); add Ba²⁺(aq) or Pb²⁺(aq) | yellow solution turns orange; yellow ppt with Ba²⁺; bright yellow ppt with Pb²⁺ |

Acid is added before the barium or silver reagent to destroy carbonate (and sulfite) ions, which would otherwise give white precipitates and a false positive. Use **nitric** acid before silver nitrate, never hydrochloric acid, because chloride ions would themselves precipitate silver chloride; and avoid sulfuric acid before barium chloride for the same reason.

## Gases

| Gas | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| ammonia, NH₃ | damp red litmus paper | turns blue |
| carbon dioxide, CO₂ | bubble through limewater | white ppt (milky); dissolves with excess CO₂ |
| chlorine, Cl₂ | damp litmus paper | bleached (blue litmus turns red, then white) |
| hydrogen, H₂ | lighted splint | squeaky pop |
| oxygen, O₂ | glowing splint | relights |
| sulfur dioxide, SO₂ | acidified potassium dichromate(VI) paper or solution (ZIMSEC); acidified potassium manganate(VII) (Cambridge) | orange to green; purple to colourless |

## Organic functional-group tests (outline)

| Reagent and conditions | Positive result | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| bromine water, room temperature | orange-brown to colourless | C=C (alkene); a white ppt with phenol |
| 2,4-DNPH (Brady's reagent) | orange or yellow ppt | C=O in an aldehyde or ketone |
| Fehling's solution, warm | blue solution gives a brick-red ppt | aldehyde (not ketone) |
| Tollens' reagent, warm | silver mirror (or black ppt) | aldehyde (not ketone) |
| acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, warm | orange to green | primary or secondary alcohol, or aldehyde |
| alkaline aqueous iodine, warm | pale yellow ppt of CHI₃ (tri-iodomethane) | CH₃CO– group, or CH₃CH(OH)– group |
| sodium carbonate solution | effervescence, CO₂ | carboxylic acid |
| neutral iron(III) chloride | violet (purple) colour | phenol |

These are developed fully in the organic lessons; here the point is to record each result accurately.

## Recording observations and deductions

Most marks in a qualitative question are lost through vague or incomplete observations, not wrong chemistry.

- Name the **colour and the state**: "white precipitate", not "white" or "goes cloudy".
- Say what happens **in excess**: "pale blue precipitate, insoluble in excess".
- Describe **gases by their test**, not their name: "effervescence; gas turned limewater milky", and only then deduce "CO₂, so carbonate present".
- Write "no visible change" or "no precipitate" when nothing happens: that is an observation too.
- Make each deduction follow from a stated observation, and keep it as narrow as the evidence allows ("Al³⁺, Zn²⁺ or Pb²⁺" until a further test decides).

:::example{title="Example 2: working through an unknown"}
Solid **X** dissolves in water to give a green solution. Results:

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| 1. NaOH(aq) added, then in excess | green ppt, insoluble in excess; the surface turns brown on standing |
| 2. dilute HNO₃ then AgNO₃(aq) | no visible change |
| 3. dilute HCl then BaCl₂(aq) | white ppt |

1. Test 1: a green hydroxide that darkens in air is iron(II) hydroxide being oxidised, so Fe²⁺ is present.
2. Test 2: no precipitate, so no Cl⁻, Br⁻ or I⁻.
3. Test 3: a white precipitate with Ba²⁺ that formed in acid is barium sulfate, so SO₄²⁻ is present.
4. **X** is iron(II) sulfate (it may be hydrated). Ionic equations: $\ce{Fe^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)2(s)}$ and $\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}$.
:::

## Safe technique

Wear eye protection throughout. Warm, never boil, small volumes in a test tube held at an angle and pointing away from people. Smell gases only by wafting. Chlorine, sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide are toxic: produce them only in small amounts, in a fume cupboard. Chromium(VI) compounds are toxic and carcinogenic and lead compounds are toxic: wear gloves and dispose of residues as instructed. Silver nitrate stains skin.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming the gas instead of describing the test"}
"CO₂ given off" is a deduction. The observation is "effervescence; the gas turned limewater milky".
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out what happens in excess"}
"White precipitate" fits five cations. Credit usually needs "white precipitate, soluble in excess" or "insoluble in excess".
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong acid before a precipitation test"}
Hydrochloric acid before silver nitrate adds chloride ions, and sulfuric acid before barium chloride adds sulfate ions, so the test always appears positive.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing iron(II) and iron(III)"}
Fe²⁺ gives a **green** precipitate (which darkens on standing); Fe³⁺ gives a **red-brown** precipitate straight away.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3 carry the practical test of an unknown; ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Cambridge Paper 1 often ask for the result of one test; theory papers ask for ionic equations and explanations of the excess-reagent behaviour.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State the observation*: colour, state, and behaviour in excess. *Deduce*: name the ion and quote the observation that proves it. *Suggest a reagent to distinguish*: name the reagent and give the result for **each** ion. *Write an ionic equation*: include state symbols and only the ions that react. *Explain* why a precipitate dissolves: link it to an amphoteric hydroxide or to complex formation with ammonia.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- NaOH(aq) and NH₃(aq) give hydroxide precipitates; their behaviour in excess identifies the cation.
- Amphoteric hydroxides (Al³⁺, Zn²⁺, Cr³⁺, Pb²⁺) dissolve in excess NaOH; Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺ dissolve in excess NH₃ by forming ammine complexes.
- Anions: acid then Ba²⁺ for sulfate; dilute nitric acid then Ag⁺ (and NH₃) for halides; NaOH and Al foil for nitrate; dilute acid and limewater for carbonate.
- Gas tests: NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue, CO₂ turns limewater milky, Cl₂ bleaches damp litmus, H₂ pops, O₂ relights a glowing splint, SO₂ reduces dichromate(VI) or manganate(VII).
- Record full observations (colour, state, excess) and make each deduction follow from them.
:::
