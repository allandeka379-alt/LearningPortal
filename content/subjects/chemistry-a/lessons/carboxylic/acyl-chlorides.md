## Introduction

Making an ester from a carboxylic acid is slow and never complete, because esterification is an equilibrium. Chemists who need a high yield, or who need an ester of phenol, which hardly reacts with carboxylic acids at all, use a more reactive relative of the acid: an **acyl chloride**. Acyl chlorides react vigorously at room temperature with water, alcohols, phenols, ammonia and amines, and the reactions go to completion. Many medicines, including paracetamol and aspirin, are made industrially using acylation reactions of this kind (often with the related acid anhydrides).

This lesson covers making acyl chlorides, their reactions, the addition-elimination mechanism and their reactivity compared with other chlorine compounds. You should know nucleophilic addition to C=O, nucleophilic substitution in halogenoalkanes, and the basic reactions of carboxylic acids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Acyl chloride"}
A compound containing the –COCl group (an acyl group RCO– bonded to chlorine); named by changing -oic acid to **-oyl chloride**, for example ethanoyl chloride, $\ce{CH3COCl}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Acylation"}
The introduction of an acyl group, RCO–, into a molecule, for example replacing the H of an –OH or –NH₂ group.
:::

:::definition{term="Addition-elimination"}
A two-stage mechanism in which a nucleophile first adds to the C=O carbon (forming a tetrahedral intermediate) and a small molecule, here HCl, is then eliminated as the C=O reforms.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Reactions to learn (all at room temperature)"}
RCOCl + H₂O → RCOOH + HCl. RCOCl + R′OH → RCOOR′ + HCl. RCOCl + 2NH₃ → RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl. RCOCl + 2R′NH₂ → RCONHR′ + R′NH₃⁺Cl⁻.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: preparation, all the reactions, the mechanism and the comparison of hydrolysis. Cambridge examines acyl chlorides at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC in Form 6 (Papers 1-3).
:::

## Preparation

Acyl chlorides are made from carboxylic acids by replacing the –OH with –Cl, using $\ce{PCl5}$ (room temperature), $\ce{PCl3}$ (heat) or $\ce{SOCl2}$; for example $\ce{CH3COOH + SOCl2 -> CH3COCl + SO2 + HCl}$. They are distilled and kept dry. All work is done in a fume cupboard: acyl chlorides fume in moist air, releasing hydrogen chloride, and they are corrosive lachrymators (they make the eyes water).

## Reactions of acyl chlorides

| Reagent | Conditions | Product type | Example equation (ethanoyl chloride) | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| water | room temperature | carboxylic acid | $\ce{CH3COCl + H2O -> CH3COOH + HCl}$ | violent; steamy white fumes of HCl |
| alcohol | room temperature | ester | $\ce{CH3COCl + C2H5OH -> CH3COOC2H5 + HCl}$ | steamy fumes; fruity smell |
| phenol | room temperature (often in NaOH(aq)) | phenyl ester | $\ce{CH3COCl + C6H5OH -> CH3COOC6H5 + HCl}$ | phenyl ethanoate forms |
| ammonia (concentrated) | room temperature | primary amide | $\ce{CH3COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl}$ | white smoke/solid of $\ce{NH4Cl}$ |
| primary amine | room temperature | N-substituted (secondary) amide | $\ce{CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3+Cl-}$ | white solid |
| secondary amine | room temperature | N,N-disubstituted (tertiary) amide | $\ce{CH3COCl + 2(CH3)2NH -> CH3CON(CH3)2 + (CH3)2NH2+Cl-}$ | white solid |

With ammonia and amines, two moles are used: the first acts as the nucleophile, the second as a base that removes the HCl.

**Phenyl esters.** Phenol reacts so slowly with carboxylic acids that phenyl esters cannot be made directly from them. With benzoyl chloride, phenol gives phenyl benzoate: $\ce{C6H5COCl + C6H5OH -> C6H5COOC6H5 + HCl}$. Doing the reaction in sodium hydroxide converts phenol into the more nucleophilic phenoxide ion and removes the HCl.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: yield of phenyl benzoate]
Thandeka shakes 2.35 g of phenol with excess benzoyl chloride in sodium hydroxide solution and obtains 3.96 g of dry, recrystallised phenyl benzoate. Calculate her percentage yield. $M_\text{r}$: phenol 94, phenyl benzoate 198.

1. n(phenol) = 2.35 ÷ 94 = 0.0250 mol.
2. Phenol : phenyl benzoate = 1 : 1, so the maximum mass = 0.0250 × 198 = 4.95 g.
3. Percentage yield = 3.96 ÷ 4.95 × 100 = 80.0%.
:::

## The addition-elimination mechanism

The carbonyl carbon in an acyl chloride is bonded to two electronegative atoms, oxygen and chlorine, so it is strongly δ+. Taking water as the nucleophile:

| Step | What happens | Curly arrows | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (addition) | water attacks the carbonyl carbon | from a lone pair on the O of $\ce{H2O}$ to the δ+ carbon; from the C=O π bond to the carbonyl oxygen | tetrahedral intermediate: O⁻ on the former carbonyl oxygen and a positive $\ce{-OH2+}$ group on the same carbon |
| 2 (elimination) | the C=O reforms and chloride leaves | from a lone pair on O⁻ back to form C=O; from the C–Cl bond to Cl | $\ce{Cl-}$ is expelled |
| 3 | loss of a proton | from the O–H bond of $\ce{-OH2+}$ to O (H⁺ removed, by Cl⁻ or water) | carboxylic acid and HCl |

:::example{title="Example 2: the mechanism with ethanol"}
Describe the mechanism for the reaction of ethanoyl chloride with ethanol.

1. The C=O carbon is δ+ (bonded to O and Cl); the oxygen of ethanol has lone pairs, so ethanol is the nucleophile.
2. Addition: a lone pair on the ethanol oxygen attacks the δ+ carbon, and the π electrons of C=O move onto the oxygen. The intermediate is tetrahedral: $\ce{CH3C(O^-)(Cl)(O^+HC2H5)}$.
3. Elimination: a lone pair from O⁻ moves back to reform C=O, and the C–Cl bond breaks heterolytically, releasing $\ce{Cl-}$.
4. Deprotonation: $\ce{Cl-}$ removes the H⁺ from the positively charged oxygen, forming HCl.
5. Product: ethyl ethanoate, $\ce{CH3COOC2H5}$. Overall the –Cl has been replaced by –OC₂H₅, so the result looks like substitution, but it happens by addition followed by elimination.
:::

## Ease of hydrolysis: acyl, alkyl and aryl chlorides

| Compound | Example | Conditions for hydrolysis | Test with $\ce{AgNO3(aq)}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| acyl chloride | ethanoyl chloride | cold water, instantly | white AgCl precipitate at once |
| alkyl chloride | 1-chlorobutane | heat with NaOH(aq) (or slow with water and $\ce{AgNO3}$ in ethanol, warm) | white precipitate only slowly on warming |
| aryl chloride | chlorobenzene | not hydrolysed under normal conditions | no precipitate |

**Acyl chlorides** are hydrolysed fastest because the carbonyl carbon is attached to two electronegative atoms and is very δ+, and because it is planar (trigonal), so the nucleophile can approach easily and add to the π bond without first breaking the C–Cl bond.

**Alkyl chlorides** have a less δ+ carbon (attached to only one electronegative atom) which is tetrahedral and surrounded by other groups; substitution needs the strong C–Cl bond to break as the nucleophile attacks, so heating with hydroxide is needed.

**Aryl chlorides** resist hydrolysis because a lone pair on chlorine overlaps with the ring's delocalised π system, giving the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character so it is stronger; and the electron-rich ring repels approaching nucleophiles.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling it nucleophilic substitution"}
The overall change looks like substitution, but the mechanism is **addition-elimination**: there is a tetrahedral intermediate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the second mole of ammonia or amine"}
HCl reacts with excess NH₃ or amine to form a salt, so the equation uses 2 mol: CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heating is not needed"}
Acyl chloride reactions occur at room temperature. "Heat under reflux" is for carboxylic acids with alcohols.
:::

:::mistake{title="Phenyl esters from acids"}
Phenol does not form esters with carboxylic acids at a useful rate; use the acyl chloride.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Acyl chlorides RCOCl are made from RCOOH with PCl₅, PCl₃ or SOCl₂ and named -oyl chloride.
- At room temperature they give acids (water), esters (alcohols and phenols), amides (ammonia) and N-substituted amides (amines), releasing HCl.
- Phenyl esters such as phenyl benzoate are made from acyl chlorides, not from acids.
- Mechanism: addition of the nucleophile to the δ+ C=O carbon (tetrahedral intermediate), then elimination of Cl⁻ and H⁺.
- Ease of hydrolysis: acyl chloride (cold water) > alkyl chloride (hot NaOH) > aryl chloride (none), explained by δ+ size, shape and C–Cl bond strength.
:::
