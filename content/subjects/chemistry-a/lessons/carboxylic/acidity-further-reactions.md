## Introduction

Ethanoic acid in vinegar is safe to eat, yet trichloroethanoic acid, which differs only by three chlorine atoms, is corrosive enough to be used to burn off warts. Why does swapping hydrogen for chlorine make an acid about ten thousand times stronger? The answer lies in the stability of the ion left behind when the acid loses its proton, and the same idea explains why carboxylic acids are stronger than phenols, which are stronger than water and alcohols.

This lesson also covers three reactions examined at A Level: making benzoic acid from methylbenzene, converting acids into the much more reactive acyl chlorides, and the unusual oxidation of methanoic and ethanedioic acids. You should know the basic reactions of carboxylic acids, Ka and pKa, the √(Ka c) approximation for weak acids, and redox titrations with manganate(VII).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Acid dissociation constant, Ka"}
For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, $K_\text{a} = \dfrac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{A-}]}{[\ce{HA}]}$; a larger Ka (smaller pKa = −log Ka) means a stronger acid.
:::

:::definition{term="Inductive effect"}
The shift of electron density along σ bonds towards a more electronegative atom (electron-withdrawing, as by Cl) or away from an alkyl group (electron-donating).
:::

:::definition{term="Acyl chloride"}
A compound containing the –COCl group, in which the –OH of a carboxylic acid is replaced by chlorine; for example ethanoyl chloride, $\ce{CH3COCl}$.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The rule for acid strength"}
The more stable the anion (conjugate base), the stronger the acid. Anions are stabilised when the negative charge is **spread out** (delocalised) or **pulled away** by electron-withdrawing groups.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: all of this lesson. Cambridge examines it at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC in Form 6 (Papers 1-3).
:::

## Comparing acid strengths

| Acid | Anion | How the negative charge is held | pKa (data) |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$ | $\ce{C2H5O-}$ | on one O; the ethyl group pushes electron density towards it | about 16 |
| water, $\ce{H2O}$ | $\ce{OH-}$ | on one O | 15.7 |
| phenol, $\ce{C6H5OH}$ | $\ce{C6H5O-}$ | partly delocalised from O into the benzene ring | 9.9 |
| ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | $\ce{CH3COO-}$ | delocalised **equally** over two O atoms | 4.76 |

In the carboxylate ion both C–O bonds are the same length, between a single and a double bond, because the π electrons and the charge are shared by the two oxygen atoms. Spreading the charge over two highly electronegative oxygen atoms stabilises the ion far more than spreading it into the carbon atoms of a ring, which is why carboxylic acids are stronger than phenols.

Alkyl groups have the opposite effect: they push electron density towards the carboxylate group, concentrating the charge. Ethanoic acid (pKa 4.76) is weaker than methanoic acid (pKa 3.75, data), and propanoic acid (4.87) is slightly weaker still.

## Chlorine substitution and the inductive effect

| Acid | Formula | pKa (data) |
|---|---|---|
| ethanoic acid | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | 4.76 |
| chloroethanoic acid | $\ce{CH2ClCOOH}$ | 2.86 |
| dichloroethanoic acid | $\ce{CHCl2COOH}$ | 1.29 |
| trichloroethanoic acid | $\ce{CCl3COOH}$ | 0.65 |

Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon. Each chlorine atom withdraws electron density through the σ bonds, away from the carboxylate group. This **spreads the negative charge** further, stabilising the anion, and also weakens the O–H bond in the acid. The more chlorine atoms, the stronger the effect and the stronger the acid. The effect falls off with distance: 3-chloropropanoic acid is much less affected than 2-chloropropanoic acid, because the chlorine is further from the COOH group.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how much stronger?]
Compare the pH of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid (pKa 4.76) and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ chloroethanoic acid (pKa 2.86), using $[\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{K_\text{a} c}$.

1. Ethanoic acid: Ka = 10⁻⁴·⁷⁶ = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³; [H⁺] = √(1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100) = 1.32 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³; pH = 2.88.
2. Chloroethanoic acid: Ka = 10⁻²·⁸⁶ = 1.38 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³; [H⁺] = √(1.38 × 10⁻³ × 0.100) = 1.17 × 10⁻² mol dm⁻³; pH = 1.93.
3. The Ka values differ by a factor of 10^(4.76 − 2.86) = 10^1.90, about 79, so the [H⁺] values differ by about √79 ≈ 8.9.
4. Note: chloroethanoic acid is about 12% dissociated here, so the approximation is less accurate, but it is the method expected in the exam.
:::

## Benzoic acid from methylbenzene

A side chain on a benzene ring is oxidised to –COOH, however long it is, by heating under reflux with **alkaline** potassium manganate(VII); the solution is then acidified to release the acid:

$\ce{C6H5CH3 + 3[O] -> C6H5COOH + H2O}$

In alkaline conditions the product is the benzoate ion, and manganate(VII) is reduced to a brown precipitate of manganese(IV) oxide, $\ce{MnO2}$. Adding dilute acid then precipitates white benzoic acid, which can be recrystallised from hot water. The benzene ring itself is not oxidised.

## Converting acids into acyl chlorides

| Reagent | Conditions | Equation (ethanoic acid) | By-products |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{PCl5}$ (solid) | room temperature; steamy fumes | $\ce{CH3COOH + PCl5 -> CH3COCl + POCl3 + HCl}$ | $\ce{POCl3}$, HCl |
| $\ce{PCl3}$ (liquid) | heat | $\ce{3CH3COOH + PCl3 -> 3CH3COCl + H3PO3}$ | $\ce{H3PO3}$ |
| $\ce{SOCl2}$ (liquid) | gentle warming | $\ce{CH3COOH + SOCl2 -> CH3COCl + SO2 + HCl}$ | $\ce{SO2}$, HCl (both gases) |

Thionyl chloride is convenient because both by-products are gases that escape, leaving the acyl chloride easy to purify by distillation. All three reactions must be done in a fume cupboard under dry conditions, because the acyl chloride reacts with water and the fumes are toxic and corrosive.

## Oxidation of methanoic acid and ethanedioic acid

Most carboxylic acids resist oxidation, but two are exceptions.

**Methanoic acid**, $\ce{HCOOH}$, can be seen as containing an aldehyde group (H–C=O) as well as –OH. It is oxidised to carbon dioxide and water: $\ce{HCOOH + [O] -> CO2 + H2O}$. It therefore gives positive results with Tollens' reagent (silver mirror), Fehling's solution (red-brown precipitate), acidified manganate(VII) (purple to colourless) and acidified dichromate(VI) (orange to green). This distinguishes it from ethanoic acid, which gives none of these.

**Ethanedioic acid**, $\ce{HOOC-COOH}$, is oxidised by warm (about 60 °C) acidified manganate(VII) to carbon dioxide:

$\ce{5H2C2O4 + 2MnO4- + 6H+ -> 10CO2 + 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O}$

The manganate(VII) is decolorised; the titration is self-indicating (first permanent pink colour = end point). Warming is needed because the reaction is slow at first; the $\ce{Mn^2+}$ formed catalyses it (autocatalysis).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: concentration of ethanedioic acid]
25.0 cm³ of ethanedioic acid solution, acidified and warmed, needs 22.40 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ potassium manganate(VII). Calculate the concentration of the ethanedioic acid.

1. n($\ce{MnO4-}$) = 22.40 × 10⁻³ × 0.0200 = 4.48 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
2. Ratio $\ce{H2C2O4}$ : $\ce{MnO4-}$ = 5 : 2, so n($\ce{H2C2O4}$) = 4.48 × 10⁻⁴ × 5/2 = 1.12 × 10⁻³ mol.
3. Concentration = 1.12 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0448 mol dm⁻³.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Explaining with the acid, not the anion"}
Say why the **anion** is more stable (charge delocalised over two O atoms; charge spread by electron-withdrawing Cl). "The acid is delocalised" earns no credit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Direction of the inductive effect"}
Chlorine **withdraws** electron density (strengthens the acid); alkyl groups **donate** it (weaken the acid).
:::

:::mistake{title="Acidic or alkaline manganate(VII)?"}
Side-chain oxidation of methylbenzene uses hot **alkaline** KMnO₄, then acidification. The ethanedioic acid titration uses **acidified** KMnO₄.
:::

:::mistake{title="Methanoic acid is special"}
Only methanoic acid among the simple monocarboxylic acids reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents; ethanoic acid does not.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Papers 1-3. *Explain* the order of acidity by comparing anion stability; *deduce* the order of pKa values for a set of substituted acids (number of Cl atoms, distance from COOH); *construct* equations for acyl chloride formation and for the oxidation of ethanedioic acid.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Calculations"}
Convert pKa to Ka first (Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ); use the 5 : 2 ratio for ethanedioic acid with manganate(VII), and give answers to three significant figures.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Acid strength: alcohols < water < phenols < carboxylic acids, because the carboxylate charge is delocalised over two oxygen atoms.
- Electron-withdrawing Cl atoms stabilise the carboxylate ion: CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > CH₂ClCOOH > CH₃COOH; alkyl groups weaken acids.
- Hot alkaline KMnO₄ oxidises methylbenzene's side chain to benzoate; acidify to obtain benzoic acid.
- PCl₅ (room temperature), PCl₃ (heat) or SOCl₂ convert RCOOH to RCOCl.
- Methanoic acid is oxidised to CO₂ by Tollens', Fehling's, MnO₄⁻/H⁺ and Cr₂O₇²⁻/H⁺; ethanedioic acid by warm acidified MnO₄⁻ (5 : 2).
:::
