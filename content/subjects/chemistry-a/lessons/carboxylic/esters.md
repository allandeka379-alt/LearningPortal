## Introduction

The fruity smell of a ripe banana or pineapple, the solvent in many glues and nail-varnish removers, and the cooking oil in every kitchen all have something in common: they are esters. Soap, one of the oldest chemical products, is made by breaking esters apart with alkali; in rural Zimbabwe soap has traditionally been made by boiling animal fat or plant oil with the alkaline liquid obtained by soaking wood ash in water.

This lesson explains how esters are named and made, why their formation is reversible, how they are hydrolysed, and how fats and oils are turned into soap. You should know the reactions of carboxylic acids and alcohols, and how to write a Kc expression.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ester"}
A compound containing the group –COO– between two hydrocarbon groups, RCOOR′, formed from a carboxylic acid (RCOOH) and an alcohol (R′OH).
:::

:::definition{term="Condensation reaction"}
A reaction in which two molecules join together with the loss of a small molecule such as water.
:::

:::definition{term="Saponification"}
The hydrolysis of an ester, especially a fat or oil, by heating with aqueous alkali, giving a carboxylate salt (soap) and an alcohol.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Making and breaking esters"}
Acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water (concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ catalyst, heat under reflux).
Acid hydrolysis (dilute acid, reflux): reversible. Alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH(aq), reflux): goes to completion, giving the carboxylate salt and the alcohol.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## Naming esters

An ester's name has two words. The **first** comes from the alcohol (as an alkyl group); the **second** from the acid, with -oic acid changed to **-oate**. In the formula, the acid part is usually written first.

| Ester | Formula | From acid | From alcohol |
|---|---|---|---|
| methyl ethanoate | $\ce{CH3COOCH3}$ | ethanoic acid | methanol |
| ethyl ethanoate | $\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}$ | ethanoic acid | ethanol |
| ethyl methanoate | $\ce{HCOOCH2CH3}$ | methanoic acid | ethanol |
| propyl propanoate | $\ce{CH3CH2COOCH2CH2CH3}$ | propanoic acid | propan-1-ol |
| methyl butanoate | $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOCH3}$ | butanoic acid | methanol |
| 3-methylbutyl ethanoate | $\ce{CH3COOCH2CH2CH(CH3)2}$ | ethanoic acid | 3-methylbutan-1-ol |

To name an ester from its formula, find the C=O carbon: everything on that side, including the C=O carbon, is the acid part; the group attached to the single-bonded oxygen is the alkyl (alcohol) part. Esters are isomers of carboxylic acids with the same formula: ethyl ethanoate and butanoic acid are both $\ce{C4H8O2}$.

## Making esters

Heat the carboxylic acid and the alcohol under reflux with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, then distil off the ester:

$\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}$

Concentrated sulfuric acid is a **catalyst** (it speeds up the slow reaction) and, because it absorbs water, it also moves the equilibrium position to the right. Using an excess of the cheaper reactant, usually the alcohol, also increases the yield. The crude ester is washed with sodium carbonate solution (to remove acid), separated, dried with anhydrous calcium chloride and redistilled.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the equilibrium constant for esterification]
1.00 mol of ethanoic acid and 1.00 mol of ethanol are mixed with a little sulfuric acid and left at a constant temperature until equilibrium is reached. Titration shows that 0.340 mol of ethanoic acid remains. Calculate Kc.

1. Ethanoic acid used = 1.00 − 0.340 = 0.660 mol; by the 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio, ethanol remaining = 0.340 mol and ester formed = water formed = 0.660 mol.
2. $K_\text{c} = \dfrac{[\ce{CH3COOC2H5}][\ce{H2O}]}{[\ce{CH3COOH}][\ce{C2H5OH}]}$.
3. The volume V cancels (two concentration terms above and below), so amounts can be used directly: Kc = (0.660 × 0.660) ÷ (0.340 × 0.340) = 3.77.
4. Kc has no units. (In a real titration the sulfuric acid catalyst also reacts with the alkali; its contribution is found by a blank titration and subtracted.)
:::

## Hydrolysis of esters

| | Acid hydrolysis | Alkaline hydrolysis |
|---|---|---|
| reagents and conditions | dilute HCl or $\ce{H2SO4}$, heat under reflux | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |
| equation (ethyl ethanoate) | $\ce{CH3COOC2H5 + H2O <=> CH3COOH + C2H5OH}$ | $\ce{CH3COOC2H5 + NaOH -> CH3COONa + C2H5OH}$ |
| extent | reversible; incomplete | goes to completion |
| products | carboxylic acid and alcohol | carboxylate salt and alcohol (acidify to get the acid) |

Alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion because the carboxylic acid formed is immediately converted into its anion by the hydroxide ions; the carboxylate ion does not react with the alcohol, so the reverse reaction cannot occur.

## Fats, oils and soap

Fats and oils are **triesters** (triglycerides) of propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) with three long-chain carboxylic acids (fatty acids). Fats such as beef tallow are solid at room temperature and mostly saturated; oils such as sunflower, groundnut and cotton-seed oil are liquid and contain more C=C bonds.

Boiling a fat or oil with sodium hydroxide hydrolyses all three ester links:

triglyceride + 3NaOH → propane-1,2,3-triol + 3 sodium salts of fatty acids (soap)

For glyceryl tristearate: $\ce{(C17H35COO)3C3H5 + 3NaOH -> 3C17H35COONa + C3H5(OH)3}$

Adding concentrated sodium chloride solution makes the soap separate as a solid (salting out). Soap works because each carboxylate ion has an ionic, water-attracting head and a long hydrocarbon tail that dissolves in grease.

**Wood ash soap (ZIMSEC suggested activity).** Water that has soaked through wood ash dissolves potassium carbonate and other alkaline compounds, giving an alkaline solution sometimes called lye. Boiling fat or oil with this liquid saponifies it slowly, giving a soft potassium soap. The alkaline liquid is corrosive: eye protection and gloves are needed.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: alkali needed to saponify a fat]
Calculate the mass of sodium hydroxide needed to saponify 10.0 g of glyceryl tristearate, $\ce{C57H110O6}$, and the mass of sodium stearate, $\ce{C17H35COONa}$, formed. $M_\text{r}$: fat 890, NaOH 40, sodium stearate 306.

1. n(fat) = 10.0 ÷ 890 = 0.01124 mol.
2. Three ester groups per molecule: n(NaOH) = 3 × 0.01124 = 0.03371 mol; mass = 0.03371 × 40 = 1.35 g.
3. n(sodium stearate) = 0.03371 mol; mass = 0.03371 × 306 = 10.3 g.
:::

## Uses of esters and carboxylic acids (ZIMSEC detail)

| Substance | Use |
|---|---|
| ethyl ethanoate | solvent in glues, paints and nail-varnish remover |
| 3-methylbutyl ethanoate | banana flavouring |
| ethyl butanoate | pineapple flavouring |
| other volatile esters | perfumes and fragrances |
| ethanoic acid (vinegar) | pickling and preserving food; making esters such as ethenyl ethanoate for polymers |
| benzoic and propanoic acids (as salts) | food preservatives |
| hexanedioic acid, benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid | making nylon and polyester polymers |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming back to front"}
$\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}$ is **ethyl ethanoate**, and $\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}$ is **methyl propanoate**. Find which part contains the C=O.
:::

:::mistake{title="Role of the sulfuric acid"}
Concentrated sulfuric acid is a catalyst; say so. It does not "react with" the acid or alcohol to form the ester.
:::

:::mistake{title="Alkaline hydrolysis products"}
NaOH(aq) gives the sodium **salt** of the acid, not the free acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Units of Kc"}
For esterification Kc has no units because the concentration terms cancel.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Esters RCOOR′ are named alkyl (from the alcohol) + -oate (from the acid).
- Acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water with a concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst under reflux; Kc for esterification has no units.
- Acid hydrolysis is reversible; alkaline hydrolysis (saponification) goes to completion, giving a carboxylate salt and an alcohol.
- Fats and oils are triesters of propane-1,2,3-triol; boiling with NaOH (or wood-ash lye) gives soap and glycerol.
- Esters are used as solvents, flavourings and perfumes; carboxylic acids as preservatives and in making esters and polymers.
:::
