## Introduction

Carboxylic acids are all around us. Vinegar, used across Zimbabwe for pickling vegetables and preserving food, is a dilute solution of ethanoic acid. The sharp taste of oranges, lemons and naartjies comes from citric acid, and the sting of an ant or a nettle comes partly from methanoic acid. In industry, carboxylic acids are used to make esters for flavourings and solvents, polyester and nylon fibres, and food preservatives.

This lesson covers naming, preparation and the typical reactions of carboxylic acids. Why some acids are stronger than others, and the A Level reactions (making acyl chlorides, oxidising methanoic and ethanedioic acids), are in the next lesson. You should know how alcohols are oxidised, what a weak acid is, and how to carry out titration calculations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Carboxylic acid"}
An organic compound containing the carboxyl group, –COOH (a C=O and an –OH on the same carbon); general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}COOH}$ for the saturated monocarboxylic acids.
:::

:::definition{term="Carboxylate ion"}
The anion $\ce{RCOO-}$ formed when a carboxylic acid loses the proton of its –OH group; its salts are named, for example, sodium ethanoate.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrolysis"}
A reaction in which a compound is broken down by reaction with water, usually catalysed by acid or alkali.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Making and naming"}
Primary alcohol or aldehyde + $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$/dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$, **heat under reflux** → carboxylic acid. Name: count every carbon including the COOH carbon and use -oic acid: $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$ is propanoic acid.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: everything in this lesson. Cambridge examines it at AS Level (Papers 1 and 2); ZIMSEC in Form 6. Uses of carboxylic acids are named by ZIMSEC and are covered in the esters lesson.
:::

## Naming and occurrence

| Formula | Name | Where it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{HCOOH}$ | methanoic acid | ant stings, nettles |
| $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | ethanoic acid | vinegar |
| $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$ | propanoic acid | its salts are used as food preservatives |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}$ | butanoic acid | smell of rancid butter |
| $\ce{C6H5COOH}$ | benzoic acid | its sodium salt is a preservative |
| $\ce{HOOCCOOH}$ | ethanedioic acid | rhubarb leaves, spinach |
| citric acid (three –COOH groups) | 2-hydroxypropane-1,2,3-tricarboxylic acid | citrus fruits |

Branched acids are numbered from the COOH carbon: $\ce{(CH3)2CHCOOH}$ is 2-methylpropanoic acid.

Carboxylic acids have high boiling points for their size because pairs of molecules hydrogen bond through both the C=O and the O–H groups (dimers). The small acids are completely soluble in water.

## Making carboxylic acids

| Method | Reagent and conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| oxidation of a primary alcohol | $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ and dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$, heat under reflux (excess oxidant) | $\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}$ |
| oxidation of an aldehyde | as above | $\ce{CH3CHO + [O] -> CH3COOH}$ |
| hydrolysis of a nitrile | heat under reflux with dilute HCl | $\ce{CH3CN + 2H2O + HCl -> CH3COOH + NH4Cl}$ |
| hydrolysis of a nitrile | heat under reflux with NaOH(aq), then acidify | $\ce{CH3CN + NaOH + H2O -> CH3COONa + NH3}$ |
| hydrolysis of an ester | heat under reflux with dilute acid (reversible), or NaOH(aq) followed by acidification | $\ce{CH3COOC2H5 + H2O <=> CH3COOH + C2H5OH}$ |

After alkaline hydrolysis, the product is the carboxylate salt; a strong acid such as dilute HCl must be added to release the free acid: $\ce{CH3COO- + H+ -> CH3COOH}$.

## Reactions as acids

Carboxylic acids are **weak** acids: in water only a small fraction of the molecules ionise, $\ce{CH3COOH <=> CH3COO- + H+}$. They still show all the typical reactions of acids, more slowly than strong acids of the same concentration:

| Reactant | Observation | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| reactive metal, e.g. Mg | fizzing; metal dissolves | $\ce{2CH3COOH + Mg -> (CH3COO)2Mg + H2}$ |
| alkali, e.g. NaOH | no visible change; temperature rises | $\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}$ |
| carbonate, e.g. $\ce{Na2CO3}$ | effervescence; gas turns limewater milky | $\ce{2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 -> 2CH3COONa + CO2 + H2O}$ |
| hydrogencarbonate, e.g. $\ce{NaHCO3}$ | effervescence | $\ce{CH3COOH + NaHCO3 -> CH3COONa + CO2 + H2O}$ |

The reaction with a carbonate or hydrogencarbonate is the standard test that distinguishes a carboxylic acid from a phenol or an alcohol, which do not release carbon dioxide.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how much ethanoic acid is in vinegar?]
Chipo dilutes 25.0 cm³ of vinegar to 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask. She titrates 25.0 cm³ portions of the diluted solution with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH using phenolphthalein. Her results:

| | 1 (rough) | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| final reading / cm³ | 21.90 | 43.15 | 21.70 | 42.95 |
| initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 21.90 | 0.50 | 21.70 |
| titre / cm³ | 21.90 | 21.25 | 21.20 | 21.25 |

1. Concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³): 21.25, 21.20 and 21.25 cm³; mean = 21.23 cm³.
2. n(NaOH) = 21.23 × 10⁻³ dm³ × 0.100 mol dm⁻³ = 2.123 × 10⁻³ mol.
3. $\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}$ is 1 : 1, so n(acid) in 25.0 cm³ of diluted solution = 2.123 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Concentration of the diluted solution = 2.123 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0849 mol dm⁻³.
5. The vinegar was diluted ten times: original concentration = 0.849 mol dm⁻³.
6. In g dm⁻³: 0.849 × 60.0 = 51.0 g dm⁻³, about 5.10 g of ethanoic acid per 100 cm³ of vinegar.

Phenolphthalein is used because this is a weak acid-strong base titration: the end point is above pH 7.
:::

## Esterification

Heat a carboxylic acid with an alcohol under reflux, with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst:

$\ce{CH3CH2COOH + CH3OH <=> CH3CH2COOCH3 + H2O}$ (methyl propanoate)

The reaction is slow and reversible. Esters, their naming and their hydrolysis are in the esters lesson.

## Reduction to primary alcohols

Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate in dry ether reduces carboxylic acids to primary alcohols (sodium tetrahydridoborate is not strong enough):

$\ce{CH3CH2COOH + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2OH + H2O}$

:::example{title="Example 2: planning a route"}
How can propan-1-ol be converted to butanoic acid?

1. Butanoic acid has four carbons; propan-1-ol has three, so one carbon must be added.
2. Convert propan-1-ol into 1-bromopropane (for example, KBr with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$, heat, or $\ce{PBr3}$).
3. Heat 1-bromopropane under reflux with KCN in ethanol: butanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}$ (one carbon added).
4. Heat the nitrile under reflux with dilute HCl: butanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}$, and ammonium chloride.
5. Oxidising propan-1-ol directly would give propanoic acid, which has one carbon too few.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reflux, not distillation"}
To make the acid from a primary alcohol, heat under reflux with **excess** oxidant. Distillation gives the aldehyde.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to acidify"}
Alkaline hydrolysis of a nitrile or ester gives the carboxylate **salt**; you must acidify to obtain the carboxylic acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting carbons"}
The COOH carbon is part of the chain: $\ce{CH3COOH}$ is ethanoic acid (two carbons), and $\ce{CH3CH2CN}$ hydrolyses to propanoic acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Weak does not mean dilute"}
"Weak" means only partly ionised. A concentrated solution of ethanoic acid is still a weak acid.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-4 and Cambridge Papers 1-3. *State* reagents and conditions for each preparation; *describe* the observations with a carbonate (effervescence, limewater milky); *calculate* concentrations from titration data to three significant figures.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Distinguishing tests"}
To tell a carboxylic acid from a phenol or alcohol, add sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate solution: only the acid fizzes. Name the gas and its test.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Carboxylic acids contain –COOH; names end in -oic acid and count the COOH carbon.
- They are made by refluxing primary alcohols or aldehydes with acidified dichromate(VI), and by hydrolysing nitriles or esters (acidifying after alkaline hydrolysis).
- As weak acids they react with reactive metals (H₂), alkalis (salt + water) and carbonates (CO₂).
- With alcohols and a concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst they form esters reversibly.
- LiAlH₄ in dry ether reduces them to primary alcohols.
:::
