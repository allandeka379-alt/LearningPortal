## Introduction

Halogenoalkanes are the "turntables" of organic synthesis. A bromine atom attached to a carbon chain can be swapped for an OH group, a CN group (adding a carbon atom to the chain), or an NH₂ group, or it can be removed with a hydrogen atom to make an alkene. That versatility is why halogenoalkanes appear in so many routes to medicines and agrochemicals, and why examiners love them in reaction schemes.

In this lesson you will learn how halogenoalkanes are made, the reagents and conditions for each of their reactions, the silver nitrate test that identifies the halogen, and why iodoalkanes react fastest. You should know how to name organic compounds, what a nucleophile is, and the curly-arrow conventions. The mechanisms (SN1 and SN2) are in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Halogenoalkane"}
A compound in which one or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane have been replaced by halogen atoms; general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}X}$ for one halogen.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleophilic substitution"}
A reaction in which a nucleophile replaces an atom or group (here the halogen, leaving as a halide ion) on a δ+ carbon atom.
:::

:::definition{term="Elimination"}
A reaction in which a hydrogen halide is removed from a halogenoalkane, forming a C=C bond.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: preparation, classification, the reactions with NaOH(aq), KCN in ethanol, NH₃ in ethanol and NaOH in ethanol, the silver nitrate test and the effect of C–X bond strength. ZIMSEC calls this topic "halogen derivatives"; Cambridge calls it "halogen compounds".
:::

## Making halogenoalkanes

| Starting material | Reagent and conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| alkane | $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$, ultraviolet light (gives a mixture) | $\ce{CH4 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + HCl}$ |
| alkene | HX at room temperature, or $\ce{X2}$ | $\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}$ |
| alcohol | HX (for example HCl with $\ce{ZnCl2}$, or HBr made in situ from KBr and concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ or $\ce{H3PO4}$), heat | $\ce{CH3CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2Br + H2O}$ |
| alcohol | $\ce{PCl5}$, room temperature | $\ce{ROH + PCl5 -> RCl + POCl3 + HCl}$ |
| alcohol | $\ce{PCl3}$, heat | $\ce{3ROH + PCl3 -> 3RCl + H3PO3}$ |
| alcohol | $\ce{SOCl2}$ | $\ce{ROH + SOCl2 -> RCl + SO2 + HCl}$ |
| alcohol | KI with concentrated $\ce{H3PO4}$ (iodoalkanes; $\ce{H2SO4}$ would oxidise HI) | $\ce{ROH + HI -> RI + H2O}$ |

Halogenoalkanes are classified like alcohols: **primary** (C–X carbon bonded to one other carbon, as in 1-bromobutane), **secondary** (two, as in 2-bromobutane), **tertiary** (three, as in 2-bromo-2-methylpropane). The four structural isomers of $\ce{C4H9Br}$ include one of each type plus 1-bromo-2-methylpropane (primary).

## Nucleophilic substitution and elimination

The C–X bond is polar ($\ce{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}$) because halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so nucleophiles attack the δ+ carbon and the halide ion leaves.

| Reagent and conditions | Type | Product from bromoethane | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | nucleophilic substitution (hydrolysis) | ethanol | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2OH + Br-}$ |
| KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux | nucleophilic substitution | propanenitrile | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CN + Br-}$ |
| excess $\ce{NH3}$ in ethanol, heat in a sealed tube (under pressure) | nucleophilic substitution | ethylamine | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br}$ |
| NaOH in ethanol, heat under reflux | elimination | ethene | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH2=CH2 + H2O + Br-}$ |

Notes:

1. The cyanide reaction **adds one carbon** to the chain; the nitrile can then be hydrolysed to a carboxylic acid or reduced to an amine. KCN is highly toxic: it is used only in supervised laboratories, and acid must be kept away from it (hydrogen cyanide forms).
2. Excess ammonia reduces further substitution (the amine product is itself a nucleophile). The sealed tube keeps the volatile ammonia in the mixture.
3. The **solvent** decides between substitution and elimination: water favours substitution; ethanol favours elimination. In reality both happen to some extent. With 2-bromopropane, ethanolic hydroxide gives mainly propene; with an unsymmetrical secondary or tertiary halide, more than one alkene can form.

:::example{title="Example 1: following a reaction scheme"}
Start with 2-bromopropane, $\ce{CH3CHBrCH3}$.

1. NaOH(aq), reflux → propan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$ (substitution).
2. NaOH in ethanol, reflux → propene, $\ce{CH3CH=CH2}$ (elimination of HBr).
3. KCN in ethanol, reflux → 2-methylpropanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH(CN)CH3}$; the chain now has four carbons including the nitrile carbon.
4. Excess $\ce{NH3}$ in ethanol, heat in a sealed tube → propan-2-amine (2-aminopropane), $\ce{CH3CH(NH2)CH3}$.
:::

## Identifying the halogen and comparing reactivity

Warm a few drops of the halogenoalkane with **aqueous silver nitrate dissolved in ethanol** (ethanol is a common solvent for both the halogenoalkane and the aqueous reagent). Water slowly hydrolyses the C–X bond, releasing $\ce{X-}$, which forms a silver halide precipitate:

| Halogenoalkane | Precipitate | Colour | Relative speed | Solubility of precipitate in ammonia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chloro- | AgCl | white | slowest | soluble in dilute $\ce{NH3(aq)}$ |
| bromo- | AgBr | cream | intermediate | soluble in concentrated $\ce{NH3(aq)}$ |
| iodo- | AgI | yellow | fastest | insoluble |

**Why iodo fastest?** The bond enthalpies are C–Cl 340, C–Br 280 and C–I 240 kJ mol⁻¹. The C–I bond is the weakest, so it breaks most easily. Bond polarity predicts the opposite order (C–Cl is the most polar, so its carbon is the most δ+), so the observed order shows that **bond strength, not polarity, controls the rate**. The C–F bond is stronger still, so fluoroalkanes are very unreactive (see the lesson on halogenoarenes and CFCs).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: percentage yield of a halogenoalkane]
7.40 g of butan-1-ol was converted into 1-bromobutane using KBr and concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$; 10.4 g of pure product was obtained. Relative molecular masses: butan-1-ol 74.0; 1-bromobutane 136.9.

1. $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + H2O}$: 1 : 1.
2. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C4H9OH}) = 74.0$; n = 7.40 ÷ 74.0 = 0.100 mol.
3. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C4H9Br}) = 136.9$; theoretical mass = 0.100 × 136.9 = 13.69 g.
4. Yield = 10.4 ÷ 13.69 × 100 = 76.0%.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Aqueous or ethanolic?"}
NaOH(aq) → alcohol (substitution); NaOH in ethanol → alkene (elimination). KCN and NH₃ are both used in ethanol but give substitution.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining reactivity with polarity"}
C–I reacts fastest because it is the weakest bond, even though it is the least polar.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the extra carbon"}
With KCN the product has one more carbon: bromoethane gives propanenitrile, not ethanenitrile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Precipitate colours"}
AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow. Do not call AgBr "yellow" or AgI "white".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-4 and Cambridge Papers 1, 2 and 3: *give reagents and conditions*, *name the type of reaction*, *identify* compounds in a scheme, and *describe and explain* the silver nitrate experiment (a common practical planning task).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Reagents and conditions"}
Always give the solvent and "heat under reflux" (or "sealed tube" for ammonia). "NaOH" alone is not enough to score.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the order of reactivity"}
Quote or refer to the bond energies, say the weakest bond breaks fastest, and contrast with what polarity would predict.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Halogenoalkanes come from alkanes (UV), alkenes (HX, X₂) and alcohols (HX, PCl₅, PCl₃, SOCl₂).
- Substitution: NaOH(aq) → alcohol; KCN in ethanol → nitrile (one extra carbon); NH₃ in ethanol, sealed tube → amine.
- Elimination: NaOH in ethanol, heat → alkene.
- AgNO₃ in ethanol: AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI yellow; iodo fastest.
- Reactivity order RI > RBr > RCl follows decreasing C–X bond strength, not polarity.
:::
