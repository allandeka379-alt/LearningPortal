## Introduction

Not every carbon–halogen bond is easy to break. A chlorine atom attached directly to a benzene ring hardly reacts with hydroxide ions at all, and carbon–fluorine bonds are so strong that fluorinated compounds survive for years. That stability made chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) ideal refrigerants and aerosol propellants in the twentieth century: cheap, non-toxic, non-flammable and unreactive. The same stability let them drift intact into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light finally broke them apart and the chlorine released began destroying the ozone layer that shields life on Earth, including crops and people in sunny Zimbabwe, from harmful ultraviolet radiation.

This lesson explains both sides of that story. You should know the reactions of halogenoalkanes, bond energies and their link to reactivity, the delocalised structure of benzene, electrophilic substitution, and free-radical mechanisms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Halogenoarene"}
A compound in which a halogen atom is bonded directly to a carbon atom of a benzene ring, for example chlorobenzene, $\ce{C6H5Cl}$.
:::

:::definition{term="CFC (chlorofluorocarbon)"}
A compound of carbon, fluorine and chlorine only (a chlorofluoroalkane), for example $\ce{CCl3F}$ or $\ce{CCl2F2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Photodissociation"}
The breaking of a covalent bond by the absorption of light (usually ultraviolet).
:::

## Making halogenoarenes

| Starting material | Reagents and conditions | Products |
|---|---|---|
| benzene | $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ (or Fe) catalyst, room temperature, dry | chlorobenzene + HCl |
| methylbenzene | $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ catalyst, room temperature, dark | 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene + HCl |

These are electrophilic substitutions (the methyl group directs to positions 2 and 4). In ultraviolet light without a catalyst, chlorine attacks the methyl side chain instead, giving (chloromethyl)benzene, which is a halogenoalkane, not a halogenoarene.

## Why chlorobenzene is unreactive

Chloroethane is hydrolysed by warm aqueous sodium hydroxide; chlorobenzene is not hydrolysed under these conditions and gives no precipitate with warm ethanolic silver nitrate.

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining the low reactivity of chlorobenzene"}
1. The chlorine atom has lone pairs in p orbitals. One of these lone pairs overlaps with the delocalised π system of the ring.
2. Electron density from chlorine is therefore partly delocalised into the ring, giving the C–Cl bond **partial double-bond character**.
3. The C–Cl bond in chlorobenzene is shorter and **stronger** than in chloroethane, so it is harder to break.
4. In addition, the C–Cl carbon is less δ+, and an approaching nucleophile is **repelled** by the electron-rich π cloud of the ring, so backside attack (SN2) is blocked by the ring itself.
5. An SN1 route is also unfavourable, because the phenyl cation $\ce{C6H5+}$ would be very unstable (its empty orbital is in the plane of the ring and cannot be stabilised by the π system).
:::

| | Chloroethane | Chlorobenzene |
|---|---|---|
| C–Cl bond | single bond, no delocalisation | partial double-bond character |
| bond strength | weaker | stronger |
| warm NaOH(aq) | hydrolysed to ethanol | no reaction |
| warm ethanolic $\ce{AgNO3}$ | white AgCl forms slowly | no precipitate |

## Fluoroalkanes and their uses

The C–F bond is the strongest single bond carbon forms with a halogen, and C–F and C–Cl bonds in small, fully halogenated molecules are not attacked by most reagents. Fluoroalkanes and fluorohalogenoalkanes are therefore chemically **inert**, non-flammable and of low toxicity, which led to many uses:

| Use | Example | Property used |
|---|---|---|
| refrigerants and air-conditioning fluids | $\ce{CCl2F2}$ (formerly); now hydrofluorocarbons such as $\ce{CH2FCF3}$ | inert, non-toxic, boiling point near room temperature |
| aerosol propellants (formerly) | CFCs | inert, volatile, non-flammable |
| anaesthetics | halothane, $\ce{CF3CHBrCl}$ | volatile, non-flammable, stable |
| non-stick coatings and insulation | poly(tetrafluoroethene), PTFE, $\ce{(-CF2-CF2-)_n}$ | very unreactive, low friction, heat resistant |
| fire extinguishers (formerly) | halons such as $\ce{CBrF3}$ | non-flammable, stable |

## CFCs and the ozone layer

Because CFCs are so unreactive, they are not removed in the lower atmosphere. Over years they diffuse up into the stratosphere, where the ultraviolet radiation is more intense.

1. **Photodissociation.** UV light breaks the weakest bond, C–Cl (not C–F), homolytically:

$$\ce{CCl2F2 -> CClF2^. + Cl^.}$$

2. **Chlorine radicals destroy ozone in a chain:**

$$\ce{Cl^. + O3 -> ClO^. + O2}$$

$$\ce{ClO^. + O -> Cl^. + O2}$$

(Oxygen atoms, O, are present in the stratosphere from the breakdown of $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{O3}$ by UV light. An alternative second step is $\ce{ClO^. + O3 -> Cl^. + 2O2}$.)

3. **Overall:** $\ce{O3 + O -> 2O2}$. The chlorine radical is regenerated, so it acts as a **catalyst**: one chlorine radical can destroy many thousands of ozone molecules before a termination step removes it.

The ozone layer absorbs much of the Sun's harmful UV-B radiation; thinning it increases the risk of skin cancer, eye cataracts and damage to crops. International agreement (the Montreal Protocol of 1987, joined by almost every country) phased out the production of CFCs.

**Replacements.** Hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs) contain C–H bonds, so most are broken down in the lower atmosphere, reducing (not removing) the harm; they are themselves being phased out. Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) such as $\ce{CH2FCF3}$ contain no chlorine, so they do not deplete ozone, but many are powerful greenhouse gases. Hydrocarbons such as butane are also used as propellants and refrigerants, but they are flammable.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: chlorine content of a CFC]
Trichlorofluoromethane, $\ce{CFCl3}$. Ar: C 12, F 19, Cl 35.5; L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

1. $M_\text{r} = 12 + 19 + 3(35.5) = 137.5$.
2. Percentage of chlorine by mass $= \dfrac{106.5}{137.5} \times 100 = 77.5\%$.
3. Molecules in 1.00 g $= \dfrac{1.00}{137.5} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 4.38 \times 10^{21}$.
4. If each molecule releases one Cl radical and each radical destroys many thousands of $\ce{O3}$ molecules, a single gram can destroy an enormous number of ozone molecules.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="UV breaks the C–F bond"}
In CFCs the C–Cl bond is weaker than C–F, so UV breaks C–Cl, releasing Cl•. Fluorine is not the problem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ozone hole and global warming are the same"}
Ozone depletion lets more UV reach the ground; the greenhouse effect traps infrared radiation. HFCs fix the first problem but may add to the second.
:::

:::mistake{title="Chlorobenzene is unreactive because Cl is less electronegative"}
The reason is the partial double-bond character (stronger C–Cl bond) and repulsion of nucleophiles by the π electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Chlorine radicals are used up"}
Cl• is regenerated in the second step, so it is a catalyst; that is why small amounts of CFCs do so much damage.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chlorobenzene (from benzene) and 2- and 4-chloromethylbenzene (from methylbenzene) are made with Cl₂ and AlCl₃.
- Chlorobenzene resists hydrolysis: a Cl lone pair overlaps with the ring, giving C–Cl partial double-bond character; the π cloud repels nucleophiles.
- Fluoroalkanes are inert, non-flammable and low in toxicity: refrigerants, propellants, anaesthetics, PTFE.
- In the stratosphere UV breaks C–Cl in CFCs; Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ and ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂; Cl• is a catalyst.
- CFCs were phased out; HCFCs and HFCs replaced them (HFCs do not deplete ozone but can be greenhouse gases).
:::
