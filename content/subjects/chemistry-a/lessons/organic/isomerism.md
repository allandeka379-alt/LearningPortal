## Introduction

Butane and 2-methylpropane are both $\ce{C4H10}$, and both are found in liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), the bottled gas many Zimbabwean households and businesses use for cooking when the electricity is off. Yet they have different boiling points: about −1 °C for butane and about −12 °C for 2-methylpropane. Same atoms, different arrangement, different properties: that is isomerism.

Isomerism matters because the structure, not just the formula, decides how a compound behaves, from the boiling point of a fuel to the way a drug fits a receptor in the body. In this lesson you will meet the types of isomerism, a reliable method for finding every isomer of a formula, and the reason some alkenes exist as cis and trans forms. You need to be able to name organic compounds and to know that a C=C bond contains a σ bond and a π bond.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Structural isomers"}
Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae.
:::

:::definition{term="Stereoisomers"}
Compounds with the same molecular formula and the same structural formula, but a different arrangement of atoms in space.
:::

:::definition{term="Cis/trans (geometrical) isomerism"}
A type of stereoisomerism in alkenes (and some rings) caused by restricted rotation about a C=C bond, when each carbon of the double bond carries two different groups.
:::

:::definition{term="Chiral centre"}
A carbon atom bonded to four different atoms or groups.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: chain, positional and functional group isomers, cis/trans isomers and recognising chiral centres. E/Z naming is accepted but not required. Optical isomerism itself is the next lesson (Cambridge A Level; ZIMSEC Form 6).
:::

## The three kinds of structural isomerism

| Kind | What differs | Example ($\ce{C4H10}$, $\ce{C4H9Br}$, $\ce{C3H6O}$) |
|---|---|---|
| chain | the carbon skeleton (straight or branched) | butane $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}$ and 2-methylpropane $\ce{(CH3)3CH}$ |
| positional | the position of the functional group on the same skeleton | 1-bromobutane $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br}$ and 2-bromobutane $\ce{CH3CHBrCH2CH3}$ |
| functional group | the functional group (a different homologous series) | propanal $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$ and propanone $\ce{CH3COCH3}$ |

Common functional group isomer pairs: aldehydes and ketones ($\ce{C_nH_{2n}O}$); carboxylic acids and esters ($\ce{C_nH_{2n}O2}$); alkenes and cycloalkanes ($\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$); alcohols and ethers ($\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}O}$).

Chain isomers have different physical properties: branching reduces the surface contact between molecules, so London (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole) forces are weaker and boiling points are lower. Functional group isomers have different chemical properties too.

## Finding every isomer: a method

1. Write the longest possible chain first.
2. Shorten the chain by one carbon and place the removed carbon as a branch in every distinct position (never on an end carbon, which would just lengthen the chain).
3. Repeat with shorter chains and larger or more branches.
4. For a functional group, place it in every distinct position on each skeleton.
5. Name each structure; two structures with the same name are the same compound.
6. Finally, check each alkene for cis/trans isomers.

:::example{title="Example 1: the isomers of C₅H₁₂"}
1. Five-carbon chain: pentane, $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3}$.
2. Four-carbon chain with one methyl branch: on carbon 2 only (carbon 3 is the same as carbon 2 counted from the other end): 2-methylbutane, $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$.
3. Three-carbon chain with two methyl branches: both must be on carbon 2: 2,2-dimethylpropane, $\ce{C(CH3)4}$.
4. There are **three** structural isomers. Their boiling points fall in the order pentane > 2-methylbutane > 2,2-dimethylpropane as branching increases.
:::

## Cis/trans isomerism

A C=C bond is one σ bond plus one π bond. The π bond is formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the plane of the molecule. Rotating one carbon relative to the other would destroy this overlap, so there is **no free rotation** about a C=C bond at room temperature. Groups fixed on the same side cannot swap to the other side.

Two conditions are needed:

1. a C=C double bond (restricted rotation), and
2. **two different groups on each carbon** of the double bond.

| Alkene | Groups on C1 | Groups on C2 | Cis/trans isomers? |
|---|---|---|---|
| but-2-ene $\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}$ | H, CH₃ | H, CH₃ | yes |
| but-1-ene $\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}$ | H, H | H, C₂H₅ | no (C1 has two H) |
| 2-methylpropene $\ce{(CH3)2C=CH2}$ | CH₃, CH₃ | H, H | no |
| 1,2-dichloroethene $\ce{ClCH=CHCl}$ | H, Cl | H, Cl | yes |

In the **cis** isomer the two similar groups (here the two $\ce{CH3}$ groups, or the two Cl atoms) are on the same side of the double bond; in the **trans** isomer they are on opposite sides. Cis and trans isomers have the same chemical reactions but can have different physical properties: cis-1,2-dichloroethene is polar (the C–Cl dipoles do not cancel) and boils higher than the non-polar trans isomer.

:::example{title="Example 2: all the isomers of C₄H₈"}
1. $\ce{C4H8}$ fits $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$: alkenes or cycloalkanes.
2. Straight-chain alkenes: but-1-ene and but-2-ene (positional isomers).
3. Branched alkene: 2-methylpropene (a chain isomer).
4. But-2-ene has cis and trans forms; but-1-ene and 2-methylpropene do not.
5. Rings: cyclobutane and methylcyclopropane (functional group isomers of the alkenes).
6. Total: four alkenes (counting cis- and trans-but-2-ene separately) and two cycloalkanes, six isomers in all.
:::

## Chiral centres: a first look

A carbon atom joined to **four different groups** is a chiral centre. Butan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}$, has one: the second carbon carries H, OH, CH₃ and C₂H₅. Propan-2-ol does not, because two of its groups are identical CH₃ groups. To spot a chiral centre, check each carbon with four single bonds and ask whether all four groups differ, looking along the whole chain, not only at the first atom. In rings, compare the two paths around the ring from the carbon in question. A chiral centre gives rise to optical isomers, studied in the next lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Counting the same molecule twice"}
$\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH3}$ and $\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3}$ are both 2-methylbutane. Name each structure to remove duplicates.
:::

:::mistake{title="Cis/trans for any alkene"}
If either carbon of the C=C carries two identical groups (such as =CH₂), there are no cis/trans isomers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the C=C cannot rotate because it is strong"}
The reason is the π bond: rotation would break the sideways overlap of p orbitals. Strength alone is not the explanation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Missing ring and functional group isomers"}
When a formula fits more than one homologous series (alkenes and cycloalkanes; acids and esters), include both families if the question asks for all isomers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to *deduce the number of isomers*, *give the structures of two isomers*, or *explain why a compound shows cis/trans isomerism*. Multiple-completion items in ZIMSEC Paper 1 often test the two conditions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain cis/trans"}
A full answer has two points: restricted rotation about the C=C (because of the π bond) and two different groups on each carbon of the double bond. Name or describe both isomers if asked.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing structures"}
When you *suggest* or *give* isomers, use unambiguous structural formulae and name each one; for cis and trans, say which groups are on the same side.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Structural isomers share a molecular formula but differ in chain, position of a group, or functional group.
- Find isomers systematically: longest chain first, then branches, then positions; name each to avoid duplicates.
- Cis/trans isomerism needs a C=C (no rotation, because of the π bond) and two different groups on each double-bond carbon.
- Cis has similar groups on the same side; trans on opposite sides. Physical properties can differ.
- A chiral centre is a carbon with four different groups; it leads to optical isomerism.
:::
