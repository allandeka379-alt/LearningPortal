## Introduction

Every medicine, dye and flavouring was first made by a chemist who worked backwards from the molecule they wanted to substances they could buy. Organic synthesis is that puzzle: choose a sequence of reactions, with the right reagents and conditions, that converts a starting material into a target. It is also where the separate reactions you have learned for alkenes, halogenoalkanes, alcohols, carbonyl compounds, acids, esters and nitriles come together.

This lesson gives you a reaction map to work from, a method for planning routes, and a checklist for analysing a route you are given. You should know the reactions (with reagents and conditions) of each AS Level functional group.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Functional group"}
The atom or group of atoms in an organic molecule that is responsible for its characteristic reactions, such as C=C, –OH, –CHO or –COOH.
:::

:::definition{term="Synthetic route"}
A sequence of reactions, each with stated reagents and conditions, that converts a starting compound into a target compound via isolated intermediates.
:::

:::definition{term="By-product"}
A substance formed in a reaction step in addition to the desired product, either as part of the equation (such as $\ce{H2O}$ or $\ce{NH4+}$) or from a competing reaction (such as an isomer or an over-oxidised product).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Cambridge AS Level (21.1, Papers 1 and 2): identify groups, predict reactions, devise and analyse routes. ZIMSEC does not have a separate synthesis topic, but its Paper 3 Section C questions often ask for a two-step conversion, so the method here helps ZIMSEC candidates too. A Level reactions (arenes, acyl chlorides, amines and nitrile reduction) are in the multi-step synthesis lesson.
:::

## The AS Level reaction map

Learn each arrow with its reagent and condition: in a route question, a missing or wrong condition loses the mark.

| From | To | Reagent and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| alkane | halogenoalkane | $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$, UV light | free-radical substitution |
| alkene | alkane | $\ce{H2}$, Ni catalyst and heat (or Pt) | addition (reduction) |
| alkene | alcohol | steam, $\ce{H3PO4}$ catalyst, about 300 °C, 60-70 atm | electrophilic addition |
| alkene | halogenoalkane | HX(g) or $\ce{X2}$, room temperature | electrophilic addition |
| alkene | diol | cold dilute acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ | oxidation |
| halogenoalkane | alcohol | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | nucleophilic substitution (hydrolysis) |
| halogenoalkane | nitrile (one more C) | KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux | nucleophilic substitution |
| halogenoalkane | amine | excess $\ce{NH3}$ in ethanol, heated under pressure | nucleophilic substitution |
| halogenoalkane | alkene | NaOH in ethanol, heat | elimination |
| alcohol | halogenoalkane | HX(g); KBr + conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ (or $\ce{H3PO4}$); $\ce{PCl5}$; $\ce{PCl3}$ + heat; $\ce{SOCl2}$ | substitution |
| primary alcohol | aldehyde | acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, distil out as it forms | oxidation |
| primary alcohol or aldehyde | carboxylic acid | acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, heat under reflux | oxidation |
| secondary alcohol | ketone | acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, heat | oxidation |
| alcohol | alkene | heated $\ce{Al2O3}$, or conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ / $\ce{H3PO4}$ with heat | elimination (dehydration) |
| alcohol + carboxylic acid | ester | conc. $\ce{H2SO4}$ catalyst, heat | condensation (esterification) |
| aldehyde or ketone | alcohol | $\ce{NaBH4}$ (or $\ce{LiAlH4}$) | reduction |
| aldehyde or ketone | hydroxynitrile (one more C) | HCN with KCN catalyst, heat | nucleophilic addition |
| carboxylic acid | primary alcohol | $\ce{LiAlH4}$ in dry ether | reduction |
| nitrile | carboxylic acid | dilute HCl and heat; or dilute NaOH and heat, then acidify | hydrolysis |
| ester | acid (or salt) + alcohol | dilute acid or dilute alkali, heat | hydrolysis |

## Identifying functional groups and predicting reactions

In a molecule with several groups, each group usually reacts as it would on its own, so you can predict reactions group by group. Distinguishing tests are also predictions:

| Group | Test or reaction | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| C=C | bromine water | orange to colourless |
| primary or secondary alcohol, aldehyde | acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, warm | orange to green |
| aldehyde or ketone | 2,4-DNPH reagent | orange-yellow precipitate |
| aldehyde only | Tollens' reagent, warm / Fehling's solution, warm | silver mirror / red-brown precipitate |
| $\ce{CH3CO-}$ or $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$ | alkaline aqueous iodine, warm | yellow precipitate of $\ce{CHI3}$ |
| –OH (alcohol or acid) | sodium metal | effervescence of $\ce{H2}$ |
| –COOH | $\ce{Na2CO3}$ or $\ce{NaHCO3}$ | effervescence of $\ce{CO2}$ |

Watch for **selectivity**: $\ce{NaBH4}$ reduces C=O in aldehydes and ketones but not C=C, carboxylic acids or esters; hydrogen with nickel reduces C=C (and can reduce C=O too).

:::example{title="Example 1: predicting the reactions of a multifunctional molecule"}
Compound W is $\ce{HOCH2CH=CHCOCH3}$.

1. Groups: a primary alcohol (–CH₂OH), a C=C, and a methyl ketone (–COCH₃).
2. Bromine water: decolorised (addition across C=C).
3. Acidified potassium dichromate(VI), warm: orange to green (the primary alcohol is oxidised).
4. 2,4-DNPH: orange-yellow precipitate (ketone C=O). Tollens' reagent: no silver mirror (no aldehyde).
5. Alkaline iodine: yellow precipitate (CH₃CO– group).
6. Sodium: hydrogen gas (O–H). Sodium carbonate: no gas (no –COOH).
7. $\ce{NaBH4}$ gives $\ce{HOCH2CH=CHCH(OH)CH3}$: the ketone is reduced, the C=C is not.
:::

## Planning a route

1. Write the starting material and the target side by side. Compare the **carbon skeleton**: same number of carbons, or one more?
2. Compare the **functional groups** and their positions.
3. If the chain grows by one carbon, a C–C bond must be made: at AS this means **cyanide**. KCN in ethanol on a halogenoalkane gives a nitrile; HCN (with KCN catalyst) on an aldehyde or ketone gives a hydroxynitrile. Hydrolysis then turns –CN into –COOH.
4. Work **backwards** from the target: "what could this be made from in one step?" Repeat until you reach the starting material.
5. Give each step its reagent, conditions, product name and reaction type.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: bromoethane to propanoic acid]
The target has three carbons; bromoethane has two, so a carbon must be added.

1. Step 1: $\ce{CH3CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CN + Br-}$, using KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux. Propanenitrile forms by nucleophilic substitution.
2. Step 2: $\ce{CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH2COOH + NH4+}$, using dilute HCl, heat under reflux. Hydrolysis gives propanoic acid.
3. By-products: $\ce{Br-}$ (as KBr) in step 1; ammonium chloride in step 2. A little ethene can also form in step 1 by elimination.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a three-step route that adds a carbon"}
Convert propan-1-ol into 2-hydroxybutanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)COOH}$.

| Step | Reagent and conditions | Product | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$, warm, distil the product off as it forms | propanal, $\ce{CH3CH2CHO}$ | oxidation |
| 2 | HCN, KCN catalyst, heat | 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, $\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CN}$ | nucleophilic addition |
| 3 | dilute HCl, heat under reflux | 2-hydroxybutanoic acid | hydrolysis |

Possible by-products: propanoic acid in step 1 if the aldehyde is not distilled off; ammonium chloride in step 3. HCN is extremely toxic, so step 2 is done in a fume cupboard.
:::

## Analysing a given route

When a question gives you a route, check each step against this list:

- **Type of reaction**: substitution, addition, elimination, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, condensation.
- **Reagent and conditions**: aqueous or ethanolic; reflux or distil; catalyst; UV; temperature and pressure.
- **By-products**: inorganic products from the equation, and organic side products (isomers from addition to an unsymmetrical alkene, elimination competing with substitution, mixtures from free-radical substitution, over-oxidation).
- **Practical problems**: a step with a low yield, a step that gives a mixture that must be separated, toxic reagents.

Fewer steps usually means a higher overall yield, because the yields multiply: two steps of 80% give 0.80 × 0.80 = 64% overall.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Aqueous versus ethanolic hydroxide"}
NaOH(aq) with a halogenoalkane gives an alcohol; NaOH in ethanol gives an alkene. Always state the solvent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reflux versus distillation"}
To stop at the aldehyde, distil it off as it forms. Heating under reflux takes a primary alcohol all the way to the carboxylic acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the extra carbon"}
Count the carbons. If the target has one more, a cyanide step is needed; no other AS reaction makes a new C–C bond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using NaBH₄ on an acid"}
$\ce{NaBH4}$ does not reduce carboxylic acids; $\ce{LiAlH4}$ does.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS), and synthesis questions on Paper 4 that build on AS reactions; ZIMSEC Paper 3 Section C two-step conversions. Mark schemes award one mark per correct intermediate and one per correct reagent with conditions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify* the functional groups: name them precisely ("primary alcohol", "ketone", not "OH group", "C=O"). *Suggest* a route: give every reagent and condition and the structure of each intermediate. *Deduce* the type of reaction: one precise term per step. *Predict* the products: apply each group's reaction separately and check selectivity.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Each functional group in a multifunctional molecule reacts as it would alone; check selectivity (e.g. NaBH₄ reduces C=O, not C=C).
- Plan backwards from the target; compare carbon skeletons and functional groups.
- At AS, the only way to add a carbon is via cyanide: KCN in ethanol on a halogenoalkane, or HCN/KCN on a carbonyl, then hydrolyse.
- For each step state reagent, conditions, product and reaction type.
- Analyse routes for by-products, competing reactions and yield; fewer steps usually give a higher overall yield.
:::
