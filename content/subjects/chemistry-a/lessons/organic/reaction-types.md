## Introduction

There are millions of organic compounds, but only a handful of reaction types. Once you can say "this is a nucleophile attacking an electron-poor carbon" or "this is an electrophile attacking a double bond", whole families of reactions make sense, and you stop memorising and start predicting. The same language is used in industry: the margarine made by hydrogenating vegetable oils (for example from sunflower or soya beans) relies on an addition reaction, while the bleaches and chlorinated solvents used in water treatment and cleaning come from substitution chemistry.

This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the curly-arrow conventions. Each mechanism (free-radical substitution, electrophilic addition, nucleophilic substitution, and so on) has its own lesson where it is described step by step. You should already know covalent bonding, electronegativity and bond polarity, and how to name organic compounds.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Homolytic fission"}
Breaking of a covalent bond in which each atom keeps one electron of the shared pair, forming two free radicals.
:::

:::definition{term="Heterolytic fission"}
Breaking of a covalent bond in which one atom takes both electrons of the shared pair, forming a cation and an anion.
:::

:::definition{term="Free radical"}
A species with an unpaired electron, shown with a dot, for example $\ce{Cl^.}$ or $\ce{CH3^.}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleophile"}
A species that donates a pair of electrons to an electron-deficient atom, forming a new covalent bond (an electron-pair donor).
:::

:::definition{term="Electrophile"}
A species that accepts a pair of electrons from an electron-rich atom or bond, forming a new covalent bond (an electron-pair acceptor).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: fission, radicals, nucleophiles and electrophiles, the reaction types and curly arrows. Electrophilic substitution in arenes and addition-elimination (acyl chlorides) are A Level for Cambridge and Form 6 for ZIMSEC.
:::

## Breaking bonds two ways

| | Homolytic fission | Heterolytic fission |
|---|---|---|
| What happens to the bonding pair | split, one electron to each atom | both electrons go to one atom (usually the more electronegative) |
| Products | two free radicals | a positive ion and a negative ion |
| Example | $\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}$ in ultraviolet light | $\ce{(CH3)3C-Br -> (CH3)3C+ + Br-}$ |
| Arrow | half-headed (fish-hook) arrows, one per electron | one full curly arrow, from the bond to the atom that takes the pair |
| Favoured by | non-polar bonds, ultraviolet light or high temperature, gas phase | polar bonds, polar solvents |

## Nucleophiles and electrophiles

Nucleophiles are "nucleus-loving": they have a lone pair (and often a negative charge) and attack δ+ carbon atoms. Electrophiles are "electron-loving": they are positive or δ+ and attack electron-rich regions such as the π bond of C=C or the delocalised ring of benzene.

| Nucleophiles (lone-pair donors) | Electrophiles (electron-pair acceptors) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$, $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{Br-}$ | $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{NO2+}$, $\ce{Br2}$ (polarised), $\ce{H^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}$, carbocations |

## Reaction types

| Type | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| addition | two reactants join to give one product; a multiple bond becomes single | $\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2BrCH2Br}$ |
| substitution | an atom or group is replaced by another | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2OH + Br-}$ |
| elimination | a small molecule is removed from one molecule, forming a multiple bond | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH2=CH2 + H2O + Br-}$ (ethanolic) |
| hydrolysis | a bond is broken by reaction with water (or $\ce{OH-}$ or acid) | an ester + water → acid + alcohol |
| condensation | two molecules join with loss of a small molecule such as water | acid + alcohol → ester + water |
| oxidation | gain of O or loss of H, shown with [O] | $\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}$ |
| reduction | gain of H or loss of O, shown with [H] | $\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}$ |

The symbols [O] and [H] stand for oxygen and hydrogen supplied by the reagent (for example acidified dichromate(VI), or $\ce{NaBH4}$). Balance an organic oxidation or reduction by counting atoms: every hydrogen removed needs an O to make water, and every [O] added appears in the product.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: balancing with [O] and [H]]
1. Propan-1-ol to propanoic acid: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ → $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$. The product has one more O and two fewer H.
2. The two H leave as $\ce{H2O}$, which needs one [O]; the extra O in the product needs another [O].
3. Balanced: $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3CH2COOH + H2O}$.
4. Butanone to butan-2-ol: $\ce{CH3COCH2CH3 + 2[H] -> CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}$ (two H added, no O lost).
:::

## Curly arrows

A curly arrow shows the movement of **a pair of electrons**. It always **starts** at a lone pair, a bond (σ or π), or a negative charge, and **ends** where the pair goes: at an atom (to form a new bond) or at an atom that will carry the pair away (when a bond breaks). Arrows never start at a positive charge or at a nucleus.

:::example{title="Example 2: describing curly arrows in words"}
The reaction of hydroxide ions with bromomethane, $\ce{CH3Br + OH- -> CH3OH + Br-}$:

1. The C–Br bond is polar: C is δ+ because bromine is more electronegative.
2. Arrow 1 starts at a lone pair on the oxygen of $\ce{OH-}$ and ends at the δ+ carbon: a new C–O bond forms.
3. Arrow 2 starts at the C–Br bond and ends at the bromine atom: the bond breaks heterolytically and Br leaves as $\ce{Br-}$, taking both electrons.
4. Classification: $\ce{OH-}$ is a nucleophile, the reaction is substitution, so this is nucleophilic substitution.
:::

## Inductive effects and carbocation stability

Alkyl groups are **electron-donating**: they push electron density along σ bonds towards a neighbouring atom (a positive inductive effect). A positively charged carbon with more alkyl groups around it has its charge spread (dispersed) more, so it is more stable:

| Carbocation | Example | Alkyl groups on C⁺ | Relative stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| tertiary | $\ce{(CH3)3C+}$ | 3 | most stable |
| secondary | $\ce{(CH3)2CH+}$ | 2 | intermediate |
| primary | $\ce{CH3CH2+}$ | 1 | least stable (of these) |

This order explains Markovnikov addition to alkenes and why tertiary halogenoalkanes react by SN1. Both are developed in later lessons.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Arrows from the wrong place"}
An arrow starts at electrons (a lone pair or a bond), never at a positive charge or an atom's symbol without a lone pair, and points to where the electrons go.
:::

:::mistake{title="Electrophile and nucleophile swapped"}
$\ce{OH-}$, $\ce{CN-}$ and $\ce{NH3}$ give electron pairs (nucleophiles). $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{NO2+}$ and $\ce{Br^{\delta+}}$ take electron pairs (electrophiles).
:::

:::mistake{title="Hydrolysis is not the same as hydration"}
Hydrolysis splits a molecule using water; hydration adds water across a C=C (an addition reaction).
:::

:::mistake{title="Unbalanced [O] equations"}
Count O and H on both sides. Oxidising a primary alcohol to a carboxylic acid needs 2[O] and forms one $\ce{H2O}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Classifying reactions"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1, 2 and 4 often ask you to *state the type of reaction* or *name the mechanism*. Give both words when a mechanism is asked: "nucleophilic substitution", not just "substitution".
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describing a mechanism"}
For *describe the mechanism*, mention each arrow (start and end), every partial charge and lone pair that matters, and the intermediate with its charge. Each of these usually earns a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain questions"}
*Explain why tertiary carbocations are more stable*: three electron-donating alkyl groups, positive inductive effect, charge dispersed. Link the structure to the property.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Homolytic fission gives two free radicals; heterolytic fission gives a cation and an anion.
- Nucleophiles donate electron pairs to δ+ atoms; electrophiles accept electron pairs from electron-rich regions.
- Reaction types: addition, substitution, elimination, hydrolysis, condensation, oxidation [O] and reduction [H].
- A curly arrow shows a pair of electrons moving from a lone pair or a bond to an atom.
- Alkyl groups are electron-donating, so carbocation stability is tertiary > secondary > primary.
:::
