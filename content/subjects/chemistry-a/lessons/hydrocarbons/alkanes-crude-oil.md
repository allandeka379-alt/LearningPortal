## Introduction

Every commuter omnibus on the road from Harare to Chitungwiza runs on alkanes. Petrol and diesel are mixtures of alkanes refined from crude oil, and the LP gas used for cooking is mainly propane and butane. Zimbabwe imports its liquid fuels, but the chemistry is the same everywhere: separate crude oil into fractions, crack the heavy fractions into more useful smaller molecules, and burn the products, ideally completely.

Burning fuels also produces pollutants, and modern vehicles carry catalytic converters to remove some of them. In this lesson you will meet the alkanes as a homologous series, the industrial processing of crude oil, combustion calculations and the environmental side of fuel use. You should know the general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, how London forces depend on molecular size, and how to use the molar gas volume.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkane"}
A saturated hydrocarbon with only single C–C and C–H bonds, general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$ (cycloalkanes $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$).
:::

:::definition{term="Fractional distillation"}
Separation of a mixture of liquids with different boiling points by repeated vaporisation and condensation in a fractionating column; crude oil is separated into fractions, each a mixture with a range of boiling points.
:::

:::definition{term="Cracking"}
The breaking of long-chain alkanes into shorter alkanes and alkenes (and sometimes hydrogen) by heating, usually with a catalyst.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete / incomplete combustion"}
Complete: burning in excess oxygen to carbon dioxide and water. Incomplete: burning in limited oxygen, giving carbon monoxide and/or carbon (soot) as well as water.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: sources, cracking, combustion and engine pollutants. ZIMSEC suggests demonstrating cracking with liquid paraffin or cooking oil passed over heated broken porcelain or aluminium oxide.
:::

## Crude oil and fractional distillation

Crude oil is heated to vaporise most of it and fed into a column that is hot at the bottom and cooler at the top. Larger molecules condense lower down; small molecules rise further.

| Fraction (approximate carbon range) | Typical use |
|---|---|
| refinery gases (C₁–C₄) | LPG for cooking and heating |
| gasoline / petrol (C₅–C₁₀) | car fuel |
| naphtha (C₇–C₁₀) | feedstock for the chemical industry and cracking |
| kerosene / paraffin (C₁₀–C₁₆) | jet fuel, paraffin for lamps and stoves |
| diesel / gas oil (C₁₄–C₂₀) | diesel engines, trucks, generators |
| residue: fuel oil, waxes, bitumen (above C₂₀) | ships, candles, road surfaces |

**Boiling point rises with chain length**: longer molecules have more electrons and more surface contact, so their London forces are stronger. **Branched isomers boil lower** than their straight-chain isomers because branching reduces the contact area between molecules.

## Cracking

The heavy fractions are in less demand than petrol and are cracked. Catalytic cracking uses heat (around 450-500 °C) with a catalyst such as aluminium oxide or zeolites; thermal cracking uses higher temperatures and pressures without a catalyst. A long alkane gives a shorter alkane plus at least one alkene, because there are not enough hydrogen atoms for all the products to be alkanes.

$$\ce{C12H26 -> C8H18 + 2C2H4}$$

The alkenes (ethene, propene) are valuable for making polymers and other chemicals; the shorter alkanes go into petrol. In a school demonstration, liquid paraffin soaked into mineral wool is heated so that its vapour passes over strongly heated broken porcelain or aluminium oxide; the gas collected over water decolorises bromine water, showing that alkenes have formed. (Remove the delivery tube from the water before stopping heating, to avoid suck-back; wear eye protection.)

Alkanes can also be made by **hydrogenating alkenes**: hydrogen with a nickel (or platinum) catalyst and heat, for example $\ce{CH2=CHCH3 + H2 -> CH3CH2CH3}$.

## Why alkanes are unreactive

C–C and C–H bonds are strong (about 350 and 410 kJ mol⁻¹), and C–H bonds are almost non-polar because carbon and hydrogen have similar electronegativities. With no δ+ or δ− sites, alkanes do not attract nucleophiles or electrophiles. They react only under vigorous conditions: combustion, and free-radical substitution with halogens in ultraviolet light (next lesson).

## Combustion

Complete combustion: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. In a limited supply of air, carbon monoxide or soot forms instead, for example $\ce{C3H8 + \frac{7}{2}O2 -> 3CO + 4H2O}$. Carbon monoxide is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin more strongly than oxygen does, reducing oxygen transport. Never run a generator or burn charcoal or gas indoors without ventilation.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: gas volumes in combustion]
25.0 cm³ of propane is exploded with 200 cm³ of oxygen (an excess). All volumes are measured at the same room temperature and pressure.

1. Equation: $\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}$. Volume ratio of gases equals mole ratio (Avogadro's law).
2. Oxygen used = 5 × 25.0 = 125 cm³; oxygen left = 200 − 125 = 75 cm³.
3. Carbon dioxide formed = 3 × 25.0 = 75 cm³.
4. After cooling to room temperature the water is liquid (negligible volume): final gas volume = 75 + 75 = 150 cm³.
5. Passing this through aqueous sodium hydroxide absorbs the $\ce{CO2}$, leaving 75 cm³ (the excess oxygen).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: carbon dioxide from burning octane]
Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced when 1.00 kg of octane, $\ce{C8H18}$, burns completely. Ar: C 12, H 1, O 16.

1. $\ce{2C8H18 + 25O2 -> 16CO2 + 18H2O}$, so 1 mol octane gives 8 mol $\ce{CO2}$.
2. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C8H18}) = 8(12) + 18(1) = 114$; n(octane) = 1000 ÷ 114 = 8.77 mol.
3. n($\ce{CO2}$) = 8 × 8.77 = 70.2 mol.
4. Mass = 70.2 × 44 = 3090 g = 3.09 kg, about three times the mass of fuel burned (the extra mass is oxygen from the air).
:::

## Pollutants from engines and catalytic converters

| Pollutant | Origin | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| carbon monoxide | incomplete combustion | toxic |
| oxides of nitrogen, NO and NO₂ | N₂ and O₂ from air combine at the high temperature in the engine | acid rain, photochemical smog, respiratory irritation |
| unburnt hydrocarbons | incomplete combustion | photochemical smog; some are carcinogenic |
| carbon dioxide | complete combustion | greenhouse gas, linked to climate change |

A catalytic converter has a honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium (platinum group metals, which also occur on the Great Dyke). Hot exhaust gases react on the metal surface:

$$\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}$$

$$\ce{C8H18 + 25NO -> 8CO2 + 9H2O + \frac{25}{2}N2}$$

Unburnt hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide are also oxidised by oxygen to $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. The converter does not reduce $\ce{CO2}$ emissions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the alkene in cracking"}
A cracking equation must balance H as well as C; at least one product is an alkene.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting water as a gas at room temperature"}
In volume problems measured at room temperature, water is a liquid and its volume is ignored.
:::

:::mistake{title="Nitrogen oxides come from the fuel"}
NOₓ forms because nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the high temperatures in the engine, not because petrol contains nitrogen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying alkanes are unreactive because bonds are polar"}
The reverse: C–H bonds are almost non-polar, so there are no partial charges to attract reagents.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2: *construct* cracking and combustion equations, *calculate* volumes or masses, *explain* boiling-point trends and *state* the reactions in catalytic converters.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explaining boiling points"}
Name the force (London or instantaneous dipole–induced dipole), link it to the number of electrons or surface contact, and say that more energy is needed to separate the molecules.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Gas volume problems"}
Write the balanced equation first, use volume ratios directly for gases at the same conditions, and state what each absorbent removes (NaOH removes CO₂; pyrogallol or other reagents remove O₂).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Crude oil is separated by fractional distillation; boiling point rises with chain length and falls with branching.
- Cracking (heat, Al₂O₃ or zeolite catalyst) turns long alkanes into shorter alkanes and alkenes; hydrogenation (H₂, Ni, heat) converts alkenes into alkanes.
- Alkanes are unreactive because C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar.
- Complete combustion gives CO₂ and H₂O; incomplete combustion gives toxic CO or soot.
- Engines emit CO, NOₓ and unburnt hydrocarbons; catalytic converters (Pt, Pd, Rh) convert them to CO₂, N₂ and H₂O.
:::
