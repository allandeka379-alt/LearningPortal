## Introduction

Ethene is one of the most important chemicals in the world: it becomes poly(ethene) for the plastic bags and water tanks seen across Zimbabwe, ethanol, and many other products. Fruit growers also know it as a plant hormone that ripens bananas and other fruit. What makes alkenes so useful is the C=C double bond. Its π electrons are exposed above and below the molecule, so alkenes undergo addition reactions that alkanes cannot.

This lesson surveys how alkenes are made and what they react with, with the reagents and conditions you must learn. The mechanism behind most of these additions, electrophilic addition, has its own lesson. You should know the structure of the C=C bond (one σ and one π bond), naming of alkenes and cis/trans isomerism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkene"}
An unsaturated hydrocarbon containing a C=C double bond; general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n}}$ for one double bond.
:::

:::definition{term="Addition reaction"}
A reaction in which two molecules combine to form a single product; for alkenes the C=C becomes a C–C single bond.
:::

:::definition{term="Dehydration"}
Elimination of a water molecule from a compound, for example an alcohol forming an alkene.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: all the preparations and reactions in this lesson, including both manganate(VII) oxidations and deducing the position of C=C from the oxidation products. Addition polymerisation is treated fully in the polymers lessons.
:::

## Making alkenes

| Method | Starting material | Reagent and conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| elimination of HX | halogenoalkane | NaOH (or KOH) in ethanol, heat under reflux | $\ce{CH3CH2Br + NaOH -> CH2=CH2 + NaBr + H2O}$ |
| dehydration | alcohol | heated $\ce{Al2O3}$ (vapour passed over it), or concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ or $\ce{H3PO4}$ with heating | $\ce{CH3CH2OH -> CH2=CH2 + H2O}$ |
| cracking | long-chain alkane | heat with $\ce{Al2O3}$ or zeolite catalyst | $\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}$ |

The solvent matters for halogenoalkanes: **ethanolic** hydroxide favours elimination to an alkene, while **aqueous** hydroxide favours substitution to an alcohol. Unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes and alcohols can give more than one alkene; butan-2-ol gives but-1-ene and both cis- and trans-but-2-ene.

## Addition reactions

| Reagent | Conditions | Product from ethene | Product from propene |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{H2}$ | Ni catalyst, about 150 °C (or Pt, room temperature) | ethane | propane |
| $\ce{H2O(g)}$ (steam) | $\ce{H3PO4}$ catalyst, about 300 °C and 60-70 atm | ethanol | mainly propan-2-ol |
| HBr (or HCl, HI) | room temperature (gas, or concentrated solution) | bromoethane | mainly 2-bromopropane |
| $\ce{Br2}$ | room temperature, in the dark | 1,2-dibromoethane | 1,2-dibromopropane |
| $\ce{Cl2}$ | room temperature | 1,2-dichloroethane | 1,2-dichloropropane |

Hydrogenation is used to harden vegetable oils into margarine. Hydration of ethene is an industrial route to ethanol; in Zimbabwe fuel ethanol is made instead by fermenting sugar from cane.

With an **unsymmetrical** alkene such as propene, HBr and steam add to give two possible products. The major one has the hydrogen added to the carbon that already has more hydrogens (Markovnikov's rule), because the reaction goes through the more stable secondary carbocation. This is explained in the electrophilic addition lesson.

### The test for C=C

Shake the compound with **bromine water** (orange-brown). An alkene decolorises it rapidly at room temperature; an alkane does not (unless left in ultraviolet light for a long time). With aqueous bromine the main organic product contains both Br and OH (for ethene, 2-bromoethanol), but the observation is the same.

## Oxidation with acidified manganate(VII)

| Conditions | What happens | Observation | Product from ethene |
|---|---|---|---|
| cold, dilute, acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ | two OH groups added across C=C | purple to colourless | ethane-1,2-diol $\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}$ |
| hot, concentrated, acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ | C=C bond broken completely | purple to colourless | $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ |

For the cold reaction: $\ce{CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O -> HOCH2CH2OH}$.

In the **hot, concentrated** reaction each carbon of the double bond becomes C=O, and the fragment is then oxidised as far as it can go:

| Group at the end of the C=C | Product after hot oxidation |
|---|---|
| $\ce{=CH2}$ | $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ (via methanoic acid) |
| $\ce{=CHR}$ | carboxylic acid $\ce{RCOOH}$ |
| $\ce{=CRR'}$ | ketone $\ce{RCOR'}$ |

:::example{title="Example 1: locating a double bond"}
An alkene, $\ce{C6H12}$, is heated with concentrated acidified manganate(VII). The only organic products are propanone and propanoic acid.

1. Propanone, $\ce{CH3COCH3}$, comes from a $\ce{=C(CH3)2}$ end.
2. Propanoic acid, $\ce{CH3CH2COOH}$, comes from a $\ce{=CHCH2CH3}$ end.
3. Join the two ends at the double bond: $\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH2CH3}$.
4. The alkene is 2-methylpent-2-ene. Check: 3 + 3 = 6 carbons, $\ce{C6H12}$. It has no cis/trans isomers, because one end carries two methyl groups.
:::

## Counting double bonds by hydrogenation

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how many C=C bonds?]
0.410 g of a hydrocarbon $\ce{C6H10}$ (an open-chain compound with no C≡C bond) reacts completely with 240 cm³ of hydrogen at room temperature and pressure, using a nickel catalyst. Ar: C 12, H 1; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C6H10}) = 6(12) + 10(1) = 82$; n = 0.410 ÷ 82 = 0.00500 mol.
2. n($\ce{H2}$) = 0.240 dm³ ÷ 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ = 0.0100 mol.
3. Ratio $\ce{H2}$ : hydrocarbon = 0.0100 : 0.00500 = 2 : 1.
4. Each C=C adds one $\ce{H2}$, so the molecule has **two** C=C bonds (a diene), consistent with $\ce{C6H10}$ being 4 H short of hexane.
:::

## Addition polymerisation in outline

Many alkene molecules join by addition to make a long saturated chain: $n\ce{CH2=CH2 -> (-CH2-CH2-)_n}$. Propene gives poly(propene) and chloroethene gives poly(chloroethene) (PVC). Details, repeat units and disposal are covered with polymers.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Aqueous versus ethanolic hydroxide"}
NaOH(aq) with a halogenoalkane gives an alcohol (substitution); NaOH in ethanol gives an alkene (elimination).
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two manganate(VII) reactions"}
Cold dilute gives a diol (C–C remains); hot concentrated breaks the carbon chain at the double bond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the steam catalyst"}
Hydration needs phosphoric acid on a support, high temperature and high pressure; ethene does not react with liquid water on standing.
:::

:::mistake{title="=CH₂ gives a carboxylic acid"}
A terminal =CH₂ group ends up as carbon dioxide (and water) on hot oxidation, not as methanoic acid in the products you report.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2: *state the reagents and conditions*, *give the product*, *deduce the structure* of an alkene from oxidation products. In reaction schemes, give both reagent and condition (for example "steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst, 300 °C, 60 atm").
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Deducing structures"}
Work backwards: replace each C=O in the oxidation products by one end of a C=C; =CH₂ ends are revealed by CO₂. Check the total carbon count against the formula.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Observations"}
Bromine water: orange (or yellow-orange) to colourless. Acidified manganate(VII): purple to colourless. Say both colours.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Alkenes are made by elimination of HX (ethanolic NaOH, heat), dehydration of alcohols (Al₂O₃ or concentrated H₂SO₄/H₃PO₄, heat) and cracking.
- Addition: H₂ (Ni, heat), steam (H₃PO₄, about 300 °C, 60-70 atm), HX and X₂ at room temperature.
- Bromine water is decolorised by C=C: the test for unsaturation.
- Cold dilute acidified KMnO₄ gives a diol; hot concentrated KMnO₄ splits the C=C into ketones, acids or CO₂, revealing its position.
- Moles of H₂ absorbed per mole show how many C=C bonds a molecule has.
:::
