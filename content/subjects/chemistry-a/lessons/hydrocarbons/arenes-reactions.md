## Introduction

Paracetamol, many dyes, explosives such as TNT, the polystyrene in takeaway containers and a large number of pesticides used on farms all start from benzene or methylbenzene. To make them, chemists must add groups to the ring without destroying it, and must control where on the ring the new group goes. This lesson collects the reactions of arenes that you need, with the exact reagents and conditions.

You should already know the delocalised structure of benzene and why it resists addition, how to name aromatic compounds, and the free-radical substitution of alkanes. The mechanism of the ring substitutions, electrophilic substitution, is in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Halogen carrier"}
A Lewis-acid catalyst such as $\ce{AlCl3}$, $\ce{AlBr3}$, $\ce{FeBr3}$ or iron filings (which form $\ce{FeBr3}$ with bromine) that polarises the halogen so it can attack a benzene ring.
:::

:::definition{term="Nitrating mixture"}
A mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid, used to substitute a nitro group ($\ce{-NO2}$) into an arene.
:::

:::definition{term="Friedel-Crafts reaction"}
Substitution of an alkyl group (alkylation) or an acyl group (acylation) into a benzene ring, using a halogenoalkane or an acyl chloride with an $\ce{AlCl3}$ catalyst.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: halogenation, nitration, side-chain oxidation, hydrogenation, ring versus side-chain halogenation and directing effects. Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation are listed in both syllabuses; Cambridge (A Level) names them explicitly with $\ce{CH3Cl}$ or $\ce{CH3COCl}$ and $\ce{AlCl3}$.
:::

## Reactions of the benzene ring

| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Equation (benzene) | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| bromination | $\ce{Br2}$, $\ce{AlBr3}$ or $\ce{FeBr3}$ (or Fe), room temperature, dry | $\ce{C6H6 + Br2 -> C6H5Br + HBr}$ | bromobenzene |
| chlorination | $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$ (or Fe), room temperature, dry | $\ce{C6H6 + Cl2 -> C6H5Cl + HCl}$ | chlorobenzene |
| nitration | concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ and concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$, 25-60 °C (below about 55 °C for mononitration of benzene) | $\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 -> C6H5NO2 + H2O}$ | nitrobenzene |
| alkylation | $\ce{CH3Cl}$ (or another halogenoalkane), $\ce{AlCl3}$, heat under reflux, dry | $\ce{C6H6 + CH3Cl -> C6H5CH3 + HCl}$ | methylbenzene |
| acylation | $\ce{CH3COCl}$, $\ce{AlCl3}$, heat under reflux, dry | $\ce{C6H6 + CH3COCl -> C6H5COCH3 + HCl}$ | phenylethanone |
| hydrogenation | $\ce{H2}$, Pt or Ni catalyst, heat (and pressure) | $\ce{C6H6 + 3H2 -> C6H12}$ | cyclohexane |

All but hydrogenation are **substitutions**: the ring keeps its delocalised π system. The catalysts must be dry because water reacts with aluminium chloride. Above about 60 °C nitration of benzene gives increasing amounts of 1,3-dinitrobenzene. Hydrogenation is an addition and needs much more forcing conditions than for an alkene, because the delocalisation must be destroyed.

**Safety:** benzene is a carcinogen and is replaced by methylbenzene in school work where possible; the nitrating mixture is highly corrosive; halogens and HBr/HCl fumes are toxic. Use a fume cupboard, gloves and eye protection.

## Methylbenzene: two different sites

Methylbenzene has two reactive regions: the **ring** (electrophilic substitution) and the **methyl side chain** (an alkane-like group).

| Reagents and conditions | Where it reacts | Product |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Cl2}$ with $\ce{AlCl3}$, room temperature, dark | ring | 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene |
| $\ce{Cl2}$ in ultraviolet light (no carrier), boiling | side chain (free radical) | (chloromethyl)benzene $\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}$, then $\ce{C6H5CHCl2}$, $\ce{C6H5CCl3}$ |
| concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ / $\ce{H2SO4}$, about 30 °C | ring | 2-nitromethylbenzene and 4-nitromethylbenzene |
| hot alkaline $\ce{KMnO4}$, reflux, then acidify with dilute $\ce{H2SO4}$ | side chain oxidised | benzoic acid $\ce{C6H5COOH}$ |

Methylbenzene nitrates at a lower temperature than benzene because the electron-donating methyl group makes the ring more electron-rich, so it reacts faster with electrophiles.

**Side-chain oxidation.** Any alkyl side chain with at least one hydrogen on the carbon attached to the ring is oxidised all the way to $\ce{-COOH}$: ethylbenzene also gives benzoic acid. The purple manganate(VII) is reduced to a brown precipitate of manganese(IV) oxide in alkaline conditions; acidifying the filtrate precipitates white benzoic acid.

$$\ce{C6H5CH3 + 3[O] -> C6H5COOH + H2O}$$

## Directing effects

A substituent already on the ring controls where the next group goes.

| 2,4-directing (activating) | 3-directing (deactivating) |
|---|---|
| $\ce{-NH2}$, $\ce{-OH}$, $\ce{-R}$ (alkyl, e.g. $\ce{-CH3}$) | $\ce{-NO2}$, $\ce{-COOH}$, $\ce{-COR}$ |
| electron-donating: ring more reactive | electron-withdrawing: ring less reactive |

So nitration of methylbenzene gives the 2- and 4-isomers, while nitration of nitrobenzene (harsher conditions) gives 1,3-dinitrobenzene, and nitration of benzoic acid gives 3-nitrobenzoic acid.

:::example{title="Example 1: planning a two-step synthesis"}
Target: 3-nitrobenzoic acid from methylbenzene.

1. If you nitrate first, the $\ce{-CH3}$ group directs to positions 2 and 4: wrong isomers.
2. Oxidise first: methylbenzene with hot alkaline $\ce{KMnO4}$, then dilute acid, gives benzoic acid.
3. Then nitrate: concentrated $\ce{HNO3}$ and $\ce{H2SO4}$ with warming; the $\ce{-COOH}$ group is 3-directing.
4. Product: 3-nitrobenzoic acid. The **order** of steps decides the isomer.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: percentage yield of nitrobenzene]
7.80 g of benzene gave 9.84 g of purified nitrobenzene. Ar: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16.

1. $\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 -> C6H5NO2 + H2O}$: 1 mol benzene gives 1 mol nitrobenzene.
2. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C6H6}) = 78$; n = 7.80 ÷ 78 = 0.100 mol.
3. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C6H5NO2}) = 123$; theoretical mass = 0.100 × 123 = 12.3 g.
4. Percentage yield = 9.84 ÷ 12.3 × 100 = 80.0%. Losses arise from side products (dinitrobenzene), transfer and purification.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ultraviolet light versus halogen carrier"}
UV light (no catalyst) substitutes the side chain of methylbenzene by free radicals; a halogen carrier in the dark substitutes the ring.
:::

:::mistake{title="Acidic or alkaline manganate(VII)?"}
Side-chain oxidation is done with hot alkaline (or acidified) KMnO₄ under reflux; with alkaline KMnO₄ the benzoate salt forms first and must be acidified to give benzoic acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Nitration conditions"}
Both acids must be concentrated, and temperature is controlled (25-60 °C). Dilute acids or high temperatures are wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the methyl group directs to 3"}
Alkyl, –OH and –NH₂ direct to 2 and 4; –NO₂, –COOH and –COR direct to 3.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ring substitutions: Br₂ or Cl₂ with a halogen carrier; nitration with concentrated HNO₃/H₂SO₄ at 25-60 °C; Friedel-Crafts with CH₃Cl or CH₃COCl and AlCl₃.
- Hydrogenation (H₂, Pt or Ni, heat) is an addition that destroys the ring's delocalisation, so it needs harsh conditions.
- Methylbenzene: UV light substitutes the side chain; a halogen carrier substitutes the ring at positions 2 and 4.
- Hot alkaline KMnO₄ then acid oxidises any alkyl side chain to –COOH (benzoic acid).
- –NH₂, –OH and alkyl groups direct to 2 and 4; –NO₂, –COOH and –COR direct to 3, so the order of steps matters in synthesis.
:::
