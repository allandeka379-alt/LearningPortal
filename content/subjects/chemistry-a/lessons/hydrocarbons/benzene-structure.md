## Introduction

Benzene, $\ce{C6H6}$, puzzled chemists for decades. Its formula suggests a highly unsaturated molecule that should decolorise bromine water instantly, yet it does not. In 1865 August Kekulé proposed a ring of six carbon atoms with alternating single and double bonds, which explained the formula but not the chemistry. The modern answer, a ring of delocalised electrons, explains why benzene and its relatives (the arenes, found in many dyes, medicines, pesticides and plastics) behave so differently from alkenes.

You should already know about σ and π bonds, sp² hybridisation, bond enthalpies and Hess's law, and the reactions of alkenes. This lesson sets up the A Level work on the reactions of arenes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Arene (aromatic compound)"}
A compound containing a benzene ring (a planar ring of six carbon atoms with a delocalised π electron system).
:::

:::definition{term="Delocalised electrons"}
Electrons that are not confined to one bond between two atoms but are spread over several atoms; in benzene six π electrons are shared by all six carbon atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Delocalisation (stabilisation) energy"}
The difference between the enthalpy change predicted for a hypothetical structure with localised double bonds and the actual enthalpy change; it measures the extra stability that delocalisation gives.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the Kekulé model and its problems, the delocalised model by orbital overlap, and the stability of the ring. Cambridge (A Level, Paper 4) also asks you to name benzene derivatives such as 3-nitrobenzoic acid and 2,4,6-tribromophenol.
:::

## The Kekulé model and its problems

Kekulé's structure is cyclohexa-1,3,5-triene: three C=C and three C–C bonds alternating around a ring. Three observations contradict it.

| Evidence | Kekulé prediction | Observed |
|---|---|---|
| bond lengths (X-ray diffraction) | three short C=C (0.134 nm) and three long C–C (0.154 nm) | all six bonds equal, 0.139 nm (between single and double) |
| enthalpy of hydrogenation | about three times that of cyclohexene | much less exothermic: benzene is more stable |
| reactions | rapid electrophilic addition, like alkenes; decolorises bromine water | does not decolorise bromine water; undergoes substitution, not addition, under harsher conditions |

## The delocalised model

1. Each carbon atom is **sp² hybridised**. It forms three σ bonds (to two neighbouring carbons and one hydrogen) at **120°**, so the ring is a flat regular hexagon and all twelve atoms lie in one plane.
2. Each carbon has one remaining **p orbital**, perpendicular to the plane of the ring, containing one electron.
3. Each p orbital overlaps **sideways with both neighbours**, not just one, so the six p orbitals form a continuous ring of overlap.
4. The six π electrons are **delocalised** in two ring-shaped regions of electron density, one above and one below the plane of the ring.
5. All C–C bonds are therefore identical, with a bond order between 1 and 2. In formulas the ring is often drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside, rather than with alternating double bonds.

Delocalisation spreads the electron density and lowers the energy of the molecule. Reactions that destroy the delocalised system (such as addition) are therefore unfavourable; benzene prefers substitution, which keeps the ring intact.

## Measuring the extra stability

:::worked-solution[Example 1: delocalisation energy of benzene]
Data: enthalpy change of hydrogenation of cyclohexene, $\ce{C6H10 + H2 -> C6H12}$, $\Delta H = -120\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$; of benzene, $\ce{C6H6 + 3H2 -> C6H12}$, $\Delta H = -208\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.

1. Kekulé's structure has three C=C bonds, each expected to behave like the one in cyclohexene.
2. Predicted $\Delta H$ for hydrogenating "cyclohexatriene" $= 3 \times (-120) = -360\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
3. Actual value for benzene $= -208\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.
4. Difference $= -208 - (-360) = +152\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$: benzene is 152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy (more stable) than the Kekulé structure.
5. This is the delocalisation (stabilisation) energy. Both hydrogenations end at cyclohexane, so the difference must come from the starting structures.
:::

In words, as an energy diagram: cyclohexane is at the bottom; "cyclohexatriene" would sit 360 kJ above it; benzene sits only 208 kJ above it, that is 152 kJ below the hypothetical Kekulé structure.

## Naming aromatic compounds

The $\ce{C6H5}$– group is called **phenyl**. Many simple derivatives are named as substituted benzenes; some have their own parent names.

| Formula | Name |
|---|---|
| $\ce{C6H5CH3}$ | methylbenzene |
| $\ce{C6H5Cl}$ | chlorobenzene |
| $\ce{C6H5NO2}$ | nitrobenzene |
| $\ce{C6H5OH}$ | phenol |
| $\ce{C6H5COOH}$ | benzoic acid |
| $\ce{C6H5NH2}$ | phenylamine |
| $\ce{C6H5CH2CH3}$ | ethylbenzene |
| $\ce{C6H5COCH3}$ | phenylethanone |

For more than one substituent, number the ring carbons 1 to 6 to give the lowest numbers. When the compound has a parent name (phenol, benzoic acid, phenylamine), the carbon carrying that group is carbon 1.

:::example{title="Example 2: naming disubstituted and trisubstituted rings"}
1. A ring with $\ce{COOH}$ on one carbon and $\ce{NO2}$ two carbons away (one carbon between them): the COOH carbon is C1, so the nitro group is on C3: **3-nitrobenzoic acid**.
2. Phenol with Br atoms on both carbons next to the OH carbon and on the carbon opposite it: **2,4,6-tribromophenol** (the white precipitate formed when phenol reacts with bromine water).
3. Two methyl groups on adjacent carbons: **1,2-dimethylbenzene**.
4. A methyl group and a chlorine atom on opposite carbons: chlorine first alphabetically, so **1-chloro-4-methylbenzene** (4-chloromethylbenzene is also used, treating methylbenzene as the parent).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying benzene has three double bonds"}
All six C–C bonds are the same length; the six π electrons are delocalised around the whole ring.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sign errors in the stability calculation"}
The Kekulé prediction is more exothermic (−360) than the real value (−208); benzene is more stable by 152 kJ mol⁻¹. Do not report −568 or −152 as the stabilisation.
:::

:::mistake{title="p orbitals in the plane"}
The p orbitals are perpendicular to the ring; the delocalised electron density lies above and below the plane, not in it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Numbering away from the parent group"}
In phenol, benzoic acid and phenylamine the carbon carrying OH, COOH or NH₂ is always C1.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Papers 2 and 3: *describe* the bonding in benzene (sp², σ framework, p orbital overlap, delocalised π ring above and below the plane, 120°, planar), *explain* its stability, and *calculate* the stabilisation energy from given data.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Evidence questions"}
For "give evidence against the Kekulé structure", give one point from bond lengths, one from thermochemistry and one from reactivity; each earns a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the shape"}
State planar, hexagonal, all bond angles 120°, all C–C bonds equal (0.139 nm). Avoid saying "trigonal planar molecule"; each carbon is trigonal planar, the molecule is a planar hexagon.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Kekulé's alternating structure fails: equal bond lengths, less exothermic hydrogenation, and no ready addition.
- Each carbon is sp²: σ bonds at 120° make a planar hexagon; one p orbital per carbon overlaps sideways all round the ring.
- Six delocalised π electrons lie above and below the ring, making all C–C bonds equal.
- Delocalisation energy = predicted (3 × cyclohexene) − actual hydrogenation enthalpy, about 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
- Name derivatives as substituted benzenes or from parents such as phenol and benzoic acid, numbering the parent group as C1.
:::
