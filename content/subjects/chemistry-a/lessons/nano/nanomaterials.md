## Introduction

A strand of human hair is roughly 80 000 nm wide. A single carbon nanotube is about 1 nm across. Materials built at this tiny scale can be stronger than steel, conduct electricity better than copper, kill bacteria, or absorb ultraviolet light while staying transparent. Sunscreens, antibacterial wound dressings, water filters and the chips in every phone already use nanomaterials.

Nanochemistry asks why the same substance behaves differently when its particles are only a few nanometres across, and how that can be used safely. You should already know giant covalent structures (diamond and graphite), metallic bonding, heterogeneous catalysis and the idea of surface area in reaction rates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nanoscale"}
The range of sizes from about 1 nm to 100 nm (1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m).
:::

:::definition{term="Nanoparticle"}
A particle with at least one dimension between 1 nm and 100 nm; it typically contains from a few hundred to a few million atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Nanoscience and nanotechnology"}
Nanoscience is the study of the properties of materials at the nanoscale; nanotechnology is the design, production and use of structures, devices and systems by controlling matter at that scale.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Nanochemistry is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 includes fullerenes, graphene and carbon nanotubes only as structures in its bonding and lattice topics.
:::

## Why the nanoscale is different

**1. A very large surface area to volume ratio.** For a cube of edge length l, the surface area is 6l² and the volume is l³, so

$$
\frac{\text{surface area}}{\text{volume}} = \frac{6l^2}{l^3} = \frac{6}{l}
$$

The ratio rises as the particles get smaller:

| cube edge | SA : V / m⁻¹ | compared with a 1 cm cube |
|---|---|---|
| 1 cm | 6.00 × 10² | 1 |
| 1 µm | 6.00 × 10⁶ | 10 000 times larger |
| 100 nm | 6.00 × 10⁷ | 100 000 times larger |
| 10 nm | 6.00 × 10⁸ | 1 000 000 times larger |
| 1 nm | 6.00 × 10⁹ | 10 000 000 times larger |

In a 10 nm particle a large fraction of the atoms are at the surface, where they have fewer neighbours and are more reactive. Surface atoms are where reactants adsorb, so nanoparticles make very active catalysts and very effective adsorbents.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: cutting a cube into nanocubes]
A 1.00 cm cube of material is divided into cubes of edge 10.0 nm. Calculate the number of nanocubes and their total surface area.

1. Edge of the big cube = 1.00 × 10⁻² m; edge of a nanocube = 1.00 × 10⁻⁸ m.
2. Number along each edge = 1.00 × 10⁻² ÷ 1.00 × 10⁻⁸ = 1.00 × 10⁶; number of cubes = (1.00 × 10⁶)³ = 1.00 × 10¹⁸.
3. Area of one nanocube = 6 × (1.00 × 10⁻⁸ m)² = 6.00 × 10⁻¹⁶ m².
4. Total area = 1.00 × 10¹⁸ × 6.00 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 600 m², compared with 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m² (6 cm²) for the original cube: a million times more surface from the same mass.
:::

**2. Size-dependent properties.** When particles are only a few nanometres across, properties that are fixed for the bulk material start to depend on size:

- **Colour:** bulk gold is yellow, but gold nanoparticles in water look red or purple, and the colour changes with particle size. Tiny semiconductor crystals (quantum dots) glow with colours set by their size.
- **Melting point:** nanoparticles melt at lower temperatures than the bulk metal, because surface atoms are held less strongly.
- **Reactivity and catalysis:** bulk gold is unreactive, but gold nanoparticles catalyse some oxidations.
- **Optical behaviour:** titanium dioxide and zinc oxide nanoparticles are too small to scatter visible light, so a sunscreen containing them looks clear on the skin while still absorbing ultraviolet radiation.

## Examples of nanomaterials

| Nanomaterial | Structure | Key properties | Example uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| fullerenes (for example C₆₀, buckminsterfullerene) | hollow cage molecules of carbon; C₆₀ has 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons, each carbon bonded to three others | molecular (simple covalent); can hold atoms or molecules inside the cage | drug delivery research, lubricants, electronic materials |
| carbon nanotubes | a graphene sheet rolled into a cylinder about 1 nm wide and up to millimetres long | very high tensile strength, light, conduct electricity and heat along the tube | strengthening composites (sports equipment), electronics, sensors |
| graphene | a single layer of graphite, carbon atoms in hexagons, one atom thick | very strong, almost transparent, excellent conductor (delocalised electrons) | flexible touch screens, sensors, batteries, filters |
| silver nanoparticles | clusters of silver atoms, 1-100 nm | release Ag⁺ ions that kill bacteria | antibacterial wound dressings, coatings, water filters |
| titanium dioxide nanoparticles | TiO₂ crystals, 1-100 nm | absorb UV but transparent to visible light; photocatalytic in UV | sunscreens, self-cleaning glass, breaking down pollutants |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how many atoms in a silver nanoparticle?]
A spherical silver nanoparticle has a diameter of 24.0 nm. The density of silver is 10.5 g cm⁻³. Calculate the number of silver atoms in it. (Ar Ag = 107.9; L = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)

1. Radius = 12.0 nm = 1.20 × 10⁻⁶ cm.
2. Volume = 4/3 π r³ = 4/3 × π × (1.20 × 10⁻⁶ cm)³ = 7.24 × 10⁻¹⁸ cm³.
3. Mass = 7.24 × 10⁻¹⁸ cm³ × 10.5 g cm⁻³ = 7.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ g.
4. Atoms = 7.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ ÷ 107.9 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 4.24 × 10⁵: under half a million atoms, a significant fraction of them at the surface.
:::

## Applications

- **Medicine – drug delivery.** Drugs can be attached to or enclosed in nanoparticles (fullerene cages, polymer or lipid nanoparticles) designed to collect in a tumour or to release the drug slowly, so smaller doses reach the target with fewer side effects. Nanoparticles are also used in diagnostic imaging.
- **Water purification.** Nanofilters and membranes remove very small particles, bacteria and some dissolved ions; silver nanoparticles in filters kill bacteria; nanoscale adsorbents with huge surface areas remove heavy-metal ions and dyes. Such methods could help supply clean water where boreholes are contaminated.
- **Pollution control and remediation.** Iron nanoparticles injected into contaminated ground reduce chlorinated organic pollutants and some metal ions to less harmful forms; TiO₂ photocatalysts break down organic pollutants in sunlight.
- **Catalysis.** Platinum and palladium nanoparticles in catalytic converters and fuel cells give a huge active surface from a small mass of expensive metal.
- **Sunscreens and cosmetics.** TiO₂ and ZnO nanoparticles block UV and are invisible on the skin.
- **Electronics.** Smaller transistors give faster, more powerful chips; graphene and nanotubes are being developed for flexible displays and better batteries.

## Risks and regulation

The properties that make nanoparticles useful also raise questions:

- **Toxicity and entry into cells.** Their tiny size means some nanoparticles can be breathed deep into the lungs, pass through cell membranes and possibly reach other organs. Some long, thin carbon nanotubes have been compared with asbestos fibres in laboratory studies.
- **Unknown long-term effects.** Many products are new, so there has not been time to study effects over decades.
- **Release into the environment.** Silver nanoparticles washed out of fabrics or coatings may harm useful bacteria, for example in sewage treatment, and their fate in soil and water is not fully known.
- **Regulation.** A substance that is safe in bulk may not be safe as nanoparticles, so many scientists argue that nanomaterials should be tested and labelled as new substances, that workers should be protected from dust, and that waste should be controlled.

A balanced view is that nanotechnology offers real benefits in medicine, clean water and energy, and that these should be developed together with careful research and regulation.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nanoparticles are a different substance"}
Silver nanoparticles are still silver. The chemistry is the same; the properties change because of size and the high proportion of surface atoms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the surface area to volume ratio the wrong way round"}
Smaller particles have a larger SA : V ratio (6/l increases as l decreases).
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing one-sided answers on risks"}
A *discuss* question needs benefits and risks, with a conclusion. Avoid claiming nanoparticles are certainly dangerous or certainly safe.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nanoscale: 1-100 nm (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m); nanoscience studies, nanotechnology uses, materials at this scale.
- SA : V = 6/l for a cube, so nanoparticles have a huge surface area and many reactive surface atoms; colour, melting point and reactivity become size-dependent.
- Fullerenes (C₆₀ cages), nanotubes (rolled graphene, strong, conducting), graphene (one atom thick), Ag (antibacterial) and TiO₂ (UV-absorbing, photocatalytic) nanoparticles.
- Uses: drug delivery, water purification, pollution remediation, catalysis, sunscreens, electronics.
- Risks: entry into cells and lungs, unknown long-term effects, environmental release; testing and regulation are needed alongside development.
:::
