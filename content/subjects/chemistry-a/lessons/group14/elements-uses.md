## Introduction

Coal from Hwange is mostly carbon. The window glass in a Bulawayo office is made largely from silica, silicon dioxide. The solar panels now common on rooftops across Zimbabwe contain very pure silicon, and the battery that starts a kombi engine is a lead-acid battery. Tin coats food cans and goes into solder. Five elements, one group: carbon, silicon, germanium, tin and lead make up **Group IV** (Group 14 in the modern numbering).

Group IV shows the clearest change from non-metal to metal of any group, and that change explains its physical properties and its uses. This is a ZIMSEC topic; Cambridge 9701 does not have a Group 14 section. You should already understand giant covalent and metallic structures, delocalised electrons, and electron configurations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Metalloid"}
An element with properties between those of metals and non-metals; in Group IV, silicon and germanium are metalloids (shiny, brittle solids that are semiconductors).
:::

:::definition{term="Semiconductor"}
A solid whose electrical conductivity is between that of a metal and an insulator and increases as the temperature rises, because more electrons gain enough energy to become mobile.
:::

:::definition{term="Allotropes"}
Different structural forms of the same element in the same physical state, for example diamond and graphite (carbon).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC only (Form 5): the Group IV elements, their structures and physical trends, and the uses of the elements and compounds. Cambridge covers diamond, graphite and silicon dioxide only as examples of giant structures in the bonding topic.
:::

## From non-metal to metal

All Group IV atoms have four electrons in their outer shell (ns² np²). Down the group the atoms get larger and the outer electrons are held less strongly, so metallic character increases.

| Element | Character | Structure of the element | Melting point / °C (data, approximate) | Electrical conductivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| carbon (diamond) | non-metal | giant covalent, tetrahedral | about 3550 (sublimes near this) | none |
| carbon (graphite) | non-metal | giant covalent layers | about 3650 (sublimes) | good, along the layers |
| silicon | metalloid | giant covalent, diamond-like | 1410 | semiconductor |
| germanium | metalloid | giant covalent, diamond-like | 937 | semiconductor |
| tin | metal | metallic (white tin) | 232 | good |
| lead | metal | metallic | 327 | good |

### Melting point

The melting point falls steeply down the group.

1. Carbon, silicon and germanium have giant covalent structures; every atom is joined to four others by strong covalent bonds, so melting means breaking many bonds.
2. Down the group the atoms are larger, so the covalent bonds are longer and weaker (C–C is stronger than Si–Si, which is stronger than Ge–Ge). Less energy is needed, and the melting point falls from carbon to germanium.
3. Tin and lead are metallic. Metallic bonding between large ions and delocalised electrons is much weaker than a giant covalent network, so their melting points are far lower. (Lead melts slightly higher than tin; the general trend still holds.)

### Electrical conductivity

- **Diamond**: all four outer electrons of every carbon atom are held in localised C–C bonds, so there are no mobile charge carriers. It is an insulator.
- **Graphite**: each carbon is bonded to three others in flat hexagonal layers; the fourth electron is delocalised over the layer, so graphite conducts along the layers.
- **Silicon and germanium**: the electrons are in covalent bonds, but the bonds are weaker than in diamond, so a few electrons gain enough energy to break free. They are semiconductors, and their conductivity increases with temperature. Germanium conducts slightly better than silicon.
- **Tin and lead**: metallic, with delocalised electrons; good conductors.

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining the properties of diamond and graphite"}
Explain why diamond is used in drill bits but graphite is used as a lubricant and in electrodes.

1. In diamond each carbon atom is covalently bonded to four others in a rigid three-dimensional tetrahedral network, so it is extremely hard: ideal for cutting and drilling rock.
2. In graphite each carbon is bonded to three others in flat layers. The layers are held together only by weak forces, so they slide over each other: graphite is soft and slippery, a good dry lubricant (and it marks paper in pencils).
3. Graphite has one delocalised electron per carbon atom, so it conducts electricity, and it has a very high melting point and is fairly unreactive, so it is used for electrodes, for example in electrolysis.
:::

## Uses of the elements and their compounds

| Substance | Use | Property it depends on |
|---|---|---|
| diamond | cutting tools, drill bits, jewellery | hardest natural substance; sparkles (high refractive index) |
| graphite | pencils, lubricants, electrodes | soft layers slide; conducts; high melting point |
| silicon | microchips, solar cells | semiconductor; can be made extremely pure and "doped" |
| silicon dioxide (silica, sand) | glass, ceramics, cement, abrasives | high melting point; hard; forms a glass when melted with other oxides and cooled |
| tin | coating steel food cans; alloys such as bronze (with copper), solder and pewter | resists corrosion; low melting point in solder |
| lead | lead-acid batteries; shielding from X-rays and radiation | Pb and PbO₂ electrodes; very dense |
| dental amalgam ("tooth filler") | filling cavities in teeth | an alloy of mercury with silver, tin and copper that is soft when mixed, then hardens and resists wear |
| tetrachloromethane, CCl₄ | formerly a solvent (dry cleaning, degreasing) and in fire extinguishers | non-polar solvent; not flammable |

**Tetrachloromethane** is no longer used in these ways. It is toxic (it damages the liver) and it is an ozone-depleting substance, so its production and use are now tightly restricted under international agreements and it has been replaced by safer solvents.

**Lead** is toxic, so its old uses in paints and as a petrol additive have been phased out, and lead batteries must be recycled responsibly. Amalgam fillings contain bound mercury; dentists handle the mercury carefully and many now use other filling materials. These are questions of safe handling and disposal; the syllabus asks you to know the uses and why they depend on the properties.

The lead-acid battery uses lead and lead(IV) oxide in sulfuric acid; when it discharges, both electrodes form lead(II) sulfate:

$$
\ce{Pb(s) + PbO2(s) + 2H2SO4(aq) -> 2PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)}
$$

### Making silicon, and an alloy calculation

Silicon for electronics starts from sand. Silicon dioxide is heated very strongly with carbon (coke) in an electric furnace: $\ce{SiO2 + 2C -> Si + 2CO}$. Carbon is the reducing agent, and the toxic carbon monoxide produced must be removed safely. The impure silicon is then purified to the very high purity needed for chips and solar cells.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: atoms in a bronze]
A bronze contains 88% copper and 12% tin by mass. Calculate the ratio of copper atoms to tin atoms. (Ar: Cu 63.5, Sn 118.7)

1. Take 100 g of bronze: 88 g Cu and 12 g Sn.
2. $n(\ce{Cu}) = 88 \div 63.5 = 1.386$ mol; $n(\ce{Sn}) = 12 \div 118.7 = 0.1011$ mol.
3. Ratio Cu : Sn $= 1.386 \div 0.1011 = 13.7$, so there are about 14 copper atoms for every tin atom. A mass ratio is not the same as an atom ratio when the Ar values differ.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying graphite conducts because it is a metal"}
Graphite is a non-metal. It conducts because each carbon atom has one delocalised electron spread over its layer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Breaking intermolecular forces when diamond melts"}
Diamond and silicon have giant covalent structures, not molecules. Melting or subliming them breaks strong covalent bonds.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating semiconductors like metals"}
A metal's conductivity falls slightly as it is heated; a semiconductor's rises. Silicon and germanium are semiconductors, not metals.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague uses"}
"Lead is used in batteries" is better as "lead and lead(IV) oxide are the electrodes of lead-acid batteries". Always link a use to a property.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Group IV changes from non-metal (C) through metalloids (Si, Ge) to metals (Sn, Pb) as atoms get larger and outer electrons are held less tightly.
- C, Si and Ge are giant covalent; Sn and Pb are metallic. Melting points fall down the group as covalent bonds weaken and then metallic bonding takes over.
- Diamond is an insulator, graphite conducts along its layers, Si and Ge are semiconductors, Sn and Pb are metallic conductors.
- Uses follow properties: diamond for cutting, graphite for electrodes and lubricants, silicon for chips and solar cells, silica for glass and ceramics, tin for plating and alloys, lead for batteries and shielding, amalgam for tooth fillings.
- CCl₄ was a solvent but is now restricted because it is toxic and ozone-depleting.
:::
