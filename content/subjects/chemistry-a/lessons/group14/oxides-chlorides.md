## Introduction

Carbon monoxide from a charcoal brazier in a closed room is a deadly poison, while carbon dioxide simply dissolves in rain to make it slightly acidic. Silicon dioxide is the hard, unreactive sand of the Zambezi's banks. The dark brown lead(IV) oxide in a car battery is a powerful oxidising agent, and tin(II) chloride is used in the laboratory as a reducing agent. All of these are Group IV compounds, and their differences come from two patterns: the change from non-metal to metal down the group, and the growing stability of the +2 oxidation state.

This is a ZIMSEC topic. You should already know the Group IV elements and their structures, the Period 3 oxides and chlorides (especially the hydrolysis of SiCl₄), the shapes of molecules, and how to use standard electrode potentials, E⦵, to predict whether a redox reaction is feasible.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inert pair effect"}
The increasing tendency, down Groups III to V, for the outer s² pair of electrons not to be used in bonding, so that the oxidation state two below the group maximum (+2 in Group IV) becomes more stable.
:::

:::definition{term="Amphoteric oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with both acids and alkalis to form salts.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrolysis"}
The breaking of a bond in a compound by reaction with water.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC only (Form 5). Cambridge 9701 meets the hydrolysis of SiCl₄ only in the Period 3 topic and does not study Group 14 oxides or the +2/+4 stability. ZIMSEC questions give the E⦵ values they need; the values used here are those of the Cambridge data booklet.
:::

## The oxides

| Oxide | Oxidation state | Structure and bonding | Acid-base character | Thermal stability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO | +2 | simple molecular, covalent | neutral | stable; burns to CO₂ |
| CO₂ | +4 | simple molecular, covalent | acidic | very stable |
| SiO₂ | +4 | giant covalent | acidic (weakly) | very stable |
| GeO₂ | +4 | giant structure | amphoteric (mainly acidic) | stable |
| SnO | +2 | giant, ionic character | amphoteric | moderately stable |
| SnO₂ | +4 | giant, ionic character | amphoteric | stable |
| PbO | +2 | giant, mainly ionic | amphoteric (mainly basic) | very stable |
| PbO₂ | +4 | giant | amphoteric | decomposes on heating |

Two trends run through this table.

1. **Acid-base character changes from acidic to amphoteric down the group**, following the change from non-metal to metal. CO₂ and SiO₂ are acidic oxides; the tin and lead oxides are amphoteric. Carbon monoxide is unusual: it is a neutral oxide that reacts with neither acids nor alkalis under ordinary conditions.
2. **For each element the +2 oxide is more basic (more ionic) than the +4 oxide**, because an M²⁺ ion has a lower charge density than a hypothetical M⁴⁺ ion and polarises the oxide ion less. So PbO is more basic than PbO₂.

### Equations for the acid-base reactions

| Oxide | With acid | With alkali |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ | none | $\ce{CO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2CO3 + H2O}$ |
| SiO₂ | none (except HF) | $\ce{SiO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SiO3 + H2O}$ (hot, concentrated) |
| SnO | $\ce{SnO + 2HCl -> SnCl2 + H2O}$ | $\ce{SnO + 2NaOH + H2O -> Na2Sn(OH)4}$ |
| PbO | $\ce{PbO + 2HNO3 -> Pb(NO3)2 + H2O}$ | $\ce{PbO + 2NaOH + H2O -> Na2Pb(OH)4}$ |
| SnO₂ | $\ce{SnO2 + 4HCl -> SnCl4 + 2H2O}$ (concentrated) | $\ce{SnO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SnO3 + H2O}$ (hot, concentrated) |

Nitric acid is used for PbO because lead(II) chloride and lead(II) sulfate are only sparingly soluble and would coat the oxide.

### Thermal stability

The +4 oxides become less stable down the group. CO₂, SiO₂ and SnO₂ do not decompose on heating, but lead(IV) oxide loses oxygen when heated:

$$
\ce{2PbO2(s) -> 2PbO(s) + O2(g)}
$$

## The tetrachlorides

| Tetrachloride | CCl₄ | SiCl₄ | GeCl₄ | SnCl₄ | PbCl₄ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| state at room temperature | colourless liquid | colourless liquid | colourless liquid | colourless liquid | yellow oil, unstable |
| boiling point / °C (data, approximate) | 77 | 58 | 87 | 114 | decomposes above about 50 °C |
| with water | no reaction | hydrolysed violently | hydrolysed | hydrolysed | hydrolysed |

All five are **simple covalent molecules**. Each has four bonding pairs and no lone pair on the central atom, so the molecule is **tetrahedral** (bond angle 109.5°) and, because the four polar M–Cl bonds are arranged symmetrically, it is non-polar overall. Only weak instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces act between the molecules, so the tetrachlorides are volatile liquids. Lead(IV) chloride is unstable and decomposes to lead(II) chloride and chlorine: $\ce{PbCl4 -> PbCl2 + Cl2}$, another sign of the stability of the +2 state.

### Reaction with water

Carbon tetrachloride does not react with water at all, but the others are hydrolysed, for example:

$$
\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{SnCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SnO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}
$$

The explanation is in the mechanism of the first step.

1. A water molecule uses a lone pair on its oxygen atom to attack the δ+ central atom.
2. Silicon (and Ge, Sn, Pb) has empty, low-energy d orbitals in its outer shell that can accept this lone pair, forming a five-coordinate intermediate; an Si–Cl bond then breaks and HCl is lost. The process repeats.
3. Carbon is in Period 2: its outer shell has no d orbitals and can hold only eight electrons, so it cannot accept the lone pair. Its small size also means the four large chlorine atoms crowd around it and shield it from attack.
4. So CCl₄ is kinetically stable towards water, even though hydrolysis would be energetically favourable.

## Relative stability of the +2 and +4 states

Down Group IV the +4 state becomes less stable and the +2 state more stable (the inert pair effect).

| Element | More stable state | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| C | +4 | CO is a reducing agent (it is oxidised to CO₂, as in a blast furnace) |
| Si | +4 | +2 compounds are not important |
| Sn | +4 (just) | Sn²⁺ is a reducing agent: it is easily oxidised to Sn⁴⁺ |
| Pb | +2 | Pb(IV) compounds such as PbO₂ are oxidising agents: they are easily reduced to Pb²⁺ |

E⦵ values show this quantitatively. For $\ce{Sn^4+/Sn^2+}$ E⦵ is only +0.15 V, so Sn⁴⁺ is a very weak oxidising agent and Sn²⁺ readily gives up electrons. For $\ce{Pb^4+/Pb^2+}$ E⦵ is +1.69 V (and +1.47 V for PbO₂/Pb²⁺), so Pb(IV) strongly attracts electrons and is reduced to Pb²⁺.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: lead(IV) oxide and hydrochloric acid]
Use E⦵ values to show that lead(IV) oxide oxidises chloride ions to chlorine, and write the equation. E⦵: PbO₂/Pb²⁺ +1.47 V; Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V.

1. PbO₂ is reduced: $\ce{PbO2 + 4H+ + 2e- -> Pb^2+ + 2H2O}$ (more positive E⦵, so this half-equation goes forward).
2. Chloride is oxidised: $\ce{2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-}$.
3. $E^\ominus_\text{cell} = 1.47 - 1.36 = +0.11$ V, which is positive, so the reaction is feasible.
4. Overall: $\ce{PbO2 + 4H+ + 2Cl- -> Pb^2+ + Cl2 + 2H2O}$, or with concentrated hydrochloric acid, $\ce{PbO2 + 4HCl -> PbCl2 + Cl2 + 2H2O}$. Do this only in a fume cupboard: chlorine is toxic.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: tin(II) as a reducing agent]
Predict whether tin(II) ions reduce iron(III) ions to iron(II) ions. E⦵: Sn⁴⁺/Sn²⁺ +0.15 V; Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ +0.77 V.

1. Fe³⁺ has the more positive E⦵, so it is reduced: $\ce{Fe^3+ + e- -> Fe^2+}$ (× 2).
2. Sn²⁺ is oxidised: $\ce{Sn^2+ -> Sn^4+ + 2e-}$.
3. $E^\ominus_\text{cell} = 0.77 - 0.15 = +0.62$ V: feasible.
4. Overall: $\ce{Sn^2+ + 2Fe^3+ -> Sn^4+ + 2Fe^2+}$. (A positive E⦵cell shows a reaction is feasible; it says nothing about its rate.)
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying CCl₄ does not react because the C–Cl bond is very strong"}
Bond strength is not the main reason. Carbon has no available d orbitals to accept a lone pair from water and is shielded by the chlorine atoms, so there is no low-energy pathway: CCl₄ is kinetically inert.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling CO an acidic oxide"}
Carbon monoxide is a neutral oxide. Carbon dioxide is the acidic oxide of carbon.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the oxidising and reducing agents"}
PbO₂ (lead in +4, unstable) is the oxidising agent; it is reduced to Pb²⁺. Sn²⁺ (tin in +2, less stable than +4) is the reducing agent; it is oxidised to Sn⁴⁺.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the tetrachlorides are polar"}
Each M–Cl bond is polar, but the tetrahedral arrangement makes the dipoles cancel, so the molecules are non-polar.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Oxides change from acidic (CO₂, SiO₂) to amphoteric (SnO, SnO₂, PbO, PbO₂) down the group; CO is neutral; the +2 oxide of each element is more basic than the +4 oxide.
- PbO₂ decomposes on heating to PbO and O₂; the +4 oxides become less thermally stable down the group.
- The tetrachlorides are simple, tetrahedral, non-polar, volatile molecules; PbCl₄ decomposes to PbCl₂ + Cl₂.
- SiCl₄, GeCl₄, SnCl₄ (and PbCl₄) are hydrolysed because the central atom has available d orbitals to accept a lone pair from water; CCl₄ has none and is not hydrolysed.
- The +2 state becomes more stable down the group: Sn²⁺ is a reducing agent (E⦵ Sn⁴⁺/Sn²⁺ +0.15 V) and PbO₂ an oxidising agent (E⦵ +1.47 V).
:::
