## Introduction

Phenol, $\ce{C6H5OH}$, looks like an alcohol on paper: an –OH group attached to carbon. But the carbon belongs to a benzene ring, and that changes everything. Phenol is a weak acid that dissolves in sodium hydroxide, it decolorises bromine water instantly without a catalyst, and it couples with diazonium salts to make brightly coloured azo dyes. Dilute solutions of phenol were among the first antiseptics used in surgery; today phenol is a starting material for plastics, resins, dyes and medicines.

This lesson explains how phenol is made in the laboratory, why it is more acidic than alcohols, and why its ring is so much more reactive than benzene. You should already know the delocalised structure of benzene, electrophilic substitution of arenes and the reactions of alcohols.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Phenol"}
A compound in which an –OH group is bonded directly to a carbon atom of a benzene ring; the simplest is $\ce{C6H5OH}$, also called phenol.
:::

:::definition{term="Phenoxide ion"}
The anion $\ce{C6H5O-}$ formed when phenol loses the proton of its –OH group; its negative charge is partly delocalised into the ring.
:::

:::definition{term="Activating group"}
A substituent that increases the electron density of a benzene ring, making electrophilic substitution faster than in benzene; –OH and –NH₂ are strongly activating and direct to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Order of acid strength"}
ethanol < water < phenol < carboxylic acids. Typical pKa values (data): ethanol about 16, water 15.7, phenol 9.9, ethanoic acid 4.76.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: all of this lesson. For Cambridge, phenol is A Level content (Paper 4); for ZIMSEC it is Form 6 organic chemistry (Papers 1-3).
:::

## Making phenol from phenylamine

Phenol is made in two stages from phenylamine, $\ce{C6H5NH2}$:

| Stage | Reagents and conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|
| 1. diazotisation | $\ce{NaNO2}$ and dilute HCl (nitrous acid made in situ), below 10 °C | benzenediazonium chloride, $\ce{C6H5N2+Cl-}$ |
| 2. hydrolysis | warm the solution (in water) | phenol, nitrogen gas and HCl |

$\ce{C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl -> C6H5N2+Cl- + 2H2O}$

$\ce{C6H5N2+Cl- + H2O -> C6H5OH + N2 + HCl}$

The diazonium salt is unstable: above about 10 °C it decomposes, so stage 1 is done in ice and stage 2 simply lets it warm. Effervescence (nitrogen) is seen in stage 2. Diazonium salts are hazardous and can explode when dry; they are always kept in cold solution.

## Why phenol is acidic

$\ce{C6H5OH(aq) <=> C6H5O^-(aq) + H+(aq)}$

In the phenoxide ion one lone pair on the oxygen overlaps with the π system of the ring. The negative charge is **delocalised** over the oxygen and the ring carbons, so the ion is stabilised and less ready to take back a proton. Phenol therefore dissociates more than water.

In ethoxide, $\ce{C2H5O-}$, the charge stays on the oxygen and the ethyl group pushes extra electron density onto it, so ethoxide is less stable than hydroxide and ethanol is a weaker acid than water. In a carboxylate ion the charge is spread equally over **two** electronegative oxygen atoms, which is more effective than spreading it into carbon atoms, so carboxylic acids are stronger acids than phenol.

| Compound | pKa (data) | Reacts with Na? | Reacts with NaOH(aq)? | Reacts with $\ce{Na2CO3(aq)}$ to give $\ce{CO2}$? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ethanol | about 16 | yes | no | no |
| phenol | 9.9 | yes | yes | no |
| ethanoic acid | 4.76 | yes | yes | yes |

$\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5O^-Na+ + H2O}$ (phenol, only slightly soluble in water, dissolves in the alkali)

$\ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5O^-Na+ + H2}$ (fizzing; done with molten phenol or a solution in a dry solvent)

:::worked-solution[Example 1: pH of a phenol solution]
Calculate the pH of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ phenol. Data: Ka = 1.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³.

1. Phenol is a weak acid, so assume $[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{C6H5O-}]$ and that $[\ce{C6H5OH}]$ stays 0.100 mol dm⁻³.
2. $[\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{K_\text{a} \times c} = \sqrt{1.3 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.100} = 3.61 \times 10^{-6}$ mol dm⁻³.
3. pH = −log(3.61 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.44.
4. Check: 3.61 × 10⁻⁶ is tiny compared with 0.100, so the approximation is valid. The solution is only weakly acidic, which is why phenol does not liberate $\ce{CO2}$ from carbonates.
:::

## Electrophilic substitution: a highly activated ring

One lone pair on the oxygen of –OH is partly delocalised into the ring. This raises the electron density of the ring, especially at the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, so electrophiles are attracted more strongly and the ring does not need a catalyst or harsh conditions.

| Reaction | Phenol | Benzene |
|---|---|---|
| bromination | bromine water, room temperature: 2,4,6-tribromophenol, **white precipitate**, bromine decolorised | liquid bromine with $\ce{FeBr3}$ (or Fe) catalyst; monosubstitution |
| nitration | **dilute** nitric acid at room temperature: mixture of 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol | concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids, 25-60 °C |

$\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr}$

$\ce{C6H5OH + HNO3 -> C6H4(OH)NO2 + H2O}$

:::worked-solution[Example 2: bromine water as a quantitative test]
0.470 g of phenol is shaken with excess bromine water. Calculate the mass of 2,4,6-tribromophenol formed and the mass of bromine used.

1. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C6H5OH}) = 94.0$; n(phenol) = 0.470 ÷ 94.0 = 0.00500 mol.
2. 1 mol phenol gives 1 mol $\ce{C6H2Br3OH}$; $M_\text{r} = 6(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 3(79.9) + 16.0 = 330.7$.
3. Mass of product = 0.00500 × 330.7 = 1.65 g.
4. n($\ce{Br2}$) = 3 × 0.00500 = 0.0150 mol; mass = 0.0150 × 159.8 = 2.40 g.
:::

## Coupling with diazonium salts: azo dyes

When a cold solution of benzenediazonium chloride is added to phenol dissolved in sodium hydroxide, a yellow-orange precipitate forms at once:

$\ce{C6H5N2+ + C6H5OH -> C6H5N=NC6H4OH + H+}$

The diazonium ion is a weak electrophile; it attacks the 4-position of the phenoxide ion, which is very electron-rich (the alkali converts phenol into phenoxide). The product, 4-hydroxyazobenzene, contains the azo group –N=N– linking two rings. Its delocalised system extends over both rings and the azo group, so it absorbs visible light and is coloured. This is the basis of azo dyes, treated fully in the diazonium salts lesson.

## Other phenols: naphthol

The same reactions apply to any phenol. 2-naphthol (naphthalen-2-ol, –OH on a naphthalene ring of two fused benzene rings) dissolves in NaOH(aq), decolorises bromine water, and couples with benzenediazonium chloride in alkali to give an intense orange-red azo dye. Predict products by treating the –OH group exactly as in phenol: acidic hydrogen, ring activated at positions next to and opposite the –OH.

:::example{title="Example 3: predicting the products for a substituted phenol"}
4-methylphenol, $\ce{CH3C6H4OH}$, is shaken with excess bromine water. Predict the product.

1. –OH activates the 2-, 4- and 6-positions relative to itself.
2. The 4-position is already occupied by $\ce{CH3}$.
3. Bromine substitutes at the two free positions next to –OH (2 and 6).
4. Product: 2,6-dibromo-4-methylphenol, $\ce{CH3C6H2Br2OH}$, with 2 mol HBr; a white precipitate forms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Phenol is not an alcohol"}
Phenol is not oxidised to an aldehyde, ketone or acid by acidified dichromate(VI) as alcohols are, and it does not give the iodoform test. Its C–O bond is also strengthened by delocalisation, so the –OH is not replaced by PCl₅ or HBr as easily.
:::

:::mistake{title="Phenol and carbonates"}
Phenol is too weak an acid to release CO₂ from sodium carbonate or hydrogencarbonate. This test distinguishes it from carboxylic acids.
:::

:::mistake{title="Delocalisation in the right species"}
Explain acidity using the stability of the **phenoxide ion** (the conjugate base), not "phenol is delocalised" alone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Conditions for nitration"}
Phenol needs only dilute nitric acid at room temperature. Using the concentrated nitrating mixture gives 2,4,6-trinitrophenol and oxidation products.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Phenol is made from phenylamine: NaNO₂ + HCl below 10 °C gives the diazonium salt, which on warming gives phenol and N₂.
- Phenol is more acidic than water and ethanol because the phenoxide charge is delocalised into the ring; it is weaker than carboxylic acids.
- It reacts with NaOH(aq) and with Na to form sodium phenoxide, but not with carbonates.
- The –OH group activates the ring at 2, 4 and 6: bromine water gives white 2,4,6-tribromophenol; dilute HNO₃ gives 2- and 4-nitrophenol.
- In alkali, phenols couple with diazonium ions at the 4-position to give coloured azo compounds; naphthol behaves the same way.
:::
