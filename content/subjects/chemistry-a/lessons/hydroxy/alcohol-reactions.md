## Introduction

Alcohols sit at the centre of organic synthesis. They can be made from alkenes, halogenoalkanes, carbonyl compounds and esters, and they can be turned into halogenoalkanes, alkenes, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids and esters. Learning the reactions of the –OH group therefore unlocks a large part of every organic paper. The same chemistry is behind the old roadside breathalyser, in which orange dichromate(VI) crystals turned green when a driver's breath contained ethanol.

This lesson lists how alcohols are produced and then works through their reactions, with the reagents, conditions, equations and observations you must learn. You should already know how to name and classify alcohols (primary, secondary, tertiary) and the idea of oxidation as gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oxidation (organic)"}
Addition of oxygen to, or removal of hydrogen from, an organic molecule; in equations the oxidising agent is written as [O].
:::

:::definition{term="Dehydration"}
An elimination reaction in which a water molecule is removed from a compound; alcohols are dehydrated to alkenes.
:::

:::definition{term="Esterification"}
The condensation reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol, forming an ester and water.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Oxidation pattern"}
Primary alcohol → aldehyde (distil off as it forms) → carboxylic acid (heat under reflux). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Tertiary alcohol → no reaction. Acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ turns from **orange to green** when it oxidises an alcohol.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: every reaction in this lesson, including the tri-iodomethane test. Making esters from acyl chlorides is in the acyl chlorides lesson (Cambridge A Level).
:::

## Producing alcohols

| Starting material | Reagent and conditions | Example product |
|---|---|---|
| alkene | steam, $\ce{H3PO4}$ catalyst, about 300 °C, 60-70 atm | ethene → ethanol |
| alkene | cold, dilute, acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ | ethene → ethane-1,2-diol |
| halogenoalkane | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | 1-bromopropane → propan-1-ol |
| aldehyde or ketone | $\ce{NaBH4}$ (aqueous or alcoholic) or $\ce{LiAlH4}$ (dry ether) | propanone → propan-2-ol |
| carboxylic acid | $\ce{LiAlH4}$ in dry ether | ethanoic acid → ethanol |
| ester | dilute acid or NaOH(aq), heat under reflux | ethyl ethanoate → ethanol |

Reductions are written with [H]: $\ce{CH3COCH3 + 2[H] -> CH3CH(OH)CH3}$ and $\ce{CH3COOH + 4[H] -> CH3CH2OH + H2O}$. $\ce{NaBH4}$ is too weak to reduce carboxylic acids.

## Combustion and reaction with sodium

Alcohols burn with a clean, blue flame: $\ce{C2H5OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}$. This is why ethanol is blended into petrol.

Sodium reacts steadily (less violently than with water) to form a sodium alkoxide and hydrogen:

$\ce{2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5O^-Na+ + H2}$

The sodium sinks, fizzes and slowly disappears; evaporating the solution leaves white sodium ethoxide. The slower reaction shows that ethanol is a weaker acid than water.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: hydrogen from an alcohol]
Excess sodium is added to 2.30 g of ethanol. Calculate the volume of hydrogen formed at room conditions. Molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 46.0$; n(ethanol) = 2.30 ÷ 46.0 = 0.0500 mol.
2. From the equation, 2 mol ethanol give 1 mol $\ce{H2}$: n($\ce{H2}$) = 0.0250 mol.
3. V = 0.0250 × 24.0 = 0.600 dm³ = 600 cm³.
4. One mole of $\ce{H2}$ per two –OH groups: a diol gives twice as much hydrogen per mole, which is used to count –OH groups.
:::

## Substitution: making halogenoalkanes

| Reagent | Conditions | Equation (from ethanol) |
|---|---|---|
| HBr (from KBr + concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$) | heat | $\ce{C2H5OH + HBr -> C2H5Br + H2O}$ |
| HCl (concentrated; $\ce{ZnCl2}$ helps for primary alcohols) | heat | $\ce{C2H5OH + HCl -> C2H5Cl + H2O}$ |
| $\ce{PCl5}$ | room temperature; steamy fumes of HCl | $\ce{C2H5OH + PCl5 -> C2H5Cl + POCl3 + HCl}$ |
| $\ce{PCl3}$ | heat | $\ce{3C2H5OH + PCl3 -> 3C2H5Cl + H3PO3}$ |
| $\ce{SOCl2}$ | room temperature | $\ce{C2H5OH + SOCl2 -> C2H5Cl + SO2 + HCl}$ |

The steamy fumes from $\ce{PCl5}$ (HCl gas, which turns moist blue litmus red and gives white smoke with ammonia) are a test for an –OH group, but carboxylic acids give them too.

## Oxidation

The oxidising agent is potassium dichromate(VI) acidified with dilute sulfuric acid (orange $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ is reduced to green $\ce{Cr^3+}$), or acidified potassium manganate(VII) (purple to colourless).

| Alcohol | Apparatus | Product | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary, e.g. ethanol | warm and **distil** immediately, excess alcohol | aldehyde (ethanal, bp 21 °C) | $\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}$ |
| primary, e.g. ethanol | heat under **reflux**, excess oxidant | carboxylic acid | $\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}$ |
| secondary, e.g. propan-2-ol | heat (reflux) | ketone (propanone) | $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH3 + H2O}$ |
| tertiary, e.g. 2-methylpropan-2-ol | heat | no reaction: stays orange | — |

The aldehyde boils lower than the alcohol (it has no O–H, so no hydrogen bonding), so distilling removes it before it is oxidised further. Under reflux the vapour condenses and returns, so the aldehyde stays in contact with the oxidant and becomes the acid. Tertiary alcohols have no hydrogen on the C–OH carbon, so they cannot be oxidised without breaking C–C bonds.

## Dehydration to alkenes

Pass the alcohol vapour over heated aluminium oxide, or heat the alcohol with concentrated sulfuric or phosphoric acid (about 170 °C for ethanol):

$\ce{CH3CH2OH -> CH2=CH2 + H2O}$

An alcohol with the –OH on a non-terminal carbon can lose H from either neighbouring carbon: butan-2-ol gives but-1-ene and cis- and trans-but-2-ene.

## Esterification

Heat the alcohol and a carboxylic acid under reflux with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid (catalyst):

$\ce{CH3COOH + C2H5OH <=> CH3COOC2H5 + H2O}$

The reaction is reversible; the ester (ethyl ethanoate) has a fruity smell. Esters are covered in their own lesson.

## The tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test

Warm the compound with iodine and aqueous sodium hydroxide (alkaline iodine). A **pale yellow precipitate** of tri-iodomethane, $\ce{CHI3}$, with an antiseptic smell, forms from alcohols containing the group $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$: ethanol and all secondary alcohols of the form $\ce{CH3CH(OH)R}$, such as propan-2-ol and butan-2-ol.

The alkaline iodine first oxidises $\ce{CH3CH(OH)R}$ to $\ce{CH3COR}$; the three hydrogens of the methyl group are then replaced by iodine and the C–C bond breaks. The products are $\ce{CHI3}$ and the sodium salt of the carboxylic acid with one carbon fewer, RCOONa:

$\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + 4I2 + 6NaOH -> CHI3 + CH3COONa + 5NaI + 5H2O}$

Methanol, propan-1-ol and pentan-3-ol give no precipitate.

:::example{title="Example 2: identifying an alcohol"}
Alcohols X and Y have the formula $\ce{C4H10O}$. X turns warm acidified dichromate(VI) green and gives a pale yellow precipitate with alkaline iodine. Y does not change the colour of the dichromate(VI). Identify X and Y.

1. X is oxidised, so it is primary or secondary.
2. The yellow precipitate ($\ce{CHI3}$) shows the $\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}$ group.
3. The only $\ce{C4H10O}$ alcohol with this group is butan-2-ol, $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}$. X is butan-2-ol; oxidation gives butanone, and the iodoform reaction gives $\ce{CHI3}$ and sodium propanoate.
4. Y is not oxidised, so it is tertiary: 2-methylpropan-2-ol, $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Distil versus reflux"}
To stop at the aldehyde, distil it off as it forms; to make the carboxylic acid, heat under reflux. Writing "heat" alone loses the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong colour change"}
Dichromate(VI) goes from **orange to green**, not to blue or colourless. Manganate(VII) goes purple to colourless.
:::

:::mistake{title="Which alcohols give iodoform"}
Propan-1-ol does **not** give the test; you need $\ce{CH3}$ next to the C–OH carbon. Ethanol is the only primary alcohol that does.
:::

:::mistake{title="Alkoxide formula"}
Sodium ethoxide is $\ce{C2H5O^-Na+}$ (ionic); the hydrogen lost comes from the O–H, not the carbon chain.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Where it is tested"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (and practical Papers 3 and 4 as functional-group tests). Expect *state the reagents and conditions*, *give the observation* (both colours), *construct an equation* using [O] and [H], and *deduce* the class of an alcohol from test results.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Reaction schemes"}
For each arrow, write reagent **and** condition: "K₂Cr₂O₇ and dilute H₂SO₄, heat under reflux" scores; "oxidise" does not.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Alcohols come from alkenes (steam/H₃PO₄; cold KMnO₄ for diols), halogenoalkanes (NaOH(aq)), reduction of carbonyls and acids, and hydrolysis of esters.
- With sodium they give alkoxides and hydrogen; with HX, PCl₅, PCl₃ or SOCl₂ they give halogenoalkanes.
- Acidified dichromate(VI) (orange → green) oxidises primary alcohols to aldehydes (distil) or acids (reflux) and secondary alcohols to ketones; tertiary alcohols are not oxidised.
- Dehydration (Al₂O₃ or concentrated acid, heat) gives alkenes; carboxylic acids with concentrated H₂SO₄ give esters.
- Alkaline iodine gives a pale yellow CHI₃ precipitate with CH₃CH(OH)– alcohols, plus RCOO⁻Na⁺.
:::
