## Introduction

Ethanol is the alcohol most people know: it is in beer brewed from sorghum and maize, in traditional fruit wines, in hand sanitiser and, in Zimbabwe, in the ethanol made from sugar cane that is blended into petrol. Chemically, ethanol is just one member of a large family. Every alcohol contains the hydroxyl group, –OH, bonded to a saturated (sp³) carbon atom, and that single group changes the physical properties of a hydrocarbon dramatically.

This lesson covers how alcohols are named and classified, why they boil far higher than alkanes of similar size and why the small ones mix with water, how ethanol is manufactured, and why alcohols are such weak acids. The reactions of alcohols have their own lesson. You should already know the naming rules for organic compounds, structural isomerism and hydrogen bonding.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alcohol"}
An organic compound in which a hydroxyl group, –OH, is bonded to a saturated carbon atom; general formula $\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}$ for the open-chain alcohols with one –OH group.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohol"}
An alcohol is primary, secondary or tertiary when the carbon atom carrying the –OH group is bonded to one, two or three other carbon atoms respectively (methanol, with no other carbon, is treated as primary).
:::

:::definition{term="Fermentation"}
The anaerobic conversion of sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide by enzymes in yeast.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Equations to learn"}
Fermentation: $\ce{C6H12O6(aq) -> 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g)}$ (yeast, about 30-40 °C, no air).

Hydration of ethene: $\ce{C2H4(g) + H2O(g) <=> C2H5OH(g)}$ (phosphoric acid catalyst, about 300 °C, 60-70 atm).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: naming, classification, hydrogen bonding and physical properties, both manufacturing methods. The acidity of alcohols compared with water is named by Cambridge (AS); ZIMSEC students meet the same idea when comparing alcohols with phenol and carboxylic acids.
:::

## Naming and classifying alcohols

The suffix **-ol** replaces the final -e of the alkane, and a number shows which carbon carries the –OH. The chain is numbered so that the –OH carbon gets the lowest number.

| Structural formula | Name | Class |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{CH3OH}$ | methanol | primary |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}$ | propan-1-ol | primary |
| $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}$ | propan-2-ol | secondary |
| $\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}$ | butan-1-ol | primary |
| $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}$ | butan-2-ol | secondary |
| $\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2OH}$ | 2-methylpropan-1-ol | primary |
| $\ce{(CH3)3COH}$ | 2-methylpropan-2-ol | tertiary |
| $\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}$ | ethane-1,2-diol | diol (two primary –OH) |
| $\ce{HOCH2CH(OH)CH2OH}$ | propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) | triol |

With two or more –OH groups the full alkane name is kept and a multiplier is added: ethane-1,2-diol is the antifreeze in car radiators, and propane-1,2,3-triol forms the backbone of fats and oils.

The four alcohols of formula $\ce{C4H9OH}$ in the table show **structural isomerism**: chain isomers (butan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-1-ol) and positional isomers (butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol). An ether such as ethoxyethane, $\ce{CH3CH2OCH2CH3}$, is a functional-group isomer of the butanols: it has the same formula $\ce{C4H10O}$ but no O–H bond.

The class matters because it controls the oxidation products: primary alcohols give aldehydes and then carboxylic acids, secondary alcohols give ketones, and tertiary alcohols resist oxidation.

## Physical properties and hydrogen bonding

The O–H bond is strongly polar (oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen) and the oxygen atom has two lone pairs. A δ+ hydrogen on one molecule is attracted to a lone pair on the oxygen of another: a **hydrogen bond**. Alkanes have only instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces.

| Compound | $M_\text{r}$ | Boiling point / °C (data, about) | Main intermolecular force |
|---|---|---|---|
| propane, $\ce{CH3CH2CH3}$ | 44 | −42 | instantaneous dipole-induced dipole |
| methoxymethane, $\ce{CH3OCH3}$ | 46 | −24 | permanent dipole-dipole (no O–H) |
| ethanol, $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$ | 46 | 78 | hydrogen bonding |

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining a boiling-point order"}
Explain why the boiling points of the isomers butan-1-ol (about 118 °C), butan-2-ol (about 100 °C) and 2-methylpropan-2-ol (about 82 °C) decrease in that order.

1. All three have the same $M_\text{r}$ (74) and the same number of electrons, and each forms hydrogen bonds through its O–H group.
2. Butan-1-ol is an unbranched chain: molecules pack closely and have the largest surface contact, so the instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces between the chains are strongest.
3. Branching makes the molecule more compact (nearer spherical). Contact between molecules is smaller, so these forces are weaker.
4. In the tertiary alcohol the –OH is surrounded by three methyl groups, which also hinders hydrogen bonding between molecules.
5. Less energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point falls as branching increases.
:::

**Solubility.** Methanol, ethanol and propanol mix with water in all proportions because their –OH groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, which replace the hydrogen bonds broken in pure water and pure alcohol. As the hydrocarbon chain grows, a larger part of each molecule cannot hydrogen bond, so solubility falls: butan-1-ol is only partly soluble, and hexan-1-ol is almost insoluble. Diols and triols, with more –OH groups per carbon, are very soluble and viscous.

## Manufacturing ethanol

### Fermentation

Yeast is added to a sugar solution, for example sugar-cane juice or molasses, crushed fruit such as marula, or maize and sorghum mash (where the starch is first broken down to sugars). The mixture is kept at about 30-40 °C, in the absence of air, for several days. Enzymes in the yeast catalyse the reaction; too high a temperature denatures them, and air would allow oxidation of the ethanol to ethanoic acid. When the ethanol concentration reaches about 12-15%, the yeast is killed and fermentation stops. The ethanol is separated by **fractional distillation**.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of ethanol from glucose]
A fermentation uses 72.0 g of glucose, $\ce{C6H12O6}$. Calculate the maximum mass of ethanol and the volume of carbon dioxide at room conditions. The student actually obtains 27.6 g of ethanol: calculate the percentage yield. Molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.

1. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C6H12O6}) = 180$; n(glucose) = 72.0 ÷ 180 = 0.400 mol.
2. From the equation, 1 mol glucose gives 2 mol ethanol and 2 mol $\ce{CO2}$: n(ethanol) = n($\ce{CO2}$) = 0.800 mol.
3. $M_\text{r}(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 46.0$; maximum mass = 0.800 × 46.0 = 36.8 g.
4. V($\ce{CO2}$) = 0.800 mol × 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ = 19.2 dm³.
5. Percentage yield = 27.6 ÷ 36.8 × 100 = 75.0%.
:::

### Hydration of ethene

Ethene (from cracking crude-oil fractions) and steam are passed over a phosphoric acid catalyst on a silica support at about 300 °C and 60-70 atm. The reaction is reversible and exothermic, so only about 5% of the ethene reacts in each pass; unreacted ethene is recycled, giving an overall conversion of about 95%.

| Feature | Fermentation | Hydration of ethene |
|---|---|---|
| raw material | sugars from crops: renewable | ethene from crude oil: finite |
| process | batch (slow, days) | continuous (fast) |
| conditions | about 30-40 °C, atmospheric pressure, yeast | about 300 °C, 60-70 atm, $\ce{H3PO4}$ catalyst |
| product | dilute ethanol (about 15%), needs distilling | nearly pure ethanol |
| atom economy | 51.1% (CO₂ is a by-product) | 100% |
| energy cost | low for reaction; distillation uses energy | high temperature and pressure |

Countries with plenty of sugar cane and little oil, such as Zimbabwe, favour fermentation; the carbon dioxide released was absorbed recently by the growing crop. Hydration is preferred where cheap ethene is available and a pure product is needed quickly.

## Alcohols as acids

An alcohol can lose the hydrogen of its –OH group: $\ce{C2H5OH <=> C2H5O- + H+}$. Ethanol is a **weaker** acid than water. The ethyl group donates electron density towards the oxygen (a positive inductive effect), which concentrates the negative charge on the oxygen of the ethoxide ion and makes it less stable than the hydroxide ion; ethoxide therefore holds on to protons more strongly. In practice alcohols are neutral to indicators, do not react with carbonates or with sodium hydroxide solution, but do react with sodium metal, giving hydrogen.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Classifying by the number of hydrogens"}
The class depends on how many **carbon** atoms are attached to the C–OH carbon, not on the total number of carbons in the molecule. 2-methylpropan-1-ol has four carbons but is primary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hydrogen bonds inside the molecule"}
Hydrogen bonds in alcohols are **between** molecules (O–H···O). Breaking them on boiling does not break any covalent bond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Conditions of fermentation"}
"Heat with yeast" is not enough: give the temperature range (about 30-40 °C), anaerobic conditions and the reason (enzymes denature when too hot; air oxidises ethanol).
:::

:::mistake{title="Alcohols are not alkalis"}
The –OH in an alcohol is covalently bonded; alcohols do not release $\ce{OH-}$ ions, so their solutions are neutral.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Alcohols contain –OH on a saturated carbon; primary, secondary or tertiary depends on the number of carbons bonded to the C–OH carbon.
- Hydrogen bonding gives alcohols much higher boiling points than alkanes of similar $M_\text{r}$; branching lowers the boiling point.
- Small alcohols mix with water through hydrogen bonding; solubility falls as the chain lengthens.
- Fermentation (yeast, about 30-40 °C, anaerobic) is renewable but slow and gives dilute ethanol; hydration of ethene (H₃PO₄, about 300 °C, 60-70 atm) is fast and continuous but uses crude oil.
- Alcohols are weaker acids than water because the alkyl group destabilises the alkoxide ion.
:::
