## Introduction

The halogens get weaker as oxidising agents down Group 17. Turn that round and you get the key idea of this lesson: their ions, the **halides**, get stronger as **reducing agents**. Chloride ions are very reluctant to give up an electron, but iodide ions give one up easily. You can see this in a test tube. Add concentrated sulfuric acid to solid sodium chloride and you get only steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride; do the same with sodium iodide and the mixture fills with purple fumes, turns black and smells of rotten eggs.

These reactions, and the silver nitrate test that identifies each halide, are standard in both syllabuses and are often combined in a single question about an unknown salt. You should already know how to assign oxidation numbers, write half-equations, and the trend in oxidising power of the halogens.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reducing agent"}
A species that gives electrons to another species, so that it is itself oxidised (its oxidation number increases).
:::

:::definition{term="Reducing power"}
The tendency of a species to give up electrons. Among the halide ions it increases in the order Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻.
:::

:::definition{term="Halide ion"}
The ion X⁻ formed when a halogen atom gains one electron, for example Cl⁻ (chloride), Br⁻ (bromide), I⁻ (iodide).
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: relative reducing power, the silver ion test followed by dilute and concentrated aqueous ammonia, and the reactions of solid halides with concentrated sulfuric acid with balanced equations and oxidation-number changes. Neither board needs the formula of the silver-ammonia complex. ZIMSEC writes "sulphuric acid".
:::

## Why reducing power increases down the group

When a halide ion acts as a reducing agent it loses an electron: $\ce{2X- -> X2 + 2e-}$.

1. Down the group the halide ion is larger: its outer electrons are in a shell further from the nucleus.
2. Those electrons are also more shielded by inner shells.
3. So the outer electron of I⁻ is held much less strongly than that of Cl⁻ and is lost more easily.
4. Iodide is therefore the strongest reducing agent of the three, and chloride the weakest.

This is the reverse of the oxidising power of the halogens: the stronger the oxidising agent (Cl₂), the weaker the reducing agent it forms (Cl⁻).

## Halide ions with silver ions and aqueous ammonia

The test is carried out on a solution of the halide, acidified with dilute nitric acid (which removes carbonate ions and adds no halide), followed by aqueous silver nitrate:

$$
\ce{Ag+(aq) + X-(aq) -> AgX(s)}
$$

| Halide | Precipitate with Ag⁺(aq) | With dilute NH₃(aq) | With concentrated NH₃(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| chloride, Cl⁻ | white AgCl | dissolves | dissolves |
| bromide, Br⁻ | cream AgBr | partially dissolves (little change) | dissolves |
| iodide, I⁻ | pale yellow AgI | insoluble | insoluble |

The colours can be hard to tell apart, which is why the ammonia step is used. Ammonia dissolves a silver halide by forming a soluble complex with the silver ions, which lowers the concentration of free Ag⁺(aq). Silver chloride is the most soluble of the three and dissolves even in dilute ammonia; silver iodide is so insoluble that even concentrated ammonia cannot lower $[\ce{Ag+}]$ enough to dissolve it. All three precipitates slowly darken in sunlight as silver is formed.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding the purity of sodium bromide]
A 0.500 g sample of impure sodium bromide was dissolved in water, acidified with dilute nitric acid, and excess aqueous silver nitrate was added. The dried precipitate of silver bromide had a mass of 0.846 g. Calculate the percentage by mass of NaBr in the sample. (Mr: AgBr 187.8, NaBr 102.9)

1. $n(\ce{AgBr}) = 0.846 \div 187.8 = 0.004505$ mol.
2. $\ce{Ag+ + Br- -> AgBr}$, so $n(\ce{NaBr}) = 0.004505$ mol.
3. Mass of NaBr $= 0.004505 \times 102.9 = 0.4635$ g.
4. Percentage $= 0.4635 \div 0.500 \times 100 = 92.7\%$ (assuming the impurities contain no halide).
:::

## Solid halides with concentrated sulfuric acid

Concentrated sulfuric acid is a strong acid and also an oxidising agent (sulfur is in its highest oxidation state, +6). Every halide first reacts with it as an acid to form the hydrogen halide, which appears as **steamy fumes** in moist air. What happens next depends on whether the hydrogen halide is a strong enough reducing agent to reduce the sulfuric acid.

### Sodium chloride: acid-base only

$$
\ce{NaCl(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HCl(g)}
$$

Observation: steamy fumes of HCl. Chloride ions are too weak a reducing agent to reduce sulfuric acid, so no redox happens and no oxidation numbers change.

### Sodium bromide: HBr reduces sulfuric acid to SO₂

$$
\ce{NaBr(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HBr(g)}
$$

$$
\ce{2HBr(g) + H2SO4(l) -> Br2(g) + SO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}
$$

Observations: steamy fumes (HBr), orange-brown fumes of bromine, and a colourless, choking gas (SO₂). Bromine changes from −1 to 0; sulfur changes from +6 to +4.

### Sodium iodide: HI reduces sulfuric acid much further

$$
\ce{NaI(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HI(g)}
$$

$$
\ce{2HI + H2SO4 -> I2 + SO2 + 2H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{6HI + H2SO4 -> 3I2 + S + 4H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{8HI + H2SO4 -> 4I2 + H2S + 4H2O}
$$

Observations: a few steamy fumes, purple fumes and a black solid (iodine), a yellow solid (sulfur) and a smell of rotten eggs (hydrogen sulfide, which is toxic). Iodine changes from −1 to 0, while sulfur is reduced from +6 to +4 (SO₂), 0 (S) and −2 (H₂S).

| Halide | Products besides NaHSO₄ | Lowest oxidation number of S reached | Reducing power of the halide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl⁻ | HCl | +6 (no reduction) | weakest |
| Br⁻ | HBr, Br₂, SO₂ | +4 | intermediate |
| I⁻ | HI, I₂, SO₂, S, H₂S | −2 | strongest |

All these reactions must be done in a fume cupboard with eye protection: concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive and HBr, SO₂, bromine and H₂S are toxic.

:::example{title="Example 2: identifying a halide from its reaction with concentrated sulfuric acid"}
A white solid **K** is a potassium halide. With concentrated sulfuric acid it gives steamy fumes, then orange-brown fumes and a choking gas that turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) from orange to green. With silver nitrate solution, its solution gives a cream precipitate that dissolves in concentrated ammonia.

1. Orange-brown fumes are bromine, so the halide has been oxidised to the halogen: **K** contains a halide that can reduce sulfuric acid.
2. The choking gas that reduces dichromate(VI) is SO₂: sulfur was reduced from +6 to +4 only, with no sulfur or H₂S, so the halide is not iodide.
3. The cream precipitate, soluble in concentrated but not dilute ammonia, confirms bromide.
4. **K** is potassium bromide: $\ce{2HBr + H2SO4 -> Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing a redox reaction for sodium chloride"}
Chloride cannot reduce concentrated sulfuric acid. The only reaction is NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using hydrochloric acid before silver nitrate"}
Hydrochloric acid adds chloride ions, so a white precipitate forms whatever is in the sample. Acidify with dilute nitric acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the halide is reduced"}
In the reactions with sulfuric acid, the halide ions are **oxidised** (−1 to 0); they are the reducing agents. Sulfuric acid is reduced.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing iodine as brown here"}
Iodine produced as a vapour is purple, and as a solid it is black (grey-black); brown is its colour in aqueous solution with iodide ions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Reducing power increases Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ because larger ions hold their outer electrons less strongly.
- With acidified Ag⁺(aq): AgCl white (dissolves in dilute NH₃), AgBr cream (dissolves in concentrated NH₃), AgI pale yellow (insoluble in NH₃).
- With concentrated H₂SO₄ every halide gives steamy HX fumes; NaCl stops there.
- HBr reduces H₂SO₄ to SO₂ (S +6 → +4) and forms orange-brown Br₂.
- HI reduces H₂SO₄ to SO₂, S and H₂S (S as low as −2) and forms purple/black I₂.
:::
