## Introduction

Cholera outbreaks have struck Zimbabwe more than once, and every time the public-health message is the same: drink water that has been boiled or treated. Treated tap water is safe because of a little chlorine; the household bleach sold in every supermarket is a solution of sodium chlorate(I) made from chlorine and sodium hydroxide. Chlorine is also the starting point for PVC water pipes, and fluoride in toothpaste protects teeth.

This lesson looks at the chemistry behind those uses. The central idea is **disproportionation**: chlorine (oxidation number 0) is simultaneously oxidised and reduced when it reacts with water or with alkali. You should already know how to assign oxidation numbers, the oxidising power of the halogens, and the idea of a reversible reaction.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Disproportionation"}
A redox reaction in which the same element in one species is simultaneously oxidised and reduced, for example chlorine (0) forming chloride (−1) and chlorate(I) (+1).
:::

:::definition{term="Chlorate(I) ion"}
The ion ClO⁻ (hypochlorite), in which chlorine has oxidation number +1; sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, is the active ingredient of household bleach.
:::

:::definition{term="Chlorate(V) ion"}
The ion ClO₃⁻, in which chlorine has oxidation number +5.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: chlorine with cold and with hot aqueous NaOH as disproportionation, interpreted by oxidation numbers, and chlorine in water purification (HOCl and ClO⁻ kill bacteria). ZIMSEC detail: the industrial importance and environmental significance of the halogens and their compounds (bleaches, PVC, solvents, refrigerants and aerosol propellants, fluoride in toothpaste). CFCs and the ozone layer are covered in the halogen-compounds lesson.
:::

## Chlorine and cold aqueous sodium hydroxide

When chlorine is bubbled into cold, dilute sodium hydroxide (about 15 °C), a mixture of sodium chloride and sodium chlorate(I) forms:

$$
\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

Ionic equation: $\ce{Cl2 + 2OH- -> Cl- + ClO- + H2O}$.

| Species | Cl₂ | Cl⁻ | ClO⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| oxidation number of Cl | 0 | −1 | +1 |

One chlorine atom is reduced (0 to −1) and the other is oxidised (0 to +1): disproportionation. The solution is bleach.

## Chlorine and hot aqueous sodium hydroxide

With hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide (about 70 °C) the chlorate(I) itself disproportionates, and the overall reaction gives chlorate(V):

$$
\ce{3Cl2(aq) + 6NaOH(aq) -> 5NaCl(aq) + NaClO3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}
$$

Ionic equation: $\ce{3Cl2 + 6OH- -> 5Cl- + ClO3- + 3H2O}$.

:::example{title="Example 1: checking the electron balance"}
Show, using oxidation numbers, that the hot-alkali equation is balanced for electrons.

1. Six chlorine atoms start at 0 (in 3Cl₂).
2. Five are reduced to Cl⁻ (−1): they gain 5 × 1 = 5 electrons.
3. One is oxidised to ClO₃⁻ (+5): it loses 5 electrons.
4. Electrons lost = electrons gained = 5, so the equation balances; this is why the ratio of Cl⁻ to ClO₃⁻ is 5 : 1.
:::

The same ratio idea works for the cold reaction: one chlorine atom goes up by 1 and one goes down by 1, so Cl⁻ and ClO⁻ form in a 1 : 1 ratio.

Temperature matters in practice. Bleach is made and stored cool, because warming converts chlorate(I) slowly to chlorate(V) and chloride ($\ce{3ClO- -> 2Cl- + ClO3-}$), and the bleach loses its strength.

## Chlorine in water purification

Chlorine dissolves in water and reacts reversibly. This is also a disproportionation:

$$
\ce{Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HCl(aq) + HOCl(aq)}
$$

Chloric(I) acid, HOCl, is a weak acid that partly ionises:

$$
\ce{HOCl(aq) <=> H+(aq) + ClO-(aq)}
$$

Both **HOCl** and **ClO⁻** are oxidising agents that kill bacteria and other micro-organisms; HOCl, which is uncharged and passes through cell walls more easily, is the more effective of the two. Water-treatment works add a small, controlled dose of chlorine (or a chlorate(I) salt) so that a little "residual" chlorine remains in the pipes to keep the water safe on its way to the tap. Too much chlorine gives an unpleasant taste and smell, and chlorine can react with organic matter in the water to form small amounts of chlorinated compounds, which is why the dose is kept low and monitored.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: dosing a storage tank]
A clinic disinfects a 2000 dm³ water tank using chlorine at a dose of 2.0 mg per dm³. Calculate the mass of chlorine needed and its volume as a gas at room conditions. (Mr Cl₂ = 71.0; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

1. Mass $= 2000 \times 2.0 = 4000$ mg $= 4.0$ g.
2. $n(\ce{Cl2}) = 4.0 \div 71.0 = 0.0563$ mol.
3. $V = 0.0563 \times 24.0 = 1.35$ dm³. (In practice a measured volume of bleach or chlorine tablets is used, because chlorine gas is toxic.)
:::

## Industrial importance and environmental significance (ZIMSEC detail)

| Product | Use | Environmental and safety points |
|---|---|---|
| sodium chlorate(I) bleach | household and industrial bleaching, disinfecting surfaces | releases toxic chlorine if mixed with acidic cleaners: $\ce{ClO- + Cl- + 2H+ -> Cl2 + H2O}$ |
| PVC, poly(chloroethene) | water and drainage pipes, window frames, cable insulation | durable and non-biodegradable; burning it releases hydrogen chloride and other toxic products, so it should not be burned in the open |
| halogenated hydrocarbons as solvents (e.g. dichloromethane, trichloroethane) | degreasing, dry cleaning, paint stripping | toxic vapours; some are persistent; use and disposal are controlled |
| chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) as refrigerants and aerosol propellants | formerly in refrigerators and spray cans | damage the ozone layer; replaced under the Montreal Protocol (see the halogen-compounds lesson) |
| fluoride (e.g. sodium fluoride) in toothpaste | reduces tooth decay | fluoride ions help form harder, more acid-resistant tooth enamel; only small amounts are used |
| chlorine itself | water treatment, making HCl, PVC and many other chemicals | very toxic gas; transported and stored with great care |

The pattern is that halogen compounds are extremely useful because they are reactive (chlorine, bleach) or because they are stable and unreactive (PVC, CFCs, solvents), and the same stability can make them persist in the environment.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the hot and cold products"}
Cold dilute NaOH gives NaCl and NaClO (Cl +1). Hot concentrated NaOH gives NaCl and NaClO₃ (Cl +5), in the ratio 5 : 1.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the reaction 'chlorine is reduced'"}
In disproportionation chlorine is **both** oxidised and reduced. Always give both oxidation-number changes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying chloride ions kill bacteria"}
The killing species are HOCl and ClO⁻, not Cl⁻ or HCl.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting state symbols and reversibility in water"}
Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl is reversible; write the equilibrium arrow.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cold dilute NaOH: Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O (Cl 0 → −1 and +1).
- Hot concentrated NaOH: 3Cl₂ + 6OH⁻ → 5Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻ + 3H₂O (Cl 0 → −1 and +5).
- Both are disproportionation reactions; electrons lost and gained balance.
- In water: Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl and HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻; HOCl and ClO⁻ kill bacteria.
- Halogen compounds are used as bleaches, PVC, solvents, (formerly) refrigerants and propellants, and fluoride toothpaste; toxicity, persistence and ozone depletion are the environmental concerns.
:::
