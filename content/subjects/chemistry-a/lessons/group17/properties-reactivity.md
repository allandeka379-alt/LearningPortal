## Introduction

Chlorine keeps the water in Harare's taps safe to drink, iodine solution is dabbed on cuts in clinics across the country, and bromine compounds have been used to protect stored grain. These three elements, with fluorine above them, are the **halogens**, Group 17 (Group VII). They are all reactive non-metals made of diatomic molecules, yet they look very different: a pale green gas, a dark red liquid and a shiny grey-black solid.

This lesson explains those differences in physical properties and the steady fall in reactivity down the group. The reactions of the halide ions come in the next lesson and the uses of chlorine in the one after. You should already know about instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces, bond energies, and how to use standard electrode potentials.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Halogen"}
An element of Group 17 (Group VII): fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine (and astatine). Each atom has seven outer electrons (ns² np⁵) and the elements exist as diatomic molecules, X₂.
:::

:::definition{term="Volatility"}
How easily a substance turns into a vapour; a more volatile substance has a lower boiling point.
:::

:::definition{term="Bond energy"}
The energy needed to break one mole of a given covalent bond in gaseous molecules, forming gaseous atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxidising agent"}
A species that accepts electrons from another species, so that it is reduced and the other species is oxidised.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: colours and volatility, bond strengths, oxidising power, reactions with hydrogen and the thermal stability of the hydrogen halides. ZIMSEC detail: explaining oxidising power with E⦵ values and the reactions of the halogens with sodium thiosulfate (a suggested activity). Cambridge uses "Group 17"; ZIMSEC uses "Group VII".
:::

## Physical properties

| Halogen | State at room temperature | Colour of element | Colour of vapour | Boiling point / °C (data) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| chlorine, Cl₂ | gas | pale green (greenish-yellow) | pale green | −34 |
| bromine, Br₂ | liquid | dark red-brown | orange-brown | 59 |
| iodine, I₂ | solid | shiny grey-black | purple | 184 |

In solution the colours are useful in tests: in water chlorine is very pale green, bromine orange-yellow and iodine brown (it dissolves only slightly in pure water but readily in potassium iodide solution); in a non-polar solvent such as cyclohexane, bromine is orange-red and iodine is violet (purple).

### Volatility

Volatility **decreases** down the group: chlorine is a gas, bromine a liquid and iodine a solid.

1. Halogen molecules are non-polar, so the only forces between them are instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces.
2. Down the group the molecules have more electrons (Cl₂ 34, Br₂ 70, I₂ 106) and larger, more easily distorted electron clouds.
3. Larger instantaneous dipoles form and induce larger dipoles in neighbouring molecules, so the forces between molecules are stronger.
4. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises and volatility falls.

## Bond strength of the halogen molecules

Data (bond energies from the data booklet):

| Bond | F–F | Cl–Cl | Br–Br | I–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 158 | 242 | 193 | 151 |

From chlorine to iodine the bond energy **decreases**. The atoms get larger, so the bonding pair of electrons is further from both nuclei and more shielded from them; the attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair is weaker and the bond is longer and weaker.

Fluorine does not fit: the F–F bond is weaker than Cl–Cl. Fluorine atoms are so small that, in the F₂ molecule, the lone pairs on the two atoms are very close together and repel each other strongly, weakening the bond. The weak F–F bond is one reason why fluorine is so reactive.

## Oxidising power

A halogen acts as an oxidising agent by gaining electrons: $\ce{X2 + 2e- -> 2X-}$. Oxidising power **decreases** down the group: chlorine is the strongest of the three, iodine the weakest.

The reason is that down the group the atoms are larger, so an incoming electron is added to a shell further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner electrons. It is attracted less strongly, so the halogen has less tendency to gain electrons.

The standard electrode potentials show the same order (ZIMSEC detail): E⦵ for $\ce{X2/X-}$ is most positive for chlorine, less positive for bromine and least positive for iodine. A more positive E⦵ means a stronger oxidising agent.

### Displacement reactions

A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from a solution of its halide ions.

| Added halogen | to Cl⁻(aq) | to Br⁻(aq) | to I⁻(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cl₂(aq) | — | orange-yellow solution: $\ce{Cl2 + 2Br- -> 2Cl- + Br2}$ | brown solution: $\ce{Cl2 + 2I- -> 2Cl- + I2}$ |
| Br₂(aq) | no reaction | — | brown solution: $\ce{Br2 + 2I- -> 2Br- + I2}$ |
| I₂(aq) | no reaction | no reaction | — |

Shaking the mixture with cyclohexane makes the product clear: an orange-red organic layer for bromine, violet for iodine.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: E⦵cell for a displacement reaction]
Calculate E⦵cell for the reaction of chlorine with bromide ions, and say whether it is feasible. E⦵: Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V; Br₂/Br⁻ +1.07 V.

1. Chlorine has the more positive E⦵, so it is reduced: $\ce{Cl2 + 2e- -> 2Cl-}$.
2. Bromide is oxidised: $\ce{2Br- -> Br2 + 2e-}$.
3. $E^\ominus_\text{cell} = 1.36 - 1.07 = +0.29$ V: positive, so the reaction is feasible.
4. Overall: $\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq)}$.
:::

## Reactions with hydrogen

All the halogens react with hydrogen to form hydrogen halides, $\ce{H2 + X2 -> 2HX}$, but the vigour falls sharply down the group.

| Halogen | Conditions and vigour |
|---|---|
| fluorine | explosive, even in the dark and at low temperatures |
| chlorine | explosive in sunlight (ultraviolet light); slow in the dark |
| bromine | needs heating (about 300 °C) and a platinum catalyst; steady reaction |
| iodine | needs heating with a catalyst; slow and reversible, so it never goes to completion |

The trend follows the falling reactivity of the halogens and the falling strength of the H–X bond that forms.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: enthalpy change from bond energies]
Use bond energies to calculate ΔH for $\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}$. Bond energies (kJ mol⁻¹): H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431.

1. Bonds broken: 1 × H–H + 1 × Cl–Cl = 436 + 242 = 678 kJ.
2. Bonds made: 2 × H–Cl = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ.
3. ΔH = 678 − 862 = −184 kJ mol⁻¹ (per mole of the equation as written): strongly exothermic, consistent with the vigorous reaction.
:::

## Thermal stability of the hydrogen halides

Data (H–X bond energies from the data booklet, kJ mol⁻¹): H–F 562, H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299.

The hydrogen halides become **less thermally stable** down the group because the H–X bond gets weaker as the halogen atom gets larger (the bonding pair is further from the halogen nucleus and the orbital overlap is poorer).

- HF and HCl are not decomposed by ordinary heating.
- HBr decomposes slightly when strongly heated, giving a little orange-brown bromine.
- HI decomposes easily: a red-hot wire plunged into hydrogen iodide produces purple fumes of iodine: $\ce{2HI -> H2 + I2}$.

## Halogens and sodium thiosulfate (ZIMSEC detail)

Iodine oxidises thiosulfate ions to tetrathionate ions, and the brown colour disappears. This reaction is used in iodine-thiosulfate titrations:

$$
\ce{I2(aq) + 2S2O3^2-(aq) -> 2I-(aq) + S4O6^2-(aq)}
$$

The average oxidation number of sulfur rises from +2 to +2.5. Chlorine, a stronger oxidising agent, oxidises thiosulfate further, all the way to sulfate (sulfur +6):

$$
\ce{S2O3^2- + 4Cl2 + 5H2O -> 2SO4^2- + 8Cl- + 10H+}
$$

Bromine behaves like chlorine here. The difference is more evidence that oxidising power decreases from chlorine to iodine.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Explaining volatility with bond strength"}
Boiling a halogen separates molecules; the X–X bonds do not break. Volatility depends on the intermolecular forces, which increase with the number of electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying F–F is the strongest halogen bond"}
F–F (158 kJ mol⁻¹) is weaker than Cl–Cl because of repulsion between lone pairs on the small fluorine atoms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the halogen and the halide"}
Chlorine (Cl₂) is the oxidising agent; chloride (Cl⁻) is what it becomes. In a displacement, the halogen is reduced and the halide ion is oxidised.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the colour of iodine wrongly"}
Solid iodine is grey-black; its vapour and its solution in cyclohexane are purple; its aqueous solution (with iodide) is brown.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cl₂ is a pale green gas, Br₂ a red-brown liquid, I₂ a grey-black solid (purple vapour); volatility falls down the group as instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces strengthen with more electrons.
- X–X bond energy falls from Cl₂ to I₂ (larger atoms); F–F is anomalously weak because of lone-pair repulsion.
- Oxidising power falls down the group (E⦵ for X₂/X⁻ becomes less positive); a halogen displaces any halide below it.
- Reactions with hydrogen become less vigorous down the group; HX thermal stability falls as the H–X bond weakens, and HI decomposes on heating.
- Iodine oxidises thiosulfate to tetrathionate; chlorine and bromine oxidise it to sulfate.
:::
