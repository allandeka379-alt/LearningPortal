## Introduction

Open a bottle of fizzy drink in Bulawayo on a hot afternoon and bubbles rush out; leave the cap on and the drink stays fizzy for weeks. Boil hard water from a borehole in a kettle and a white crust of limescale builds up. Both are the same idea at work: a reversible change that has settled into **dynamic equilibrium**, and then responds when the conditions are changed.

Chemical industry depends on this. Ammonia for fertiliser and sulfuric acid are both made in equilibrium reactions, and the conditions chosen in those plants come straight from the principle in this lesson. You should already know how to write balanced equations with state symbols and what exothermic and endothermic mean. The equilibrium constants $K_\text{c}$ and $K_\text{p}$ have their own lesson; here you only need the idea that, at a given temperature, the ratio of product to reactant concentrations at equilibrium is fixed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reversible reaction"}
A reaction in which the products can react to re-form the reactants, so it can go in both directions. It is shown with the symbol $\rightleftharpoons$.
:::

:::definition{term="Dynamic equilibrium"}
The state of a reversible reaction in a closed system in which the forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, so the concentrations of reactants and products stay constant.
:::

:::definition{term="Position of equilibrium"}
The relative amounts of reactants and products present at equilibrium. "The position moves to the right" means that more products and less reactants are present once equilibrium is re-established.
:::

:::definition{term="Le Chatelier's principle"}
If a change is made to the conditions of a system in dynamic equilibrium, the position of equilibrium moves so as to oppose (reduce the effect of) that change.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards need everything in this lesson. Cambridge places it at AS Level (Papers 1 and 2); ZIMSEC teaches it in Form 5 and examines it in Papers 1, 2 and 3. ZIMSEC also suggests household and food examples, such as fizzy drinks and limescale.
:::

## What makes an equilibrium dynamic

Imagine hydrogen and iodine vapour sealed in a flask at 450 °C: $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$. At the start only the forward reaction can happen, and it is fast because the concentrations of $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{I2}$ are high. As hydrogen iodide builds up, the reverse reaction starts and speeds up while the forward reaction slows. Eventually the two rates become equal.

From then on, nothing appears to change: the purple colour of iodine stays the same shade. But at the particle level both reactions are still happening. If a little radioactive iodine were added, it would soon be found in the hydrogen iodide as well as in the iodine. That is why the equilibrium is called **dynamic**.

The features of a dynamic equilibrium are:

1. the forward and reverse reactions happen at the **same rate**;
2. the concentrations of all reactants and products remain **constant** (they are not usually equal);
3. macroscopic properties such as colour, pressure and density stay constant;
4. it can be reached from either direction (starting with reactants or with products);
5. it needs a **closed system**.

### Why a closed system is needed

A closed system allows energy to move in and out but not matter. If calcium carbonate is heated in an open crucible, the carbon dioxide escapes, so the reverse reaction $\ce{CaO + CO2 -> CaCO3}$ cannot happen and decomposition goes to completion. In a sealed container the carbon dioxide stays, and an equilibrium $\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$ is set up. A fizzy drink bottle with its cap on is a closed system; an open glass is not.

## Using Le Chatelier's principle

The principle predicts the **direction** in which the position of equilibrium moves. The table summarises the rules.

| Change made | How the system responds | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Add a reactant (or remove a product) | Position moves right, using up some of the added reactant | adding acid to yellow chromate(VI) turns it orange |
| Add a product (or remove a reactant) | Position moves left | adding water to blue $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ turns it pink |
| Increase total pressure (gases) | Position moves to the side with fewer moles of gas | $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$ moves right |
| Decrease total pressure | Position moves to the side with more moles of gas | opening a fizzy drink releases $\ce{CO2(g)}$ |
| Increase temperature | Position moves in the endothermic direction | $\ce{N2O4 <=> 2NO2}$ ($\Delta H$ positive) moves right |
| Decrease temperature | Position moves in the exothermic direction | ammonia yield rises at lower temperature |
| Add a catalyst | No change in position; equilibrium is reached sooner | iron in the Haber process |

Pressure only matters when gases are involved **and** the number of moles of gas differs on the two sides. For $\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}$ there are 2 mol of gas on each side, so changing the pressure changes the rates but not the position.

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions by the same factor, because it lowers the activation energy of both directions equally. The position and the equilibrium constant are unchanged.

### Concentration and the equilibrium constant

At A Level you should be able to explain the rule, not just apply it. Adding more reactant makes the ratio [products] ÷ [reactants] smaller than the equilibrium constant. The forward reaction then runs faster than the reverse until the ratio is restored to the constant value. Pressure works the same way: squeezing a gas mixture changes the concentration terms by different amounts on the two sides when the numbers of moles of gas differ.

### Temperature is different

Concentration, pressure and catalysts change the position of equilibrium without changing the **value** of the equilibrium constant. Temperature is the only one of these changes that alters the constant itself.

- For an **exothermic** forward reaction, raising the temperature **decreases** K: less product at equilibrium.
- For an **endothermic** forward reaction, raising the temperature **increases** K: more product at equilibrium.

Whatever the direction, a higher temperature always makes equilibrium arrive faster, because both rates increase.

## Colour changes you can see

:::example{title="Example 1: chromate(VI) and dichromate(VI)"}
$\ce{2CrO4^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) <=> Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}$ (chromate(VI) is yellow; dichromate(VI) is orange).

1. **Add dilute sulfuric acid.** $[\ce{H+}]$ increases, so the position moves right to use up the added $\ce{H+}$: the solution turns orange.
2. **Add aqueous sodium hydroxide.** $\ce{OH-}$ removes $\ce{H+}$ as water, so $[\ce{H+}]$ falls; the position moves left to replace it: the solution turns yellow.
3. No oxidation states change: chromium is +6 in both ions. This is an acid-base equilibrium, not a redox one.

Wear eye protection and gloves: chromium(VI) compounds are toxic.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: the cobalt(II) chloride equilibrium and temperature"}
$\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+(aq) + 4Cl-(aq) <=> [CoCl4]^2-(aq) + 6H2O(l)}$; the forward reaction is endothermic. $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ is pink and $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ is blue.

1. **Warm the mixture.** The position moves in the endothermic direction (right) to absorb the added energy: the solution turns blue, and K increases.
2. **Cool it in ice.** The position moves in the exothermic direction (left): the solution turns pink.
3. **Add concentrated hydrochloric acid** (fume cupboard). $[\ce{Cl-}]$ rises, so the position moves right: blue.
4. **Add water.** Water is a product, so the position moves left: pink.
:::

## Household and food examples

- **Fizzy drinks.** Carbon dioxide is dissolved under pressure: $\ce{CO2(g) <=> CO2(aq)}$. Opening the bottle lowers the pressure of gas above the drink, so the position moves left and bubbles form. A warm drink goes flat faster because gases are less soluble at higher temperature.
- **Limescale in kettles.** Hard water contains calcium hydrogencarbonate: $\ce{Ca(HCO3)2(aq) <=> CaCO3(s) + CO2(g) + H2O(l)}$. Boiling drives carbon dioxide out of the open kettle, so the position moves right and insoluble calcium carbonate forms as scale.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equal concentrations"}
At equilibrium the rates are equal; the concentrations are constant but almost never equal to each other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the reactions stop"}
Write "the forward and reverse reactions continue at the same rate". "The reaction has stopped" scores nothing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Catalysts and yield"}
A catalyst does not increase the yield at equilibrium. It only lets the system reach equilibrium sooner.
:::

:::mistake{title="Changing K with concentration or pressure"}
Only temperature changes the value of an equilibrium constant. Concentration and pressure change the position, not K.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting all species for pressure"}
Count only moles of **gas**. Solids, liquids and dissolved species do not count when you predict the effect of pressure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 1 (multiple choice, including multiple-completion items), Paper 2 and Paper 3 Section A; Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 (AS). Questions often start from an unfamiliar equation with a given ΔH, so practise reading the equation rather than remembering examples.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* Le Chatelier's principle: one sentence, including "oppose the change". *Predict* the effect of a change: say which way the position moves and what happens to the yield or colour. *Explain*: give the reason, such as "the forward reaction is exothermic, so lowering the temperature moves the position right" or "there are fewer moles of gas on the right". *Deduce* the sign of ΔH from data on yield at different temperatures.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A dynamic equilibrium has equal forward and reverse rates and constant concentrations, and needs a closed system.
- Le Chatelier's principle: the position of equilibrium moves to oppose a change in conditions.
- Higher pressure favours the side with fewer moles of gas; higher temperature favours the endothermic direction.
- A catalyst speeds up both directions equally: no change in position or yield, but equilibrium is reached sooner.
- Only temperature changes the value of the equilibrium constant.
:::
