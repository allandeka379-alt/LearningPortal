## Introduction

Zimbabwe's Great Dyke holds chromite and ores of nickel, cobalt, copper and the platinum group metals, and much of the country's export earnings come from metals in the middle of the Periodic Table. The same block of elements gives us the iron in steel and in haemoglobin, the vanadium(V) oxide catalyst of the Contact process and the deep blue of copper sulfate solution.

These elements share a set of properties that s-block metals such as calcium do not have. This lesson defines a transition element, works out the electron configurations of the first row, and explains where the four characteristic properties come from. You should already know how to write electron configurations using s, p and d sub-shells, and the meaning of ionisation energy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="d-block element"}
An element whose highest-energy electron, in the ground state, occupies a d sub-shell.
:::

:::definition{term="Transition element"}
A d-block element that forms one or more stable ions with an incomplete (partially filled) d sub-shell.
:::

:::definition{term="Paramagnetism"}
Weak attraction of a substance into a magnetic field, caused by unpaired electrons; transition metal ions with unpaired d electrons are paramagnetic.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the definition, electron configurations of Ti to Cu and their ions, the four characteristic properties and why the radius and first ionisation energy change little across the series. Cambridge: the shapes of the 3dxy and 3dz² orbitals (ZIMSEC students should know them too). ZIMSEC detail: the contrast with calcium (melting point, density, radius, ionisation energy, conductivity) and paramagnetism.
:::

## Which d-block elements are transition elements?

Scandium to zinc fill the 3d sub-shell. Two of them fail the definition:

- **Scandium** forms only Sc³⁺, [Ar]: its d sub-shell is empty.
- **Zinc** forms only Zn²⁺, [Ar] 3d¹⁰: its d sub-shell is full.

So the first-row transition elements are titanium to copper. Copper qualifies because Cu²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁹, even though the copper atom has a full 3d sub-shell.

## Electron configurations of the atoms and ions

The 4s sub-shell fills before 3d, but once 3d contains electrons the 4s electrons are the outermost and highest in energy, so **ions lose 4s electrons first**.

| Element | Atom | M²⁺ | M³⁺ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ti | [Ar] 3d² 4s² | [Ar] 3d² | [Ar] 3d¹ |
| V | [Ar] 3d³ 4s² | [Ar] 3d³ | [Ar] 3d² |
| Cr | [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ | [Ar] 3d⁴ | [Ar] 3d³ |
| Mn | [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² | [Ar] 3d⁵ | [Ar] 3d⁴ |
| Fe | [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² | [Ar] 3d⁶ | [Ar] 3d⁵ |
| Co | [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s² | [Ar] 3d⁷ | [Ar] 3d⁶ |
| Ni | [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s² | [Ar] 3d⁸ | — |
| Cu | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ | [Ar] 3d⁹ | — |

Chromium and copper are the two exceptions among the atoms: a half-filled (3d⁵) or full (3d¹⁰) d sub-shell with one 4s electron is lower in energy than the expected arrangement.

:::example{title="Example 1: the iron ions"}
Write the configurations of Fe, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺, and use them to suggest why iron(III) is more stable than iron(II) towards oxidation in many conditions.

1. Fe has 26 electrons: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
2. Fe²⁺ loses the two 4s electrons: [Ar] 3d⁶.
3. Fe³⁺ loses one 3d electron as well: [Ar] 3d⁵.
4. A half-filled 3d⁵ sub-shell, with one electron in each d orbital, has no paired electrons in the same orbital and so less repulsion. Removing the paired sixth d electron from Fe²⁺ is relatively easy, which is one reason iron(II) is readily oxidised to iron(III). (Whether oxidation actually happens depends on the conditions and the E⦵ values, met in the iron lesson.)
:::

## Shapes of the d orbitals

There are five 3d orbitals of equal energy in an isolated atom (they are **degenerate**). Two shapes are examinable:

- **3dxy**: four lobes in the xy plane, pointing **between** the x and y axes (a four-leaf clover shape).
- **3dz²**: two lobes pointing **along** the z axis, with a ring (doughnut) of electron density around the middle in the xy plane.

The other three (3dxz, 3dyz and 3dx²−y²) have four lobes like 3dxy; 3dx²−y² points along the x and y axes. How these orbitals point relative to ligands explains colour (see the lesson on colour).

## The four characteristic properties

| Property | Example | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| variable oxidation states | Mn: +2, +4, +6, +7 | 3d and 4s electrons are close in energy, so different numbers can be lost or shared without a huge jump in ionisation energy |
| catalytic activity | Fe in the Haber process; V₂O₅ in the Contact process | they change oxidation state easily, and their surfaces can adsorb reactants using d orbitals |
| complex formation | [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ | small, highly charged ions with vacant d orbitals of suitable energy accept lone pairs from ligands |
| coloured compounds | [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is blue | a partially filled d sub-shell is split by ligands, and electrons absorb visible light as they move between the split levels |

The highest oxidation state rises from Ti (+4) to Mn (+7), the number of 3d and 4s electrons, and then falls, because the increasing nuclear charge holds the later 3d electrons too strongly to lose them all.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: ionisation energies of calcium and iron]
Data (kJ mol⁻¹): Ca 590, 1150, 4940; Fe 762, 1560, 2960 (first, second and third ionisation energies). Explain why iron forms Fe³⁺ compounds but calcium never forms Ca³⁺.

1. Calcium: IE₃ − IE₂ = 4940 − 1150 = 3790 kJ mol⁻¹. The third electron comes from the 3p sub-shell of the argon core, much closer to the nucleus and less shielded.
2. Iron: IE₃ − IE₂ = 2960 − 1560 = 1400 kJ mol⁻¹. The third electron comes from 3d, almost the same energy level as the 4s electrons already removed.
3. The sum of the first three ionisation energies is 6680 kJ mol⁻¹ for Ca but only 5282 kJ mol⁻¹ for Fe.
4. For iron, the extra energy is recovered by the larger lattice or hydration energy of the 3+ ion, so iron(III) compounds are stable. For calcium the huge third ionisation energy can never be repaid, so only Ca²⁺ exists.
:::

## Trends across the series

Across titanium to copper the atomic radius, the ionic radius of the M²⁺ ions and the first ionisation energy change **very little**. Each step adds one proton to the nucleus, but the extra electron goes into the inner 3d sub-shell, where it shields the outer 4s electrons fairly well. The increase in nuclear charge is almost cancelled by the extra shielding, so the attraction for the 4s electrons hardly changes. Compare Period 3, where each extra electron enters the same outer shell and the radius falls steadily.

## Comparison with calcium (ZIMSEC detail)

| Property | Calcium | Iron (typical transition metal) |
|---|---|---|
| melting point / °C (textbook data) | 842 | 1538 |
| density / g cm⁻³ (textbook data) | 1.55 | 7.87 |
| metallic radius / nm (textbook data) | 0.197 | 0.126 |
| first ionisation energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 590 | 762 |
| oxidation states in compounds | +2 only | +2, +3 (and others) |
| colour of compounds | white | coloured |

Transition metals have **higher melting points** because both 3d and 4s electrons can be delocalised, giving stronger metallic bonding. They are **denser** because their atoms are heavier and smaller, so more mass is packed into each unit volume. Their atoms are **smaller** and their first ionisation energies **higher** because the nuclear charge is larger and the 3d electrons shield poorly. Both kinds of metal **conduct electricity** well through delocalised electrons; copper is a far better conductor than calcium, but this is not a clean contrast across the whole series (iron and titanium conduct less well than calcium).

**Paramagnetism.** Ions with unpaired d electrons are attracted into a magnetic field. Fe³⁺ (3d⁵, five unpaired electrons) and Mn²⁺ are strongly paramagnetic; Zn²⁺ and Sc³⁺, with no unpaired electrons, are not. Iron, cobalt and nickel metals are also **ferromagnetic**: they can be permanently magnetised.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Removing 3d electrons before 4s"}
Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². In ions of transition elements the 4s electrons are always lost first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling zinc a transition element because it is in the d block"}
Zinc is a d-block element but not a transition element: Zn²⁺ has a full 3d¹⁰ sub-shell, and zinc forms no other ion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'the d orbitals are empty' to explain complex formation"}
Copper(II) has nine d electrons. What matters is that there are vacant orbitals of suitable energy (3d, 4s, 4p) to accept lone pairs.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A transition element forms at least one stable ion with an incomplete d sub-shell; Sc (3d⁰) and Zn (3d¹⁰) are excluded.
- Atoms fill 4s before 3d, but ions lose 4s electrons first; Cr and Cu atoms are 3d⁵ 4s¹ and 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
- Four properties: variable oxidation states, catalysis, complex formation and coloured compounds, all linked to 3d and 4s electrons of similar energy and partly filled d orbitals.
- Radius and first ionisation energy change little across Ti-Cu because extra 3d electrons shield the 4s electrons.
- Compared with calcium: higher melting point and density, smaller atoms, higher ionisation energy, several oxidation states, coloured and often paramagnetic compounds.
:::
