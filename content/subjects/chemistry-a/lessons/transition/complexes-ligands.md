## Introduction

Drop a crystal of copper(II) sulfate into water and it dissolves to give a pale blue solution. Add ammonia solution and the colour becomes a deep, inky blue. Add concentrated hydrochloric acid to another sample and it turns yellow-green. Nothing has been oxidised or reduced: the copper(II) ion has simply swapped the molecules or ions bonded to it.

These bonded groups are **ligands**, and the unit they make with the metal ion is a **complex**. Complexes explain the colours of transition metal solutions, many tests in qualitative analysis, the way haemoglobin carries oxygen and how EDTA removes toxic metal ions from the body. You should already know dative (coordinate) bonding, lone pairs and the shapes predicted by electron-pair repulsion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ligand"}
A molecule or ion with at least one lone pair of electrons that forms a dative (coordinate) bond to a central metal atom or ion.
:::

:::definition{term="Complex"}
A central metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands joined to it by dative bonds; a complex ion carries an overall charge.
:::

:::definition{term="Coordination number"}
The number of dative bonds from ligands to the central metal atom or ion (not the number of ligands).
:::

:::definition{term="Monodentate, bidentate and polydentate ligands"}
Ligands that form one, two or several dative bonds to the same central ion. Bidentate and polydentate ligands are also called chelating ligands.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: definitions, the four shapes with bond angles, deducing formulae and charges, and ligand exchange in copper(II) and cobalt(II) complexes (Cambridge A Level, Paper 4; ZIMSEC Form 6). ZIMSEC calls the bond "dative"; Cambridge says "coordinate (dative covalent)".
:::

## Ligands

| Ligand | Formula | Donor atoms | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| water | H₂O | O | monodentate |
| ammonia | NH₃ | N | monodentate |
| chloride | Cl⁻ | Cl | monodentate |
| cyanide | CN⁻ | C | monodentate |
| hydroxide | OH⁻ | O | monodentate |
| 1,2-diaminoethane ("en") | H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂ | two N | bidentate |
| ethanedioate | C₂O₄²⁻ | two O | bidentate |
| EDTA⁴⁻ | (four carboxylate groups and two amine groups) | four O and two N | hexadentate (polydentate) |

1,2-Diaminoethane has a lone pair on each nitrogen, and the two nitrogen atoms are far enough apart to bond to the same metal ion, making a five-membered ring. One EDTA⁴⁻ ion wraps around a metal ion and occupies all six positions of an octahedron.

## Why transition metal ions form complexes

Transition metal ions are small and carry a high charge, so they strongly attract lone pairs. They also have vacant orbitals of suitable energy (3d, 4s, 4p) to accept the lone pairs. s-block ions such as Na⁺ form only weak, short-lived associations with water.

## Shapes and bond angles

| Coordination number | Shape | Bond angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180° | $\ce{[Ag(NH3)2]+}$, $\ce{[CuCl2]-}$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$, $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ |
| 4 | square planar | 90° | $\ce{[Pt(NH3)2Cl2]}$ (cisplatin), $\ce{[Ni(CN)4]^2-}$ |
| 6 | octahedral | 90° | $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$, $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$, $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}$ |

Large ligands such as Cl⁻ usually give four-coordinate complexes, because only four fit around the metal ion. Small ligands such as H₂O and NH₃ usually give six-coordinate complexes.

## Formula and charge of a complex

The charge on a complex is the oxidation state of the metal plus the sum of the charges on the ligands. Neutral ligands (H₂O, NH₃, en) add nothing.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: deducing the charges of complexes]
Deduce the formula, with its charge, of each complex.

1. Iron(III) with six cyanide ions: $+3 + 6 \times (-1) = -3$, so $\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}$.
2. Cobalt(III) with three 1,2-diaminoethane molecules: $+3 + 3 \times 0 = +3$, so $\ce{[Co(en)3]^3+}$; coordination number $3 \times 2 = 6$.
3. Copper(II) with one EDTA⁴⁻ ion: $+2 + (-4) = -2$, so $\ce{[Cu(EDTA)]^2-}$; coordination number 6.
4. Platinum(II) with two ammonia molecules and two chloride ions: $+2 + 2 \times 0 + 2 \times (-1) = 0$, so the neutral complex $\ce{[Pt(NH3)2Cl2]}$; coordination number 4.
:::

## Ligand exchange

In **ligand exchange** (ligand substitution) one ligand replaces another around the central ion. Each step is an equilibrium, and the colour usually changes because the new ligands split the d orbitals differently (see the colour lesson).

### Copper(II)

| Reagent added to [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) (pale blue) | What forms | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| a little NaOH(aq) or NH₃(aq) | $\ce{[Cu(OH)2(H2O)4]}$ | pale blue precipitate |
| excess NH₃(aq) | $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ | precipitate dissolves; deep blue solution |
| concentrated HCl | $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ | yellow-green, then yellow with a large excess |

$$
\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+ + 4NH3 <=> [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+ + 4H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+ + 4Cl- <=> [CuCl4]^2- + 6H2O}
$$

Only four of the six water molecules are replaced by ammonia in aqueous solution: this is **partial substitution**. The chloride exchange also changes the coordination number from 6 to 4 and the shape from octahedral to tetrahedral; the charge changes from 2+ to 2−. Adding water reverses it.

The pale blue precipitate is formed first because ammonia solution contains hydroxide ions: $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+ + 2OH- -> [Cu(OH)2(H2O)4] + 2H2O}$. In excess ammonia, ligand exchange removes Cu²⁺ from the solid and it dissolves.

### Cobalt(II)

| Reagent added to [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺(aq) (pink) | What forms | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH(aq) | $\ce{[Co(OH)2(H2O)4]}$ | blue precipitate, which slowly darkens in air |
| a little NH₃(aq) | $\ce{[Co(OH)2(H2O)4]}$ | blue precipitate |
| excess NH₃(aq) | $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$ | precipitate dissolves; straw-coloured (pale yellow-brown) solution, darkening in air |
| concentrated HCl | $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ | blue solution |

$$
\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+ + 6NH3 <=> [Co(NH3)6]^2+ + 6H2O}
$$

$$
\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+ + 4Cl- <=> [CoCl4]^2- + 6H2O}
$$

Unlike copper(II), cobalt(II) exchanges all six water molecules for ammonia. The hexaamminecobalt(II) ion is slowly oxidised by air to the cobalt(III) complex, which is why the solution darkens.

:::example{title="Example 2: interpreting a sequence of observations"}
Rudo adds aqueous ammonia drop by drop to a pale blue solution until it is in excess, then adds concentrated hydrochloric acid to a second sample of the original solution. Explain her observations.

1. First drops of ammonia: a pale blue precipitate. Ammonia solution provides OH⁻ ions, and copper(II) hydroxide, $\ce{[Cu(OH)2(H2O)4]}$, precipitates.
2. Excess ammonia: the precipitate dissolves to give a deep blue solution. Four NH₃ molecules replace water ligands, forming $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$, which is soluble.
3. Concentrated HCl: the pale blue solution turns yellow-green. Chloride ions replace water, forming tetrahedral $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ (yellow); the green tint is a mixture of yellow $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ and blue $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$.
4. The solution contained Cu²⁺ ions, for example from copper(II) sulfate.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Coordination number equals the number of ligands"}
In $\ce{[Co(en)3]^3+}$ there are three ligands but six dative bonds. Count bonds, not ligands.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing [Cu(NH3)6]2+ for the deep blue ion"}
In aqueous ammonia only four water ligands are replaced: the ion is $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$. Cobalt(II), by contrast, forms $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the charge change with chloride"}
Replacing six neutral water molecules by four Cl⁻ ions changes the charge from 2+ to 2−: $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$, not $\ce{[CuCl4]^2+}$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A ligand donates a lone pair to a central metal ion through a dative bond; monodentate (H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻), bidentate (en, C₂O₄²⁻) and hexadentate (EDTA⁴⁻) ligands form one, two or six bonds.
- Coordination number counts dative bonds: 2 linear (180°), 4 tetrahedral (109.5°) or square planar (90°), 6 octahedral (90°).
- Charge of a complex = oxidation state of the metal + charges of the ligands.
- Copper(II): pale blue $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$ → deep blue $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ with excess NH₃; yellow $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ with concentrated HCl.
- Cobalt(II): pink $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ → straw $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$ with excess NH₃; blue $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ with concentrated HCl.
:::
