## Introduction

The Great Dyke, a band of ancient igneous rock running roughly north-north-east through the middle of Zimbabwe, contains one of the world's important deposits of the **platinum group metals** (PGMs). These rare, dense, unreactive metals clean the exhaust gas of cars, make laboratory electrodes and jewellery, and are found in many electronic devices. Platinum also forms the anticancer drug cisplatin.

This lesson looks at what the PGMs are, how they are extracted from ore that contains only tiny amounts of them, what they are used for and why, and why the question of processing them inside the country is an economic issue. You should already know transition element properties, heterogeneous catalysis, redox and the standard hydrogen electrode.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Platinum group metals (PGMs)"}
The six transition metals platinum, palladium, rhodium, ruthenium, iridium and osmium, which occur together in nature and have similar chemical properties: high density, high melting point, resistance to corrosion and catalytic activity.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiation (value addition)"}
Processing a mineral further within the producing country (for example refining matte to pure metals, or making products from them) so that more of the final value is earned locally rather than by exporting raw ore or concentrate.
:::

:::definition{term="Heterogeneous catalyst"}
A catalyst in a different phase from the reactants, usually a solid with gaseous or liquid reactants, which works by adsorbing reactants on its surface.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 meets platinum only in catalytic converters, the standard hydrogen electrode and cisplatin, which appear in other lessons.
:::

## The metals and their companions

| Metal | Symbol | A typical use |
|---|---|---|
| platinum | Pt | catalytic converters, jewellery, electrodes, cisplatin |
| palladium | Pd | catalytic converters, electronic components |
| rhodium | Rh | catalytic converters (reduction of nitrogen oxides) |
| ruthenium | Ru | electronics (hard-disk layers, contacts) |
| iridium | Ir | spark-plug tips, crucibles for high temperatures |
| osmium | Os | very hard alloys (pen nibs, instrument pivots) |

PGMs occur with **base metals** in the same ores: **nickel, copper and cobalt** (as sulfide minerals) and **chromium** (in chromite seams in the same layered rocks). The base metals are recovered too and add to the value of the ore.

## Occurrence

PGMs are found in **layered igneous intrusions**: bodies of magma that cooled slowly underground, allowing different minerals to settle into distinct layers. One layer, rich in sulfide minerals of nickel and copper, carries most of the PGMs. Zimbabwe's Great Dyke is such an intrusion, and its PGM-bearing layer is mined at several places along its length. The PGM content of these ores is very low, a few grams in every tonne of rock, so enormous masses of rock must be handled.

## Extraction in outline

| Stage | What happens | Why |
|---|---|---|
| mining | ore is blasted and brought to the surface | the PGM layer is underground or close to the surface |
| crushing and milling | rock is ground to a fine powder | frees the tiny mineral grains |
| concentration | froth flotation separates the sulfide minerals (carrying the PGMs) from the waste rock | reduces the mass to be smelted many times over |
| smelting | concentrate melted in electric furnaces at high temperature; silicates form a slag and a dense **matte** of nickel, copper and iron sulfides collects the PGMs | the PGMs dissolve in the molten sulfide |
| converting | air blown through the matte oxidises iron (to slag) and some sulfur (to SO₂, which is captured) | gives a richer nickel-copper matte |
| base-metal refining | nickel, copper and cobalt are dissolved out (leached) and recovered | leaves a PGM-rich concentrate |
| precious-metal refining | the concentrate is dissolved in strong oxidising acid mixtures, and the individual metals are separated by selective precipitation and solvent extraction | each metal is obtained pure |

For platinum, one classic step precipitates ammonium hexachloroplatinate(IV), (NH₄)₂PtCl₆, which is then heated strongly to leave platinum metal.

**Why it is energy-intensive.** Because the ore is so low-grade, every kilogram of PGMs requires the mining, crushing and milling of hundreds of tonnes of hard rock; milling uses large amounts of electricity. Smelting needs very high temperatures in electric furnaces, and the refining involves many heating, dissolving and separation steps. Much of this energy in southern Africa comes from coal-fired power, so the industry is linked to carbon dioxide emissions as well as sulfur dioxide, which must be captured.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how much ore for a kilogram of PGMs?]
In an exercise, an ore contains 3.50 g of PGMs per tonne and the whole process recovers 80.0% of them. Calculate the mass of ore needed to produce 1.00 kg of PGMs.

1. Mass recovered per tonne = 3.50 g × 0.800 = 2.80 g.
2. Ore needed = 1000 g ÷ 2.80 g t⁻¹ = 357 tonnes (3 s.f.).
3. Conclusion: hundreds of tonnes of rock are mined and milled for each kilogram, which explains the energy cost.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: platinum from ammonium hexachloroplatinate(IV)]
Calculate the mass of platinum left when 2.00 g of (NH₄)₂PtCl₆ is heated to constant mass. (Ar: H 1.0, N 14.0, Cl 35.5, Pt 195.1)

1. Mr = 2(14.0 + 4.0) + 195.1 + 6(35.5) = 444.1.
2. Each formula unit contains one platinum atom.
3. Mass of Pt = 2.00 g × 195.1 ÷ 444.1 = 0.879 g (3 s.f.).
:::

## Uses and the properties behind them

**Catalytic converters.** A ceramic honeycomb coated with a thin layer of platinum, palladium and rhodium removes the main pollutants from petrol-engine exhaust. Gases adsorb on the metal surface, bonds weaken, reactions occur and the products desorb:

$$
\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}
$$

$$
\ce{2CO + O2 -> 2CO2}
$$

$$
\ce{2C8H18 + 25O2 -> 16CO2 + 18H2O}
$$

Rhodium is especially good at reducing nitrogen oxides; platinum and palladium oxidise carbon monoxide and unburnt hydrocarbons. A large surface area on a thin coating makes the most of a very expensive metal. Lead compounds in fuel poison the catalyst by adsorbing strongly on the active sites.

**Jewellery.** Platinum is lustrous, dense, very unreactive (it does not tarnish) and does not cause skin reactions in most people.

**Electronics.** Palladium, ruthenium and platinum are used in small amounts in capacitors, contacts and data-storage layers because they conduct well and resist corrosion.

**The platinum electrode.** In the standard hydrogen electrode, hydrogen gas at 1 atm bubbles over **platinised platinum** in 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq). Platinum is inert (it does not react or dissolve), conducts electrons, and catalyses the equilibrium 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂. Platinum electrodes are also used for redox couples between two ions, such as Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺.

**Anticancer drugs.** Cisplatin, cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂], is a square planar platinum(II) complex. In the cell, chloride ligands are replaced and the platinum binds to two nitrogen atoms on neighbouring guanine bases of DNA, stopping the DNA from being copied, so cancer cells cannot divide. It has serious side effects because it also affects healthy cells.

## Value addition: an economic issue

Countries that mine PGMs can export ore concentrate or matte, or refine the metals themselves. Arguments are made on both sides, and a balanced answer weighs them:

| Possible benefits of refining locally | Possible difficulties |
|---|---|
| more of the final value earned in the country; tax revenue | refineries are very expensive to build |
| skilled jobs and training; related industries (chemicals, jewellery, catalysts) | need a large, reliable supply of electricity |
| by-products (nickel, copper, cobalt, sulfuric acid) recovered locally | need specialist skills and technology |
| less dependence on world prices for unrefined products | environmental controls for SO₂, acids and waste must be strict |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing nickel or chromium as platinum group metals"}
They occur with the PGMs and are valuable by-products, but the PGMs are only Pt, Pd, Rh, Ru, Ir and Os.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the catalytic converter removes carbon dioxide"}
It converts CO, NOₓ and hydrocarbons into CO₂, N₂ and H₂O. Carbon dioxide is a product, not a pollutant it removes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing production figures from memory"}
Questions on the Great Dyke want general facts: layered igneous ore, low grade, PGMs with Ni, Cu, Co and Cr, energy-intensive extraction. Do not invent tonnages.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- PGMs: Pt, Pd, Rh, Ru, Ir, Os; found with Ni, Cu, Co (sulfides) and Cr (chromite) in layered igneous rocks such as the Great Dyke.
- Extraction: mine → crush and mill → froth flotation → smelt to a matte → convert → base-metal refining → precious-metal refining.
- Energy-intensive because the ore is very low-grade (a few grams per tonne) and smelting and refining need high temperatures and many steps.
- Uses: catalytic converters (2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂), jewellery, electronics, inert platinum electrodes (SHE), cisplatin.
- Value addition (local refining) brings revenue, jobs and skills but needs capital, power, skills and strict environmental control.
:::
