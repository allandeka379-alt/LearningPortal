## Introduction

Copper carries the electricity in the wiring of every house in Bulawayo, and farmers spray blue copper(II) sulfate mixtures on crops such as potatoes and tomatoes to control fungal diseases. Brass taps, bronze statues and the copper-nickel coins of many countries show how useful its alloys are. In the laboratory, copper(II) ions are behind Fehling's test for aldehydes and an accurate titration method for copper.

Copper is unusual among the first-row transition elements: its +1 state is unstable in water and its +2 state is the familiar blue one. This lesson explains both with E⦵ values. You should already know E⦵cell, complex ions, iodine-thiosulfate titrations and the colours of copper(II) complexes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blister copper"}
Impure copper (about 98-99% Cu) produced in the converter; it has a blistered surface because sulfur dioxide escapes as it solidifies.
:::

:::definition{term="Iodometric titration"}
A titration in which an oxidising agent liberates iodine from excess iodide ions, and the iodine is titrated with standard sodium thiosulfate using starch as the indicator.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Copper chemistry is ZIMSEC content (Form 6). Cambridge 9701 uses copper(II) complexes in its transition element topics and iodine-thiosulfate titrations in practical work; the extraction, disproportionation and uses here are ZIMSEC.
:::

## Ores, extraction and purification

Copper occurs mainly as **sulfide ores**, especially **chalcopyrite**, CuFeS₂ (34.6% Cu by mass when pure), and in oxidised ores such as malachite (a basic copper carbonate).

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| concentration | crushed ore is concentrated by froth flotation |
| smelting | the concentrate is heated in air with silica; iron forms a slag (iron silicate) and a matte of copper(I) sulfide and iron sulfide remains |
| converting | air blown through the molten matte removes the rest of the iron as slag, then $\ce{Cu2S + O2 -> 2Cu + SO2}$ gives blister copper |
| purification | electrolysis: impure copper anode, pure copper cathode, copper(II) sulfate in dilute sulfuric acid |

In the purification cell the anode dissolves, $\ce{Cu -> Cu^2+ + 2e-}$, and pure copper is deposited at the cathode, $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$. More reactive impurities (iron, zinc, nickel) dissolve and stay in solution; less reactive ones (silver, gold, platinum) fall as **anode slime**, which is refined for these metals. Electrical copper must be very pure, because small amounts of impurities greatly increase the electrical resistance.

The sulfur dioxide from smelting is captured and converted into sulfuric acid; released to the air, it would cause acid rain.

## Copper(I) and copper(II)

| Species | Oxidation state | Colour | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$ | +2 | blue | d⁹: coloured |
| $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ | +2 | deep blue | excess aqueous ammonia |
| $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ | +2 | yellow-green | concentrated HCl |
| $\ce{Cu(OH)2}$ | +2 | pale blue precipitate | with NaOH(aq) or a little NH₃(aq) |
| $\ce{Cu2O}$ | +1 | red-brown solid | formed in Fehling's test |
| $\ce{CuI}$, $\ce{CuCl}$ | +1 | white solids | insoluble |
| $\ce{[CuCl2]-}$ | +1 | colourless (solutions often look brown when made from Cu²⁺) | stable complex |

Copper(I) has the configuration 3d¹⁰. With a full d sub-shell there are no d-d transitions, so copper(I) compounds of simple ions such as CuI and CuCl are white.

**Disproportionation of copper(I).** In water, Cu⁺(aq) is unstable: it disproportionates into copper metal and copper(II).

$$
\ce{2Cu+(aq) -> Cu(s) + Cu^2+(aq)}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 1: why Cu⁺(aq) disproportionates]
Data (E⦵ / V): $\ce{Cu+ + e- <=> Cu}$ +0.52; $\ce{Cu^2+ + e- <=> Cu+}$ +0.15.

1. Reduction half: Cu⁺ + e⁻ → Cu, E⦵ = +0.52 V.
2. Oxidation half: Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + e⁻, the reverse of the +0.15 V couple.
3. E⦵cell = 0.52 − 0.15 = +0.37 V. Positive, so the reaction is feasible: Cu⁺(aq) disproportionates.
4. Example: warming copper(I) oxide with dilute sulfuric acid gives a pink-brown deposit of copper and a blue solution: $\ce{Cu2O + H2SO4 -> Cu + CuSO4 + H2O}$.
:::

**Stabilising copper(I).** Copper(I) survives when it is removed from solution as an **insoluble compound** or held in a **stable complex**, so that the concentration of free Cu⁺(aq) is tiny:

- Precipitation: copper(I) iodide and copper(I) chloride are insoluble white solids.
- Complex formation: boiling copper(II) chloride with copper turnings in concentrated hydrochloric acid gives the copper(I) complex: $\ce{Cu^2+ + Cu + 4Cl- -> 2[CuCl2]-}$. Pouring this solution into water precipitates white CuCl.

## Copper(II) and iodide: iodometric analysis

When potassium iodide is added to a copper(II) solution, a white precipitate of copper(I) iodide forms in a brown solution of iodine (the mixture looks off-white to buff):

$$
\ce{2Cu^2+(aq) + 4I-(aq) -> 2CuI(s) + I2(aq)}
$$

From E⦵ values alone this looks impossible: Cu²⁺/Cu⁺ (+0.15 V) is less positive than I₂/I⁻ (+0.54 V), giving E⦵cell = −0.39 V. It happens because CuI is so insoluble that removing Cu⁺ raises the effective potential of the copper couple (E⦵ for Cu²⁺ + I⁻ + e⁻ ⇌ CuI is +0.86 V, data: textbook value, so E⦵cell = 0.86 − 0.54 = +0.32 V).

The iodine released is titrated with sodium thiosulfate: $\ce{I2 + 2S2O3^2- -> 2I- + S4O6^2-}$. Starch is added near the end-point, when the solution is pale yellow, and the end-point is the change from blue-black to white (the CuI precipitate remains). The overall ratio is **Cu²⁺ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 1** (2Cu²⁺ → I₂ → 2S₂O₃²⁻).

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the purity of a sample of copper(II) sulfate crystals]
2.50 g of impure CuSO₄·5H₂O was dissolved in water and made up to 250 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions were added to excess potassium iodide and the iodine titrated with 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sodium thiosulfate; the mean titre was 19.70 cm³. Calculate the percentage purity (Mr CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.6).

1. n(S₂O₃²⁻) = 0.01970 dm³ × 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ = 9.85 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
2. n(Cu²⁺) in 25.0 cm³ = 9.85 × 10⁻⁴ mol (1 : 1).
3. n(Cu²⁺) in 250 cm³ = 9.85 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O = 9.85 × 10⁻³ × 249.6 = 2.46 g.
5. Purity = 2.46 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = 98.3%.
:::

## Uses of copper and its compounds

| Material | Use | Property used |
|---|---|---|
| copper | electrical wiring, cables, motor windings | excellent electrical conductor; ductile |
| copper | water pipes, heat exchangers | unreactive with water; good thermal conductor |
| brass (Cu + Zn) | taps, valves, musical instruments, ornaments | harder than copper; resists corrosion; easily machined |
| bronze (Cu + Sn) | statues, bearings, ships' propellers | hard, resists corrosion |
| cupronickel (Cu + Ni) | coins | hard-wearing, resists corrosion |
| Fehling's solution | test for aldehydes | Cu²⁺ (complexed, in alkali) is reduced to red-brown Cu₂O by an aldehyde; ketones give no change |
| copper(II) sulfate | fungicide (for example Bordeaux mixture, with lime) | Cu²⁺ ions are toxic to fungi |

:::example{title="Example 3: interpreting a sequence of copper reactions"}
Tendai warms a blue solution with propanal and Fehling's reagent: a red-brown precipitate **R** forms. **R** is warmed with dilute sulfuric acid: a pink-brown solid **S** and a blue solution form.

1. Propanal (an aldehyde) reduces copper(II) to copper(I): **R** is copper(I) oxide, Cu₂O.
2. In acid, Cu⁺ disproportionates: **S** is copper and the blue solution contains Cu²⁺(aq).
3. Equation for step 2: Cu₂O + 2H⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺ + H₂O.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using a 2 : 1 ratio for copper and thiosulfate"}
Two Cu²⁺ give one I₂, which reacts with two S₂O₃²⁻: the ratio Cu²⁺ : S₂O₃²⁻ is 1 : 1.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding starch at the start"}
Starch added when the iodine is concentrated forms a complex that releases iodine slowly, blurring the end-point. Add it when the solution is pale yellow.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying copper(I) compounds are coloured because of d-d transitions"}
Cu⁺ is d¹⁰, so there are no d-d transitions. CuI and CuCl are white; Cu₂O is coloured for a different reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chalcopyrite (CuFeS₂) → concentrate → smelt to matte → converter (Cu₂S + O₂ → 2Cu + SO₂) → electrolytic purification (impure anode, pure cathode, CuSO₄ electrolyte, anode slime).
- Cu⁺(aq) disproportionates: 2Cu⁺ → Cu + Cu²⁺ (E⦵cell = +0.37 V); copper(I) is stabilised in insoluble CuI and CuCl and in [CuCl₂]⁻.
- 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I₂; the iodine is titrated with thiosulfate (starch, blue-black to white): Cu²⁺ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 1.
- Copper for wiring and pipes; brass (Cu/Zn), bronze (Cu/Sn) and cupronickel; Fehling's solution for aldehydes; copper(II) sulfate fungicide.
:::
