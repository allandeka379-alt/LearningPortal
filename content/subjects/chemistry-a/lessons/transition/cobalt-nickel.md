## Introduction

Zimbabwe mines nickel from sulfide ores, and the nickel ends up in stainless-steel sinks, coins and rechargeable batteries. Cobalt is found with nickel and copper and is usually recovered as a by-product when those metals are refined. Two everyday reminders of these elements are in every school laboratory: the blue cobalt chloride paper that turns pink when it touches water, and the margarine in the tuck-shop sandwich, made by hardening vegetable oil over a nickel catalyst.

This lesson covers how ligands and temperature change the colour, geometry and stability of cobalt complexes, and how nickel is produced, purified and used. You should already know complex ions and ligand exchange, Le Chatelier's principle, E⦵ values and electrolysis calculations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Matte"}
A molten mixture of metal sulfides (for example of nickel, copper and iron) produced by smelting a sulfide ore concentrate; it is richer in the valuable metals than the ore.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrogenation"}
The addition of hydrogen across C=C double bonds, catalysed by finely divided nickel; it converts liquid vegetable oils into solid fats such as margarine.
:::

:::definition{term="Alloy"}
A mixture of a metal with one or more other elements, made to improve properties such as hardness, strength, corrosion resistance or magnetism.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The descriptive chemistry of cobalt and nickel is ZIMSEC content (Form 6). Cambridge 9701 uses the [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺/[CoCl₄]²⁻ ligand exchange and nickel complexes in its transition element topics, which are covered in the complexes and stereoisomerism lessons.
:::

## Cobalt

**Occurrence.** Cobalt occurs in arsenide and sulfide minerals, nearly always together with nickel and copper, so it is produced mainly as a by-product of their refining.

**Oxidation states.** The common states are +2 and +3. In water, cobalt(II) is the stable state: Co³⁺(aq) is such a strong oxidising agent (E⦵ Co³⁺/Co²⁺ = +1.82 V) that it oxidises water to oxygen (E⦵cell = 1.82 − 1.23 = +0.59 V). Changing the ligands changes this. With ammonia, cobalt(III) becomes the stable state, as Example 2 shows.

### Ligands, geometry and temperature

| Complex | Oxidation state | Shape | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ | +2 | octahedral | pink |
| $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ | +2 | tetrahedral | blue |
| $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$ | +2 | octahedral | pale brown (straw) |
| $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$ | +3 | octahedral | darker brown in solution (yellow-orange when pure) |
| $\ce{Co(OH)2}$ | +2 | precipitate | blue, turning pink on standing |

Adding concentrated hydrochloric acid to pink aqueous cobalt(II) chloride gives a blue solution:

$$
\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+(aq) + 4Cl-(aq) <=> [CoCl4]^2-(aq) + 6H2O(l)}\qquad \Delta H \text{ positive}
$$

The coordination number falls from 6 to 4 because chloride ions are much larger than water molecules, so only four fit round the Co²⁺ ion, and the shape changes from octahedral to tetrahedral. The forward reaction is endothermic, so **heating** moves the position to the right (blue) and **cooling** in ice moves it back to the left (pink). Adding water also moves it to the left.

:::example{title="Example 1: explaining a colour change with temperature"}
A purple solution containing both cobalt(II) complexes is divided into three tubes. Tube 1 is put in boiling water, tube 2 in ice and tube 3 is diluted with water.

1. Tube 1 turns blue: the forward reaction is endothermic, so raising the temperature moves the equilibrium to the right, forming more tetrahedral [CoCl₄]²⁻.
2. Tube 2 turns pink: lowering the temperature favours the exothermic reverse reaction, forming octahedral [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺.
3. Tube 3 turns pink: water is a product of the forward reaction, so adding it moves the position to the left (and the chloride is diluted).
:::

**Ammine complexes and oxidation by air.** Aqueous ammonia first gives a blue precipitate of Co(OH)₂; in excess it dissolves to give $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$, which darkens on standing in air as oxygen oxidises it to $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$:

$$
\ce{4[Co(NH3)6]^2+ + O2 + 2H2O -> 4[Co(NH3)6]^3+ + 4OH-}
$$

:::worked-solution[Example 2: why air oxidises cobalt(II) in ammonia but not in water]
Data (E⦵ / V): Co³⁺/Co²⁺ +1.82; [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺/[Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ +0.11 (data: textbook value); O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ ⇌ 2H₂O +1.23; O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ ⇌ 4OH⁻ +0.40.

1. In water (acid conditions): E⦵cell for oxygen oxidising Co²⁺(aq) = 1.23 − 1.82 = −0.59 V, not feasible. Co²⁺(aq) is stable in air.
2. In ammonia (alkaline): E⦵cell = 0.40 − 0.11 = +0.29 V, feasible. [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ is oxidised by air.
3. Conclusion: ammonia ligands lower the electrode potential so much that cobalt(III) becomes the more stable state.
:::

**Uses of cobalt.**

- Alloys: hard, heat-resistant alloys for cutting tools and jet-engine parts; alnico magnets (aluminium, nickel, cobalt with iron).
- **Anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride** is blue and turns pink when it absorbs water, forming $\ce{CoCl2.6H2O}$: the test for water (it shows water is present, not that the liquid is pure).
- **Vitamin B₁₂** contains a cobalt ion at the centre of a ring of nitrogen atoms; it is needed to make red blood cells.
- Blue pigments for glass and ceramics, and electrode materials in rechargeable batteries.

## Nickel

**Occurrence.** Nickel occurs mainly as **sulfide ores** (with iron and copper sulfides, as in pentlandite) and as oxide ores. Zimbabwe produces nickel from sulfide ores, and nickel, copper and cobalt also occur with the platinum group metals of the Great Dyke.

**Production and purification (outline).**

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| concentration | crushed ore is concentrated (for example by froth flotation) |
| roasting | heating in air converts part of the sulfide to oxide and removes sulfur as sulfur dioxide (captured to make sulfuric acid) |
| smelting | the concentrate is melted with silica; iron oxide forms a slag and a **matte** of nickel, copper and iron sulfides remains |
| converting | air blown through the matte removes more iron (as slag) and sulfur |
| refining | impure nickel is purified by electrolysis: impure nickel anode, pure nickel cathode, nickel(II) sulfate or chloride electrolyte; Ni → Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ at the anode and Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni at the cathode; precious metals collect as anode slime |

:::worked-solution[Example 3: nickel deposited in electrolytic refining]
A refining cell runs at 200 A for 10.0 hours. Calculate the mass of nickel deposited on the cathode. (Ar Ni = 58.7; F = 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹)

1. Charge Q = It = 200 A × (10.0 × 3600) s = 7.20 × 10⁶ C.
2. Amount of electrons = 7.20 × 10⁶ ÷ 9.65 × 10⁴ = 74.6 mol.
3. Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni, so amount of nickel = 74.6 ÷ 2 = 37.3 mol.
4. Mass = 37.3 mol × 58.7 g mol⁻¹ = 2190 g = 2.19 kg (3 s.f.).
:::

**Nickel compounds.** [Ni(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is green. Aqueous sodium hydroxide gives a green precipitate of Ni(OH)₂, insoluble in excess; excess aqueous ammonia dissolves it to give blue-violet [Ni(NH₃)₆]²⁺.

**Uses of nickel.**

- **Stainless steel** (with chromium): nickel makes it tougher and more resistant to corrosion.
- **Alnico magnets** (aluminium, nickel, cobalt, iron): strong permanent magnets.
- **Coinage**: cupronickel (copper-nickel) alloys are hard-wearing and resist corrosion.
- **Hydrogenation catalyst:** finely divided nickel catalyses the addition of hydrogen to the C=C bonds of vegetable oils at about 150 °C, raising their melting point to make margarine. The catalyst works because hydrogen and the oil are adsorbed on the nickel surface, where bonds are weakened.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying heating changes the cobalt oxidation state"}
Pink [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ and blue [CoCl₄]²⁻ both contain cobalt(II). The colour change is ligand exchange with a change of shape, not redox.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction of the temperature effect wrong"}
The forward reaction (to blue) is endothermic, so warming gives blue and cooling gives pink.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating cobalt chloride paper as a purity test"}
Blue to pink shows that water is present. To show that a liquid is pure water, test its boiling point (100 °C at 1 atm).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (pink, octahedral) + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ (blue, tetrahedral) + 6H₂O is endothermic: heat gives blue, cooling or water gives pink.
- Co³⁺(aq) oxidises water (+1.82 V), but [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ is oxidised by air (E⦵cell +0.29 V): ammonia stabilises cobalt(III).
- Cobalt: alloys, alnico magnets, anhydrous CoCl₂ (blue → pink) as a test for water, vitamin B₁₂.
- Nickel from sulfide ores: concentrate, roast, smelt to a matte, convert, refine by electrolysis (impure anode, pure cathode).
- Nickel: stainless steel, alnico magnets, coinage, and the catalyst for hydrogenating oils to margarine.
:::
