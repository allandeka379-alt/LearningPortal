## Introduction

Every zinc-carbon or alkaline torch battery sold in a Harare supermarket contains a black paste of **manganese(IV) oxide**, and every steelworks adds manganese to its molten iron. Water-treatment works and clinics use purple **potassium manganate(VII)** (permanganate) as an oxidising agent and disinfectant, and in the laboratory it is the self-indicating titrant you meet in redox titrations.

Manganese shows more oxidation states than any other element in the first transition series, from +2 to +7, and its compounds change colour dramatically as the oxidation state changes. This lesson uses those changes to practise predicting redox reactions from E⦵ values. You should already know oxidation numbers, half-equations, E⦵cell and the general properties of transition elements.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Disproportionation"}
A redox reaction in which atoms of the same element in one oxidation state are simultaneously oxidised and reduced, forming two products with different oxidation states.
:::

:::definition{term="Pyrolusite"}
The main ore of manganese, consisting mostly of manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-indicating titrant"}
A titrant whose own colour change shows the end-point; acidified manganate(VII) is purple, while its product Mn²⁺ is almost colourless, so the first permanent pale pink marks the end-point.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Manganese chemistry is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 uses manganate(VII) in redox titrations and E⦵ questions but does not examine the descriptive chemistry of manganese.
:::

## Occurrence and uses

Manganese never occurs free. Its main ore is **pyrolusite** (MnO₂); other oxide and carbonate minerals are also mined. Most manganese goes into steel:

- It removes oxygen and sulfur from molten iron (it is a **deoxidiser and desulfuriser**), forming oxides and sulfides that pass into the slag.
- **Manganese steels** containing a high proportion of manganese are very hard and resist wear, so they are used for rock crushers, excavator teeth and railway points.
- **Dry cells:** MnO₂ is the material at the positive electrode of zinc-carbon and alkaline cells, where it is reduced as the cell discharges.
- Potassium manganate(VII) is used to treat water and as a mild disinfectant.

## Oxidation states, colours and stability

Manganese ([Ar] 3d⁵4s²) can lose or share up to seven electrons. The four states in the syllabus are:

| Oxidation state | Species | Colour | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| +2 | $\ce{Mn^2+(aq)}$, $\ce{[Mn(H2O)6]^2+}$ | very pale pink (almost colourless) | most stable state; d⁵ |
| +2 | $\ce{Mn(OH)2(s)}$ | off-white precipitate, darkens in air | oxidised by air to brown Mn(III)/Mn(IV) oxides |
| +4 | $\ce{MnO2(s)}$ | black-brown solid | oxidising agent; catalyst |
| +6 | $\ce{MnO4^2-}$ (manganate(VI)) | dark green | stable only in strongly alkaline solution |
| +7 | $\ce{MnO4-}$ (manganate(VII)) | deep purple | powerful oxidising agent |

**Relative stability.** Mn²⁺ is the most stable state in acid solution, partly because the half-filled d⁵ configuration is stable: Mn²⁺ is hard to oxidise, and Mn³⁺(aq) is a strong oxidising agent (E⦵ Mn³⁺/Mn²⁺ = +1.49 V). The high states +6 and +7 exist only as oxo-anions, where the charge on manganese is spread over the oxygen atoms; both are strong oxidising agents. Manganate(VI) survives only in strongly alkaline solution: in neutral or acid solution it disproportionates.

## Manganese(IV) oxide

**As a catalyst.** MnO₂ catalyses the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide; it is recovered unchanged in mass at the end:

$$
\ce{2H2O2(aq) -> 2H2O(l) + O2(g)}
$$

**As an oxidising agent.** Warmed with concentrated hydrochloric acid, MnO₂ oxidises chloride ions to chlorine (a fume cupboard is essential):

$$
\ce{MnO2 + 4HCl -> MnCl2 + Cl2 + 2H2O}
$$

Under standard conditions this reaction is not feasible (E⦵cell = 1.23 − 1.36 = −0.13 V). It goes because the acid is concentrated and the mixture is heated: a high [H⁺] makes the MnO₂/Mn²⁺ electrode potential more positive, and chlorine escapes from the mixture. It is a reminder that E⦵ values apply only to standard conditions.

**Making manganate(VI).** Fusing MnO₂ with potassium hydroxide in the presence of an oxidant such as air or potassium nitrate gives green potassium manganate(VI):

$$
\ce{2MnO2 + 4KOH + O2 -> 2K2MnO4 + 2H2O}
$$

## Disproportionation of manganate(VI)

When the green solution of manganate(VI) is neutralised or acidified, it turns purple and a brown-black precipitate forms. Manganese(+6) is both oxidised to +7 and reduced to +4:

$$
\ce{3MnO4^2- + 4H+ -> 2MnO4- + MnO2 + 2H2O}
$$

In neutral solution the water itself supplies the oxygen: $\ce{3MnO4^2- + 2H2O -> 2MnO4- + MnO2 + 4OH-}$. Hydroxide ions are a product, which is why a large excess of alkali pushes the reaction back and keeps manganate(VI) stable.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: predicting the disproportionation of manganate(VI)]
Data (E⦵ / V): $\ce{MnO4- + e- <=> MnO4^2-}$ +0.56; $\ce{MnO4^2- + 4H+ + 2e- <=> MnO2 + 2H2O}$ +2.26. Show that manganate(VI) disproportionates in acid solution.

1. Reduction half (Mn +6 → +4): $\ce{MnO4^2- + 4H+ + 2e- -> MnO2 + 2H2O}$, E⦵ = +2.26 V.
2. Oxidation half (Mn +6 → +7): $\ce{MnO4^2- -> MnO4- + e-}$, the reverse of the +0.56 V couple.
3. E⦵cell = E⦵(reduction) − E⦵(oxidation) = 2.26 − 0.56 = +1.70 V. Positive, so the reaction is feasible.
4. Balance electrons: one MnO₄²⁻ reduced (2 e⁻ gained) for every two oxidised (2 × 1 e⁻ lost), giving 3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O. Check: Mn 3 = 3; O 12 = 8 + 2 + 2; charge −6 + 4 = −2 on both sides.
:::

## Manganate(VII) as an oxidising agent

**In acid solution** (dilute sulfuric acid) manganate(VII) is reduced to Mn²⁺: purple → colourless.

$$
\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}\qquad E^\ominus = +1.52\ \text{V}
$$

It oxidises iron(II) (E⦵cell = 1.52 − 0.77 = +0.75 V), ethanedioate, hydrogen peroxide, sulfur dioxide and alcohols. Hydrochloric acid is not used to acidify it, because manganate(VII) also oxidises chloride ions to chlorine (E⦵cell = 1.52 − 1.36 = +0.16 V), so more titrant would be used than the analyte needs. Nitric acid is not used either, because it is itself an oxidising agent.

**In neutral or alkaline solution** less H⁺ is available and manganate(VII) is reduced only to MnO₂, a brown-black precipitate (three electrons):

$$
\ce{MnO4- + 2H2O + 3e- -> MnO2 + 4OH-}
$$

In very concentrated alkali it may stop at green manganate(VI) (one electron). Hot alkaline manganate(VII) oxidises the side-chains of arenes to benzoic acid, and cold dilute manganate(VII) is the test for C=C (purple to colourless, or brown precipitate in alkali).

| Conditions | Product of reduction | Electrons gained per Mn | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| dilute H₂SO₄ | Mn²⁺(aq) | 5 | purple → colourless |
| neutral or dilute alkali | MnO₂(s) | 3 | purple → brown-black precipitate |
| concentrated alkali | MnO₄²⁻(aq) | 1 | purple → green |

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the purity of a sample of pyrolusite]
A 0.500 g sample of powdered pyrolusite was warmed with 50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanedioate (an excess) and dilute sulfuric acid: $\ce{MnO2 + C2O4^2- + 4H+ -> Mn^2+ + 2CO2 + 2H2O}$. The excess ethanedioate needed 24.20 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ potassium manganate(VII): $\ce{2MnO4- + 5C2O4^2- + 16H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}$. Calculate the percentage by mass of MnO₂ in the ore (Mr MnO₂ = 86.9).

1. Ethanedioate added = 0.0500 dm³ × 0.100 mol dm⁻³ = 5.00 × 10⁻³ mol.
2. Manganate(VII) used = 0.02420 dm³ × 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ = 4.84 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
3. Excess ethanedioate = 4.84 × 10⁻⁴ × 5/2 = 1.21 × 10⁻³ mol.
4. Ethanedioate that reacted with MnO₂ = 5.00 × 10⁻³ − 1.21 × 10⁻³ = 3.79 × 10⁻³ mol = amount of MnO₂ (1 : 1).
5. Mass of MnO₂ = 3.79 × 10⁻³ mol × 86.9 g mol⁻¹ = 0.329 g.
6. Percentage = 0.329 ÷ 0.500 × 100 = 65.9%.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: identifying manganese species from observations"}
Chipo warms a black powder **X** with concentrated hydrochloric acid in a fume cupboard; a green-yellow gas that bleaches damp litmus is given off and a very pale pink solution remains. A second portion of **X** is fused with KOH and KNO₃, giving a green solid; its solution turns purple, with a brown-black precipitate, when dilute sulfuric acid is added.

1. Chlorine (bleaches damp litmus) shows that **X** is an oxidising agent; the pale pink solution is Mn²⁺(aq).
2. The green solid is manganate(VI), K₂MnO₄, made by oxidation in alkali.
3. Purple MnO₄⁻ and brown-black MnO₂ on acidifying are the products of disproportionation.
4. **X** is manganese(IV) oxide, MnO₂.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing five electrons for manganate(VII) in alkali"}
Five electrons (to Mn²⁺) apply only in acid. In neutral or alkaline solution the product is MnO₂ and the half-equation has three electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Acidifying manganate(VII) with hydrochloric acid"}
Chloride ions are oxidised to chlorine, which wastes titrant and gives a toxic gas. Use dilute sulfuric acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling MnO₂ a reactant in the hydrogen peroxide reaction"}
In H₂O₂ decomposition MnO₂ is a catalyst (unchanged in mass). With concentrated HCl it is an oxidising agent and is used up. Read the question.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ore: pyrolusite (MnO₂); uses: steel (deoxidiser, hard manganese steels), dry cells (MnO₂), water treatment (KMnO₄).
- Mn²⁺ pale pink (most stable, d⁵); MnO₂ black-brown; MnO₄²⁻ green (stable only in strong alkali); MnO₄⁻ purple.
- MnO₂ catalyses H₂O₂ decomposition and oxidises concentrated HCl to Cl₂ on warming.
- Manganate(VI) disproportionates in neutral or acid solution: 3MnO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO₄⁻ + MnO₂ + 2H₂O (E⦵cell = +1.70 V).
- Manganate(VII) is reduced to Mn²⁺ in acid (5 e⁻), to MnO₂ in neutral or alkaline solution (3 e⁻) and to MnO₄²⁻ in concentrated alkali (1 e⁻).
:::
