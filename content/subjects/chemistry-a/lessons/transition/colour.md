## Introduction

Copper(II) sulfate solution is pale blue, potassium dichromate(VI) is orange and cobalt chloride paper changes from blue to pink when it meets water. Gemstones get their colours in a similar way: a trace of chromium(III) in aluminium oxide gives the red of ruby. Water treatment laboratories use the same colours to measure tiny concentrations of iron or copper in drinking water.

This lesson explains where these colours come from. The key idea is that ligands split the five d orbitals into two groups of different energy, and electrons absorb visible light when they move between them. You should know the shapes of complexes, ligand exchange in copper(II) and cobalt(II) complexes, and the shapes of the 3d orbitals.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Degenerate orbitals"}
Orbitals that have the same energy. The five 3d orbitals of an isolated gaseous ion are degenerate.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-degenerate orbitals"}
Orbitals of different energy. Ligands make the d orbitals non-degenerate by splitting them into two groups.
:::

:::definition{term="ΔE (d-orbital splitting energy)"}
The energy gap between the lower and the higher group of d orbitals in a complex.
:::

:::definition{term="Complementary colour"}
The colour seen when one colour of light is removed from white light; for example, removing orange-red light leaves light that looks blue.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: splitting in octahedral and tetrahedral complexes, absorption and the complementary colour, colourless d⁰ and d¹⁰ ions, and the effect of changing the ligand (Cambridge A Level, Paper 4; ZIMSEC Form 6). Colorimetry is examined in outline; no equations for absorbance are needed.
:::

## Splitting the d orbitals

In an isolated ion all five 3d orbitals have the same energy. When ligands approach, their lone pairs repel the d electrons, and the orbitals that point towards the ligands are raised in energy more than the others.

![Energy diagram of d orbitals: in a free transition metal ion the five d orbitals have the same energy; in an octahedral complex the ligands split them into three lower and two higher orbitals with an energy gap ΔE. A photon of visible light with energy ΔE is absorbed when an electron moves up, so the complex appears coloured with the remaining wavelengths.](media:diagrams/d-orbital-splitting.png "Figure: d-orbital splitting in a complex; absorption of light across the gap ΔE gives colour")

**Octahedral complexes.** The six ligands lie along the x, y and z axes. The $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$ orbitals point straight at them, so these **two** orbitals are raised. The $d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$ and $d_{yz}$ orbitals point between the axes, so these **three** are lower.

**Tetrahedral complexes.** The four ligands lie between the axes, so the pattern is reversed: **three** orbitals ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$) are higher and **two** are lower. With fewer ligands, none pointing straight at an orbital, ΔE is **smaller** than in an octahedral complex of the same ion.

| Shape | Higher group | Lower group | Size of ΔE |
|---|---|---|---|
| octahedral | 2 orbitals | 3 orbitals | larger |
| tetrahedral | 3 orbitals | 2 orbitals | smaller (about half or less) |

## From splitting to colour

1. An electron in the lower group absorbs a photon of visible light whose energy equals ΔE, and is promoted to the higher group.
2. The energy absorbed fixes the frequency: $\Delta E = hf$, where $h$ is the Planck constant. A larger ΔE means higher-frequency (shorter-wavelength) light is absorbed.
3. The light that is not absorbed is transmitted or reflected. We see the **complementary colour** of the light absorbed.

| Colour mainly absorbed | Colour seen |
|---|---|
| red-orange | blue (cyan) |
| orange-yellow | deep blue to violet-blue |
| green | red-purple (pink to magenta) |
| violet-blue | yellow |

So $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$ absorbs mainly in the red-orange part of the spectrum and looks pale blue.

## Colourless ions

A d-d transition needs an electron in the lower group **and** a space in the higher group:

- **Sc³⁺** (3d⁰) and **Ti⁴⁺** (3d⁰): no d electrons to promote.
- **Zn²⁺** and **Cu⁺** (3d¹⁰): every d orbital is full, so there is nowhere to promote an electron to.

Their compounds are white and their solutions colourless, for example zinc sulfate and copper(I) iodide.

## Changing the ligand changes the colour

ΔE depends on the metal, its oxidation state, the ligands and the shape. Ammonia splits the d orbitals of copper(II) more than water does; chloride ligands give a small splitting and a tetrahedral shape.

| Complex | Shape | Colour seen |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}$ | octahedral | pale blue |
| $\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}$ | octahedral | deep blue |
| $\ce{[Cu(OH)2(H2O)4]}$ | octahedral | pale blue precipitate |
| $\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}$ | tetrahedral | yellow (yellow-green when mixed with the aqua ion) |
| $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ | octahedral | pink |
| $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$ | octahedral | straw (pale yellow-brown) |
| $\ce{[Co(OH)2(H2O)4]}$ | octahedral | blue precipitate |
| $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ | tetrahedral | blue |

:::example{title="Example 1: pink and blue cobalt"}
Explain why $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ is pink but $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ is blue.

1. Co²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁷: a partly filled d sub-shell, so d-d transitions are possible.
2. $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ is octahedral, with a relatively large ΔE. It absorbs mainly green light (fairly high frequency), so the transmitted light looks pink.
3. $\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}$ is tetrahedral and has chloride ligands, so ΔE is much smaller. It absorbs lower-frequency light, in the orange-red region, and so looks blue.
4. The change of ligand and shape changes ΔE, which changes the frequency absorbed and so the colour seen.
:::

## Colorimetry in outline

The intensity of colour of a solution depends on the concentration of the coloured complex. A **colorimeter** shines light of one colour through a sample and measures how much is absorbed (the absorbance).

1. Choose a filter of the colour the solution absorbs most strongly (the complementary colour of the solution).
2. Often add a ligand that forms an intensely coloured complex, for example excess ammonia for copper(II) or thiocyanate ions for iron(III).
3. Measure the absorbance of several standard solutions of known concentration and plot a **calibration graph** of absorbance against concentration (usually a straight line through, or very close to, the origin).
4. Measure the absorbance of the unknown under the same conditions and read its concentration from the graph.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: copper in a water sample]
Standards of copper(II) in excess ammonia gave these absorbances:

| [Cu²⁺] / mol dm⁻³ | absorbance |
|---|---|
| 0.020 | 0.11 |
| 0.040 | 0.22 |
| 0.060 | 0.34 |
| 0.080 | 0.45 |
| 0.100 | 0.56 |

A treated sample gave an absorbance of 0.39. Find its copper(II) concentration.

1. The best-fit straight line has gradient $5.65\ \text{dm}^3\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and intercept $-0.003$.
2. Rearrange absorbance $= 5.65c - 0.003$: $c = \dfrac{0.39 + 0.003}{5.65}$.
3. $c = 0.0696\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$.
4. Reading the value from a carefully drawn calibration graph gives the same answer to the precision of the graph.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the colour seen is the colour absorbed"}
A blue solution absorbs red-orange light. The colour you see is what is left: the complementary colour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Electrons 'falling back' to produce the colour"}
Colour comes from light being absorbed as electrons are promoted; it is not light emitted, as in a flame test.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the splitting the wrong way round"}
Octahedral: two up, three down. Tetrahedral: three up, two down, and a smaller ΔE.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ligands split the degenerate d orbitals: octahedral two higher and three lower; tetrahedral three higher and two lower, with a smaller ΔE.
- An electron absorbs visible light of energy ΔE = hf and is promoted; the complex shows the complementary colour.
- d⁰ (Sc³⁺) and d¹⁰ (Zn²⁺, Cu⁺) ions are colourless because no d-d transition is possible.
- Changing the ligand, the shape or the oxidation state changes ΔE and therefore the colour (pale blue to deep blue with NH₃; pink to blue for cobalt with Cl⁻).
- Colorimetry: filter of the complementary colour, standards, calibration graph, read off the unknown.
:::
