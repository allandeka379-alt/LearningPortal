## Introduction

The Great Dyke, the long ridge of ancient igneous rock that runs through the centre of Zimbabwe, contains seams of **chromite**, an ore of chromium. Chromite is smelted to ferrochrome, an alloy of iron and chromium that steelmakers add to iron to make stainless steel for kitchen sinks, hospital equipment and food-processing plants.

Vanadium is less familiar but just as useful: a small amount makes steel tough enough for tools and springs, and vanadium(V) oxide is the catalyst that makes sulfuric acid in the Contact process. Both metals have several oxidation states with striking colours, which makes them ideal for practising redox predictions with E⦵ values. You should already know oxidation numbers, half-equations, E⦵ and E⦵cell, and the Contact process.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chromite"}
The main ore of chromium, an iron chromium oxide, FeCr₂O₄ (FeO·Cr₂O₃).
:::

:::definition{term="Amphoteric hydroxide"}
A hydroxide that reacts with both acids and bases; chromium(III) hydroxide is an example.
:::

:::definition{term="Feasible reaction (from E⦵ values)"}
A redox reaction for which E⦵cell = E⦵(reduction) − E⦵(oxidation) is positive under standard conditions. It may still be too slow to observe.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This is ZIMSEC content (Form 6, Applied Chemistry). Cambridge 9701 meets vanadium and chromium only as examples in redox, E⦵ and transition element questions, so Cambridge students may still find the redox examples useful.
:::

## Vanadium

**Occurrence (outline).** Vanadium is never found free. It occurs in minerals such as vanadinite and in some iron ores, and it is also recovered from the residues of some crude oils.

**Oxidation states and colours.** Vanadium shows every state from +2 to +5 in aqueous solution:

| Oxidation state | Ion in acid solution | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| +5 | $\ce{VO2+}$ (dioxovanadium(V)) | yellow |
| +4 | $\ce{VO^2+}$ (oxovanadium(IV)) | blue |
| +3 | $\ce{V^3+}$ | green |
| +2 | $\ce{V^2+}$ | violet |

In neutral solution vanadium(V) is present as the vanadate(V) ion, $\ce{VO3-}$ (ammonium vanadate(V), NH₄VO₃, is a white solid).

**Reduction by zinc.** Zinc and dilute acid reduce vanadium(V) all the way to vanadium(II). The colours seen are yellow, green (a mixture of yellow VO₂⁺ and blue VO²⁺), blue, green (V³⁺) and finally violet (V²⁺). The flask is loosely plugged with cotton wool, because air re-oxidises V²⁺.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: why zinc reaches vanadium(II)]
Data (E⦵ / V): $\ce{VO2+ + 2H+ + e- <=> VO^2+ + H2O}$ +1.00; $\ce{VO^2+ + 2H+ + e- <=> V^3+ + H2O}$ +0.34; $\ce{V^3+ + e- <=> V^2+}$ −0.26; $\ce{Zn^2+ + 2e- <=> Zn}$ −0.76. Show that zinc can reduce vanadium(V) to vanadium(II).

1. VO₂⁺ to VO²⁺: E⦵cell = +1.00 − (−0.76) = +1.76 V, feasible.
2. VO²⁺ to V³⁺: E⦵cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V, feasible.
3. V³⁺ to V²⁺: E⦵cell = −0.26 − (−0.76) = +0.50 V, feasible.
4. Every step is positive, so zinc reduces vanadium(V) to violet vanadium(II). The overall equation for the last step is $\ce{2V^3+ + Zn -> 2V^2+ + Zn^2+}$.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: a reducing agent that stops part-way"}
E⦵(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.77 V. Predict how far iron(II) ions reduce vanadium(V).

1. VO₂⁺ to VO²⁺: E⦵cell = 1.00 − 0.77 = +0.23 V, feasible: yellow turns blue.
2. VO²⁺ to V³⁺: E⦵cell = 0.34 − 0.77 = −0.43 V, not feasible.
3. Iron(II) stops at vanadium(IV): $\ce{VO2+ + 2H+ + Fe^2+ -> VO^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O}$.
:::

**Relative stability.** The E⦵ values show that V²⁺ is a strong reducing agent (it is oxidised even by air and by water slowly) and that VO₂⁺ is a moderately strong oxidising agent. Vanadium(IV) is the most stable state in air in acid solution.

**Uses.** Vanadium steels are tough and resist wear, so they are used for tools, springs and axles. Vanadium(V) oxide catalyses the oxidation of sulfur dioxide in the Contact process by changing oxidation state:

$$
\ce{SO2 + V2O5 -> SO3 + V2O4}
$$

$$
\ce{2V2O4 + O2 -> 2V2O5}
$$

## Chromium

**Occurrence and extraction.** Chromium is extracted from chromite, FeCr₂O₄, which occurs in seams on the Great Dyke. For stainless steel the chromite is smelted with carbon (coke) in electric furnaces to ferrochrome. Pure FeCr₂O₄ contains $\dfrac{2 \times 52.0}{223.8} \times 100 = 46.5\%$ chromium by mass.

**Oxidation states and colours.** The common states are +3 and +6 (chromium(II), Cr²⁺, is blue and easily oxidised).

| Species | Oxidation state | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]^3+}$ | +3 | violet (solutions often look green, because sulfate or chloride replaces some water ligands) |
| $\ce{Cr(OH)3}$ | +3 | grey-green precipitate |
| $\ce{[Cr(OH)6]^3-}$ | +3 | dark green solution |
| $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ (chromate(VI)) | +6 | yellow |
| $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ (dichromate(VI)) | +6 | orange |

**The chromate-dichromate equilibrium.** In solution the two chromium(VI) ions are in equilibrium:

$$
\ce{2CrO4^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) <=> Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

Adding acid moves the position to the right (orange); adding alkali removes H⁺ and moves it to the left (yellow). This is **not** a redox reaction: chromium is +6 on both sides.

**Dichromate(VI) as an oxidising agent.** In acid solution:

$$
\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H+ + 6e- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\qquad E^\ominus = +1.33\ \text{V}
$$

The colour changes from orange to green. It oxidises Fe²⁺ (E⦵cell = 1.33 − 0.77 = +0.56 V) in redox titrations, and it is used in two tests:

- **Alcohols:** warm with acidified potassium dichromate(VI); a primary or secondary alcohol turns it from orange to green, a tertiary alcohol does not.
- **Sulfur dioxide:** filter paper soaked in acidified potassium dichromate(VI) turns from orange to green: $\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 3SO2 + 2H+ -> 2Cr^3+ + 3SO4^2- + H2O}$.

**Amphoteric chromium(III) hydroxide.** Aqueous sodium hydroxide gives a grey-green precipitate, which dissolves in excess to give a dark green solution, and which also dissolves in acid:

$$
\ce{Cr^3+ + 3OH- -> Cr(OH)3}
$$

$$
\ce{Cr(OH)3 + 3OH- -> [Cr(OH)6]^3-}
$$

$$
\ce{Cr(OH)3 + 3H+ -> Cr^3+ + 3H2O}
$$

Warming the dark green solution with hydrogen peroxide oxidises chromium(III) to yellow chromate(VI): $\ce{2[Cr(OH)6]^3- + 3H2O2 -> 2CrO4^2- + 2OH- + 8H2O}$.

**Uses and hazards.** Chromium makes stainless steel (a thin, self-repairing layer of chromium(III) oxide stops corrosion) and is electroplated onto steel as chrome plating for a hard, shiny, protective finish. Chromium(VI) compounds are **toxic and carcinogenic**: handle dichromate and chromate with gloves and eye protection, avoid breathing dust, and never pour residues down the sink. Chromium(III) compounds are much less harmful.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the chromate-dichromate change a redox reaction"}
Chromium is +6 in both CrO₄²⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻. The colour change is an acid-base equilibrium.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the first green colour V³⁺"}
The first green seen when zinc reduces vanadium(V) is a mixture of yellow VO₂⁺ and blue VO²⁺. V³⁺ appears only after the blue stage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Subtracting E⦵ values the wrong way"}
E⦵cell = E⦵ of the species being reduced − E⦵ of the species being oxidised. A negative answer means the reaction is not feasible.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Vanadium: VO₂⁺ (+5) yellow, VO²⁺ (+4) blue, V³⁺ (+3) green, V²⁺ (+2) violet; zinc in acid reduces V(V) to V(II), as E⦵ values predict.
- V₂O₅ catalyses the Contact process (V₂O₅ ⇌ V₂O₄); vanadium steels are tough and wear-resistant.
- Chromium comes from chromite (FeCr₂O₄) on the Great Dyke; it is used in stainless steel and chrome plating.
- 2CrO₄²⁻ (yellow) + 2H⁺ ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange) + H₂O is not redox; acidified dichromate(VI) is an oxidising agent (orange to green) used to test for alcohols and SO₂.
- Cr(OH)₃ is amphoteric (grey-green precipitate, dark green [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ in excess alkali); chromium(VI) compounds are toxic and carcinogenic.
:::
