## Introduction

Crush a eucalyptus leaf or peel an orange and you smell its **essential oil**. These oils are valuable in perfumes, flavourings, soaps and medicines, and Zimbabwe grows plenty of the raw materials: eucalyptus trees, citrus fruit and herbs such as lemongrass. The oils boil at around 170-250 °C, but many of their compounds decompose or oxidise if heated that much. Steam distillation solves the problem: it distils the oil out of the plant at a temperature **below 100 °C**.

This lesson explains why mixtures of immiscible liquids boil at such low temperatures, how steam distillation is carried out, and where it is used. You should know what vapour pressure and boiling point mean, and the meaning of immiscible liquids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Vapour pressure"}
The pressure exerted by a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature; it increases as the temperature rises.
:::

:::definition{term="Boiling point"}
The temperature at which the total vapour pressure of a liquid (or mixture) equals the external (atmospheric) pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Immiscible liquids"}
Liquids that do not dissolve in each other and form separate layers, such as water and an essential oil.
:::

:::definition{term="Steam distillation"}
Distillation of a water-immiscible substance by passing steam through it (or boiling it with water), so that it distils together with water at a temperature below 100 °C.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
ZIMSEC only (Form 6, 6.4.2.1). Qualitative treatment: no Raoult's law calculations are required. Cambridge does not include steam distillation.
:::

## Why immiscible liquids boil below either boiling point

In a **miscible** mixture the molecules of each liquid are diluted by the other, so each liquid's vapour pressure is lowered. In an **immiscible** mixture, such as water and an oil, the two liquids form separate layers. Each layer behaves as a pure liquid and exerts its **full** vapour pressure, independent of how much of the other liquid is present:

$$
p_\text{total} = p^\circ_\text{water} + p^\circ_\text{oil}
$$

The mixture boils when $p_\text{total}$ reaches atmospheric pressure (about 101 kPa at sea level). Because both liquids contribute, the total reaches 101 kPa at a temperature where **each** vapour pressure is still well below 101 kPa: that is, below the boiling point of water and far below that of the oil.

The boiling temperature stays **constant** as long as both liquids are present, whatever their proportions, because each still exerts its full vapour pressure.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding the boiling temperature of a mixture]
Data: vapour pressure of water at 96, 97 and 98 °C is 87.7, 90.9 and 94.3 kPa. In an experiment, the vapour pressure of a water-immiscible oil Y at the same temperatures was 9.0, 9.5 and 10.0 kPa. Atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa.

| Temperature / °C | p(water) / kPa | p(Y) / kPa | Total / kPa |
|---|---|---|---|
| 96 | 87.7 | 9.0 | 96.7 |
| 97 | 90.9 | 9.5 | 100.4 |
| 98 | 94.3 | 10.0 | 104.3 |

1. Add the two vapour pressures at each temperature.
2. The total reaches 101.3 kPa between 97 °C (100.4 kPa) and 98 °C (104.3 kPa).
3. So the mixture boils at just above 97 °C: below 100 °C (water) and far below the boiling point of Y.
:::

## The apparatus

1. **Steam generator.** A flask of water is boiled; a long **safety tube** dips into the water so that pressure cannot build up if the steam outlet blocks.
2. **Distillation flask.** Steam is passed through a tube reaching to the bottom of a flask containing the plant material (chopped leaves or peel) with some water. The flask may be warmed gently so that steam does not condense too much in it.
3. **Condenser and receiver.** The mixed vapour of water and oil passes into a water-cooled condenser, and the distillate is collected.
4. **Separation.** The distillate is cloudy or forms **two layers**: oil and water. The oil is separated with a **separating funnel** (or extracted with a little organic solvent), dried with an anhydrous drying agent such as anhydrous sodium sulfate, and filtered.

![Steam distillation apparatus: a flask of boiling water with a safety tube makes steam, which is passed through a tube to the bottom of a second flask holding plant material and water; the mixed vapour of oil and water goes through a water-cooled condenser into a receiver as a cloudy distillate that forms two layers, and the oil is separated with a separating funnel.](media:diagrams/steam-distillation-apparatus.png "Figure: steam distillation of an essential oil, from steam generator to distillate")

A simpler version for a school laboratory boils the plant material directly with water in a flask fitted for distillation.

Safety: steam and hot apparatus cause burns; the safety tube prevents pressure build-up; organic solvents used for extraction are flammable.

## The composition of the distillate

The vapour contains water and oil in proportion to their vapour pressures, so in terms of **molecules** the distillate is mostly water (the oil's vapour pressure is small). However, essential-oil molecules are much heavier than water molecules (relative molecular masses of about 130-160, against 18). So even a few oil molecules for every hundred water molecules carry a useful **mass** of oil into the distillate. This is why steam distillation works well for high-molecular-mass, water-immiscible substances, but the distillate is still mainly water by mass, and large volumes of steam are needed.

## Why use steam distillation?

| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| low temperature (below 100 °C) | heat-sensitive substances distil without decomposing, oxidising or charring |
| no organic solvent needed for the distillation | the product is not contaminated with solvent; cheaper and safer |
| separates volatile from non-volatile material | leaves, sugars, salts and tars remain in the flask |

Limits: the substance must be **immiscible with water**, must have some vapour pressure near 100 °C, and must not react with water. Very dilute oils need large amounts of steam (and energy).

## Applications

- **Essential oils** for perfumes, cosmetics and soaps: from eucalyptus leaves, citrus peel (mainly limonene), lemongrass, rose petals and lavender.
- **Food industry**: natural flavourings (citrus oils, clove oil, peppermint).
- **Pharmaceutical industry**: medicinal oils such as eucalyptus oil (used in inhalants); purifying heat-sensitive organic compounds.
- **Laboratory**: purifying a water-immiscible product such as phenylamine (b.p. 184 °C), which steam distils at about 98 °C, away from non-volatile tars.

:::example{title="Example 2: oil from orange peel"}
Chipo wants to extract the oil from orange peel collected from a citrus farm.

1. She grates the peel, puts it in a round-bottomed flask with water and passes steam through it.
2. The distillate collects as a cloudy liquid with a thin oily layer on top (the oil is less dense than water and immiscible with it).
3. She separates the oil layer with a separating funnel, shakes the aqueous layer with a little organic solvent to recover dissolved droplets, combines and dries the organic material, and evaporates the solvent gently.
4. Analysing the oil by gas-liquid chromatography would show limonene as the main component.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using Raoult's law for immiscible liquids"}
Immiscible liquids each exert their **full** vapour pressure. Raoult's law (vapour pressure lowered by mole fraction) applies to miscible mixtures.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the mixture boils at 100 °C"}
The mixture boils when the **sum** of the vapour pressures reaches atmospheric pressure, so it boils **below** 100 °C.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the distillate is mostly oil"}
By number of molecules the distillate is mostly water; the oil is a useful fraction by mass only because its molecules are heavy.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In an immiscible mixture each liquid exerts its full vapour pressure: p(total) = p°(water) + p°(other).
- The mixture boils when p(total) equals atmospheric pressure, below the boiling point of either liquid, and at a constant temperature.
- Steam distillation separates heat-sensitive, water-immiscible substances at below 100 °C; the oil is then separated from the water.
- The distillate is mainly water, but heavy organic molecules give a useful mass of product.
- Uses: essential oils for perfumes, flavourings and medicines; purifying compounds such as phenylamine.
:::
