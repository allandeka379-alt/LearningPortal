## Introduction

Farmers in Mashonaland lime acidic soils, citrus growers in the Mazowe valley know the sharp taste of citric acid, and every swimming pool and water treatment works watches its pH. At O Level you met acids as substances that produce hydrogen ions in water. At A Level that definition is too narrow: ammonia gas reacting with hydrogen chloride gas to make white fumes of ammonium chloride is clearly an acid-base reaction, yet no water is involved.

The **Brønsted-Lowry theory** fixes this by focusing on what actually moves: a proton, $\ce{H+}$. This lesson sets out that theory, the idea of conjugate pairs, and the difference between strong and weak acids, which is the foundation for pH, buffers and titration curves later. You should already know the common laboratory acids and alkalis and how to write ionic equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Brønsted-Lowry acid"}
A proton ($\ce{H+}$) donor.
:::

:::definition{term="Brønsted-Lowry base"}
A proton ($\ce{H+}$) acceptor. A base must have a lone pair of electrons to bond to the proton.
:::

:::definition{term="Conjugate acid-base pair"}
Two species that differ by one proton, $\ce{H+}$. When an acid donates a proton it becomes its conjugate base; when a base accepts a proton it becomes its conjugate acid.
:::

:::definition{term="Strong acid"}
An acid that is fully (completely) dissociated into ions in aqueous solution, for example hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids.
:::

:::definition{term="Weak acid"}
An acid that is only partially dissociated in aqueous solution, so that an equilibrium exists between the undissociated acid and its ions, for example ethanoic acid.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: Brønsted-Lowry definitions, strong and weak acids and bases, and the experiments that distinguish them. Cambridge examines the terms *conjugate acid* and *conjugate base* at A Level (Paper 4); ZIMSEC teaches them in Form 5. ZIMSEC uses "sulphuric acid"; Cambridge uses "sulfuric acid".
:::

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| hydrochloric | $\ce{HCl}$ | strong | sodium hydroxide | $\ce{NaOH}$ | strong |
| nitric | $\ce{HNO3}$ | strong | potassium hydroxide | $\ce{KOH}$ | strong |
| sulfuric | $\ce{H2SO4}$ | strong (first proton) | ammonia | $\ce{NH3}$ | weak |
| ethanoic | $\ce{CH3COOH}$ | weak | | | |

## Acids and bases as proton transfer

When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water, it donates a proton to a water molecule:

$$
\ce{HCl(g) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
$$

$\ce{HCl}$ is the acid; water is the base. The oxonium ion $\ce{H3O+}$ is what we usually write as $\ce{H+(aq)}$.

When ammonia dissolves, water acts as the **acid**:

$$
\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

Water can therefore behave as an acid or a base depending on its partner; it is described as **amphoteric** (amphiprotic). The reaction of ammonia with hydrogen chloride gas, $\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}$, is a Brønsted-Lowry acid-base reaction even though no water is present.

## Conjugate pairs

In every Brønsted-Lowry reaction there are two conjugate pairs.

:::example{title="Example 1: identifying conjugate pairs"}
$\ce{CH3COOH(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}$

1. $\ce{CH3COOH}$ donates a proton, so it is acid 1; its conjugate base is $\ce{CH3COO-}$.
2. $\ce{H2O}$ accepts the proton, so it is base 2; its conjugate acid is $\ce{H3O+}$.
3. Pairs: $\ce{CH3COOH}$/$\ce{CH3COO-}$ and $\ce{H3O+}$/$\ce{H2O}$. Each pair differs by exactly one $\ce{H+}$.

$\ce{HNO3 + H2SO4 <=> H2NO3+ + HSO4-}$ (in a mixture of the two concentrated acids)

1. Sulfuric acid donates the proton, so here it is the acid, and nitric acid acts as the **base**.
2. Pairs: $\ce{H2SO4}$/$\ce{HSO4-}$ and $\ce{H2NO3+}$/$\ce{HNO3}$. Whether a species is an acid or a base depends on what it reacts with.
:::

A stronger acid has a weaker conjugate base. Because $\ce{HCl}$ gives up its proton completely, $\ce{Cl-}$ has almost no tendency to take one back.

## Strong and weak acids

A strong acid is fully dissociated: $\ce{HNO3(aq) -> H+(aq) + NO3-(aq)}$ (single arrow). A weak acid sets up an equilibrium that lies well to the left: $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H+(aq)}$. In a 0.1 mol dm⁻³ solution of ethanoic acid only around one molecule in a hundred is dissociated.

Strength is not the same as concentration. *Concentrated* and *dilute* describe how much acid is dissolved per dm³; *strong* and *weak* describe the extent of dissociation.

Bases are classified in the same way: sodium hydroxide is fully ionised in solution, while ammonia accepts protons from water only partially.

### Telling strong and weak acids apart

Compare solutions of the **same concentration**, for example 0.10 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid and 0.10 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid:

| Test | Strong acid (HCl) | Weak acid (CH₃COOH) | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| pH (meter or universal indicator) | lower, about 1 | higher, about 3 | higher $[\ce{H+}]$ in the strong acid |
| Reaction with magnesium ribbon | vigorous fizzing | slow fizzing | rate depends on $[\ce{H+}]$ |
| Electrical conductivity | high | low | more ions in solution |
| Total volume of hydrogen with excess Mg | same | same | the same amount of acid can eventually give up all its protons |

The last row surprises many students. As the weak acid's $\ce{H+}$ ions are used up by the magnesium, the equilibrium moves to the right and releases more, so in the end the same total amount of hydrogen forms, only more slowly.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: equal volumes of hydrogen]
50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid and 50.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid each react with excess magnesium. Calculate the volume of hydrogen, at room conditions, from each. (molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

1. $n(\text{acid}) = \dfrac{50.0}{1000} \times 0.100 = 0.00500$ mol in each flask.
2. Both are monobasic: $\ce{Mg + 2H+ -> Mg^2+ + H2}$, so $n(\ce{H2}) = 0.00500 \div 2 = 0.00250$ mol.
3. $V = 0.00250 \times 24.0 = 0.0600\ \text{dm}^3 = 60.0\ \text{cm}^3$ from **each** acid; the ethanoic acid simply takes longer.
:::

## Neutralisation and salts

The essential reaction when any acid neutralises an alkali in aqueous solution is

$$
\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}
$$

The other ions form a **salt**: $\ce{NaOH + HNO3 -> NaNO3 + H2O}$; $\ce{2KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + 2H2O}$; $\ce{NH3 + HCl -> NH4Cl}$. A pH of 7 is neutral at 298 K, below 7 is acidic and above 7 is alkaline. Salt solutions are not always neutral: ammonium chloride is slightly acidic and sodium ethanoate slightly alkaline, which matters when choosing indicators.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing strong with concentrated"}
A dilute solution of a strong acid is still fully dissociated; a concentrated weak acid is still only partially dissociated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Conjugate pairs that differ by more than one H⁺"}
$\ce{H2SO4}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ are not a conjugate pair: they differ by two protons. The conjugate base of $\ce{H2SO4}$ is $\ce{HSO4-}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a weak acid gives less hydrogen"}
With excess magnesium, equal amounts of a weak and a strong monobasic acid give the same total volume of hydrogen; only the rate differs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling water only a solvent"}
In acid-base reactions water often acts as a base (with HCl) or an acid (with NH₃).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3; Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 (AS) for definitions and strong/weak comparisons, and Paper 4 for conjugate pairs. Multiple-choice items often ask you to pick the species acting as a base in an unfamiliar equation.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Define*: "proton donor" or "proton acceptor" is enough. *Identify* the conjugate pairs: label acid 1/base 1 and acid 2/base 2. *Explain* a difference between strong and weak acids: link the observation to $[\ce{H+}]$ or the number of ions, and to the extent of dissociation. *Describe* an experiment: name the test, state that concentrations are equal, and give both observations.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Brønsted-Lowry acid: proton donor; base: proton acceptor (needs a lone pair).
- Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺; a strong acid has a very weak conjugate base.
- Strong acids and bases are fully dissociated; weak ones only partially, with an equilibrium.
- At equal concentration a weak acid has higher pH, reacts more slowly with Mg and conducts less, but gives the same total amount of H₂.
- Neutralisation: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l); the remaining ions form a salt.
:::
