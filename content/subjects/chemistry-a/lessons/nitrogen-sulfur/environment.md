## Introduction

Traffic jams on Samora Machel Avenue in Harare at rush hour, a coal-fired power station at Hwange, fertiliser washing off farmland into a reservoir, a packet of dried mango slices: all of these involve oxides or compounds of nitrogen and sulfur. Some are useful, such as sulfur dioxide keeping dried fruit from spoiling. Others cause real environmental harm: smog, acid rain and green, oxygen-starved lakes.

This lesson explains where the oxides of nitrogen and sulfur come from, what chemistry they take part in once they are in the air, how catalytic converters remove some of them, and why nitrate fertilisers affect lakes and rivers. Methods for removing sulfur dioxide from power-station flue gases belong to the air-pollution lesson. You should already know the Contact process reaction, how catalysts work, and how to balance redox equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Acid rain"}
Rain with a pH below about 5.6 (the pH of rain in equilibrium with atmospheric carbon dioxide), made more acidic by dissolved sulfur and nitrogen oxides forming sulfuric, sulfurous and nitric acids.
:::

:::definition{term="Photochemical smog"}
A brownish haze formed in sunlight from oxides of nitrogen and unburnt hydrocarbons, containing ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN).
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
The over-enrichment of water with nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates, which causes rapid growth of algae and, after they die and decay, a fall in dissolved oxygen that kills fish and other aquatic life.
:::

ZIMSEC detail: the environmental consequences of nitrate fertilisers (eutrophication) and sulfur dioxide as a food preservative. Cambridge lists PAN and the catalytic oxidation of SO₂ in 12.1.
:::

## Sources of oxides of nitrogen

Nitrogen and oxygen do not react at ordinary temperatures (the N≡N bond is very strong), but they do at very high temperatures:

$$
\ce{N2(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO(g)}
$$

| Source | Type | Why NO forms |
|---|---|---|
| lightning | natural | the very high temperature of the discharge |
| soil bacteria | natural | microbial processes in soil release some nitrogen oxides |
| internal combustion engines | man-made | air (N₂ and O₂) is compressed and heated to high temperatures in the cylinders |
| power stations and furnaces | man-made | fuels are burned in air at high temperatures |

Nitrogen monoxide, a colourless gas, is then oxidised slowly in air to brown nitrogen dioxide:

$$
\ce{2NO(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)}
$$

Together NO and NO₂ are often written **NOₓ**.

## Catalytic converters

A catalytic converter in a car exhaust contains a ceramic honeycomb coated with a thin layer of platinum, palladium and rhodium, which gives a very large surface area. Exhaust gases are adsorbed on the metal surface, where bonds are weakened and the gases react; the products then desorb. Three pollutants are removed at once (a "three-way" converter):

$$
\ce{2CO(g) + 2NO(g) -> 2CO2(g) + N2(g)}
$$

Unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water, for example $\ce{C8H18 + 12.5O2 -> 8CO2 + 9H2O}$. In the reaction with carbon monoxide, nitrogen monoxide is reduced (N from +2 to 0) and carbon monoxide is oxidised (C from +2 to +4). Converters work only when hot, and lead compounds poison the catalyst, which is one reason leaded petrol was phased out.

## Photochemical smog and PAN

On sunny days in busy cities, ultraviolet light splits nitrogen dioxide, and the oxygen atoms formed make ground-level ozone:

$$
\ce{NO2 ->[UV] NO + O}
$$

$$
\ce{O + O2 -> O3}
$$

Oxides of nitrogen also react with **unburnt hydrocarbons** from vehicle exhausts to form **peroxyacetyl nitrate**, PAN, CH₃CO–O–O–NO₂. PAN and ozone are the main harmful components of photochemical smog: they irritate the eyes and lungs, worsen asthma and damage plants. So NOₓ from traffic are harmful both directly and through the secondary pollutants they help to form.

## Acid rain

Rain is naturally slightly acidic (about pH 5.6) because carbon dioxide dissolves in it. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide make it much more acidic.

**Sulfur dioxide** comes mainly from burning fossil fuels, especially coal, that contain sulfur compounds, and from smelting sulfide ores:

$$
\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}
$$

**Directly**, the oxides dissolve and react with water and oxygen:

$$
\ce{SO2 + H2O -> H2SO3}
$$

$$
\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}
$$

**Catalytically**, nitrogen oxides speed up the oxidation of sulfur dioxide to sulfur trioxide, which forms sulfuric acid:

1. $\ce{NO2 + SO2 -> NO + SO3}$ (NO₂ oxidises SO₂; N goes from +4 to +2)
2. $\ce{NO + 1/2O2 -> NO2}$ (NO is reoxidised by air, so NO₂ is regenerated)
3. $\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}$

Adding steps 1 and 2 gives the overall reaction $\ce{SO2 + 1/2O2 -> SO3}$: NO₂ is used in step 1 and remade in step 2, so it acts as a **homogeneous catalyst** (the same phase as the reactants).

**Effects of acid rain** include damage to limestone and marble buildings ($\ce{CaCO3 + H2SO4 -> CaSO4 + H2O + CO2}$), corrosion of metal structures, harm to trees as nutrients are leached from soil, and the death of fish in lakes as the water becomes more acidic and toxic aluminium ions are released from rocks.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: sulfur in coal]
A sample of coal contains 2.0% sulfur by mass. Calculate the mass of sulfur dioxide formed when 1.00 kg of this coal is burned, and the mass of sulfuric acid it could eventually form. (Ar: H 1.0, O 16.0, S 32.1)

1. Mass of sulfur $= 1000 \times 0.020 = 20$ g; $n(\ce{S}) = 20 \div 32.1 = 0.623$ mol.
2. $\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}$: $n(\ce{SO2}) = 0.623$ mol; Mr(SO₂) = 64.1; mass $= 0.623 \times 64.1 = 39.9$ g.
3. Each SO₂ gives one H₂SO₄ (via SO₃), Mr(H₂SO₄) = 98.1; mass $= 0.623 \times 98.1 = 61.1$ g.
:::

## Nitrate fertilisers and eutrophication (ZIMSEC detail)

Nitrate and ammonium fertilisers are very soluble. When too much is applied, or heavy rain falls soon after spreading, nitrates are washed (leached) into rivers, dams and lakes.

:::example{title="Example 2: the stages of eutrophication"}
1. Nitrates (and phosphates) enter a lake and act as nutrients for algae and water plants.
2. Algae grow rapidly (an algal bloom), forming a green layer that blocks light from plants lower down, which die.
3. When the algae die, aerobic bacteria decompose them and multiply, using up the dissolved oxygen.
4. Oxygen levels fall so low that fish and other aquatic animals die.
5. Lakes near cities and farmland, such as Lake Chivero near Harare, have suffered algal blooms linked to nutrient pollution from sewage and run-off; water treatment then becomes more difficult and expensive.
:::

Good practice limits these effects: applying the right amount of fertiliser at the right time, avoiding spreading before heavy rain, and leaving buffer strips of vegetation beside rivers. High nitrate levels in drinking water are also controlled by health regulations.

## Sulfur dioxide as a food preservative (ZIMSEC detail)

Sulfur dioxide, and sulfites that release it, are added in small amounts to dried fruit (such as dried mango and apricots), fruit juices, jams and wine. SO₂ helps preserve food because:

- it is **toxic to micro-organisms**, inhibiting the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds;
- it is a **reducing agent (antioxidant)**, so it reacts with oxygen and prevents oxidation that would discolour fruit and spoil flavour.

Its use is controlled by food regulations and it must be listed on labels (often as E220 or "sulfites"), because a small number of people, particularly some asthmatics, are sensitive to it.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nitrogen oxides come from the fuel"}
Most NOₓ from engines comes from nitrogen in the **air**, which reacts with oxygen at the high temperature in the cylinder, not from nitrogen in the petrol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that NO₂ is regenerated"}
In the catalytic oxidation of SO₂, NO₂ is used up in one step and remade in the next, which is why it is a catalyst. Write both steps.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing ground-level ozone with the ozone layer"}
Ozone in photochemical smog at ground level is a harmful pollutant; ozone in the stratosphere is beneficial because it absorbs ultraviolet radiation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing eutrophication as 'nitrates poison the fish'"}
Fish die from lack of dissolved oxygen, used up by bacteria decomposing the dead algae, not from the nitrate itself.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- NO forms from N₂ and O₂ at high temperatures (lightning, engines, furnaces) and is oxidised in air to NO₂.
- Catalytic converters (Pt, Pd, Rh) remove NO and CO (2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂) and oxidise unburnt hydrocarbons.
- NOₓ, unburnt hydrocarbons and sunlight form ozone and PAN, the harmful parts of photochemical smog.
- SO₂ and NO₂ cause acid rain; NO₂ catalyses SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃ by NO₂ + SO₂ → NO + SO₃ then NO + ½O₂ → NO₂.
- Leached nitrate fertilisers cause eutrophication; SO₂ preserves food as an antimicrobial and antioxidant.
:::
