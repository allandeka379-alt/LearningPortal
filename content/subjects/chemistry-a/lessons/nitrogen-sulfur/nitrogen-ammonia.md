## Introduction

Every breath you take is about 78% nitrogen, yet a maize plant growing in a field in Mashonaland can starve for nitrogen. The reason is that the nitrogen molecule is extraordinarily unreactive: plants cannot use N₂ directly. They need nitrogen in a "fixed" form, such as ammonium or nitrate ions, which is why ammonium nitrate (AN) and compound fertilisers are so important to Zimbabwean agriculture, and why groundnuts and soya beans, which host nitrogen-fixing bacteria, are valued in crop rotations.

This lesson explains the unreactivity of nitrogen and then turns to ammonia and the ammonium ion: how ammonia behaves as a base, how the ammonium ion is formed and shaped, and how ammonia is released from ammonium salts. The industrial manufacture of ammonia, the Haber process, is a separate lesson. You should already know about bond energy, dative (coordinate) bonding, VSEPR shapes and Brønsted-Lowry acids and bases.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Brønsted-Lowry base"}
A proton (H⁺) acceptor. Ammonia is a base because the lone pair on its nitrogen atom can accept a proton.
:::

:::definition{term="Dative (coordinate) bond"}
A covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom, as when the lone pair on nitrogen in NH₃ bonds to H⁺ to form NH₄⁺.
:::

:::definition{term="Nitrogen fixation"}
The conversion of unreactive atmospheric nitrogen, N₂, into nitrogen compounds such as ammonia, ammonium ions or nitrates.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the lack of reactivity of nitrogen, the basicity of ammonia, the formation and structure of the ammonium ion, and the displacement of ammonia from ammonium salts. Cambridge says "coordinate (dative covalent) bond"; ZIMSEC says "dative bond". ZIMSEC also suggests preparing ammonia and ammonium sulfate in the laboratory.
:::

## Why nitrogen is unreactive

Nitrogen gas is used to fill food packets and to flush fuel tanks precisely because it is so inert. Two features of the N₂ molecule explain this.

1. **A very strong triple bond.** The N≡N bond energy is 944 kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet), one of the strongest bonds known. Any reaction must start by breaking or weakening it, so the activation energy is very high.
2. **No polarity.** Both atoms are identical, so the molecule is non-polar: there is no δ+ or δ− site to attract nucleophiles or electrophiles.

Nitrogen does react under extreme conditions: with oxygen at the very high temperatures of lightning or a car engine, and with hydrogen in the Haber process using high temperature, high pressure and an iron catalyst.

## Ammonia as a base

Ammonia is a pyramidal molecule (bond angle about 107°) with a lone pair on nitrogen. It dissolves very readily in water, and a small fraction reacts:

$$
\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}
$$

Water donates a proton and ammonia accepts it, so ammonia is a Brønsted-Lowry base. The equilibrium lies well to the left, so ammonia is a **weak base**, and its solution is only moderately alkaline (pH about 11 for a 0.1 mol dm⁻³ solution).

Ammonia neutralises acids to form ammonium salts, for example:

$$
\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{2NH3(aq) + H2SO4(aq) -> (NH4)2SO4(aq)}
$$

The first reaction, between two gases, produces dense white smoke of ammonium chloride; it is a useful test for either gas.

## The ammonium ion

When ammonia accepts a proton, the lone pair on nitrogen forms a **dative bond** to the H⁺ ion, which has an empty 1s orbital:

$$
\ce{H3N: + H+ -> NH4+}
$$

1. Before: NH₃ has three bonding pairs and one lone pair around N, so it is pyramidal (107°).
2. After: NH₄⁺ has four bonding pairs and no lone pair.
3. Four bonding pairs repel equally, so NH₄⁺ is **tetrahedral** with bond angles of 109.5°.
4. Once formed, the dative bond is identical to the other three N–H bonds: you cannot tell which hydrogen arrived last.

## Displacing ammonia from ammonium salts

Warming any ammonium salt with an alkali releases ammonia gas:

$$
\ce{NH4Cl(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + NH3(g) + H2O(l)}
$$

Ionic equation: $\ce{NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> NH3(g) + H2O(l)}$.

This is an **acid-base reaction**: the ammonium ion is a weak acid and donates a proton to the hydroxide ion, a stronger base than ammonia. Warming drives the ammonia, which is very soluble, out of solution.

The same reaction is the **test for ammonium ions**: warm the substance with aqueous sodium hydroxide and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube; it turns blue. It also explains why ammonium fertilisers should not be mixed with lime (calcium hydroxide) or spread on freshly limed soil: nitrogen would be lost as ammonia gas.

In the laboratory, ammonia is prepared by heating a mixture of ammonium chloride and calcium hydroxide: $\ce{2NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O}$. It is dried with calcium oxide (not concentrated sulfuric acid, which would react with it) and collected by upward delivery because it is less dense than air. Ammonia is toxic and irritating: work in a fume cupboard.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: ammonia from ammonium chloride]
5.35 g of ammonium chloride is heated with excess calcium hydroxide. Calculate the volume of ammonia formed at room conditions. (Mr NH₄Cl = 53.5; molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)

1. $n(\ce{NH4Cl}) = 5.35 \div 53.5 = 0.100$ mol.
2. $\ce{2NH4Cl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O}$: the ratio NH₄Cl : NH₃ is 2 : 2 = 1 : 1, so $n(\ce{NH3}) = 0.100$ mol.
3. $V = 0.100 \times 24.0 = 2.40$ dm³.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: nitrogen content of a fertiliser]
Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃. (Ar: H 1.0, N 14.0, O 16.0)

1. Mr(NH₄NO₃) = 14.0 + 4 × 1.0 + 14.0 + 3 × 16.0 = 80.0.
2. Mass of nitrogen in one mole = 2 × 14.0 = 28.0 g (one N in NH₄⁺ and one in NO₃⁻).
3. Percentage N = 28.0 ÷ 80.0 × 100 = 35.0%. This high nitrogen content is one reason ammonium nitrate is a popular fertiliser.
:::

## Nitrogen fixation in outline

| Route | What happens |
|---|---|
| lightning | the very high temperature makes N₂ and O₂ combine: $\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}$; NO is oxidised to NO₂, which dissolves in rain to form nitric acid and nitrates in the soil |
| nitrogen-fixing bacteria | bacteria in root nodules of legumes (groundnuts, beans, soya) convert N₂ into ammonium compounds that the plant can use |
| industrial (Haber process) | $\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}$ with an iron catalyst; the ammonia is made into fertilisers such as ammonium nitrate (see the Haber process lesson) |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nitrogen is unreactive because it is a noble gas"}
Nitrogen is not a noble gas. It is unreactive because of the very strong N≡N triple bond and the lack of polarity in the molecule.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving NH₄⁺ a pyramidal shape"}
NH₃ is pyramidal (one lone pair); NH₄⁺ has four bonding pairs and no lone pair, so it is tetrahedral, 109.5°.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the reaction of NH₄⁺ with OH⁻ a redox reaction"}
No oxidation numbers change. It is an acid-base reaction: NH₄⁺ donates a proton to OH⁻.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drying ammonia with concentrated sulfuric acid"}
Ammonia is a base and would react with the acid. Use calcium oxide.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- N₂ is unreactive: very strong N≡N bond (944 kJ mol⁻¹) and a non-polar molecule, so the activation energy of its reactions is very high.
- Ammonia is a weak Brønsted-Lowry base: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻; it neutralises acids to give ammonium salts.
- NH₄⁺ forms by a dative bond from the nitrogen lone pair to H⁺; it is tetrahedral (109.5°).
- Alkalis displace ammonia from ammonium salts on warming (NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O), the basis of the test for NH₄⁺.
- Nitrogen is fixed by lightning, by bacteria in legume root nodules and industrially by the Haber process.
:::
