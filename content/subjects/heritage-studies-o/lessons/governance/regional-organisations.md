## Introduction

Every day, lorries queue at Beitbridge, Chirundu and Plumtree carrying goods between Zimbabwe and its neighbours. Zimbabweans travel to Botswana, Zambia, Mozambique and South Africa to trade, study, work and visit family. On 25 May each year, Africa Day is celebrated across the continent. All of this is shaped by the regional and continental organisations to which Zimbabwe belongs.

In this Form 4 lesson you learn about three of them: the **Southern African Development Community (SADC)**, the **Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA)** and the **African Union (AU)**. For each you study its history, aims, structure in outline, benefits and obligations for Zimbabwe, and challenges. The United Nations and the Non-Aligned Movement are studied in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regional organisation"}
An organisation of states in one part of the world that cooperate on shared aims such as trade, development, peace and security.
:::

:::definition{term="Regional integration"}
The process by which neighbouring countries join their economies and policies more closely, for example by reducing trade barriers.
:::

:::definition{term="Protocol"}
A formal agreement among member states on a particular area of cooperation, such as trade, gender or the movement of people.
:::

:::definition{term="Free trade area"}
A group of countries that remove or reduce customs duties on goods traded among themselves.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
SADCC formed **1980** (Zimbabwe a founder member); SADC Treaty **1992**; SADC headquarters **Gaborone, Botswana**. COMESA treaty **1993**, in force **1994**; headquarters **Lusaka, Zambia**. OAU founded **25 May 1963** in Addis Ababa; African Union launched **2002**; AU headquarters **Addis Ababa, Ethiopia**. Africa Day: **25 May**.
:::

## SADC

**History.** The **Southern African Development Coordination Conference (SADCC)** was formed in **1980** by the independent states of the region, including newly independent Zimbabwe. Its main aim was to reduce the region's economic dependence on apartheid South Africa and to coordinate development projects. In **1992** the SADC Treaty turned SADCC into the **Southern African Development Community (SADC)**, a community aiming at deeper integration. South Africa joined after the end of apartheid.

**Aims.** Economic growth and development; reduction of poverty; regional integration and trade; peace, security and democracy; common use of shared resources such as rivers; and support for the socially disadvantaged.

**Structure in outline.**

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| Summit of Heads of State or Government | The highest decision-making body |
| Organ on Politics, Defence and Security Cooperation | Promotes peace and security, mediates in conflicts |
| Council of Ministers | Oversees policies and programmes |
| Secretariat (in Gaborone), led by the Executive Secretary | Runs day-to-day work |

## COMESA

The **Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa** grew out of the earlier Preferential Trade Area. Its treaty was signed in **1993** and came into force in **1994**; its headquarters are in **Lusaka**. Its main aim is to build a large common market by reducing barriers to trade and investment among member states, many of which are in eastern and southern Africa. COMESA operates a free trade area among some of its members and promotes cooperation in transport, communications and customs procedures.

## The OAU and the African Union

The **Organisation of African Unity (OAU)** was founded on **25 May 1963** in Addis Ababa. Its aims included ending colonialism and apartheid, defending the independence of African states and promoting unity. Through its **Liberation Committee** it supported liberation movements, including those of Zimbabwe. In **2002** the OAU was replaced by the **African Union**, which places more emphasis on economic development, good governance, human rights and peace and security.

| AU body | Role |
|---|---|
| Assembly of Heads of State and Government | Supreme organ; makes the main decisions |
| Executive Council | Ministers who coordinate policies |
| AU Commission (Addis Ababa) | The secretariat that runs day-to-day work |
| Pan-African Parliament | Gives African peoples a voice in the AU |
| Peace and Security Council | Prevents and resolves conflicts |

## Benefits and obligations for Zimbabwe

| Benefits | Obligations |
|---|---|
| Wider markets for Zimbabwean goods and lower duties on trade | Pay annual membership contributions |
| Regional projects in transport, energy and water (e.g. shared use of the Zambezi) | Implement treaties and protocols agreed |
| Cooperation on peace, security and disaster response | Contribute to peacekeeping and election observation |
| A stronger collective voice in world affairs | Respect agreed principles such as democracy and human rights |
| Exchange of skills, education and health cooperation | Attend and host meetings and summits |

:::example{title="How regional trade benefits a Zimbabwean business"}
A fictional company in Bulawayo makes cooking oil and wants to sell it in Zambia.

1. **Without integration:** high customs duties make the oil expensive in Zambia, so it cannot compete.
2. **With a free trade area:** duties are reduced or removed, so the price falls.
3. **Results:** the company sells more, employs more workers and buys more sunflower seed from Zimbabwean farmers.
4. **But:** Zambian companies can also sell more easily in Zimbabwe, so Zimbabwean firms must be competitive.
:::

Ordinary citizens feel these organisations in daily life: in lower prices for some imported goods, in regional sports and cultural events, in shared projects such as power lines and dams, and in agreements that make it easier to travel between member states for short visits.

## Challenges facing these organisations

- **Shortage of funds:** some members pay late; programmes depend on donors.
- **Overlapping membership:** many countries belong to both SADC and COMESA, with different rules and commitments.
- **Unequal economies:** larger economies may benefit more from free trade than smaller ones.
- **Conflicts and political crises** in member states take time and resources.
- **Poor infrastructure:** bad roads, railways and slow border posts raise trading costs.
- **National interests** sometimes come before agreed commitments, so protocols are slow to be implemented.

:::example{title="Assessing an organisation's success"}
Question: *How far has SADC achieved its aims?*

1. **Successes:** peace-making and election observation; regional infrastructure and energy cooperation; protocols on trade and gender; a strong regional identity.
2. **Limits:** slow implementation of protocols; trade among members is still limited; funding problems.
3. **Judgement:** SADC has been more successful in political cooperation than in full economic integration.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up SADCC and SADC"}
SADCC (1980) was a coordination conference aimed at reducing dependence on apartheid South Africa. SADC (1992) is a development community aiming at integration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the headquarters"}
SADC: Gaborone. COMESA: Lusaka. AU: Addis Ababa.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State* the aims of SADC (Paper 2 Section A): one phrase each. *Explain the benefits to Zimbabwe*: name the benefit and show how Zimbabwe gains. For a Section B essay (*how far has the AU achieved its aims?*), weigh successes against challenges and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SADCC (1980) became SADC (1992), with headquarters in Gaborone; it promotes development, integration, peace and security.
- COMESA (treaty 1993, in force 1994; headquarters Lusaka) aims at a common market through freer trade.
- The OAU (25 May 1963) supported liberation and was replaced by the African Union in 2002 (headquarters Addis Ababa).
- Zimbabwe benefits from wider markets, regional projects and a collective voice, and must pay contributions and implement agreements.
- Challenges include limited funds, overlapping membership, unequal economies, conflicts and slow implementation.
:::
