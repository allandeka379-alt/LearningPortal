## Introduction

Think about your journey to school this morning. You may have drunk water from a council tap or a borehole, walked along a road built with public money, and arrived at a school whose teachers are paid by government. If you were sick last term, you may have been treated at a public clinic. These are **social services**: services that government provides, or helps to provide, so that every citizen can live a healthy and dignified life.

In this Form 4 lesson you learn which social services government provides, why it provides them, how they are paid for, what makes them difficult to provide, and how citizens and partners help. You should already know the structure of central and local government.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Social services"}
Services provided or supported by government for the welfare of citizens, such as education, health care, housing, water and sanitation, and help for vulnerable people.
:::

:::definition{term="Social welfare"}
Help given by government and partners to people who cannot meet their basic needs, such as orphans, older people, people with disabilities and families in poverty.
:::

:::definition{term="Vulnerable groups"}
People who are more likely to suffer harm or hardship, such as orphans and vulnerable children, older people, people with disabilities and people with chronic illnesses.
:::

:::definition{term="Tax"}
A compulsory payment to government by individuals and businesses, used to fund public services.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
**BEAM** (Basic Education Assistance Module) pays school fees for vulnerable children. The **AIDS levy** is a charge on income tax that funds the national HIV response. **ZIMRA** (the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority) collects national taxes. The Constitution protects rights such as the **right to education** and the **right to health care**.
:::

## Social services and who provides them

| Service | Examples | Main providers |
|---|---|---|
| Education | Primary and secondary schools, teachers' salaries, examinations, colleges and universities, early childhood development | Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education; Ministry of Higher and Tertiary Education; councils; churches and private schools |
| Health | Clinics, district and provincial hospitals, immunisation, maternal care, HIV testing and treatment | Ministry of Health and Child Care; councils; mission hospitals |
| Housing and shelter | Serviced stands, council houses, housing schemes, emergency shelter after disasters | Ministry responsible for housing; urban councils |
| Water and sanitation | Piped water, boreholes, sewage systems, toilets | Councils; ZINWA (Zimbabwe National Water Authority); RDCs with partners |
| Social welfare | School fees assistance (BEAM), food aid, care for orphans, help for older people and people with disabilities | Department of Social Welfare; partners |
| Transport and electricity (outline) | Roads, bridges, public buses and trains, rural electrification | Ministries, councils, state-owned companies |

## Why government provides social services

1. **To protect rights.** The Constitution protects rights such as education, health care and safe water; government has a duty to work towards them.
2. **To reduce poverty and inequality.** Free or low-cost services help poor families who could not pay full prices.
3. **To build a productive nation.** Educated and healthy people can work, earn and contribute to development.
4. **To protect public health.** Clean water and sanitation prevent diseases such as cholera that can spread to everyone.
5. **Because the market would not provide enough.** Private businesses may not build clinics or roads in remote areas where profits are low.
6. **To keep peace and unity.** Fair access to services across regions builds trust in government.

:::example{title="Why water is everyone's business"}
In a fictional suburb of Chinhoyi, burst sewer pipes go unrepaired for weeks.

1. **Immediate effect:** sewage flows into a stream used for washing.
2. **Health risk:** cholera and typhoid can spread quickly.
3. **Wider effect:** disease spreads to other suburbs, schools and markets, and the clinic is overwhelmed.
4. **Conclusion:** sanitation is a public good; government and the council provide it because everyone depends on it, not only the people in that suburb.
:::

## How social services are funded

| Source | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Taxes | Income tax, value added tax (VAT), customs duty and other taxes collected by ZIMRA and allocated in the national budget |
| Special levies | e.g. the AIDS levy, which funds the HIV response |
| Local authority revenue | Rates and service charges pay for council clinics, water and refuse |
| User fees | School fees and some health charges (with exemptions for vulnerable people) |
| Loans and grants | From international lenders and development partners |
| Partners | NGOs, churches, UN agencies and businesses fund or run some services |

## Challenges in providing social services

- **Limited funds:** tax revenue is small compared with needs, especially in a weak economy.
- **Rapid population growth and urbanisation:** more people need schools, water and housing.
- **Brain drain:** doctors, nurses and teachers leave for better pay abroad.
- **Natural disasters and disease:** droughts, cyclones and pandemics increase demand and damage infrastructure.
- **Old infrastructure:** pipes, hospitals and roads need costly maintenance.
- **Corruption and mismanagement:** money meant for services can be lost.
- **Unequal access:** remote rural areas often have fewer services than towns.

These challenges are linked. When the economy is weak, tax revenue falls just as more families need help, and skilled staff are more likely to leave. Solutions therefore usually combine several measures: better collection and honest use of revenue, maintenance before collapse, incentives for staff to work in rural areas, and partnerships with communities and organisations.

:::example{title="Solving a problem with partners"}
A fictional rural ward near Binga has one clinic, 25 km from the farthest village.

1. **Government:** the Ministry of Health provides nurses and medicines; the RDC provides land.
2. **Partner:** an NGO funds a new outreach post.
3. **Community:** villagers mould bricks and form a health committee.
4. **Result:** the walking distance is halved and more mothers give birth with a skilled attendant.
5. **Lesson:** social services work best when government, partners and citizens cooperate.
:::

## The role of citizens and partners

**Citizens** pay taxes, rates and bills; use services responsibly (for example, not vandalising water points); take part in school development committees and health committees; volunteer; and report problems and corruption. **Partners** such as churches (which run many mission schools and hospitals), NGOs, UN agencies (UNICEF, WHO, WFP) and businesses provide money, skills and services alongside government.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying services are free"}
Services are paid for, mainly through taxes. Free at the point of use does not mean free to provide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing services without explaining why"}
"Education" alone earns little. "Government provides education because educated citizens can find work and contribute to development" is explained.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify* social services (Paper 2 Section A): name them. *Explain why government provides them*: give the reason and its result. *Suggest how challenges could be overcome*: give practical, realistic measures, each with how it helps. For a Section B essay on whether government alone should provide services, weigh government's role against that of partners and citizens.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Social services include education, health, housing, water and sanitation, social welfare, transport and electricity.
- Government provides them to protect rights, reduce poverty, build a productive nation and protect public health.
- They are funded by taxes collected by ZIMRA, levies such as the AIDS levy, council revenue, fees, loans and partners.
- Challenges include limited funds, population growth, brain drain, disasters, old infrastructure and corruption.
- Citizens and partners (churches, NGOs, UN agencies, businesses) help to provide and protect services; BEAM pays fees for vulnerable children.
:::
