## Introduction

On a Saturday morning in a village near Bindura, a group of men in jackets waits politely under a tree outside a homestead. One of them carries a small bag of money and a notebook. They are not visitors in the usual sense: they have come to ask for a daughter of the house in marriage, and the day's talks will be long, formal and good-humoured. By evening the two families will be linked for life.

Scenes like this happen across Zimbabwe every weekend, in towns as well as villages, and among families who will also hold a church wedding or register a civil marriage. Indigenous marriage practices remain one of the strongest living parts of Zimbabwean heritage. In this lesson you learn the main steps, the people involved, the counselling that prepares a couple for married life, and why marriage is valued. You should already know the roles of aunts and uncles in the family and what courtship is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Go-between (munyai / umkhongi)"}
A trusted man chosen by the groom's family to lead marriage negotiations with the bride's family. He speaks for the groom's side, and the two families do not negotiate face to face without him.
:::

:::definition{term="Bride wealth (roora / lobola)"}
Gifts of cattle, money and goods given by the groom's family to the bride's family. It shows the groom's respect and commitment, thanks the bride's family and binds the two families together. It is not the purchase of a person.
:::

:::definition{term="Handing over the bride (kuperekwa / umthimba)"}
The ceremony at which the bride is accompanied by her relatives, led by her aunt, to her husband's family and formally received there.
:::

:::definition{term="Marriage counsellor"}
A relative who prepares a couple for marriage and helps them with problems afterwards. In indigenous society the bride's counsellor is her paternal aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*) and the groom's is usually his maternal uncle (*sekuru/umalume*).
:::

:::keyfact{title="The law on marriage"}
No one under **18** may marry (section 78 of the Constitution), and marriage needs the free and full consent of both people. Marriage law was reformed by the Marriages Act of 2022, which in outline brought civil and customary marriages under one law and made marrying or pledging a child an offence.
:::

## The steps of an indigenous marriage

The details differ from family to family and between communities, but the main steps are widely shared.

| Step | What happens | Who is involved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The couple agree | After courtship, the young woman tells her aunt that a man intends to marry her | The couple; her aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*) |
| 2. Choosing a go-between | The groom's family selects a respected, skilled speaker | Groom's father and uncles; the *munyai/umkhongi* |
| 3. Opening talks | The go-between arrives and pays a small token to "open the mouth" so talks can start (*vhuramuromo* in Shona, *isivulamlomo* in Ndebele) | Go-between; bride's family |
| 4. Negotiating bride wealth | Items are named and amounts agreed: a main payment (*rutsambo* in Shona), cattle for the father, and the mother's cow (*mombe yeumai / inkomo yohlanga*); part may be paid later | Both families, through the go-between |
| 5. Handing over the bride | The bride is escorted by her aunt and relatives to her husband's home and welcomed with food, songs and gifts (*kuperekwa / umthimba*) | Bride's aunt and relatives; groom's family |
| 6. Counselling continues | Aunts and uncles advise the couple and help settle problems | *vatete/ubabakazi*, *sekuru/umalume* |

Bride wealth has changed over time. Cattle were once the main item; today much of it is paid in money, sometimes in instalments, and families vary widely in what they ask. Some people argue that very high demands turn roora/lobola into a business; others point out that families can and do agree on modest amounts. You should be able to present both views.

:::example{title="A go-between at work in Gwanda"}
Themba's family sends his uncle's friend, a well-known speaker, as *umkhongi* to Nokuthula's family.

1. He greets the family and pays *isivulamlomo* so the talks can begin.
2. The bride's family lists what they ask; he listens, requests time, and consults Themba's family by phone.
3. He negotiates politely, and part of the lobola is paid that day, with an agreed plan for the rest.
4. He reports back. Both families now see each other as in-laws.

The go-between protects both sides: nobody bargains in anger, and the elders' dignity is kept.
:::

## Marriage counsellors and the counselling process

Counselling begins before the wedding and continues for life.

**The aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*)** listens to the young woman during courtship, meets the suitor, attends the negotiations and leads the bride to her new home. She teaches respect for in-laws, household management, care of the husband and children, good conduct, and how to handle disagreements without shaming either family. In private and age-appropriate terms, she also prepares the bride for married life.

**The uncle (*sekuru/umalume*)** advises the young man on providing for his family, respecting his wife and her relatives, controlling his temper, and his duties as a husband and future father.

**When problems arise**, the couple go first to these counsellors rather than to outsiders. The aunt and uncle listen to both sides, call the families together if needed and try to reconcile the couple. Only if this fails would the matter go further, for example to a traditional court or, today, a magistrates court.

:::example{title="An aunt settles a dispute"}
Six months after their marriage, Chipo and Tatenda in Masvingo quarrel because Tatenda's mother criticises Chipo's cooking in front of guests.

1. Chipo tells her *vatete* instead of leaving her home in anger.
2. The aunt meets Tatenda and his uncle. She listens to both sides.
3. The uncle advises Tatenda to speak to his mother privately; the aunt advises Chipo on how to raise the matter respectfully.
4. Peace is restored without the quarrel becoming public.

Indigenous counselling works because the counsellors are trusted, know both families and are expected to be fair.
:::

## Forms of indigenous marriage

Besides the standard process above, communities recognised other ways in which marriages were formed. Learn them neutrally, and know which are now illegal.

| Form (Shona term) | What it was | Status today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Kutizira* (elopement) | The young woman goes to the man's home without the usual negotiations; the families then negotiate | Lawful if both are adults and consent; bride wealth is usually still paid |
| *Kuganha* | A woman goes to a man's home of her own accord and offers to marry him | Lawful only if both are adults and both consent |
| *Kutema ugariri* | A poor young man works for his in-laws instead of paying bride wealth | Rare today; lawful if both are adults and consent |
| *Chimutsamapfihwa* | A relative of a deceased wife (often her sister or niece) marries the widower to care for the children | Lawful only with the free consent of an adult woman |
| *Kugara nhaka* | A relative of a deceased husband takes on the widow as a wife | Lawful only with the widow's free consent; she may refuse |
| *Kuzvarira* | Pledging a girl in marriage, sometimes as a child or to settle a debt or dispute | **Illegal**: child marriage and forced marriage |
| *Musengabere* | Seizing a woman by force to marry her | **Illegal**: abduction and forced marriage |

Practices that involved children or a lack of consent were harmful. Today they are against the law, and anyone who knows of a child being pledged or forced into marriage should report it to a trusted adult, a teacher or the police.

## The value of marriage

Marriage is valued in Zimbabwean communities because it:

- gives **dignity** and respect to the couple, who are seen as responsible adults;
- builds **cooperation**, both between husband and wife and between two families who become in-laws;
- provides a stable home for raising and socialising **children**;
- ensures the **continuity** of the family name, clan and heritage;
- creates a **support network**, since in-laws help each other at funerals, ceremonies and in hard times.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing roora/lobola as buying a wife"}
Bride wealth is a token of respect and a bond between families. Describe it accurately, and if you discuss commercialisation, present it as a debate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the counsellors"}
The bride's counsellor is her aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*); the groom's is usually his uncle (*sekuru/umalume*). The go-between (*munyai/umkhongi*) negotiates; he is not a counsellor.
:::

:::mistake{title="Presenting harmful practices as normal"}
*Kuzvarira* and *musengabere* must be described as harmful and illegal, not simply as "forms of marriage".
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An indigenous marriage is arranged through a go-between (*munyai/umkhongi*) who negotiates bride wealth (*roora/lobola*) with the bride's family.
- The bride is handed over to her husband's family (*kuperekwa/umthimba*), escorted by her aunt.
- Aunts (*vatete/ubabakazi*) and uncles (*sekuru/umalume*) are marriage counsellors before, during and after the marriage.
- Other forms of indigenous marriage existed; pledging girls (*kuzvarira*) and forced marriage (*musengabere*) are harmful and illegal.
- Marriage brings dignity, cooperation, a home for children and the continuity of the family.
:::
