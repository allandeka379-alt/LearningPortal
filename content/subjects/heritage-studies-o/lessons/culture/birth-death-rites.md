## Introduction

Every family marks the arrival of a child and the death of a loved one. When a baby is born, relatives visit with gifts and the elders discuss the child's name. When someone dies, the news travels fast, neighbours gather, and for days the home is full of people cooking, singing and sitting with the bereaved. Months later, some families hold a ceremony to "bring home" the spirit of the person who has died.

These **rites of birth and death** carry deep meaning. They express what a community believes about life, family and the ancestors, and they teach the young how to behave at the most important moments of life. This lesson describes the main indigenous practices, how they vary between communities and faiths, and why they matter. Treat every practice and every faith with equal respect: there is no single "correct" way to welcome a child or bury the dead. You should already know about community gatherings and the roles of family members.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rite"}
A set of actions with a set meaning, carried out at an important moment. Rites of birth and death mark the start and the end of life.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceremony"}
A formal gathering at which a rite is carried out, often with songs, food and speeches.
:::

:::definition{term="Mourning"}
The period and practices by which the bereaved express grief and show respect for the dead, such as wearing mourning clothes or avoiding celebrations.
:::

:::definition{term="Kurova guva / umbuyiso"}
The ceremony held some time after burial, often about a year later, to bring the spirit of the deceased home as an ancestor who watches over the family. Matters of inheritance are often settled at this time.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Terms to recognise"}
*Masungiro*: in many Shona families, the custom of a woman going to her parents' home for the birth of her first child. *Nyamukuta*: a traditional midwife (Shona). *Vakwasha / abakhwenyana*: sons-in-law, who carry out many duties at a funeral.
:::

## Indigenous birth rites

### Care of the mother before and after birth

Pregnancy was treated with care. Older women advised the expectant mother on diet, rest and behaviour, and in many Shona families a first-time mother went to her own parents' home to give birth (*masungiro*), accompanied by gifts from her husband's family. A traditional midwife (*nyamukuta*) or experienced older women helped at the birth. Afterwards, mother and baby stayed indoors for a period, often until the umbilical cord fell off, so they could rest and be protected from infection and harm. Relatives cooked for the mother and did her work.

### Naming the child

Names were chosen with care by the father, grandparents or other elders. A name often records the family's feelings or circumstances at the time of the birth, or honours a relative.

| Name | Language | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tendai | Shona | Be thankful |
| Rudo | Shona | Love |
| Tafadzwa | Shona | We have been made happy |
| Sipho | Ndebele | Gift |
| Thandeka | Ndebele | Loved one |
| Themba | Ndebele | Hope |

### Protecting the child

Families took steps to protect the baby, such as keeping strangers away in the first days, carrying out family rituals and, in some families, using protective charms or herbs. These are cultural practices; for a baby's health, modern families also take the child to a clinic for check-ups and immunisation, as the Ministry of Health and Child Care advises.

### Birth in other faiths and today

Christian families may hold a baptism, dedication or thanksgiving service. In many Muslim families the baby is named on the seventh day after birth. Most babies are now born in clinics and hospitals, and parents must register the birth with the Registrar General to obtain a birth certificate, which a child needs for school and an identity document later.

## Indigenous death rites

| Stage | Common indigenous practice | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Announcing the death | Wailing and messengers sent to relatives; today also phone calls and messages | Gathers relatives and neighbours quickly |
| Preparing the body | Washed and dressed by senior relatives; kept in the main hut | Respect for the dead |
| The wake | Relatives and neighbours gather, sing and sit with the bereaved through the night | Comfort and support |
| Burial | Sons-in-law (*vakwasha/abakhwenyana*) dig the grave and help with the burial; some belongings may be buried with the person | Honours the dead and prepares the journey to the ancestors |
| Cleansing | Mourners wash their hands, often at the homestead entrance, after the burial | Marks the end of contact with death |
| Mourning | Widow or widower wears mourning clothes; the family avoids celebrations for a time | Shows grief and respect |
| Bringing home | *Kurova guva / umbuyiso*, often about a year later; the deceased's belongings may be shared out | The spirit is welcomed back as an ancestor; inheritance is settled |

Customs differ between communities. Among different groups the details of burial (for example, how the grave is prepared and who leads) and the timing and form of the later ceremony vary, and some families hold no bringing-home ceremony at all.

:::example{title="A funeral in a village near Bindura"}
Tatenda's grandfather dies at home.

1. The family wails and phones relatives in Harare and abroad; neighbours arrive within hours.
2. Women cook for the mourners; men collect firewood; the sons-in-law dig the grave.
3. On the third day the body is buried after prayers led by the church, followed by speeches from the family.
4. Mourners wash their hands before entering the homestead.
5. A year later, the family debates whether to hold *kurova guva*: some relatives want it, while church members prefer a memorial service and the unveiling of a tombstone.

The example shows indigenous and Christian practices side by side, and a family negotiating between them respectfully.
:::

### Death rites in other faiths

Many Christian families hold a funeral service with hymns and prayers, and later a memorial service and tombstone unveiling. Some churches discourage *kurova guva*, while some Christian families hold both. Muslim families usually bury the dead quickly, often within a day, after washing the body and wrapping it in white cloth, with prayers at the mosque or graveside.

## Significance of birth and death rites

- **Identity**: a name links the child to the family and its history.
- **Unity and support**: neighbours and relatives share work and grief, an expression of Unhu.
- **Respect**: for the mother and baby, for the dead and for elders.
- **Belief**: for many families, the rites keep the link between the living and the ancestors (*midzimu/amadlozi*).
- **Education**: children learn how to behave at births and funerals by watching and helping.
- **Order**: the bringing-home ceremony settles inheritance and responsibilities in the family.

## Threats to birth and death rites

- **Urbanisation and migration**: families live far apart, homes are small, and relatives cannot always travel.
- **Cost**: funerals, transport and food have become expensive; burial societies and funeral policies help, but not everyone can afford them.
- **Religious change**: some faiths reject certain practices.
- **Modern institutions**: hospitals, mortuaries and funeral companies take over tasks once done by relatives.
- **Public health rules**: during the COVID-19 pandemic, limits on gatherings meant many funerals were small.

:::example{title="Reading a change"}
A burial society in a Mutare suburb collects a small monthly amount from each member. When a member's mother dies, the society pays for the coffin and transport and members cook at the funeral.

The **form** has changed (a society with a bank account), but the **value** is the indigenous one: neighbours share the cost and the work of mourning.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ranking beliefs"}
Do not describe indigenous rites as "superstition" or one faith as better than another. Describe each practice and its meaning respectfully.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up burial and kurova guva"}
Burial happens within days of death. *Kurova guva/umbuyiso* is a separate ceremony held later, often about a year after the death.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing rather than explaining significance"}
"People cook at funerals" is description. Significance explains why: shared work comforts the bereaved and strengthens unity.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Birth rites cared for mother and baby (for example *masungiro* and a period indoors), named the child with meaning and protected the child.
- Death rites include announcing the death, the wake, burial with roles for sons-in-law, cleansing, mourning and, later, *kurova guva/umbuyiso*.
- Practices differ between communities and faiths; Christian and Muslim families have their own rites.
- The rites give identity, unity, respect, education and, for many, a link with the ancestors.
- Urbanisation, cost, religious change and modern institutions threaten them, but many families adapt them.
:::
