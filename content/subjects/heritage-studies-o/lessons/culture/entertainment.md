## Introduction

Before television and phones, evenings in a Zimbabwean village were not silent. Children gathered around the fire while a grandmother told the story of Hare and the other animals, pausing so they could sing the chorus. Older children tested each other with riddles. In the day, girls played *nhodo* with small stones and boys moved seeds across holes in the ground in *tsoro*. At ceremonies, drums, *mbira* and *hosho* played while people danced.

This was **indigenous entertainment**. It was fun, but it was never *only* fun: almost every story, game, song and dance taught a lesson, built a skill or brought people together. In this lesson you learn the main forms and, just as importantly, the other uses they served. You should already know what socialisation is and how the family passes on values.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Entertainment"}
Activities that people enjoy in their free time, such as stories, games, music and dance.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous entertainment"}
The forms of entertainment that developed among Zimbabwe's own communities, passed on by word of mouth and example from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Folklore"}
The traditional stories, riddles, proverbs, songs and sayings of a people, passed on orally.
:::

:::definition{term="Folk tale (ngano / inganekwane)"}
A traditional story, often with animal characters and a song, told to entertain and to teach a lesson.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Terms to learn"}
Folk tales: *ngano* (Shona), *inganekwane* (Ndebele). Riddles: *zvirahwe* (Shona), *iziphicaphicwano* (Ndebele). Proverbs: *tsumo* (Shona), *izaga* (Ndebele).
:::

## Folklore and storytelling

Folk tales were usually told in the evening by grandparents. Many feature animals with human qualities: the clever Hare (*Tsuro/uMvundla*), the greedy Hyena, the slow but patient Tortoise. Listeners join in a song that runs through the story, so everybody takes part.

Each tale carries a message. A hyena that is punished for greed teaches that greed brings shame; a hare that tricks a stronger animal teaches that wit can beat strength, but also that tricks can backfire. Children learn values without being lectured.

**Riddles** sharpen thinking: the challenger gives a puzzling description and the other person must find the answer. **Proverbs** sum up wisdom in a few words and are used by adults in advice and in the court of elders.

:::example{title="An evening story in Hwange"}
Gogo tells Nyasha and her cousins a story about Hyena, who eats all the food meant for the village and then cannot hide his swollen stomach.

1. The children sing the chorus each time Hyena eats more.
2. They laugh when he is caught.
3. Gogo asks what Hyena should have done. The children answer: share the food.

In one story the children were **entertained**, took part in **song**, and learned the value of **sharing**, an attribute of Unhu.
:::

## Games

| Game | How it is played (in outline) | Skills and values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Nhodo* | Players toss a stone in the air and pick up other stones from a hole before catching it | Hand-eye coordination, concentration, counting |
| *Tsoro / isafuba* | A board game of rows of holes in the ground or on a board; players move seeds or stones and capture the opponent's | Planning, strategy, mental arithmetic |
| *Mahumbwe* | Children play at running a home, copying the roles of adults | Learning adult roles and responsibilities (in outline) |
| Wrestling and running games | Contests of strength and speed among boys and girls | Fitness, courage, fair play |
| Singing and clapping games | Groups chant, clap and move in turns | Rhythm, cooperation, language |

Games were played with materials that were free and close at hand: stones, seeds, sticks and the ground itself.

## Song, dance and music

Songs accompanied almost every activity: work songs at a *nhimbe/ilima* kept the rhythm of hoeing; lullabies calmed babies; praise songs honoured clans and leaders; and songs at ceremonies expressed joy, grief or prayer. Dances were performed at harvest festivals, weddings, funerals, rain-making and other ceremonies, and each community had its own dances.

| Instrument | Description | Common uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Mbira* | Metal keys fixed to a wooden board, often placed in a gourd to make the sound louder | Ceremonies such as *bira*, entertainment, storytelling in song |
| Drums (*ngoma*) | Wooden body with a skin stretched over the top | Dances, ceremonies, gatherings, sending messages |
| *Hosho* | Gourd shakers filled with seeds | Keeping rhythm with mbira and drums |
| Horns and whistles | For example, the antelope-horn trumpets of Tonga *ngoma buntibe* music | Ceremonies and dances |

Music and dance were community events. Everybody could join: by singing, clapping, ululating or dancing.

## Other uses of indigenous entertainment

Examiners often ask for the **purposes** or **importance** of entertainment. Learn these:

- **Education**: stories, riddles and proverbs taught history, values and problem-solving; games taught counting and strategy.
- **Socialisation**: children learned adult roles and how to behave (for example through *mahumbwe*).
- **Passing on values**: tales showed the results of greed, laziness or pride and rewarded honesty and sharing.
- **Exercise and health**: dancing, running and wrestling kept people fit.
- **Identity and unity**: shared songs, dances and stories gave a community a sense of who it was.
- **Relaxation and relief**: after hard work, entertainment brought rest and joy, and songs at funerals helped people express grief.
- **Worship and ceremony**: music and dance were part of religious ceremonies.

:::example{title="A tsoro match in Chinhoyi"}
Farai's grandfather teaches him *tsoro* on holes dug in the ground.

1. Farai loses the first three games because he moves without planning.
2. His grandfather shows him how to count seeds ahead and think about his opponent's reply.
3. By the end of the week Farai wins a game.

Farai has been **entertained**, but he has also practised **mental arithmetic** and **planning**, and spent time learning from an elder.
:::

## Indigenous entertainment today

Television, phones, music streaming and modern sport now compete with indigenous entertainment, especially in towns. Some people argue that this is a loss of heritage; others point out that *mbira* music is recorded and performed worldwide, that schools teach traditional dance and that stories are told on radio and online in local languages. Both views can be supported.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing only the fun"}
A question on the importance of entertainment wants its other purposes: education, socialisation, values, exercise and identity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up instruments"}
The *mbira* has metal keys; drums have skins; *hosho* are shakers. Describe each correctly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing folk tales and proverbs"}
A folk tale (*ngano/inganekwane*) is a story; a proverb (*tsumo/izaga*) is a short saying; a riddle (*zvirahwe/iziphicaphicwano*) is a puzzle.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous entertainment includes folklore (folk tales, riddles, proverbs), games, song, dance and music.
- Folk tales are *ngano/inganekwane*; riddles are *zvirahwe/iziphicaphicwano*; proverbs are *tsumo/izaga*.
- Games such as *nhodo* and *tsoro/isafuba* build coordination, counting and strategy.
- Instruments include the *mbira*, drums and *hosho*.
- Entertainment also educated, socialised, passed on values, kept people fit and built identity and unity.
:::
