## Introduction

Every life passes through stages: birth, childhood, adolescence, adulthood, marriage, old age and death. Most societies mark the move from one stage to the next with a ceremony or a period of teaching. In Zimbabwe a baby may be formally introduced to the family, a young person may be taken aside by an aunt or uncle for guidance at puberty, a couple marries with ceremonies on both sides, and the dead are honoured with funeral rites.

These are **rites of passage**. This Form 1 lesson looks at the rites linked to **adolescence**, the move from childhood towards adulthood. It explains what they teach and why they matter, and then turns to something every young person needs to know: how the law protects children through the age of consent and the minimum age of marriage. Birth and death rites have their own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rite of passage"}
A ceremony, ritual or period of instruction that marks a person's move from one stage of life to another, such as from childhood to adulthood.
:::

:::definition{term="Adolescence"}
The stage between childhood and adulthood, beginning with puberty, when the body matures and a young person develops a more adult identity.
:::

:::definition{term="Initiation"}
A formal rite in which young people are taught the knowledge, values and responsibilities of adults, often in a group and away from the home for a period.
:::

:::definition{term="Age of consent"}
The age below which the law treats a person as a child who cannot lawfully agree to sexual activity. The law exists to protect children.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The law in Zimbabwe"}
- **Marriage:** no person under 18 may marry (section 78 of the Constitution). In 2016 the Constitutional Court confirmed that child marriage is unconstitutional.
- **Sexual activity:** the law protects children under 18. A 2022 Constitutional Court ruling led to the criminal law being amended in 2024 to raise the age of consent to 18.
- **Adulthood:** a person becomes a legal adult at 18 (Legal Age of Majority Act, 1982).
:::

## Rites of adolescence in Zimbabwe

Communities in Zimbabwe mark adolescence in different ways. Some hold formal initiation; in others, guidance is given privately within the family. The examples below are described in general terms; details are private to the communities that practise them and are not given here.

| Community | Practice (in outline) | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Many Shona and Ndebele families | Private guidance at puberty from the paternal aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*) for girls and from uncles or grandfathers (*sekuru/umalume*) for boys | Teaching about growing up, behaviour, respect, hygiene and future family responsibilities |
| Venda communities | Initiation for girls (such as *vhusha* at puberty) and an initiation school for boys (*murundu*) | Teaching adult roles, discipline, songs and community values |
| Remba (Lemba) communities | Male circumcision as part of an initiation that marks entry into adulthood | Identity, religious and cultural tradition, adult responsibility |

Other communities have their own customs. Where you do not know a group's practice, it is better to say so than to guess.

### What rites of adolescence teach

- **Body and health:** understanding puberty and personal hygiene, age-appropriately.
- **Behaviour and values:** respect for elders, self-control, honesty, hard work, Unhu.
- **Responsibilities:** duties to the family and community as an adult.
- **Identity:** pride in one's culture, songs, history and group.
- **Preparation for marriage and family life**, in the future, as an adult.

## Significance and implications

### Positive significance

- They give young people **guidance** from trusted elders at a confusing time.
- They build **identity and belonging**, often with others of the same age.
- They pass on **culture**: language, songs, history, values.
- They mark a clear change of **status and responsibility**.

### Concerns and changes

- Some practices have carried **health risks** when done without trained health workers; health authorities work with communities to make them safer.
- Initiation can clash with the **school calendar**; some communities have adjusted timing.
- Any practice that pushes children towards **early marriage** or harm is unlawful. Practices such as pledging a girl in marriage (*kuzvarira*) or forcing a girl to marry are illegal and harmful.
- In towns, families often rely more on schools, churches and youth programmes for guidance than on traditional rites.

:::example{title="Guidance from vatete in Mutare"}
Chipo, aged 13, has started puberty. Her paternal aunt visits.

1. **The rite:** her aunt spends time with her privately, as is the custom in many Shona families.
2. **What is taught:** how her body is changing, personal hygiene, respect for herself and others, the importance of school, and that she can come to her aunt with any problem.
3. **The protective message:** her aunt tells her that no one may touch her without her consent, that the law protects girls under 18, and that she should tell a trusted adult if anyone tries.
4. **Significance:** Chipo gains knowledge and confidence, and a trusted adult outside her parents.
:::

## Why the age of consent and the marriage age matter

The law draws a clear line at 18 to protect children because:

- Children are **not mature** enough, physically or emotionally, to make decisions about sex and marriage.
- Early pregnancy carries **serious health risks** for girls whose bodies are still developing.
- Child marriage usually ends a girl's **education**, limiting her future and income.
- Children can be **pressured or exploited** by older people; the law places the responsibility on the adult.
- It upholds **children's rights** under the Constitution and international agreements such as the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child.

:::example{title="A community responds to a child marriage"}
In a fictional ward near Gokwe, a family arranges for a 15-year-old girl to marry an older man.

1. **The law:** this is illegal; no one under 18 may marry, and the man commits a crime.
2. **Who can act:** a teacher who notices the girl is missing from school, a village head, the police Victim Friendly Unit, or a child protection committee.
3. **Outcome:** the marriage is stopped, the girl returns to school and receives support, and the community holds a meeting about the law.
4. **Lesson:** culture and the law can work together. Many traditional leaders now speak out against child marriage.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing age of consent and age of majority"}
The age of majority (18) is when a person becomes a legal adult. The age of consent concerns sexual activity. Both are now 18.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing one community's rite as Zimbabwean in general"}
Practices differ. Name the community and say "in many families" or "in some communities".
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating harmful practices as acceptable culture"}
Child marriage and pledging girls are harmful and illegal. Say so clearly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A rite of passage marks a move from one stage of life to another; adolescence rites mark the move towards adulthood.
- Practices vary: private guidance by aunts and uncles in many Shona and Ndebele families, initiation among some communities such as the Venda and Remba.
- They teach values, responsibilities, identity and culture, but harmful practices must change.
- No one under 18 may marry (section 78; confirmed by the Constitutional Court in 2016), and the law protects children under 18 from sexual activity.
- These laws protect children's health, education and rights; children at risk can turn to a trusted adult, the school, the police Victim Friendly Unit or a clinic.
:::
