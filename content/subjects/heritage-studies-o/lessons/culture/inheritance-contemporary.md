## Introduction

When Mr Ncube, a bus driver in Bulawayo, died suddenly, his widow and three children were still grieving when relatives arrived from the rural areas. Within a week the family car, the furniture and the title papers to the house had been taken "for safe keeping". The widow did not know her rights. A neighbour told her to go to the Master's office at the courts. Months later, the estate was wound up according to the law, and the house and property went to the widow and children.

This lesson follows on from indigenous inheritance. Families today often own houses, cars, bank accounts, pensions and businesses, and many live far from the extended family. The law now sets out how a dead person's property is passed on, and the most important tool a person has is a **will**. You will learn what a will is, what happens when there is none, the role of the Master of the High Court, the harm done by property-grabbing, and how contemporary and indigenous practices compare.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Will"}
A written document in which a person states how his or her property is to be shared after death and who should manage the estate. The person who makes it is the **testator**.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiary"}
A person who receives property or benefits from an estate.
:::

:::definition{term="Executor"}
The person appointed to collect the deceased's property, pay the debts and share what is left among the beneficiaries. An executor can be named in a will or appointed through the Master of the High Court.
:::

:::definition{term="Intestate"}
Dying **without a valid will**. The estate is then shared according to the law.
:::

:::definition{term="Property-grabbing"}
Relatives or others seizing a dead person's property, often from the widow, widower or children, without lawful authority.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The Master of the High Court"}
Deceased estates are reported to and supervised by the Master of the High Court; in many districts the magistrates court handles this work for the Master. The Master's office registers estates, oversees executors and makes sure property is shared lawfully.
:::

## Wills

### Why a will matters

- It makes the testator's **wishes clear**, so there is less room for quarrels.
- It **protects the spouse and children**, who can show the will to anyone who tries to take property.
- It speeds up the **winding up** of the estate.
- It allows the testator to provide for particular people: a child with a disability, an elderly parent, a church or a charity.

### Making a valid will (in outline)

- It is **written**, not just spoken.
- It is **signed** by the testator, normally at the end of each page.
- It is signed by **two witnesses**, who see the testator sign. It is safest that a witness is not someone who benefits from the will.
- The testator must be of **sound mind** and make the will freely, without pressure.
- It should be kept safely, and someone trusted should know where it is. A lawyer can help, but a will does not have to be drawn up by one.

:::example{title="A will written in Kariba"}
Tafadzwa, a fisherman in Kariba, owns a house, a boat and some cattle in his home village.

1. He writes a will leaving the house to his wife, the boat to be used to earn income for the children's education, and the cattle to be shared among his four children.
2. He names his sister as executor.
3. He signs it in front of two neighbours, who also sign. Neither is a beneficiary.
4. He tells his wife and sister where the will is kept.

If Tafadzwa dies, his wishes are clear and written down, and anyone who tries to take the boat can be shown the will.
:::

## Inheriting without a will

When a person dies intestate, the estate is shared according to the law. In outline:

- The **surviving spouse** (or spouses) and the **children** are the main beneficiaries.
- Since changes to the law in the late 1990s, this applies to estates governed by customary law as well, and the surviving spouse has strong rights to the matrimonial home and household goods.
- An **heir** may still be named by the family for customary purposes (the name, ceremonies, family leadership), but this does not give him ownership of the property.
- If there is no spouse or child, other relatives such as parents and siblings may inherit.

The exact shares are set out in the law and applied by the Master's office; they depend on the size of the estate and the family's circumstances.

## Property-grabbing

Property-grabbing is one of the most serious inheritance problems in Zimbabwe. It most often hurts widows and orphans.

| Effect on the widow or widower | Effect on the children | Effect on society |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Loss of home and income; poverty | School drop-out because fees cannot be paid | More poverty and destitution |
| Stress and conflict with in-laws | Children may be separated from the surviving parent | Breakdown of family trust |
| Some forced into risky ways of earning a living | Child labour or early marriage in the worst cases | Pressure on social services and charities |

Taking or dealing with a deceased estate's property without lawful authority is against the law. Victims can go to the Master of the High Court, the courts, the police or legal aid organisations, and the courts can order property returned.

:::example{title="Stopping a property grab"}
After Chipo's husband dies in Marondera, his brother takes the car and announces that he will live in the house.

1. **Step 1:** Chipo reports the death and the estate to the Master's office (here, at the magistrates court), with the death certificate and marriage certificate.
2. **Step 2:** An executor is appointed and takes charge of all the property, including the car.
3. **Step 3:** Under the law, Chipo and her children are the main beneficiaries; the brother has no right to the house.
4. **Step 4:** With help from a legal aid organisation, Chipo obtains a court order and the car is returned.
:::

## Comparing indigenous and contemporary inheritance

| Feature | Indigenous practice | Contemporary practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who decides | The extended family | The deceased (through a will) or the law |
| Main beneficiaries | The heir, often a male relative, on behalf of the family | Surviving spouse and children |
| Women | Often excluded from property | Equal rights of widows and daughters protected by law |
| Record | Unwritten custom | Written will; estate registered with the Master |
| Supervision | Elders and the family | The Master of the High Court and the courts |
| Strength | Collective care, continuity of name and values | Clear rights, legal protection, fewer disputes |
| Weakness | Depended on the heir's honesty | Costs, delays, travel to offices; many people still do not write wills |

Many families now combine both. They hold *kurova guva/umbuyiso* and name a customary heir to carry the family name, while the property is shared through the Master's office according to the will or the law.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking the customary heir owns everything"}
A customary heir may carry the name, but under the law the spouse and children are the main beneficiaries of property.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a will must be written by a lawyer"}
A lawyer helps, but any person of sound mind can make a valid will if it is written, signed and properly witnessed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing percentages"}
Do not quote exact shares unless you are sure. Say that the law sets the shares and the Master applies them.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A will is a written, signed and witnessed document stating how a person's property is to be shared after death.
- Without a will (intestate), the law makes the surviving spouse and children the main beneficiaries.
- The Master of the High Court, often working through magistrates courts, supervises estates and executors.
- Property-grabbing harms widows, widowers and orphans and is unlawful; victims can turn to the Master, the courts, the police and legal aid.
- Contemporary practice gives clearer legal rights, especially to women, while indigenous practice offered collective care; many families combine both.
:::
