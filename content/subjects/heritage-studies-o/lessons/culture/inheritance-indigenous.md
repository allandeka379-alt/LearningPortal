## Introduction

Some months after the funeral of a respected grandfather in a village near Gutu, his relatives gather again. The family's elders, the late man's sisters and brothers, his widow and his children sit together. By the end of the day it is agreed who will take his name and responsibilities, which cattle go to whom, and who will look after his youngest children's school fees.

Every family must answer the same question when a member dies: what happens to what he or she leaves behind, and who takes up the duties? Indigenous Zimbabwean families developed detailed customs to answer it. This lesson explains those customs: the meaning of inheritance and heirship, what can be inherited, how an heir was chosen and an estate shared, and how well these practices worked. The next lesson covers wills and the law in contemporary families.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inheritance"}
Whatever is passed on to the living when a person dies: property, rights, duties, a name, a position and knowledge.
:::

:::definition{term="Heir"}
The person chosen to succeed the deceased, taking over his or her name, position or responsibilities, and often a share of the property. In Shona the heir is often called *mugari wenhaka*; in Ndebele, *indlalifa*.
:::

:::definition{term="Heirship"}
The position, rights and duties of an heir, and the customs by which an heir is chosen.
:::

:::definition{term="Estate"}
Everything a dead person owned or was owed (property, livestock, money) and also what he or she owed to others.
:::

:::definition{term="Tangible and intangible inheritance"}
**Tangible** inheritance can be seen and touched: land-use rights, livestock, a homestead, tools, household goods. **Intangible** inheritance cannot be touched: a name, a totem, a language, norms and values, a family history, a traditional office such as a chieftainship.
:::

## What is inherited

| Tangible inheritance | Intangible inheritance |
| --- | --- |
| Cattle, goats and other livestock | The family name and the deceased's name taken by the heir |
| Rights to use fields and the homestead | The totem and clan identity |
| Granaries and stored crops | Language, norms, values and customs |
| Tools, weapons, ceremonial items | Family history and knowledge, e.g. of herbs or crafts |
| Household goods, clothing | Traditional offices such as chieftainship, passed by custom within a ruling family |

In indigenous society land was not owned as private property and sold; families held rights to use land allocated through the chief or headman. The heir took over the household's land-use rights and responsibilities.

## Indigenous inheritance and heirship practices

Practices differed between communities, and patrilineal customs (where descent is traced through the father) were the most common. The main features were these.

### Choosing the heir

- The heir was usually a **male relative**: often the eldest son, or a brother of the deceased if the sons were young. In some families a nephew or another relative was chosen.
- The choice was made by the **extended family**, with the deceased's paternal aunts and uncles playing key roles, not by one person alone.
- A dead person's wishes, spoken before death, were respected where possible.

### Sharing the estate

- The sharing of the estate is called *kugova nhaka* in Shona. Among many Shona families it took place at the ceremony held some months after the burial (*kurova guva*; the Ndebele equivalent is *umbuyiso*), when the deceased's spirit was believed to be brought home.
- Personal items were shared among children and relatives; livestock and other property were held by the heir for the benefit of the whole household.

### The heir's duties

The heir was not simply a person who gained property. He took on **responsibilities**:

- caring for the widow or widows and the children, including feeding them and paying for schooling or marriage costs;
- keeping the family together and settling disputes;
- representing the family at gatherings and in ceremonies;
- looking after livestock and fields for the household's benefit, not for himself.

In the past, some communities also practised widow inheritance, in which a relative of the deceased could take the widow as a wife to provide for her and her children. Today it cannot be forced on anyone: a widow has the right to choose, and the practice also carries a risk of spreading HIV, so health workers advise against it.

:::example{title="Choosing an heir in a family near Chivhu"}
Mr Moyo dies, leaving a widow, two sons aged 12 and 9, and a daughter aged 16.

1. **The problem:** the sons are too young to run the household.
2. **The family meeting:** paternal relatives, the widow and the children's aunt meet after the funeral.
3. **The decision:** the late man's younger brother is chosen as heir until the eldest son is grown; he takes care of the cattle and promises to pay school fees for all three children.
4. **Intangible inheritance:** the eldest son is told he will one day take his father's name and responsibilities; the children keep their father's totem.

The family has used custom to protect the children. The case also shows a weakness: the arrangement depends on the brother's honesty.
:::

## Strengths and weaknesses

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- |
| Widows and children were not left alone; someone had a duty to care for them | Daughters and widows were often left out of the sharing of property |
| Property stayed in the family and kept supporting it | It relied on the heir's honesty; some heirs took property for themselves |
| Decisions were made collectively, which reduced conflict | Decisions could be disputed when relatives disagreed, causing long quarrels |
| Names, totems, offices and values were passed on | Widow inheritance could be unwelcome and carried health risks |
| Continuity: the family's place in the community was maintained | Customs were unwritten, so they could be bent by powerful relatives |

:::example{title="Intangible inheritance in action"}
Rudo's grandmother in Mutoko was known for her knowledge of traditional songs and her skill in basket-weaving.

1. Before she died she taught Rudo the songs and the weaving patterns.
2. Rudo inherited no cattle, but she inherited knowledge and skill: **intangible inheritance**.
3. Today Rudo teaches the patterns at her school's culture club, passing the inheritance on.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking inheritance is only about property"}
Names, totems, language, values and offices are inherited too. Always mention intangible inheritance.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the heir as an owner"}
In indigenous practice the heir held property on behalf of the family and had heavy duties. Explain the duties, not only the gains.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing every community as the same"}
Customs varied. Use words such as "often", "usually" and "in many communities".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inheritance is whatever passes to the living at a death; an heir succeeds to the deceased's name, position and duties.
- Tangible inheritance (livestock, land-use rights, goods) can be touched; intangible inheritance (name, totem, language, values, office) cannot.
- In indigenous families the extended family chose an heir, usually a male relative, and shared the estate (*kugova nhaka*), often at *kurova guva/umbuyiso*.
- The heir had duties to care for widows and children and keep the family together.
- Strengths: security, collective decisions and continuity. Weaknesses: women often excluded, reliance on the heir's honesty, disputes, and practices such as forced widow inheritance.
:::
