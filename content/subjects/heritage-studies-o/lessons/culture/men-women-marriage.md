## Introduction

In Nokuthula's home in Gweru, her mother is a nurse who works night shifts, and her father, a teacher, cooks supper on those nights and checks the children's homework. At her grandparents' homestead in Gwanda, her grandmother still runs the kitchen and her grandfather speaks for the family at village meetings. Both households are happy, and both are Zimbabwean. The difference shows how the roles of men and women in marriage have changed over two generations.

This lesson compares roles in indigenous and contemporary marriages, explains why they are changing and sets out what the law says about husbands and wives. It builds on what you know about the family as an agent of socialisation and about marriage practices. Gender equality in society generally is studied in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Role"}
The part a person is expected to play in a group, such as a husband, wife, parent or child.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsibility"}
A duty a person is expected to carry out, such as providing food, caring for children or keeping the home safe.
:::

:::definition{term="Shared responsibility"}
A duty that both spouses carry out together or take turns to do, such as earning income, caring for children or making decisions about money.
:::

:::definition{term="Equality of spouses"}
The principle that husband and wife have equal rights and obligations during the marriage and when it ends.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The law"}
- Section 26 of the Constitution (a national objective) says the State must make sure that no marriage takes place without the free and full consent of both people, and that spouses have **equal rights and obligations** during marriage and at its end.
- The **Legal Age of Majority Act of 1982** made every person a legal adult at 18. Before it, many African women were treated as minors under customary law, whatever their age.
:::

## Roles in indigenous marriage

In indigenous society, roles were divided clearly between husband and wife. Each role was respected, and the household depended on both.

| Area | Husband | Wife |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership | Head of the household; spoke for the family at the court (*dare/inkundla*) | Managed the kitchen and the inner life of the home; advised her husband privately |
| Production | Cleared fields, herded cattle, hunted, built and repaired huts and granaries | Planted, weeded and harvested; cared for her own field (*tseu*); processed and stored food |
| Children | Taught boys herding, hunting and men's skills; disciplined the children | Cared for babies and young children; taught girls household skills |
| Protection | Defended the family and property | Cared for the sick and elderly; kept family secrets |
| Ceremonies | Led family rituals and represented the family at gatherings | Prepared food and beer for ceremonies; took part in songs and dances |

These roles were tied to a farming economy in which everybody's labour was needed. They gave each spouse clear duties and status. At the same time, men held most of the authority, and women usually had little say over cattle, land and major decisions.

## Roles in contemporary marriage

In many families today, roles are less fixed. Both spouses may work for wages, run businesses or farm, and both may cook, care for children and make decisions. Common patterns include:

- **Both spouses earn income**, and both contribute to rent, food and school fees.
- **Decisions are made jointly**, especially about money, children's schooling, property and family planning.
- **Childcare and housework are shared**, at least in part, especially when both work.
- **Property may be registered in both names**, such as a house or a plot.

Roles still vary widely. Many rural households keep a division of labour close to the indigenous one, and many urban households combine old and new patterns.

:::example{title="Two working parents in Harare"}
Tendai is a mechanic and Chipo runs a small clothing business from home.

1. They agree a monthly budget together, and both contribute.
2. Chipo collects the children from school because her hours are flexible; Tendai cooks on weekends.
3. They registered their house in both names.

Their marriage shows **shared responsibilities**: the duties are divided by agreement and circumstance, not only by sex.
:::

## Why roles are changing

| Cause | How it changes roles |
| --- | --- |
| Education | Girls and boys attend school; educated women enter professions and earn income |
| Employment | Wage work and business draw both spouses out of the home, so housework and childcare must be shared |
| The law | The Legal Age of Majority Act (1982), the Constitution (2013) and other laws give women equal rights in marriage, property and decisions |
| Urbanisation | In towns, families are smaller and live away from relatives who once helped with children, so spouses share more |
| Religion and the media | Teaching and examples of partnership in marriage spread new expectations |
| Economic pressure | Rising costs mean many households need two incomes |

## Effects of the changes

**Positive effects** include more income for families, better education for children, women's greater independence and confidence, and decisions that reflect both spouses' views.

**Challenges** include conflict when one spouse expects the old roles and the other expects the new; tiredness when a working wife still does all the housework (sometimes called the "double burden"); and less time for children. Some people feel that respect for the husband as head has weakened; others answer that respect can be mutual, and that partnership strengthens marriage.

:::example{title="Reading a disagreement"}
Farai tells his wife Rudo, who earns more than he does, that as head of the house he alone should decide how her salary is spent.

1. **Indigenous view he is drawing on:** the husband as head and main decision-maker.
2. **Contemporary and legal view:** spouses have equal rights and obligations; Rudo has a say over money she earns and over joint property.
3. **A balanced solution:** they agree a joint budget, so both incomes meet family needs and both are respected.

The case shows why a good answer explains both the older expectation and the present law.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying indigenous roles gave women no value"}
Women's roles in farming, food, children and ceremonies were essential and respected. The weakness was unequal authority, not a lack of value.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing equality with sameness"}
Equality of spouses means equal rights and obligations. It does not mean that husband and wife must do exactly the same tasks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining them"}
"Education" alone earns little. Explain the link: educated women find paid work, so both spouses share income and housework.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In indigenous marriage, husbands led the household, herded, built and protected; wives farmed, managed food and the kitchen and cared for children.
- In contemporary marriage, many spouses share income, housework, childcare and decisions.
- Roles are changing because of education, employment, the law, urbanisation, religion, the media and economic pressure.
- The Constitution (section 26) and the Legal Age of Majority Act (1982) support equal rights and obligations of spouses.
- Changing roles bring benefits and challenges; a good answer gives both.
:::
