## Introduction

Think about a family gathering at your grandparents' home. People greet one another with clapping and the right forms of address. Someone praises a relative using the family totem. The meal is sadza with relish, eaten from a shared plate. Later an uncle tells a folk tale, the children sing, and before everyone leaves the elders give advice about school and behaviour.

Almost everything in that scene is **culture**: the shared way of life a group passes from one generation to the next. This lesson gives an overview of the main features of indigenous Zimbabwean culture, the threads that run through the cultures of the Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Kalanga, Nambya, Shangani, Ndau, Sotho, Tswana, Chewa and other communities. Each feature (totems, ceremonies, marriage, religion) has its own lesson; here you see how they fit together.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Culture"}
The total way of life of a people: their language, beliefs, customs, norms, values, food, dress, arts, tools and institutions, learnt and passed on from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous culture"}
The culture that developed among the original African communities of a place over many generations, before and alongside later outside influences.
:::

:::definition{term="Kinship"}
The network of relationships by blood and marriage, with its duties and forms of address, that links members of the extended family and clan.
:::

:::definition{term="Material and non-material culture"}
**Material culture** is the things people make and use (pots, tools, houses, clothing, instruments). **Non-material culture** is the ideas, beliefs, norms, values and language that guide life.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Many cultures, shared features"}
The Constitution recognises 16 official languages, each tied to a cultural community. Indigenous Zimbabwean cultures differ in detail but share many features, such as the extended family, respect for elders, belief in ancestral spirits and communal values.
:::

## The main features

| Feature | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language | Carries identity, history and wisdom through proverbs, idioms, riddles and folk tales | Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Kalanga and other languages |
| Family and kinship | The extended family, with clear roles and forms of address | The aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*) and maternal uncle (*sekuru/umalume*) as advisers |
| Totems | Clan symbols, usually animals or body parts, with praise poetry | Totems such as the eland, the zebra and the monkey; totem praises at gatherings |
| Religion and beliefs | Belief in God (Mwari/uMlimo), ancestral spirits and territorial spirits | Family rituals, rain-making ceremonies |
| Food | Staple foods and traditional preparation | Sadza/isitshwala from maize, sorghum or millet; wild vegetables, mopane worms |
| Dress and adornment | Traditional clothing and ornaments | Skins and woven cloth in the past; beadwork; cloths worn at ceremonies |
| Music, dance and crafts | Instruments, songs, dances and skilled crafts | Mbira, drums and rattles (*hosho*); pottery, basketry, wood carving, stone sculpture |
| Social organisation and leadership | Chiefs, headmen and village heads, with councils of elders | Traditional courts settling disputes; community granaries |
| Values | Respect for elders, communal sharing, hospitality, Unhu | Work parties (*nhimbe/ilima*); caring for orphans within the family |

The features are connected. Totems link to kinship (people of the same totem do not marry); religion links to leadership (traditional leaders have ritual as well as political roles); values run through all of them.

## A closer look at four features

### Family and kinship

The **extended family** (grandparents, parents, uncles, aunts, cousins) is the basic unit. Each relative has duties: the aunt advises girls and helps in marriage matters; the maternal uncle advises boys and plays a role in ceremonies. Forms of address show respect and relationship. Orphans were traditionally cared for within the extended family, so no child was left alone.

### Social organisation and leadership

Communities were organised under **chiefs**, **headmen** and **village heads**, advised by councils of elders. Leaders settled disputes in traditional courts, allocated land and led ceremonies. Today traditional leaders are recognised in Chapter 15 of the Constitution and still play a role in rural communities.

### Communal values

Indigenous culture emphasises the group: work parties, shared harvests, and the chief's granary (*zunde ramambo/isiphala senkosi*), a field worked by the community whose harvest helped people in need. These are Unhu in practice.

### Arts and crafts

Music and dance accompany work, ceremonies and celebration. The **mbira** is closely linked with religious ceremonies; drums and rattles accompany dance. Crafts such as pottery, basketry and carving meet daily needs and display skill. Stone sculpture has made Zimbabwean artists known around the world.

:::example{title="Reading culture at a harvest celebration"}
In a village near Bindura, families celebrate a good harvest.

1. **Language and wisdom:** an elder opens with a proverb about working together.
2. **Kinship:** relatives arrive from town and are greeted in order of seniority.
3. **Religion:** thanks are given to God and to the ancestors.
4. **Food:** the first grain is cooked and shared.
5. **Arts:** drumming, singing and dancing follow.
6. **Values:** a share of the harvest is set aside for an elderly widow.

One event displays at least six features of culture at once.
:::

:::example{title="Material or non-material?"}
Thandeka sorts items for a school culture day in Gwanda.

1. A clay water pot, a wooden headrest and a beaded necklace: **material culture** (things made and used).
2. A folk tale, the rule that children greet visitors first, and a totem praise: **non-material culture** (ideas, norms and language).
3. The mbira is material culture, but the songs and the beliefs linked to its music are non-material: many features have both sides.
:::

## Why indigenous norms and values matter

- They give people **identity and belonging**: knowing your language, totem and family history tells you who you are.
- They provide **moral guidance** through proverbs, folk tales and elders' advice.
- They build **social security**: the extended family and communal values care for orphans, the elderly and the sick.
- They promote **unity and peace** through respect, dialogue and traditional dispute settlement.
- They preserve **knowledge** about farming, health, the environment and history.
- They are a **heritage** that can attract tourism and creative industries (music, crafts, sculpture).

Some indigenous practices have been criticised or changed because they conflict with human rights, such as forced or child marriage. Valuing a culture includes judging which practices to keep and which to change.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing one group as the whole of Zimbabwe"}
Zimbabwe has many cultures. Give examples from more than one group, and use Shona and Ndebele terms side by side where they exist.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing features without description"}
"Language, food, dress" earns identification marks only. Describe each: what it is and an example.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating culture as fixed"}
Culture changes over time. Indigenous culture today includes old and new elements, and that is normal.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe the main features** (Paper 2): name a feature, then give a sentence of description and an example. Four well-described features beat ten bare names.
:::

:::exam-tip
In **explain why norms and values matter**, link each value to its effect: communal sharing means orphans and the elderly are cared for.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Culture is the total way of life of a people, passed from generation to generation; it has material and non-material parts.
- Main features of indigenous Zimbabwean culture: language, family and kinship, totems, religion and beliefs, food, dress, music, dance and crafts, social organisation and leadership, and communal values.
- The features are connected: totems shape marriage, religion shapes leadership, values run through everything.
- Indigenous norms and values give identity, moral guidance, social security, unity and knowledge.
- Cultures change; some practices are kept, others changed where they harm people.
:::
