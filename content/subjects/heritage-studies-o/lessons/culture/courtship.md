## Introduction

Ask your grandmother how she met your grandfather and you may hear a story about a well, a long walk home from the fields, a string of beads and a meeting with her aunt. Ask an older cousin the same question and the answer may be a church youth camp, a college class or a message on a phone. Both stories describe **courtship**: the period in which a young man and a young woman get to know each other before deciding whether to marry.

Courtship matters in Heritage Studies because marriage in Zimbabwe has always joined families, not only two people. How a couple meets, who advises them and what they are taught about respect shape the marriage that follows. In this lesson you compare indigenous and contemporary courtship and weigh their strengths and weaknesses. You should already know the roles of family members such as aunts and uncles from your work on the family.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Courtship"}
The period and the practices by which two unmarried people get to know each other, with a view to marriage.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous courtship"}
Courtship as practised in Zimbabwe's communities before colonial and modern influences: guided by elders, carried out in known places and openly linked to the families of both young people.
:::

:::definition{term="Contemporary courtship"}
Courtship as it is commonly practised today, shaped by schooling, towns, religion, the media and technology, and usually chosen and managed by the couple themselves.
:::

:::definition{term="Intermediary (go-between)"}
A trusted person who carries messages between two people or two families. In courtship this was often an aunt, an uncle or a friend; in marriage negotiations it is the *munyai/umkhongi*.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Terms to recognise"}
In Shona, a young man courting a young woman is said to *pfimba* (to propose love). In Ndebele, *ukuqoma* describes a young woman choosing and accepting a suitor. *Chuma* (beads) were one well-known token of acceptance in Shona communities.
:::

## Indigenous courtship

Indigenous courtship differed in detail from one community to another, but most of Zimbabwe's groups shared these features.

### Meeting places

Young people met where daily work brought them together: at the river or well when fetching water, in the fields, on the way to the grinding stone, at a work party (*nhimbe/ilima*), at a beer party for elders, or at dances held under the moon. These were public places, so the community could see who was courting whom.

### Proposals and tokens of love

A young man showed interest with praise poetry, riddles and patient persuasion; a young woman was expected not to accept at once. When she accepted, she gave a token as a sign, such as beads, a bracelet or a small cloth. The token told the young man, and later his family, that she had agreed.

### Aunts, uncles and go-betweens

Courtship was guided. A girl reported a suitor to her aunt (*vatete/ubabakazi*), who checked the young man's family, character and totem and advised her on behaviour. A young man sought advice from his uncle (*sekuru/umalume*) or older brothers. Friends sometimes carried messages. Once the couple agreed to marry, the young man's family sent a formal go-between to open marriage talks; that stage belongs to marriage practices, not courtship.

### Rules of behaviour

Elders taught self-control and respect. Sexual relations before marriage were strongly discouraged, and the families of both young people kept watch. Couples were also checked for close kinship, since totems ruled out marriage within the clan.

| Feature | Indigenous courtship | Contemporary courtship |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where couples meet | River, well, fields, work parties, village dances | School, college, church, workplace, social events, social media and dating apps |
| Who guides the couple | Aunts, uncles, older siblings, the wider family | Mainly the couple; sometimes parents, church leaders or counsellors |
| Tokens of love | Beads, bracelets, a cloth, small gifts | Gifts, cards, phone messages, outings |
| Checks on the partner | Family background, character, totem, health | Often left to the couple; may be limited, especially online |
| Pace | Slow and public | Varies; can be fast and private |
| Main concern | Respect, family approval, preparation for marriage | Personal choice, love and compatibility |

## Contemporary courtship

Today courtship takes several forms, and many couples combine them.

- **Social courtship.** Couples meet at school, college, work, weddings, sports events or through friends, and get to know each other through outings and conversation.
- **Religious courtship.** Many churches, mosques and other faith communities teach rules for courtship, run youth fellowships where young people meet, and offer premarital counselling by religious leaders.
- **Western-influenced courtship.** Dating, gifts on special days, public displays of affection and choosing a partner without family involvement reflect influences from films, magazines and travel.
- **Online courtship.** Social media, messaging and dating apps let people meet across long distances, including Zimbabweans abroad.

:::example{title="Rudo and Farai meet online"}
Rudo, a nursing student in Bulawayo, begins chatting with Farai, who works in Mutare, after they meet in a church group on social media.

1. **Advantage:** they can communicate cheaply and often despite the distance, and they share a faith.
2. **Risk:** Rudo cannot see how Farai behaves at home or how he treats others, and photographs and stories online can be false.
3. **What Rudo does:** she tells her aunt, arranges to meet Farai in a public place with friends present, and lets her family meet him before the relationship becomes serious.

Rudo combines a modern way of meeting with an indigenous safeguard: involving a trusted elder.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages

### Indigenous courtship

**Advantages:** elders checked the families, character and totems, which reduced the risk of marrying a close relative or a person of bad character; the young people received advice on respect and family life; courtship was public, which discouraged deception; and it prepared the couple and both families for marriage.

**Disadvantages:** family pressure could limit a young person's free choice; some young people were pushed into relationships they did not want; and in some communities girls had less say than boys. Practices such as pledging a girl in marriage (*kuzvarira*) were harmful and are now illegal.

### Contemporary courtship

**Advantages:** couples choose freely and get to know each other well; many meet partners with similar education, faith and interests; technology connects people across distances; and many faith groups offer counselling.

**Disadvantages:** less family guidance means fewer checks on a partner's background; online relationships can involve deception, fraud and, at worst, exploitation; peer pressure and the media may push young people into early sexual relationships, with risks of unplanned pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections; and relationships may begin and end quickly.

:::example{title="Weighing the two in an answer"}
Question: *Which is better, indigenous or contemporary courtship?*

1. State one strength of indigenous courtship, explained: elders' checks protected young people.
2. State one strength of contemporary courtship, explained: free choice respects the rights of both partners.
3. Note a weakness of each: family pressure; lack of safeguards online.
4. Conclude: many families now combine free choice with the involvement of aunts, uncles and parents, which keeps the strengths of both.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The law and young people"}
No one under **18** may marry in Zimbabwe (section 78 of the Constitution), and the law protects children under 18 from sexual exploitation. Courtship should never involve pressure, threats or payment.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up courtship and marriage procedures"}
Courtship is getting to know each other. Sending a go-between to negotiate *roora/lobola* is part of the marriage process, studied separately.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling one form simply good and the other bad"}
Both forms have advantages and disadvantages. An answer that only praises the past or only praises the present will not reach the top marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing one group as typical"}
Practices differed across communities. Use phrases such as "in many communities" and give Shona and Ndebele terms side by side where you know them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State** or **identify** (Paper 2 Section A): one word or phrase each, such as "the river", "the aunt", "beads". **Describe**: say what happened and who was involved.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** an advantage: give the feature and its result, for example "the aunt checked the young man's family, so the girl was protected from marrying into a family with a bad reputation".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Compare** or **evaluate** (Paper 2 Section B): set out points on both forms, then give a judgement. A balanced conclusion often notes that families today combine the two.
:::

:::exam-tip
Paper 1 multiple-choice items often describe a scene (a well, a token, an aunt's advice) and ask what practice it shows. Link the role to the person: aunts advise girls, uncles advise boys.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Courtship is the period in which two people get to know each other before deciding to marry.
- Indigenous courtship took place in public settings such as rivers, wells, fields and dances, with tokens of love such as beads and guidance from aunts and uncles.
- Contemporary courtship is social, religious, western-influenced or online, and is usually managed by the couple.
- Indigenous courtship offered family checks and guidance but could limit free choice; contemporary courtship offers free choice but fewer safeguards.
- No one under 18 may marry, and many families today combine free choice with elders' advice.
:::
