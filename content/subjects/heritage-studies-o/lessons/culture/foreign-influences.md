## Introduction

Farai, who lives in Harare, wakes up to an alarm on a phone made in Asia, eats bread made from imported wheat, puts on jeans and a T-shirt, and spends the bus ride watching music videos from Nigeria, South Africa and the United States. At the weekend he visits his grandmother in Murehwa, greets her on his knees, eats sadza with his hands and listens to her stories in Shona.

Farai lives in two cultural worlds at once, as many young Zimbabweans do. This Form 4 lesson compares Zimbabwean and foreign norms and values, looks at the forces that threaten indigenous culture, and asks how it can be preserved. It builds on what you know about the main features of indigenous culture and about norms and values. One idea runs through it: **"foreign" does not mean "bad"**, and "indigenous" does not mean "perfect". Good answers weigh both.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Foreign norms and values"}
Ways of behaving and beliefs that come from outside a society, for example through colonialism, religion, trade, migration, education and the media.
:::

:::definition{term="Globalisation"}
The growing connection of the world's economies and cultures through trade, travel, communication technology and the media.
:::

:::definition{term="Individualism"}
A way of life that puts the rights, wishes and success of the individual first.
:::

:::definition{term="Communalism"}
A way of life that stresses the group (family, clan, community) and shared duties.
:::

:::definition{term="Cultural erosion"}
The gradual loss of a people's language, customs, norms and values, often as they are replaced by others.
:::

:::definition{term="Acculturation"}
The process by which people of one culture take on features of another culture through contact.
:::

## Comparing Zimbabwean and foreign norms and values

"Foreign" here mainly means Western norms that arrived through colonialism, missionaries, schools and the media, but influences also come from other African countries, Asia and elsewhere. The table compares general tendencies; real families mix both.

| Area | Indigenous Zimbabwean tendency | Common foreign (Western) tendency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outlook | Communalism: the family and community come first | Individualism: personal rights and choices come first |
| Family | Extended family with shared duties | Nuclear family (parents and children) |
| Greetings and respect | Clapping, kneeling or bowing; indirect address of elders by title | Handshake; first names may be used even with older people |
| Dress | Modest dress expected, especially before elders | Wide freedom of personal dress |
| Roles | Clear roles by age and sex | More flexible roles; emphasis on equality |
| Decisions | Elders and family consulted, e.g. on marriage | Individuals decide, e.g. choosing a partner |
| Old age | Elders cared for at home by the family | Old people's homes and pensions are more common |

### Significance: what each brings

Indigenous norms give a strong sense of identity, belonging and security: no one should be left alone. Foreign norms have brought ideas and institutions that many Zimbabweans value: formal schooling, modern medicine, new technology, written law and the language of human rights, including equal rights for women and children. Some foreign values, such as excessive materialism, can weaken communal ties; some indigenous practices, such as child marriage, conflict with children's rights. The task is to judge each practice, not each culture as a whole.

:::example{title="Two greetings, one respect"}
Sipho greets his grandfather in Lupane by clapping softly and waiting to be spoken to. At a job interview in Bulawayo he shakes hands and makes eye contact.

1. Both norms express the same **value**: respect.
2. Sipho has learnt which norm fits which setting.
3. Being able to move between cultures in this way is a skill, not a loss of identity.
:::

## Threats to indigenous culture

| Threat | How it affects indigenous culture |
| --- | --- |
| Foreign influence through colonial history and education | Indigenous ways were often dismissed as backward; some people still rank foreign ways above their own |
| Media and the internet | Films, music, social media and advertising spread foreign dress, language and lifestyles, especially among the young |
| Urbanisation | Families move to towns, live in nuclear households and lose daily contact with elders and ceremonies |
| Migration abroad | Children grow up outside the country, sometimes without their parents' language |
| Globalisation | Imported goods, foods and fashions replace local ones |
| Some religious attitudes | Some believers reject indigenous ceremonies or instruments as un-Christian, while others accept many customs |
| Loss of language | Parents speak only English at home; children cannot understand proverbs, totem praises or elders' advice |
| Commercialisation | Ceremonies and crafts are changed to suit tourists |

Language loss is especially serious, because language carries proverbs, folk tales, totem praises and history. When a language is lost, much of the culture goes with it.

:::example{title="A family in Mutare decides on language"}
Chipo and her husband speak English to their children so that they will do well at school.

1. **Effect noticed:** at a family gathering the children cannot understand their grandmother's stories or reply to greetings in Manyika.
2. **Concern:** the children are losing contact with family history and wisdom carried in the language.
3. **Decision:** the family agrees that Shona is spoken at home and English at school.
4. **Result:** the children become bilingual. Their English does not suffer, and they can talk with their grandparents.
:::

## Preserving indigenous culture

- **Teaching indigenous languages** at home and in school, including the officially recognised minority languages.
- **Family practice:** greetings, totem praises, folk tales and family histories passed on by parents and grandparents.
- **Festivals and culture days**, traditional music, dance and food at schools and in communities.
- **Museums, archives and monuments** that record and display culture.
- **Media in indigenous languages:** radio, television, books and online content.
- **Arts and creative industries:** mbira music, sculpture, crafts and drama earning a living from heritage.
- **Selective adoption:** keeping foreign ideas that help (technology, medicine, rights) while keeping indigenous values that bind communities.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying everything foreign is bad"}
Foreign influences include schools, hospitals, technology and human rights. A balanced answer shows benefits as well as threats.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all indigenous practices must be kept unchanged"}
Practices that harm people, such as child marriage, are illegal and should change. Valuing culture means choosing wisely.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming threats without explaining how they damage culture"}
"Urbanisation" alone earns one mark. Explain: families in towns lose daily contact with elders and ceremonies.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Compare** (Paper 2 Section A): use matching pairs, such as communalism versus individualism, or extended versus nuclear family. A comparison needs both sides in the same sentence.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss threats** (Paper 2 Section B): explain several threats, then weigh them: some foreign influences enrich culture, and culture can adapt. End with a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip
For **ways of preserving culture**, give practical measures and say who carries them out (families, schools, government, media, artists).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous Zimbabwean norms tend towards communalism, the extended family and respect shown through specific greetings; Western norms tend towards individualism and the nuclear family.
- Foreign influence has brought both benefits (schooling, medicine, technology, rights) and threats (individualism, materialism, language loss).
- Threats to indigenous culture include the media and internet, urbanisation, migration, globalisation, some religious attitudes and loss of language.
- Culture can be preserved through language teaching, family practice, festivals, museums, local-language media and the arts.
- Judge each practice on its merits; neither culture is all good or all bad.
:::
