## Introduction

On the first day of Form 1 at a secondary school in Bindura, nobody needed to tell Rudo to stand when the head teacher walked into assembly. She had learnt at home to stand when an elder enters the room. She did, however, have to learn new rules: wearing the school tie, walking on the left of the corridor, and handing in homework on Mondays.

Every group of people has expectations about how its members should behave and what they should treasure. Heritage Studies calls these **norms** and **values**. They make life predictable and peaceful, and they carry a community's heritage from one generation to the next. This lesson builds on what you learnt about Unhu: the attributes of Unhu are values, and the everyday rules that put them into practice are norms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Value"}
A belief about what is important, good or worth protecting, which guides how people behave. Examples: respect, honesty, hard work, cooperation.
:::

:::definition{term="Norm"}
An accepted rule or standard of behaviour in a group, often unwritten, that tells members how to act in a particular situation. Example: greeting elders first, or standing when a teacher enters.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanction"}
A reward or a punishment used to encourage people to keep norms. A **positive sanction** rewards (praise, a prize); a **negative sanction** punishes (a scolding, a fine, detention).
:::

:::definition{term="Socialisation"}
The lifelong process by which people learn the norms and values of their society, mainly from the family, school, peers, religion and the media.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The link between them"}
Values are the beliefs; norms are the rules that put them into practice. The value of **respect** is shown through norms such as greeting elders, not interrupting and using polite forms of address.
:::

## Norms and values in three settings

| Setting | Examples of values | Examples of norms | Who teaches and enforces them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Home | Respect, obedience, honesty, hard work, sharing | Greeting elders, helping with chores, eating together, asking permission to go out | Parents, grandparents, aunts and uncles (*vatete/ubabakazi*, *sekuru/umalume*), older siblings |
| School | Discipline, punctuality, hard work, honesty, cooperation | Wearing the uniform, arriving on time, no cheating in tests, raising a hand to speak | Teachers, prefects, the head, school rules and the disciplinary committee |
| Community | Respect, cooperation, sharing, unity, care for the environment | Attending funerals, joining communal work, respecting sacred places, not cutting trees carelessly | Elders, traditional leaders, religious leaders, neighbours, councils |

Notice that some values, such as respect and honesty, appear in every setting. What changes is the norm: respect at home may mean kneeling to serve an elder; at school it means listening when a teacher speaks; in the community it means attending a neighbour's funeral.

## How norms and values are taught and enforced

Norms are learnt mainly by **example and instruction**: children copy adults, listen to stories, proverbs and folk tales, and are corrected when they make mistakes. Religious teaching, school assemblies and the media also play a part.

They are kept through **sanctions**:

- **Positive sanctions:** praise, a smile, a gift, a school prize, being trusted with responsibility.
- **Negative sanctions:** a scolding, being made to apologise, losing a privilege, detention, and in the community a fine imposed at a traditional court (for example, for disrespecting a sacred place).

Many norms are kept without any sanction at all, because people have **internalised** them: they behave well because they believe it is right, not because someone is watching. That is the goal of socialisation.

:::example{title="A broken norm at the borehole"}
In a village near Lupane, families take turns at the borehole. One morning Themba pushes ahead of an elderly woman.

1. **Norm broken:** waiting your turn and giving way to elders.
2. **Value behind it:** respect and fairness.
3. **Sanction:** a neighbour quietly tells him to step back, and his mother later makes him apologise.
4. **Result:** Themba learns the norm; the community sees it upheld, so others are less likely to break it.
:::

:::example{title="Rules and values at a Masvingo school"}
A school introduces a rule that phones must be handed in at the gate.

1. The **norm** is the rule: no phones in class.
2. The **values** behind it are discipline, concentration on learning and respect for teachers.
3. Pupils who follow it are praised at assembly (positive sanction); pupils caught with phones lose them for a week (negative sanction).
4. Some pupils argue the rule ignores phones' value for research; the school agrees to allow them in the library under supervision. Norms can change when a community discusses them.
:::

## Why norms and values matter

- **Order and peace:** people know what to expect, so there is less conflict.
- **Identity and belonging:** shared norms mark people as members of a family, school or community.
- **Cooperation:** values such as sharing and unity make communal work and mutual help possible.
- **Protection of the vulnerable:** norms of care for children, the elderly and people with disabilities protect those who cannot protect themselves.
- **Passing on heritage:** norms carry language, customs and beliefs from one generation to the next.
- **Development:** discipline, honesty and hard work at school and in the community make progress possible.

Norms are not fixed for ever. Some old norms are changed because they harm people or conflict with the law and human rights, for example practices that denied girls schooling. Values such as respect and fairness are used to judge which norms should change.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using norm and value as if they were the same"}
A value is a belief (respect); a norm is the behaviour that shows it (greeting elders). When asked to distinguish them, define both and give an example of each.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking only negative sanctions enforce norms"}
Praise, reward and trust are sanctions too, and they are often more effective than punishment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the home"}
Questions often name all three settings. Give at least one norm and one value for each setting named.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Distinguish** between norms and values (Paper 2 Section A): one definition each plus one linked example scores well. "Respect is a value; greeting elders is a norm that shows it."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Identify** norms of the school: short phrases are enough (wearing uniform, punctuality, no cheating). **Explain** their importance: say what each achieves (punctuality means lessons start on time and nobody misses learning).
:::

:::exam-tip
In **analyse the importance** questions, organise by benefit (order, identity, cooperation, protection, heritage) rather than by listing random norms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A value is a belief about what is important; a norm is an accepted rule of behaviour that puts values into practice.
- The home, school and community each have their own norms, but share values such as respect, honesty and cooperation.
- Norms are taught by example, instruction, stories and religion, and enforced by positive and negative sanctions.
- The aim of socialisation is that people keep norms because they believe in them, not out of fear.
- Norms and values bring order, identity, cooperation, protection of the vulnerable and continuity of heritage; harmful norms can and should change.
:::
