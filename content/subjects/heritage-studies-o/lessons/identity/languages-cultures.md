## Introduction

Walk through a busy bus terminus in Harare or Bulawayo and you may hear Shona, Ndebele, English, Tonga, Venda, Shangani, Nambya and Chewa within a few minutes. Zimbabwe is home to many peoples, each with its own language, history and customs, and most Zimbabweans speak more than one language.

This variety is part of the nation's heritage. The Constitution of 2013 recognises 16 languages, which tells every citizen that their language and culture belong to Zimbabwe. In this Form 2 lesson you identify the indigenous languages and cultures of Zimbabwe's peoples and explain why they should be upheld. You should already know how language carries identity, from the Form 1 lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Culture"}
The way of life of a group of people: their language, beliefs, values, customs, food, dress, music, dance, crafts and ceremonies, passed from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous language"}
A language that developed among, and has long been spoken by, a people of a particular area, such as Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, Kalanga or Nambya in Zimbabwe.
:::

:::definition{term="Officially recognised language"}
A language named in the Constitution, which the State must treat equitably and promote.
:::

:::definition{term="Cultural diversity"}
The existence of many different cultures, languages and ways of life side by side in one society.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Section 6 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013)"}
The officially recognised languages are **Chewa, Chibarwe, English, Kalanga, Koisan, Nambya, Ndau, Ndebele, Shangani, Shona, sign language, Sotho, Tonga, Tswana, Venda and Xhosa**: 16 in all. The State must treat them equitably and promote the use of all languages used in Zimbabwe, including sign language.
:::

## The peoples and languages of Zimbabwe

Shona and Ndebele are the most widely spoken indigenous languages, and English is widely used in government, business and education. Many other peoples have their own languages and long histories in particular parts of the country. The table gives the areas where each language is **mainly** associated; people of every group also live in towns and other areas.

| Language | Areas mainly associated with it (in general) |
| --- | --- |
| Shona (with dialects such as Zezuru, Karanga, Manyika, Korekore) | Much of central, northern and eastern Zimbabwe |
| Ndebele | Matabeleland North and South, Bulawayo and parts of the Midlands |
| Tonga | The Zambezi valley, especially Binga |
| Nambya | Hwange district |
| Venda | Beitbridge district |
| Shangani (Tsonga) | The south-eastern lowveld, such as Chiredzi |
| Kalanga | The south-west, around Plumtree |
| Ndau | Chipinge and nearby areas of Manicaland |
| Sotho and Tswana | Parts of Matabeleland South |
| Koisan (San languages) | Small communities in western Zimbabwe, such as Tsholotsho |
| Chewa | Many towns, farms and mining areas; many speakers trace their families to Malawi or Zambia |
| Chibarwe | Small communities in eastern Zimbabwe near the Mozambique border |
| Xhosa | A small community, mainly around Mbembesi near Bulawayo |
| Sign language | Used by Deaf people across the country |

Ndau is sometimes grouped with Shona, but it is recognised in the Constitution as a language in its own right.

## Cultural features in outline

Every group has distinctive features, and many are shared across groups.

| Feature | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Food | Sadza made from maize, sorghum or millet, eaten across the country; mopane worms (madora in Shona, amacimbi in Ndebele); traditional vegetables and wild fruits |
| Music | The mbira, closely linked with Shona ceremonies; drums used by most groups; the Tonga **ngoma buntibe**, played with drums and horns; the Venda **tshikona** reed-pipe dance |
| Dance | Many groups have dances for ceremonies, weddings and celebrations, such as the Ndebele **isitshikitsha** |
| Crafts | Baskets (Tonga baskets from Binga are well known), pottery, wood carving, stone sculpture, beadwork, mats |
| Dress | Cloth, beads and skins worn on special occasions; beadwork patterns that show identity |
| Beliefs and ceremonies | Belief in a creator (Mwari / uMlimo) and the ancestors, rain-making ceremonies, naming, marriage and funeral customs; many people also follow Christianity, Islam or other faiths |

:::example{title="The Tonga and the Zambezi"}
The Tonga people have lived for centuries along the Zambezi River. When Lake Kariba was created by the Kariba Dam in the late 1950s, many Tonga families were moved from the river to drier land away from it.

1. **Culture linked to place**: river fishing, farming on the riverbanks and shrines along the river were part of Tonga life, so the move affected their way of life as well as their homes.
2. **Heritage kept alive**: Tonga communities continue to make well-known baskets, play ngoma buntibe music and tell stories such as those of Nyami Nyami, the river spirit.
3. **Language**: Tonga is officially recognised and is used in schools and on community radio, which helps keep it alive.

This shows how a people's culture is tied to its history and land, and how it can be upheld.
:::

## Why uphold indigenous languages and cultures?

1. **Identity and self-respect**: people who know their language and culture know who they are and feel proud of it.
2. **Heritage**: languages carry history, values, songs, folk tales and knowledge about plants, animals and the land; losing them means losing that heritage.
3. **Unity in diversity**: when every group's language and culture are respected, all citizens feel they belong, which builds peace and national unity.
4. **Rights and equality**: the Constitution requires the State to treat the recognised languages equitably; speakers of smaller languages deserve the same respect as others.
5. **Education**: children often learn best when they begin school in a language they understand well.
6. **Tourism and the creative economy**: music, dance, crafts and festivals attract visitors and earn income.

### Threats and ways of upholding

Smaller languages can be weakened when children grow up speaking only a larger language or English, when media and books in them are scarce, and when young people move to towns. They can be upheld by:

- speaking indigenous languages at home and teaching children their culture;
- teaching more languages in schools and producing books in them;
- community radio and television in local languages;
- cultural festivals, galas and school culture days;
- recording elders' stories, songs and knowledge;
- respect: never mocking another person's language, accent or customs.

:::example{title="A culture day in Chiredzi"}
A fictional school in Chiredzi, where learners speak Shangani, Shona, Ndebele and other languages, plans a culture day.

1. Each group presents food, songs and a dance from its culture.
2. Learners from each group teach the others a greeting in their language.
3. Elders are invited to tell stories and explain their meanings.
4. Result: learners speaking smaller languages feel proud, and everyone learns respect for the others' heritage.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Zimbabwe has only two indigenous languages"}
Shona and Ndebele are the most widely spoken, but the Constitution recognises 16 languages, including Tonga, Venda, Kalanga, Nambya, Shangani, Ndau, Sotho, Tswana, Chewa, Chibarwe, Xhosa, Koisan and sign language.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ranking cultures"}
No culture is better than another. Describe each respectfully and avoid stereotypes about any group.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing language and culture"}
Language is one part of culture. Culture also includes beliefs, food, dress, music, dance, crafts and ceremonies.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe is culturally diverse: section 6 of the Constitution recognises 16 languages, including sign language.
- Besides Shona and Ndebele, languages such as Tonga, Nambya, Venda, Shangani, Kalanga, Ndau, Sotho, Tswana, Chewa, Chibarwe, Xhosa and Koisan are spoken, often linked with particular areas.
- Cultures differ in food, music, dance, crafts, dress and ceremonies, and also share many features.
- Upholding indigenous languages and cultures protects identity, heritage, unity, equality and learning.
- Families, schools, the media and communities can all help keep every language and culture alive.
:::
