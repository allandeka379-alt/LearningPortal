## Introduction

In one family in Gweru, Nyasha's grandparents married in a customary ceremony in 1970, after the families had agreed on *roora*. Her parents had a customary marriage, later registered, followed by a church wedding. Her aunt married at the magistrates court. Her great-grandfather had two wives, both of whom are remembered with respect at family gatherings.

Marriage is one of the most important institutions in every Zimbabwean community: it joins two families, creates a home for children and passes on name, totem and property. This Form 4 lesson describes the **types of marriage**: indigenous monogamous and polygamous marriages, and contemporary civil, customary and religious marriages. It sets out their features, advantages and disadvantages, and the legal rules every young person should know, especially the minimum marriage age of 18. How marriages are arranged (courtship, *roora/lobola*) and the roles of spouses are covered in other lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Marriage"}
A union between two people, recognised by their families, community and the law, that creates rights and duties between the spouses and links their families.
:::

:::definition{term="Monogamy"}
A marriage in which a person has only one spouse at a time.
:::

:::definition{term="Polygamy"}
A marriage in which a person has more than one spouse at the same time. In Zimbabwe the form practised is **polygyny**, in which a man has more than one wife.
:::

:::definition{term="Civil marriage"}
A marriage solemnised and registered under the law by a marriage officer (for example a magistrate or an authorised minister of religion). It is monogamous.
:::

:::definition{term="Customary marriage"}
A marriage concluded according to the customs of a community, such as the payment of *roora/lobola*, which may be registered under the law. Under customary law it may be polygamous.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The law on marriage"}
- No person under **18** may marry (section 78 of the Constitution), and both parties must consent freely; forced marriage and child marriage are unlawful.
- Marriage law was reformed by the **Marriages Act of 2022**, which brought civil and customary marriages under one law. In outline, it kept the minimum age of 18 and made it an offence to marry or pledge a child.
:::

## Indigenous marriages

In indigenous society marriage was a union of **families**, not only of two individuals. It was arranged with the involvement of relatives and go-betweens (*munyai/umkhongi*) and sealed by *roora/lobola*. Two forms existed.

### Monogamous marriage

One husband and one wife. This was the most common form, as many men could not afford more than one marriage.

### Polygamous marriage

A husband with two or more wives, each usually with her own hut and fields. It was often associated with wealth and status. Views on polygamy differ, and both sides deserve a fair hearing.

| | Monogamy | Polygamy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possible advantages | Closer partnership between two spouses; easier to share resources and decisions; lower risk of sexually transmitted infections where both are faithful; fewer jealousy conflicts | Large family provides labour for farming; more children were seen as wealth and security in old age; a wife unable to have children was not necessarily divorced; widows and unmarried women could be cared for within a household; status for the family |
| Possible disadvantages | Smaller family labour force in a farming economy; if a spouse dies or cannot have children, the family may face hardship | Jealousy and rivalry between wives and between children; resources (land, school fees, attention) spread thin; disputes over inheritance; higher health risks if any partner has other partners; unequal power between husband and wives |

Some people argue that polygamy suited a farming society and provided care for women; others point out that it often left women with less say and created conflict. Today many religious groups reject polygamy, while others accept it, and customary law still allows it for customary marriages. In an examination, present both sides and judge.

## Contemporary marriages

| Type | How it is made | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil marriage | Solemnised by a marriage officer, such as a magistrate, or by an authorised minister of religion, and registered | Monogamous; marriage certificate issued; clear legal rights to property and maintenance |
| Customary marriage | According to custom, usually with *roora/lobola*; may be registered | May be polygamous under customary law; registration gives proof of the marriage |
| Religious marriage | A wedding in a church, mosque or other faith, according to its teachings | Many religious weddings are also registered as civil marriages when the minister is authorised; the faith's teaching shapes the marriage |

Many couples combine types: a customary marriage with *roora/lobola*, followed by a church wedding and civil registration.

### Advantages and disadvantages of contemporary marriages

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil | Legal protection; clear property rights; marriage certificate helps with inheritance, documents and maintenance claims | Divorce must go through the courts; can be costly; some families feel their customs are left out |
| Customary (registered) | Respects culture and involves both families; recognised by law | May be polygamous; unregistered unions are harder to prove, which can weaken a spouse's claims to property |
| Religious | Shared faith and spiritual support; community of believers | Some faiths restrict divorce; differences of faith between partners can cause tension |

:::example{title="Why registration matters"}
Thandeka married Sipho under custom in a village near Gwanda. Roora/lobola was paid, but the marriage was never registered.

1. Years later Sipho dies, and his relatives claim that Thandeka was never really his wife.
2. Without a marriage certificate, Thandeka must find witnesses to prove the marriage, which takes time.
3. Her friend Nokuthula, whose marriage was registered, could show her certificate at once to the Master's office.

Registration does not replace custom; it gives legal proof that protects both spouses and the children.
:::

:::example{title="Weighing a decision"}
Tendai and Rudo, both 26, are planning to marry in Masvingo.

1. Their families want *roora* and a customary ceremony: this respects both families and their culture.
2. Rudo's church expects a church wedding: this matters for their faith.
3. They choose a customary marriage first, then a church wedding performed by an authorised minister, which also registers the marriage as a civil one.
4. Result: culture, faith and legal protection are all respected, and the marriage is monogamous by their choice and under civil law.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling polygamy simply 'bad' or 'good'"}
Polygamy is a contested matter. Give advantages and disadvantages fairly and reach a reasoned judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing roora/lobola with the marriage type"}
*Roora/lobola* is a marriage practice; customary marriage is the type of marriage it helps to make.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the wrong marriage age"}
The minimum age is 18 for everyone. Older laws that allowed younger marriage no longer apply.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Explain advantages and disadvantages** (Paper 2): each point needs development ("a large family provides labour, so more land can be farmed").
:::

:::exam-tip
In a Section B essay on **monogamy and polygamy**, organise both sides clearly, consider modern conditions (cost of living, health, law, religion) and finish with a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous marriages were monogamous or polygamous and joined two families, sealed by *roora/lobola*.
- Monogamy offers close partnership and fewer conflicts; polygamy provided labour and care for women in a farming society but can cause rivalry and spread resources thin; views differ.
- Contemporary marriages are civil (monogamous, registered), customary (registered, may be polygamous) and religious; many couples combine them.
- Registration gives legal proof of a marriage and protects spouses and children.
- No one under 18 may marry, both parties must consent, and marriage law was reformed by the Marriages Act of 2022.
:::
