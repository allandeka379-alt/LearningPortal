## Introduction

At a wedding in Masvingo, the bride's family asks the groom's family, *"Mutupo wenyu ndiani?"* (What is your totem?). When a visitor is given food, he thanks the host not only by name but by the host's totem: *"Maita, Shumba!"* At a school in Bulawayo, two learners who have just met discover they are both Ndlovu, and one of them says, "Then we are relatives."

In many Zimbabwean communities, a person's **totem** is one of the first things others want to know. It tells them which clan you belong to, who your ancestors were, whom you may marry and how you should be greeted and praised. In this Form 2 lesson you learn what totems and sub-totems are, how totem praises work, how to trace your genealogy, and why the totem system matters.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Totem (mutupo / isibongo)"}
An animal, part of the body or other object adopted as the symbol of a clan. All members of the clan share the totem, which is usually passed from father to children.
:::

:::definition{term="Clan"}
A large group of families who believe they descend from a common ancestor and share the same totem.
:::

:::definition{term="Sub-totem (chidawo)"}
A further name within a totem that identifies a particular branch of the clan, for example different branches of the Shumba (lion) clan.
:::

:::definition{term="Totem praises (detembo / izibongo)"}
Poetic praises recited for members of a clan, naming the totem, ancestors, places of origin and qualities of the clan, used to thank, honour or comfort a person.
:::

:::definition{term="Genealogy"}
The study and record of a family's line of descent from its ancestors, often shown as a family tree.
:::

## Common totems

Totems are usually animals, but some are parts of the body or other objects. Shona and Ndebele totems often correspond: the same animal has a name in each language.

| Totem | Language | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shumba | Shona | Lion |
| Nzou | Shona | Elephant |
| Ndlovu | Ndebele | Elephant |
| Moyo | Shona (also common in Matabeleland) | Heart |
| Dube | Ndebele | Zebra |
| Nyathi | Ndebele | Buffalo |
| Soko | Shona | Monkey |
| Gumbo | Shona | Leg |
| Ngwenya | Ndebele (Ngwena in Shona) | Crocodile |
| Mhofu | Shona | Eland |

Ncube and Sibanda are also common totems among Ndebele-speaking families. Other peoples of Zimbabwe, such as the Kalanga, Venda, Tonga and Nambya, also have clan names and totems of their own.

:::keyfact{title="How totems are passed on"}
In Shona and Ndebele families a child usually takes the **father's** totem. A woman keeps her own totem after marriage; she does not take her husband's.
:::

## Totem praises

Totem praises (detembo in Shona, izibongo in Ndebele) are a form of oral poetry. They are recited:

- to **thank** someone, for example after a meal or a gift;
- to **honour** a person at a wedding, a ceremony or when they have done well;
- to **comfort** someone at a time of sorrow;
- by a wife to praise her husband, or by parents to praise their children.

A praise poem usually names the totem animal and its qualities (the lion's strength, the elephant's size and dignity), the clan's ancestors and places of origin, and its famous deeds. When people hear their praises, they feel pride, belonging and respect. You should be able to present the praises of your own totem in outline, or describe what they contain, without needing to recite another clan's poem word for word.

## Tracing genealogy and drawing a family tree

Genealogy links a person to their ancestors. Elders, especially grandparents, are the best source: they remember names going back several generations, the places the family lived, and the totem and sub-totem.

A family tree shows generations from oldest to youngest. Without a diagram, it can be written as a table.

| Generation | Members (fictional Moyo family, Gweru) | Totem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1: great-grandparents | Great-grandfather Moyo and great-grandmother (Nzou) | Moyo |
| 2: grandparents | Grandfather Moyo and grandmother (Shumba) | Moyo |
| 3: parents | Father Moyo, his sister (vatete), and mother (Dube) | Moyo |
| 4: children | Nyasha and Farai Moyo | Moyo |

Notice that every person born into the family is Moyo, the father's line, while each wife has her own totem shown in brackets.

:::example{title="Drawing your own family tree"}
Rudo wants to trace her genealogy for a school project.

1. **Ask elders**: she visits her grandmother, who names her own parents, her husband's parents and their home areas.
2. **Record**: Rudo writes each generation in order, oldest at the top, with each person's totem.
3. **Check**: her vatete confirms the sub-totem and adds a great-uncle Rudo did not know.
4. **Present**: Rudo draws the tree, linking parents and children with lines, and writes the totem beside each name.
5. **Result**: she now knows her ancestors over four generations and understands her place in the clan.
:::

## Purposes of the totem system

| Purpose | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | A totem tells a person which clan they belong to and links them to their ancestors |
| Preventing marriage between close relatives | People of the same totem are regarded as relatives, so marriage between them is discouraged or forbidden; this prevents incest and keeps family lines healthy |
| Conservation | Members of a clan usually do not kill or eat their totem animal, which helps protect it |
| Unity and hospitality | People who share a totem treat one another as relatives, giving help and hospitality even to strangers |
| Respect and social order | Addressing people by their totem and reciting praises shows respect; the clan system organises relationships and roles |
| Link with the ancestors | Totems and praises remember the ancestors and their history |

:::example{title="A totem check before marriage"}
Themba Ndlovu and Nokuthula meet at college in Bulawayo and want to marry.

1. **Question**: the families first ask each other's totems.
2. **Discovery**: Nokuthula's totem is Nzou, the Shona name for elephant, and Themba's is Ndlovu, elephant in Ndebele.
3. **Elders consulted**: because the totems refer to the same animal, the elders carefully investigate whether the two families are related.
4. **Outcome**: they find no blood relationship between the families, and a ceremony is held according to their custom before the marriage goes ahead.

This shows how the totem system is used to prevent marriage between close relatives, and how families and elders take responsibility for checking.
:::

### The totem system today

Some people, especially in towns, know little about their totems, and some do not follow the food taboos. Yet totems remain important at weddings, funerals and ceremonies, as surnames (many surnames such as Moyo, Ncube, Ndlovu and Dube are totem names), and in daily greetings and thanks. Interest in genealogy has grown, and many families now record their history in writing or online.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing totem and surname"}
Many surnames are totem names, but not all. A person's surname may differ from their totem, so always ask for the totem itself.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a wife takes her husband's totem"}
In Shona and Ndebele custom, a woman keeps her own totem after marriage; her children take their father's.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing purposes without explaining"}
"Conservation" alone earns little. Explain: "People do not eat their totem animal (1), so it is protected from over-hunting (1)."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A totem (mutupo/isibongo) is the symbol of a clan, usually an animal or body part, passed from father to children; a sub-totem (chidawo) identifies a branch of the clan.
- Common totems include Shumba, Nzou/Ndlovu, Moyo, Dube, Nyathi, Soko, Gumbo, Ngwenya and Mhofu.
- Totem praises (detembo/izibongo) thank, honour and comfort people by naming the totem, ancestors and clan history.
- Genealogy is traced through elders and shown as a family tree, generation by generation.
- Totems give identity, prevent marriage between close relatives, conserve animals, build unity, show respect and link people to their ancestors.
:::
