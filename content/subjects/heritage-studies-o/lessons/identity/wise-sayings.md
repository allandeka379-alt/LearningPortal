## Introduction

Two brothers in Marondera are arguing about who should fetch water. Their grandfather says only: *"Chara chimwe hachitswanyi inda."* The boys stop, think, and fetch the water together. A grandmother in Lupane ends a quarrel between cousins by saying *"Izandla ziyagezana."* Neither elder gave a long lecture. A few words carried a whole lesson.

For centuries the peoples of Zimbabwe have stored their wisdom in short sayings, puzzles and stories. These **indigenous wise sayings** teach values, give advice, entertain and sharpen the mind. They are introduced in Form 1 and studied more deeply in Form 4. In this lesson you learn the types of wise sayings, their features and meanings, and why they still matter. Games and dances, other forms of entertainment, are studied in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Proverb (tsumo / izaga)"}
A short, fixed saying that states a general truth or piece of advice, usually in picture language, for example *"one thumb cannot crush a louse"*.
:::

:::definition{term="Idiom (dimikira / isisho)"}
A group of words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual words, for example *"to have a long heart"*, meaning to be patient.
:::

:::definition{term="Riddle (chirahwe / isiphicaphicwano)"}
A puzzling question or description, often of an everyday object or animal, which the listener must solve.
:::

:::definition{term="Folk tale (ngano / inganekwane)"}
A traditional story, usually told by elders in the evening, often with animal characters and songs, that teaches a lesson.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The four types in Shona and Ndebele"}
Proverbs: **tsumo** (Shona), **izaga** (Ndebele). Idioms: **madimikira** (Shona), **izisho** (Ndebele). Riddles: **zvirahwe** (Shona), **iziphicaphicwano** (Ndebele). Folk tales: **ngano** (Shona), **inganekwane** (Ndebele).
:::

## Proverbs

Proverbs are short and fixed: the words are not changed. They use images from everyday life (animals, farming, the body, the home) to state a truth. Elders use them to advise, warn, praise or settle arguments without causing offence.

| Proverb | Language | Literal meaning | Lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chara chimwe hachitswanyi inda | Shona | One thumb cannot crush a louse | Cooperation: people achieve more together |
| Kuziva mbuya huudzwa | Shona | To know your grandmother, you must be told | Learn from others; ask when you do not know |
| Rina manyanga hariputirwe | Shona | What has horns cannot be wrapped up | Wrongdoing or a secret will eventually come out |
| Kupa kuturika | Shona | To give is to store | Generosity is repaid; those you help will help you |
| Izandla ziyagezana | Ndebele | Hands wash each other | Cooperation and helping one another |
| Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu | Ndebele | A person is a person through other people | Ubuntu: we become fully human through our relationships |
| Inkosi yinkosi ngabantu | Ndebele | A king is a king through the people | Leaders depend on, and must serve, their people |

## Idioms

Idioms are phrases whose meaning is figurative. A learner who translates them word for word will misunderstand them. They make speech colourful and show that the speaker knows the language well.

For example, in Shona *kuva nemoyo murefu* and in Ndebele *ukuba lenhliziyo ende*, both "to have a long heart", mean to be **patient**. English has idioms too: "to let the cat out of the bag" means to reveal a secret.

## Riddles

Riddles are a game of wits, usually between children or between an elder and children in the evening. One person describes something in a puzzling way, and the others must guess what it is. Riddles use comparison and exaggeration.

An example of the kind of riddle told in many African communities: *"A small house full of food, but it has no door."* Answer: an egg.

Riddles train observation, memory, imagination and quick thinking, and they teach children the names and features of things around them.

## Folk tales

Folk tales are told by grandparents and other elders, often around the fire at night. Many have animal characters with human qualities:

- the **hare** (Tsuro in Shona, uLogwaja in Ndebele): small but clever, a trickster who often outwits larger animals, though greed or pride can bring him down;
- the **lion** or the **elephant**: strong but sometimes foolish;
- the **tortoise**: slow but patient and wise.

Folk tales often include a song with a chorus in which the listeners join, so everyone takes part. They end with a lesson, such as that greed is punished, kindness is rewarded, or the weak can win through wisdom.

:::example{title="Reading a folk tale for its lesson"}
A grandmother tells this story. During a drought, all the animals agree to dig a well together. The hare refuses to help but later sneaks to the well at night to drink. The animals set a trap, catch him and make him promise to work.

1. **Character**: the hare is clever but lazy and selfish.
2. **Problem**: he wants the benefit without the work.
3. **Ending**: he is caught and shamed.
4. **Lesson**: everyone must contribute to shared work; selfishness is found out (compare the proverb *rina manyanga hariputirwe*).

The tale teaches cooperation and honesty in a way children remember.
:::

## Significance of wise sayings

| Purpose | How wise sayings achieve it |
| --- | --- |
| Teaching values | Folk tales and proverbs teach honesty, hard work, respect, cooperation and generosity |
| Advice and warning | Elders use proverbs to advise or warn tactfully, without direct criticism |
| Settling disputes | A proverb can calm a quarrel by reminding people of a shared value |
| Entertainment | Folk tales, songs and riddles entertain families, especially in the evening |
| Sharpening thinking | Riddles train memory, observation and reasoning; proverbs need interpretation |
| Language skills | They enrich vocabulary and teach the beauty of the language |
| Identity and heritage | They pass on the history, beliefs and wisdom of a people and show pride in their language |

:::example{title="A proverb settles a dispute"}
At a fictional village meeting near Bindura, two families argue over who should repair a shared footpath. The village head says *"Chara chimwe hachitswanyi inda."*

1. **Meaning**: one thumb alone cannot crush a louse; both are needed.
2. **Application**: neither family can repair the path alone; both benefit, so both should work.
3. **Effect**: the families agree to work together without either being blamed.

A proverb allowed the leader to give advice politely and settle the matter.
:::

### Wise sayings today

Fewer families sit around the fire telling stories, and young people spend more time with television and phones. Yet wise sayings live on in books, school lessons, radio and television programmes, music, speeches and online videos. Many school competitions include storytelling and poetry in indigenous languages.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing proverbs and idioms"}
A proverb is a complete saying that states a general truth or advice. An idiom is a phrase with a figurative meaning used within a sentence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only the literal meaning"}
"Hands wash each other" is the literal meaning. Examiners want the lesson: people should help one another.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying folk tales are only for entertainment"}
They entertain, but their main purpose is to teach values and pass on heritage.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify the types"}
For *identify types of wise sayings*, name proverbs, idioms, riddles and folk tales, ideally with the Shona or Ndebele term.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain significance"}
For *explain the significance*, give the purpose and develop it with an example: "Proverbs give advice tactfully (1); for example, 'hands wash each other' reminds quarrelling relatives to cooperate (1)."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The main types of wise sayings are proverbs (tsumo/izaga), idioms (madimikira/izisho), riddles (zvirahwe/iziphicaphicwano) and folk tales (ngano/inganekwane).
- Proverbs are short fixed sayings with a lesson; idioms have figurative meanings; riddles are puzzles; folk tales are stories with a moral, often with animal characters and songs.
- Wise sayings teach values, give advice, settle disputes, entertain, sharpen thinking, build language and pass on identity and heritage.
- They survive today in books, schools, media, music and competitions.
:::
