## Introduction

In many rural kitchens in Zimbabwe you can still find a wooden mortar and pestle, a winnowing basket and a clay pot for cool drinking water. At a school culture day, learners may dance holding knobkerries or carved axes. In the Museum of Human Sciences in Harare and in other museums, you can see iron hoes and spearheads made by local blacksmiths long ago.

These objects are **indigenous tools**. They were made from local materials with local skills, and they allowed people to hunt, farm, cook and build long before factory-made tools arrived. In this Form 1 lesson you identify indigenous hunting and production tools, explain how they were made and used, and consider why they still matter for identity and heritage. How iron working developed in the Iron Age is covered in the history lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indigenous tool"}
A tool made by local people from local materials (wood, iron, stone, clay, grass, reeds, skins) using skills passed down through generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Hunting tool"}
A tool or method used to kill or catch wild animals for meat, skins and other products, such as a spear, bow and arrow, club, trap, snare or net.
:::

:::definition{term="Production tool"}
A tool used to produce or process food and other goods, such as a hoe for farming, grinding stones for flour or a clay pot for cooking.
:::

:::definition{term="Blacksmith"}
A skilled worker who heats iron in a fire and hammers it into tools and weapons. In Shona a skilled craftsperson is a **mhizha**.
:::

:::definition{term="Bellows"}
A device, traditionally made from animal skins attached to clay pipes, that pumps air into a furnace or fire to make it hot enough to smelt or work iron.
:::

## Hunting tools

Hunting provided meat, skins for clothing and bags, horns, and ivory for trade. It was also a test of skill and courage, and it was controlled by custom: chiefs regulated hunting, and totem taboos protected certain animals.

| Tool or method | Shona / Ndebele | How it was made | How it was used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spear | pfumo / umkhonto | Iron blade forged by a blacksmith, fixed to a wooden shaft | Thrown at or thrust into large animals such as antelope |
| Bow and arrow | uta nemuseve (Shona) | Bow of bent, flexible wood with a string of sinew; arrows of reed or wood with iron or bone tips, sometimes poisoned | Shot from a distance at birds and animals |
| Club (knobkerrie) | tsvimbo / induku | Carved from hard wood with a heavy knob at the end | Thrown at small animals and birds; also used for defence |
| Axe | demo / izembe | Iron head forged and fitted into a wooden handle | Cutting and finishing animals; also for clearing bush |
| Deadfall trap | riva (Shona) | A heavy flat stone propped up on sticks with bait beneath | The stone falls and traps or kills small animals such as rats and hares |
| Pit trap | — | A deep hole dug on an animal path and covered with branches and grass | Larger animals fall in and are caught |
| Snare | — | A noose of bark fibre or plant string tied to a bent branch | The noose tightens around an animal's neck or leg |
| Net | mambure (Shona) | Woven from plant fibre | Used to catch fish, birds or small animals driven into it |
| Hunting dogs | — | Trained by the hunter | Track, chase and hold animals during a hunt |

Hunting was often a group activity. Men of the village would go together with dogs and spears, share the meat according to custom, and give a portion to the chief.

:::keyfact{title="Hunting today"}
Today wildlife is protected by law. Hunting without a licence or permit, and setting wire snares, is illegal poaching. The Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority protects wildlife. Indigenous hunting tools are now studied as heritage, not used to hunt protected animals.
:::

## Production tools

| Tool | Shona / Ndebele | How it was made | How it was used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hoe | badza / ikhuba | Iron blade forged by a blacksmith, fitted to a wooden handle | Digging, planting and weeding fields |
| Axe | demo / izembe | Iron head in a wooden handle | Cutting trees, clearing land, firewood, building |
| Grinding stones | guyo (lower stone) and huyo (upper stone) in Shona | A flat or hollowed lower stone and a smaller round upper stone | Grain was ground into flour by rubbing the upper stone on the lower |
| Mortar and pestle | duri and mutswi (Shona) | Mortar carved from a hollowed log; pestle a long, heavy wooden pole | Pounding grain to remove husks or make flour; pounding groundnuts |
| Winnowing basket | rusero (Shona) | Woven from grass, reeds or palm leaves into a flat tray | Tossing grain so that the wind blows away the husks (chaff) |
| Basket | tswanda (Shona) | Woven from grass, reeds, bark or palm leaves | Carrying and storing grain, vegetables and other goods |
| Clay pot | hari / imbiza | Clay coiled into shape by hand, smoothed, decorated and fired | Cooking, brewing, storing water and grain |
| Bellows | — | Goat or other animal skins attached to clay pipes | Pumping air into a furnace to smelt iron ore and work iron |

### How iron tools were made

1. **Mining**: iron ore was dug from the ground.
2. **Smelting**: the ore was heated with charcoal in a clay furnace. Bellows pumped air in to make the fire very hot, and the iron separated from the rock.
3. **Forging**: the blacksmith reheated the iron and hammered it on a stone anvil into hoes, axes, spearheads and arrowheads.
4. **Finishing**: wooden handles and shafts were carved and fitted.

Iron working was a respected skill, often kept within certain families, and iron hoes were valuable enough to be used in exchange and sometimes in marriage payments.

:::example{title="From field to meal with indigenous tools"}
Follow a crop of sorghum through a family's year using only indigenous tools.

1. **Clearing**: the axe clears bush from the field.
2. **Planting and weeding**: the hoe (badza/ikhuba) digs the soil and removes weeds.
3. **Harvesting and carrying**: heads of sorghum are cut and carried home in baskets.
4. **Processing**: the grain is pounded in the mortar (duri) with the pestle (mutswi), winnowed in the rusero, then ground into flour on the grinding stones.
5. **Cooking and storing**: the flour is cooked in a clay pot, and spare grain is stored in a granary.

Each tool had its job, and together they supported the whole food system.
:::

## Importance of indigenous tools for identity and heritage today

- **Heritage and identity**: the tools show the skill, creativity and knowledge of our ancestors, and they are part of who Zimbabweans are.
- **Continued use**: many rural families still use hoes, axes, mortars, winnowing baskets and clay pots, which are cheap, repairable and need no electricity.
- **Ceremonies and culture**: knobkerries, carved axes and spears are carried in dances and at ceremonies; clay pots are used for traditional beer at some ceremonies.
- **Crafts and income**: basket weavers, potters and carvers sell their work locally and to tourists.
- **Education and museums**: tools in museums and schools teach young people about the past.
- **Sustainable technology**: they use local, renewable materials and show ways of living without waste.

:::example{title="A craft group in Binga"}
A fictional women's craft group in Binga weaves baskets using palm leaves and natural dyes.

1. **Skill passed on**: older members teach girls and young women the weaving patterns.
2. **Identity**: the patterns are recognised as part of local heritage.
3. **Income**: the baskets are sold to visitors and shops, paying school fees.
4. **Conservation**: the group harvests palm leaves carefully so the plants regrow.

An indigenous production tool has become a source of pride, income and heritage.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up hunting and production tools"}
Some tools, such as the axe, were used for both. When asked for a production tool, say how it was used to produce or process goods (clearing land, chopping firewood).
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a tool without its use"}
"Mortar and pestle" earns little alone. Add its use: "used to pound grain to remove husks and make flour".
:::

:::mistake{title="Suggesting snares are acceptable today"}
Indigenous hunting is studied as history and heritage. Today, hunting without a permit and setting snares are illegal.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous tools were made from local materials such as wood, iron, stone, clay, grass and skins.
- Hunting tools included spears, bows and arrows, knobkerries, axes, traps (such as the riva), pits, snares, nets and hunting dogs.
- Production tools included hoes, axes, grinding stones, mortar and pestle (duri and mutswi), winnowing baskets, baskets, clay pots and bellows.
- Iron tools were made by mining ore, smelting it with charcoal and bellows, and forging it.
- Indigenous tools matter today for heritage, identity, rural use, ceremonies, crafts, education and sustainability; hunting itself is now controlled by law.
:::
