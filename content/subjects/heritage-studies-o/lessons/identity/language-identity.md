## Introduction

At a school sports day in Bulawayo, a runner hears someone in the crowd cheering in her home language. She turns her head at once: that voice sounds like home. At a funeral, people who have never met before discover that they share a language and immediately treat one another as relatives. When Zimbabwe's national team plays abroad, supporters sing in Shona, Ndebele and English together.

Language does much more than pass on information. It tells others, and ourselves, who we are and where we belong. This Form 1 lesson explains how language carries identity at the personal, community and national level and why it matters for unity and heritage. The many languages and cultures of Zimbabwe's peoples, and the kinds of wise sayings, are studied in their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Language"}
A system of words, sounds, signs and rules that people use to communicate thoughts, feelings and knowledge. Sign language is a full language that uses hand shapes, movements and facial expressions.
:::

:::definition{term="Identity"}
The characteristics that make a person or group who they are and set them apart from others, such as name, family, totem, language, culture, religion and nationality.
:::

:::definition{term="Mother tongue"}
The first language a person learns at home, usually from their parents or carers.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral tradition"}
Knowledge, history, stories, songs and sayings passed by word of mouth from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="National identity"}
A shared sense of belonging to a nation, expressed through shared history, symbols, values and languages.
:::

## Aspects of language that carry identity

### Names

Many Zimbabwean names carry meaning in an indigenous language and often record an event, a hope or a feeling at the time of birth.

| Name | Language | Common meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tendai | Shona | Be thankful |
| Rudo | Shona | Love |
| Nyasha | Shona | Grace or mercy |
| Chipo | Shona | Gift |
| Sipho | Ndebele | Gift |
| Themba | Ndebele | Hope or trust |
| Thandeka | Ndebele | Loved or lovable |
| Nokuthula | Ndebele | Peace |

A name links a child to a language, a family's history and its hopes.

### Greetings

Greetings show respect and belonging. In Shona, people greet with words such as *mangwanani* (good morning) and *makadii?* (how are you?), often with clapping. In Ndebele, people say *salibonani* (hello, literally "we see you") and *linjani?* (how are you?). Younger people may kneel or bow slightly when greeting elders. The way a person greets shows their upbringing and respect for others.

### Songs, music and dance

Lullabies, work songs, church hymns, wedding songs and protest songs of the liberation struggle are sung in indigenous languages. Singing together builds a shared identity.

### Oral traditions and wise sayings

Folk tales, proverbs, riddles, idioms, praise poetry and family histories are passed on through language. They hold the community's knowledge and values.

### Ways of thinking and respect

Indigenous languages carry ideas that are hard to translate, such as *unhu* (Shona) and *ubuntu* (Ndebele), the idea that a person's humanity is shown through respect and care for others. Many languages also have respectful forms of address, for example using the plural to speak politely to an elder. Learning these forms teaches respect.

## Levels of identity carried by language

| Level | How language expresses it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal | Name, mother tongue, accent | A child named Tafadzwa ("we have been made happy") knows her family celebrated her birth |
| Family and clan | Totem names and praise poetry, family stories | Reciting totem praises at a ceremony |
| Community | Local dialect, songs, sayings, ceremonies | A community's own songs at a rain-making ceremony |
| National | Officially recognised languages, the national anthem, national songs | Singing the anthem in Shona, Ndebele or English at Independence Day |

:::example{title="Language and belonging in a new town"}
Mthulisi moves from Lupane to Harare for work.

1. **Personal**: at first he speaks Ndebele only at home, but he keeps his name and accent, which remind him of his roots.
2. **Community**: he joins a church where some members speak Ndebele, and quickly feels at home.
3. **National**: at work he learns Shona phrases and uses English for official tasks, so he can relate to colleagues from many backgrounds.

Language links Mthulisi to his own community while helping him belong to the wider nation.
:::

## Language, unity and heritage

Language can unite people. When people understand each other's languages, they build trust, trade, marry and work together. Zimbabwe's Constitution recognises 16 languages, which shows that the nation values all its peoples. Learning a second indigenous language is a way of respecting fellow citizens.

Language also passes on heritage. When a language is no longer spoken, the stories, songs, names for plants and animals and knowledge it carried may be lost. This is why families, schools and the media are encouraged to use and teach indigenous languages.

Language can also divide if people use it to exclude, mock or look down on others. Respectful attitudes towards every language are part of good citizenship.

:::example{title="A school language day"}
A fictional school in Gweru holds an annual language day.

1. Learners present poems, greetings and songs in Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Venda, sign language and other languages spoken by their families.
2. Learners who speak smaller languages feel proud rather than shy.
3. Others learn greetings in a new language and gain respect for classmates.

The day strengthens personal identity and national unity at the same time.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying language is only for communication"}
Language also carries identity, values, history and heritage. Exam answers should go beyond "it helps people talk".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting sign language"}
Sign language is a full language and one of Zimbabwe's officially recognised languages.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing aspects without linking them to identity"}
"Names" earns little. Explain: "Names carry meaning in an indigenous language, so they link a child to the family's history and language."
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify aspects of language"}
For *identify aspects of language that carry identity* (Paper 2 Section A), name names, greetings, songs, oral traditions, proverbs, praise poetry, ways of addressing elders.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the link"}
For *explain the link between language and identity*, give the aspect and how it shows who a person is or where they belong: "Totem praises are recited in the family's language (1), which tells a person their clan and ancestry (1)."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Language is a system of words, sounds or signs used to communicate, and it carries identity.
- Names, greetings, songs, oral traditions, wise sayings and respectful forms of address all express identity.
- Language links a person to their family, community and nation.
- Shared and respected languages promote unity; losing a language means losing heritage.
- Every language deserves respect, including sign language.
:::
