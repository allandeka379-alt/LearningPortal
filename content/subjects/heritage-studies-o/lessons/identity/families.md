## Introduction

When Tatenda's class was asked to describe their families, the answers were all different. Tatenda lives with his parents and two sisters in a flat in Harare. Thandeka lives on a homestead near Gwanda with her grandparents, two uncles, their wives and many cousins. Farai lives with his mother only. Nokuthula, whose parents have died, looks after her two younger brothers with help from neighbours and an aunt who visits.

All of these are families. A family is the first place where we learn who we are, and the family we belong to shapes our name, our totem, our language and our place in the community. In this lesson you learn to identify the different types of families, describe the relationships within them, and assess the roles family members play in the home, the community and the nation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Family"}
A group of people related by blood, marriage or adoption who usually live together or keep close ties, and who care for and support one another.
:::

:::definition{term="Nuclear family"}
A family made up of parents and their children only.
:::

:::definition{term="Extended family"}
A family that includes, as well as parents and children, other relatives such as grandparents, uncles, aunts and cousins, often living together or close by and sharing responsibilities.
:::

:::definition{term="One-parent (single-parent) family"}
A family in which one parent brings up the children, because of death, divorce, separation, work away from home or because the parents never married.
:::

:::definition{term="Child-headed family"}
A household in which a child under 18 takes the main responsibility for running the home and caring for younger brothers and sisters, usually because the parents have died or are absent.
:::

:::definition{term="Monogamous and polygamous families"}
A **monogamous** family is based on the marriage of one husband to one wife. A **polygamous** (polygynous) family is based on the marriage of one husband to two or more wives.
:::

## Types of families and the relationships within them

| Type | Who lives in it | Typical relationships |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nuclear | Father, mother and their children | Close parent-child bonds; decisions made by the parents; common in towns |
| Extended | Parents, children, grandparents, uncles, aunts, cousins | Many adults share care and discipline; elders are respected and consulted; cousins grow up like brothers and sisters |
| One-parent | One parent and the children | The parent carries all roles; older children often take on extra responsibilities; relatives may help |
| Child-headed | Children only, led by an older child | The eldest child acts as parent; siblings depend heavily on each other; relatives, neighbours and organisations may support them |
| Monogamous | Husband, one wife and their children | One set of parents; children share the same mother and father |
| Polygamous | Husband, two or more wives and their children | Each wife may have her own house; the senior (first) wife often has special respect; children of different mothers are brothers and sisters |

Families can belong to more than one type. A monogamous family living with grandparents is both monogamous and extended. A **blended** family forms when a parent remarries and the children of earlier relationships live together.

Many Zimbabwean families move between types over time. A nuclear family in town often stays closely tied to the extended family in the rural home, sending money, attending ceremonies and caring for elderly relatives.

:::example{title="Identifying a family type"}
Sipho lives in Bulawayo with his father, his father's wife and their three children. His grandmother joined them last year.

1. **Marriage**: one husband and one wife, so the family is monogamous.
2. **Members**: parents, children and a grandparent live together, so it is also an extended family.
3. **Relationships**: Sipho's grandmother helps with childcare and tells stories; his parents still make the main decisions.

Always look at both the marriage (monogamous or polygamous) and who lives in the household (nuclear or extended).
:::

## Roles of family members

### In the home

- **Parents and guardians** provide food, shelter, clothing, school fees and health care; protect, love and discipline children; and teach values and skills.
- **Grandparents** care for children, pass on history, totems, customs and wise sayings, and give advice.
- **Uncles and aunts** (such as vatete/ubabakazi and sekuru/umalume) advise young people and help settle disputes.
- **Children** respect and obey parents and elders, help with household chores, study hard and care for younger siblings and elderly relatives.

### In the community

- Families take part in community work parties (nhimbe/ilima), funerals, weddings and ceremonies.
- Family members serve on school development committees, church committees and residents' associations.
- They care for vulnerable neighbours, such as the elderly, orphans and the sick.
- They obey community rules and help keep the area clean and safe.

### In the nation

- Families raise law-abiding, hard-working citizens: they are the foundation of the nation.
- Adult members pay taxes, vote in elections and obey the law.
- Families produce food and goods, run businesses and provide workers.
- They pass on national values, languages and heritage, and teach respect for national symbols.

:::example{title="Assessing roles in a child-headed household"}
Nokuthula, aged 16, heads a household in a village near Gwanda after her parents died.

1. **In the home**: she cooks, budgets the little money the family has, and makes sure her brothers go to school, taking on a parent's roles.
2. **Strain**: she sometimes misses school herself, which shows the burden on child heads.
3. **Support**: her aunt visits monthly, the village head checks on them, and the school helps with fees.
4. **Assessment**: the household survives because the extended family and community share the roles that parents would normally carry.

This shows why all members of a family and community have roles to play, not only parents.
:::

### Changing roles

Roles in Zimbabwean families are changing. Many women work outside the home and many men share household tasks. Both girls and boys are expected to study and to do chores. Migration has left grandparents caring for many children. The Constitution recognises the equality of men and women, and many families now share roles more equally than in the past.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing extended and polygamous families"}
An extended family includes relatives beyond parents and children. A polygamous family has one husband with more than one wife. A family can be one, both or neither.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only the roles of parents"}
Questions on roles of family members expect children, grandparents, aunts and uncles too, and roles in the community and nation, not only in the home.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating one family type as the only normal type"}
All family types exist in Zimbabwe. Describe each fairly; the advantages and disadvantages of monogamy and polygamy are studied in a separate lesson.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify and distinguish"}
For *identify the types of families* (Paper 2 Section A), name the type and give its members: "Extended family: parents, children and other relatives such as grandparents."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Assess the roles"}
*Assess* means judge how well roles are carried out and how important they are. Give the role, its effect, and any limits (for example, migration of parents).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Types of families include nuclear, extended, one-parent, child-headed, monogamous, polygamous and blended families.
- Relationships differ: many adults share roles in extended families, while one person carries most roles in one-parent and child-headed households.
- Family members have roles in the home (care, provision, discipline, chores), the community (work parties, ceremonies, care for the vulnerable) and the nation (law-abiding citizens, taxes, production, heritage).
- Roles are changing as more women work, men share household tasks and grandparents care for children of migrant parents.
:::
