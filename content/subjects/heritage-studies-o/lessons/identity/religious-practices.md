## Introduction

Zimbabwe is a country of many faiths. On a Sunday morning churches are full; on Fridays Muslims gather for prayer; in many homes families also honour their ancestors at important moments; and in some places communities still gather before the rains to ask for a good season. Many Zimbabweans hold more than one of these traditions in their lives.

This Form 4 lesson describes **indigenous religion**: the beliefs and practices that existed in Zimbabwe's communities long before Christianity and Islam arrived, and which many people still follow. It covers belief in God and in spirits, the role of spirit mediums, the main rituals and sacred places, why they matter, and how indigenous religion relates to other faiths. As with every faith, it is described here with respect: you are learning what people believe and do, not being asked to believe it or to rank faiths.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indigenous religion"}
The beliefs and practices about God, spirits and the sacred that developed among a community's own people over many generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Ancestral spirits (midzimu / amadlozi)"}
The spirits of a family's departed elders, believed to watch over, guide and protect their living descendants.
:::

:::definition{term="Territorial spirits (mhondoro)"}
Spirits believed to be those of founding or royal ancestors, who are guardians of a whole territory or clan's land and are linked to rain and the fertility of the land.
:::

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro)"}
A person through whom a spirit is believed to speak to the living, giving guidance to a family or community.
:::

:::definition{term="Ritual"}
A set of actions, words and offerings performed in a fixed way for a religious purpose.
:::

:::keyfact{title="God in indigenous belief"}
God is known by several names, including **Mwari** and **Nyadenga** in Shona and **uMlimo** (also Mwali) in Ndebele. God is seen as the creator and the source of rain and life, usually approached through the ancestors rather than directly.
:::

## Beliefs: God and the spirits

In indigenous belief there is one supreme God, the creator. Below God, the **spirits of the dead** play a large part in daily life. The living and the departed are seen as one continuing family: the ancestors care for their descendants, and descendants honour and remember them.

### Forms of spirits

| Form of spirit | Who they are believed to be | Role in belief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family ancestral spirits (*midzimu* / *amadlozi*) | Departed parents, grandparents and elders of a family | Protect and guide the family; are honoured at family rituals |
| Territorial or clan spirits (*mhondoro*) | Spirits of founding or royal ancestors of a clan or territory | Guardians of the land; linked to rain, harvests and the welfare of a whole community |
| Alien or wandering spirits (*mashave*) | Spirits of people or beings not of the family | Believed to give special skills, such as healing, hunting or dancing |
| Avenging spirit (*ngozi*) | The spirit of a person who was seriously wronged, such as someone killed | Believed to bring misfortune until the wrong is put right through compensation and reconciliation |

The belief in the avenging spirit has a strong moral side: it teaches that serious wrongs, especially taking a life, must be acknowledged and put right between families.

### Spirit mediums

A **spirit medium** (*svikiro*) is a person through whom a spirit, especially a *mhondoro*, is believed to speak. Mediums are consulted at important times, lead or take part in ceremonies, give advice on community matters, and are expected to live by strict rules. In Zimbabwe's history, spirit mediums played important roles, for example in the risings of 1896-97 and in the liberation struggle; those events are studied in the history topics.

## Religious practices and rituals

| Practice | What happens | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family offerings (*kupira*) | Family members address the ancestors, sometimes with snuff, traditional beer or a meal | Thanks, requests for protection, informing the ancestors of family events |
| Bira | An all-night ceremony with mbira music, song and dance; a spirit may be believed to come through a medium | Honouring and consulting ancestral or territorial spirits |
| Rain-making (*mukwerera* / *ukucela izulu*) | Before the rains, the community, led by elders and mediums, makes offerings | Asking God and the ancestors for rain; community unity |
| *Kurova guva* / *umbuyiso* | Some time after a burial, the spirit of the deceased is believed to be brought home | Welcoming the deceased as a family ancestor |
| Respect for sacred places | Certain hills, caves, pools and forests are protected by rules | Honouring the spirits; in practice also conserving the environment |

### Sacred places

Some places are regarded as holy. The best known is **Njelele** in the **Matobo Hills**, a shrine associated with Mwari/uMlimo, where people have long come to seek rain. Many communities also have local sacred hills, pools, caves or groves where trees may not be cut, animals may not be hunted, or noise is forbidden. These rules protect the places and, in effect, the environment around them.

:::example{title="A family informs the ancestors"}
Tatenda's family near Mutoko is about to send him to university in Bulawayo.

1. **The ritual:** the evening before, his grandfather gathers the family and, speaking respectfully, tells the family's ancestors of Tatenda's journey and asks for their protection.
2. **Symbolism:** a little snuff is placed on the ground as an offering.
3. **Meaning for the family:** Tatenda leaves knowing his family and his ancestors are with him.
4. **Other faiths:** the next morning the family also prays at their church. For them, honouring the ancestors and Christian prayer sit side by side.
:::

:::example{title="Sacred rules and conservation"}
A fictional community near Chimanimani protects a forest around a sacred pool.

1. **The rule:** no cutting trees, no hunting and no washing in the pool.
2. **Religious reason:** the place is believed to belong to the territorial spirits.
3. **Practical effect:** the forest has survived while surrounding land has been cleared; it protects a spring that supplies water in the dry season.
4. **Significance:** indigenous religion has also acted as a system of environmental care.
:::

## Significance of indigenous religious practices

- **Identity and continuity:** they link the living with their ancestors and history.
- **Moral guidance:** belief that ancestors watch over the family encourages good behaviour; the *ngozi* belief teaches that wrongs must be put right.
- **Unity:** ceremonies bring families and communities together.
- **Comfort:** rituals help people cope with death, illness and uncertainty.
- **Environmental care:** sacred places and seasonal rules protect forests, water and wildlife.
- **Heritage:** music (mbira), dance and oral tradition are kept alive through ceremonies.

## Relations with other faiths

Christianity spread widely from the nineteenth century; Islam has been present through trade and later migration; other faiths are also found. Relations have taken several forms:

- **Opposition:** some missionaries and some churches today condemned indigenous practices, which led some converts to abandon them.
- **Coexistence:** many Zimbabweans practise more than one tradition, for example attending church and also holding *kurova guva/umbuyiso*.
- **Blending:** many African-initiated churches combine Christian teaching with African forms of worship, music and healing.
- **Dialogue:** the Constitution protects freedom of conscience and religion, and many faith leaders work together on community issues.

Different faiths answer the same human questions about God, death and how to live well. Respecting other people's beliefs, while holding your own, is part of Unhu and of citizenship.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying indigenous religion has no belief in God"}
Indigenous religion has one supreme God (Mwari/uMlimo/Nyadenga), approached through the ancestors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing midzimu with mhondoro"}
*Midzimu/amadlozi* are family ancestors; *mhondoro* are territorial or clan spirits concerned with the land and rain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing beliefs disrespectfully"}
Use neutral language: "people believe", "is regarded as". The same respect is owed to every faith.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous religion believes in one God (Mwari/uMlimo/Nyadenga), approached through ancestral spirits.
- Forms of spirits include family ancestors (*midzimu/amadlozi*), territorial spirits (*mhondoro*), alien spirits (*mashave*) and the avenging spirit (*ngozi*); spirit mediums (*svikiro*) are believed to speak for spirits.
- Practices include family offerings, bira, rain-making (*mukwerera/ukucela izulu*), *kurova guva/umbuyiso* and respect for sacred places such as Njelele in the Matobo Hills.
- They give identity, moral guidance, unity, comfort, environmental care and heritage.
- Indigenous religion has met opposition, coexistence, blending and dialogue with other faiths; the Constitution protects freedom of religion.
:::
