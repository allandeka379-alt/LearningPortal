## Introduction

In many Zimbabwean homes there is an aloe plant by the door, a bundle of dried leaves in the kitchen for making tea on a cold evening, or a grandmother who knows which wild plants are good to eat. Long before clinics and pharmacies, communities built up detailed knowledge of the plants around them: which were food, which were poisonous, and which were used to care for the sick.

This knowledge is part of Zimbabwe's **heritage** and of people's **identity**. This lesson describes some herbs and their traditional uses, how the knowledge is passed on, why it is valuable, and why the plants need protecting. It also explains clearly why **herbs are not a substitute for trained health care**: some plants are harmful, doses are uncertain, and anyone who is ill should see a trained health worker.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Herb"}
A plant whose leaves, roots, bark, flowers or seeds are used for food, flavouring, fragrance or in traditional health practices.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous knowledge"}
Knowledge developed by local communities over generations through experience of their environment, passed on mainly by word of mouth and practice.
:::

:::definition{term="Traditional medicine"}
The knowledge, skills and practices based on a community's beliefs and experience, used to maintain health and to treat illness. In Zimbabwe it is practised alongside modern health care.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
Protecting and wisely using natural resources so that they are not destroyed and remain available for future generations.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Regulation in outline"}
Traditional medical practitioners in Zimbabwe are registered and regulated under the law, and the Medicines Control Authority of Zimbabwe (MCAZ) regulates medicines, including the registration of herbal products sold as medicines.
:::

## Some herbs and their traditional uses

The syllabus names aloe, ndorani, ginger, blackjack and isihaqa among the herbs to study. Local names differ between areas and languages, so the same name can refer to different plants in different places. Ask an elder or visit a herbal garden to learn which plants the names refer to where you live.

| Herb | Traditional uses (as reported) | Safety note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aloe (*gavakava* in Shona) | The gel from the leaves is applied to minor burns, cuts and dry skin; also used in cosmetics | Some aloe species are poisonous if swallowed; do not swallow the sap |
| Blackjack | Young leaves eaten as a vegetable relish; traditionally applied to minor wounds | Wash well; a wound that does not heal needs a clinic |
| Ginger | Root used in cooking and in hot drinks for colds and upset stomachs | A food plant; persistent illness needs a health worker |
| Zumbani (Shona) / umsuzwane (Ndebele), also called fever tea | Leaves brewed as a tea for colds and flu symptoms, and their scent used to repel insects | Tea eases symptoms for some people but cures no disease |
| Moringa | Leaves eaten as a nutritious vegetable or dried as a powder | A food; not a cure for disease |
| Mufandichimuka (resurrection bush) | Leaves brewed as a tea | Not a substitute for treatment |

These are **traditional uses**, not proven cures. Some plants contain substances that are studied by scientists, and a few modern medicines were first developed from plants. But using an unknown plant, or too much of a known one, can cause poisoning, and a herb can interfere with other medicines.

:::keyfact{title="Safety first"}
Herbs do not cure diseases such as HIV, TB, malaria, diabetes or COVID-19. Anyone who is ill should be seen by a trained health worker at a clinic or hospital, and should tell the health worker about any herbs they are taking.
:::

## How indigenous knowledge of herbs is passed on

- **Within families:** grandparents and parents show children plants while gathering firewood, herding or cooking.
- **Apprenticeship:** traditional healers (*n'anga*/*inyanga* and others) train apprentices over many years.
- **Practice and observation:** children learn by helping prepare foods and remedies.
- **Stories, songs and names:** plant names often describe the plant's use or appearance, helping people remember.
- **Today:** herbal gardens, community projects, books and research recording the knowledge.

Because the knowledge is often unwritten, it can be lost when elders die without passing it on.

:::example{title="Recording a grandmother's knowledge"}
Nyasha's school in Bindura runs a heritage project.

1. Nyasha interviews her grandmother about plants used in the family for food and care.
2. She records the local names, how each plant is recognised, which part is used, and any warnings her grandmother gives.
3. Her class plants a small school garden of common food herbs such as moringa and ginger.
4. The teacher reminds the class that the project records **heritage**, not medical advice: nobody should treat an illness with the plants, and anyone who is unwell must see a health worker.

Nyasha's family knowledge is now preserved, and the class has learnt to respect both indigenous knowledge and modern medicine.
:::

## Value of indigenous knowledge of herbs

- **Heritage and identity:** knowledge of plants is part of who a community is.
- **Food and nutrition:** wild vegetables and herbs add vitamins and minerals to the diet.
- **Primary care and comfort:** for minor complaints, some households use herbs alongside clinic care.
- **Research:** scientists study traditional plants; some may lead to new medicines.
- **Income:** herbal teas, cosmetics and oils (aloe products, for example) create jobs, if products are made and sold safely and legally.
- **Environmental knowledge:** knowing plants encourages care for the land.

## The need to conserve herbs

Many useful plants are under threat from:

- **over-harvesting**, especially when roots or bark are taken for sale and the plant dies;
- **deforestation** for farming, firewood and settlements;
- **veld fires** and **climate change**;
- **loss of knowledge**, so people no longer recognise or value the plants.

Ways of conserving them include harvesting only leaves or part of a plant, planting herbal gardens, protecting sacred and community forests, community rules against over-harvesting, and recording knowledge before it is lost.

:::example{title="A community rule in Chimanimani"}
Villagers notice that a valued tree is dying because traders strip its bark.

1. **Problem:** over-harvesting threatens the plant.
2. **Action:** the village head and the community agree a rule: bark may be taken only from one side of a tree and only by people who know how.
3. **Support:** the community starts a nursery to plant seedlings, with advice from forestry officers.
4. **Outcome:** the trees recover, showing that indigenous rules and modern conservation can work together.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Claiming herbs cure diseases"}
Write "traditionally used for" or "used to ease", never "cures". Always mention seeing a trained health worker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the conservation part"}
Questions often ask why herbs must be conserved. Name the threat and the method of conservation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring dosage and safety"}
A natural plant can still be harmful. Mention dosage risks and poisonous species.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Herbs such as aloe, blackjack, ginger and zumbani/umsuzwane have traditional uses in food and health practices.
- Indigenous knowledge of herbs is passed on in families, through apprenticeship to healers, by practice and through names and stories.
- This knowledge has value for heritage, nutrition, research and income, but herbs are not proven cures.
- Herbs face threats from over-harvesting, deforestation, fires and loss of knowledge; they can be conserved through careful harvesting, gardens, community rules and recording.
- Safety: some plants are poisonous and doses are uncertain; anyone who is ill should see a trained health worker.
:::
