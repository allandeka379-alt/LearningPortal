## Introduction

Ask a Zimbabwean "Who are you?" and the answer often begins with family: "I am Tendai, son of …, of the Shumba totem, from Chivi." Identity is not something a person builds alone. It is shaped by the family that names and raises you, and by the community whose gatherings you grow up attending: funerals, weddings, work parties, ceremonies for rain or harvest.

This Form 3 lesson explains how the family and the community shape identity, and describes the main **indigenous community gatherings, ceremonies and events**, their purpose, the ceremonial tools used, and how they build belonging. It builds on what you know about totems, Unhu and norms and values. Birth and death rites as rites of passage are covered in their own lesson; here the focus is on the gathering and what it does for the community.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Identity"}
The sense of who a person is: name, family, totem, language, culture, community and nation, and how others recognise them.
:::

:::definition{term="Community gathering"}
An occasion when members of a community come together for a shared purpose: work, worship, celebration, mourning or decision-making.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceremony"}
A formal event with set actions, words and often music, held to mark something important, such as asking for rain or honouring the dead.
:::

:::definition{term="Ceremonial tools"}
Objects used in a ceremony for a practical or symbolic purpose: drums, mbira, rattles, clay pots, ceremonial axes, staffs, cloths and snuff.
:::

## How the family and community shape identity

| Shaped by the family | Shaped by the community |
| --- | --- |
| Name and surname | Membership of a village, ward or chiefdom |
| Totem and clan praises | Shared language and dialect |
| Family history and genealogy | Shared customs, ceremonies and sacred places |
| First language | Norms and values enforced by elders and leaders |
| Norms and values learnt at home | Roles in community events (e.g. drummers, singers, cooks) |
| Religious beliefs and practices | A sense of belonging and mutual support |

The family gives a person **who they are**; the community gives them **where they belong**. Gatherings are where both meet: at a funeral or ceremony, a young person sees relatives, hears totem praises, learns their role and is recognised by others.

## Indigenous gatherings, ceremonies and events

| Gathering | What happens | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Work party (*nhimbe* in Shona, *ilima* in Ndebele) | Neighbours help one household with a big task, such as weeding, harvesting or building; the host provides food and drink | Cooperation; getting heavy work done quickly; strengthening ties |
| The chief's granary (*zunde ramambo/isiphala senkosi*) | Community members work a field under the chief; the grain is stored | A food reserve for orphans, widows, the elderly and the needy, and for drought years |
| Rain-making ceremony (*mukwerera/ukucela izulu*) | Before the rains, the community gathers, led by elders and spirit mediums, to ask God and the ancestors for rain; traditional beer is brewed and offered | Religious: seeking rain and good harvests; unity of the community |
| Bira | An all-night ceremony with mbira music, singing and dancing to communicate with ancestral spirits | Religious: honouring and consulting ancestors; seeking guidance and protection |
| Funeral | The community mourns, digs the grave, cooks, comforts the bereaved | Honouring the dead; supporting the family; showing Unhu |
| *Kurova guva / umbuyiso* | Held some time after a burial; the spirit of the deceased is believed to be brought home to the family | Welcoming the deceased as an ancestor; often the time to share the estate |
| Harvest and thanksgiving | Celebrations after harvest; first fruits may be offered with thanks | Thanking God and the ancestors; sharing; celebration |

In the Ndebele state of the nineteenth century, the **inxwala** was an important annual first-fruits ceremony held at the king's capital, bringing the nation together.

### Ceremonial tools

| Tool | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Drums (*ngoma*; Ndebele *isigubhu*) | Rhythm for singing and dancing; calling people together |
| Mbira | Music at bira and other ceremonies; believed to help communication with ancestors |
| Rattles (*hosho*) | Keeping rhythm with the mbira and drums |
| Clay pots | Brewing and serving traditional beer offered at ceremonies |
| Ceremonial axes and staffs | Symbols of authority carried by elders and leaders |
| Cloths in particular colours and snuff | Used by spirit mediums and in offerings |

:::example{title="A nhimbe in Murehwa"}
Farai's grandmother has a large maize field to weed but is too old to do it alone.

1. **Invitation:** she lets neighbours know, and her family brews traditional beer and prepares food.
2. **The work:** on the day about twenty neighbours weed the whole field by midday, singing as they work.
3. **The meal:** everyone eats together; elders give thanks.
4. **Identity and belonging:** young people learn work songs, see Unhu in action and know that their grandmother's household belongs to a community that cares.
5. **Reciprocity:** the grandmother's family will help the others when their turn comes.
:::

:::example{title="A rain-making ceremony near Matobo"}
In a dry year, villagers in a fictional community near the Matobo Hills gather before the rains.

1. **Preparation:** elders agree on the date; grain is collected from each household to brew traditional beer.
2. **The ceremony:** led by elders and a spirit medium, people sing, dance and make offerings, asking God (Mwari/uMlimo) and the ancestors for rain.
3. **Rules:** during the season, the community observes customs such as respecting sacred places.
4. **Significance:** whatever the weather brings, the ceremony unites the community, honours its beliefs and reminds everyone of their shared dependence on the land.
:::

:::example{title="A funeral in Bulawayo shapes a young person's identity"}
When Themba's grandfather dies, the family gathers at the home in Bulawayo before travelling to the rural home for the burial.

1. **Family identity:** at the funeral, elders recite the family's totem praises and history; Themba hears names of ancestors he has never heard before.
2. **Community identity:** neighbours, church members and the burial society arrive with food and money, and the women of the area take turns cooking.
3. **Roles:** Themba and his cousins are given tasks, such as fetching water and helping elderly guests, and are thanked publicly.
4. **Result:** Themba leaves knowing more about who he is (his clan and ancestors) and where he belongs (a community that stood by his family). The gathering has taught him identity in a way no textbook could.
:::

## Significance of indigenous gatherings

- **Identity:** people learn and display totems, praises, songs, dances and language.
- **Belonging and unity:** working, mourning and celebrating together builds bonds between households.
- **Social security:** work parties, the chief's granary and funeral support help the weak and the bereaved.
- **Religious expression:** ceremonies express beliefs about God and the ancestors.
- **Passing on culture:** the young learn by watching and taking part.
- **Conflict resolution:** gatherings bring people together and give elders a chance to settle disputes.

Today, some gatherings are less common, especially in towns. Communities have new forms of gathering, such as burial societies, church groups, savings clubs and village development meetings, which do many of the same jobs.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nhimbe and zunde ramambo"}
*Nhimbe/ilima* is a work party for one household; *zunde ramambo/isiphala senkosi* is a communal field whose grain is stored for the needy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing ceremonies disrespectfully"}
Describe beliefs neutrally: "people believe the spirit is brought home", not "people pretend".
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing events without purpose"}
Examiners want the purpose or significance of each gathering, not just its name.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** gatherings (Paper 2 Section A): give Shona and Ndebele names together where you can (*mukwerera/ukucela izulu*).
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the significance**: link each gathering to an effect on identity, unity, security or culture.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The family shapes identity through name, totem, history, language and values; the community through belonging, language, customs and shared events.
- Indigenous gatherings include *nhimbe/ilima*, *zunde ramambo/isiphala senkosi*, *mukwerera/ukucela izulu*, bira, funerals, *kurova guva/umbuyiso* and harvest celebrations.
- Ceremonial tools include drums, mbira, rattles, clay pots, ceremonial axes and staffs, and cloths.
- Gatherings build identity, unity, social security, religious expression and the passing on of culture.
- New forms such as burial societies and church groups now do some of the same work, especially in towns.
:::
