## Introduction

Think of a Sunday lunch at your grandmother's home. Before anyone eats, a younger child brings water so that the elders can wash their hands. The children greet each visitor properly, perhaps by clapping or kneeling. An aunt quietly corrects a nephew who interrupted an adult, and an older sister shows a little brother how to carry plates without dropping them. Nobody calls this a lesson, yet everyone at that table is learning how to behave.

This everyday learning is called **socialisation**. It is the first topic of Heritage Studies because everything else in the subject (identity, norms and values, citizenship, rights and responsibilities) begins with how a person learns to live with others. You already know a great deal about it from your own life: in this lesson you learn the words and ideas that let you explain it in an examination.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Socialisation"}
The lifelong process by which a person learns the norms, values, language, skills and roles of their family and society, so that they can live and work well with other people.
:::

:::definition{term="Agent of socialisation"}
A person, group or institution that teaches an individual how to behave in society, for example the family, the school, peers, the community, religious groups and the media.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary socialisation"}
The first stage of socialisation, in early childhood, mainly within the family. The child learns language, basic manners, personal care and the family's values.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary socialisation"}
Socialisation that continues later in life outside the home, through the school, peers, religious groups, the community, the workplace and the media.
:::

:::definition{term="Norms and values"}
**Norms** are the accepted rules of behaviour in a group (greeting elders, sharing food). **Values** are the beliefs about what is good and important that lie behind the norms (respect, honesty, hard work).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Family words to learn in two languages"}
Paternal aunt: **vatete** (Shona), **ubabakazi** (Ndebele). Maternal uncle: **sekuru** (Shona), **umalume** (Ndebele). Grandmother: **ambuya** (Shona), **ugogo** (Ndebele).
:::

## Why socialisation matters

A newborn baby does not know how to speak, greet, share or work. Without socialisation, a person could not take part in family life, go to school or hold a job. Socialisation:

- passes on **culture and heritage** (language, customs, songs, beliefs) from one generation to the next;
- teaches **acceptable behaviour**, so that people can live together peacefully;
- builds **identity**: a child learns who they are, which family and clan they belong to, and their totem;
- prepares young people for **adult roles** at home, at work and as citizens.

## The agents of socialisation

The family is the **first** agent, but it is not the only one. As a child grows, other agents add to (and sometimes compete with) what the home teaches.

| Agent | Stage | What it mainly teaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family (the home) | Primary | Language, manners, personal care, family values, identity, household work |
| School | Secondary | Knowledge and skills, rules, punctuality, discipline, teamwork |
| Peers (friends of the same age) | Secondary | Friendship, sharing, fashions and interests; can push in good or bad directions |
| Community | Secondary | Respect for elders and leaders, community work, ceremonies, local customs |
| Religious groups | Secondary | Moral teachings, worship, rites of passage |
| Media | Secondary | Information, entertainment, role models, advertising messages |

Each of the other agents has its own lesson in this topic. Here we look closely at the home.

## The home as the first agent of socialisation

The home matters most in the early years because the child spends most of their time there, learns by copying the people they love, and depends on them completely. What is learned at home often lasts for life.

The family socialises children in several ways:

1. **Instruction**: telling a child what to do and why ("Greet your uncle properly").
2. **Example (role modelling)**: children copy what adults actually do, not only what they say.
3. **Rewards and sanctions**: praise, a small gift or extra responsibility for good behaviour; a warning or the loss of a privilege for bad behaviour.
4. **Stories, songs and sayings**: folk tales, proverbs and lullabies carry the family's values.
5. **Participation**: children learn work by doing it alongside adults, such as cooking, herding, planting or fixing things.

### The role of each family member

In Zimbabwean families, many relatives share the work of bringing up a child. Roles differ from family to family, and today many tasks are shared by men and women alike, but the table shows the roles commonly described.

| Family member | Common roles in socialisation |
| --- | --- |
| Mother (amai / umama) | First teacher of language and personal care; teaches cooking, cleanliness, manners and care for others; gives emotional support |
| Father (baba / ubaba) | Provides for and protects the family; teaches responsibility, work skills and family history; guides decisions |
| Grandparents (sekuru and ambuya / ugogo) | Tell folk tales and family history; teach totems, praise poetry, proverbs and customs; give advice and comfort |
| Paternal aunt (vatete / ubabakazi) | Advises girls (and boys) on growing up, behaviour, courtship and marriage; helps settle family disputes |
| Maternal uncle (sekuru / umalume) | Advises boys (and girls) on growing up and responsibility; plays a part in family ceremonies and decisions |
| Older brothers and sisters | Care for younger children; act as role models; teach games, chores and how to behave at school |

:::example{title="Learning respect at a family gathering"}
Nyasha, aged eight, runs into the room while her grandfather is speaking and asks loudly for a drink.

1. **Instruction**: her mother tells her quietly to wait until the elder has finished.
2. **Example**: Nyasha watches her older brother kneel to greet the visitors and copies him.
3. **Sanctions and rewards**: later her vatete praises her for serving the visitors politely.
4. **Result**: Nyasha has learned a norm (do not interrupt elders) and the value behind it (respect).

This shows primary socialisation using several methods at once.
:::

:::example{title="Learning work and responsibility at home"}
In a family in Lupane, Themba, aged twelve, helps his father look after the goats every afternoon, and his sister Thandeka helps their mother and grandmother in the garden.

1. Both children learn practical skills: herding, counting animals, planting and watering.
2. They learn values: hard work, responsibility and caring for the family's property.
3. On weekends the two children swap tasks, so each learns all the skills of the home.

The family is preparing both children for adult life, and the swap shows how many families today avoid dividing work strictly by gender.
:::

### When the home does not socialise well

Not every home is a good agent of socialisation. Parents may work far away (some in other countries), a child may live in a child-headed household, or there may be conflict, violence or substance abuse at home. Children may then learn harmful behaviour or miss important lessons. In such cases the extended family, the school, religious groups and the community often step in to support the child. Recognising this helps you answer questions about the **limitations** of the family as an agent.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Defining socialisation as making friends"}
Socialisation is not the same as socialising (meeting friends). It is the process of learning the norms, values, skills and roles of society.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying socialisation stops in childhood"}
Primary socialisation happens in childhood, but socialisation is lifelong: adults keep learning at work, in church or mosque, and in the community.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing roles without explaining them"}
"The mother teaches" earns little. Say what she teaches and why it matters: "The mother teaches the child its first language, so the child can communicate and take part in family life."
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the aunts and uncles"}
Vatete/ubabakazi is the father's sister; sekuru/umalume is the mother's brother. Check which side of the family each belongs to.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Define and state"}
For *define socialisation* (Paper 2 Section A), include two parts: learning the norms, values and skills of society, and the purpose (to live and function in it).
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the role of a family member"}
For *explain*, give the role and then develop it: "The grandmother tells folk tales (1), which teach children values such as honesty and keep the family's heritage alive (1)."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Discuss or how far"}
In a Section B essay on the family, show both sides: the family is the first and most lasting agent, but its influence is shared with or weakened by the school, peers and the media. End with a clear judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Socialisation is the lifelong process of learning the norms, values, language, skills and roles of society.
- Primary socialisation happens in the family in early childhood; secondary socialisation happens through the school, peers, community, religion, the workplace and the media.
- The family socialises through instruction, example, rewards and sanctions, stories and participation.
- Mothers, fathers, grandparents, vatete/ubabakazi, sekuru/umalume and older siblings each have roles in teaching language, manners, work, values and identity.
- When the home cannot socialise a child well, the extended family and community often step in.
:::
