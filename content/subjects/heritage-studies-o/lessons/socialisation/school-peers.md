## Introduction

At 07:00 on a Monday the bell rings at a secondary school in Masvingo. Learners line up for assembly in clean uniforms, a prefect notes who is late, and the head announces that the netball team won its match on Saturday. Later, in the break, a group of friends decides whether to join the debate club or spend the afternoon at the shops.

In one morning these learners have been shaped by two powerful agents of socialisation: the **school** and their **peers**. You already know that the family is the first agent (primary socialisation). The school and peers are the main agents of **secondary socialisation** during the teenage years, when young people spend more time away from home than ever before.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Formal education"}
Organised learning in an institution such as a school, following a set curriculum, timetable and rules, and leading to examinations and certificates.
:::

:::definition{term="Hidden curriculum"}
The lessons a school teaches without putting them on the timetable, such as punctuality, obedience, respect for authority, teamwork and how to win and lose fairly.
:::

:::definition{term="Peer group"}
A group of people of about the same age and status who interact closely, such as classmates, friends or teammates.
:::

:::definition{term="Peer pressure"}
The influence that members of a peer group have on one another to behave, dress or think in a certain way. It can be positive or negative.
:::

:::definition{term="Clique"}
A small, closed group of friends who keep others out and expect members to follow the group's ways.
:::

## How the school socialises learners

The school continues and widens what the home began. It brings together children from many families, languages and faiths and teaches them to live by shared rules.

| What the school uses | What learners learn | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| School rules and a code of conduct | Discipline, obedience to lawful authority, accepting consequences | No fighting; detention for breaking rules |
| The timetable, bells and registers | Punctuality, time management, routine | Arriving before assembly; handing in homework on time |
| Uniform | Neatness, equality, belonging to a community | Every learner wears the same uniform, whatever their family's wealth |
| Lessons and examinations | Knowledge, skills, hard work, honesty | Studying for tests; no cheating |
| Teachers | Role models of conduct, speech and responsibility | A teacher who is fair, patient and well prepared |
| Prefects, monitors and class captains | Leadership and responsibility | A monitor keeps the class register |
| Sport, clubs and societies | Teamwork, fair play, talents, healthy competition | A football team, a drama club, a Junior Farmers club |
| Assemblies, national events and the National Schools Pledge | Patriotism, respect for national symbols and shared values | Singing the national anthem; marking national days |

Much of what a school teaches is part of the **hidden curriculum**. Nobody writes "punctuality" on the timetable, but a learner who is marked late every morning soon learns it.

:::example{title="The school socialises through a club"}
Farai joins the school's environmental club in Form 2.

1. **Skills**: he learns to plant and care for trees and to sort waste.
2. **Values**: he learns care for the environment and responsibility.
3. **Teamwork**: the club plans a clean-up day together, so members learn to share tasks.
4. **Leadership**: in Form 3 he is elected secretary, so he learns to keep minutes and speak in meetings.

The club teaches far more than its subject: it prepares Farai for adult life as a citizen.
:::

## The role of peers

Peers matter more and more as children grow. Teenagers want to belong, to be accepted and to try new identities, so friends become very influential. Peers socialise each other by:

- **sharing interests and information**: music, sport, fashion, new technology and ideas;
- **giving acceptance and support**: friends listen, encourage and help in difficult times;
- **setting group norms**: how the group dresses, speaks and behaves;
- **acting as role models**: a hard-working or kind friend can inspire others, while a reckless one can lead others astray;
- **teaching social skills**: sharing, compromising, settling arguments and working in teams.

### Positive and negative peer pressure

| Positive peer pressure | Negative peer pressure |
| --- | --- |
| Encouraging friends to study and attend lessons | Pushing friends to bunk lessons or cheat |
| Joining a sports team or club together | Encouraging drinking, smoking or drug and substance abuse |
| Persuading a friend to report bullying | Bullying or excluding others to fit in with a clique |
| Supporting a friend to resist early sexual activity | Pressuring friends into risky relationships or early sexual activity |
| Volunteering together for community work | Encouraging theft, vandalism or disrespect for elders |

:::example{title="Resisting negative pressure"}
After school, Chipo's friends invite her to a party where alcohol will be served, and they tease her when she hesitates.

1. **Recognise the pressure**: they are trying to make her act against her values and the law on alcohol for under-18s.
2. **Think of the consequences**: her health, her safety, her family's trust and her studies.
3. **Say no clearly**: "No thanks, I'm going home." She does not need to give a long excuse.
4. **Offer an alternative**: she suggests they watch a match together at the community hall instead.
5. **Choose supportive friends**: she spends more time with friends who respect her decisions.

This shows the skills of assertiveness and good decision-making that help young people use peer influence well.
:::

### Choosing friends wisely

Good friends share your values, respect your decisions, encourage your studies and are honest with you. A young person can test a friendship by asking: does this friend make me a better person, or do I have to hide what we do from my family and teachers? Talking to a parent, a teacher, a counsellor or a trusted adult helps when peer pressure becomes difficult.

## School and peers compared

Both agents work at the same time and influence each other. The school is a **formal** agent with official rules, set times and trained staff; peers are an **informal** agent with unwritten rules. The school tries to channel peer influence into good directions through clubs, sports, prefects and peer educators, while peers can either support the school's values or work against them.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating peer pressure as always bad"}
Peer pressure can be positive (encouraging study, sport, volunteering) as well as negative. Exam answers that mention only the negative side lose marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the school only teaches subjects"}
The school also socialises through the hidden curriculum: punctuality, discipline, teamwork, leadership and respect for authority.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing school rules without the lesson behind them"}
"Uniform" alone earns little. Explain: "Uniform teaches neatness and equality, because rich and poor learners look the same."
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Explain the role of the school"}
For a Paper 2 Section A *explain* question, name the method and the value it teaches: "School rules teach discipline (1) because learners who break them face consequences such as detention (1)."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Analyse peer influence"}
When asked to *analyse* or *assess* the impact of peers, give positive and negative effects, each with an example, and finish with a judgement: the effect depends on the friends a young person chooses.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The school socialises through rules, routines, uniform, lessons, teachers, prefects, sport and clubs.
- The hidden curriculum teaches punctuality, discipline, teamwork and respect for authority.
- Peers are people of similar age; they share interests, give support, set group norms and act as role models.
- Peer pressure can be positive (study, sport, volunteering) or negative (substance abuse, bunking, bullying).
- Young people can resist negative pressure by thinking of consequences, saying no clearly and choosing supportive friends.
:::
