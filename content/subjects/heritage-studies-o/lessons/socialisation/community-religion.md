## Introduction

In a village near Gwanda, a teenager walking home from school passes an elderly neighbour carrying firewood. Before he has decided what to do, two other adults call out to him to help. That evening his mother has already heard about it, and she thanks him. Many Zimbabweans believe that a child belongs to the whole community, not only to its parents, so every adult may guide, correct and praise the children around them.

On Sunday the same teenager may go to church; his friend may attend the mosque on Friday; another neighbour's family may take part in a ceremony for the ancestors. Each of these faith communities also teaches young people how to live.

In this lesson you study two agents of secondary socialisation: the **community** and **religious beliefs and groups**. You should already know the meaning of socialisation and the role of the family.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Community"}
A group of people living in the same area, or sharing the same interests or beliefs, who interact and support one another, such as a village, a ward, a suburb or a congregation.
:::

:::definition{term="Elders"}
Older, respected members of a family or community who give advice, settle disputes and pass on knowledge and customs.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanction"}
A reaction that rewards good behaviour (a positive sanction, such as praise) or punishes bad behaviour (a negative sanction, such as a warning, a fine or ridicule).
:::

:::definition{term="Work party (nhimbe / ilima)"}
A gathering at which community members work together on one family's task, such as weeding or harvesting, and are thanked with food and drink.
:::

:::definition{term="Religion"}
A set of beliefs about God, the divine or the spiritual world, with practices of worship and teachings about how people should live.
:::

## How the community socialises young people

### Elders and neighbours

Elders correct children's behaviour, give advice and tell the history of the area. Neighbours watch over children and report problems to parents. Because many adults share the same expectations, children learn that good behaviour is expected everywhere, not only at home.

### Traditional leaders

Chiefs, headmen and village heads uphold customs and settle disputes. Their courts and meetings show young people how conflicts are resolved and why rules matter. Young people learn respect for authority and for the community's values.

### Work parties and community projects

At a **nhimbe** (Shona) or **ilima** (Ndebele), neighbours help one family weed, harvest or build. Young people learn cooperation, hard work and the value of helping others, because one day the community will help their family in turn. Modern communities do the same through clean-up campaigns, school building projects and community gardens.

### Ceremonies and gatherings

Ceremonies such as weddings, funerals, rain-making ceremonies (**mukwerera** in Shona, **ukucela izulu** in Ndebele) and harvest celebrations teach young people their roles, songs, dances and customs. Attending a funeral, for example, teaches how to comfort the bereaved and how the community supports a family in grief.

### Praise and sanctions

The community rewards good conduct with praise, respect and responsibility, and discourages bad conduct with warnings, ridicule, fines imposed by a traditional court or exclusion from activities. Fear of shame in front of the community is a strong reason to behave well.

| Community agent or activity | What young people learn |
| --- | --- |
| Elders and neighbours | Respect, good manners, local history, obedience to advice |
| Traditional leaders and their courts | Respect for authority and custom; how disputes are settled fairly |
| Nhimbe / ilima and community projects | Cooperation, hard work, generosity, responsibility to others |
| Ceremonies (weddings, funerals, mukwerera / ukucela izulu) | Roles, songs, dances, customs; how to support others |
| Praise and sanctions | Which behaviour is accepted and which is not |

:::example{title="A community clean-up in Marondera"}
A residents' association in a fictional Marondera suburb organises a monthly clean-up and asks young people to join.

1. **Cooperation**: young people work alongside adults from many families.
2. **Responsibility**: they learn that a clean environment is everyone's duty, not only the council's.
3. **Role models**: they see adults volunteering their time without pay.
4. **Praise**: the association thanks them at its meeting, which encourages them to come again.

The community has taught citizenship and care for the environment through action, not only words.
:::

## The role of religious beliefs in socialisation

Zimbabwe is home to several faiths. Many people practise **Christianity** (in mission churches such as Catholic, Methodist, Anglican, Lutheran and Reformed churches, Pentecostal churches, and African Initiated Churches such as the Apostolic and Zionist churches). Many people also hold **indigenous religious beliefs**, and some combine them with Christianity. There are **Muslim** communities in towns and some rural areas, and smaller **Hindu**, **Jewish**, **Bahá'í** and other communities. The Constitution protects freedom of conscience and religion, so every faith is to be respected.

All of these faiths socialise their members in similar ways:

| How religion socialises | Indigenous religion | Christianity | Islam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Belief in God or the divine | Mwari (Shona) or uMlimo / uNkulunkulu (Ndebele), approached through ancestral spirits | God, revealed in the Bible and through Jesus Christ | Allah, revealed in the Qur'an through the Prophet Muhammad |
| Teachings on conduct | Respect for elders and ancestors, taboos (such as resting on chisi days), unhu/ubuntu | The Ten Commandments, love of neighbour | The Five Pillars, honesty, charity, respect for parents |
| Worship and gatherings | Family and community ceremonies for the ancestors (midzimu / amadlozi) | Sunday or Sabbath services, prayer, hymns | Daily prayers, Friday prayers at the mosque, Ramadan |
| Rites of passage | Naming, coming of age, marriage and funeral customs | Baptism, confirmation, church weddings and funerals | Naming, marriage and funeral rites |
| Youth learning | Elders, ceremonies, stories | Sunday school, youth fellowships, school Christian unions | Madrasa classes, youth groups |

Religion socialises people by:

- teaching **moral values**: honesty, kindness, forgiveness, respect, self-control and care for the poor;
- giving **rules for conduct** (commandments, taboos) and a sense of right and wrong;
- providing **identity and belonging** to a congregation or faith community;
- marking **rites of passage**, which teach people the duties of each stage of life;
- offering **youth organisations** where young people learn leadership, singing, service and friendship.

:::example{title="Two faiths, shared values"}
Mthulisi attends a Christian youth fellowship in Bulawayo; his classmate Nyasha attends madrasa classes at a mosque.

1. Both learn to pray and to respect their parents and elders.
2. Both are taught honesty and the duty to help the needy (through church collections or giving to charity in Islam).
3. Both take part in their communities' service projects.

Different faiths socialise young people in different ways, yet they share many values. Understanding this helps young Zimbabweans live together with respect.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Ranking religions"}
Exam answers describe what each faith teaches; they never say that one faith is better or worse than another.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting negative effects"}
Communities and religious groups can also pass on harmful practices (for example forced or child marriage, which is illegal). A balanced answer mentions this.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nhimbe and a ceremony"}
A nhimbe/ilima is a work party for a practical task; a ceremony such as mukwerera/ukucela izulu is a religious or cultural event.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Describe the role of religion"}
For Paper 2 Section A, give a function and an example: "Religion teaches moral values (1), for example the Ten Commandments teach Christians not to steal (1)."
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Assess the community"}
In a Section B essay, weigh the community's strengths (shared values, cooperation, support) against its weaknesses today (urban life, weaker ties, harmful practices), then conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The community socialises young people through elders, neighbours, traditional leaders, work parties, ceremonies, praise and sanctions.
- Nhimbe/ilima teach cooperation and hard work; ceremonies teach customs and roles.
- Indigenous religion, Christianity, Islam and other faiths teach moral values, rules of conduct, identity and rites of passage.
- Youth fellowships, Sunday schools and madrasa classes socialise young people in their faith.
- Every faith is described with respect and none is ranked; the Constitution protects freedom of religion.
:::
