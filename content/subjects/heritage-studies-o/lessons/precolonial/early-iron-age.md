## Introduction

When a farmer near Masvingo ploughs a new field and turns up pieces of old, thick, decorated pottery, an archaeologist may be called. Such broken pots, together with lumps of iron slag, grinding stones and the remains of hut floors made of daga (clay), are the main evidence for the **Early Iron Age**: the time when the first farming communities settled in what is now Zimbabwe.

These communities brought a new way of life. Instead of moving with game like the San, they built villages, grew crops, kept cattle, sheep and goats, and made tools of **iron**. In this Form 2 lesson you learn who they were, how they lived and why their arrival changed the history of the region. You should already know how the San and the Khoikhoi lived, so you can compare their way of life with that of farmers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Iron Age"}
The period in which people made and used tools and weapons of iron. In Zimbabwe it is usually divided into the Early Iron Age (roughly the first millennium CE) and the Later Iron Age (from about 1000 CE, when states such as Great Zimbabwe developed).
:::

:::definition{term="Smelting"}
Heating iron ore with charcoal in a clay furnace until the iron separates from the rock, so that it can be worked by a smith.
:::

:::definition{term="Archaeology"}
The study of the past through the material remains people left behind, such as pottery, tools, bones, seeds and the remains of buildings.
:::

:::definition{term="Tradition (in archaeology)"}
A group of sites that share the same style of pottery and way of life, often named after the place where it was first studied, such as Gokomere or Ziwa.
:::

:::definition{term="Subsistence farming"}
Growing crops and keeping animals mainly to feed one's own family and community rather than for sale.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to remember (approximate)"}
Early Iron Age farmers were living in Zimbabwe in the **first millennium CE** (roughly 2,000 to 1,000 years ago). The **Later Iron Age** begins at about **1000 CE**. These are approximate ranges: archaeologists date sites by methods such as radiocarbon dating, which give ranges rather than exact years.
:::

## Who were the Early Iron Age communities?

Early Iron Age farmers were **Bantu-speaking** peoples whose ancestors spread over much of central, eastern and southern Africa, bringing farming and iron working. Archaeologists group their sites into **traditions** by pottery style.

| Tradition | Area of Zimbabwe | Known for |
|---|---|---|
| Gokomere | South-central Zimbabwe, near Masvingo | Thick pottery decorated with comb-stamped and incised patterns; villages, iron working, livestock |
| Ziwa | Eastern highlands, around Nyanga | Distinctive decorated pottery; farming villages and iron working |
| Zhizo | South-western Zimbabwe and the Limpopo valley | Later in the first millennium; cattle-keeping and early trade links; glass beads at some sites |

Gokomere sites have been found near Great Zimbabwe, showing that farmers lived in the area long before the stone city was built. Other names of sites and traditions exist; you need to know that there were several regional traditions, and be able to name one or two.

## Economic activities

**Farming.** They grew crops such as **sorghum, finger millet and pearl millet**, and also beans, cowpeas and gourds, using iron hoes. Shifting cultivation was common: when the soil became tired, new fields were cleared.

**Livestock.** They kept **cattle, sheep and goats** for meat, milk, skins and exchange. Cattle later became an important form of wealth.

**Iron working.** Smiths smelted iron ore in clay furnaces using charcoal, then forged hoes, axes, spears, arrowheads, knives and ornaments. Iron tools made it easier to clear forests, cultivate fields and hunt.

**Pottery and crafts.** Women usually made pots by hand from clay, decorating them with patterns. Pots were used for cooking, storing grain and water, and brewing beer. People also made baskets, mats and clay figurines.

**Hunting, gathering and trade.** They still hunted and gathered wild foods. Goods such as iron tools, salt, pots and copper were exchanged between communities; in the later first millennium some communities began to receive glass beads and shells that came from the coast.

:::example{title="How archaeologists reconstruct a village"}
A fictional excavation near Gweru uncovers the following.

1. **Finds:** pieces of decorated pottery; burnt clay with pole impressions; iron slag; bones of cattle and goats; charred sorghum seeds; grinding stones.
2. **Inferences:** the decorated pottery identifies the tradition; burnt clay with pole marks shows pole-and-daga huts; slag shows iron smelting nearby; bones and seeds show livestock and crops; grinding stones show grain was ground into flour.
3. **Conclusion:** this was a settled farming village that made its own iron tools. The finds are evidence, not guesses, but each inference should be checked against other sites.
:::

## Social and political activities

**Villages.** People lived in **permanent or semi-permanent villages** of round huts built of poles and daga with thatched roofs, often with a cattle enclosure (kraal) and grain bins. Villages were larger and more settled than San camps, because fields and herds tied people to one place.

**Family and kinship.** Society was organised around the extended family and the lineage. Work was divided: men herded cattle, hunted, cleared land and worked iron; women farmed, made pottery and prepared food; children helped with herding and fieldwork.

**Leadership.** Leadership was mainly in the hands of **lineage or village heads** and elders. Communities were small and there were no large states yet. Over time, people who controlled more cattle or trade gained more influence, which prepared the way for the chiefdoms and states of the Later Iron Age.

**Beliefs.** Burials with grave goods and clay figurines suggest religious beliefs and respect for the dead, though their exact meaning is not known.

| Feature | San hunter-gatherers | Early Iron Age farmers |
|---|---|---|
| Food | Hunting and gathering | Crops and livestock, plus some hunting and gathering |
| Tools | Stone, bone and wood | Iron hoes, axes and spears, plus stone grinders |
| Settlement | Nomadic bands in shelters | Villages of pole-and-daga huts |
| Leadership | No chiefs; discussion among adults | Lineage and village heads; growing differences in wealth |
| Crafts | Rock art, tanning, ostrich eggshell containers | Pottery, iron smithing, basketry |

:::example{title="Why iron changed everything"}
Compare clearing one hectare of bush with a stone axe and with an iron axe.

1. **Stone axe:** slow, breaks easily; only small clearings are possible.
2. **Iron axe and hoe:** faster clearing and deeper digging; larger fields can be planted.
3. **Result:** more food is produced, villages grow, people can store surplus grain and keep more cattle.
4. **Long-term effect:** surplus food and wealth allowed some families to become leaders, leading in time to chiefdoms and states.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving exact dates"}
Do not write "the Early Iron Age began in 200 CE". Archaeological dates are approximate: say "in the first millennium CE" or "about 2,000 years ago".
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing Early and Later Iron Age"}
Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, Torwa and Rozvi are **Later** Iron Age states. Early Iron Age communities lived in villages without large stone buildings or states.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the evidence"}
When you describe an activity, you can strengthen the answer by naming the evidence: "they smelted iron, as shown by slag and furnace remains".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Early Iron Age farmers lived in Zimbabwe in the first millennium CE; their sites are grouped into traditions such as Gokomere, Ziwa and Zhizo.
- They grew sorghum and millets, kept cattle, sheep and goats, smelted iron and made decorated pottery.
- They lived in villages of pole-and-daga huts, organised by family and lineage, led by village or lineage heads.
- Iron tools allowed larger fields and surplus food, which in time led to chiefdoms and states.
- Evidence comes from archaeology: pottery, slag, bones, seeds and hut remains.
:::
