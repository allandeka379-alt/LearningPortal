## Introduction

In a rural village near Lupane, two neighbours argue about where a field boundary lies. They do not go straight to a magistrate. First they take the matter to the **village head**, who calls both families and hears them out under a tree. If the dispute cannot be settled there, it goes to the **headman**, and then to the **chief's** court. Across Zimbabwe, millions of people in communal areas still live under this chain of traditional leadership.

Long before colonial rule, the peoples of Zimbabwe organised themselves under leaders whose authority came from their family line, the ancestors and the community. In this Form 1 lesson you learn the hierarchy of indigenous leaders, what each does, how chiefs are chosen and installed, and why chieftainship still matters. You should already know from Topic 1 how families and communities teach norms; leaders are the people who uphold those norms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chieftainship"}
The office and authority of a chief: the right, usually inherited within a ruling family, to lead a defined area and its people according to custom.
:::

:::definition{term="Hierarchy"}
An arrangement of positions from the highest to the lowest, in which each level answers to the one above it.
:::

:::definition{term="Succession"}
The way in which a new leader is chosen to take the place of one who has died or left office.
:::

:::definition{term="Regalia"}
The special objects and dress that are signs of a leader's office, such as a staff, a skin, a hat or, today, an official sash or uniform.
:::

:::definition{term="Customary law"}
The rules and practices of a community, passed down by tradition and applied by traditional leaders, for example in family, land and inheritance matters.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Traditional leaders in the Constitution"}
**Chapter 15** of the Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013) recognises the institution of traditional leaders (chiefs, headmen and village heads), sets out their functions and requires them to act fairly and without party-political bias.
:::

## The hierarchy of indigenous leaders

Leadership in pre-colonial societies was arranged like a ladder. The names differ between languages and between states, but the pattern is similar.

![Hierarchy of indigenous leaders from top to bottom: king or paramount ruler (mambo, inkosi), chief (ishe, inkosi or induna), headman (sadunhu, induna of a smaller area) and village head (sabhuku), each with its own area and roles.](media:diagrams/indigenous-leadership-hierarchy.png "Figure: the hierarchy of indigenous leaders, from king to village head")

| Level | Shona term | Ndebele term (where known) | Main roles |
|---|---|---|---|
| King or paramount ruler | Mambo (for example, the Mutapa or Rozvi rulers) | Inkosi (the king) | Ruled the whole state; controlled land, trade and the army; led national ceremonies |
| Chief | Ishe / mambo of a chiefdom | Inkosi / induna | Led a chiefdom; allocated land; judged serious cases; led rain and harvest ceremonies |
| Headman | Sadunhu | Induna of a smaller area | Ruled a ward (dunhu) under the chief; settled disputes; reported to the chief |
| Village head | Sabhuku | (English: village head) | Led a village; settled small disputes; welcomed newcomers; reported deaths and births |

In the Ndebele state the king appointed **izinduna** to lead regiments and districts; many were chosen for ability and loyalty rather than only by birth. In Shona chiefdoms, chieftainship was usually **hereditary** within a ruling family. Other peoples, such as the Tonga, Venda and Kalanga, also had chiefs and village leaders.

## Roles of traditional leaders

Each level had duties to the people below it and to the leader above it.

- **Land**: chiefs held land on behalf of the community and allocated it for homes, fields and grazing; no one could sell it.
- **Justice**: leaders settled disputes in their courts (dare in Shona, inkundla in Ndebele), using customary law; minor cases started at the lowest level.
- **Religion and ceremonies**: chiefs led ceremonies such as **mukwerera / ukucela izulu** (rain-asking) and harvest thanksgiving, often working with spirit mediums.
- **Security**: kings and chiefs organised the defence of the community.
- **Welfare**: chiefs kept a granary (**Zunde raMambo**) whose grain fed orphans, widows, the elderly and visitors in times of hunger.
- **Custodians of culture**: leaders protected sacred places, forests and wells, and enforced taboos that conserved resources.

:::example{title="A dispute moves up the ladder"}
Tendai's goats eat Farai's maize in a village near Bindura.

1. **Village head (sabhuku):** hears both families; Tendai agrees to pay one goat, but Farai says the loss is bigger.
2. **Headman (sadunhu):** listens again, inspects the field and orders Tendai to pay two goats.
3. **Chief:** would hear the case only if one side still refused. Under customary law the chief's court could confirm the fine.
4. **Lesson:** cases start at the lowest level, which keeps justice close to the people and saves time and money.
:::

## How chiefs are chosen and installed

**Succession.** In many Shona chiefdoms the chieftainship rotates between the **houses** (branches) of the ruling family descended from the founding ancestor, so different houses take turns. Among others, the eldest son of the senior wife may succeed. The family elders discuss and nominate the heir.

**Spirit mediums.** In many communities a **spirit medium** (svikiro) of the founding ancestor is consulted to confirm that the ancestors accept the choice. This gives the chief religious as well as political authority.

**Installation.** The new chief is installed at a public ceremony. He receives the **regalia** of office, takes an oath or receives instructions from the elders on how to rule, and the people show their acceptance. Beer is brewed, and the ancestors are informed.

**Today**, after the family chooses an heir under customary rules, the appointment is made official by the State, and the new chief is installed in a public ceremony attended by officials and the community.

:::example{title="Choosing an heir: a fictional chiefdom"}
The chief of the fictional Chidziva chiefdom dies.

1. **The family meets:** elders of the three houses agree that it is the turn of the second house.
2. **The medium is consulted:** the spirit medium confirms the choice at a ceremony.
3. **The district office is informed:** the name is sent through government channels for official appointment.
4. **Installation:** the new chief receives his regalia before the community.
5. **Result:** because custom was followed, the people accept him and disputes over succession are avoided.
:::

## The significance of chieftainship

| Significance | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Unity and identity | The chief is a symbol of the community, its history and its link to the ancestors |
| Law and order | Disputes are settled quickly and cheaply, close to home, by people who know the families |
| Land administration | Communal land is allocated fairly so that every family has a place to live and farm |
| Preserving culture | Ceremonies, taboos and customs are kept alive under the chief's guidance |
| Welfare | Zunde raMambo and community support protect the vulnerable |
| Link with government | Today traditional leaders help to explain government programmes and bring development to their areas |

### Traditional leaders today

Chapter 15 of the Constitution says traditional leaders should promote and uphold cultural values, help development, administer communal land under the law, and resolve disputes under customary law. Chiefs meet in provincial assemblies of chiefs and in the National Council of Chiefs, and chiefs are represented in the Senate. The Constitution requires traditional leaders not to act in a partisan way, so that they can serve everyone in their area. Some people argue that traditional leaders have too little power today; others worry about leaders being drawn into politics. Both views show how important the office remains.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the levels"}
The **sabhuku** is the village head, not the chief; the **sadunhu** is the headman of a ward. Learn the ladder from top to bottom.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying chiefs own the land"}
Chiefs **hold and allocate** communal land on behalf of the people; they do not own it and may not sell it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing instead of explaining"}
"Settles disputes" is a list point. Explain it: "The chief settles disputes under customary law (1), which keeps peace cheaply and close to home (1)."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The hierarchy runs from king (mambo/inkosi) to chief, headman (sadunhu) and village head (sabhuku).
- Leaders allocated land, judged disputes under customary law, led ceremonies, organised defence and cared for the needy through Zunde raMambo.
- Chiefs were chosen by the ruling family, often in rotation between houses, confirmed by spirit mediums and installed with regalia.
- Chieftainship gave unity, order, fair land use and a link with the ancestors.
- Chapter 15 of the Constitution recognises traditional leaders today and requires them to be non-partisan.
:::
