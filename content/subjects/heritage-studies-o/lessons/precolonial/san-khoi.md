## Introduction

On a school trip to the Matobo Hills near Bulawayo, a Form 1 class climbs to a rock shelter. On the smooth granite wall are paintings in red, brown and white: a line of hunters with bows, a herd of kudu, a giraffe, people who seem to be dancing. The guide explains that these paintings were made long before anyone in the area farmed or built in stone. Who painted them, and what do they tell us?

The answer takes us to the **first peoples** of southern Africa: the **San**, who lived by hunting and gathering, and the **Khoikhoi**, who kept herds of cattle and sheep. Their way of life lasted for many thousands of years. In this lesson you learn how they lived, how their communities were organised, and what they have passed on to Zimbabwe. You should already know from Topic 1 that every society teaches its members a way of life; here you see one of the oldest ways of life in our region.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hunter-gatherers"}
People who get their food by hunting wild animals and collecting wild plants, fruits, roots, honey and insects, rather than by farming or keeping livestock.
:::

:::definition{term="Herders (pastoralists)"}
People whose way of life depends mainly on keeping domestic animals such as cattle, sheep and goats, moving with them to find grazing and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Band"}
A small group of related families, usually a few dozen people, who live and move together. The band was the main social and political unit of the San.
:::

:::definition{term="Nomadic"}
Moving from place to place rather than living in one permanent settlement, usually following food, water or grazing through the seasons.
:::

:::definition{term="Rock art"}
Paintings and engravings made on the walls of rock shelters and on boulders. In Zimbabwe most rock paintings are linked to the ancestors of the San.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Where to see rock art in Zimbabwe"}
The **Matobo Hills** in Matabeleland South hold one of the richest collections of rock paintings in southern Africa, and are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (2003). Rock paintings are also found in many other places, for example at **Domboshava** near Harare. Koisan is one of the sixteen officially recognised languages in section 6 of the Constitution.
:::

## The San: hunter-gatherers

The San lived in small **bands** of related families. Because they followed game and the ripening of wild plants, they were **nomadic**: they camped in rock shelters, caves or simple grass and branch shelters, and moved on when food became scarce. They owned few possessions, since everything had to be carried.

### Economic organisation

Work was shared according to age and gender. Men usually hunted, using bows and arrows tipped with poison made from plants and insects, as well as spears, traps and snares. Women and children gathered most of the daily food: roots, bulbs, berries, nuts, wild fruits, honey, caterpillars and other insects. Women used **digging sticks**, sometimes weighted with a bored stone, to dig up roots. Ostrich eggshells were used to carry and store water. Skins were tanned and made into clothing and bags. Because food was gathered rather than grown, the San knew a great deal about plants: which were edible, which were poisonous and which could be used as medicine.

### Social organisation

Food from a hunt was **shared** among all members of the band, so no family went hungry while others ate. Elders were respected for their knowledge. Marriage often linked one band to another, which helped bands to share hunting areas peacefully. Dancing, music and storytelling were important; some rock paintings show people dancing, which many researchers link with healing and religious ceremonies.

### Political organisation

The San had **no chiefs or kings**. Decisions were made by discussion among adults, and the advice of elders and of skilled hunters carried weight. Disputes were often settled by talking, and sometimes by a family moving to another band. This form of leadership by agreement suited small groups who owned little property.

| Feature | San (hunter-gatherers) | Khoikhoi (herders) |
|---|---|---|
| Main source of food | Wild animals and wild plants | Milk and meat from cattle and sheep, plus some gathering |
| Settlement | Small nomadic bands in shelters and caves | Larger camps that moved with the herds |
| Wealth | Very little personal property; sharing | Livestock was wealth and gave status |
| Leadership | No chiefs; decisions by discussion, elders respected | Clans with headmen; leaders often owned more livestock |
| Key tools | Bow and poisoned arrows, digging stick, ostrich eggshell containers | Tools for herding, milking and tanning; also bows and spears |

## The Khoikhoi: herders

The Khoikhoi were related in language and culture to the San, but their way of life was built around **livestock**, especially fat-tailed sheep and cattle. They lived mainly further south and west in southern Africa, and they are taught in Heritage Studies as the herding people of the region in this early period. They moved with their herds to find grazing and water, building portable huts of mats over a frame of poles.

Because livestock could be counted and inherited, the Khoikhoi had more differences in **wealth** than the San. Families with large herds had more influence, and groups were organised into clans, each with a leader. Cattle were used for milk and meat, for exchange and for gifts at important events. Like the San, they also hunted and gathered when needed.

:::example{title="Reading a rock painting as a source"}
A painting in a rock shelter shows six figures with bows following a large antelope, and two figures bending over the ground with sticks.

1. **What it shows:** men hunting with bows; people digging with sticks, probably for roots.
2. **What we can infer:** hunting and gathering were both part of daily life, and work was divided.
3. **What it does not tell us:** the exact date, the names of the people, or the meaning the artist intended.
4. **Conclusion:** rock art is valuable evidence about the San way of life, but it must be read carefully and compared with other evidence, such as tools found by archaeologists.
:::

## The heritage passed on

Although later farming communities settled the land, the San and Khoikhoi left a lasting **heritage**.

| Heritage | Why it matters today |
|---|---|
| Rock art | A record of early life and belief; part of the Matobo Hills World Heritage Site; attracts tourism and research |
| Knowledge of plants | Knowledge of edible and medicinal plants that later communities also used |
| Hunting skills and tools | Tracking, trapping and use of the bow and arrow |
| Tool making | Skilful use of stone, bone, wood and shell |
| Tanning skins | Making clothing, bags and blankets from animal skins |
| Living in balance with nature | Taking only what was needed and moving on, which let plants and animals recover |
| Languages and communities | Small San communities still live in western Zimbabwe, for example in Tsholotsho District; Koisan is an officially recognised language |

:::example{title="Protecting a rock art site"}
A fictional Form 1 club in Gwanda finds that visitors have scratched names on a painted rock shelter and some have splashed water on the paintings to make them brighter.

1. **Problem:** the paintings are fragile and cannot be replaced.
2. **Responsible body:** National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe is responsible for protecting such sites.
3. **Action the club suggests:** report the damage, put up a sign explaining why the paintings must not be touched or wetted, and teach other learners about the site.
4. **Lesson:** heritage belongs to everyone, so everyone has a duty to protect it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the San \"primitive\""}
Do not describe the San as backward. Their way of life required deep knowledge of animals, plants and the land, and it lasted far longer than farming has. Use respectful, factual language.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the San and the Khoikhoi"}
The San were mainly **hunter-gatherers** with no livestock; the Khoikhoi were mainly **herders** of cattle and sheep. Many answers lose marks by describing one as the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the San had chiefs"}
San bands had no chiefs or kings. Leadership came from elders and experienced members, and decisions were reached by discussion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The San were nomadic hunter-gatherers living in small bands; the Khoikhoi were herders of cattle and sheep.
- San men usually hunted with bows and poisoned arrows; women gathered wild plants with digging sticks.
- San bands had no chiefs: decisions were made by discussion and elders were respected; food was shared.
- Livestock gave the Khoikhoi wealth and status, so their society had more differences in wealth.
- Their heritage includes rock art (for example in the Matobo Hills), knowledge of plants and medicines, hunting and tool-making skills, and tanning.
:::
