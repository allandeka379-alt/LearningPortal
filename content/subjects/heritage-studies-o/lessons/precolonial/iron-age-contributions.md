## Introduction

Think about a single day in a Zimbabwean home. Breakfast is sadza made from a grain first grown here by Iron Age farmers (or from maize, which came later). A grandmother stores seed in a clay pot like those made a thousand years ago. A neighbour takes a dispute to the village head. On the news, a mining company announces a new gold mine on a site where gold was dug centuries before. In the evening, the national flag with the Zimbabwe Bird flies over the school.

Each of these is a **contribution** of the Iron Age communities you have studied: the Early Iron Age farmers and the Later Iron Age states of Great Zimbabwe, Mutapa, Torwa, Rozvi and the Ndebele. In this Form 2 lesson you list these contributions and **assess their significance**: how much do they still matter in Zimbabwe today?

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Contribution"}
Something that a community gave or passed on to later generations, such as a skill, an idea, an institution or a material object.
:::

:::definition{term="Contemporary society"}
Society as it is today, in our own time.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous knowledge systems"}
The knowledge, skills and practices developed by local communities over many generations, for example in farming, medicine, conservation and building.
:::

:::definition{term="Heritage"}
What is inherited from the past and valued in the present, both tangible (buildings, objects) and intangible (skills, beliefs, customs).
:::

:::definition{term="Significance"}
How important something is, judged by how widely it is used, how much it affects people's lives, and how much it shapes identity.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Heritage you can see"}
**Great Zimbabwe** (1986) and **Khami** (1986) are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The **Zimbabwe Bird**, carved in soapstone at Great Zimbabwe, appears on the national flag and coat of arms. The country's name comes from *dzimba dza mabwe*, houses of stone.
:::

## The main contributions

| Contribution | What the Iron Age communities did | How it continues today |
|---|---|---|
| Farming | Grew sorghum and millets, beans and gourds; kept cattle, sheep and goats | Small grains are promoted as drought-tolerant crops; cattle remain wealth and are used in roora/lobola |
| Iron working and crafts | Smelted iron; made hoes, axes, spears; pottery, basketry, weaving, wood and soapstone carving | Blacksmiths, potters and carvers still work; crafts are sold to tourists and exported |
| Mining | Dug gold, copper and iron ore | Many modern mines were opened on or near ancient workings; mining is a major part of the economy |
| Trade | Traded gold and ivory with the coast; exchanged goods between communities | Zimbabwe still trades minerals with the world; local markets continue old patterns of exchange |
| Stone building | Built Great Zimbabwe, Khami and other stone sites without mortar | Sites attract tourists; dry-stone skills are admired; national identity and name |
| Political structures | Kings, chiefs, headmen, village heads; tribute; councils | Traditional leaders are recognised in Chapter 15 of the Constitution |
| Traditional medicine | Knowledge of medicinal plants and healing | Traditional healers are consulted by many people; plants are researched |
| Religious beliefs | Belief in Mwari and the ancestors; mhondoro mediums; rain-asking | Mukwerera/ukucela izulu, bira and kurova guva/umbuyiso are still practised by many families |

### Farming and food

Iron Age farmers developed ways of farming in dry conditions: choosing **drought-tolerant crops** such as sorghum and millets, and storing grain in raised granaries. These crops are still grown and encouraged in dry areas. Cattle remain central to family life, used for ploughing, milk, meat and **roora/lobola**.

### Mining and trade

Iron Age miners followed gold reefs underground using fire-setting and iron tools. Prospectors in the colonial period searched for these **ancient workings** to find gold, and many mines began there. The trade in gold and ivory linked the plateau to the Indian Ocean world, a pattern of export trade that continues with minerals today.

### Crafts and skills

Iron Age smiths, potters, weavers and carvers developed skills that are still practised. Village blacksmiths still make and repair hoes and axes in some areas. Women potters make clay pots for cooking, brewing and storing water, and many families say food and water taste better from them. Baskets, mats and wooden plates are made from local materials, and soapstone carving, which goes back to the birds of Great Zimbabwe, is now an art form sold to visitors and exported. These crafts provide income, especially in rural areas, and keep indigenous knowledge alive.

### Traditional medicine

Iron Age communities built up a great deal of knowledge about plants used for healing. Many people still consult traditional healers, and the knowledge is valued as part of the nation's heritage. Scientists study some plants to find out whether they contain useful medicines. However, claims that a plant cures a serious disease must be tested, and anyone who is ill should see a trained health worker.

### Political and religious institutions

The hierarchy of **chief, headman and village head** grew out of Iron Age political systems. It is still the main form of local leadership in communal areas. Religious ideas such as belief in **Mwari**, respect for the **ancestors** and the role of **spirit mediums** remain part of the faith of many Zimbabweans, alongside Christianity, Islam and other faiths.

:::example{title="Tracing a contribution: the Zimbabwe Bird"}
A Form 2 learner is asked how a carving made centuries ago matters today.

1. **Origin:** soapstone birds were carved at Great Zimbabwe, probably linked to royal ancestors.
2. **History:** several birds were taken away in the colonial period; some have since been returned to Zimbabwe.
3. **Today:** the bird is on the national flag, the coat of arms, coins and banknotes.
4. **Significance:** it is a symbol of national identity and pride in African achievement.
:::

## Assessing significance

To *assess* means to weigh how important each contribution is today. Some contributions remain very strong; others have changed or weakened.

| Contribution | Arguments that it is still very significant | Arguments that its significance has changed |
|---|---|---|
| Farming | Small grains are promoted as drought-tolerant crops; cattle still matter | Maize, introduced later, is now the main staple; modern machinery and fertilisers are widely used |
| Mining | Many mines began on ancient workings; mining earns foreign currency | Modern methods are entirely different |
| Political structures | Chiefs are in the Constitution and settle many disputes | Elected councils, courts and Parliament hold most power |
| Medicine | Many people consult traditional healers | Modern clinics and hospitals are the main health system; claims about herbs need testing |
| Stone building | Tourism, identity, the national name | Few people build in dry stone today |

:::example{title="Weighing an argument"}
A learner claims, "Iron Age political structures are no longer significant."

1. **For:** in towns, councils and courts provide services and justice.
2. **Against:** in communal areas chiefs, headmen and village heads allocate land, settle disputes and lead ceremonies; Chapter 15 recognises them.
3. **Judgement:** the claim is only partly true: significance is lower in towns, high in rural areas.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing without assessing"}
Questions with *assess* want a judgement. Do not just list contributions; say how important each is today, with evidence, and reach a conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming maize is an Iron Age crop"}
Maize came to Africa from the Americas much later. Iron Age staples were sorghum and millets.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming herbs cure diseases"}
Say that traditional medicine is valued and that plants are researched; do not claim that herbs cure serious illnesses. Anyone who is ill should see a trained health worker.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="List and assess"}
For *list the contributions* (Paper 2 Section A (b)), give one-word or short points: farming, mining, iron working, trade, stone building, leadership, medicine, religion. For *assess their significance* (Section B), take each contribution, give evidence that it matters today and evidence of change, then judge.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use examples"}
Name examples: Great Zimbabwe and Khami (stone building), the Zimbabwe Bird (identity), Chapter 15 (traditional leaders), small grains (farming).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Iron Age communities passed on farming methods, crops and cattle-keeping; iron working and crafts; mining; trade; and stone building.
- They also passed on political structures (chiefs, headmen, village heads), traditional medicine and religious beliefs.
- Many modern mines began on ancient workings; Great Zimbabwe and Khami are World Heritage Sites; the Zimbabwe Bird is a national symbol.
- Traditional leaders are recognised in Chapter 15 of the Constitution.
- Assessing significance means weighing what still matters against what has changed, and reaching a judgement.
:::
