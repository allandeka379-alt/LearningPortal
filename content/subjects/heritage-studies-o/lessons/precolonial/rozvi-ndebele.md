## Introduction

Just west of Bulawayo, on the banks of the Khami River, stand the decorated stone platforms of **Khami**, once the capital of the **Torwa** state. A short drive away, Bulawayo itself grew from the royal town of the **Ndebele** kings. Many families in the south-west trace their history to the Rozvi, the Kalanga, the Ndebele or the many peoples who lived together under these states.

After Great Zimbabwe declined, power on the south-western part of the plateau passed to the Torwa, then to the **Rozvi**, and in the nineteenth century to the **Ndebele**. In this Form 2 lesson you learn how these Later Iron Age states rose, how they were organised and how they changed. You should already know about Great Zimbabwe and the Mutapa state, and about the hierarchy of chiefs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Torwa state"}
A Later Iron Age state in south-western Zimbabwe, with its capital at Khami, which grew as Great Zimbabwe declined and was later taken over by the Rozvi.
:::

:::definition{term="Rozvi state"}
A state established in the late seventeenth century by Changamire Dombo, which became the dominant power on much of the Zimbabwean plateau until the early nineteenth century.
:::

:::definition{term="Mfecane"}
The period of wars and migrations in south-eastern Africa in the early nineteenth century, during which several groups, including Mzilikazi's followers, moved northwards.
:::

:::definition{term="Amabutho (regiments)"}
Age-based military units in the Ndebele state, each living in its own military town under an induna and serving the king.
:::

:::definition{term="Inxwala"}
The great annual ceremony of the Ndebele state, held around the time of the first fruits, at which the king was honoured, the nation was united and the regiments gathered.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
**Khami**: the Torwa capital near Bulawayo, a World Heritage Site (1986). **Changamire Dombo**: founder of the Rozvi state; drove the Portuguese from the plateau in the 1690s. **Mzilikazi**: founder of the Ndebele state, settled in the south-west in the late 1830s. **Lobengula**: king of the Ndebele from 1870.
:::

## The Torwa state (outline)

The Torwa state rose in the south-west around the fifteenth century. Its capital **Khami** is famous for terraced **stone platforms** with decorative patterns such as chequer and herringbone designs, on which royal and important houses were built. Its economy was based on cattle, farming, gold mining and trade; imported goods such as glass beads and Chinese porcelain have been found at Khami. Other Torwa and Rozvi sites include **Danangombe** (Dhlo-Dhlo) and **Naletale**. In the late seventeenth century the Torwa state was taken over by the Rozvi.

## The Rozvi state

### Rise

**Changamire Dombo** rose to power in the second half of the 1600s; Portuguese records and tradition suggest he was at first a subordinate of the Mwenemutapa before breaking away. In the 1680s and 1690s he took over the Torwa lands and defeated the Portuguese, driving them from their trading posts on the plateau (for example at Dambarare in the 1690s). His soldiers were known for their discipline and fierce reputation; the name *Rozvi* is often linked to the Shona verb *kurozva*, to destroy or plunder.

### Organisation

| Area | Organisation of the Rozvi state |
|---|---|
| Political | The **Mambo** ruled with a council of advisers; subject chiefs governed their own areas and paid tribute |
| Military | A strong army defended the state, collected tribute and kept the Portuguese away from the plateau |
| Economic | Cattle, crop farming, gold mining, hunting, crafts, and trade; tribute in cattle, grain, ivory and skins |
| Religious | The **Mwari** shrines in the Matobo Hills were consulted; priests gave the state religious support |
| Social | The Rozvi ruling group lived alongside many subject peoples, who kept their own chiefs and customs |

### Decline

The Rozvi state weakened in the early nineteenth century. **Succession disputes** divided the ruling family; subject chiefs began to refuse tribute; **drought** may have added pressure; and in the 1830s groups of **Nguni** migrants moving north during the Mfecane attacked the state, which never recovered. Many Rozvi families still live across Zimbabwe today.

:::example{title="Why tribute held a state together"}
A fictional Rozvi subject chief in the Chivi area sends twenty head of cattle and baskets of grain to the Mambo each year.

1. **What the Mambo gives:** protection by the army and recognition of the chief's right to rule.
2. **What the chief gives:** tribute and loyalty, and men for the army when asked.
3. **When it breaks down:** if the army is weak, the chief stops paying and becomes independent.
4. **Lesson:** a state built on tribute stays strong only while the ruler can protect and reward subject chiefs.
:::

## The Ndebele state

### Rise and settlement

**Mzilikazi** of the Khumalo family led his followers away from the Zulu kingdom of Shaka in the 1820s. During the Mfecane they moved through the area that is now South Africa, absorbing people from other groups. After conflicts there, they crossed the Limpopo and settled in south-western Zimbabwe in the **late 1830s**, making the area around the Matobo Hills and later Bulawayo their home. Mzilikazi died in 1868; after a dispute over succession, his son **Lobengula** became king in **1870** and ruled from **Bulawayo** (koBulawayo).

### Political organisation

- The **king (inkosi)** was head of the state, the army and religious ceremonies; he controlled land and cattle.
- **Izinduna** were appointed by the king to lead regiments and districts, and advised him in council.
- The **amabutho** (regiments) lived in **military towns**; young men served in them, and the regiments both defended the state and supervised the areas around them.
- The annual **Inxwala** ceremony united the nation around the king.

### Social organisation

Ndebele society included people who had come with Mzilikazi from the south and people who joined or were incorporated along the way and in Zimbabwe. Historians describe three groups: the **Zansi** (those from the south), the **Enhla** (those who joined on the journey) and the **Hole** (those incorporated in Zimbabwe, many of them Kalanga and Shona). Over time, service in the same regiments, marriage and the shared Ndebele language united these groups into one nation.

### Economic organisation

| Activity | Description |
|---|---|
| Cattle keeping | Cattle were the main wealth; the king owned many herds, some lent to subjects to care for |
| Crop farming | Sorghum, millet, maize and other crops grown mainly by women |
| Raiding and tribute | Some neighbouring communities paid tribute in cattle, grain or other goods |
| Hunting and trade | Ivory and skins were traded with European traders and hunters for guns, cloth and beads |
| Crafts | Iron working, pottery, woodwork and basketry |

:::example{title="Reading change over time in the Ndebele state"}
Compare the Ndebele state in 1840 and in 1880.

1. **1840:** newly settled; strong distinction between Zansi, Enhla and Hole; many young people from incorporated groups.
2. **1880:** a generation later; many people born in Zimbabwe; regiments mixed; Ndebele spoken widely; Lobengula king; European traders, hunters and missionaries visiting Bulawayo.
3. **What changed:** the state had become more united, but it was facing new pressure from outsiders who wanted land and minerals, which leads to the topic of colonisation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the Hole \"slaves\""}
Avoid this word. Hole were people incorporated into the state who served in regiments and could rise in society; over time the groups intermarried and became one nation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up Mambo and Changamire"}
Changamire is linked with Dombo, the founder of the Rozvi state; Rozvi rulers are usually called **Mambo**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong dates for the Ndebele"}
The Ndebele settled in Zimbabwe in the late 1830s, not in 1890. Lobengula became king in 1870.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Torwa state, with its capital at Khami near Bulawayo, rose in the south-west and was taken over by the Rozvi in the late 1600s.
- Changamire Dombo founded the Rozvi state and drove the Portuguese from the plateau; the Mambo ruled through tribute, an army and the support of the Mwari shrines.
- The Rozvi state declined through succession disputes, refusal of tribute and Nguni attacks in the 1830s.
- Mzilikazi settled the Ndebele in south-western Zimbabwe in the late 1830s; Lobengula became king in 1870.
- The Ndebele state was organised around the king, izinduna, regiments in military towns, cattle and the Inxwala ceremony; Zansi, Enhla and Hole became one nation.
:::
