## Introduction

Our country takes its name from a place. **Zimbabwe** comes from the Shona *dzimba dza mabwe* (houses of stone), and the most famous houses of stone are the ruins of **Great Zimbabwe**, near Masvingo. The soapstone **Zimbabwe Bird** found there appears on our national flag, coat of arms, coins and banknotes. Every year thousands of learners, tourists and researchers walk through its great walls, which were built without any mortar.

Great Zimbabwe was the centre of a powerful state built by the **ancestors of the Shona**. After it declined, the **Mutapa state** rose in the north and traded with Swahili merchants and later with the Portuguese. In this Form 2 lesson you learn how these Later Iron Age states rose, how they were organised and why they declined. You should already know how Early Iron Age farmers lived and how chiefs ruled.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="State"}
A large, organised political unit with a ruler, a government and control over a defined territory and its people, usually including many chiefdoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Tribute"}
Payments in goods (cattle, grain, ivory, gold, skins) or in labour that subject chiefs and people gave to a ruler as a sign of loyalty.
:::

:::definition{term="Dry-stone walling"}
Building walls from carefully shaped and fitted stone blocks without mortar to hold them together.
:::

:::definition{term="Mhondoro"}
Spirits of departed royal ancestors (lion spirits) believed to guard the land and the state, speaking through spirit mediums (masvikiro).
:::

:::definition{term="Mwenemutapa"}
The title of the ruler of the Mutapa state; the state takes its name from the title.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Key facts (approximate dates)"}
Great Zimbabwe grew from the **1100s** and was at its height between about **1300 and 1450 CE**. The Mutapa state was founded in the **fifteenth century** in northern Zimbabwe. Great Zimbabwe became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in **1986**.
:::

## Great Zimbabwe

### Rise

Great Zimbabwe grew on a site where Gokomere farmers had lived earlier. Several factors helped it to rise:

- **Cattle**: the area had good grazing, and cattle were the main form of wealth.
- **Gold and ivory trade**: rulers controlled trade in gold and ivory with the East African coast.
- **Good farmland and rainfall** for crops such as sorghum and millet.
- **Religion**: the rulers were linked with the ancestors and with rain-making, which gave them authority.
- **Strong leadership** that could organise thousands of people to build and farm.

### The buildings

There are three main parts. The **Hill Complex** stands on a granite hill and is believed to have been the earliest centre, linked with the rulers and religious ceremonies. The **Great Enclosure** in the valley has an outer wall about 250 metres long and a solid **conical tower**. The **Valley Ruins** are smaller enclosures. Walls were built of granite blocks without mortar, some with chevron and other decorative patterns. Ordinary people lived in daga huts around the stone walls.

### Organisation

| Area | How Great Zimbabwe was organised |
|---|---|
| Political | A king ruled with the help of chiefs, advisers and relatives; subject chiefs paid tribute |
| Economic | Cattle keeping, crop farming, gold mining, ivory hunting, crafts (iron, copper, soapstone carving, weaving) and long-distance trade |
| Trade | Gold and ivory went to the coast (through towns such as Sofala and Kilwa); imports included glass beads, cloth, and Chinese and Persian pottery found at the site |
| Social | The ruling class lived inside the stone enclosures; most people lived outside as farmers and craftsmen |
| Religious | Belief in Mwari (God) and the ancestors; the rulers led ceremonies; the soapstone birds are believed to have been linked with royal ancestors |

### Decline (theories)

Great Zimbabwe was largely abandoned by about the mid-1400s. No written record says why, so historians and archaeologists offer **theories**:

- **Overpopulation and exhaustion of resources**: grazing, firewood, soils and salt may have been used up around a large settlement.
- **Shift in trade**: gold trade may have moved north to the Zambezi valley, reducing the wealth of the rulers.
- **Drought** and pressure on food supplies.
- **Succession disputes** within the ruling family.

Most writers argue that several of these causes worked together. You should present them as theories, not as facts.

:::example{title="Reading the evidence of trade"}
Archaeologists found Chinese porcelain, Persian pottery and glass beads at Great Zimbabwe.

1. **What the finds are:** goods made far away in Asia and the Middle East.
2. **How they arrived:** through Swahili merchants at coastal towns, in exchange for gold and ivory from the interior.
3. **What they show:** Great Zimbabwe was part of a trade network across the Indian Ocean, and its rulers controlled valuable exports.
4. **Caution:** the finds show contact through trade, not that foreigners built the city. Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
:::

## The Mutapa state

### Origins in tradition

Oral tradition says that **Nyatsimba Mutota**, a prince linked to the Great Zimbabwe area, moved north in the fifteenth century, possibly in search of salt, and conquered the people of the Dande area near the Zambezi. He was given the title *Mwenemutapa*. His son **Matope** is said to have expanded the state further. Traditions differ in detail, so these are treated as traditions rather than exact history.

### Organisation

- **Political**: the Mwenemutapa ruled with a court of officials and advisers, royal wives with important roles, and provincial chiefs who paid tribute. An army defended the state.
- **Religious**: **mhondoro** spirit mediums spoke for the royal ancestors. They advised the ruler, helped to confirm successors and could criticise rulers, which helped to check abuse of power.
- **Economic**: farming, cattle, gold mining, ivory hunting, iron working, and trade. Gold was traded through Swahili merchants and later through the Portuguese at trading fairs.
- **Tribute**: subject chiefs sent cattle, grain, ivory, gold and labour to the ruler.

### Contact with the Portuguese

The **Portuguese** reached the East African coast around 1500 and moved inland along the Zambezi. They came for **gold and ivory**, and missionaries came to spread Christianity. Traders set up markets (fairs) in the state. Over the 1600s they became more involved in Mutapa politics, supporting rival claimants to the throne in return for concessions of land and trade.

### Decline

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Civil wars and succession disputes | Rival members of the royal family fought for the throne, weakening the state |
| Portuguese interference | Portuguese support for rival claimants and the power of Portuguese landholders reduced the Mwenemutapa's authority |
| Breakaway of provinces | Distant provinces stopped paying tribute and became independent |
| Rise of the Rozvi | In the late 1600s the Rozvi under Changamire Dombo drove the Portuguese from the plateau and became the dominant power |

:::example{title="Why the mhondoro mattered"}
A fictional Mutapa ruler wants to raise tribute sharply.

1. **The medium's role:** the mhondoro medium warns that the royal ancestors are displeased with unfair rule.
2. **The effect:** because people believe the ancestors speak through the medium, the ruler must listen or lose support.
3. **Significance:** religion acted as a check on the ruler and helped keep the state united.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying foreigners built Great Zimbabwe"}
Colonial writers once claimed this, but archaeology shows Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona. Foreign goods show trade, not foreign builders.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating one cause of decline as fact"}
The causes of the decline of Great Zimbabwe are theories. Write "one theory is…" and give more than one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the states"}
Great Zimbabwe was centred near Masvingo; the Mutapa state was in the north near the Zambezi. They were not the same state at the same time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Great Zimbabwe, near Masvingo, rose from the 1100s and was at its height about 1300-1450; it was built by the ancestors of the Shona.
- Its wealth came from cattle, farming, gold, ivory and trade with the coast; imports included beads and Asian pottery.
- Its decline is explained by theories: exhaustion of resources, a shift in trade, drought and succession disputes.
- The Mutapa state, founded in tradition by Nyatsimba Mutota, was ruled by the Mwenemutapa with tribute, an army and mhondoro mediums.
- Civil wars, Portuguese interference and the rise of the Rozvi weakened the Mutapa state.
:::
