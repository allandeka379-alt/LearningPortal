## Introduction

Every household throws things away every day: vegetable peelings, plastic bags, bottles, tins, old clothes, broken phones. Multiply that by millions of homes, plus shops, markets, factories, farms and hospitals, and you begin to see the size of the problem. Where refuse trucks come regularly, rubbish disappears from the street; where they do not, it piles up at corners, blocks drains, attracts flies and rats, and is often burnt.

Waste is a Form 4 topic. It brings together ideas from earlier lessons on pollution and on the environment, and it asks you to judge which ways of managing waste work best for Zimbabwe. It also connects with citizenship: keeping our surroundings clean is a shared civic responsibility. In this lesson you will learn the types of waste, the problems of poor disposal, the main strategies and approaches, who is responsible, and how to evaluate the approaches.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Waste"}
Any material that is no longer wanted by its owner and is thrown away.
:::

:::definition{term="Waste management"}
The collection, transport, treatment, recycling and safe disposal of waste, and efforts to reduce the amount of waste produced.
:::

:::definition{term="Recycling"}
Processing used materials (such as paper, plastic, glass and metal) so that they can be made into new products.
:::

:::definition{term="Landfill"}
A site where waste is buried in layers and covered with soil, ideally lined and managed to stop pollution. An open dump with no lining or cover is not a proper landfill.
:::

:::definition{term="Composting"}
Allowing organic waste such as peelings, leaves and manure to rot into a rich material that improves soil.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- Urban councils and rural district councils are responsible for collecting and disposing of refuse in their areas.
- The Environmental Management Agency (EMA) sets and enforces rules on waste disposal, licenses waste sites and can fine people who litter or dump illegally.
- The waste hierarchy: reduce first, then reuse, then recycle and recover, and dispose safely only as a last resort.
:::

## Types of waste

| Type | Sources | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Domestic (household) | Homes, markets, shops, schools | Food scraps, plastic packaging, paper, bottles, tins, old clothes |
| Industrial | Factories, mines, building sites | Chemicals, metal scraps, mine tailings, rubble, effluent |
| Agricultural | Farms | Crop residues, animal manure, empty pesticide and fertiliser containers |
| Medical | Hospitals, clinics, pharmacies | Used needles, bandages, expired medicines, body tissue |
| Electronic (e-waste) | Homes, offices, schools | Old phones, computers, batteries, televisions, light bulbs |

Some waste is **hazardous**: medical waste, chemicals, batteries and e-waste can poison people and the environment or spread infection, so they need special handling.

## Problems of poor waste disposal

| Problem | How it arises |
|---|---|
| Disease | Uncollected rubbish breeds flies, rats and mosquitoes; blocked drains and dirty water help cholera and dysentery spread |
| Air pollution | Burning rubbish and tyres releases smoke and poisonous gases |
| Water and soil pollution | Rain washes chemicals and rotting waste from dumps into rivers and underground water |
| Flooding | Plastic and litter block drains, so storm water floods streets and homes |
| Harm to animals | Cattle, goats and wild animals swallow plastic; animals are injured by tins and glass |
| Ugliness and lost income | Dirty streets and markets discourage tourists, investors and customers |
| Injury | Children and waste pickers are cut by needles and glass |

## Strategies and approaches

### The waste hierarchy

| Step | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce | Produce less waste in the first place | Carry a reusable shopping bag; buy goods with less packaging; avoid single-use plastics |
| Reuse | Use items again | Refill bottles; reuse containers for storage; repair clothes and furniture |
| Recycle | Turn used materials into new products | Collect plastic, paper, glass and metal for recycling companies |
| Recover | Get value such as energy or compost | Composting; biogas from manure and food waste |
| Dispose | Safely get rid of what is left | Managed landfill; incineration of medical waste |

### Collection and disposal

| Approach | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Regular collection by councils | Keeps streets clean; reduces burning and illegal dumping | Expensive (trucks, fuel, workers); often irregular where councils lack money |
| Managed landfill | Can hold large amounts; lining and soil cover reduce pollution and pests | Needs land; poorly managed dumps pollute water and air and release methane |
| Composting | Cheap; turns organic waste (a large share of household waste) into fertiliser for gardens | Only for organic waste; needs space and care |
| Incineration | Destroys medical and hazardous waste and reduces volume | Costly; produces smoke unless properly controlled |
| Recycling | Saves raw materials and energy; creates income for collectors and small businesses | Not all materials have buyers; collection systems are limited |

:::example{title="A market cleans up"}
A fictional market in Chinhoyi has heaps of rotting vegetables and plastic at its edges.

1. **Problem:** flies, a bad smell, blocked drains and customers going elsewhere.
2. **Plan:** vendors agree to separate waste into organic and plastic; the council provides two skips and collects twice a week; a local youth group collects plastic for a recycler.
3. **Composting:** a nearby school takes the vegetable waste to compost for its garden.
4. **Result:** less waste is buried or burnt, the market is cleaner and young people earn some income. The approach works because it uses several steps of the hierarchy at once.
:::

## Who is responsible?

- **Local authorities** (urban councils and rural district councils): collect refuse, run disposal sites, make and enforce by-laws, provide bins and skips.
- **EMA**: sets standards, licenses waste sites and handlers of hazardous waste, inspects, educates and fines offenders; it has banned some polluting products such as expanded polystyrene (kaylite) food containers.
- **Central government**: makes laws and national campaigns, such as regular national clean-up days.
- **Businesses**: manage their own industrial waste safely; reduce packaging; take part in recycling.
- **Citizens**: reduce, reuse and recycle; put rubbish in bins; do not burn or dump; pay rates; take part in clean-ups; report illegal dumping.

## Evaluating the approaches

No single approach solves the problem. Collection and landfills deal with waste after it is made but are expensive and can pollute. Reducing and reusing are the cheapest and best for the environment, but they depend on changing the habits of millions of people. Recycling creates jobs but needs buyers and collection systems. Composting suits Zimbabwe well, because much household waste is organic, but it cannot deal with plastics or e-waste.

:::example{title="Judging a clean-up campaign"}
A national clean-up day brings residents, schools, churches and businesses together to collect litter.

1. **Strengths:** streets and drains are cleaned quickly; people see the scale of the problem; it builds civic pride.
2. **Weaknesses:** if collection is irregular, rubbish builds up again within days; collected waste may simply be moved to an open dump.
3. **Judgement:** clean-ups raise awareness, but lasting results need regular collection, enforcement and less waste being produced.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing waste management with pollution"}
Pollution is the harm; waste management is the system for handling waste so that it does not cause harm.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating recycling as the first step"}
Reducing and reusing come before recycling in the waste hierarchy because they avoid waste altogether.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting all the blame on councils"}
Councils must collect refuse, but citizens and businesses produce the waste and share responsibility for it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Waste may be domestic, industrial, agricultural, medical or electronic; some is hazardous.
- Poor disposal causes disease, pollution, flooding, harm to animals, injury and lost income.
- The waste hierarchy: reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, then dispose safely.
- Councils collect and dispose, EMA regulates and enforces, businesses and citizens reduce and handle waste responsibly.
- Each approach has strengths and limits; the best results come from combining them.
:::
