## Introduction

In March 2020 schools across Zimbabwe closed their gates. Markets, churches and sports grounds fell silent, buses stopped running between towns and families gathered around radios and phones for news of a new disease, COVID-19. Within weeks a virus first reported in one country had reached almost every country in the world. For many young people it was the first time they understood how quickly an infectious disease can change daily life.

COVID-19 was not the first such disease. Zimbabweans also remember the large cholera outbreak of 2008-2009, and HIV and AIDS have affected almost every family over the past forty years. This lesson, taught in Forms 1 and 3, explains the words used to describe the spread of disease, the main diseases named in the syllabus, how they affect society and how they are prevented and controlled. It gives general public health information only. Anyone who feels unwell should seek a trained health worker at a clinic or hospital.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Infectious disease"}
A disease caused by germs (such as viruses, bacteria or parasites) that can pass from one person to another, or from animals, water or food to people.
:::

:::definition{term="Endemic"}
A disease that is always present in a particular area or population at a fairly steady level, for example malaria in some low-lying parts of Zimbabwe.
:::

:::definition{term="Epidemic"}
A sudden rise in the number of cases of a disease in a community or region, well above what is normally expected, for example a cholera outbreak in a city.
:::

:::definition{term="Pandemic"}
An epidemic that spreads across many countries or continents and affects very large numbers of people, for example COVID-19.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a pandemic in March 2020; Zimbabwe recorded its first case in March 2020.
- Zimbabwe suffered a major cholera outbreak in 2008-2009.
- The Ministry of Health and Child Care leads the national response to disease; the National AIDS Council coordinates the response to HIV and AIDS.
:::

## Endemic, epidemic, pandemic

| Term | Spread | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Endemic | Always present in one area at a steady level | Malaria in some low-lying, hot areas |
| Epidemic | Sudden large rise in one community, country or region | Cholera outbreak in Zimbabwe, 2008-2009; Ebola in West Africa, 2014-2016 |
| Pandemic | Across many countries and continents | COVID-19 (from 2020); HIV and AIDS worldwide |

A disease can move between these categories. An epidemic that crosses borders and continents becomes a pandemic; a disease that never disappears from an area becomes endemic there.

## The diseases in outline

| Disease | Cause | How it spreads | Main prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| HIV and AIDS | A virus (HIV) that weakens the body's defences; AIDS is the advanced stage | Unprotected sex; infected blood; sharing needles or sharp instruments; from mother to child during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. **Not** by hugging, shaking hands, sharing plates or mosquitoes | Abstinence, faithfulness and condoms; testing; treatment that also prevents passing it on; preventing mother-to-child transmission; never sharing sharp instruments |
| Ebola | A virus | Direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a sick or dead person, or infected animals | Isolating and treating patients, protective clothing, safe burials, contact tracing, vaccination |
| Cholera | Bacteria | Drinking water or eating food contaminated with faeces; poor sanitation | Safe water (boiling or treating), toilets, hand washing with soap, food hygiene, vaccination in outbreaks |
| Dysentery | Bacteria or amoebae | Contaminated food and water; poor hygiene | Hand washing, safe water, clean toilets, food hygiene |
| COVID-19 | A coronavirus | Droplets and tiny particles in the air when an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks or breathes; contaminated hands | Vaccination, ventilation, masks, hand washing, staying home when sick, testing |

HIV has no cure, but **antiretroviral therapy (ART)** taken every day as prescribed allows people living with HIV to lead long, healthy lives. Cholera and dysentery cause severe diarrhoea, and the loss of water can kill quickly; oral rehydration and prompt treatment at a clinic save lives.

:::example{title="Tracing a cholera outbreak"}
A fictional high-density suburb has had no piped water for weeks, and sewage from a burst pipe runs near a shallow well.

1. **Cause:** cholera bacteria from human waste enter the well.
2. **Spread:** families draw drinking water from the well; vendors sell food prepared with it.
3. **Response:** health workers set up a treatment point, give oral rehydration, test the water, close the well, deliver safe water by truck and teach hand washing. The council repairs the sewer.
4. **Lesson:** cholera is a disease of unsafe water and poor sanitation; medicine treats the sick, but only safe water and sanitation stop the outbreak.
:::

## Impacts of pandemics and epidemics

| Social | Economic | Political |
|---|---|---|
| Deaths and illness; orphans and child-headed households (especially from AIDS); schools closed and learning lost; funerals and gatherings restricted; stigma and fear; strain on health workers | Workers fall ill or die; businesses close during lockdowns; informal traders lose income; tourism collapses; governments spend heavily on health; families spend savings on care | Governments make emergency regulations (lockdowns, curfews, border closures); gatherings, including political and religious ones, are restricted; cooperation between countries through WHO and regional bodies; debate about rights versus public health |

Stigma, the unfair labelling or rejection of people because of a disease, makes epidemics worse: people who fear rejection hide symptoms and avoid testing. Respectful, accurate language, such as "a person living with HIV", helps everyone.

## Prevention and mitigation

- **Personal hygiene:** washing hands with soap at key times; covering coughs.
- **Safe water and sanitation:** treating or boiling water, using toilets, repairing sewers, collecting refuse.
- **Testing and treatment:** knowing one's status; early treatment; ART for HIV.
- **Vaccination:** routine childhood vaccines and vaccines against diseases such as COVID-19 and cholera in outbreaks.
- **Public health measures:** isolation of the sick, contact tracing, quarantine, restrictions on gatherings, border screening.
- **Education and awareness:** health campaigns in schools, churches, media and communities; fighting myths and stigma.
- **Funding and partnerships:** the AIDS levy on income tax supports the HIV response; partners such as WHO and the Global Fund support testing, treatment and vaccines.

### How effective are these measures?

:::example{title="Evaluating the COVID-19 lockdown"}
1. **Strengths:** restrictions on movement and gatherings slowed the spread and gave hospitals time to prepare.
2. **Weaknesses:** many families depend on daily informal trade, so lockdowns caused hunger and lost income; learning was lost where children had no phones, data or radios for lessons.
3. **Balance:** most people agree that some restrictions saved lives, but the costs fell hardest on the poor. Measures work best when combined with support for those who lose income and with clear, trusted information.
:::

Other measures also have strengths and limits. Vaccines are very effective but depend on supply, cold storage and public trust. Hygiene campaigns are cheap but need safe water and soap. ART has turned HIV into a manageable condition, but it must be taken every day for life, and stigma still stops some people from testing.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using epidemic and pandemic as the same word"}
An epidemic is a large outbreak in one area; a pandemic spreads across many countries and continents.
:::

:::mistake{title="Spreading myths"}
HIV is not spread by hugging, sharing plates or mosquitoes. No herb or prayer replaces testing and treatment by a trained health worker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing measures without evaluating them"}
For *evaluate* or *how far*, say what each measure achieved AND what its limits were.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Endemic diseases are always present; epidemics are sudden large outbreaks; pandemics spread across countries and continents.
- HIV and Ebola are spread by body fluids, cholera and dysentery by contaminated water and food, COVID-19 mainly through the air.
- Pandemics and epidemics have social, economic and political impacts, and stigma makes them worse.
- Prevention combines hygiene, safe water and sanitation, testing and treatment, vaccination, public health measures and education.
- Measures work best together and when they protect livelihoods as well as health; always seek a trained health worker when ill.
:::
