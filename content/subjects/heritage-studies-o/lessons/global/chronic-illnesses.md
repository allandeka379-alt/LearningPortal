## Introduction

Many families keep a small bag of tablets on a shelf for a grandmother's "BP" or an uncle's "sugar". At a family gathering you may hear relatives talk about a cousin who had a stroke, or an aunt who must check her blood sugar every day. These are signs of **chronic illnesses**, conditions that last a long time and usually cannot be passed from one person to another.

For many years most attention in Zimbabwe went to infectious diseases such as HIV, malaria and cholera. Today chronic illnesses such as diabetes, high blood pressure and cancers are a growing cause of illness and death, in towns and in rural areas. Many of them can be prevented or controlled by the choices people make every day, starting when they are young. This lesson, taught in Forms 1 and 3, explains what chronic illnesses are, what raises the risk, how they affect people and the nation, and how they are prevented and managed. It gives general information only: anyone with symptoms, or anyone already on treatment, must follow the advice of a trained health worker.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chronic illness"}
An illness that lasts a long time (months, years or for life) and usually develops slowly, for example diabetes or hypertension.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-communicable disease (NCD)"}
A disease that cannot be passed from one person to another. Most chronic illnesses are non-communicable.
:::

:::definition{term="Diabetes"}
A condition in which the body cannot control the level of sugar (glucose) in the blood, because it makes too little insulin or cannot use insulin properly.
:::

:::definition{term="Hypertension"}
Blood pressure that stays too high over a long time. It is often called a "silent killer" because many people feel no symptoms until serious damage is done.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk factor"}
Anything that increases the chance of developing a disease, such as smoking, an unhealthy diet or lack of exercise.
:::

:::definition{term="Screening"}
Testing people who feel well in order to find a disease early, for example a blood pressure check or a blood sugar test.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The main chronic illnesses in the syllabus are diabetes and hypertension; others include cancers, asthma, heart disease, stroke and chronic kidney disease.
- The main modifiable risk factors are unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, tobacco use and harmful use of alcohol.
- World Diabetes Day is marked on 14 November.
:::

## The main chronic illnesses

| Illness | What happens | Possible complications if not controlled |
|---|---|---|
| Diabetes | Blood sugar stays too high. Type 1 usually starts in childhood or youth (the body makes little or no insulin); type 2 usually develops in adults and is strongly linked to overweight and inactivity | Damage to eyes (blindness), kidneys, nerves and feet (wounds that do not heal, amputation); heart disease and stroke |
| Hypertension | The heart pumps blood with too much force, straining blood vessels | Stroke, heart attack, heart failure, kidney failure |
| Cancers | Cells grow out of control and can spread; common types include cervical, breast and prostate cancer | Depend on the type; early detection greatly improves the chance of successful treatment |
| Asthma | Airways in the lungs become narrow and inflamed, causing wheezing and breathlessness; often triggered by smoke and dust | Severe attacks can be life-threatening without treatment |

Some chronic illnesses run in families and some come with age, but many are made more likely by how people live.

## Risk factors

| Risk factors we can change | Risk factors we cannot change |
|---|---|
| Unhealthy diet: too much salt, sugar, fat and processed food; too few vegetables and fruit | Age: risk rises as people grow older |
| Physical inactivity: sitting for long hours, little exercise | Family history: a parent or sibling with the illness |
| Being overweight | Sex: some illnesses are more common in men or in women |
| Tobacco use (smoking, snuff) | Some genetic conditions |
| Harmful use of alcohol | |
| Long-term stress; poor sleep | |

:::example{title="Changing diets, rising risk"}
Nyasha's grandmother grew up eating sadza made from small grains, leafy vegetables, beans and little meat, and walked long distances every day. Nyasha's family in town buys more white bread, fizzy drinks, fried food and salty snacks, and travels by kombi.

1. **Change in diet:** more sugar, salt and fat; fewer vegetables and whole grains.
2. **Change in activity:** less walking and physical work.
3. **Result:** the family's risk of type 2 diabetes and hypertension is higher than the grandmother's was at the same age.
4. **What helps:** returning to traditional whole grains and vegetables, cutting sugary drinks and salt, and walking more. Some indigenous foods are part of a healthy diet.
:::

## Effects of chronic illnesses

| On the individual | On the family | On the nation |
|---|---|---|
| Pain, disability (e.g. after a stroke or amputation), lifelong treatment, inability to work, early death | Cost of transport, tests and medicines; relatives (often women and girls) become carers; loss of a breadwinner's income | More pressure on clinics and hospitals; more spending on medicines; loss of skilled workers; lower productivity |

Because chronic illnesses last for years, their costs keep adding up. A family may spend money on medicines every month for decades.

## Prevention and management

**Prevention** aims to stop the illness from developing:

- eat a balanced diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit and whole grains; limit salt, sugar and fatty foods;
- be physically active every day (walking, sport, farm and garden work);
- never start smoking; avoid alcohol abuse (for young people, the law forbids buying alcohol under 18);
- keep a healthy weight; manage stress and sleep well;
- go for **screening**: blood pressure and blood sugar checks for adults, and cancer screening such as cervical cancer screening for women;
- support health education in schools, churches and the media.

**Management** aims to control an illness that has already developed:

- take medicines exactly as prescribed by a health worker and never stop on your own;
- attend regular check-ups;
- keep up a healthy diet and exercise;
- learn the warning signs of complications and seek help quickly.

Herbal and traditional remedies are part of many families' heritage, but no herb has been shown to cure diabetes or hypertension. Stopping prescribed treatment in favour of an untested remedy can be dangerous. Anyone using other remedies should tell their health worker.

:::example{title="A support group in action"}
At a fictional clinic in Gweru, people living with diabetes and hypertension meet once a month.

1. **Learning:** a nurse explains how to read food labels and why salt raises blood pressure.
2. **Checking:** members have their blood pressure and blood sugar recorded.
3. **Encouraging:** members share recipes using traditional grains and vegetables and walk together after the meeting.
4. **Result:** members are more likely to take their treatment and keep to healthy habits, which reduces complications.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling chronic illnesses infectious"}
Diabetes and hypertension cannot be caught from another person. They are non-communicable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking only old or rich people get them"}
Chronic illnesses affect young people too, in towns and in rural areas, and risk factors begin in youth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a herb or diet cures diabetes"}
Healthy eating helps prevent and control diabetes, but there is no cure. Treatment must be guided by a trained health worker.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chronic illnesses last a long time and are usually non-communicable; diabetes and hypertension are the main examples in the syllabus.
- Risk factors we can change include unhealthy diet, inactivity, tobacco, alcohol abuse and overweight; age and family history cannot be changed.
- They affect individuals (disability, early death), families (cost, care, lost income) and the nation (health spending, lost workers).
- Prevention: healthy diet, exercise, no tobacco, no alcohol abuse, screening and education.
- Management: treatment as prescribed, regular check-ups and healthy habits, always under a trained health worker.
:::
