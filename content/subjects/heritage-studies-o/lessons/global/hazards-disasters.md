## Introduction

In March 2019 Cyclone Idai crossed from Mozambique into eastern Zimbabwe. In Chimanimani and Chipinge districts, days of extremely heavy rain caused floods and landslides that swept away homes, roads, bridges, schools and clinics. Hundreds of people lost their lives and many more lost their homes and crops. Families, churches, schools, businesses, the security forces, the Department of Civil Protection and people from all over the country and the world responded with rescue, food, shelter and money.

Cyclone Idai showed that a natural event becomes a disaster when it strikes people who are exposed to it and not prepared. It also showed the power of Zimbabweans helping one another. This lesson explains the difference between a hazard, a risk and a disaster, the main types of hazard, the causes and effects of droughts and floods, and how disasters can be reduced and managed. It is taught in Forms 1 and 2. Climate change, which is making some hazards more frequent, has its own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hazard"}
Something that could cause harm to people, property or the environment, such as a flood, a drought, a disease outbreak or a fire.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk"}
The chance that a hazard will cause harm. Risk is higher when a hazard is likely and when people are exposed and vulnerable to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Disaster"}
A serious event, caused by a hazard, that disrupts the life of a community, causing widespread loss of life, property or livelihoods beyond what the community can cope with using its own resources.
:::

:::definition{term="Vulnerability"}
How easily people or places are harmed by a hazard, for example because of poverty, poor housing or living on a flood plain.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions taken in advance to reduce the harm a hazard can cause.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- Cyclone Idai struck eastern Zimbabwe (Chimanimani and Chipinge) in March 2019.
- The Department of Civil Protection coordinates disaster preparedness and response in Zimbabwe.
- The Meteorological Services Department issues weather forecasts and warnings of heavy rain, storms and dry spells.
:::

## Types of hazard

| Type | Examples | Zimbabwean examples |
|---|---|---|
| Natural (environmental) | Droughts, floods, cyclones, storms and hail, lightning, earthquakes, landslides | Cyclone Idai (2019); floods in the Tokwe-Mukosi and Muzarabani areas; recurrent droughts in the south |
| Biological | Disease outbreaks, pests and plant or animal diseases | Cholera outbreaks; COVID-19 (from 2020); fall armyworm attacking maize; tick-borne cattle diseases |
| Man-made (mechanical, technological) | Road and rail accidents, mine collapses, building collapses, fires, explosions, chemical spills | Bus accidents on major highways; collapses of mine shafts used by illegal miners |

A single event can involve several types. A flood (natural) may be followed by cholera (biological) when toilets and wells are flooded, and roads and bridges washed away make rescue (and accidents) harder.

## Droughts and floods

| | Drought | Flood |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A long period of below-normal rainfall that leaves too little water for people, crops and animals | Water covering land that is normally dry |
| Natural causes | Low and unreliable rainfall; long dry spells in the growing season; weather patterns such as El Niño | Very heavy or prolonged rain; cyclones; rivers overflowing their banks |
| Human factors | Deforestation and degraded land hold less water; overuse of water | Building on flood plains and wetlands; blocked drains; siltation of rivers; deforestation increases run-off |
| Effects | Crop failure and hunger; cattle deaths; water shortages; low dam levels and power cuts; children leave school; rising food prices | Loss of life; homes, roads, bridges and crops destroyed; disease outbreaks; people displaced; schools closed |

:::example{title="Why the same rain harms some more than others"}
Two fictional families live in the same district when a week of very heavy rain falls.

1. **Family A** lives in a brick house on higher ground, has a radio and heard the Meteorological Services warning. They move their grain and livestock to safety.
2. **Family B** lives in a mud house close to the river bank, has no radio and is away from home when the warning is given. Their house collapses and their crops are washed away.
3. **Lesson:** the hazard (the rain) was the same; the *risk* and the damage were different because of **vulnerability** and **preparedness**.
:::

## Impact of disasters

Disasters affect people in many ways at once.

- **Social:** deaths and injuries, people displaced into camps, families separated, schools and clinics closed, trauma and grief.
- **Economic:** crops, livestock, homes and businesses lost; roads and bridges destroyed, cutting off trade; the government spends on relief instead of development.
- **Environmental:** soil washed away, landslides scar hillsides, rivers change course, wildlife killed, water polluted.
- **Health:** water-borne diseases after floods; hunger and malnutrition after droughts.

## Disaster management

Disaster management is often shown as a cycle.

| Stage | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Mitigation | Reducing the harm before a hazard strikes | Not building on flood plains; stronger houses; planting trees on slopes; drought-tolerant crops; grain reserves |
| Preparedness | Getting ready to respond | Early warning systems; evacuation plans; drills in schools; stocking food and medicines; training volunteers |
| Response | Acting during and just after the disaster | Search and rescue, evacuation, emergency shelter, food, clean water and medical care |
| Recovery | Rebuilding and returning to normal, ideally better than before | Rebuilding homes, roads and schools; seed and livestock for farmers; counselling |

### Who is involved

- **Government:** the Department of Civil Protection coordinates; the Meteorological Services Department gives warnings; the security forces help with rescue; ministries provide food, health care and rebuilding; the Grain Marketing Board keeps grain reserves for droughts.
- **Local authorities and traditional leaders:** identify those in need and organise local help.
- **Non-governmental and international organisations:** the Zimbabwe Red Cross Society, United Nations agencies and churches provide relief.
- **Communities and individuals:** neighbours are usually the first rescuers; people give money, food and clothing; volunteers help clean up.

:::example{title="Lessons from Cyclone Idai"}
1. **Response:** rescuers, including local people, reached cut-off villages; churches, schools and businesses collected food and clothes across the country.
2. **Difficulties:** roads and bridges were destroyed, so help took time to arrive; many people had not understood how dangerous the warnings were.
3. **Lessons often drawn:** warnings must reach every village in a language people understand and say clearly what to do; houses should not be built on unstable slopes or in river channels; communities need rehearsed evacuation plans.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using hazard and disaster as if they mean the same"}
A hazard is a *possible* source of harm; a disaster is what happens when a hazard actually causes serious loss that a community cannot cope with on its own.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the human side"}
Floods and droughts are natural, but human actions such as deforestation and building on flood plains make their effects worse.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing only relief"}
Disaster management includes mitigation and preparedness *before* a disaster and recovery afterwards, not only food and tents.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A hazard can cause harm; risk is the chance of harm; a disaster is serious loss a community cannot cope with alone.
- Hazards may be natural, biological or man-made, and one event can trigger others.
- Droughts and floods have natural causes, but human actions and vulnerability make them worse.
- Disasters have social, economic, environmental and health impacts.
- Disaster management covers mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery; Civil Protection, the Meteorological Services Department, communities and relief organisations all play a part.
:::
