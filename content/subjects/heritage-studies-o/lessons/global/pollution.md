## Introduction

Walk past a market at the end of a busy day and you may see plastic bags caught in a fence, smoke rising from a heap of burning rubbish and grey water running into a drain. Drive along a busy road in Harare or Bulawayo at rush hour and you will smell exhaust fumes and hear hooting and engines. Each of these is a form of **pollution**, and each one harms someone: the child who breathes the smoke, the cattle that swallow the plastic, the family downstream that drinks the water.

Pollution matters to a young Zimbabwean because it affects health, food, water and the beauty of the country, and because much of it can be prevented by everyday choices. In Form 1 you learn the types and sources of pollution and its effects; in Form 3 you also learn to judge how well different ways of reducing it work. You should already know the main environmental issues facing Zimbabwe. How waste is collected and managed is studied separately under waste management.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollution"}
The release of harmful substances or energy (such as noise, heat or light) into the environment, making the air, water or land dirty, unsafe or unpleasant for living things.
:::

:::definition{term="Pollutant"}
A substance or form of energy that causes pollution, for example smoke, sewage, plastic, pesticides or loud noise.
:::

:::definition{term="Point source"}
A single, identifiable place from which pollution comes, such as a factory pipe or a sewage works.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-point source"}
Pollution that comes from many scattered places, such as fertiliser washed off many farms by rain or exhaust from thousands of vehicles.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The Environmental Management Agency (EMA), under the Environmental Management Act, licenses and monitors activities that discharge waste and can fine polluters.
- Section 73 of the Constitution gives every person the right to an environment that is not harmful to their health or well-being.
- The "polluter pays" principle means that whoever causes pollution should pay for the damage and the clean-up.
:::

## Types and sources of pollution

| Type | Common sources in Zimbabwe | Examples of pollutants |
|---|---|---|
| Air pollution | Vehicle exhaust; burning rubbish and tyres; veld fires; coal-fired power stations and factories; dust from mines and dirt roads; cooking with wood or coal indoors | Smoke, soot, dust, carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide |
| Water pollution | Raw or poorly treated sewage; industrial effluent; fertiliser and pesticides washed off farms; mercury used in gold panning; litter in rivers; washing in streams | Sewage, chemicals, mercury, plastics, excess nutrients |
| Land (soil) pollution | Illegal dumping; plastics and litter; spilt oil and chemicals; mine waste; overuse of chemicals on farms | Plastics, tins, batteries, oil, chemicals |
| Noise pollution | Traffic and hooting; loud music at bars, churches and shops; machinery, mills and building sites; aircraft | Loud or constant sound |

### Levels of pollution

Pollution is not the same everywhere. It is usually **highest** where many people and activities are crowded together: city centres, industrial areas and busy roads, high-density suburbs where refuse is not collected, and mining areas. It is often **lower** in rural areas, though rural people may face indoor smoke from cooking fires, pesticide spills and polluted rivers from upstream towns and mines. Levels also change with time: smoke builds up on still winter mornings, and rivers are more polluted when water levels are low and sewage is less diluted.

:::example{title="Tracing a polluted river"}
A fictional Form 3 class in Harare walks along a stream that flows towards a city dam.

1. **Upstream (farmland):** the water is fairly clear, but green algae grow where fertiliser has washed in.
2. **Middle (high-density suburb):** plastic bottles, nappies and grey water from a broken sewer pipe.
3. **Downstream (industrial area):** an oily sheen and a bad smell near a factory outlet.
4. **Conclusion:** the stream has non-point sources (farms, households) and point sources (the broken sewer, the factory). Each needs a different solution.

Lake Chivero, Harare's main water supply, has long suffered from sewage and nutrient pollution of this kind, which makes treating the water more difficult and expensive.
:::

## Effects of pollution

| On people | On animals and plants | On the environment and economy |
|---|---|---|
| Breathing problems from smoke and dust; water-borne diseases such as cholera and dysentery from sewage; poisoning from chemicals and mercury; hearing damage and stress from noise | Fish die when water lacks oxygen; cattle and wild animals swallow plastics; birds and insects are poisoned by chemicals | Rivers and dams become unusable; water treatment costs rise; soil becomes less fertile; towns look dirty, which harms tourism; water weeds and algae spread |

Pollution often hits the poor hardest. Families without piped water may have to use polluted streams, and families living near dumpsites breathe the smoke from burning rubbish.

## Reducing pollution

| Measure | How it reduces pollution |
|---|---|
| Laws and enforcement | The Environmental Management Act sets rules on discharges and dumping; EMA licenses polluters, monitors them and fines offenders |
| Awareness and education | Campaigns, school clubs and clean-up days change behaviour, e.g. not burning rubbish or littering |
| Reduce, reuse, recycle | Less waste is produced or dumped; plastic, paper, glass and metal are collected for recycling |
| Cleaner technology | Filters on factory chimneys, treatment of effluent before release, better-maintained vehicles, solar energy instead of coal |
| Proper sanitation | Repaired sewers, working treatment plants and toilets keep sewage out of rivers |
| By-laws on noise | Councils limit loud music and noisy work at night |

### How well do these measures work?

Each measure has strengths and limits, which is what *assess* and *how far* questions want you to weigh up.

- **Laws** are strong on paper and fines deter some polluters, but enforcement is limited when there are few inspectors, and fines may be cheaper than cleaning up.
- **Awareness** is cheap and can change habits for life, but people may know what is right and still burn rubbish if refuse is not collected.
- **Recycling** turns waste into income for collectors and small businesses, but there are not enough collection points or buyers for every material.
- **Cleaner technology** cuts pollution at the source, but it is expensive, so struggling factories and councils delay it.

:::example{title="Assessing a measure"}
A fictional town council in Kwekwe bans the burning of rubbish in residential areas.

1. **Strength:** smoke falls in suburbs where refuse is collected regularly.
2. **Weakness:** where trucks come rarely, residents keep burning because heaps of rubbish attract flies and rats.
3. **Judgement:** the ban works only when it is backed by regular collection. A law succeeds best when people have a realistic alternative.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up types and sources"}
"Air pollution" is a type; "vehicle exhaust" is a source. Exam answers that ask for sources must name where the pollution comes from.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing measures without assessing them"}
For *assess* or *how far*, give strengths AND weaknesses of each measure, then a judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting noise"}
Noise is a form of pollution even though nothing is spilt or dumped. Constant loud noise damages hearing and causes stress.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name a type and a matching source: "water pollution — mercury from gold panning".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the effects** (Paper 2 Section A): link the pollutant to the harm: "Sewage in rivers spreads cholera because people drink the untreated water."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Assess / how far** (Paper 2 Section B): take each measure in turn, give one strength and one limitation, then conclude which measures work best together.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pollution is the release of harmful substances or energy into air, water or land.
- The four main types are air, water, land and noise pollution, each with many sources.
- Pollution is highest in crowded urban, industrial and mining areas, but rural areas are also affected.
- It harms human health, animals, plants, water supplies, soil and the economy.
- Laws and EMA, awareness, recycling, cleaner technology and sanitation reduce pollution; each works best when combined with the others.
:::
