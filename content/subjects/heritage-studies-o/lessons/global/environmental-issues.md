## Introduction

Ask an older relative what the land around their village looked like when they were your age. Many will describe thick woodland where there are now bare fields, rivers that flowed all year that now dry up by August, and wild animals that their own grandchildren have never seen outside a national park. These changes did not happen overnight. They are the result of many small decisions made every day by millions of people: cutting a tree for firewood, leaving a tap running, setting a snare, clearing a new field.

The **environment** is everything around us: the land, the air, water, plants, animals and the people who depend on them. When the way people use the environment damages it, we speak of an **environmental issue**. This lesson introduces the main environmental issues facing Zimbabwe and the world, the natural resources they affect, and the practical steps that you, your school and your community can take. You should already know what natural resources are and the difference between renewable and non-renewable resources. Pollution, land degradation and climate change are studied in their own lessons; here they are mentioned only where they link to the bigger picture.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Environment"}
The natural and human surroundings in which people, animals and plants live: land, air, water, plants, animals and the buildings and activities of people.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental issue"}
A problem caused when the environment is damaged or its resources are used faster than they can recover, for example deforestation or poaching.
:::

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The clearing or cutting down of forests and woodland without replacing them.
:::

:::definition{term="Poaching"}
The illegal hunting, killing or capture of wild animals, or illegal harvesting of plants.
:::

:::definition{term="Biodiversity"}
The variety of living things (plants, animals and other organisms) found in an area.
:::

:::definition{term="Preservation"}
Keeping a natural resource in its present state and protecting it from damage, so that it remains for future generations.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- Section 73 of the Constitution gives every person the right to an environment that is not harmful to health and to have it protected for present and future generations.
- The Environmental Management Agency (EMA), set up under the Environmental Management Act, is the main body that protects the environment in Zimbabwe.
- National Tree Planting Day is held on the first Saturday of December.
:::

## The main environmental issues

| Issue | What happens | Main causes in Zimbabwe | Resource affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deforestation | Trees are cut faster than they regrow | Firewood for cooking, wood for curing tobacco, clearing land for farms and settlements, timber, brick-making | Forests, soils, wildlife |
| Poaching | Animals are killed or captured illegally | Demand for ivory, rhino horn and bush meat; poverty; organised criminal groups | Wildlife (elephants, rhinos, pangolins) |
| Overuse of water | Water is taken faster than rain refills rivers, dams and underground stores | Growing towns, irrigation, leaking pipes, wasteful use | Rivers, dams, boreholes |
| Loss of wildlife and biodiversity | Plant and animal species become rare or disappear from an area | Loss of habitat to farming and settlement, poaching, veld fires, pollution | Wildlife, plants, fish, birds |
| Unsustainable mining | Land is dug up and left open; rivers are damaged | Illegal and unregulated gold panning, pits that are not refilled | Land, rivers, minerals |

### Deforestation

Most rural households and many town households cook with firewood. When families grow, more trees are cut. Trees are also cut to cure tobacco, to clear new fields and to make bricks. When the trees go, the soil is exposed to rain and wind, wild fruits and medicinal plants disappear, animals lose their homes and women and girls walk further every year to collect fuel.

### Poaching

Zimbabwe has large populations of elephants and other wildlife in parks such as Hwange, Gonarezhou and Mana Pools. Poachers kill animals for ivory, rhino horn, skins and meat. The pangolin is one of the most heavily trafficked animals in the world. Poaching takes away animals that belong to the whole nation, reduces tourism income and can push species towards extinction. Snares set for bush meat also kill and injure animals they were not meant for.

### Overuse and waste of water

Water is a renewable resource, but only when it is used no faster than rain refills it. In dry years, dams fall low and towns ration water. Leaking pipes, taps left running, washing cars with hosepipes and irrigating in the heat of the day all waste water that others need.

### Loss of wildlife and biodiversity

Every plant and animal has a place in the web of life. Bees pollinate crops; vultures clean up carcasses; trees hold the soil and provide shade. When one species disappears, others are affected. Biodiversity is also part of heritage: many clans have animal totems, and plants are used in food, crafts and traditional remedies.

:::example{title="Reading an environmental problem"}
A fictional ward near Bindura notices that its river now stops flowing in July instead of October.

1. **What has changed?** Trees along the banks have been cut and gardens are cultivated right up to the water's edge.
2. **Why does it matter?** Without roots to hold it, soil washes into the river and fills it with sand, so it holds less water.
3. **Who is affected?** Cattle owners, gardeners, families who draw drinking water, and fish and birds.
4. **What could be done?** Leave a strip of trees and grass along the banks, move gardens away from the edge and plant indigenous trees.
:::

## Strategies to preserve natural resources

Preservation is not only the job of the government. Individuals, schools and communities all have a part to play.

| Level | Strategies |
|---|---|
| Individual | Turn off taps; use energy-saving stoves (tsotso stoves); plant and care for a tree; do not buy bush meat or ivory; report poaching and illegal tree-cutting |
| School | Environment clubs; tree-planting and woodlots; school gardens with water harvesting; clean-ups; debates, drama and posters on National Tree Planting Day |
| Community | By-laws and traditional rules on cutting trees and protecting sacred places; community woodlots; fencing and caring for springs; anti-poaching patrols with ZimParks; sharing benefits from wildlife (e.g. CAMPFIRE) |
| Government | Laws (Environmental Management Act, Parks and Wild Life Act, Forest Act); EMA, ZimParks and the Forestry Commission enforce them; national parks and protected areas |

:::example{title="A school environment club plans its year"}
The environment club of a fictional secondary school in Marondera chooses three projects.

1. **Tree planting:** each Form 1 learner plants and labels an indigenous tree on National Tree Planting Day and waters it through the dry season.
2. **Water:** the club fixes dripping taps with the help of the school groundsman and puts a tank under the hall gutters to catch rainwater for the garden.
3. **Awareness:** the club performs a short play at assembly about a family that stops buying bush meat.

Each project uses a different strategy: replacing a resource, saving a resource and changing behaviour.
:::

### Indigenous and modern ways together

Before colonial rule, many communities protected resources through beliefs and rules: sacred forests and pools where no tree could be cut, totems that forbade eating certain animals, and chiefs who controlled hunting seasons. Today these practices sit alongside modern laws and institutions. Both aim at the same thing: using resources so that they remain for the children not yet born.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating every environmental issue as pollution"}
Pollution is one environmental issue among many. Deforestation, poaching and overuse of water damage the environment without polluting it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing strategies without saying who does them"}
Questions often ask what individuals, schools *or* communities can do. Match the strategy to the level asked about.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that renewable resources can run out"}
Forests, water and wildlife are renewable only if they are used no faster than they recover.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State / identify** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name the issue and the resource it affects, e.g. "deforestation — forests".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Describe / explain** (Paper 2 Section A): give a cause, then its effect, with a Zimbabwean example: "Wood is used to cure tobacco, so woodland around farms is cleared, exposing the soil to erosion."
:::

:::exam-tip
**Suggest** (problem solving): match each strategy to who carries it out and say how it helps, e.g. "A school woodlot provides firewood so pupils' families cut fewer wild trees."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Environmental issues arise when resources are damaged or used faster than they recover.
- The main issues are deforestation, poaching, overuse of water, loss of wildlife and biodiversity, and damage from unregulated mining.
- They affect land, forests, wildlife, rivers, dams and minerals, and so affect food, income, health and heritage.
- Individuals, schools, communities and government can all preserve resources through saving, replacing, protecting and teaching.
- Section 73 of the Constitution protects the environment for present and future generations; EMA leads environmental protection.
:::
