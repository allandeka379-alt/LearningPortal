## Introduction

Ask farmers in almost any part of Zimbabwe about the rains and many will say that the seasons are not what they used to be. The rains seem to start later, stop suddenly in the middle of the growing season and then fall in violent storms. Hot days seem hotter. Some years bring drought and others bring floods. Farmers, scientists and governments around the world are describing the same kind of change, and the main scientific explanation is **climate change**.

Climate change matters to a young Zimbabwean because most families depend on rain-fed farming, because water and electricity from Lake Kariba depend on rainfall, and because the decisions made in your lifetime will shape the climate your children inherit. In this Form 2 lesson you will learn the difference between weather and climate, what causes climate change, how it affects Zimbabwe and the world, and what people are doing to **adapt** to it and to **mitigate** (reduce) it. You should already know about droughts and floods from the lesson on hazards and disasters.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weather"}
The condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time: temperature, rainfall, wind, cloud and humidity today or this week.
:::

:::definition{term="Climate"}
The average pattern of weather in a place over a long period, usually about 30 years or more.
:::

:::definition{term="Climate change"}
A long-term change in the average temperature and weather patterns of the Earth or a region, now mainly caused by human activities that add greenhouse gases to the atmosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Greenhouse gases"}
Gases in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide, that trap heat from the sun and keep the Earth warm. More of them traps more heat.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
Changing the way we live and work to cope with the effects of climate change, for example by growing drought-tolerant crops.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions that reduce the causes of climate change, mainly by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or absorbing them, for example using solar energy or planting trees.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The UN Framework Convention on Climate Change was agreed in 1992; the Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to keep global warming well below 2 °C.
- Zimbabwe is among the countries that have joined the Paris Agreement.
- Zimbabwe produces only a small share of the world's greenhouse gases, but it is very vulnerable to the effects of climate change.
:::

## Weather and climate

| | Weather | Climate |
|---|---|---|
| Time scale | Hours, days, weeks | Decades (about 30 years or more) |
| Example | "It rained heavily in Mutare yesterday." | "Mutare usually receives more rain than Bulawayo, mostly between November and March." |
| Changes | Changes every day | Changes slowly over many years |

One hot week or one drought does not prove that the climate has changed. Climate change is about the **long-term pattern**: more hot days, less reliable rain, and more frequent or more severe droughts and storms over many years.

## Causes of climate change

The Earth is kept warm by a natural **greenhouse effect**: gases in the atmosphere let sunlight in but trap some of the heat that the Earth gives off, rather like the glass of a greenhouse. Without them the Earth would be too cold for life. Since the industrial revolution, human activities have added large amounts of these gases, so more heat is trapped and the Earth is warming.

| Human activity | Greenhouse gas released | How |
|---|---|---|
| Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, petrol, diesel, gas) | Carbon dioxide | Power stations, vehicles, factories and aircraft burn fuel |
| Deforestation and veld fires | Carbon dioxide | Trees store carbon; cutting and burning releases it, and fewer trees remain to absorb it |
| Agriculture | Methane and nitrous oxide | Cattle produce methane during digestion; rice paddies release methane; some fertilisers release nitrous oxide |
| Waste | Methane | Rotting rubbish in dumpsites releases methane |

The countries that industrialised first have released most of the greenhouse gases over history. African countries, including Zimbabwe, have released far less, yet many of them are among the most affected. This is why international agreements speak of *common but differentiated* responsibilities: every country must act, but those that caused more of the problem, and can afford more, are expected to do more.

## Effects of climate change

| On Zimbabwe | On the world |
|---|---|
| Less reliable rainfall; rains starting late and long dry spells mid-season | Rising global temperatures and more heatwaves |
| More frequent droughts, especially in the south and west | Melting glaciers and ice sheets; rising sea levels threaten coastal cities and islands |
| More intense storms and floods, such as those linked to tropical cyclones | Stronger storms and floods in many regions |
| Crop failure and hunger; livestock deaths | Food shortages and higher world food prices |
| Low water levels in dams, including Lake Kariba, causing power cuts | Loss of plants and animals (biodiversity) |
| Health: heat stress; diseases such as malaria may spread to new areas; water-borne disease after floods | Migration of people away from affected areas |

:::example{title="Climate change and electricity"}
1. **Fact:** much of Zimbabwe's electricity comes from the hydroelectric power station at Kariba.
2. **Link:** in drought years, less water flows into Lake Kariba from the Zambezi.
3. **Effect:** the power station must generate less, so homes, schools, hospitals and factories face long power cuts.
4. **Wider effect:** factories produce less, jobs are lost and families switch to firewood, which increases deforestation, which adds more carbon dioxide.

This shows how climate change can start a cycle that makes itself worse.
:::

## Responding to climate change

### Adaptation (coping with the effects)

- **Drought-tolerant crops and varieties:** small grains such as sorghum and millets, and early-maturing maize varieties, survive dry spells better.
- **Conservation farming:** planting basins, mulching and minimum tillage keep moisture in the soil. Zimbabwe's Pfumvudza/Intwasa programme (from 2020) promotes this method.
- **Water harvesting:** tanks, small dams and ponds catch rainwater; drip irrigation saves water.
- **Early warning and climate information:** seasonal forecasts help farmers decide when and what to plant.
- **Diversifying livelihoods:** keeping small livestock, beekeeping or nutrition gardens so that a family does not depend on one crop.

### Mitigation (reducing the causes)

- **Renewable energy:** solar panels and solar water heaters, mini-hydro and other clean sources instead of coal and diesel.
- **Afforestation and protecting forests:** trees absorb carbon dioxide; National Tree Planting Day encourages planting.
- **Energy efficiency:** efficient stoves and light bulbs; maintaining vehicles; using public transport.
- **Better waste management:** recycling and composting reduce methane from dumpsites.
- **International agreements:** countries set targets to cut emissions and richer countries promise money and technology to help others.

:::example{title="A farmer adapts"}
Chipo farms in a fictional ward near Gwanda, where rain has become less reliable.

1. **Problem:** her long-season maize failed twice in five years.
2. **Adaptation:** she plants part of her field with sorghum and an early-maturing maize variety in planting basins with mulch.
3. **Extra measure:** her family digs a small pond to catch run-off for a nutrition garden.
4. **Result:** in a dry year she still harvests some grain and vegetables. Each measure alone helps a little; together they make the family much less vulnerable.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing weather and climate"}
Weather is the day-to-day condition; climate is the long-term average. A cold week does not disprove climate change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up adaptation and mitigation"}
Adaptation copes with the effects (drought-tolerant crops). Mitigation tackles the causes (solar power, planting trees). Some actions, such as tree planting, do both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing climate change with the ozone hole"}
Damage to the ozone layer is a different problem. Climate change is caused mainly by greenhouse gases trapping heat.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Weather is short-term; climate is the long-term average; climate change is a long-term change in that average.
- Human activities, mainly burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture, add greenhouse gases that trap more heat.
- Zimbabwe faces less reliable rain, droughts, floods, crop failure, low dam levels, power cuts and health risks.
- Adaptation helps people cope (drought-tolerant crops, conservation farming, water harvesting).
- Mitigation reduces the causes (renewable energy, afforestation, efficiency), supported by agreements such as the Paris Agreement (2015).
:::
