## Introduction

In most Zimbabwean homes children help: they sweep the yard, fetch water, wash plates, look after younger brothers and sisters, and help in the fields during planting and harvest. Elders see this as part of growing up. Through it children learn skills, responsibility and *unhu/ubuntu*, the values of caring for others and belonging to a family and community.

But not all work done by children is good for them. A twelve-year-old who misses school to herd cattle every day, a girl sent to town to work as a full-time maid, or a boy who digs for gold in an unsafe pit is doing **child labour**. The difference between helping at home and child labour is one of the most important ideas in this Form 4 lesson. You will learn what child labour is, its forms, causes and effects, the laws and conventions that protect children, and how to tell child labour apart from a child's normal responsibilities. You should already know the rights of the child from the lesson on children's rights.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Child"}
In Zimbabwe, under section 81 of the Constitution, every person under the age of 18.
:::

:::definition{term="Child labour"}
Work that deprives children of their childhood, their education or their dignity, or that harms their physical or mental health and development. It is too heavy, too long, too dangerous or too early for the child's age.
:::

:::definition{term="Children's responsibilities (child work)"}
Light, age-appropriate tasks that children do to help their families, which do not harm their health or interfere with their schooling, rest and play.
:::

:::definition{term="Worst forms of child labour"}
Slavery and practices like it (including trafficking of children and debt bondage), using children in prostitution, pornography or illegal activities such as drug trafficking, and work likely to harm children's health, safety or morals.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- Section 81 of the Constitution gives every child the right to be protected from economic and sexual exploitation, from child labour and from abuse.
- ILO Convention No. 138 (1973) sets minimum ages for work; ILO Convention No. 182 (1999) calls for urgent action against the worst forms of child labour. Zimbabwe has ratified both.
- The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) and the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child (1990) protect children from economic exploitation.
- World Day Against Child Labour is 12 June.
:::

## Child labour or children's responsibilities?

| Question to ask | Children's responsibilities | Child labour |
|---|---|---|
| Is it suitable for the child's age and strength? | Yes: light tasks | No: heavy, dangerous or adult work |
| Does the child still attend school and do homework? | Yes | No, or the child is too tired to learn |
| How long does it take? | A short time each day | Long hours, often every day |
| Is it safe? | Yes | No: chemicals, machines, deep pits, night work, abuse |
| Who benefits? | The family, and the child learns skills | Often an employer who profits from cheap labour |
| Does the child have time to rest and play? | Yes | No |

:::example{title="Applying the test"}
1. **Tafadzwa (13)** sweeps the yard and fetches water before school, and helps weed the family field on Saturday mornings. She attends school every day. **Children's responsibilities**: light, short, safe, and school comes first.
2. **Farai (12)** was sent to work on a neighbour's farm in exchange for food. He herds cattle from dawn to dusk and has not been to school this term. **Child labour**: long hours, no school, and someone else benefits.
3. **Nokuthula (15)** works nights selling goods at a bus terminus to pay her school fees. **Child labour**: although she wants to stay in school, night work in an unsafe place harms her health, safety and learning.
:::

## Forms of child labour

| Form | Examples | Main dangers |
|---|---|---|
| Agricultural labour | Long hours on commercial farms, herding cattle instead of attending school, handling pesticides | Chemicals, heat, injury, missed schooling |
| Mining | Digging and carrying ore in unsafe small-scale and illegal gold mines; using mercury | Pit collapses, poisoning, injury |
| Domestic labour | Children, mostly girls, working as full-time maids or nannies in other people's homes | Long hours, isolation, abuse, no schooling |
| Street work and vending | Selling goods, begging, carrying loads at markets and bus termini | Traffic, violence, exploitation, night work |
| Worst forms | Trafficking of children, sexual exploitation, use in crime | Severe physical and emotional harm |

## Causes of child labour

- **Poverty:** families depend on the child's income or labour to survive.
- **School costs:** fees, uniforms and books can push children out of school and into work.
- **Orphanhood and family breakdown:** orphans and children in child-headed households may have to earn a living.
- **Demand for cheap labour:** some employers want workers who are cheap and less likely to complain.
- **Cultural attitudes:** some people believe children should earn early or that girls' education matters less.
- **Weak enforcement:** laws are not always known or enforced, especially in remote areas and informal work.
- **Disasters and economic hardship:** droughts and job losses push families to rely on children.

## Effects of child labour

| On the child | On the family and nation |
|---|---|
| Missed or interrupted schooling; low skills | A cycle of poverty: uneducated children become poorly paid adults |
| Injury, illness, poisoning, stunted growth | A less skilled workforce, slowing national development |
| Exhaustion, stress, low self-esteem | Children's labour may replace adult workers, lowering adult wages |
| Exposure to abuse, exploitation, drugs and crime | Costs to health and social services |
| Loss of childhood: no time to rest or play | Rights in the Constitution and international conventions are not met |

## Laws and conventions against child labour

| Law or convention | What it does (in outline) |
|---|---|
| Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013), section 81 | Protects every child from economic and sexual exploitation, child labour and abuse; gives children rights to education, health care and parental care |
| Children's Act | Provides for the protection, welfare and care of children, including children in need of care |
| Labour Act | Sets rules on the employment of young persons and limits the work they may do |
| ILO Convention No. 138 (1973) | Sets minimum ages for admission to employment |
| ILO Convention No. 182 (1999) | Calls for immediate action to end the worst forms of child labour |
| UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) | Protects children from economic exploitation and harmful work; right to education |
| African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child (1990) | Protects African children from all forms of economic exploitation |

### Measures that reduce child labour

- Keeping children in school: assistance with fees for vulnerable children, school feeding, flexible support for orphans.
- Supporting poor families so they do not depend on children's earnings.
- Enforcing labour laws through inspections of farms, mines and workplaces.
- Awareness in communities, churches and schools about the harm of child labour.
- Reporting cases to the police Victim Friendly Unit, the Department of Social Welfare or a trusted adult.

:::example{title="A community responds"}
A fictional village in Mashonaland Central notices that several boys have stopped attending school to work at an illegal gold mining site.

1. **Village meeting:** the headman, teachers and parents discuss the dangers of pits and mercury.
2. **Support:** the school helps the families apply for assistance with fees.
3. **Reporting:** the case is reported to the police and the local social welfare office.
4. **Outcome:** most boys return to school, and the community agrees to watch out for children at mining sites.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all work by children child labour"}
Light household chores that leave time for school and play are part of growing up. Child labour harms the child or stops their education.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up rights and responsibilities"}
Children have a right to protection from child labour AND responsibilities at home. The two do not contradict each other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Citing laws you are unsure of"}
Name section 81 of the Constitution and the ILO conventions confidently; describe other laws in outline rather than inventing section numbers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A child is anyone under 18; child labour is work that harms a child's health, development or education.
- Children's responsibilities are light, safe, age-appropriate tasks that leave time for school and play.
- Forms include farm work, mining, domestic labour, street work and the worst forms such as trafficking.
- Causes include poverty, school costs, orphanhood, demand for cheap labour and weak enforcement; effects include lost schooling, harm and a cycle of poverty.
- Section 81 of the Constitution, the Children's Act, the Labour Act, ILO Conventions 138 and 182, the UNCRC and the African Charter protect children.
:::
