## Introduction

A message arrives on a phone: "Jobs abroad for young women and men. Hotel and farm work. Good pay, free transport, no experience needed. Leave next week." For a young person who has finished school and cannot find work, such an offer can look like the answer to every problem. Sometimes it is a genuine job. Sometimes it is a trap set by **human traffickers**, criminals who use lies, threats and debts to move people and then exploit them.

Human trafficking happens in every region of the world, including Southern Africa, and it can also happen inside one country. Knowing the warning signs is one of the best protections. This lesson, taught in Forms 1 and 3, explains what human trafficking is, how it differs from smuggling, the forms it takes, why it happens, the harm it does and how it can be prevented. It does not describe crimes in detail. If you or someone you know is in danger, speak to a trusted adult, a teacher, a clinic or the police Victim Friendly Unit.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Human trafficking"}
Recruiting, moving, receiving or holding people by using threats, force, deception, debt or abuse of their vulnerable position, in order to exploit them. When the victim is a child (under 18), moving or holding the child for exploitation is trafficking even without threats or lies.
:::

:::definition{term="People smuggling"}
Helping a person, who agrees to it and usually pays, to cross a border illegally. The relationship normally ends once the person has crossed.
:::

:::definition{term="Exploitation"}
Unfairly using another person for one's own benefit, for example through unpaid or forced work or sexual abuse.
:::

:::definition{term="Safe migration"}
Moving to another place or country legally and with full information, valid documents, a real contract and contacts at home.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The main international agreement is the UN Trafficking in Persons Protocol (the Palermo Protocol) of 2000.
- Zimbabwe passed a Trafficking in Persons Act in 2014.
- The UN World Day against Trafficking in Persons is 30 July.
:::

## Three parts of trafficking

Trafficking is usually explained in three parts. All three are present when an adult is trafficked.

| Part | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| The act | What is done? | Recruiting, transporting, transferring, hiding or receiving a person |
| The means | How is it done? | Threats, force, kidnapping, false promises, debt, taking away documents, abusing a person's poverty or youth |
| The purpose | Why is it done? | Exploitation: forced labour, sexual exploitation, domestic servitude, removal of organs |

## Trafficking and smuggling compared

| | Human trafficking | People smuggling |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | No real consent: the person is deceived or forced (and a child can never consent) | The person agrees to be moved |
| Border | May cross a border, or happen inside one country | Always involves crossing a border illegally |
| After the journey | Exploitation continues for a long time | The relationship usually ends on arrival |
| Crime against | The person | The state's border laws |

The two can overlap: a person who agreed to be smuggled can be trapped and exploited on arrival, and then becomes a victim of trafficking.

## Forms of trafficking

| Form | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Forced labour | Work in farms, mines, factories, construction or fishing for little or no pay, under threat, often to repay a "debt" that never ends |
| Sexual exploitation | Forcing people, often women and girls, into the sex trade |
| Domestic servitude | Working in a household as a maid or nanny with no freedom, little or no pay, documents taken away |
| Child trafficking | Moving children for labour, begging, sexual exploitation or forced marriage |
| Organ removal | Taking a person's organ for sale, by force or deception |

## Causes of trafficking

Trafficking has **push factors** that make people vulnerable and **pull factors** that traffickers use as bait.

- **Poverty and unemployment:** people take risks to support their families.
- **False job and study offers:** promises of work as waiters, nurses' aides, farm workers or domestic workers abroad.
- **Lack of information:** people do not know the warning signs or their rights.
- **Family breakdown and orphanhood:** children without protection are easier to target.
- **Conflict and disasters:** people who have lost homes and documents are vulnerable.
- **Demand:** some employers and buyers want cheap labour or exploit others for profit.
- **Weak enforcement and corruption:** criminals believe they will not be caught.

:::example{title="Spotting the warning signs"}
Sipho, aged 19 and living in Bulawayo, sees an online advert for factory work in a neighbouring country.

1. **Sign 1:** the "agent" asks him to pay a fee and says a contract is "not needed".
2. **Sign 2:** the agent will hold his passport "for safekeeping".
3. **Sign 3:** he must leave in two days and tell no one.
4. **Safe response:** Sipho shows the advert to his aunt, checks whether the company and agency are registered, asks for a written contract and refuses to hand over his passport. He decides not to go.

Any one of these signs is a reason to stop and check. Together they point strongly to trafficking.
:::

## Effects of trafficking

| On victims | On families and society |
|---|---|
| Physical harm and illness; injuries from dangerous work | Families lose members and income; some fall into debt |
| Deep emotional trauma, fear and shame | Communities lose young, productive people |
| Loss of freedom, documents and contact with home | Organised crime and corruption grow |
| No pay; trapped by debts | Damage to a country's reputation; costs of rescue and care |
| Exposure to HIV and other infections | Survivors may face stigma when they return |

Survivors need care, not blame. A person who was trafficked is a victim of crime, even if they first agreed to travel.

## Prevention and mitigation

| Measure | Who | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Schools, churches, media, NGOs | People learn the warning signs and how to check job offers |
| Laws and enforcement | Parliament, police, courts | The Trafficking in Persons Act makes trafficking a serious crime; traffickers are prosecuted |
| Reporting | Individuals, families, transport workers | Suspicious adverts and situations are reported to the police |
| Safe migration | Individuals, government, recruitment agencies | Valid passports, registered agencies, written contracts, contacts at home |
| Border control and cooperation | Immigration officers; SADC and other countries | Victims are identified and traffickers caught across borders |
| Support for survivors | Government, NGOs, IOM, communities | Shelter, counselling, health care and help to return home and rebuild lives |
| Reducing vulnerability | Government, communities | Jobs, skills training and support for orphans and poor families |

:::example{title="A school awareness day"}
A fictional school in Gwanda holds an awareness day on 30 July.

1. **Drama:** learners act out a false job offer, stopping the play to ask the audience which warning signs they noticed.
2. **Checklist:** each learner takes home a card: *Is there a written contract? Is the agency registered? Do I keep my own documents? Does my family know where I am going?*
3. **Help:** the guidance teacher explains how to report concerns to the police Victim Friendly Unit or a trusted adult.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating trafficking and smuggling as the same"}
Smuggling is an agreed, illegal border crossing; trafficking is about exploitation and can happen without crossing any border.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming the victim"}
Agreeing to travel does not mean agreeing to be exploited. Trafficked people are victims of crime.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining them"}
"Poverty" alone is a list item. Explain: "Poverty makes young people accept risky job offers abroad without checking them."
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define / distinguish** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): give the act, means and purpose; contrast trafficking with smuggling on consent, borders and what happens after the journey.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain causes and effects** (Paper 2 Section A): link each cause to how it makes people vulnerable; give effects on victims AND on society.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Suggest / assess measures** (Paper 2 Section B): name who carries out each measure and judge how well it works, e.g. laws work only when cases are reported.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Human trafficking is recruiting, moving or holding people by force, threats or deception in order to exploit them; children are victims even without force or lies.
- It differs from smuggling, which is an agreed illegal border crossing.
- Forms include forced labour, sexual exploitation, domestic servitude, child trafficking and organ removal.
- Causes include poverty, unemployment, false job offers, lack of information, conflict and demand for cheap labour.
- Prevention combines awareness, laws (the Trafficking in Persons Act, 2014), reporting, safe migration, cooperation and support for survivors.
:::
