## Introduction

In central Harare stands a bronze statue of a woman, unveiled in 2021. It honours **Mbuya Nehanda**, the spirit medium who became one of the most famous leaders of the First Chimurenga. Hospitals, schools, streets and songs across Zimbabwe carry the names of the men and women who resisted colonial rule in 1896-97. During the liberation war of the 1960s and 1970s, fighters sang about them and saw themselves as finishing what they had started.

Who were these leaders, what did they do, and why are they still remembered more than a century later? In this Form 3 lesson you identify the heroes and heroines of the First Chimurenga/Umvukela, explain their roles and consider why remembering them matters. You should already know the causes and events of the risings of 1896-97.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hero / heroine"}
A man (hero) or woman (heroine) admired for courage, sacrifice and outstanding service to their people.
:::

:::definition{term="Spirit medium (svikiro)"}
A person through whom an ancestral spirit is believed to speak. Nehanda and Kaguvi were the names of powerful spirits; the mediums who carried them in 1896-97 were Charwe Nyakasikana and Gumboreshumba.
:::

:::definition{term="Commemoration"}
Honouring the memory of a person or event, for example through monuments, names, holidays, songs and ceremonies.
:::

:::definition{term="Martyr"}
A person who suffers or is killed because of their beliefs or their cause, and is honoured for it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Names and dates to learn"}
**Mbuya Nehanda** (medium Charwe Nyakasikana) and **Sekuru Kaguvi** (medium Gumboreshumba) were captured in late 1897 and **executed in April 1898**. **Mkwati** was linked with the Mwari shrines in the Matobo Hills. Chiefs **Mashayamombe** and **Makoni** led resistance in Mashonaland. A statue of Mbuya Nehanda was unveiled in Harare in **2021**.
:::

## The leaders and their roles

| Leader | Who they were | Role in 1896-97 |
|---|---|---|
| Mbuya Nehanda (Charwe Nyakasikana) | Medium of the Nehanda spirit in the Mazowe area | Encouraged people in her area to resist; regarded by many as a leader of the rising there; captured, tried and executed in April 1898 |
| Sekuru Kaguvi (Gumboreshumba) | Medium of the Kaguvi spirit, west of Salisbury | Encouraged chiefs to rise and spread the call to fight; executed in April 1898 |
| Mkwati | Priest/medium linked with the Mwari shrines in the Matobo Hills | Inspired Ndebele fighters and is believed to have linked the Ndebele and Shona risings |
| Chief Mashayamombe | Chief near the Mhondoro area, south-west of Salisbury | Led armed resistance from his fortified stronghold; held out until he was killed in 1897 |
| Chief Makoni (Chingaira) | Chief in the Makoni area, in the east | Led resistance from caves and hills; captured and executed in 1897 |
| Ndebele leaders | Izinduna and members of the royal family | Led regiments in Matabeleland in 1896 and negotiated with Rhodes at the Matobo indabas |

Many other chiefs, mediums, men and women took part. The ordinary people who carried food, hid fighters and suffered in the caves are also part of the story.

### Mbuya Nehanda

Charwe Nyakasikana lived in the Mazowe area. As the medium of Nehanda, one of the most respected spirits in Mashonaland, she had great authority. During the rising she encouraged resistance in her area. After the Company regained control, she was accused of involvement in the killing of a Company official, tried and sentenced to death. She was executed in **April 1898**. According to tradition, she said before her death that her bones would rise again; liberation fighters later saw themselves as fulfilling this.

### Sekuru Kaguvi

Gumboreshumba, the medium of Kaguvi, lived west of Salisbury. He is regarded as one of the main organisers of the rising in central Mashonaland, sending messages to chiefs and encouraging them to fight. He was captured in late 1897 and executed in April 1898.

:::example{title="Reading a commemoration as a source"}
A fictional school calendar says: "18 April: Independence Day. Assembly theme: from Nehanda to independence."

1. **Link made:** the school connects the First Chimurenga with independence in 1980.
2. **Why:** nationalists presented the liberation struggle as a continuation of 1896-97.
3. **What learners gain:** a sense of history and of the sacrifices made over generations.
4. **Caution:** a commemoration presents a message; historians also study evidence about what each leader actually did.
:::

## How they are remembered today

| Form of remembrance | Examples |
|---|---|
| Monuments | The statue of Mbuya Nehanda in Harare (2021) |
| Names | Hospitals, schools, streets and buildings named after leaders of 1896-97, such as the Mbuya Nehanda Maternity Hospital in Harare |
| Songs, poems and literature | Liberation-war songs, poems and novels refer to Nehanda and the 1896 risings |
| Education | The heroes are studied in History and Heritage Studies |
| Commemorations | Heroes Day and other national events recall the long history of resistance |

## Why remembering them matters

- **National identity:** they show that resistance to colonial rule began as soon as it was imposed, giving Zimbabweans a shared history.
- **Inspiration:** the nationalists and fighters of the Second Chimurenga drew courage from their example.
- **Recognition of women:** Mbuya Nehanda shows that women played leading roles in the struggle.
- **Respect for indigenous religion:** the mediums' role shows the importance of indigenous beliefs in community life.
- **Values:** courage, sacrifice, unity and love of one's land are values young people can learn.
- **Learning from history:** studying them, with evidence, helps people understand how colonial rule affected the country.

:::example{title="Choosing a name for a new school block"}
The fictional Lupane Valley Secondary School is naming its new science block. Learners propose several names.

1. **Proposal:** a learner suggests naming it after Mkwati, because he linked the Ndebele and Shona risings.
2. **Discussion:** others suggest Nehanda or a local hero of the area.
3. **Criteria agreed:** the name should honour a real contribution, be supported by evidence and unite the school community.
4. **Outcome:** the school chooses a name and writes a short, accurate plaque explaining the person's role.
5. **Lesson:** commemoration works best when it is accurate and inclusive.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the spirit and the medium"}
Nehanda and Kaguvi are the names of spirits. The mediums in 1896-97 were **Charwe Nyakasikana** and **Gumboreshumba**. In exam answers you may use "Mbuya Nehanda" and "Sekuru Kaguvi", but know the mediums' names.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing quotations"}
Do not invent speeches for real leaders. Describe what they did and, if you mention Nehanda's words, say "according to tradition".
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the Ndebele leaders and ordinary people"}
The First Chimurenga/Umvukela began in Matabeleland. Mention Mkwati and the Ndebele leaders, and the ordinary people who supported the fighters.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Identify and explain roles"}
For *identify the heroes and heroines* (Paper 2 Section A (a) or (b)), name Nehanda, Kaguvi, Mkwati, Mashayamombe and Makoni. For *explain the roles they played* (Section A (c)), take two or three leaders and say what each did, with a place or date.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Significance"}
For a Section B question on the significance of remembering them, cover identity, inspiration for the Second Chimurenga, the role of women and indigenous religion, and reach a judgement.
:::
