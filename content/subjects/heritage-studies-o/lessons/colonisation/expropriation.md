## Introduction

Ask an elderly relative where their grandparents lived and you may hear a story of a move: a family that once farmed a fertile valley and was later told to leave for a dry, crowded "reserve". Stories like this are found in many parts of Zimbabwe, in Shona, Ndebele, Tonga, Kalanga and other communities. They explain why land is still one of the most important and most debated issues in the country.

**Expropriation** means taking property away from its owners, usually by the state and often without fair payment. Between 1890 and 1980 the colonial governments of Southern Rhodesia took land, cattle, minerals, forests and wildlife from indigenous people, and used taxes and laws to force them to work for settlers. In this Form 3 lesson you learn how this was done, which laws were used, and what the effects were. You should already know how the British South Africa Company (BSAC) occupied the country in 1890 and about the war of 1893-94 and the risings of 1896-97.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Expropriation"}
The taking of property such as land, cattle or minerals from its owners by a government or company, usually without their consent and without fair compensation.
:::

:::definition{term="Land alienation"}
The process by which land that belonged to indigenous communities was taken and given to settlers, companies or the state.
:::

:::definition{term="Native reserve"}
An area set aside by the colonial government for African occupation, usually on poorer, drier land. Reserves were later renamed Tribal Trust Lands and are today's communal lands.
:::

:::definition{term="Forced labour"}
Work that people are made to do against their will, under threat of punishment. In Shona it was often called *chibharo*.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Laws and dates to learn"}
First reserves (Gwaai and Shangani) **1894**. Land Apportionment Act **1930**. Native Land Husbandry Act **1951**. Land Tenure Act **1969**. Taxes on Africans were introduced in the **1890s**.
:::

## How land was taken

After the occupation of 1890, pioneers were promised farms and gold claims. After the war of 1893-94 the Company took over most of the land around Bulawayo and moved many Ndebele people to two reserves, the **Gwaai and Shangani reserves**, which were far from water and not good for farming. Across the country, settlers and companies took the most fertile, well-watered land on the higher ground, and Africans who stayed on it became tenants who had to pay rent or work for the new owner.

Later governments turned this into law. Each new law divided the land more firmly by race.

| Law | Year | Main provisions (outline) | Effects on Africans |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land Apportionment Act | 1930 | Divided the land into European areas, Native Reserves, Native Purchase Areas (where a few Africans could buy farms) and unassigned or forest land; Europeans received about half the land, including most of the best | Africans could not own land in European areas; many families were moved to reserves; reserves became overcrowded |
| Native Land Husbandry Act | 1951 | In the reserves: set limits on the number of cattle a family could keep (destocking), gave individual rights to small plots, forced conservation works such as contour ridges, and reduced the role of chiefs in allocating land | Young men often received no land; people lost cattle; resentment grew and helped the rise of nationalism; the Act was abandoned in the early 1960s |
| Land Tenure Act | 1969 | Replaced the 1930 Act; divided the land into roughly equal European and African areas, even though Africans were the great majority of the population | Kept the best land for a small settler minority; evictions continued; became a major grievance in the liberation struggle |

:::example{title="Reading a law as a historian"}
Imagine a family of eight in a reserve near Gwanda in the 1950s. Under the Native Land Husbandry Act an officer counts their cattle and tells them they must sell four, because the reserve is "overstocked".

1. **What the law said it was for:** conservation, to stop soil erosion.
2. **What the family experienced:** loss of wealth, of draught power and of cattle needed for roora/lobola.
3. **The real cause of overstocking:** too many people and cattle had been crowded onto too little land by earlier laws.
4. **Conclusion:** the Act treated a symptom (erosion) but not the cause (land alienation), so Africans saw it as another form of expropriation.
:::

## Cattle, minerals, forests and wildlife

**Cattle.** After the 1893-94 war the Company seized large numbers of Ndebele cattle, which were the main form of wealth and were needed for roora/lobola, ploughing and ceremonies. Later, destocking under the 1951 Act reduced herds further.

**Minerals.** Under the Rudd Concession and the Royal Charter, mineral rights belonged to the Company and later to the colonial state, which issued claims to settlers and mining companies. Africans who had mined gold, copper and iron for centuries were no longer allowed to mine for themselves. They worked in the mines as low-paid labourers, often living in crowded compounds.

**Forests and wildlife.** Forests were declared reserves and timber was cut by companies. Game laws made hunting by Africans illegal without a licence, so a source of meat and income became "poaching". National parks and forest areas were created on land from which communities were removed.

## Taxes and forced labour

Settler farms and mines needed workers, but many Africans preferred to farm their own land. The government therefore used **taxes** to force them into wage labour:

| Measure | How it worked |
|---|---|
| Hut tax (1890s) and later poll (head) tax | Had to be paid in cash, so men had to work for wages to earn money |
| Forced labour (chibharo) | Chiefs were told to supply workers; police and officials rounded up men for farms, mines and roads |
| Labour recruitment | Workers were also brought from neighbouring territories; wages were kept low |
| Pass laws | Africans needed passes to move and to seek work in towns |
| Dipping, grazing and other fees | Added further cash needs in the reserves |

:::example{title="Why a tax can force people to work"}
Tatenda's great-grandfather grew enough food for his family and sold a little grain. A new tax of a fixed amount of cash is announced, payable every year.

1. He cannot pay in grain or cattle; only cash is accepted.
2. Grain prices for Africans are low, so selling grain does not raise enough.
3. He leaves home for months to work on a mine near Kwekwe.
4. Result: the tax has done what the government intended, supplying cheap labour, while his family's farm suffers without him.
:::

## Effects on indigenous people

- **Loss of land and homes**: families were moved, often more than once, to poorer land; graves and sacred places were left behind.
- **Overcrowding and soil erosion** in the reserves, leading to poverty and hunger.
- **Loss of cattle**, and with it wealth, draught power and the means of paying roora/lobola.
- **Migrant labour**: men worked away from home; women carried more of the farm work and family care.
- **Loss of authority** of chiefs, who became agents of the colonial administration.
- **Resistance**: communities resisted evictions. In the late 1960s **Chief Rekayi Tangwena** and his people resisted removal from their land in the Nyanga area. Anger over land helped build the nationalist movement and the liberation struggle.

Land redistribution after independence is covered in the lesson on land ownership.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the three land laws"}
1930: Land Apportionment Act (land divided by race). 1951: Native Land Husbandry Act (destocking and individual plots in the reserves). 1969: Land Tenure Act (roughly equal halves, replacing the 1930 Act).
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing instead of explaining"}
"Hut tax" is a list item. "The hut tax had to be paid in cash, which forced men to work on settler farms and mines" is an explanation that earns full marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking expropriation was only about land"}
Cattle, minerals, forests, wildlife and labour were also taken or controlled.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify* or *state* (Paper 2 Section A): name a law and its year, or a resource. *Describe*: say what the law or measure did. *Explain the effects*: give the effect and develop it ("…so the reserves became overcrowded and the soil was eroded"). For a Section B essay asking *how far* land was the main grievance, weigh land against labour, taxes, cattle and political exclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Dates"}
Paper 1 multiple-choice items often test which law came in which year. Learn 1930, 1951 and 1969 together.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Expropriation is the taking of property without consent or fair compensation; colonial rule took land, cattle, minerals, forests, wildlife and labour.
- The first reserves (Gwaai and Shangani) were set up in 1894; the Land Apportionment Act (1930) divided land by race.
- The Native Land Husbandry Act (1951) imposed destocking and individual plots; the Land Tenure Act (1969) split the land into roughly equal halves.
- Taxes payable in cash, forced labour (chibharo) and pass laws pushed Africans into low-paid work.
- The effects were overcrowding, erosion, poverty, migrant labour and resentment that fed the liberation struggle.
:::
