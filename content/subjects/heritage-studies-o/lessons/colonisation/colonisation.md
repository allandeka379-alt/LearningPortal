## Introduction

Look at a map of Africa and you will see many borders drawn as straight lines across deserts and forests. Few of those lines were drawn by Africans. Most were agreed by European governments in the late nineteenth century, often in meeting rooms thousands of kilometres away. Zimbabwe's own borders were shaped in that period.

In just a few years, between 1887 and 1890, the independent Ndebele and Shona societies of the plateau came under the control of a British company. It began with treaties and a concession signed at Lobengula's court, and ended with a column of armed men raising a flag at Fort Salisbury, now Harare. In this Form 3 lesson you learn why colonisation happened, what the Berlin Conference decided, what each treaty said and how the occupation took place. You should already know about the Ndebele state and the foreigners who visited Lobengula's court.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Colonisation"}
The process by which a foreign power takes control of a territory and its people, ruling them and using their land and resources for its own benefit.
:::

:::definition{term="Scramble for Africa"}
The rush by European powers in the late nineteenth century to claim and occupy African territory.
:::

:::definition{term="Treaty"}
A formal written agreement between two or more rulers or states.
:::

:::definition{term="Royal Charter"}
A document from the British Crown giving a company the right to govern, make laws, keep a police force and develop a territory on Britain's behalf.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective occupation"}
The rule agreed at the Berlin Conference that a European power could claim African territory only if it actually controlled it, for example through officials, police or settlers.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
Berlin Conference **1884-85**. Grobler Treaty **1887**. Moffat Treaty **February 1888**. Rudd Concession **30 October 1888**. Royal Charter to the British South Africa Company **29 October 1889**. Pioneer Column reached **Fort Salisbury on 12 September 1890**.
:::

## Causes of colonisation

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| Economic | Europe's industries wanted raw materials (minerals, cotton, rubber), new markets for manufactured goods and places to invest; rumours of gold north of the Limpopo, after the gold discoveries in the Transvaal |
| Political | Rivalry between European powers; colonies brought prestige; Britain wanted to stop the Portuguese, the Germans and the Boers from controlling the interior |
| Strategic | Protecting trade routes and existing colonies; Rhodes's dream of British territory from the Cape to Cairo |
| Social | Surplus population and unemployment in Europe; settlers seeking land; missionary and "civilising" ideas that Europeans used to justify their rule |

## The Berlin Conference (1884-85)

The German Chancellor **Otto von Bismarck** called a conference of European powers in Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885 to manage their competition in Africa. **No African ruler was invited.** Its main resolutions, in outline, were:

- a power claiming African territory must **notify** the other powers;
- claims must be backed by **effective occupation**;
- **free navigation** on the Congo and Niger rivers, and free trade in the Congo basin;
- a commitment to end the **slave trade**.

The conference did not divide all of Africa, but its rules sped up the scramble: each power rushed to sign treaties with African rulers and to occupy territory so that its claims would be recognised.

## The treaties that led to the colonisation of Zimbabwe

| Date | Treaty or document | Main terms (outline) | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1887 | Grobler Treaty | A treaty of friendship between Lobengula and the Transvaal (South African Republic), negotiated by Pieter Grobler | Alarmed the British, who feared Boer expansion north |
| February 1888 | Moffat Treaty | Lobengula agreed not to make any treaty with, or give land to, any other foreign power without British approval | Placed Matabeleland in the British sphere of influence and kept rivals out |
| 30 October 1888 | Rudd Concession | Gave Rudd, Maguire and Thompson (for Rhodes) exclusive rights to all metals and minerals in the kingdom, and power to do whatever was necessary to win them; in return Lobengula was promised £100 a month, 1,000 rifles, 100,000 rounds of ammunition and a steamboat on the Zambezi (or £500) | Used by Rhodes to obtain the Royal Charter |
| 29 October 1889 | Royal Charter | Granted to the British South Africa Company (BSAC): the right to govern, make laws, keep a police force, make treaties and develop the territory | Gave the Company power to occupy the land |

### The Rudd Concession and its problems

Lobengula's people were told that only a small number of men would come to dig, and that they would obey the king's laws. These **verbal promises** were not written into the document. When Lobengula learned what the document actually said, he **repudiated** it: he announced that he had been deceived, and he sent two izinduna to London to protest to Queen Victoria. Nevertheless, the British government used the concession as the basis for granting the Royal Charter.

:::example{title="Reading a treaty clause as a source"}
A paraphrased clause: "The grantees shall have complete and exclusive charge over all metals and minerals in the king's territories, with full power to do all things they deem necessary to win and procure the same."

1. **What it gives:** all minerals and the power to do "all things necessary".
2. **What it leaves open:** how many men may come, where they may live, and whether they may occupy land.
3. **Why it mattered:** the open wording allowed the Company to send an armed column and occupy territory.
4. **Lesson:** written terms and spoken promises did not match, which explains Lobengula's protest.
:::

## The Pioneer Column and occupation

The BSAC organised the **Pioneer Column**: around two hundred pioneers, supported by several hundred Company police, led by Company officers and guided by the hunter **Frederick Selous**. It set out from Bechuanaland in mid-1890 and avoided Bulawayo, passing to the east through Mashonaland to avoid direct conflict with the Ndebele army. Along the way it built forts, including **Fort Tuli, Fort Victoria and Fort Charter**. On **12 September 1890** it reached the site it named **Fort Salisbury** (now Harare) and raised the British flag the following day. The pioneers were then disbanded and each was promised land and gold claims.

## African responses

| Response | Examples |
|---|---|
| Diplomacy | Lobengula played rivals against each other, repudiated the Rudd Concession and sent izinduna to London |
| Restraint | Lobengula held back his regiments when the column passed, to avoid giving the Company an excuse for war |
| Accommodation | Some Shona leaders traded with the newcomers or allowed them to pass, at first seeing them as temporary visitors or possible allies |
| Resistance | Communities resisted Company demands for labour, tax and land; full-scale war followed in 1893 and the risings in 1896-97 |

:::example{title="Why Lobengula held back his army"}
In 1890 many young Ndebele soldiers wanted to attack the Pioneer Column.

1. **The king's view:** an attack would give the Company and Britain a reason for war.
2. **His strategy:** use diplomacy and protest instead, hoping Britain would restrain the Company.
3. **Outcome:** the column reached Fort Salisbury without a battle, but the Company was now established.
4. **Assessment:** historians debate whether this restraint was wise; it avoided war in 1890 but did not prevent it in 1893.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the Berlin Conference divided Zimbabwe"}
The Berlin Conference set rules for claiming territory. Zimbabwe was colonised through the treaties, the Charter and the occupation that followed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the Moffat Treaty and the Rudd Concession"}
The **Moffat Treaty** kept out other foreign powers; the **Rudd Concession** gave mineral rights to Rhodes's agents.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong dates"}
Rudd Concession: 30 October 1888. Royal Charter: 29 October 1889. Fort Salisbury: 12 September 1890.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Colonisation had economic, political, strategic and social causes; Europeans used "civilising" ideas to justify it.
- The Berlin Conference (1884-85) set rules such as notification and effective occupation; no Africans attended.
- The Grobler Treaty (1887), Moffat Treaty (February 1888) and Rudd Concession (30 October 1888) led to the Royal Charter (29 October 1889).
- Lobengula repudiated the Rudd Concession, saying he had been deceived, and sent izinduna to London.
- The Pioneer Column reached Fort Salisbury on 12 September 1890; Africans responded with diplomacy, restraint, accommodation and resistance.
:::
