## Introduction

Every December in a fictional suburb of Bulawayo, a group of young people spends a Saturday painting the walls of the local clinic. Nobody pays them. The paint comes from a hardware shop that donated it, the lunch from a church, and the ladders from a neighbour. By evening the clinic looks new, and the nurses say patients are more cheerful.

This is a **voluntary community activity**: work done freely, without payment, for the good of others. Zimbabwean communities have a long tradition of it, from the work party (*nhimbe/ilima*) and the chief's field (*zunde ramambo*) to today's clean-up campaigns and youth clubs. In this lesson you learn what voluntary activities are, examples of them, the community needs they meet in rural and urban areas, and why they matter, both to the community and to the volunteer. You should already know about civic responsibilities and Unhu.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Volunteer"}
A person who freely offers time, skills or labour for the benefit of others without expecting payment.
:::

:::definition{term="Voluntary community activity"}
Work done freely and without payment by members of a community to meet a shared need or to help people in need.
:::

:::definition{term="Community need"}
Something a community lacks or a problem it faces that affects many people, such as unsafe water, litter, a damaged road or neglected elderly people.
:::

:::definition{term="Intervention"}
Action taken to improve a situation or solve a problem.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Roots in heritage"}
Communal labour has deep roots in Zimbabwe: the work party (*nhimbe* in Shona, *ilima* in Ndebele) brought neighbours together to hoe, harvest or build, and the chief's field (*zunde ramambo*) produced food for orphans, widows and the needy. Voluntary activity today continues this tradition of Unhu.
:::

## Examples of voluntary community activities

| Activity | What volunteers do | Who benefits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean-up campaigns | Collect litter, clear drains, sweep markets and bus stops | Whole community: less disease, cleaner environment |
| Helping the elderly and the sick | Fetch water and firewood, collect medicine, cook, visit and keep company (home-based care) | Elderly people, the chronically ill and their families |
| Tree planting | Plant and water trees at schools, clinics and on bare land | Environment, future generations |
| Community gardens | Grow vegetables on shared land for poor households or a school feeding scheme | Vulnerable households, pupils |
| School projects | Mould bricks, build classrooms, repair desks, paint walls | Pupils and teachers |
| Disaster relief | Collect and distribute food, clothes and blankets after floods or storms | Affected families |
| Repairing infrastructure | Fill potholes, fix boreholes, repair footbridges | Whole community |
| Mentoring and tutoring | Older pupils help younger ones with reading or maths | Learners |

Volunteers may act alone, in informal groups or through organised bodies such as churches, youth clubs, school clubs, service clubs, the Red Cross and other non-governmental organisations.

## Community needs: rural and urban

Needs differ between rural and urban areas, so voluntary activities differ too.

| Rural areas | Urban areas |
| --- | --- |
| Long distances to water; broken boreholes | Burst pipes, water shortages, sewage spills |
| Soil erosion, gullies and deforestation | Litter, illegal dumping and blocked drains |
| Poor roads and footbridges, especially in the rainy season | Potholes, unsafe crossings, poor street lighting |
| Distant clinics; elderly people caring for orphans | Overcrowding; elderly people living alone |
| Classrooms short of desks and buildings | Crowded schools; youth unemployment and drug abuse |
| Drought and food shortages | Crime and insecurity; flooding of low-lying settlements |

:::example{title="Matching a project to a need in Lupane"}
Farmers in a fictional village near Lupane notice that a gully is spreading across the road to the school.

1. **Need identified:** soil erosion is cutting off the road.
2. **Voluntary activity chosen:** a work party fills the gully with stones and branches and plants grass and trees along its edges.
3. **Support:** the village head organises the day; an agricultural extension officer gives advice; families bring food.
4. **Result:** the road stays open through the rainy season and the soil is saved.

Good voluntary projects start with a real need and use local knowledge and resources.
:::

:::example{title="A youth club in Harare"}
Young people in a high-density suburb are worried about uncollected rubbish and rising drug use among their peers.

1. They organise a monthly clean-up with the residents' association and the council, which provides a truck.
2. They start a football tournament on clean-up days, giving young people something positive to do.
3. A local business donates refuse bags and prizes.

The club meets two urban needs, cleanliness and youth activity, with one voluntary programme.
:::

## The importance of volunteering

### For the community

- **Meets needs quickly** when government or councils lack funds or staff.
- **Saves money**, since labour is given free.
- **Builds unity and Unhu**: people of different backgrounds work side by side.
- **Supports vulnerable people**, who might otherwise be neglected.
- **Protects the environment and public property**.
- **Encourages self-reliance** instead of waiting for outside help.

### For the volunteer

- **New skills and experience**, such as building, gardening, first aid, leadership and teamwork, which help in finding work.
- **Personal satisfaction** and confidence from helping others.
- **Friendships and networks** in the community.
- **Respect and recognition**, and a good record for references.
- **Good citizenship**: a volunteer learns responsibility and develops empathy.

## Planning a voluntary project

Successful projects usually follow a few simple steps:

1. **Identify the need** by talking to residents, the village head or councillor, the clinic or the school, and by observing the problem.
2. **Plan** what will be done, by whom, when and with what materials, and agree the plan at a community meeting.
3. **Mobilise resources**: volunteers' labour, local materials such as stones and poles, and donations from businesses, churches or the council.
4. **Carry out the work** safely, with tasks shared fairly between young and old, women and men.
5. **Review and sustain** the project: report results, keep simple records, thank volunteers and agree who will maintain what has been built.

## Challenges

Volunteering faces challenges: people may lack time because they must earn a living; materials, tools and transport cost money; volunteers can lose interest; and some projects fail because they were not planned around a real need. Clear planning, local leadership, simple record-keeping and recognition of volunteers help to overcome these.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing voluntary work with paid work"}
If a person is paid wages for the job, it is not voluntary. Volunteers may receive food or transport, but not a wage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the volunteer's benefits"}
Questions often ask for the importance to *the volunteer* as well as to *the community*. Give both when asked.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the same needs for rural and urban areas"}
Show the difference: rural areas may need boreholes and gully control; urban areas may need drain-clearing and youth activities.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Define** (Paper 2 Section A): work done freely, without payment, for the benefit of others.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Identify** or **list** examples or needs: one activity or need per point, with rural or urban examples if the question asks.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** the importance: give the benefit and its effect. **Suggest** a project: name the need, the activity and who would help.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Voluntary community activities are done freely, without payment, to meet community needs.
- Examples include clean-ups, helping the elderly and sick, tree planting, community gardens, school projects and disaster relief.
- Rural needs (boreholes, erosion, roads, distant clinics) differ from urban needs (litter, drains, crime, youth unemployment).
- Volunteering meets needs, saves money, builds unity and supports the vulnerable; volunteers gain skills, satisfaction, networks and respect.
- It continues the tradition of *nhimbe/ilima* and *zunde ramambo* and the value of Unhu.
:::
