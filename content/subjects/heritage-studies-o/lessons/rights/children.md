## Introduction

At a school assembly in Gweru, the head teacher announces a new suggestion box. "If something worries you about safety, bullying or your lessons," she says, "write it down. We will read every note." A Form 1 pupil whispers to her friend, "Why would they listen to us?" The answer is that children have the **right to be heard**, and good schools and families take it seriously.

You are a child in law until you turn 18, and the Constitution of Zimbabwe gives you rights that adults and the state must respect. Those rights come with **responsibilities**: to respect others, to learn, to help at home and to keep school rules. This lesson explains both, at home and at school, and the difference between them. You will need these ideas again when you study vulnerable groups, child labour and citizenship.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Child"}
Under the Constitution of Zimbabwe, every person under the age of 18 years.
:::

:::definition{term="Right"}
Something every person is entitled to, protected by law, which others and the state must respect. Example: the right to education.
:::

:::definition{term="Responsibility (duty)"}
Something a person is expected to do for others or for the community. Example: the responsibility to respect other pupils and obey school rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Best interests of the child"}
The principle that in every matter concerning a child, what is best for the child must come first.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Where children's rights are written"}
- **Section 81** of the Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013), in the Declaration of Rights (Chapter 4), sets out the rights of children.
- The **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child** (1989) is the main international treaty on children's rights.
- The **African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child** (1990) sets out children's rights for Africa and, unusually, also lists the responsibilities of children.
:::

## Children's rights in the Constitution

Section 81 says, in summary, that every child has the right:

- to equal treatment before the law and to be heard;
- to a name and a family name, and to the prompt provision of a birth certificate;
- to family or parental care, or to appropriate care when removed from the family;
- to be protected from economic and sexual exploitation, from child labour and from maltreatment, neglect or any form of abuse;
- to education, health care services, nutrition and shelter;
- not to be recruited into a militia force or made to take part in armed conflict, and not to be compelled to take part in political campaigns;
- not to be detained except as a last resort, and then for the shortest time and apart from adults.

It also says that a child's **best interests are paramount** in every matter concerning the child. Other sections add to this: section 75, for example, gives every citizen the right to a basic education.

## Rights at home and at school

| Setting | Rights (examples) | Who must uphold them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home | Shelter, food and clothing; care and love; security and protection from abuse; a name and birth registration; health care; time to play and rest | Parents, guardians and the extended family; the state when families cannot |
| School | Education; a safe environment free from bullying and abuse; information; to be heard (for example through a suggestion box or school council); equal treatment | Teachers, head teachers, school development committees, the Ministry of Primary and Secondary Education |

## Children's responsibilities

| Setting | Responsibilities (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Home | Respect parents, elders and siblings; help with household chores suitable for their age; look after younger siblings; care for family property; keep themselves safe; take part in family life |
| School | Attend school and be punctual; work hard and do homework; obey school rules; respect teachers and other pupils; care for school property; help keep the school clean; avoid bullying |
| Community | Respect elders and neighbours; take part in community activities; protect the environment |

Responsibilities are not payment for rights. A child does not "earn" the right to food by doing chores. But a society works well when everyone both enjoys rights and carries out responsibilities.

## Rights and responsibilities: the difference

| A right | A responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Something you are entitled to | Something you are expected to do |
| Others and the state must respect it | You must carry it out for others |
| Example: the right to education | Example: attending school and working hard |
| Example: the right to be safe from abuse | Example: not bullying others |

Many rights carry a matching responsibility. Your right to education is matched by your responsibility to learn and to let others learn. Your right to be heard is matched by your responsibility to listen to others respectfully.

:::example{title="Rudo's family: which right is being denied?"}
Rudo, aged 12, lives with her aunt in Kariba. Her aunt keeps her at home to sell vegetables at the market every school day, and Rudo has not been to school for a term.

1. **Rights denied:** the right to education, and protection from child labour and economic exploitation (section 81).
2. **Rudo's responsibility, where it fits:** helping at home with tasks suitable for her age is reasonable; missing school to work every day is not.
3. **What can be done:** a teacher or village head can speak to the aunt; if that fails, the matter can be reported to the Department of Social Welfare or the police. Programmes such as the Basic Education Assistance Module (BEAM) may help with fees.

The example shows how to apply section 81 to a real situation and distinguish fair household help from exploitation.
:::

:::example{title="A school council at work"}
Pupils at a fictional school in Lupane complain that the toilets are dirty and unsafe.

1. Class representatives raise the issue at the school council: the **right to be heard**.
2. The head teacher and school development committee arrange repairs: the school upholds the **right to a safe environment**.
3. The pupils agree a cleaning rota and promise to use the toilets properly: they carry out their **responsibility** to care for school property.

Rights and responsibilities work together to solve the problem.
:::

## Why children's rights and responsibilities matter

- Children are vulnerable: they depend on adults and cannot always protect themselves.
- Rights protect children's growth, health and education, which shape their whole future.
- Responsibilities prepare children to be good citizens who respect others.
- When both are respected, homes and schools are peaceful, safe and productive.

If a child's rights are abused, help is available: a trusted adult, a teacher, a religious leader, the village head, a clinic, the Department of Social Welfare or the police Victim Friendly Unit.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing rights and responsibilities"}
"Doing homework" is a responsibility, not a right. "Education" is a right. Check which the question asks for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying children have rights only if they behave well"}
Rights belong to every child. Responsibilities are expected, but rights are not removed as a punishment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all children's work as child labour"}
Light chores suitable for a child's age are part of family life. Work that harms a child's health or keeps them out of school is child labour, which is studied separately.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**State** or **list** (Paper 2 Section A): one right or one responsibility per point, clearly labelled, e.g. "the right to education".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** the importance of a right: say what it protects and how it helps the child, e.g. "the right to health care means a sick child is treated, so the child survives and can learn".
:::

:::exam-tip
In a scenario question (Paper 1 or Paper 2), first name the right being denied, then the section (section 81) if you are sure, then suggest who can help.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A child is any person under 18; section 81 of the Constitution sets out children's rights.
- Rights include equal treatment, being heard, a name and birth registration, family care, education, health care, nutrition, shelter and protection from abuse, exploitation and child labour.
- The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) and the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child (1990) also protect children.
- Children's responsibilities include respecting others, helping at home, working hard and obeying school rules.
- A right is an entitlement; a responsibility is a duty; many rights carry a matching responsibility.
:::
