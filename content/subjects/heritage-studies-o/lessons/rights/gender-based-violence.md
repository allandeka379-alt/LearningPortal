## Introduction

Nokuthula notices that her friend, a quiet girl in her class, has started missing school and flinches when anyone raises their voice. One day her friend admits that things are very difficult at home. Nokuthula does not know what to say, but she knows that her friend should not have to face this alone.

**Gender-based violence (GBV)** is harm done to a person because of their sex or gender, or because of unequal power between men and women. Most survivors are women and girls, but men and boys can also be affected. It happens in homes, schools, workplaces and online, and it is against the law. This lesson explains the types, causes and effects of GBV, the main law that deals with domestic violence, where survivors can get help, and the work of organisations that advocate for gender equality. It describes these matters factually, without graphic detail. You should already know the meaning of gender equality.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gender-based violence (GBV)"}
Any act that causes physical, sexual, emotional or economic harm to a person because of their sex or gender, or that arises from unequal power between men and women.
:::

:::definition{term="Domestic violence"}
Abuse by a person against someone they live with or are related to, such as a spouse, partner, child, parent or other family member.
:::

:::definition{term="Survivor"}
A person who has experienced violence. Many organisations prefer this word to "victim" because it recognises the person's strength.
:::

:::definition{term="Advocacy"}
Speaking up and campaigning publicly to change laws, policies, attitudes or practices.
:::

:::definition{term="Pressure group"}
An organisation that tries to influence government and society on particular issues without seeking political office itself.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The Domestic Violence Act (2007)"}
- Defines domestic violence widely, covering physical, sexual, emotional, verbal, psychological and economic abuse, intimidation and harassment, among others.
- Lets a person who is being abused apply to a magistrates court for a **protection order**, which orders the abuser to stop and may keep the abuser away.
- Makes domestic violence a criminal offence that can be reported to the police.
- Set up the **Anti-Domestic Violence Council** to promote awareness and monitor the Act.
:::

## Types of gender-based violence

| Type | What it involves (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Hitting, kicking, burning or any use of force that injures a person |
| Sexual | Any sexual act without consent, sexual harassment, forcing a child into marriage |
| Emotional or psychological | Insults, threats, humiliation, isolating a person from friends and family, controlling behaviour |
| Economic | Taking a person's earnings, refusing money for food or school fees as punishment, preventing a person from working, property grabbing |

Some harmful practices are also forms of GBV, such as forced and child marriage and pledging girls (*kuzvarira*). These are illegal.

## Causes of gender-based violence

- **Unequal power** between men and women, and beliefs that a husband may "discipline" his wife.
- **Cultural attitudes and stereotypes** that excuse violence or tell survivors to stay silent to protect the family's name.
- **Alcohol and drug abuse**, which reduce self-control.
- **Poverty and economic dependence**, which make it hard for survivors to leave or report.
- **Stress, unemployment and jealousy** within relationships.
- **Lack of awareness** of rights and the law, and fear of the police or courts.
- **Learned behaviour**: children who grow up with violence may repeat it.

## Effects of gender-based violence

| On the survivor | On the family | On the community and nation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Injuries, disability and in some cases death | Children are frightened, harmed or learn violent behaviour | Health services and police must deal with cases |
| Fear, depression, loss of confidence | Family breakdown, divorce or separation | Lost work and productivity |
| Risk of sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, and unplanned pregnancy | Loss of income and property | Weakened Unhu and trust |
| Dropping out of school or losing a job | Children may drop out of school | Slower progress towards gender equality |

## Where survivors can get help

- **A trusted adult**: a parent, aunt or uncle, teacher, school counsellor or religious leader.
- **The police Victim Friendly Unit**, which deals with cases involving women and children in a private and sensitive way.
- **Clinics and hospitals**, for medical care and to record injuries.
- **The magistrates court**, for a protection order under the Domestic Violence Act.
- **The Department of Social Welfare**, especially where children are involved.
- **Organisations** that offer counselling, shelter and legal advice.

:::example{title="Applying the law"}
Tafadzwa's mother in Mutare is regularly threatened and has her wages taken by her partner.

1. **Types of GBV:** emotional abuse (threats) and economic abuse (taking her wages).
2. **The law:** both are forms of domestic violence under the Domestic Violence Act (2007).
3. **Help:** she can report to the police Victim Friendly Unit and apply to the magistrates court for a protection order; a counselling organisation can support her and the children.

The example shows that GBV is not only physical: threats and economic control are also abuse.
:::

## Pressure groups and advocates for gender equality

| Organisation | Main work (in outline) |
| --- | --- |
| Msasa Project | Supports survivors of gender-based violence with counselling, shelter and legal information; campaigns against GBV |
| Padare/Enkundleni Men's Forum on Gender | Works with men and boys to challenge harmful ideas of masculinity and to support gender equality and the prevention of GBV |
| Zimbabwe Women Lawyers Association | Provides legal advice and representation, especially to women, and campaigns for law reform |

Other advocates include churches, community leaders, traditional leaders, schools, the media and the Zimbabwe Gender Commission. Campaigns such as the annual **16 Days of Activism against Gender-Based Violence** (25 November to 10 December) raise awareness worldwide, including in Zimbabwe.

:::example{title="An advocacy campaign in Bindura"}
A fictional youth group plans a campaign against GBV.

1. It invites a counsellor and a police officer from the Victim Friendly Unit to speak at a community meeting.
2. It performs a short play showing how emotional and economic abuse damage a family.
3. Men from the community lead a discussion on respect in relationships, following the approach of men's forums.
4. Leaflets list where to get help, in Shona, Ndebele and English.

The campaign uses several advocacy methods: education, drama, engaging men and providing information.
:::

## Strengths and weaknesses of advocacy

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- |
| Raises awareness of rights and of where to get help | Many organisations depend on donor funding, which may be short-term |
| Has contributed to law reform, such as the Domestic Violence Act | Reaches towns more easily than remote rural areas |
| Supports survivors with counselling, shelter and legal aid | Cultural attitudes change slowly; some people resist the message |
| Involves men and boys, which helps change attitudes | Survivors may still fear reporting because of stigma or economic dependence |
| Gives a voice to people who might not be heard | Laws and protection orders are not always enforced |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking GBV is only physical"}
Emotional, sexual and economic abuse are also GBV and are covered by the Domestic Violence Act.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying GBV affects only women"}
Most survivors are women and girls, but men and boys can also experience GBV. Use accurate, balanced language.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a protection order with a criminal conviction"}
A protection order from a magistrates court orders the abuser to stop and stay away. Criminal charges are a separate step that can also be taken.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Gender-based violence is physical, sexual, emotional or economic harm linked to sex, gender or unequal power.
- Causes include unequal power, cultural attitudes, alcohol and drugs, poverty, stress and lack of awareness; effects reach survivors, families and the nation.
- The Domestic Violence Act (2007) defines domestic violence widely, allows protection orders and set up the Anti-Domestic Violence Council.
- Help comes from trusted adults, the police Victim Friendly Unit, clinics, courts, social welfare and organisations.
- Advocates such as Msasa Project, Padare/Enkundleni Men's Forum on Gender and the Zimbabwe Women Lawyers Association raise awareness and support survivors, but face funding, reach and attitude challenges.
:::
