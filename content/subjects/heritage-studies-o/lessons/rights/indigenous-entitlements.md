## Introduction

When Rudo's eldest sister married, her family received roora. Among the cattle was one set aside, not for Rudo's father, but for her mother: the **mother's cow**. Years later, that cow and its calves still belonged to her mother, and nobody, not even her father, could sell them without her agreement. Rudo's grandmother also had her own small field where she grew groundnuts and round nuts, and what she harvested was hers to use, sell or give away.

People sometimes say that women in indigenous society owned nothing. That is not accurate. Men and women each had **entitlements**: things they could claim as theirs by custom. They were not equal, but they were real and respected. In this lesson you learn the main entitlements of women and men, why they mattered, and how they compare with the rights set out in the Constitution today. You should already know the roles of men and women in the family.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Entitlement"}
Something a person has a recognised claim to, by custom or by law, because of their position in society, for example as a wife, mother or head of household.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous entitlement"}
An entitlement recognised by the customs of Zimbabwe's communities before colonial and modern law.
:::

:::definition{term="Right"}
An entitlement protected by law, which the state must respect and protect, such as the rights in the Declaration of Rights (Chapter 4 of the Constitution).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Terms to learn"}
- The mother's cow: *mombe yeumai* (Shona), *inkomo yohlanga* (Ndebele).
- *Tseu*: a wife's own field, whose produce she controls (Shona).
- *Mavoko* property: in Shona customary practice, property a woman earns through her own skills, such as pottery or midwifery.
:::

## Entitlements of women

| Entitlement | What it was | Why it mattered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The mother's cow (*mombe yeumai / inkomo yohlanga*) | A beast from a daughter's bride wealth, given to the mother; it and its offspring were hers | Honoured her for raising the daughter; gave her property and security of her own |
| Her own field (*tseu*) | A piece of land given to a wife, where she grew crops such as groundnuts, round nuts and pumpkins | Gave her food and income she controlled; allowed her to support her children and help relatives |
| The kitchen | The wife's own hut and domain, where she cooked, stored food and received women visitors | Gave her authority, privacy and a respected space in the homestead |
| Property from her skills (*mavoko*) | Goods or payments earned through her own work, such as pottery, basketry or midwifery | Let her own and keep the fruits of her own labour |
| Respect and protection | As a mother and wife, she was entitled to respect from children and in-laws, and to protection by her family and her husband's | Gave her dignity and security; her own family could intervene if she was mistreated |

The mother's cow had special status. In many communities it could not be taken to pay the husband's debts or used without the mother's agreement, and it was not simply part of the husband's estate. Some families believed that mistreating the mother's cow could bring misfortune, which helped to protect it.

## Entitlements of men

| Entitlement | What it was | Why it mattered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority | Head of the household; spoke for the family at the court (*dare/inkundla*) | Gave leadership and order; he answered for the family |
| Land | Allocated land by the chief or village head for homestead, fields and grazing | Allowed him to feed and house his family |
| Cattle and inheritance | Owned most of the family's cattle; an heir took over his responsibilities when he died | Wealth, status, bride wealth for sons; continuity of the family |
| Tools and weapons | Axe, spear, hoe and other tools for building, farming, hunting and defence | Allowed him to provide for and protect the family |
| Identity of the family | Children took his clan name and totem | Gave children a clear identity and place in the clan |

These entitlements came with **duties**. A man's authority meant he had to provide land, food and shelter, protect the family and settle disputes fairly. A woman's entitlements rested on her work in the fields and home and her care of children.

:::example{title="Whose cow is it?"}
Tatenda's father wants to sell a heifer to pay for a new plough. His wife points out that the heifer is a calf of her *mombe yeumai*.

1. **Indigenous entitlement:** the mother's cow and its offspring belong to her.
2. **What custom expects:** he must ask her; she may agree, refuse or ask for something in return.
3. **Outcome:** after discussion, she agrees to sell a different animal of her own and keeps the heifer for their daughter's future needs.

The case shows that a wife's entitlements gave her real property and a voice in family decisions.
:::

### Entitlements and duties together

Indigenous society saw entitlements and duties as two sides of one relationship. A husband who received land from the chief was expected to farm it and feed his household; if he neglected his family, elders at the *dare/inkundla* could rebuke him. A wife who controlled her *tseu* used its produce to feed her children and to help her own relatives, which strengthened ties between the two families. Children, in turn, were expected to respect and help both parents, and an heir who took over a dead man's position also took on his duty to care for the widow and children. Understanding this link helps you explain why entitlements mattered and why the community enforced them.

## The importance of indigenous entitlements

- **Security**: women had property and food of their own, which protected them and their children in hard times.
- **Dignity and respect**: entitlements recognised the contribution of each spouse.
- **Order and stability**: clear claims reduced quarrels over property within families.
- **Continuity**: land, cattle and heirship passed on the family's wealth and identity.
- **Mutual dependence**: husband and wife each had something the household needed, so cooperation was necessary.

## Comparing indigenous entitlements with rights today

| Area | Indigenous society | Today, under the Constitution and the law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property | Women owned specific items (mother's cow, *tseu* produce, *mavoko*); most cattle and land under men's control | Women and men have equal rights to own property, including land, in their own names |
| Decision-making | The husband had final authority | Spouses have equal rights and obligations (section 26) |
| Legal status | Many women were treated as minors under customary law | Everyone is a legal adult at 18 (Legal Age of Majority Act, 1982) |
| Land | Allocated to men by the chief | Equal access to land for women and men is a national objective (section 17, gender balance) |
| Inheritance | Mainly through male heirs | Spouses and children of both sexes inherit; estates are supervised by the Master of the High Court |

:::example{title="Then and now"}
Nyasha, a teacher in Marondera, buys a residential stand and registers it in her own name.

- In indigenous society she would have had her *tseu* and kitchen but would not have owned land in her own name.
- Today the law recognises her as an adult who can own land and property and sign contracts herself.

Some indigenous entitlements, such as the mother's cow, are still honoured by many families alongside these legal rights.
:::

Views differ on how to judge the indigenous system. Some argue that it gave women more security and respect than is often recognised; others point out that women depended on men for land and cattle and had little say in major decisions. A good answer presents both views.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying women had no entitlements"}
Women had clear entitlements: the mother's cow, the *tseu*, the kitchen and property from their own skills. Name them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing entitlements with rights"}
An indigenous entitlement was recognised by custom; a right in the Constitution is protected by law and can be enforced in court.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the duties"}
Entitlements came with responsibilities. Men's authority required them to provide and protect; women's entitlements came with work in the fields and home.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Women's indigenous entitlements included the mother's cow (*mombe yeumai / inkomo yohlanga*), her own field (*tseu*), the kitchen and property from her own skills.
- Men's entitlements included authority as household head, land, cattle, tools, inheritance and giving the family its name and totem.
- Entitlements gave security, dignity, order and continuity, and came with duties.
- Today the Constitution and the law give men and women equal rights to property, land and decisions.
- Many families still honour indigenous entitlements such as the mother's cow alongside legal rights.
:::
