## Introduction

Think of a woman in Mutare who bakes and sells bread. She needs a place to work and flour, water and firewood (**land** and natural resources). She needs her own effort and perhaps a helper (**labour**). She needs an oven, tins and some money to buy ingredients (**capital**). And she needs the idea, the planning and the courage to start and run the business (**entrepreneurship**). Without any one of these, there is no bread to sell.

Economists call these four things the **factors of production**. Every good or service in Zimbabwe, from a bag of maize in Gokwe to a hotel room at Victoria Falls, is produced by combining them. In this lesson, which joins Form 1 and Form 4 work, you learn what each factor is, what its owner earns, why it matters, and how people in Zimbabwe gain access to it. The things that hold production back (power cuts, drought, skills shortages and others) are studied in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Production"}
The creation of goods and services to satisfy people's needs and wants.
:::

:::definition{term="Factors of production"}
The resources used to produce goods and services: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship.
:::

:::definition{term="Land"}
All natural resources used in production: the soil, minerals, water, forests, wildlife and the space on which activities take place.
:::

:::definition{term="Labour"}
Human effort, physical or mental, used in production.
:::

:::definition{term="Capital"}
Man-made resources used to produce other goods and services, such as tools, machines, buildings and vehicles, and the money used to buy them.
:::

:::definition{term="Entrepreneurship"}
The ability to bring the other factors together, organise production and take the risk of running a business.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Factors and their rewards"}
Land earns **rent**. Labour earns **wages** (or salaries). Capital earns **interest**. Entrepreneurship earns **profit**.
:::

## The four factors and their rewards

| Factor | Examples in Zimbabwe | Reward | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land | Farmland in Mashonaland, gold and lithium deposits, Lake Kariba, Hwange coal, forests | Rent | Supplies the raw materials and the space for every activity |
| Labour | Farm workers, miners, teachers, nurses, artisans, vendors | Wages and salaries | Nothing is produced without people's effort and skills |
| Capital | Tractors, irrigation pipes, sewing machines, grinding mills, delivery trucks | Interest | Makes labour more productive and allows larger output |
| Entrepreneurship | A young person starting a poultry project; a company opening a factory | Profit | Combines the other factors and takes the risk |

Labour is often described by its **quality** as well as its quantity. A skilled welder or a trained nurse produces more, or better, than someone without training. That is why education and training are called investment in **human capital**.

Capital can be **fixed** (machines and buildings used again and again) or **working** (raw materials and money used up in one cycle of production). A tailor's sewing machine is fixed capital; the cloth and thread are working capital.

:::example{title="Combining the factors: Tendai's chicken project"}
Tendai, a school leaver in Lupane, starts a broiler chicken project.

1. **Land:** a plot behind the family homestead and water from the borehole.
2. **Labour:** Tendai and his younger sister feed and water the birds.
3. **Capital:** a fowl run built from poles and wire, feeders, drinkers and US\$150 saved from piece jobs to buy chicks and feed.
4. **Entrepreneurship:** Tendai plans when to buy chicks so that the birds are ready before the school holidays, finds buyers and takes the risk that some birds may die.
5. **Rewards:** what is left after paying for feed and chicks is his profit; if he had borrowed the money, he would have had to pay interest.
:::

## Access to the factors of production in Zimbabwe

Having the factors is one thing; being able to **obtain** them is another. Access is uneven: some people own land, machines and savings, while many young people, women and rural families have little.

| Factor | How people gain access | Common difficulties |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Communal land allocated through traditional leaders and rural district councils; resettlement land (A1 and A2); buying or leasing urban stands; inheritance | Shortage of land near towns; lack of title deeds; disputes; some women denied inheritance in the past |
| Labour and skills | Schools, vocational training centres, polytechnics, universities, apprenticeships, learning from parents and elders | Cost of fees; skills that do not match jobs; many skilled people leaving the country |
| Capital | Savings, family support, savings clubs (rounds), bank loans, microfinance, government and donor funds for small enterprises | Banks ask for collateral; high interest; small amounts available |
| Entrepreneurship | Experience, mentoring, business training, enterprise clubs at school | Fear of risk; little information on markets |

### Government efforts to widen access

Government has taken several steps to help more people gain access to the factors of production. In outline:

- **Land:** redistribution of land since 1980, especially the fast-track land reform programme from 2000, allocated land to many families (land ownership is studied in its own lesson).
- **Finance:** state-backed bodies such as SMEDCO (the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Corporation) lend to small businesses, and funds and microfinance institutions have been set up to target women and young people.
- **Skills:** vocational training centres, technical colleges and polytechnics train artisans; the education curriculum includes enterprise and practical subjects.
- **Workspaces:** councils and government have built markets and workspaces for small-scale manufacturers and vendors.
- **Inputs:** input support schemes have supplied seed and fertiliser to many small farmers.

Communities also help themselves. Savings clubs, known in many places as **rounds** or mukando, allow members to pool money so each can buy equipment or stock in turn.

:::example{title="Thandeka seeks capital"}
Thandeka, a trained hairdresser in Bulawayo, wants to buy two hair dryers and rent a small salon.

1. **Problem:** a bank asks for collateral, such as a title deed, which she does not have.
2. **Option 1:** join a savings club with five friends; each month one member receives the pooled money.
3. **Option 2:** apply to a microfinance institution or a state-backed fund for small enterprises, with a simple business plan.
4. **Option 3:** start with one dryer in a shared space and reinvest her profits.
5. **Lesson:** access to capital shapes how quickly a small business can grow, and there is usually more than one route.
:::

## Why access to the factors matters

When more people can obtain land, skills, capital and support for enterprise:

- more goods and services are produced, which helps **economic growth**;
- more people are **employed** or self-employed, so household incomes rise;
- **poverty and inequality** fall, because wealth is not held by a few;
- the country relies less on **imports**, saving foreign currency;
- historical **imbalances** from the colonial period are corrected.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling money 'land' or 'labour'"}
Money used to buy machines and materials is part of **capital**. Land means natural resources; labour means human effort.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the rewards"}
Land earns rent, labour earns wages, capital earns interest and entrepreneurship earns profit. Learn them as pairs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing instead of explaining"}
"Land, labour, capital" earns little in an *explain* question. Say why each matters, with an example.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The factors of production are land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship.
- Their rewards are rent, wages, interest and profit.
- Labour quality improves through education and training (human capital).
- Zimbabweans gain access through land allocation, training, savings, loans and support schemes, but access is uneven.
- Wider access raises output, employment and incomes and helps correct historical imbalances.
:::
