## Introduction

At harvest time there is plenty of food. A few months later, in the dry season, fields are bare and fresh vegetables are scarce. For centuries, families in Zimbabwe have solved this problem by **storing** and **preserving** food: drying vegetables in the sun to make mufushwa, smoking fish, keeping grain in a raised granary and sealing it in clay pots. Today, many households also use fridges, freezers, tins and sealed packets bought from shops.

This Form 1 lesson looks at **indigenous** and **contemporary** foods, the methods used to store and preserve them, how to handle food **hygienically**, and why these foods and methods matter for health, food security and heritage.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indigenous food"}
Food that has been grown, gathered or prepared by local communities for generations, such as small grains, wild fruits, edible insects and traditional relishes.
:::

:::definition{term="Contemporary food"}
Food that is commonly eaten today, often processed and sold in shops, such as bread, rice, packaged mealie-meal, tinned foods and soft drinks.
:::

:::definition{term="Food preservation"}
Treating food so that it does not go bad quickly, by stopping or slowing the growth of bacteria, moulds and yeasts.
:::

:::definition{term="Food storage"}
Keeping food in a suitable place and container so that it stays safe and is protected from pests, damp and theft until it is needed.
:::

:::definition{term="Food security"}
A situation in which all people at all times have enough safe and nutritious food.
:::

## Indigenous and contemporary foods

Zimbabwe's communities share many indigenous foods. Names differ between languages; where you know the name in another language, such as Tonga, Venda, Kalanga or Nambya, use it.

| Indigenous food (Shona / Ndebele, or English) | What it is |
|---|---|
| Madora / amacimbi | Mopane worms, the caterpillars of a moth, gathered from mopane trees, cleaned and dried |
| Mufushwa / umfushwa | Leafy vegetables, such as pumpkin or cowpea leaves, boiled briefly and sun-dried |
| Dovi / idobi | Peanut butter made by roasting and grinding groundnuts, used in relishes |
| Small grains: sorghum (mapfunde / amabele), finger millet (rapoko: rukweza or zviyo / uphoko), pearl millet (mhunga) | Drought-tolerant cereals for sadza, porridge and traditional drinks |
| Nyimo / indlubu | Round nuts (Bambara groundnuts) |
| Wild fruits | Mazhanje (wild loquat), baobab fruit, marula, masau and others |
| Honey, game meat, fish, mushrooms | Gathered, hunted or caught in season |

Contemporary foods include refined maize meal, bread, rice, pasta, tinned fish and beans, frozen chicken, dairy products, breakfast cereals, sugar, cooking oil and snacks.

## Indigenous methods of storage and preservation

Indigenous methods use sun, smoke, salt, ash and well-designed containers. They need little or no money and rely on knowledge passed from elders.

| Method | How it works | Foods |
|---|---|---|
| Sun-drying | Removes water, so bacteria and moulds cannot grow | Vegetables (mufushwa), mopane worms, fish, meat strips, fruits, pumpkin |
| Smoking | Dries food and coats it with smoke, which slows decay and keeps insects away | Fish, meat, grain stored above the cooking fire |
| Salting | Salt draws water out of food | Meat, fish |
| Granaries (dura / ingqolobane) | Raised on stones or poles with a sealed roof, keeping grain dry, cool and away from rats and termites | Maize, sorghum, millets, groundnuts |
| Clay pots and gourds | Sealed pots keep out insects and damp | Grain, seeds, dried foods, honey |
| Pits | Grain sealed in dry underground pits, safe from fire and theft | Grain |
| Mixing with ash or certain leaves | Discourages weevils and other storage pests | Grain, beans |

:::example{title="Making mufushwa in Mutare"}
Chipo's grandmother prepares mufushwa from cowpea leaves after the rains.

1. **Harvest:** she picks young, healthy leaves in the morning.
2. **Clean and blanch:** she washes them in clean water and boils them briefly.
3. **Dry:** she spreads them on a clean mat or rack in the sun, covered with a cloth against flies and dust, until they are completely dry.
4. **Store:** she packs them in a clean, dry, sealed container in a cool place.
5. **Use:** in the dry season, she soaks and cooks them with dovi. The family eats green vegetables months after the harvest.
:::

## Contemporary methods of storage and preservation

| Method | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Refrigeration | Cold slows the growth of bacteria | Milk, meat, leftover food, vegetables |
| Freezing | Very low temperatures stop bacteria from multiplying | Meat, fish, chicken, vegetables |
| Canning (tinning) | Food is heated in sealed tins, killing microorganisms and keeping air out | Beans, fish, fruit |
| Packaging | Sealed plastic, paper or vacuum packs keep out air, damp and pests | Mealie-meal, rice, snacks, sealed meat |
| Pasteurisation | Heating to a set temperature kills harmful bacteria | Milk, juice |
| Chemical preservatives and grain protectants | Approved substances slow decay or kill pests; they must be used exactly as labelled | Bread, drinks; grain in storage |
| Modern storage | Metal silos and airtight (hermetic) bags keep grain dry and pest-free | Maize stored by households and the Grain Marketing Board |

## Food hygiene

Stored food is only useful if it is safe. Good hygiene rules include:

- Wash hands with soap and clean water before handling food.
- Use clean, dry containers with tight lids.
- Dry food completely before storing; damp grain and groundnuts can grow moulds, and some moulds produce poisons (aflatoxins) that cause serious illness.
- Keep raw meat away from cooked and ready-to-eat food.
- Keep cold food cold, and do not refreeze food that has thawed.
- Throw away food that smells bad or shows mould, and tins that are swollen, rusted or dented badly.
- Check expiry dates on packaged food.
- Keep stores clean and protected from rats, insects and animals.

:::example{title="Comparing two stores of maize"}
Tendai's family in Bindura stores maize in a traditional granary; his aunt in Harare buys 10 kg bags of mealie-meal.

1. **Granary:** costs little and uses local materials; grain lasts months if dry and protected, but rats, weevils or damp can spoil it.
2. **Packaged mealie-meal:** clean, convenient and labelled with a date, but it costs money and depends on shops and transport.
3. **Food security:** the granary protects the family when prices rise or roads are cut; the bag is easier for a city household without storage space.
4. **Lesson:** each method suits different circumstances, and both need hygiene.
:::

## Why indigenous and contemporary foods matter

**Indigenous foods** matter because they:

- are **nutritious**: small grains, groundnuts, round nuts, mopane worms and leafy vegetables provide energy, protein, vitamins and minerals;
- support **food security**: small grains survive drought better than maize, and dried foods last through the dry season;
- are **cheap and local**, grown or gathered by families;
- preserve **heritage** and identity, as recipes and methods are passed between generations;
- earn **income** when sold, for example dried mopane worms and peanut butter.

**Contemporary foods and methods** matter because they:

- are **convenient** for busy urban households;
- allow food to be **stored longer and transported** across the country and abroad;
- give a **variety** of foods all year;
- support **industry and jobs** in food processing and retail.

However, some processed foods are high in sugar, salt and fat, and too much of them can contribute to illness. A balanced diet combines the best of both.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing storage and preservation"}
Preservation slows decay (drying, smoking, salting, freezing). Storage is where and how food is kept (granary, pot, fridge, silo). Many methods do both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying indigenous foods are only for the poor"}
Indigenous foods are highly nutritious and increasingly valued, and many are sold in supermarkets and restaurants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting hygiene"}
A preservation method only works if food is clean and fully dry, and stored in clean containers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Indigenous foods include madora/amacimbi, mufushwa/umfushwa, dovi/idobi, small grains and wild fruits.
- Indigenous methods include sun-drying, smoking, salting, granaries (dura/ingqolobane), pots and pits.
- Contemporary methods include refrigeration, freezing, canning, packaging and pasteurisation.
- Food must be clean, dry and protected; damp storage can cause dangerous moulds.
- Indigenous foods support nutrition, food security and heritage; contemporary methods add convenience and variety.
:::
