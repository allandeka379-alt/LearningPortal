## Introduction

Long before factories and supermarkets, the people of Zimbabwe produced the goods they needed. Smiths smelted iron to make hoes, axes and spears. Potters shaped clay into pots for cooking, brewing and storing grain. Weavers and basket makers turned grass, palm leaves, reeds and bark into mats, baskets and cloth. Miners dug gold that was traded to the coast and beyond.

Today Zimbabwe has mines, farms, factories, banks, hotels and telecommunications networks. Yet many indigenous skills survive: Binga baskets made by Tonga weavers, clay pots sold at roadside markets, and stone and wood carvings sold to tourists. In this lesson (Forms 1 and 2) you learn what an **industry** is, the **indigenous** and **contemporary** industries of Zimbabwe, and how industries are grouped into **primary, secondary and tertiary** types. How goods are manufactured is the subject of a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Industry"}
A group of businesses or people that produce the same kind of goods or services, such as the mining industry or the tourism industry. More generally, any productive economic activity.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenous industry"}
An industry based on the knowledge and skills developed by local communities before colonial rule, such as iron smelting, pottery, weaving, basketry and traditional mining.
:::

:::definition{term="Contemporary industry"}
A present-day industry, usually using modern technology, machines and large-scale organisation.
:::

:::definition{term="Primary industry"}
Industry that takes natural resources directly from the land or water: farming, mining, fishing and forestry.
:::

:::definition{term="Secondary industry"}
Industry that turns raw materials into finished or partly finished goods: manufacturing, processing and construction.
:::

:::definition{term="Tertiary industry"}
Industry that provides services rather than goods: trade, transport, banking, tourism, education and health.
:::

## Indigenous industries

Indigenous industries were based on local materials, skills passed from elders to the young, and the needs of the community. They also had social and spiritual meaning: some skills belonged to particular families, and certain work was accompanied by rituals and rules.

| Indigenous industry | Materials and methods (in outline) | Products and uses |
|---|---|---|
| Iron smelting and blacksmithing | Iron ore heated with charcoal in clay furnaces, with air pumped by bellows; the iron was then hammered into shape | Hoes, axes, spears, arrowheads, knives; hoes were also used in exchange and bridewealth in some communities |
| Pottery | Clay dug from riverbanks, shaped by hand, dried and fired in open fires; usually women's work | Pots for cooking, carrying water, brewing and storing grain |
| Weaving and basketry | Grass, reeds, palm leaves, sisal and bark fibres woven or plaited | Mats, baskets, winnowing trays, sleeping mats, bark cloth |
| Mining | Gold, copper and iron dug from shallow pits and shafts; gold also panned from rivers | Gold for trade with coastal merchants; copper for ornaments |
| Wood and stone carving | Hand tools used on local woods and soapstone | Stools, headrests, plates, spoons, mortars and pestles; the Zimbabwe Birds of Great Zimbabwe were carved in soapstone |
| Salt making and leatherwork | Salt from salty soils and springs; hides cured and softened | Salt for food and trade; clothing, bags and shields |

:::example{title="The value of an indigenous hoe"}
Mthulisi's grandfather tells the class about the hoe that his own grandfather made.

1. **Materials:** iron ore from a nearby hill and charcoal from local trees.
2. **Skills:** building the furnace, working the bellows and hammering the hot iron, passed from father to son.
3. **Uses:** the hoe was used to cultivate fields, and hoes could also be exchanged for grain or livestock.
4. **What it shows:** indigenous industry supported farming, trade and family life, and it depended on knowledge of local resources.
:::

### What happened to indigenous industries?

During the colonial period many indigenous industries declined. Cheap imported and factory goods (enamel pots, steel hoes, cotton cloth) replaced some local products, people moved to towns and mines for wage work, and the skills were taught less often. Some survive strongly, especially pottery, basketry, carving and small-scale mining, and some have become part of the modern craft and tourism economy.

## Contemporary industries in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe's present-day economy includes:

- **Mining:** gold, platinum, diamonds, coal (Hwange), chrome, lithium and others.
- **Agriculture:** tobacco, maize, cotton, sugar cane, horticulture, cattle, poultry and dairy.
- **Manufacturing:** food processing, beverages, textiles and clothing, fertilisers, cement, metal products, furniture.
- **Construction:** roads, dams, houses and other buildings.
- **Tourism:** Victoria Falls, Great Zimbabwe, Hwange National Park, Lake Kariba and the Eastern Highlands.
- **Services:** banking, insurance, transport, telecommunications, retail, education and health.

## Types of industry

Economists group industries according to what they do with resources.

| Type | What it does | Examples in Zimbabwe |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Extracts natural resources | Gold mining, tobacco farming, kapenta fishing on Lake Kariba, timber from plantations in Manicaland |
| Secondary | Processes raw materials into goods, or builds | Cotton ginning and clothing factories, sugar refining, cement making, furniture making, building houses |
| Tertiary | Provides services | Banks, buses and trucks, hotels and tour operators, shops, schools, clinics |

The three types are **linked** in a chain. A good can pass through all three.

:::example{title="From field to shop: a cotton chain"}
Chipo grows cotton on her smallholding in the Gokwe area.

1. **Primary:** Chipo grows and picks the cotton.
2. **Secondary:** a ginnery removes the seeds, and a textile factory spins and weaves the lint into cloth; a clothing company makes shirts.
3. **Tertiary:** trucks transport the shirts, a bank finances the factory, and a shop in Bulawayo sells them.
4. **Lesson:** each type depends on the others; if the factory closes, Chipo loses a buyer and the shop has fewer local goods to sell.
:::

## Why industry matters

Industries of all kinds are important because they:

- **create employment** and income for workers and families;
- **produce goods and services** that people need, reducing dependence on imports;
- **earn foreign currency** through exports such as minerals and tobacco;
- **pay taxes** that fund schools, clinics and roads;
- **add value** to raw materials, so the country earns more than by exporting them raw;
- **develop skills and technology**;
- in the case of indigenous industries, **preserve heritage**, identity and knowledge, and supply crafts to tourists.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting manufacturing in the primary group"}
Primary industry extracts raw materials. Turning them into goods (processing, manufacturing, construction) is secondary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking indigenous industry has disappeared"}
Many indigenous industries survive and have adapted: pottery, basketry, carving and small-scale mining are all active today.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling services 'not industry'"}
Tourism, banking and transport are tertiary industries; they produce services rather than goods.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An industry is a group of producers making similar goods or services.
- Indigenous industries included iron smelting, pottery, weaving, basketry, mining and carving, using local materials and skills.
- Contemporary industries include mining, agriculture, manufacturing, construction, tourism and services.
- Primary industry extracts, secondary processes and builds, and tertiary provides services; the three are linked.
- Industry creates jobs, earns foreign currency, pays taxes, adds value and preserves heritage.
:::
