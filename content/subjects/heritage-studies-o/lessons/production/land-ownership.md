## Introduction

Land is at the heart of life in Zimbabwe. It feeds families, holds the graves of ancestors, provides grazing for cattle and supports farming, mining, tourism and industry. It was also a central cause of the liberation struggle. Since independence, who owns land and how it is used have been among the most important and most debated questions in the country.

In this lesson, which joins Form 3 (production) and Form 4 (national heritage), you learn the different **types of land tenure**, how land was redistributed after 1980, and the aims, benefits and problems of land reform. On some of these matters people hold different views; your task is to know the facts and present the views fairly. The colonial land laws themselves are studied in detail in the lesson on colonial expropriation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land tenure"}
The system of rules that decides who may own, use or occupy land, for how long and on what conditions.
:::

:::definition{term="Freehold"}
Private ownership of land, proved by a **title deed**; the owner may sell it, pass it on to heirs or use it as security for a loan.
:::

:::definition{term="Leasehold"}
The right to use land for a fixed period under a lease from the owner, often the State, in return for rent and conditions of use.
:::

:::definition{term="Communal land"}
Land held for communities, on which families are allocated plots to live and farm by traditional leaders and the rural district council; it cannot be sold by individuals.
:::

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
Changes to land ownership and use, usually by redistributing land to people who had little or none.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
Lancaster House (1979): land could be bought only on a **willing-buyer willing-seller** basis for **ten years**. Fast-track land reform began in **2000**. **Chapter 16** of the Constitution deals with agricultural land. Resettlement models: **A1** (smallholder) and **A2** (commercial).
:::

## Types of land tenure

| Tenure | Who holds the rights | Main features | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communal | Communities; allocated by traditional leaders and the RDC | Family plots for homes and fields; shared grazing; cannot be sold | Communal areas in most districts |
| Freehold | Private owners with title deeds | Can be sold, inherited or used as security for loans | Urban residential stands; some farms |
| Leasehold | Users holding a lease, usually from the State | Use for a fixed period; must meet conditions | Many A2 farms on long leases |
| State land | Government | Used for public purposes | National parks, forests, research stations |
| Resettlement land | State land allocated to families and farmers | Held under permits, offer letters or leases | A1 and A2 farms since 2000 |

## The legacy of colonial land laws (outline)

Under colonial rule, the Land Apportionment Act (1930) and the Land Tenure Act (1969) kept most of the best, wettest land for a small settler minority, while Africans were crowded onto poorer communal land. At independence in 1980, a few thousand white commercial farmers held much of the best agricultural land, while most African families lived in crowded communal areas. Correcting this imbalance was a central aim of the new government.

## Land redistribution since 1980

| Period | Measures | Outcomes (outline) |
|---|---|---|
| 1980-1990 | **Willing-buyer willing-seller**, as required by the Lancaster House constitution; government bought farms offered for sale, partly with British funding, and resettled families | Tens of thousands of families resettled, but land was expensive and much of it of lower quality; progress was slower than planned |
| 1990s | The Land Acquisition Act (1992) allowed compulsory acquisition with compensation; farms were designated for acquisition in 1997 | Slow progress, legal challenges and disagreement over funding |
| From 2000 | After a draft constitution was rejected in the referendum of February 2000, farm occupations began; the **fast-track land reform programme** acquired most large commercial farms and allocated them under the **A1** (smallholder) and **A2** (commercial) models | A large transfer of land to many thousands of families in a short time; great disruption to commercial agriculture |
| 2013 Constitution | **Chapter 16**: agricultural land acquired for resettlement is vested in the State; compensation is payable for improvements made on the land | Land reform made part of the Constitution; compensation remains debated |

:::example{title="Two farmers, two models"}
Tafadzwa receives an A1 plot near Bindura; Nokuthula receives an A2 farm near Chinhoyi.

1. **A1 (Tafadzwa):** a smallholding for crops and some cattle, often with shared grazing; held under a permit; aimed at decongesting communal areas.
2. **A2 (Nokuthula):** a larger commercial farm; held under an offer letter or long lease; aimed at creating new commercial farmers.
3. **Common problem:** neither holds a title deed, so banks are often reluctant to lend money for inputs and equipment.
4. **Lesson:** the type of tenure affects investment and production.
:::

### Land, culture and the community

Land is not only an economic resource. In many communities it carries spiritual and cultural meaning: it holds the graves of ancestors, sacred places such as pools and hills, and the history of the families who have lived there. This is why the loss of land under colonial rule was felt so deeply, and why resettlement involves more than drawing new boundaries. New farmers have had to build new communities, schools, clinics and leadership structures, and to work with traditional leaders and rural district councils. Good relations among neighbours, fair settlement of boundary disputes and care for the soil, water and trees all decide whether resettled land supports families for generations.

## Aims of land reform

- To correct the colonial imbalance in land ownership.
- To decongest overcrowded communal areas.
- To give land to landless people, including war veterans, women and young people.
- To increase production by more people and promote black commercial farmers.
- To fulfil a central goal of the liberation struggle.

## Benefits and problems: different views

Land reform is one of the most contested subjects in Zimbabwe's history. The table summarises points widely made on each side.

| Recognised benefits | Recognised problems |
|---|---|
| Many thousands of families gained land and a livelihood | Commercial farm output fell sharply in the early 2000s, contributing to food shortages and loss of export earnings |
| A historic injustice was addressed; land ownership now reflects the population | Many farm workers lost jobs and homes |
| New smallholder and commercial farmers emerged; smallholder tobacco production grew strongly | Lack of title deeds limits loans and investment |
| Greater sense of sovereignty and national pride | Some cases of multiple farm ownership and disputes; lack of inputs, skills and equipment for some new farmers |
| Communal areas decongested in some places | Compensation for improvements remained unresolved for many years; relations with some Western countries worsened |

Supporters argue that the programme corrected a deep injustice and that production by new farmers has recovered in several crops. Critics argue that the speed and methods disrupted agriculture and the economy. Many researchers conclude that the results varied greatly between areas, models and crops.

:::example{title="Weighing the evidence"}
Question: *How far has land reform benefited Zimbabwe?*

1. **For:** land redistributed to many families; colonial imbalance addressed; growth of smallholder tobacco.
2. **Against:** fall in commercial output in the 2000s; job losses for farm workers; tenure insecurity.
3. **Judgement:** e.g. "Land reform achieved its central aim of redistribution, but its economic benefits have been uneven and depend on support for the new farmers."
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Taking only one side"}
Section B essays on land reform need both benefits and problems, and a supported judgement. Avoid slogans for or against.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing freehold and leasehold"}
Freehold is ownership with a title deed; leasehold is use for a fixed period under a lease.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing figures"}
Use "many thousands" and "fell sharply" unless you are certain of an official figure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify* types of land tenure (Paper 2 Section A). *Explain the aims of land reform*: give the aim and the reason. For *how far* or *assess* questions (Section B), weigh benefits against problems and conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Land tenure in Zimbabwe includes communal, freehold, leasehold, state and resettlement land.
- Colonial laws left a deep imbalance in land ownership at independence.
- Redistribution moved from willing-buyer willing-seller (1980s-1990s) to compulsory acquisition and the fast-track programme from 2000 (A1 and A2 models).
- Chapter 16 of the 2013 Constitution vests acquired agricultural land in the State; compensation is for improvements.
- Land reform redistributed land to many families but also brought disruption; its results are debated.
:::
