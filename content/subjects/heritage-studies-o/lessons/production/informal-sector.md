## Introduction

Walk through any Zimbabwean town and you will see the informal sector at work: a woman selling tomatoes and airtime at a street corner, a young man repairing phones under an umbrella, a carpenter making wardrobes in an open yard, a welder building gates beside the road, and a cross-border trader unloading bags of goods from a bus. Many families in towns and rural areas earn their living this way.

In this lesson, which draws on Forms 1, 2 and 3, you learn what the **informal sector** is, how it differs from the **formal sector**, why it matters, what holds it back and how its problems could be solved. You should already know the factors of production (land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
The part of the economy made up of small, mostly unregistered businesses and self-employed people who operate outside full government regulation, licensing and taxation.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal sector"}
The part of the economy made up of registered businesses and organisations that are licensed, pay taxes, keep records and employ workers on contracts.
:::

:::definition{term="Vending"}
Selling goods, such as fruit, vegetables, clothes or airtime, from a stall, a table or on foot, usually in public places.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-border trading"}
Buying goods in a neighbouring country to sell at home, or taking local goods to sell abroad, usually in small quantities.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Where to find official figures"}
The size of the informal sector is measured in labour force surveys by the **Zimbabwe National Statistics Agency (ZIMSTAT)**. These surveys have shown that a large share of working people are in informal employment. Quote official figures only if you know them and their year.
:::

## Characteristics of the informal sector

| Characteristic | What it means |
|---|---|
| Small scale | One person or a few workers, often family members |
| Easy to enter | Little capital, few qualifications and no licence needed to start |
| Often unregistered | Many businesses are not registered and pay few or no formal taxes |
| Little capital | Simple tools and small stocks of goods |
| Skills learned informally | On the job, from relatives or by watching others |
| Cash-based | Most sales are in cash or by mobile money; few written records |
| Insecure | No contracts, pensions, medical aid or insurance |
| Flexible | Hours, place and goods change quickly with demand |

## Examples in Zimbabwe

- **Vending:** fruit, vegetables, second-hand clothes, airtime, snacks, phone accessories.
- **Cross-border trading:** goods brought from neighbouring countries such as South Africa, Botswana, Zambia and Mozambique.
- **Small-scale manufacturing:** furniture, metal gates and window frames, school uniforms, soap, peanut butter, candles.
- **Repairs and services:** motor mechanics, shoe repairs, phone and electronics repairs, hairdressing, tailoring.
- **Transport:** some taxi and small-vehicle operators.
- **Small-scale mining:** panning and digging for gold in some areas (where it is not licensed, it is informal and often unsafe).

Well-known clusters of small manufacturers have grown up in high-density suburbs of Harare and other towns, where many workshops share space, customers and suppliers.

## The formal and informal sectors compared

| Feature | Formal sector | Informal sector |
|---|---|---|
| Registration | Registered and licensed | Often unregistered |
| Taxes | Pays taxes regularly | Pays few or no formal taxes |
| Size | Medium to large | Small, often one person |
| Capital | Large, often borrowed from banks | Small, from savings and family |
| Workers | Contracts, payslips, benefits | Family or casual workers, no contracts |
| Premises | Offices, factories, shops | Streets, homes, open yards, markets |
| Records | Written accounts | Few or no records |
| Examples | A bank, a mine, a supermarket chain | A street vendor, a backyard welder |

:::example{title="Two tailors in Masvingo"}
Rudo works for a registered clothing company. Nokuthula sews school uniforms at home.

1. **Rudo (formal):** has a contract and a payslip, belongs to a pension scheme and works fixed hours in a factory.
2. **Nokuthula (informal):** uses her own sewing machine, sets her own hours, sells to neighbours for cash and keeps no written accounts.
3. **Nokuthula's advantages:** she is her own boss, starts with little money and can change her products quickly.
4. **Nokuthula's risks:** no pension or insurance; if her machine breaks or she falls ill, she earns nothing.
5. **Lesson:** both sectors produce goods and incomes, but with different security and opportunities.
:::

## Importance of the informal sector

- **Employment:** it provides work for many people who cannot find formal jobs, including school leavers and retrenched workers.
- **Income and poverty reduction:** families pay school fees, food and rent from informal earnings.
- **Cheap and convenient goods and services:** vendors sell in small quantities close to people's homes.
- **Skills and enterprise:** people learn trades and business skills, and some informal businesses grow into formal companies.
- **Use of local materials:** small manufacturers use local and recycled materials.
- **Links with the formal sector:** informal traders buy from wholesalers and factories, and formal businesses hire informal mechanics and transporters.

## Constraints and possible solutions

| Constraint | Effect | Possible solution |
|---|---|---|
| Lack of capital | Cannot buy machines or larger stocks | Microfinance, savings clubs, funds for small enterprises |
| Lack of skills (business and technical) | Poor quality, poor record-keeping | Short courses, vocational training, mentoring |
| Lack of premises | Work in the open; goods damaged by rain; conflict with councils | Councils build markets and workspaces with water, toilets and shelter |
| Regulation and licensing | Costly or slow licences; goods confiscated; fines | Simpler, cheaper registration; dialogue between councils and traders' associations |
| No insurance or social protection | Illness, fire or theft can end the business | Group insurance and savings schemes; associations |
| Competition and small markets | Low prices and profits | Co-operatives, better products, new markets |
| Poor infrastructure | Power cuts, poor roads and water | Investment in services in markets and industrial sites |

:::example{title="A council and its vendors"}
A fictional town council in Chinhoyi is concerned about crowded pavements and uncollected rubbish.

1. **The problem:** vendors sell on pavements, blocking pedestrians; there are no toilets, and rubbish piles up.
2. **Option 1, removal by force:** quick, but vendors lose goods and income and soon return.
3. **Option 2, dialogue:** the council meets the vendors' association, builds a covered market with water and toilets, and charges an affordable daily fee.
4. **Outcome of option 2:** cleaner streets, safer trading, some council income, and vendors keep their livelihoods.
5. **Lesson:** solutions work best when traders are consulted.
:::

## Different views

Some people see the informal sector as a sign of economic difficulty, because people turn to it when formal jobs are scarce, and because it pays little tax and offers little security. Others see it as a source of enterprise and resilience that keeps families afloat and could grow into formal businesses. Both views can be supported; good answers recognise the strengths and the weaknesses.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the informal sector is illegal"}
Most informal activities, such as selling vegetables or repairing shoes, are legal work. What makes them informal is that they are small and mostly unregistered or unregulated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing constraints without solutions"}
When asked how to help the sector, link each solution to a constraint: "Councils could build covered markets, because vendors lack premises."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The informal sector is made up of small, mostly unregistered businesses and self-employed people.
- Examples include vending, cross-border trading, small-scale manufacturing and repairs.
- It differs from the formal sector in registration, taxes, size, capital, contracts and premises.
- It provides jobs, incomes, cheap goods and skills, and links with the formal sector.
- Constraints include lack of capital, skills, premises and insurance, and regulation; solutions include finance, training, workspaces and simpler registration.
:::
