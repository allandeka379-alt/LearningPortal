## Introduction

Look around your classroom. The desks, exercise books, pens, uniforms and even the cooking oil and bread in your lunchbox were all **manufactured**: raw materials were changed into finished goods that people can use. Some were made by hand by a single carpenter or tailor; others came off long, fast production lines in factories in Harare, Bulawayo or abroad.

In this Form 2 lesson you learn what manufacturing is, the **processes** goods pass through, the **methods** used to produce them, and the **advantages and disadvantages** of modern methods. You should already know that manufacturing is part of **secondary industry**, and that indigenous industries such as pottery and iron working were early forms of manufacturing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Manufacturing"}
Changing raw materials or parts into finished goods, by hand or by machine, usually on a large scale.
:::

:::definition{term="Raw materials"}
Natural or partly processed materials from which goods are made, such as cotton, timber, maize, hides, iron ore and sugar cane.
:::

:::definition{term="Handicraft production"}
Making goods mainly by hand, using simple tools, usually in small quantities.
:::

:::definition{term="Mechanised production"}
Making goods mainly with machines powered by electricity or engines.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass production"}
Making very large numbers of identical goods, usually on an assembly line, with machines and workers each doing one task.
:::

:::definition{term="Division of labour"}
Splitting production into separate tasks, each done by a different worker or group of workers who specialise in it.
:::

## The processes of manufacturing

Most manufactured goods pass through three main processes.

| Process | What happens | Zimbabwean examples |
|---|---|---|
| Processing | Raw materials are changed in form: cleaned, cut, mixed, cooked, refined, spun or smelted | Milling maize into mealie-meal; crushing sugar cane into sugar; tanning hides into leather; ginning and spinning cotton |
| Assembling | Ready-made parts are put together to make a product | Assembling buses, trucks, furniture, solar lamps, electrical goods |
| Packaging | Finished goods are wrapped, labelled and boxed for storage, transport and sale | Bagging mealie-meal, bottling drinks, boxing soap, labelling cooking oil |

Packaging is more than a wrapper. It protects goods, keeps food clean, carries information (ingredients, weight, expiry date, maker) and helps sell the product.

## Methods of production

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Handicraft | Goods made by hand, one at a time, with simple tools | A potter shaping pots; a carver making a stone sculpture; a basket weaver |
| Job production | One unique item made to a customer's order | A tailor making a wedding dress; a welder making a gate to fit a client's yard |
| Batch production | A set number of identical items made, then the machines are changed for the next product | A bakery making 200 loaves, then 100 buns |
| Mass production | Huge numbers of identical goods made continuously on an assembly line | Bottled drinks, soap, cigarettes, tinned foods |
| Mechanised production | Machines do most of the work; workers operate and supervise them | A textile mill; a cement plant |

Many factories use several methods. A furniture maker in Gweru might make most chairs in batches, but also take special orders (job production).

:::example{title="From maize to mealie-meal"}
A fictional milling company in Bulawayo buys maize from farmers.

1. **Processing:** the maize is cleaned, the hulls removed and the grain ground into mealie-meal by machines.
2. **Packaging:** the mealie-meal is weighed into 10 kg bags, sealed and labelled with the brand, weight and date.
3. **Method:** large machines run continuously, producing thousands of identical bags a day, so this is **mechanised mass production**.
4. **Division of labour:** some workers run the mill, others operate the bagging machines, check quality, drive trucks or keep accounts.
5. **Result:** cheap, standard mealie-meal reaches shops across the country.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of modern methods

Modern manufacturing (mechanised and mass production) has changed how goods are made all over the world.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Large quantities produced quickly | Machines are expensive to buy and maintain |
| Lower cost per item, so goods are cheaper | Fewer workers are needed: some jobs are lost |
| Standard, uniform quality | Work can be repetitive and boring |
| Workers specialise and become skilled at one task | Workers depend on one narrow skill |
| Goods can be exported, earning foreign currency | Needs a reliable power supply; power cuts stop production |
| Uses technology that raises productivity | Pollution of air and water if waste is not managed |
| Creates related jobs in transport, sales and maintenance | Handicraft skills and indigenous designs may be lost; goods may be less unique |

:::example{title="Two ways to make school uniforms"}
Sipho runs a small tailoring business in Kariba; a fictional clothing factory operates in Harare.

1. **Sipho (job and handicraft):** measures each learner and sews each uniform with one machine; good fit, but only a few uniforms a day at a higher price.
2. **The factory (mass production):** cuts hundreds of pieces at once and sews them on a line of machinists, each doing one seam; thousands of uniforms in standard sizes at a lower price.
3. **The factory's risk:** when electricity is cut, the whole line stops.
4. **Sipho's advantage:** flexibility, personal service and alterations.
5. **Lesson:** both methods have a place, depending on the market.
:::

## Quality control, safety and standards

Modern manufacturing does not end when a product leaves the machine. Factories check samples of their goods at each stage, a step called **quality control**, so that faulty items are removed before they reach customers. Food and drink factories must follow strict hygiene rules: workers wear protective clothing, equipment is cleaned regularly and products are tested. Many products are made to agreed national or international **standards**, so buyers know what to expect and exports are accepted in other countries.

Factories also have a duty to protect their workers. Machines need guards, workers need training and protective equipment such as gloves, goggles and ear protection, and fire exits must be kept clear. Waste water and smoke should be treated so that rivers and air are not polluted. A factory that cares for quality, safety and the environment is more likely to keep its customers, its workers and its licence to operate.

## Manufacturing in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe manufactures processed foods and drinks, textiles and clothing, leather goods and footwear, furniture, fertilisers and chemicals, cement and building materials, metal products and some assembled vehicles and equipment. Manufacturing adds value to local raw materials (maize, cotton, sugar cane, timber, hides, minerals) so that the country earns more than by exporting them raw.

Manufacturers have faced difficulties, including power shortages, old machinery, shortage of capital and competition from imports. These difficulties are studied in the lesson on factors that inhibit production.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling packaging 'not part of manufacturing'"}
Packaging is one of the processes of manufacturing: it protects the product, carries information and helps sell it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing batch and mass production"}
Batch production makes a set number of one item and then changes to another. Mass production makes the same item continuously in huge numbers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only advantages"}
Questions on modern methods usually expect advantages **and** disadvantages, such as job losses and dependence on electricity.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Manufacturing changes raw materials or parts into finished goods.
- Its main processes are processing, assembling and packaging.
- Methods include handicraft, job, batch, mass and mechanised production.
- Modern methods produce large quantities cheaply and to a standard quality.
- They also cost a lot to set up, reduce some jobs, depend on power and can pollute.
:::
