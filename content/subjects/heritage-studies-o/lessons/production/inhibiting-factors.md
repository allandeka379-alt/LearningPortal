## Introduction

A factory in Bulawayo has orders to fill, but the power goes off for eight hours and the machines stand idle. A farmer in Chinhoyi has land and seed but cannot afford fertiliser, and the bank will not lend without security. A hospital trains nurses, and some of them leave to work in other countries. A small-scale miner's water pump breaks, and there are no spare parts. In each case, something is **inhibiting production**: holding back the amount of goods and services the country produces.

This Form 4 lesson identifies the main factors that inhibit production in Zimbabwe, explains their effects and looks at possible solutions. Some factors, such as the effect of sanctions, are **contested**: people hold different views, and you will be expected to present those views fairly. You should already know the factors of production (land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship), since every inhibiting factor reduces access to one or more of them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inhibiting factor"}
Anything that limits or slows down the production of goods and services.
:::

:::definition{term="Brain drain"}
The emigration of skilled and educated people, such as doctors, nurses, teachers and engineers, to work in other countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Capacity utilisation"}
The share of a factory's or industry's possible output that it actually produces. Low capacity utilisation means machines and workers are idle.
:::

:::definition{term="Sanctions / restrictive measures"}
Economic or political measures imposed by one country or group of countries on another, or on particular people and companies, such as travel bans, asset freezes and limits on loans or trade.
:::

:::definition{term="Capacity building"}
Developing the skills, equipment, institutions and systems that people and organisations need to work effectively.
:::

## The main factors inhibiting production

| Factor | Effect on production |
|---|---|
| Lack of capital | Businesses cannot buy machines, raw materials or inputs; banks ask for collateral and charge high interest |
| Restrictive or unclear legislation and procedures | Slow, costly licensing and registration; uncertain rules discourage investment |
| Sanctions / restrictive measures | Their effect is debated: some say they cut access to loans, investment and markets; others say they target only individuals and companies (see below) |
| Skills shortages and brain drain | Fewer engineers, doctors, artisans and managers; training costs are lost when skilled people leave |
| Outdated technology | Old machines break down, use more power and produce lower-quality goods at higher cost |
| Power shortages | Load-shedding stops factories, irrigation pumps and mines; firms pay extra for generators |
| Water shortages | Industries, towns and irrigated farms cannot operate fully |
| Drought and climate shocks | Crops fail, livestock die and hydro-electric power falls when lake levels drop |
| Poor infrastructure | Damaged roads and railways raise transport costs and delay deliveries |
| Economic instability | Rapid price rises and currency changes make planning, saving and borrowing difficult |
| Competition from imports | Cheaper imported goods reduce sales of local products |

### Capital

Production needs money for equipment, raw materials, wages and inputs. Many businesses, especially small ones and new farmers, cannot borrow because they have no collateral such as title deeds, and interest rates can be high. When firms cannot raise capital, they cannot replace old machines or expand.

### Legislation and procedures

Laws and procedures that are slow, costly or keep changing can discourage investors. An example often discussed is the indigenisation policy, which required most larger businesses to be majority-owned by indigenous Zimbabweans. Supporters argued that it widened ownership of the economy by Zimbabweans; critics argued that it discouraged foreign investment. The requirement was later relaxed for most sectors. Lengthy business registration and licensing procedures have also been criticised, and governments have tried to make them simpler.

### Sanctions or restrictive measures: different views

From the early 2000s, the United States, the European Union and some other Western countries placed measures on Zimbabwe.

- **The Zimbabwean government's view** is that these are **sanctions** that have damaged the whole economy, by limiting access to international loans, credit and investment and by making foreign companies and banks cautious. SADC has called for them to be lifted and marks **25 October** as an Anti-Sanctions Day.
- **The view of the countries that imposed them** is that they are **targeted restrictive measures** aimed at particular individuals and companies (travel bans and asset freezes), not at ordinary citizens, and that economic difficulties were caused mainly by policies, governance and other factors.
- **Many economists and researchers** argue that the measures and Zimbabwe's other economic problems interacted, so their exact effect is hard to separate from other causes.

In an exam, present these views fairly, use neutral language, and reach your own judgement from the evidence.

### Skills shortages and brain drain

Zimbabwe has a well-educated population, but many skilled workers have emigrated in search of better pay and conditions. Hospitals, schools, universities and industries lose experienced staff, and the cost of their training is lost to the country. On the other hand, money sent home by Zimbabweans abroad (**remittances**) supports many families and businesses.

### Power, water and drought

Much of Zimbabwe's electricity comes from the hydro-electric power station at **Kariba** and the coal-fired power station at **Hwange**. When drought lowers the level of Lake Kariba, less power can be generated, and older thermal units break down more often. Power cuts stop production lines, mines and irrigation, and businesses pay more for generators and diesel. Droughts also reduce crop and livestock production directly, and water shortages affect towns and industry.

:::example{title="A factory in a difficult year"}
A fictional food-processing factory in Gweru faces several problems at once.

1. **Power cuts:** the production line stops for hours each day; the factory runs a diesel generator, raising costs.
2. **Old machinery:** two machines break down; spare parts must be imported and take weeks to arrive.
3. **Raw materials:** drought reduces the local harvest of groundnuts, so supply falls and prices rise.
4. **Effect:** output falls, some workers are put on short time, and shop prices rise.
5. **Response:** the factory installs solar panels for part of its power, trains its own technicians to maintain machines, and signs contracts with irrigating farmers for a steadier supply.
:::

## Possible solutions

| Factor | Possible solutions |
|---|---|
| Lack of capital | Microfinance, development banks and funds for small businesses; title deeds or other secure tenure that can be used as collateral; attracting local and foreign investment |
| Legislation | Simpler, quicker and cheaper registration and licensing; stable and predictable policies |
| Sanctions / restrictive measures | Diplomatic engagement and re-engagement; trade with a wide range of partners, including regional partners in SADC and COMESA; reforms that build investor confidence |
| Skills and brain drain | Better pay and conditions; vocational and technical training; incentives for skilled people to stay or return; using the skills of Zimbabweans abroad |
| Outdated technology | Investment in new equipment; research and innovation; partnerships with technology suppliers |
| Power and water | New and repaired power stations; solar and other renewable energy; dams and irrigation; efficient water use |
| Drought | Irrigation, drought-tolerant crops such as small grains, conservation farming, early warning, insurance |
| Infrastructure | Repairing roads, railways and bridges |

:::example{title="A farmer adapts"}
Mthulisi farms near Gwanda, in a dry region.

1. **Problem:** repeated drought ruins his maize crop.
2. **Solutions he uses:** he plants drought-tolerant sorghum and millet, digs planting basins to hold moisture, and joins a group sharing a small solar-powered irrigation pump.
3. **Effect:** his harvest is smaller in bad years but no longer fails completely.
4. **Lesson:** solutions are often combined: technology (solar pumps), knowledge (conservation farming) and co-operation.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Presenting sanctions from one side only"}
The effect of sanctions or restrictive measures is debated. State the main views fairly and support your judgement with evidence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without effects"}
"Power cuts" earns little. Explain: "Power cuts stop production lines, so output falls and costs rise because firms buy diesel for generators."
:::

:::mistake{title="Solutions that do not match the problem"}
Each solution should address a named cause. Training solves skills shortages, not drought.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Command words for this topic"}
*Identify* or *state* factors that inhibit production (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A). *Explain* their effects with developed points. *Suggest* solutions linked to each factor. In Section B, a *discuss* or *how far* question (for example, "To what extent are sanctions the main factor inhibiting production?") needs several factors, the different views on sanctions, and a supported conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Production in Zimbabwe is held back by lack of capital, restrictive procedures, skills shortages, outdated technology, power and water shortages, drought and poor infrastructure.
- Sanctions or restrictive measures are contested: some see them as a major cause of decline, others as targeted measures with limited effect.
- Brain drain reduces skills, though remittances support many families.
- Power shortages are linked to drought at Kariba and ageing thermal plants.
- Solutions include finance, training, new technology, renewable energy, irrigation, simpler procedures and engagement with partners.
:::
