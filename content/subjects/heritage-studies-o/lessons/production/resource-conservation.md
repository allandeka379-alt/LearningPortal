## Introduction

A ranger in Gonarezhou walks a patrol route at dawn, checking for snares. An inspector visits a new quarry to see whether the company has done what its environmental assessment promised. A forestry officer marks which trees in a plantation may be cut this year. A team from the national museums repairs a collapsed section of a stone wall at an ancient site. None of these people produce food or goods directly, yet all of them protect the resources on which production and heritage depend.

In Form 4 you study how Zimbabwe conserves and preserves its natural resources (land, water, forests, wildlife, minerals) and its cultural resources (monuments, sites and objects of history and belief) today. You already know from earlier lessons how indigenous communities protected resources through beliefs, totems, sacred places and the authority of chiefs; that is studied in detail in the lesson on indigenous conservation methods. Here you will learn the modern methods, the institutions and laws involved, how well they are enforced, and how modern and indigenous methods compare.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
The careful and sustainable use and management of resources so that they continue to be available, for example harvesting timber in rotation and replanting.
:::

:::definition{term="Preservation"}
Protecting a resource from use or change so that it stays in its present state, for example protecting an ancient stone wall or a sacred forest.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural resources"}
Things found in nature that people use, such as land, water, forests, wildlife and minerals.
:::

:::definition{term="Cultural resources"}
Things made or given meaning by people that carry history and identity, such as monuments, rock art, archaeological sites, shrines, artefacts and traditional knowledge.
:::

:::definition{term="Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)"}
A study, required by law for certain projects such as mines, dams and large buildings, that identifies the likely effects of the project on the environment and how to reduce them before work begins.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The Environmental Management Agency (EMA) was set up under the Environmental Management Act and is the lead body for protecting the environment.
- The Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (ZimParks) manages national parks and wildlife under the Parks and Wild Life Act.
- The Forestry Commission manages and protects forests under the Forest Act.
- National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe (NMMZ) protects monuments, sites and museum collections.
- Section 73 of the Constitution gives every person the right to have the environment protected for present and future generations.
:::

## Institutions and their roles

| Institution | Resources | Main roles |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental Management Agency (EMA) | Environment in general: land, air, water, waste | Requires and approves environmental impact assessments; licenses polluting activities and waste sites; inspects, fines and prosecutes offenders; runs awareness campaigns (veld fires, litter, stream-bank cultivation) |
| Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (ZimParks) | Wildlife and national parks | Manages national parks and safari areas; anti-poaching patrols; issues hunting and fishing permits; protects endangered species; supports tourism |
| Forestry Commission | Forests and woodland | Manages state forests and plantations; regulates timber cutting and movement of forest produce; promotes tree planting and woodlots; fights deforestation |
| National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe (NMMZ) | Cultural resources | Protects and restores national monuments such as Great Zimbabwe and Khami; runs museums; conserves artefacts; controls excavations and the export of heritage objects |
| Local authorities (councils) and traditional leaders | Local land, water and woodland | Make and enforce by-laws; manage communal resources; settle disputes |
| Zimbabwe National Water Authority (ZINWA) | Water | Manages dams and water allocation; monitors water use |

## Contemporary methods of conservation and preservation

| Method | Example |
|---|---|
| Protected areas | National parks (Hwange, Gonarezhou, Mana Pools), safari areas, forest reserves and national monuments where use is controlled |
| Laws, permits and licences | Licences for mining, hunting, fishing and cutting timber; bans on certain products and activities |
| Environmental impact assessments | Projects must show how they will limit damage before they start |
| Anti-poaching and enforcement | Ranger patrols, prosecutions, cooperation with police and neighbouring countries |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Plantations, woodlots, National Tree Planting Day (first Saturday of December) |
| Soil and water conservation | Contour ridges, conservation farming, water harvesting, protection of stream banks and wetlands |
| Community-based natural resource management | CAMPFIRE (from 1989): rural communities share the income from wildlife in their areas and so have a reason to protect it |
| Recycling and waste control | Reducing waste and pollution, recycling materials |
| Restoration of cultural sites | Repairing collapsed walls, controlling visitor numbers, keeping records and research |
| Museums and archives | Storing and displaying artefacts, documents and recordings safely |
| Education and awareness | School environment clubs, media campaigns, guided visits to parks and monuments |

:::example{title="CAMPFIRE in a wildlife area"}
A fictional rural district near Hwange National Park has elephants that damage crops.

1. **Old problem:** farmers saw elephants only as a threat, so some helped poachers.
2. **CAMPFIRE approach:** the rural district council manages wildlife for the community; income from regulated safari hunting and tourism is shared, and part of it builds a clinic and a school block.
3. **Change:** villagers now report poachers, because the wildlife brings benefits to them.
4. **Debate:** supporters point to income and conservation; critics say benefits do not always reach households and that crop damage continues. The scheme shows that people conserve resources they benefit from.
:::

## Laws and enforcement

Zimbabwe has many conservation laws, including the Environmental Management Act, the Parks and Wild Life Act, the Forest Act and the National Museums and Monuments Act, and the Constitution protects the environment in section 73. Laws work when they are **enforced**: offenders are caught, prosecuted and punished, and the public knows the rules.

Enforcement faces challenges:

- too few rangers, inspectors and vehicles to cover large areas;
- poverty, which pushes people to cut trees, hunt or pan for gold despite the law;
- organised criminal groups behind poaching and illegal trade;
- fines that are sometimes lower than the profit from breaking the law;
- limited public knowledge of the laws;
- corruption, which allows some offenders to escape punishment.

## Modern and indigenous methods compared

| | Indigenous methods | Contemporary methods |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | Beliefs, taboos, totems, sacred places, ancestral authority | Written laws, science, institutions |
| Who enforces | Chiefs, headmen, spirit mediums, elders, the community | EMA, ZimParks, Forestry Commission, NMMZ, police, courts |
| Strengths | Respected locally; cheap; part of daily life and identity | National coverage; backed by science and the courts; can deal with large-scale threats such as industry and trade |
| Weaknesses | Weakened by urbanisation, migration and changing beliefs; may not cover new threats | Expensive; may be resented if imposed without consultation; difficult to enforce in remote areas |

The two approaches can support each other. Many communities still respect sacred forests and pools, and modern bodies increasingly work with traditional leaders, who under the Constitution have a role in protecting the environment in their areas.

:::example{title="Working together at a sacred site"}
A fictional hill with rock paintings and a sacred spring lies within a communal area.

1. **Indigenous protection:** the chief forbids cutting trees near the spring and holds ceremonies there.
2. **Modern protection:** NMMZ records the rock paintings and asks visitors not to touch or wet them; EMA prevents a quarry from opening nearby after an impact assessment.
3. **Result:** both natural and cultural resources are protected, with the community involved rather than excluded.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the institutions"}
EMA protects the environment generally; ZimParks manages parks and wildlife; the Forestry Commission manages forests; NMMZ protects monuments and museums.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting cultural resources"}
Conservation is not only about trees and animals. Monuments, rock art, shrines and artefacts must also be preserved.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying laws alone conserve resources"}
Laws must be enforced and accepted by communities to work. Explain enforcement and community involvement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Conservation is careful, sustainable use; preservation keeps a resource unchanged.
- EMA, ZimParks, the Forestry Commission, NMMZ, councils, traditional leaders and ZINWA all protect resources.
- Methods include protected areas, laws and permits, impact assessments, anti-poaching, afforestation, CAMPFIRE, restoration and museums.
- Enforcement is limited by too few officers, poverty, organised crime, low fines and corruption.
- Indigenous and contemporary methods each have strengths; they work best when combined with community involvement.
:::
