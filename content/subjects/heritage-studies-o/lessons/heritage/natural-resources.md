## Introduction

Think about your breakfast this morning. The maize in your porridge grew in Zimbabwean soil, watered by rain. The water came from a river, a dam or a borehole. The electricity that boiled the kettle may have come from the water of Lake Kariba or from coal mined at Hwange. Even the wooden spoon was once a tree. Before you left home, you had already used several of the country's **natural resources**.

Natural resources are the gifts of nature that people use to live and to develop. In Zimbabwe they are also **heritage**: they were passed down by earlier generations, they are tied to culture and belief, and they must be passed on in good condition to the generations still to come. In this lesson you will learn what natural resources are, how they are classified, which resources Zimbabwe has, why they are important and why they are part of national heritage. You should already know what heritage means and what the green and yellow stripes of the national flag stand for.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural resource"}
Anything found in nature that people use to meet their needs, such as land, water, minerals, plants and animals.
:::

:::definition{term="Renewable resource"}
A resource that can be replaced naturally within a human lifetime if it is used carefully, such as forests, water, wildlife, fish and solar energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-renewable resource"}
A resource that exists in a fixed amount and cannot be replaced once used up, such as gold, coal, diamonds and other minerals.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable use"}
Using resources in a way that meets present needs without spoiling them for future generations.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The green stripe of the flag stands for land and agriculture; the yellow stripe for mineral wealth.
- **Section 73** of the Constitution gives every person the right to an environment that is not harmful to health, and to have it protected for the benefit of present and future generations.
- Lake Kariba, on the Zambezi, supplies water for hydroelectric power; Hwange is the main coal-mining area.
:::

## Zimbabwe's natural resources

| Resource | Examples in Zimbabwe | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| Land and soils | Fertile soils of the highveld; grazing land in the drier south and west | Farming (maize, small grains, tobacco, cotton), grazing, settlement |
| Minerals | Gold, platinum and chrome on and around the Great Dyke; diamonds; coal at Hwange; lithium; nickel | Exports, jobs, energy, raw materials for industry |
| Water | The Zambezi, Limpopo, Save and Runde rivers; Lake Kariba and other dams; underground water | Drinking, irrigation, hydroelectric power, fishing, tourism |
| Forests and plants | Teak woodlands in Matabeleland North; miombo woodland; plantations in the Eastern Highlands; wild fruits and grasses | Timber, firewood, building poles, wild foods, thatching grass, traditional crafts |
| Wildlife | Elephants, buffalo, lions and antelope in Hwange, Gonarezhou, Mana Pools and other parks | Tourism, meat, conservation, culture |
| Birds and fish | Many bird species; kapenta and bream in Lake Kariba | Tourism (bird-watching), food, income |
| Sunlight and wind | Long hours of sunshine for most of the year | Solar energy for homes, schools and clinics |

### Renewable and non-renewable

| Renewable (if used carefully) | Non-renewable |
|---|---|
| Forests, grasses and wild fruits | Gold, platinum, diamonds, chrome |
| Water in rivers and dams | Coal |
| Wildlife, birds and fish | Lithium, nickel and other minerals |
| Sunlight and wind | |

Note the words "if used carefully". A forest is renewable, but cutting trees faster than they grow destroys it. Soil can be renewed, but once it is washed away by erosion it takes many years to form again.

## Why natural resources are important

- **Food.** Land, water and soils allow farming; rivers and lakes provide fish; forests give wild fruits, mushrooms and honey.
- **Income and exports.** Minerals, tobacco and other farm products are among the country's main exports and earn foreign currency.
- **Employment.** Mining, farming, forestry, fishing and tourism give work to many people.
- **Energy.** Water at Kariba drives hydroelectric power; coal at Hwange is burned to make electricity; firewood is the main fuel in many rural homes; sunshine powers solar panels.
- **Tourism.** Wildlife, Victoria Falls, Lake Kariba and the national parks attract visitors from around the world.
- **Raw materials for industry.** Timber, cotton, minerals and farm products are processed in factories.
- **Culture and belief.** Many clans have animal totems; some forests, pools, hills and trees are sacred; rain-asking ceremonies (mukwerera/ukucela izulu) link communities to the land.
- **Medicine.** Many plants are used in traditional remedies and studied by scientists; anyone who is ill should still seek a trained health worker.

:::example{title="A river as heritage"}
A Form 1 class in Masvingo studies the Runde River.

1. **Uses today:** water for cattle, gardens and homes; fish; sand for building.
2. **Cultural meaning:** elders explain that some pools are respected as sacred and that the river is linked to stories of the ancestors.
3. **Heritage lesson:** the river was used by earlier generations and must be kept clean and flowing for the next. Dumping waste or digging sand without control damages a shared inheritance.
:::

## Natural resources as heritage

Natural resources are heritage for four reasons. First, they were **inherited**: the land and its wealth were used and protected by earlier generations, and the struggle for independence was in large part a struggle over land. Second, they are part of **identity**: the flag, the coat of arms and the pledge all speak of the land and its riches, and totems link families to animals. Third, they are **shared**: rivers, wildlife and forests belong to the nation, not only to whoever is using them today. Fourth, they must be **passed on**: section 73 of the Constitution speaks of protecting the environment for future generations, and indigenous rules once protected sacred forests and certain animals for the same reason.

:::example{title="Applying sustainable use"}
A fictional community near Lupane relies on the surrounding woodland for firewood and building poles.

1. **Problem:** trees are being cut faster than they regrow, and women walk further each year for firewood.
2. **Sustainable plan:** the community agrees to cut only dead wood or mature trees in rotation, plants a woodlot near the village and tries energy-saving stoves.
3. **Result:** the woodland supplies the community's needs and remains for its children.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling all resources renewable"}
Minerals such as gold and coal cannot be replaced. Forests and water are renewable only when they are used carefully.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the cultural importance"}
Natural resources are not only economic. Totems, sacred places and ceremonies show their cultural value, which makes them heritage.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up this topic with ownership and conservation"}
Who owns resources and how access changed after 1980 is a separate topic, as is how to conserve them. Here, focus on what the resources are and why they matter.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Natural resources are things in nature that people use: land, water, minerals, plants, animals, sunlight.
- Renewable resources can be replaced if used carefully; non-renewable resources such as minerals cannot.
- Zimbabwe's resources include fertile land, gold, platinum, chrome, diamonds, coal, lithium, rivers and dams, forests, wildlife and fish.
- They provide food, income, jobs, energy, tourism, raw materials and cultural and religious meaning.
- They are heritage because they are inherited, tied to identity, shared by the nation and held for future generations.
:::
