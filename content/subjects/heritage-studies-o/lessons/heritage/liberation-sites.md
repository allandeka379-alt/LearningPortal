## Introduction

Every year, small groups of Zimbabweans travel across the border into Mozambique and Zambia. Some are elderly men and women who lived in camps there during the liberation war; some are relatives of people who died there; others are students and historians. At a quiet memorial they lay flowers, read names and stand in silence. A Form 4 pupil from Mutare who joins such a visit asks: "Why are some of our heroes buried in other countries?"

The answer lies in the history of the Second Chimurenga/Umvukela. During the war, thousands of young Zimbabweans crossed into neighbouring countries to train as fighters or to escape danger as refugees. Camps were set up in Mozambique, Zambia, Tanzania, Botswana and Angola. Some camps were attacked by Rhodesian forces, and many people died. Those places are now **liberation war heritage sites**. This lesson describes the main sites, what happened there, why they matter, and how countries in the region work together to preserve them. You should already know the outline of the Second Chimurenga and the role of the Frontline States.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Liberation war heritage site"}
A place linked to the struggle for independence, such as a former camp, battle site or burial ground, that is preserved because of its historical and emotional importance.
:::

:::definition{term="Refugee"}
A person who has fled his or her country because of war, persecution or danger and lives in another country for safety.
:::

:::definition{term="Frontline States"}
The independent countries next to or near the white-ruled states of southern Africa (including Tanzania, Zambia, Botswana, Mozambique and Angola) that supported the liberation movements, often at great cost to themselves.
:::

:::definition{term="Commemoration"}
An act or ceremony that honours and remembers people or events, such as laying wreaths on an anniversary.
:::

## The main sites

During the war, the Zimbabwe African National Union (ZANU) and its army ZANLA operated mainly from Mozambique (and earlier from Zambia and Tanzania), while the Zimbabwe African People's Union (ZAPU) and its army ZIPRA operated mainly from Zambia, with training also in other countries. Both movements had camps where refugees, recruits, students and fighters lived together. The figures below are estimates: sources disagree, and you should present them as approximate.

| Date | Site and country | What happened (approximate estimates) |
|---|---|---|
| 9 August 1976 | Nyadzonia, Mozambique | Rhodesian forces attacked a camp that held thousands of people, many of them refugees, including young people; estimates commonly given are over 1,000 dead |
| 23 November 1977 | Chimoio, Mozambique | Rhodesian air and ground forces attacked a large ZANU camp complex; a camp at Tembue was attacked in the same operation; estimates range from over 1,000 to several thousand dead |
| 19 October 1978 | Freedom Camp, near Lusaka, Zambia | Rhodesian aircraft attacked a ZIPRA camp; estimates commonly given are in the hundreds of dead |
| October 1978 | Mkushi, Zambia | A ZIPRA camp, which housed many young women, was attacked in the same series of raids; many were killed |

The Rhodesian government described the camps it attacked as military bases. The liberation movements, and many survivors, have stressed that the camps also held large numbers of refugees, students and unarmed people. Both views appear in histories of the war; what is not in doubt is that a great many people, many of them young, died at these sites.

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **Nyadzonia:** 9 August 1976 (Mozambique)
- **Chimoio:** 23 November 1977 (Mozambique)
- **Freedom Camp:** 19 October 1978 (Zambia)
:::

## Why the sites are important

- **Remembrance and respect for the dead.** The sites are graves. Visiting and caring for them honours those who died and comforts their families.
- **History and evidence.** The sites, and the memories of survivors, are evidence of what the war cost and of how ordinary young people took part in it.
- **Identity.** They help Zimbabweans understand how independence was won and the sacrifices behind the national flag and Heroes Day.
- **Regional solidarity.** They remind Zimbabweans that Mozambique, Zambia, Tanzania, Botswana and Angola gave shelter to Zimbabwean refugees and liberation movements and suffered attacks and economic hardship as a result.
- **Healing and reconciliation.** Commemoration lets survivors and families grieve; telling the story honestly helps the nation learn from the past.
- **Education and heritage tourism.** School and university groups, researchers and other visitors learn at the sites, and visits can bring some income to the local communities that look after them.

:::example{title="Reading a commemoration notice"}
A fictional church bulletin in Bindura announces a bus trip to a memorial in Mozambique on the anniversary of an attack.

1. **What it shows:** families still mourn people who died outside the country.
2. **Why the trip matters:** relatives can pray at the place where loved ones died and tell their story to young people.
3. **Link to heritage:** the site keeps the memory alive for the whole nation, not only for the families.
:::

## Preserving the sites

| Action | Example |
|---|---|
| Building and maintaining memorials | Monuments, plaques with names and fenced burial areas at former camps |
| Recording history | Collecting the testimonies of survivors, former fighters and local residents before they are lost |
| Commemoration | Ceremonies on anniversaries attended by Zimbabwean and host-country officials, survivors and families |
| Education | Visits by schools and universities; museum displays |
| Regional cooperation | Agreements between Zimbabwe and Mozambique, Zambia and other neighbours to care for the sites, and joint commemorations |

Preserving sites in another country needs **cooperation**: the land is under the host country's law, while the memory is shared. Zimbabwe and its neighbours, which work together through bilateral agreements and through SADC, cooperate on access, maintenance and ceremonies. There are difficulties: the sites are far away and costly to reach, records are incomplete, some graves are unmarked, and survivors who remember the events are growing old.

:::example{title="A class plans a heritage project"}
A Form 4 class in Marondera is asked how young people can help preserve the memory of a camp in Zambia that they cannot easily visit.

1. Interview older relatives or neighbours who lived in the camps, with their permission, and write down their memories.
2. Hold a short commemoration at school on the anniversary.
3. Share accurate, respectful information rather than rumours.

The class learns that preserving heritage is not only about buildings; it is also about memory and truth.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the countries"}
Nyadzonia and Chimoio are in Mozambique; Freedom Camp and Mkushi are in Zambia.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating casualty figures as exact"}
Numbers of dead are estimates and sources disagree. Write "over a thousand people are estimated to have died", not an exact figure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Telling the story from one side only"}
Both ZANU/ZANLA and ZAPU/ZIPRA had camps that were attacked. A good answer treats both movements fairly and shows respect for all who died.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name the site, its country and the date, e.g. "Chimoio, Mozambique, 23 November 1977".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the importance** (Paper 2 Section A): give a reason (remembrance, history, identity, solidarity, education) and develop it.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Examine** or **how far** (Paper 2 Section B): weigh the value of preserving the sites against the difficulties (distance, cost, lost records) and suggest how cooperation can help.
:::

:::summary
- Liberation war heritage sites include former camps in Mozambique (Nyadzonia, Chimoio) and Zambia (Freedom Camp, Mkushi).
- Nyadzonia was attacked on 9 August 1976, Chimoio on 23 November 1977 and Freedom Camp on 19 October 1978; casualty figures are approximate estimates in the hundreds to thousands.
- The camps held refugees, students and fighters of both ZANU/ZANLA and ZAPU/ZIPRA.
- The sites matter for remembrance, history, identity, regional solidarity, healing and education.
- Preserving them requires memorials, recorded testimony, commemoration and cooperation with host countries.
