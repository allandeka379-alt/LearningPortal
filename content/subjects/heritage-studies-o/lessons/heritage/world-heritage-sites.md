## Introduction

A tour guide in Victoria Falls town greets a group of visitors who have flown in from three continents. "You are standing beside a place that belongs to all of humanity," she tells them, "and we Zimbabweans are its keepers." A Form 3 pupil from Hwange, on a school trip, is surprised: how can a place in Zimbabwe belong to the whole world?

The answer lies in the idea of a **World Heritage Site**: a place so important that the international community agrees it must be protected for all people and all future generations. Zimbabwe has five such sites, more than many larger countries. In this lesson you will learn what World Heritage status means, which body grants it, why each of Zimbabwe's sites was listed, what the country gains and what threatens the sites. You should already know what national monuments are and the history of Great Zimbabwe and the Torwa state.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="World Heritage Site"}
A place of outstanding value to all humanity, natural or cultural, that is listed by UNESCO and that the country concerned promises to protect.
:::

:::definition{term="UNESCO"}
The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, the UN agency that keeps the World Heritage List.
:::

:::definition{term="Cultural heritage site"}
A place listed because of what people built, made or believed there, such as ancient stone buildings, rock art or sacred landscapes.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural heritage site"}
A place listed because of its landforms, plants, animals or natural beauty, such as a waterfall or a wildlife area.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Zimbabwe's five World Heritage Sites (year listed)"}
- **Mana Pools National Park, Sapi and Chewore Safari Areas** (1984), natural
- **Great Zimbabwe National Monument** (1986), cultural
- **Khami Ruins National Monument** (1986), cultural
- **Mosi-oa-Tunya / Victoria Falls** (1989), natural, shared with Zambia
- **Matobo Hills** (2003), cultural
:::

## How a site becomes World Heritage

Countries that sign the World Heritage Convention (adopted by UNESCO in 1972) can nominate places they believe have **outstanding universal value**. Experts study the nomination, and the World Heritage Committee decides whether to add the place to the list. Once a site is listed, the country promises to protect it, to report on its condition and to manage it well. If a site is badly damaged, it can be placed on a "List of World Heritage in Danger" so that the world pays attention and helps.

In Zimbabwe, the cultural sites are managed by National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe, while the natural sites in national parks are managed by the Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority. Local authorities, communities and tour operators also play a part.

## Zimbabwe's World Heritage Sites

| Site | Type, year | Where | Why it was listed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mana Pools, Sapi and Chewore | Natural, 1984 | Middle Zambezi valley, northern Zimbabwe | Floodplain pools of the Zambezi and large numbers of wild animals such as elephants, buffalo, hippos and crocodiles in a largely unspoilt landscape |
| Great Zimbabwe | Cultural, 1986 | Near Masvingo | The stone enclosures of the capital of a powerful state, built without mortar; evidence of African achievement in building, trade and government |
| Khami | Cultural, 1986 | Near Bulawayo | Stone platforms of the Torwa capital, which followed Great Zimbabwe; finds of imported goods show trade with distant lands |
| Victoria Falls / Mosi-oa-Tunya | Natural, 1989 | On the Zambezi, on the border with Zambia | One of the world's greatest waterfalls, with its spray, gorges and the rainforest the spray supports; the name means "the smoke that thunders" |
| Matobo Hills | Cultural, 2003 | South of Bulawayo | Granite hills and balancing rocks with one of the richest collections of rock art in southern Africa, and a living religious landscape with shrines still in use |

Notice the variety: two natural sites linked to the Zambezi, two ancient stone-built capitals and one landscape where nature, art and religion meet. The Matobo Hills was listed as a cultural site because of the long relationship between people and the hills, shown in the rock paintings and in the shrines that communities still use.

:::example{title="Which type of site?"}
A Form 4 pupil is asked to classify Mana Pools and the Matobo Hills.

1. **Mana Pools:** listed for its pools, floodplain and wildlife: a **natural** site.
2. **Matobo Hills:** the granite scenery is striking, but it was listed for its rock art and living religious traditions: a **cultural** site.
3. **Lesson:** classify by the reason for listing, not by whether a place is outdoors.
:::

## Benefits of World Heritage status

- **Tourism and income.** International recognition attracts visitors who pay park and entry fees and spend money on hotels, guides, crafts and transport. Victoria Falls is one of the country's busiest tourist destinations.
- **Jobs.** Tourism creates employment for guides, rangers, hotel staff, craft sellers and drivers.
- **Conservation support.** Listing can bring expert advice, training and sometimes funding for protection.
- **Pride and identity.** Listing tells the world that Zimbabwe's history and nature are of global importance, which builds national pride.
- **Education and research.** Scientists, archaeologists and students from Zimbabwe and abroad study the sites.
- **Cooperation.** Sharing Victoria Falls with Zambia encourages the two countries to cooperate in managing it.

## Threats to the sites

| Threat | Example of how it affects a site |
|---|---|
| Poaching | Illegal hunting reduces wildlife in the Zambezi valley |
| Weathering and plant growth | Rain, roots and creepers loosen stones in ancient walls |
| Vandalism and theft | Graffiti on rock art; removal of stones or artefacts |
| Pressure from tourism and building | Crowds wear paths; hotels and roads built too close change the setting |
| Mining and other development proposals | Exploration near protected areas could damage landscapes and wildlife |
| Fire and invasive plants | Uncontrolled fires burn vegetation; plants from elsewhere crowd out local species |
| Drought and climate change | Low river flows reduce the spectacle of the Falls and the water available to wildlife |

:::example{title="Weighing tourism at the Falls"}
A fictional tour company in Victoria Falls plans a new lodge with a view of the gorge.

1. **Benefit:** more beds, more visitors and jobs for local people.
2. **Risk:** buildings close to the Falls spoil the natural setting, increase waste and put pressure on the rainforest.
3. **Balanced answer:** build further back, follow the management plan for the site and limit numbers, so that income grows without destroying the value for which the Falls was listed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every monument a World Heritage Site"}
Zimbabwe has five World Heritage Sites. Naletale, Domboshava and the National Heroes Acre are national heritage, not World Heritage Sites.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the years"}
Mana Pools 1984; Great Zimbabwe and Khami 1986; Victoria Falls 1989; Matobo Hills 2003. Learn them in order.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that the Falls are shared"}
Mosi-oa-Tunya / Victoria Falls is a single site shared by Zimbabwe and Zambia.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name a site and give one fact, e.g. "Khami, near Bulawayo, listed in 1986".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the significance** (Paper 2 Section A): link a benefit to a named site and develop it, e.g. "Victoria Falls attracts international tourists, bringing foreign currency and jobs".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Assess the importance** (Paper 2 Section B): weigh the benefits against the costs and threats (crowding, damage, uneven sharing of income with local communities) and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A World Heritage Site is a place of outstanding universal value, listed by UNESCO under the 1972 World Heritage Convention.
- Zimbabwe has five: Mana Pools, Sapi and Chewore (1984), Great Zimbabwe (1986), Khami (1986), Victoria Falls/Mosi-oa-Tunya (1989, shared with Zambia) and the Matobo Hills (2003).
- Two are natural and three are cultural.
- Benefits: tourism income, jobs, conservation support, pride, research and international cooperation.
- Threats: poaching, weathering, vandalism, tourism pressure, development, fire, invasive plants and drought.
:::
