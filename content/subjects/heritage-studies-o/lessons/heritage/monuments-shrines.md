## Introduction

A Form 3 class from Gweru sets off on a school trip. Their first stop is Naletale, where they run their hands along a stone wall decorated with chevron and chequer patterns laid by builders hundreds of years ago. On the way home they pass a small provincial heroes acre where wreaths have been laid on a grave. The guide on the bus says, "Both places are part of our heritage, but people visit them for different reasons."

This lesson explains that difference. **Monuments** are mostly places and structures we keep because of their history, beauty or scientific value. **Shrines** are places held sacred, where people honour the dead or approach God and the ancestors. You will learn examples of both, why they matter, who looks after them and how every citizen can help to protect them. You should already know what heritage and national symbols are, and have met Great Zimbabwe in the history topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="National monument"}
A place, structure or object of historical, cultural, archaeological or natural importance that is protected by law for the whole nation, for example the Great Zimbabwe walls or Chinhoyi Caves.
:::

:::definition{term="Shrine"}
A sacred place where people remember and honour the dead, or worship and pray, for example the National Heroes Acre or a traditional rain shrine.
:::

:::definition{term="Archaeological site"}
A place where people lived or worked in the past and left remains (walls, tools, pottery, rock paintings) that can be studied.
:::

:::definition{term="Vandalism"}
Deliberately damaging or destroying property, such as writing graffiti on rock paintings or removing stones from an ancient wall.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Who looks after monuments"}
- **National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe (NMMZ)** is the state body that manages national museums and protects national monuments and archaeological sites.
- Damaging, defacing or removing objects from a protected monument is against the law.
- The **National Heroes Acre** is in Harare; each province also has a provincial heroes acre, and districts have their own heroes acres.
:::

## Examples of national monuments

| Monument | Location | Why it is important |
|---|---|---|
| Great Zimbabwe | Masvingo (near Masvingo town) | Stone enclosures of the capital of the Great Zimbabwe state; the largest ancient stone buildings south of the Sahara; source of the soapstone Zimbabwe Birds |
| Khami | Bulawayo area (west of the city) | Stone platforms of the capital of the Torwa state, which followed Great Zimbabwe in the south-west |
| Naletale | Midlands | A stone wall famous for its decorative patterns (chevron, chequer and herringbone) |
| Danangombe (Dhlo Dhlo) | Midlands | Another stone-walled site of the south-western tradition |
| Ziwa | Manicaland (Nyanga) | Terraces, pit structures and settlement remains on the hills |
| Chinhoyi Caves | Mashonaland West | Limestone caves with a deep blue pool; a natural monument with history and legend attached |
| Domboshava | Mashonaland East (near Harare) | A granite hill with rock paintings made by San hunter-gatherers |

Rock paintings are found in many parts of the country, especially in the Matobo Hills. They show animals, people and scenes of hunting and ritual, and they are a record of the San (also called the Khoisan or BaTwa), the earliest inhabitants of the land.

## Examples of shrines

| Shrine | What it is | How it is used |
|---|---|---|
| National Heroes Acre, Harare | Burial ground for people declared national heroes, with a tomb of the Unknown Soldier and a statue of fighters | Burials of national heroes; Heroes Day commemorations; visits by schools and foreign guests |
| Provincial and district heroes acres | Burial grounds for those declared heroes at provincial or district level | Local commemorations on Heroes Day |
| Njelele (Matobo Hills) | A shrine of the Mwari/Mwali religion in the Matobo Hills | Rain-asking and other rituals led by custodians of the shrine |
| Family graves and sacred hills, pools and forests | Places linked to ancestors (midzimu/amadlozi) and spirit mediums | Ceremonies such as kurova guva/umbuyiso and mukwerera/ukucela izulu (rain-asking) |

Christians, Muslims and members of other faiths also have holy places, such as churches, mosques and the sites of large annual gatherings. Heritage Studies treats them all with equal respect: a sacred place is important to the people who hold it sacred.

:::example{title="Monument or shrine?"}
A pupil is asked whether Chinhoyi Caves and the National Heroes Acre are monuments or shrines.

1. **Chinhoyi Caves:** protected mainly for its natural beauty and history, and visited by tourists: a **monument**.
2. **National Heroes Acre:** a burial ground where the nation honours its heroes: a **shrine** (it is also a national monument).
3. **Conclusion:** some places are both. The test is the main purpose: preserving the past (monument) or honouring the dead and the sacred (shrine).
:::

## Why monuments and shrines are significant

- **History.** They are evidence of how people lived, built and governed in the past. Great Zimbabwe proved that Africans built a powerful state and great stone buildings, a fact some colonial writers tried to deny.
- **Identity and pride.** They show Zimbabweans where they come from; the country itself is named after Great Zimbabwe.
- **Religion and culture.** Shrines are places where people pray, ask for rain and honour ancestors and heroes.
- **Remembrance.** Heroes acres keep alive the memory of those who sacrificed for independence.
- **Tourism and income.** Visitors pay entry fees and spend money on guides, crafts, food and lodging, which creates jobs for local people.
- **Education and research.** Schools, archaeologists and historians use them as outdoor classrooms and sources of evidence.

## Threats and protection

Monuments and shrines face many threats: vandalism and graffiti, theft of artefacts, people climbing on or taking stones from walls, uncontrolled fires and plants growing into walls, erosion and weathering, too many visitors in one place, and development such as mining or roads close to a site. Some sacred sites are also disturbed when visitors ignore the rules of the custodians.

| Who | How they protect heritage sites |
|---|---|
| National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe | Declares and manages monuments, employs guides and guards, fences sites, repairs walls, runs museums and research |
| The government and Parliament | Makes and enforces laws against damage and theft; funds the heritage sector |
| Local communities and traditional leaders | Guard sacred places, keep custom-based rules, report damage, guide visitors |
| Schools and citizens | Learn about sites, visit respectfully, keep to paths, never write on walls or remove objects, report vandalism |

:::example{title="A community protects its rock paintings"}
In a fictional village near Marondera, herd boys shelter from the rain under a granite overhang and light fires there; the smoke begins to blacken old paintings on the rock.

1. **Threat identified:** fire and smoke damage to rock art.
2. **Community action:** the village head and the school explain why the paintings matter, and the community agrees a rule against fires in the shelter.
3. **Official action:** the case is reported to the regional office of National Museums and Monuments, which can advise on protection.
4. **Result:** local people become guardians of the site.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating every old place as a shrine"}
A shrine is sacred. Khami is a monument; Njelele is a shrine. Ask what the place is mainly used for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing national monuments with World Heritage Sites"}
All World Heritage Sites in Zimbabwe are national heritage, but most national monuments (such as Naletale or Domboshava) are not World Heritage Sites.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing significance without explaining"}
"Tourism" alone earns little. Explain: "Tourists pay entry fees and buy crafts, which brings income and jobs to the area."
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name a monument or shrine and its location, e.g. "Khami, near Bulawayo".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain the significance** (Paper 2 Section A): give a reason (history, identity, religion, tourism, education) and develop it with an example from a named site.
:::

:::exam-tip
**Discuss** or **how far** (Paper 2 Section B): weigh the value of monuments and shrines against the threats and the difficulty of protecting them, then give a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Monuments are protected places of history, culture or nature; shrines are sacred places for honouring the dead or worship.
- Examples: Great Zimbabwe, Khami, Naletale, Ziwa, Chinhoyi Caves, Domboshava; the National Heroes Acre, provincial heroes acres and Njelele.
- They matter for history, identity, religion, remembrance, tourism and education.
- Threats include vandalism, theft, fire, weathering, overcrowding and development.
- National Museums and Monuments of Zimbabwe protects them, with the help of laws, communities, schools and citizens.
:::
