## Introduction

On the morning of 18 April, a family in Bulawayo switches on the radio. The grandmother, who was a young woman in 1980, tells her grandchildren how people danced in the streets on the first Independence Day. Her grandson asks why the day is still a public holiday more than forty years later. "Because," she says, "a nation that forgets how it was born forgets who it is."

National events are the days on which a whole nation stops to remember, give thanks and celebrate together. Some recall great achievements, such as independence; others honour people who sacrificed for the country, or celebrate unity, Africa or the environment. In this lesson you will learn the main national events of Zimbabwe, their dates, what they commemorate and how they are celebrated, and you will think about why it matters that citizens take part. You should already know the outline of the liberation struggle and the meaning of the national symbols.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="National event"}
An occasion observed by the whole nation, usually on a fixed date, to remember, honour or celebrate something important to the country.
:::

:::definition{term="Commemorate"}
To honour and remember a person or event publicly, for example by holding a ceremony on its anniversary.
:::

:::definition{term="Public holiday"}
A day set by law on which most workplaces and schools close so that people can observe a national event or rest.
:::

:::definition{term="Patriotism"}
Love of and loyalty to one's country, shown by respecting and contributing to it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **Independence Day:** 18 April (independence in 1980)
- **Africa Day:** 25 May (founding of the Organisation of African Unity in 1963)
- **Heroes Day:** the second Monday of August
- **Defence Forces Day:** the Tuesday after Heroes Day
- **National Tree Planting Day:** the first Saturday of December
- **Unity Day:** 22 December (the Unity Accord of 1987)
:::

## The main national events

| Event | Date | What it commemorates | How it is commonly observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independence Day | 18 April | Zimbabwe's independence on 18 April 1980, after the liberation struggle | National celebrations at a stadium with the raising of the flag, speeches, music, dance and parades; provincial and community celebrations; sports competitions; church services |
| Africa Day | 25 May | The founding of the Organisation of African Unity (OAU) on 25 May 1963, now continued by the African Union | Talks, cultural events and school activities on African unity and history |
| Heroes Day | Second Monday of August | The men and women who died or sacrificed in the liberation struggle | Main ceremony at the National Heroes Acre in Harare; ceremonies at provincial and district heroes acres; laying of wreaths |
| Defence Forces Day | Tuesday after Heroes Day | The role of the Zimbabwe Defence Forces in defending the country | Displays and parades by the defence forces, usually at a national stadium |
| National Tree Planting Day | First Saturday of December | The importance of trees and forests | Tree planting in schools, communities and workplaces; a "tree of the year" is often chosen to promote |
| Unity Day | 22 December | The Unity Accord of 22 December 1987 between ZANU (PF) and PF-ZAPU | Speeches and events promoting peace and national unity |

Other public holidays, such as New Year's Day, Good Friday and Easter, Workers' Day (1 May) and Christmas, are also observed, but the events in the table are the ones most closely linked to national heritage.

## Unity Day and remembering hard history

Unity Day marks the agreement by which the two main nationalist parties of the liberation struggle united. It followed years of conflict in the 1980s, including the disturbances in Matabeleland and parts of the Midlands (often called Gukurahundi), in which many civilians died. The Accord ended that conflict and is remembered as a step towards peace. Commemorating it honestly means celebrating unity while also acknowledging the suffering that came before it, which is part of the work of national healing and reconciliation.

:::example{title="Why two days in August?"}
A Form 2 pupil asks why there are two holidays together in August.

1. **Heroes Day** (Monday) honours those who died or sacrificed in the liberation struggle, especially at the heroes acres.
2. **Defence Forces Day** (Tuesday) recognises the armed forces that defend the independence those heroes won.
3. **Link:** together they connect the past (the struggle) with the present (defending sovereignty).
:::

## Why taking part matters

- **Unity.** People of every language, region, faith and background celebrate together, which strengthens their sense of being one nation.
- **Remembrance.** Heroes Day and liberation commemorations honour those who died, and keep their story alive for young people.
- **Patriotism and pride.** Celebrating independence builds love of the country and pride in what has been achieved.
- **Education.** Speeches, school activities and exhibitions teach history and values to the young.
- **Peace and reconciliation.** Unity Day reminds citizens that peace and tolerance must be protected.
- **Africa and the world.** Africa Day connects Zimbabweans to the wider African family and to the ideals of the African Union.
- **Environment.** Tree Planting Day turns heritage into action that benefits future generations.
- **Culture and economy.** National events showcase music, dance and dress, and bring business to vendors, transport operators and artists.

## Taking part in practice

Citizens can take part in many ways: attending national or local celebrations, joining school activities, laying wreaths, planting a tree, observing a minute of silence, displaying the flag correctly, or simply learning and talking about what the day means. Taking part should be voluntary and respectful. In a diverse society, people may remember the same event in different ways; respecting those differences is itself a sign of unity.

:::example{title="A school plans Tree Planting Day"}
A fictional school in Lupane plans its Tree Planting Day.

1. **Choose trees:** indigenous fruit and shade trees suited to the area.
2. **Organise:** each class plants and names a tree and makes a rota to water it through the dry season.
3. **Link to heritage:** a teacher explains which trees are traditionally protected and why, connecting the event to indigenous knowledge.
4. **Result:** the event becomes an ongoing responsibility rather than a single day.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the dates"}
Unity Day is 22 December, not 18 April. Africa Day is 25 May. Heroes Day moves each year because it is the second Monday of August.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the celebration but not the significance"}
"People sing and dance" describes how the event is observed. An *explain* question also needs *why* it matters: unity, remembrance, patriotism.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Heroes Day with Independence Day"}
Independence Day celebrates the birth of the nation; Heroes Day honours those who sacrificed to win it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Independence Day (18 April) celebrates independence in 1980; Africa Day (25 May) recalls the founding of the OAU in 1963.
- Heroes Day (second Monday of August) honours those who sacrificed in the liberation struggle; Defence Forces Day follows on the Tuesday.
- National Tree Planting Day is the first Saturday of December; Unity Day (22 December) marks the Unity Accord of 1987.
- Events are observed with ceremonies, speeches, music, wreath-laying, tree planting and school activities.
- Taking part builds unity, remembrance, patriotism and knowledge of history.
:::
