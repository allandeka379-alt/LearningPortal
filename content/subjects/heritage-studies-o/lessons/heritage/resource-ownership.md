## Introduction

In a village in the Zambezi valley, the school has a new block of classrooms. A signboard explains that it was built with money the rural district council earned from wildlife: safari hunters and tourists paid to visit the area, and part of the money came back to the community. Not far away, in a mining district, residents complain that a large mine has operated near their homes for years, yet the road to the clinic is still unpaved. Both stories raise the same question: **who owns Zimbabwe's natural resources, and who benefits from them?**

This lesson looks at that question over time. You will see how ownership of land, minerals, wildlife, forests and water was organised in the colonial era, what governments have done since 1980 to give indigenous Zimbabweans more access to these resources, and how well those efforts have worked. This is a topic on which people hold different views, so you will learn to weigh evidence and give a balanced judgement. You should already know what natural resources are and why they are heritage, and have met the colonial land laws in the history topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ownership"}
The legal right to possess, use and control a resource and to decide who else may use it.
:::

:::definition{term="Access"}
The ability to use a resource or to benefit from it (for example to graze cattle, collect firewood, mine gold or share in income), whether or not one owns it.
:::

:::definition{term="Indigenisation"}
Policies that increase the share of the economy, including resource-based businesses, owned and controlled by indigenous Zimbabweans.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic empowerment"}
Giving people who were previously excluded the means (ownership, skills, credit, markets) to take part fully in the economy.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- **CAMPFIRE** (Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources) began in **1989**: rural district councils manage wildlife in communal areas and share the income with communities.
- The **Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act** was passed in **2007**; it required most larger businesses to be at least 51 per cent owned by indigenous Zimbabweans, a rule later narrowed by amendments.
- The **Water Act of 1998** ended private ownership of water: water is held by the state, and users obtain permits.
- The Constitution's national objectives (Chapter 2) call for local communities to benefit from the resources in their areas.
:::

## Ownership in the colonial era (outline)

| Resource | How ownership and access were organised under colonial rule |
|---|---|
| Land | The Land Apportionment Act (1930) and later the Land Tenure Act (1969) divided land by race; most of the best-watered, most fertile land was reserved for white settlers, and Africans were crowded into reserves (later Tribal Trust Lands) |
| Minerals | Mineral rights were claimed by the British South Africa Company on the basis of the Rudd Concession, and later held by the colonial state; large mines were owned mainly by foreign and settler companies, with Africans employed as labourers |
| Wildlife | Game laws made hunting by Africans illegal in most cases, while parks and hunting were run for the benefit of the settler state and visitors |
| Forests | The best forests were reserved by the state or owned by settlers and companies; Africans' traditional use was restricted |
| Water | Rights to river water were tied to land ownership, so large farms held most rights |

The details of land expropriation belong to the history of colonisation; here, the key point is that the majority were owners of very little and had limited, controlled access to the country's wealth.

## Efforts since 1980

| Area | Main efforts | Intended benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Minerals and business | Indigenisation and Economic Empowerment Act (2007); community share ownership trusts in mining areas; registration and support for small-scale and artisanal miners; state mining companies; government buying of gold from small producers | Indigenous ownership of mines and businesses; income for communities near mines; legal livelihoods for small miners |
| Wildlife | CAMPFIRE (from 1989): councils earn income from safari hunting and tourism and share it with villages | Communities benefit from wildlife and so protect it |
| Forests | Community woodlots; local management of forests with the Forestry Commission; permits for timber harvesting | Fair access to timber and firewood with conservation |
| Water | Water Act (1998); catchment councils; the Zimbabwe National Water Authority; dams and irrigation schemes in communal areas | Water for all users, not only large landowners |
| The Constitution (2013) | National objectives on empowerment and on communities benefiting from local resources; environmental rights (section 73); devolution to provinces and local authorities | A legal basis for fairer access and sharing |
| Land | Land redistribution (studied separately) | Wider access to farmland |

:::example{title="How CAMPFIRE works in a ward"}
In a fictional ward in the Zambezi valley:

1. The rural district council has authority over the wildlife in the ward.
2. A safari operator pays the council for a hunting quota and photographic tourists pay fees.
3. The council keeps a share for administration and passes most of the rest to the ward.
4. The ward committee decides with residents how to spend it: a borehole, school fees for orphans, a grinding mill or cash dividends.
5. Because wildlife now earns money for them, residents have a reason to report poachers and tolerate elephants.
:::

## How effective have the efforts been?

People disagree about this, and a good answer gives both sides.

**Recognised benefits**

- Many more indigenous Zimbabweans now own and manage mines, businesses and farms than in 1980.
- Small-scale and artisanal miners, many of them local people, have become important producers of gold.
- CAMPFIRE brought income for classrooms, clinics, boreholes and grinding mills in some districts, and helped change attitudes to wildlife.
- Water reform ended the link between land ownership and water rights.
- Communities and young people became more aware of their right to benefit from local resources.

**Recognised problems**

- Benefits have often gone to a small, well-connected group rather than to ordinary people (sometimes called **elite capture**), and there have been cases of corruption.
- Some community share ownership trusts received far less money than promised, and residents near some mines, including people moved from the diamond fields, have complained of receiving little benefit.
- Critics argue that the 51 per cent rule discouraged foreign investment at a time when the economy needed capital, which is one reason it was later narrowed.
- Illegal mining has caused land degradation, river siltation and violence in some areas.
- In CAMPFIRE areas, income has varied with tourism and hunting markets, and crop losses and danger from wild animals remain a burden on villagers.
- Weak enforcement and limited funds affect forestry and water management.

:::example{title="Weighing evidence from two villages"}
Thandeka compares two fictional villages for a project.

1. **Village A** (wildlife area): the ward received regular CAMPFIRE income and built a clinic, but elephants destroyed part of the maize crop.
2. **Village B** (mining area): a share ownership trust was set up, but residents say they have seen little money, and the river is silted by illegal diggings.
3. **Judgement:** the policies widened access in law and sometimes in practice, but how much ordinary people benefit depends on honest management, fair sharing and enforcement.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing ownership with access"}
A villager may not own the wildlife but can still have access to its benefits through CAMPFIRE. Use the right word.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
Describing only successes or only failures will not reach the top level. Give recognised benefits and problems, then judge.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about land"}
Land redistribution is a separate topic. Here, include minerals, wildlife, forests and water.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In the colonial era, land, minerals, wildlife, forests and water were owned or controlled mainly by the settler state, settlers and foreign companies.
- Since 1980, measures to widen access include indigenisation and economic empowerment (2007), community share ownership trusts, support for small-scale miners, CAMPFIRE (1989), the Water Act (1998) and the 2013 Constitution's objectives.
- Benefits: wider indigenous ownership, community income from wildlife, fairer water rights, legal livelihoods.
- Problems: elite capture, corruption, broken promises to communities, reduced investment, illegal mining and human-wildlife conflict.
- A balanced judgement weighs both and recognises that results depend on fair, honest management.
:::
