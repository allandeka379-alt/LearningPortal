## Introduction

Every group of people lives by rules. At home you may have to be in before dark and wash your own plate. At school there are rules about uniform, punctuality and respect for teachers. In a village, the headman may say that cattle must be kept out of the fields until the harvest is in. In town, the council says where vendors may sell and when refuse is collected.

Some of these rules are only family or school rules. Others are **laws**, made by bodies with legal authority and enforced by the state through the police and the courts. Knowing the difference, knowing where Zimbabwe's laws come from and knowing which court deals with which case helps you to protect your own rights and respect the rights of others.

This Form 2 lesson builds on what you know about the Constitution as the supreme law. How Parliament passes a bill is studied with central government; here you learn the **types of law**, the **courts** and the **rule of law**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rule"}
A guide to behaviour set by a family, school, club, church or workplace. Breaking it may lead to a punishment inside that group, but not to a court case.
:::

:::definition{term="Law"}
A rule made or recognised by the state, which applies to everyone in the country and is enforced by the police and the courts.
:::

:::definition{term="By-law"}
A law made by a local authority (an urban council or a rural district council) that applies only in its area.
:::

:::definition{term="Jurisdiction"}
The power of a court to hear a particular kind of case, in a particular area or up to a particular level of seriousness.
:::

:::definition{term="Rule of law"}
The principle that everyone, including the government and its leaders, is under the law, that laws are known in advance and that disputes are settled by independent courts.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
The **Constitution of Zimbabwe (2013)** is the supreme law (section 2). **Chapter 8** deals with the judiciary, and **section 162** lists the courts: the Constitutional Court, the Supreme Court, the High Court, the Labour Court, the Administrative Court, the magistrates courts and the customary law courts. The **Chief Justice** heads the judiciary.
:::

## Rules and laws compared

Rules and laws both guide behaviour, but they differ in who makes them, who must obey them and what happens when they are broken.

| Feature | Rule | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Made by | A family, school, church, club or employer | Parliament, a minister under an Act, a local authority, or recognised custom and court decisions |
| Applies to | Members of that group only | Everyone in the country or area |
| Enforced by | Parents, teachers, leaders of the group | The police, prosecutors and courts |
| Penalty | Detention, a fine inside the club, losing a privilege | A fine, imprisonment, community service or an order to pay compensation |
| Example | "No phones in class" | "Do not drive without a licence" |

A school rule must still respect the law. A school cannot, for example, make a rule that denies a learner the protections the Constitution gives to children.

## Sources and types of law in Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe's law comes from several sources. They fit together in a clear order: the Constitution is at the top, and any other law that conflicts with it is invalid to the extent of the conflict.

| Source | Who makes it | Example of what it covers |
|---|---|---|
| The Constitution | Adopted by the people in the 2013 referendum; amended only by special procedures | Rights, the arms of government, elections, citizenship |
| Acts of Parliament (statutes) | Parliament, signed into law by the President | Crimes, marriage, labour, the environment |
| Statutory instruments | Ministers and other authorities, using powers given by an Act | Detailed regulations, such as fees or road rules |
| By-laws | Urban councils and rural district councils | Vending sites, building plans, refuse, dog licences |
| Customary law | The customs and practices of communities, recognised by the courts | Some family, inheritance and community matters |
| Common law | Roman-Dutch law received in the colonial period and developed by judges' decisions | Contracts, damages, many general principles |

Two further points help in exams. First, **case law** (judicial precedent) means that lower courts follow the decisions of higher courts on the same point of law. Second, customary law applies only where it is consistent with the Constitution: a custom that discriminates against women or harms children cannot be enforced.

:::example{title="Which law applies?"}
Nyasha sells fruit at a street corner in Gweru. A municipal officer tells her to move to the designated market.

1. **The rule she is said to have broken** is a by-law of the city council about where vending is allowed.
2. **Where the council's power comes from:** an Act of Parliament gives urban councils the power to make by-laws.
3. **The limit on that power:** the by-law must not conflict with an Act or with the Constitution.
4. **If she disagrees:** she may raise the matter with her ward councillor or, if she is charged, defend herself in a magistrates court.
:::

## The courts of Zimbabwe

The courts make up the **judiciary**, the third arm of government. They interpret the law and settle disputes between people, between people and organisations, and between citizens and the state. The Constitution says the courts are independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law.

| Court | Main jurisdiction (in outline) |
|---|---|
| Constitutional Court | The highest court on constitutional matters: whether a law or an action is consistent with the Constitution, and disputes about rights |
| Supreme Court | The final court of appeal in non-constitutional matters; hears appeals from the High Court and other courts |
| High Court | Hears serious criminal and civil cases throughout Zimbabwe; hears appeals and reviews from magistrates courts |
| Labour Court | Disputes between employers and employees, such as unfair dismissal |
| Administrative Court | Appeals against decisions of certain public authorities and officials under particular Acts |
| Magistrates courts | Most everyday criminal cases and smaller civil claims, in towns across the country |
| Customary law courts | Local disputes under customary law, such as some family and community matters, presided over by traditional leaders |

The courts form a **hierarchy**. A person who loses a case in a lower court can usually appeal to a higher one. A case may start in a magistrates court, go on appeal to the High Court and then to the Supreme Court. If it raises a question about the Constitution, it may go to the Constitutional Court.

:::example{title="Following a case up the hierarchy"}
Themba, a worker in Bulawayo, is dismissed and believes it was unfair.

1. His dispute is first handled through the labour dispute procedures and then the **Labour Court**, which specialises in employment matters.
2. If either side believes the Labour Court got the law wrong, the law allows an appeal to the **Supreme Court**.
3. If Themba argues that a law used against him breaks a right in the Declaration of Rights, the **Constitutional Court** can decide that question.
4. **Lesson:** each court has its own jurisdiction, and higher courts correct the mistakes of lower ones.
:::

### Who works in the courts?

Judges sit in the higher courts and magistrates in the magistrates courts. Prosecutors present criminal cases on behalf of the state, and lawyers represent the parties. The Judicial Service Commission has an important role in the appointment of judges and in the running of the courts. Traditional leaders preside over customary law courts in their areas.

## Why the rule of law matters

The founding values in section 3 of the Constitution include the rule of law. It matters because:

- **It protects rights.** No one can be punished except under a law and after a fair hearing.
- **It limits power.** Leaders and officials must act within the law, and the courts can set aside unlawful actions.
- **It brings equality.** Section 56 says every person is equal before the law.
- **It keeps peace and order.** People settle disputes in court instead of by violence.
- **It encourages investment.** Businesses invest where contracts are enforced and property is protected.

Without the rule of law, the strong could ignore the rights of the weak, and citizens would have no peaceful way to challenge unfair treatment.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every rule a law"}
A school or family rule is not a law. A law is made or recognised by the state and applies to everyone; breaking it can lead to a court case.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the arms of government"}
Parliament makes Acts, the Executive carries them out, and the courts interpret them and settle disputes. Courts do not make Acts of Parliament.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the Supreme Court and the Constitutional Court"}
The Constitutional Court is the highest court on constitutional matters; the Supreme Court is the final court of appeal in other matters.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rules guide behaviour in groups; laws are made or recognised by the state and enforced by courts.
- Zimbabwe's law comes from the Constitution, Acts, statutory instruments, by-laws, customary law and common law.
- Section 162 lists the courts, from the Constitutional Court down to customary law courts.
- Courts form a hierarchy, and appeals go from lower to higher courts.
- The rule of law puts everyone, including the government, under the law.
:::
