## Introduction

Chipo turned sixteen last month, and today she is queueing at the Registrar General's office in Masvingo with her mother, her birth certificate in hand. She is applying for her national identity card. Ahead of her in the queue, a man is collecting a passport so that he can visit his sister in Botswana, and a woman who was born in Malawi but has lived in Zimbabwe for many years is asking how she can become a citizen. Each of them is dealing, in a different way, with **citizenship**.

Citizenship is the legal bond between a person and a country. It gives the person rights and privileges and places duties on him or her. In this lesson you will learn what citizenship means, the three ways of becoming a Zimbabwean citizen set out in Chapter 3 of the Constitution, what citizens are entitled to and expected to do, how citizenship is proved, and why it matters. You should already know that the Constitution is the supreme law and that Chapter 4 protects rights.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Citizenship"}
The legal status of being a member of a country, which gives a person rights and privileges and places duties on him or her.
:::

:::definition{term="Citizen"}
A person who is legally recognised as a member of a country, for example a Zimbabwean citizen.
:::

:::definition{term="Foreign national"}
A person who lives in or visits a country but is a citizen of another country.
:::

:::definition{term="Stateless person"}
A person who is not recognised as a citizen by any country, and who may therefore struggle to obtain documents, education or work.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- Citizenship is set out in **Chapter 3** of the Constitution (sections 35 to 43).
- There are three ways of becoming a Zimbabwean citizen: **by birth** (section 36), **by descent** (section 37) and **by registration** (section 38).
- Identity documents are issued by the **Registrar General**: birth certificates, national identity cards (from age 16) and passports.
:::

## Ways of becoming a Zimbabwean citizen

| Way | Who qualifies (in outline) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| By birth (section 36) | A person born in Zimbabwe when either parent was a Zimbabwean citizen, or when any grandparent was a Zimbabwean citizen by birth or descent; a young child found in Zimbabwe whose parents and nationality are unknown is presumed to be a citizen by birth; some children born abroad also count, for example when a citizen parent was working outside Zimbabwe for the state | Tendai is born in Mutare to a Zimbabwean mother |
| By descent (section 37) | A person born outside Zimbabwe when a parent or grandparent was a Zimbabwean citizen by birth or descent | Nyasha is born in South Africa to a Zimbabwean father who has settled and works there |
| By registration (section 38) | A person who applies and qualifies, for example by having been married to a Zimbabwean citizen for at least five years, or by having lived in Zimbabwe lawfully and continuously for at least ten years, and who meets the conditions set by an Act of Parliament; a child adopted by a Zimbabwean citizen may also be registered | A woman from Malawi who has lived lawfully in Zimbabwe for over ten years applies to be registered |

Citizenship by birth and by descent are **automatic** in the sense that they come from the facts of a person's birth and family; the person proves them with documents. Citizenship by registration is **applied for**, and the state decides whether the conditions are met.

:::example{title="Which way applies?"}
Three cases are brought to a Form 2 class.

1. **Themba**, born in Bulawayo to Zimbabwean parents: citizen **by birth**.
2. **Rudo**, born in the United Kingdom to a Zimbabwean mother, born in Harare, who had settled and worked in the United Kingdom for many years: citizen **by descent**.
3. **Mr Banda**, born in Zambia, married to a Zimbabwean citizen for six years and living in Kariba: he may apply for citizenship **by registration**.

The key questions are: Where was the person born? Was a parent or grandparent a Zimbabwean citizen? Has the person applied and qualified?
:::

## Rights, privileges and duties of citizens

All citizens are equally entitled to the rights, privileges and benefits of citizenship, and equally subject to its duties. Many rights in the Declaration of Rights belong to **every person** in Zimbabwe, citizen or not, such as the right to life and freedom from torture. Other rights and privileges belong to **citizens** in particular.

| Rights and privileges of citizens | Duties of citizens |
|---|---|
| To vote, from the age of 18, and to stand for public office | To be loyal to Zimbabwe |
| To obtain a passport and national identity documents | To observe the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions |
| To live in, enter and leave Zimbabwe freely | To respect the national flag and national anthem |
| To be protected by the state, including help from embassies when abroad | To defend Zimbabwe and its sovereignty, to the best of their ability |
| To take part fully in public life, for example by forming or joining political parties | To obey the law, pay taxes and take part in community life |

## Proving citizenship: identity documents

| Document | When obtained | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Birth certificate | Soon after birth (registration of the birth) | Proves name, date and place of birth and parents; needed to enrol in school, sit public examinations and obtain an identity card |
| National identity card | From age 16 | Proves identity and citizenship; needed to register as a voter, open a bank account, obtain a passport and much more |
| Passport | When needed for travel | Proves citizenship abroad and allows travel |

People who lack documents face serious problems: they may be unable to sit examinations, register as voters, open a bank account, get a formal job or claim an inheritance. For this reason, section 81 gives every child the right to the prompt provision of a birth certificate, and communities, schools and the Registrar General hold registration drives.

## Loss of citizenship (outline)

The Constitution allows citizenship acquired **by registration** to be revoked in limited circumstances set out in the Constitution, for example if it was obtained by fraud, false representation or concealment of a material fact. Details of other situations are governed by the Constitution and Acts of Parliament.

## Why citizenship matters

- **Identity and belonging:** citizenship gives a person a legal home and a sense of belonging to a nation.
- **Rights and protection:** citizens can claim the protection of the state at home and abroad.
- **Participation:** citizens choose their leaders, stand for office and help shape the nation's future.
- **Access to services and opportunities:** documents linked to citizenship open the way to education, jobs, banking and travel.
- **Responsibility:** citizenship comes with duties that keep the nation united, lawful and secure.

:::example{title="A registration drive"}
A fictional ward in Lupane finds that many children do not have birth certificates.

1. **Problem:** without certificates the children cannot sit public examinations or later obtain identity cards.
2. **Action:** the school, the village heads and the Registrar General's office arrange a registration day at the ward centre, and parents bring clinic cards and witnesses.
3. **Result:** children's rights to a name and documents are upheld, and their path to full citizenship is protected.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing birth and descent"}
By birth: born **in** Zimbabwe. By descent: born **outside** Zimbabwe to a Zimbabwean parent or grandparent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking only citizens have rights"}
Many rights in Chapter 4 belong to every person in Zimbabwe. Voting and a passport, however, are for citizens.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing rights without duties"}
A question on citizenship often asks for both. Make sure you can give duties such as loyalty, obeying the law and respecting national symbols.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Citizenship is the legal bond between a person and a country, with rights, privileges and duties; it is set out in Chapter 3 of the Constitution.
- Zimbabwean citizenship is acquired by birth, by descent or by registration.
- Citizens may vote from 18, stand for office, obtain passports and live in the country freely; they must be loyal, observe the Constitution, respect national symbols, defend the country and obey the law.
- Birth certificates, national identity cards (from 16) and passports, issued by the Registrar General, prove citizenship.
- Citizenship by registration can be revoked in limited cases, such as fraud.
:::
