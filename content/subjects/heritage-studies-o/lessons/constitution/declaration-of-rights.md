## Introduction

Farai's mother runs a small shop in Gweru. One evening she tells the family that a neighbour was arrested and held at a police station for three days without being told why or taken to court. "Can they do that?" asks Farai. His older sister, who is studying law, answers: "Not lawfully. The Constitution says a person who is arrested must be told the reason and brought before a court quickly. That is in the Declaration of Rights."

The **Declaration of Rights** is Chapter 4 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe. It sets out the rights and freedoms that belong to every person in Zimbabwe, says who must respect them, explains when they may be limited and tells people how to defend them. In this lesson you will learn what fundamental rights are, the main rights in Chapter 4, how they are limited and enforced, and why rights come with duties. You should already know the structure of the Constitution and that it is the supreme law.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fundamental human rights and freedoms"}
Basic entitlements that every person has because he or she is human, such as the right to life, equality and freedom of expression, which the state and others must respect.
:::

:::definition{term="Declaration of Rights"}
Chapter 4 of the Constitution of Zimbabwe (sections 44 to 87), which sets out fundamental human rights and freedoms, their application, limitation and enforcement.
:::

:::definition{term="Limitation of rights"}
Restricting a right by a law that applies generally, only as far as is fair, reasonable, necessary and justifiable in a democratic society (section 86).
:::

:::definition{term="Enforcement"}
The means by which a person whose right has been, or is likely to be, violated can obtain protection, for example by approaching a court.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
- The Declaration of Rights is **Chapter 4**, sections **44 to 87**.
- **Section 44:** the state, every person and every institution must respect, protect, promote and fulfil the rights.
- **Section 85:** a person may approach a court if a right is infringed or threatened.
- **Section 86:** rights may be limited only in certain ways, and some rights may never be limited.
- Rights of specific groups: section 80 women, 81 children, 82 the elderly, 83 persons with disabilities, 84 veterans of the liberation struggle.
:::

## Examples of rights in Chapter 4

| Type of right | Examples | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Life and security | Right to life; personal liberty; freedom from torture and slavery | No one may be killed, detained or ill-treated unlawfully |
| Equality | Equality and non-discrimination (section 56) | Everyone is equal before the law; no unfair discrimination on grounds such as sex, race, tribe, religion or disability |
| Freedoms | Expression and the media; assembly and association; conscience and religion; movement | People may speak, meet, form groups, worship and travel freely within the law |
| Political rights | Forming and joining parties; free, fair and regular elections; voting from age 18 | Citizens choose their leaders |
| Justice | Rights of arrested and detained persons; a fair and public hearing | Arrested people are told why, may contact a lawyer and must be brought before a court promptly |
| Property | Right to own property; protection from arbitrary eviction | Property cannot be taken arbitrarily |
| Social and economic | Education (section 75); health care (section 76); food and water; labour rights | The state must take reasonable steps, within its resources, to provide these |
| Environment | A clean and safe environment (section 73) | The environment must be protected for present and future generations |
| Marriage and family | Marriage rights (section 78): no one may be compelled to marry, and the minimum age is 18 | Marriage is by free consent of adults |
| Language and culture | Use of one's language and taking part in cultural life | People may practise their culture in ways consistent with the Constitution |

The Declaration also says that no person may be refused emergency medical treatment in a health institution, and that every citizen and permanent resident has a right to a basic, state-funded education. The rights of children, women, the elderly, persons with disabilities and veterans are covered in more detail in the Rights and Responsibilities topic.

## Limitation of rights

Rights are not unlimited. Freedom of expression does not allow a person to incite violence or destroy another person's reputation with lies; freedom of movement can be restricted to stop the spread of a dangerous disease. Section 86 says a right may be limited only by a **law of general application** and only as far as the limitation is **fair, reasonable, necessary and justifiable** in a democratic society, taking into account things such as the importance of the purpose and whether less restrictive means are available.

Some rights can **never** be limited by any law, including: the right to life (except as section 48 provides), the right to human dignity, freedom from torture and from cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, freedom from slavery or servitude, the right to a fair trial, and the right to habeas corpus (an order that a detained person be brought before a court).

:::example{title="Is this limitation fair?"}
During a disease outbreak, a fictional town council in Kariba bans large gatherings for one month.

1. **Right affected:** freedom of assembly.
2. **Purpose:** protecting life and health, an important aim.
3. **Test:** the ban is set out in a law that applies to everyone, lasts a limited time and is lifted when the danger passes.
4. **Judgement:** such a limitation is likely to be reasonable and justifiable. A permanent ban on all meetings, or one applied only to some groups, would not be.
:::

## Enforcing rights

| Who or what | How it protects rights |
|---|---|
| The courts | Under section 85 any person may approach a court, alone or through others, if a right is infringed or likely to be infringed; the Constitutional Court is the highest court on constitutional matters |
| Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission (Chapter 12) | Promotes awareness of rights, monitors the state of human rights, receives and investigates complaints and recommends action |
| Other independent commissions | The Gender Commission (gender equality), the Media Commission (media freedom) and others protect particular rights |
| Parliament and the media | Debate, question ministers and expose abuses |
| Civil society and citizens | Educate people about rights, give legal help, report abuses |

## Rights and duties

Every right comes with **duties**. Section 44 places a duty on the state and on every person to respect the rights of others. In practice, my freedom of expression carries the duty not to spread hate or falsehoods about others; my right to a clean environment carries the duty not to litter or pollute; my right to education carries the duty to attend and learn; my freedom of religion carries the duty to respect others' beliefs. Citizens also have duties to obey the law, pay taxes and respect national symbols and institutions.

:::example{title="Rudo's complaint"}
Rudo, who uses a wheelchair, is told by a fictional private college in Bulawayo that it will not admit her because "the building has stairs".

1. **Rights involved:** equality and non-discrimination (section 56); the rights of persons with disabilities (section 83); education.
2. **Steps she can take:** raise the matter with the college; complain to the Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission; seek legal help and, if necessary, approach a court.
3. **Duty of the college:** to respect her rights by making reasonable arrangements, such as a ground-floor classroom or a ramp.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all rights are absolute"}
Most rights can be limited under section 86, but only fairly and by general law. A few, such as freedom from torture, can never be limited.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting duties"}
A Section B answer on rights is stronger when it shows that rights depend on everyone respecting others' rights.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing rights with founding values"}
Founding values (section 3) are national principles; the Declaration of Rights (Chapter 4) gives enforceable rights to individuals.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Identify** rights (Paper 1, Paper 2 Section A): name the right precisely, e.g. "freedom of assembly and association".
:::

:::exam-tip
**Explain** a right (Paper 2 Section A): say what it protects, give an example and, where certain, the section.
:::

:::exam-tip
**How far** does the Declaration protect citizens (Paper 2 Section B)? Weigh what the law provides and how it is enforced against problems such as low awareness, cost of legal action and violations, then judge.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Declaration of Rights is Chapter 4 (sections 44-87) of the Constitution.
- It protects rights such as life, liberty, equality, expression, assembly, conscience, political rights, a fair hearing, property, education, health care, a clean environment and marriage by free consent at 18 or over.
- Sections 80-84 protect women, children, the elderly, persons with disabilities and veterans.
- Rights may be limited only by general law that is fair, reasonable, necessary and justifiable (section 86); some rights can never be limited.
- Rights are enforced through the courts (section 85) and the Zimbabwe Human Rights Commission, and they come with duties to respect the rights of others.
:::
