## Introduction

When a new football club is formed in a township in Gweru, the first meeting does not choose the team. It writes rules: who may join, how the committee is elected, how money is kept, what happens when members disagree. Without those rules, the strongest or loudest members would decide everything. A country faces the same problem on a much larger scale, and its most important set of rules is called a **constitution**.

Zimbabwe's current Constitution was made between 2009 and 2013 through a long process in which ordinary citizens were invited to give their views, and was approved in a national referendum. In this lesson you will learn what a constitution is, why every country needs one, why Zimbabwe decided to replace the constitution it had used since independence, the stages by which the 2013 Constitution was made, and the part citizens played. You should already know that Zimbabwe became independent in 1980 and that the Lancaster House conference of 1979 produced the independence constitution.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Constitution"}
The supreme law of a country: a set of basic rules that sets up the institutions of government, shares and limits their powers, and protects the rights of the people.
:::

:::definition{term="Referendum"}
A vote in which all registered voters accept or reject a single proposal, such as a new constitution.
:::

:::definition{term="Select committee"}
A small group of members of Parliament chosen to carry out a particular task and report back to Parliament.
:::

:::definition{term="Outreach"}
Meetings held across the country to collect the views of ordinary citizens.
:::

:::definition{term="Home-grown constitution"}
A constitution written by a country's own people, through its own institutions, rather than negotiated abroad or imposed.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
- **2008:** the Global Political Agreement (GPA) between the main political parties included a commitment to make a new constitution.
- **2009:** Parliament set up a select committee, known as **COPAC**, to lead the process.
- **16 March 2013:** referendum; the draft was approved by a large majority.
- **22 May 2013:** the new Constitution was signed into law; its remaining provisions took effect after the harmonised elections of 2013.
:::

## Why countries need a constitution

- It **sets up the institutions** of government (the Executive, the Legislature and the Judiciary) and says how leaders are chosen.
- It **limits power**: it says what each institution may and may not do, so that no person or body can rule without checks.
- It **protects rights** and freedoms, and says how people can defend them in court.
- It is the **supreme law**: any other law or conduct that is inconsistent with it is invalid.
- It expresses the **values and identity** of the nation: its founding principles, languages and national symbols.
- It provides **stability**: everyone knows the rules, and disputes can be settled peacefully.

## Why Zimbabwe needed a new constitution

The constitution adopted at independence was negotiated at the Lancaster House conference in London in 1979. It brought the war to an end, but over time many Zimbabweans felt it should be replaced:

- It was **negotiated abroad** under the supervision of Britain, with special provisions agreed as part of a peace settlement, rather than written by the people.
- It had been **amended many times**, which made it difficult for citizens to follow.
- Many people wanted **stronger protection of rights**, limits on executive power, independent commissions and the devolution of power to provinces.
- An earlier draft constitution was **rejected in a referendum in February 2000**, so the question of a new constitution remained open.
- After the disputed elections of 2008, the political parties agreed in the GPA that a new constitution should be made by a people-driven process.

## How the 2013 Constitution was made

| Stage | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1. Agreement | The GPA (2008) committed the parties to a new constitution |
| 2. Select committee | Parliament set up COPAC (2009), with members from the parties in Parliament |
| 3. Stakeholders' conference | Representatives of churches, unions, business, women's groups, youth, traditional leaders and others met to discuss the process |
| 4. Outreach | Teams held meetings in wards and districts across the country to record citizens' views on many topics |
| 5. Drafting | Drafters wrote the text using the views collected, and the parties negotiated the disputed areas |
| 6. Second stakeholders' conference and Parliament | The draft was discussed by stakeholders and laid before Parliament |
| 7. Referendum | On 16 March 2013 voters approved the draft |
| 8. Enactment | Parliament passed the Constitution and it was signed into law on 22 May 2013 |

:::example{title="Following one idea through the process"}
At a fictional outreach meeting in Lupane, residents say they want local people to have more say in decisions about their province.

1. **Outreach:** the view is recorded with many similar views from other areas.
2. **Drafting:** the drafters include **devolution** of power to provincial and metropolitan councils and local authorities.
3. **Referendum:** voters approve the whole draft, including devolution.
4. **Lesson:** a view given at a village meeting can become part of the supreme law, which is what "people-driven" means.
:::

## The role of citizens

Citizens played, and can play in future, several roles in constitution-making:

- **Attending outreach meetings** and stating their views honestly and peacefully.
- **Sending written submissions** as individuals, churches, unions, schools or associations.
- **Taking part in civic education**: learning what the draft says and explaining it to others.
- **Voting in the referendum**, after registering, to accept or reject the draft.
- **Monitoring** the process through civil society organisations, the media and observers.
- **Respecting others' views** and avoiding violence or intimidation, so that everyone can speak freely.

Not everyone agreed with every part of the process. Some people felt the outreach meetings were too short or that the parties had too much control over the final text, and some areas had better attendance than others. These criticisms are part of the history. What is widely agreed is that far more citizens took part in making the 2013 Constitution than in making the Lancaster House constitution.

:::example{title="Advising a first-time voter"}
Nokuthula, aged 19, will vote in a referendum for the first time and is unsure what to do.

1. **Register** as a voter in good time.
2. **Read** a summary of the proposal from neutral civic education sources, not rumours.
3. **Discuss** it respectfully with family and friends.
4. **Vote** on the day, freely and in secret.

Her participation helps make the result truly the people's decision.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the dates"}
The referendum was on 16 March 2013; the Constitution was signed into law on 22 May 2013. The rejected draft was in February 2000.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing COPAC as a political party"}
COPAC was a parliamentary select committee, made up of members from the parties in Parliament.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing stages without explaining citizens' roles"}
When asked about the role of citizens, explain what citizens did and why it mattered, not just the stages.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A constitution is the supreme law that sets up government, limits power and protects rights.
- Zimbabwe replaced the Lancaster House constitution because it was negotiated abroad, much amended, and many people wanted stronger rights, limits on power and devolution.
- The process: GPA (2008), COPAC (2009), stakeholders' conferences, outreach, drafting, referendum (16 March 2013) and signing into law (22 May 2013).
- Citizens took part through outreach meetings, submissions, civic education, voting and monitoring.
- The process was criticised by some, but it involved far more citizens than the 1979 constitution.
:::
