## Introduction

When you turn 18 you gain one of the most important rights a citizen has: the right to vote. Every five years Zimbabweans queue at schools, tents and community halls to choose their President, their Member of Parliament and their local councillor. Between those elections, a seat may fall vacant and a by-election is held, or the whole nation may be asked to vote on a single question in a referendum.

This Form 3 lesson explains **how** these elections work: the rules, the institutions and the features that make an election free and fair. It does not deal with which parties took part in particular elections or who won them; Heritage Studies looks at the system, not at party politics. You should already know that the Constitution of 2013 is the supreme law and that government has three arms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Election"}
A process in which registered voters choose people to hold public office, such as the President, Members of Parliament and councillors.
:::

:::definition{term="Electoral system"}
The set of rules and institutions that decide who may vote, how votes are cast and counted, and how votes are turned into seats or offices.
:::

:::definition{term="Constituency"}
An area whose registered voters elect one Member of the National Assembly. A **ward** is a smaller area that elects a councillor.
:::

:::definition{term="By-election"}
An election held to fill a seat that has become vacant between general elections, for example after the death or resignation of the holder.
:::

:::definition{term="Referendum"}
A vote in which all registered voters accept or reject a single proposal, such as a draft constitution.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
**Chapter 7** of the Constitution deals with elections and **Chapter 12** sets up the **Zimbabwe Electoral Commission (ZEC)**. Citizens may vote from the age of **18**. Elections are **harmonised** and held every **five years**. A presidential candidate needs **more than half** of the votes cast; otherwise a **run-off** is held. The National Assembly has **210** constituency seats, plus seats reserved for women elected by proportional representation.
:::

## The Zimbabwe Electoral Commission

ZEC is one of the independent commissions supporting democracy in Chapter 12. It is meant to act impartially and not under the direction of any person or party.

| Function of ZEC | What it involves |
|---|---|
| Voter registration | Registering eligible citizens and keeping the voters' roll up to date |
| Delimitation | Drawing the boundaries of constituencies and wards |
| Running elections and referendums | Setting up polling stations, employing and training polling officers, printing ballot papers |
| Voter education | Teaching citizens how and where to register and vote |
| Accreditation | Accrediting observers and the media to watch the process |
| Results | Counting, collating and announcing results |

## Registering and voting

To vote, a person must be a citizen of Zimbabwe, aged 18 or over, and registered on the voters' roll. Registration usually requires a national identity document and proof of where the person lives, because people vote in the ward and constituency where they are registered.

On polling day, voters go to their polling station. Officials check their names on the roll, they receive ballot papers and mark them in secret behind a screen, and they place them in sealed ballot boxes. After the polls close, ballots are counted at the polling station in the presence of agents of the candidates and observers.

:::example{title="Rudo registers to vote"}
Rudo turned 18 in June and lives in Marondera.

1. **Is she eligible?** Yes: she is a citizen and aged 18.
2. **What does she take to the registration centre?** Her national identity document and proof of residence.
3. **Where will she vote?** At a polling station in the ward where she is registered.
4. **Why it matters:** if she does not register before the roll closes for an election, she cannot vote in it, even though she has the right.
:::

## Harmonised elections

Since 2008 Zimbabwe has held **harmonised elections**: the President, Members of the National Assembly, senators and councillors are all chosen in the same general election, every five years. Voters receive separate ballot papers for each office.

| Office | How it is filled (in outline) |
|---|---|
| President | Elected directly by voters across the whole country; the winner needs more than half of the valid votes cast, or a run-off is held between the two leading candidates |
| National Assembly constituency seats | 210 seats; in each constituency the candidate with the most votes wins (first-past-the-post) |
| Seats reserved for women | Filled by **proportional representation** (PR) from party lists, according to each party's share of the vote |
| Senate | Mostly filled from party lists in each province by PR, together with members representing chiefs and persons with disabilities |
| Local authority councillors | One per ward; the candidate with the most votes wins |

The two main methods are worth comparing. In **first-past-the-post**, the candidate with the most votes in an area wins, even without a majority; it gives each area its own representative. In **proportional representation**, parties win seats in proportion to their votes; it helps make Parliament reflect the whole electorate and is used to increase the number of women.

## By-elections and referendums

A **by-election** fills a single vacant seat between general elections. It keeps the people of that constituency or ward represented. A **referendum** asks voters to decide one question; Zimbabwe held referendums on draft constitutions in February 2000 (the draft was rejected) and on 16 March 2013 (the draft was approved).

## Free and fair elections

An election is **free** when voters can register, campaign and vote without fear or pressure, and **fair** when all candidates compete on equal terms and the result reflects the votes cast.

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Secret ballot | No one can know how a person voted, so voters are not pressured |
| Independent electoral body | Rules are applied impartially to all candidates |
| Accurate and open voters' roll | Every eligible voter can vote once, and no one votes twice |
| Freedom to campaign and assemble | Voters hear all candidates' ideas |
| Fair access to the media | All candidates can reach voters |
| No violence or intimidation | Voters choose freely and safely |
| Observers and party agents | The process can be checked and trusted |
| Transparent counting and results | Results can be verified at each polling station |
| Peaceful settlement of disputes | Complaints go to the courts, not the streets |

:::example{title="Checking a polling station"}
An observer team visits a fictional polling station in Lupane on election day.

1. **Voters mark ballots behind a screen:** the ballot is secret.
2. **Agents of different candidates are present at the count:** counting is transparent.
3. **A voter is turned away because her name is not on the roll:** the team notes a possible problem with the roll, which ZEC can investigate.
4. **Judgement:** the observers report what was done well and what needs improvement, rather than simply saying "free" or "not free".
:::

## Responsibilities of voters

Rights come with responsibilities. Citizens should register, check their details on the voters' roll, learn about candidates' policies, respect other people's choices, vote peacefully and accept lawful procedures for challenging results.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing a by-election with a referendum"}
A by-election fills one vacant seat; a referendum is a national vote on one proposal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the most votes always wins the presidency"}
A presidential candidate needs more than half of the votes cast. If no one gets this, a run-off is held.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about parties instead of the system"}
Exam questions on the electoral system test institutions and processes. Avoid writing about which party won or is right.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An electoral system is the rules and institutions that turn votes into seats and offices.
- ZEC registers voters, draws boundaries, runs elections and referendums and announces results.
- Citizens vote from 18; harmonised elections every five years choose the President, Parliament and councillors.
- The President needs more than half of the votes cast or a run-off; constituency seats use first-past-the-post and reserved seats use proportional representation.
- Free and fair elections need a secret ballot, an independent commission, freedom to campaign, no intimidation and transparent counting.
:::
