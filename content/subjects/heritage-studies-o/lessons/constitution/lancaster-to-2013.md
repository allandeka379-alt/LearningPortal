## Introduction

Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980 with a constitution that was not written in Zimbabwe. It was agreed at a conference at Lancaster House in London, as part of the settlement that ended the liberation war. For more than thirty years it was the supreme law of the country, but it was changed again and again, and many citizens argued that the nation needed a constitution of its own.

This Form 4 lesson looks at what the Lancaster House constitution contained, why people wanted to replace it, the first attempt to do so in 2000, and why a **home-grown** constitution matters. The process that produced the 2013 Constitution (COPAC, outreach and the referendum) is studied in the lesson on constitution-making; here it is mentioned only briefly.

You should already know the outline of the liberation struggle and the meaning of a constitution as the supreme law.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lancaster House constitution"}
The constitution agreed at the Lancaster House conference in London in 1979, under which Zimbabwe became independent in 1980.
:::

:::definition{term="Constitutional amendment"}
A formal change to a constitution, made by a special procedure laid down in the constitution itself.
:::

:::definition{term="Entrenched clause"}
A part of a constitution that is protected so that it cannot be changed easily, or not at all for a fixed period.
:::

:::definition{term="Home-grown constitution"}
A constitution written by the citizens of a country, through their own institutions and processes, reflecting their own values and history.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Facts to learn"}
Lancaster House conference: **September-December 1979**; agreement signed **21 December 1979**. Reserved seats: **20 of the 100** House of Assembly seats for whites for **seven years**. Land: acquisition on a **willing-buyer willing-seller** basis for **ten years**. Executive presidency: from **1987**. Draft constitution rejected in a referendum: **February 2000**. New Constitution approved by referendum: **16 March 2013**.
:::

## Key provisions of the Lancaster House constitution

The Lancaster House constitution was a compromise between the liberation movements, the Rhodesian government and the British government. It aimed to end the war and reassure the white minority.

| Provision | What it said (in outline) | Why it was included |
|---|---|---|
| Reserved seats | 20 of the 100 seats in the House of Assembly were reserved for white voters for seven years | To give the white minority a guaranteed voice after independence |
| Parliament | Two houses: a Senate and a House of Assembly | Copied from the British-style system |
| Head of state and government | A ceremonial President as head of state and a Prime Minister as head of government | Based on the Westminster model |
| Property and land | Private property protected; land could be acquired only on a willing-buyer willing-seller basis, with compensation, for ten years | To reassure white farmers and investors |
| Declaration of Rights | Protected civil and political rights, such as freedom of expression and protection from torture | To protect individuals from abuse of power |
| Entrenchment | Some clauses, including the reserved seats and the Declaration of Rights, could not be changed easily for a fixed period | To make the settlement last |

## Amendments after independence

Once the protected periods ended, Parliament amended the constitution many times. Some of the best-known changes were:

| Date | Change (in outline) |
|---|---|
| 1987 | The reserved seats for whites were abolished |
| 1987 | An executive presidency replaced the offices of ceremonial President and Prime Minister |
| 1990 | The Senate was abolished, leaving a single house of Parliament |
| 2000s | Amendments changed the land provisions, and a Senate was brought back in 2005 |
| 2009 | An amendment gave effect to the inclusive government under the Global Political Agreement |

Because it was changed so often, critics said the constitution had become a patchwork, and that changes were made to suit governments of the day rather than after wide consultation with citizens.

## Why a new constitution was needed

Several reasons were given for replacing the Lancaster House constitution. Different groups stressed different reasons.

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Foreign drafting | It was negotiated in London under British chairmanship, so many felt it was not made by Zimbabweans for Zimbabwe |
| A compromise for a ceasefire | It was designed to end a war, not to set out the long-term vision of an independent nation |
| Many amendments | Frequent changes made it hard to follow and weakened public confidence in it |
| Land | Its property clauses had limited land redistribution in the first decade |
| Demands for reform | Churches, trade unions, students, women's groups and other organisations called for stronger rights, limits on power and wider participation |
| National identity | Citizens wanted a supreme law that reflected their history, languages, cultures and values |

:::example{title="Reading a source for its message"}
A fictional letter to a newspaper in 1998 says: *"Our constitution was written in a foreign city to stop a war. It has been changed many times by those in power. We, the people, should now write our own."*

1. **Message:** the writer wants a new, people-driven constitution.
2. **Reasons given:** foreign drafting, its purpose as a ceasefire deal, and frequent amendments.
3. **What the writer leaves out:** the constitution also brought independence and majority rule and contained a Declaration of Rights.
4. **Use in an answer:** quote the reasons, then explain each with your own knowledge.
:::

## The first attempt: the referendum of February 2000

In the late 1990s, civil society organisations campaigned for a new constitution, and the government appointed a Constitutional Commission which consulted the public and produced a draft. The draft was put to voters in a referendum in **February 2000**, and voters **rejected** it. Supporters of the draft argued that it contained important changes, including on land; opponents argued that it kept too much power in the executive and that the process was not fully people-driven. After the vote, the Lancaster House constitution, as amended, stayed in force.

The rejection showed that citizens expected to be fully involved in making their supreme law, and that a constitution needs wide acceptance to be legitimate.

## Towards 2013 (in outline)

The Global Political Agreement of 2008 committed the main parties to a new constitution. A parliamentary select committee (COPAC) led consultations from 2009, the draft was approved in a referendum on **16 March 2013**, and the new Constitution was signed into law on **22 May 2013**. The details of this process belong to the lesson on constitution-making.

## Why a home-grown constitution matters

:::example{title="Comparing two constitutions"}
Farai's class compares the two constitutions.

1. **Who made it?** Lancaster House: negotiators in London in 1979. 2013: Zimbabweans through outreach, drafting and a national referendum.
2. **What it reflects:** Lancaster House reflected the need to end a war; the 2013 Constitution reflects values chosen by citizens, such as recognition of 16 languages, devolution and a wide Declaration of Rights.
3. **Legitimacy:** a constitution approved by the people in a referendum carries strong authority.
4. **Conclusion:** a home-grown constitution is more likely to be respected and defended by citizens.
:::

A home-grown constitution matters because it:

- gives citizens **ownership**, so they are more willing to respect and defend it;
- reflects the nation's **history, cultures and values**, including its languages and traditional institutions;
- has **legitimacy**, especially when approved in a referendum;
- protects **rights** that citizens themselves asked for, including those of women, children, persons with disabilities and veterans;
- supports **national unity and sovereignty**, as the supreme law is made at home.

Some people point out that a home-grown constitution is only valuable if it is fully put into practice and if amendments are made through wide consultation. A good constitution on paper still depends on citizens and institutions to make it work.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two referendums"}
The draft of **February 2000** was rejected; the draft of **16 March 2013** was approved.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Lancaster House had nothing good in it"}
It brought independence and majority rule and contained a Declaration of Rights. Balanced answers recognise this before explaining its weaknesses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing about the 2013 process instead"}
A question on why Lancaster House was replaced needs its provisions and weaknesses; a short sentence on 2013 is enough.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Lancaster House constitution (agreed 21 December 1979) was a compromise to end the war and bring independence.
- It reserved 20 of 100 seats for whites for seven years and protected property with a willing-buyer willing-seller land clause for ten years.
- It was amended many times, including the executive presidency from 1987.
- Calls for a new constitution grew; a draft was rejected in February 2000, and a new Constitution was approved in March 2013.
- A home-grown constitution gives citizens ownership, legitimacy and a supreme law that reflects their values.
:::
