## Introduction

At a family gathering in Lupane or Mt Darwin you may hear older relatives talk about the war years: a grandmother who cooked sadza at night for fighters, an uncle who carried messages as a boy, a cousin who walked to Botswana or Mozambique to join the struggle. Their stories remind us that the liberation struggle was not fought by soldiers alone. It was a **people's war**.

In this Form 3 lesson you learn the roles played by different groups in the Second Chimurenga/Umvukela, inside and outside the country, and what it means to be declared a national hero or heroine. You should already know the causes and course of the struggle and the names of the two liberation armies, ZANLA and ZIPRA.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="The masses"}
The ordinary people, especially rural villagers, whose support the liberation movements depended on.
:::

:::definition{term="Mujibha and chimbwido"}
Young people who helped the freedom fighters: **mujibhas** were boys and young men, **chimbwidos** (plural *zvimbwido*) were girls and young women. They carried messages, gathered information, cooked and carried supplies.
:::

:::definition{term="Frontline States"}
The independent African countries close to Rhodesia that supported the liberation struggle: Tanzania, Zambia, Botswana, Mozambique (from 1975) and Angola (from 1975).
:::

:::definition{term="National hero"}
A person whom the State recognises for outstanding service to the nation, especially in the liberation struggle, and who may be buried at the National Heroes Acre in Harare.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Places and days to learn"}
The **National Heroes Acre** is in Harare. **Heroes Day** is the second Monday of August, followed by **Defence Forces Day** on the Tuesday. Provinces have **provincial heroes acres** for provincial heroes.
:::

## Roles inside the country

| Group | Roles in the struggle |
|---|---|
| The masses (peasants) | Fed, hid and clothed the fighters; gave information about enemy movements; attended political meetings (*pungwe*); many suffered punishment for their support |
| Freedom fighters (ZANLA and ZIPRA) | Fought the Rhodesian forces; politicised the people; trained recruits; many died or were wounded |
| Mujibhas and chimbwidos | Carried messages and supplies, cooked, washed clothes, acted as lookouts and guides, warned fighters of danger |
| Spirit mediums | Gave spiritual guidance and blessing; linked the struggle to the ancestors and to the First Chimurenga/Umvukela; advised fighters on local customs and sacred places |
| Chiefs and traditional leaders | Some quietly supported the fighters and mobilised people; others were caught between the government and the movements; some were punished for their support |
| Churches and missions | Mission hospitals and clinics treated the wounded; some church bodies recorded and publicised abuses; church schools educated many future leaders; some missionaries were deported for their views |
| Workers and trade unions | Strikes and protests in towns; workers sent money and supplies; some joined the movements |
| Students | Protests at schools and the university; many students crossed the borders to join the struggle |
| Women | Fought as combatants, carried weapons, cared for the wounded and kept families and farms going |

:::example{title="How the masses made guerrilla war possible"}
Imagine a ZIPRA section arriving at a village near Tsholotsho after a long night's walk.

1. **Food:** villagers cook for them, even though food is scarce.
2. **Information:** a mujibha reports that soldiers were seen at the business centre that morning.
3. **Shelter:** the fighters rest in a hidden place chosen by the villagers.
4. **Risk:** if the soldiers learn of the visit, the village may be punished.
5. **Conclusion:** without the people's support the fighters could not have survived, which is why the struggle is called a people's war.
:::

## Roles outside the country

| Group | Roles |
|---|---|
| Refugees and exiles | Thousands left the country; many trained as fighters, teachers, nurses or administrators in camps |
| Frontline States | Gave bases, transit routes, shelter for refugees and diplomatic support; paid a heavy price when Rhodesian forces attacked them |
| The OAU | Its Liberation Committee, based in Dar es Salaam, gave money, supplies and recognition |
| Other supporters | Countries such as China and the Soviet Union gave training and weapons; some Nordic and other countries, churches and solidarity groups gave humanitarian help; the UN imposed sanctions on Rhodesia |

Life in the camps was hard. Refugees, many of them young people and children, lived in crowded conditions with little food or medicine. Yet the camps also ran schools and clinics, and many refugees trained as teachers, nurses, clerks and administrators. When they returned after 1980, they brought skills that helped to build the new nation. Women were present at every level: as combatants and instructors, as nurses and teachers in the camps, and as mothers who kept families together at home while husbands and sons were away. Their contribution is now widely recognised, although many individual stories have never been recorded.

Mozambique and Zambia suffered especially. Rhodesian forces attacked camps such as Nyadzonia and Chimoio in Mozambique and Freedom Camp in Zambia, and also attacked bridges and roads.

## National heroes and heroines

After independence, the State began to honour people for outstanding service to the nation. Hero status is given at different levels:

| Level | Where the person may be buried |
|---|---|
| National hero or heroine | National Heroes Acre, Harare |
| Provincial hero or heroine | A provincial heroes acre |
| District or liberation war hero | A local heroes acre or the family's chosen place |

The **National Heroes Acre** includes the graves of national heroes and heroines of both ZANU and ZAPU, an eternal flame, a museum and the **Tomb of the Unknown Soldier**, which honours the many fighters and civilians whose graves are unknown. Among those buried there are liberation leaders such as **Joshua Nkomo**, **Herbert Chitepo**, **Josiah Tongogara**, **Jason Moyo** and **Leopold Takawira**.

Heroes are honoured by burial with state honours, commemorations on Heroes Day, the naming of roads, schools and buildings, and support for their families. Who should be given hero status has sometimes been debated, and many people point out that most of those who served, including ordinary villagers, mujibhas and chimbwidos, will never be named. The memory of the First Chimurenga/Umvukela heroes, such as Mbuya Nehanda and Sekuru Kaguvi, is also honoured.

:::example{title="Reading a commemoration"}
At a Heroes Day event at a provincial heroes acre near Gweru, a teacher asks her class why the Unknown Soldier is honoured.

1. **Fact:** many fighters and civilians died far from home, and their graves are unknown.
2. **Meaning:** the tomb honours all of them, not just famous leaders.
3. **Link to roles:** it reminds us that the struggle depended on ordinary people.
4. **Answer:** honouring the unknown makes the commemoration belong to every family that lost someone.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating the war as the fighters' story only"}
Examiners expect the masses, mujibhas and chimbwidos, spirit mediums, churches, workers, students, refugees and the Frontline States.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mujibhas and chimbwidos"}
Mujibhas were boys and young men; chimbwidos were girls and young women.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a role without explaining it"}
"Spirit mediums helped" is weak. "Spirit mediums gave the fighters spiritual guidance and linked the struggle to the ancestors, which won the trust of rural people" is developed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Roles"}
For *identify* (Paper 2 Section A), name the group and one role. For *explain the roles of the masses*, give each role and why it mattered to the fighters. For a Section B *how far* essay on whether the fighters won independence alone, weigh the fighters against the masses, the Frontline States and international pressure, and reach a judgement.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Heroes"}
Describe how heroes are honoured in neutral terms: burial, Heroes Day, naming of places, support for families.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The liberation struggle was a people's war: the masses fed, hid and informed the fighters.
- Mujibhas and chimbwidos carried messages and supplies; spirit mediums gave spiritual guidance; churches, workers and students also contributed.
- The Frontline States, the OAU and other supporters provided bases, shelter, training and diplomatic support.
- Hero status is given at national, provincial and district level; national heroes may be buried at the National Heroes Acre in Harare.
- Heroes are honoured on Heroes Day (second Monday of August); the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier honours those whose graves are unknown.
:::
