## Introduction

Every year on 18 April, Zimbabweans celebrate Independence Day, and in August the nation honours its heroes. Behind both holidays lies the **Second Chimurenga** (in Ndebele, **Umvukela**): the armed liberation struggle fought between 1966 and 1979 against the white minority government of Rhodesia. Many families have a relative who fought, fed the fighters, crossed the border as a refugee, or lost someone in the war.

The name "Second Chimurenga" links the struggle to the **First Chimurenga/Umvukela** of 1896-97, when the Ndebele and Shona rose against Company rule. In this Form 3 lesson you learn why Africans turned to armed struggle, how the war developed, how it ended at Lancaster House, and what it cost. You should already know how land was taken under colonial rule and the main colonial land laws.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nationalism"}
A movement in which the people of a country work together to win self-rule and independence from colonial control.
:::

:::definition{term="UDI (Unilateral Declaration of Independence)"}
The declaration of 11 November 1965 by which the Rhodesian Front government of Ian Smith declared Rhodesia independent of Britain without British agreement, in order to keep white minority rule.
:::

:::definition{term="Guerrilla warfare"}
A way of fighting in which small, mobile groups of fighters attack and withdraw, relying on the support of the local people rather than meeting a large army in open battle.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Dates to learn"}
UDI **11 November 1965**. Battle of Chinhoyi **28 April 1966**. Attack on Altena Farm **21 December 1972**. Nyadzonia **9 August 1976**. Chimoio **23 November 1977**. Freedom Camp **19 October 1978**. Lancaster House Agreement signed **21 December 1979**. Independence **18 April 1980**.
:::

## Causes of the liberation struggle

| Cause | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Land | Laws such as the Land Apportionment Act (1930) and the Land Tenure Act (1969) kept the best land for a small settler minority |
| Political exclusion | Voting depended on income, property and education, so very few Africans could vote; Africans had no real say in government |
| Racial discrimination | Separate and unequal schools, hospitals, housing and jobs; pass laws; low wages for Africans |
| Banning of nationalist parties | Peaceful parties were banned one after another: the ANC (1959), the NDP (1961) and ZAPU (1962); ZANU, formed in 1963, was banned in 1964; many leaders were detained for years |
| UDI | Smith's declaration of 1965 showed that the settler government would not accept majority rule |
| Failed talks | Talks between Britain and the Rhodesian government (on HMS *Tiger* in 1966 and HMS *Fearless* in 1968) and the proposals tested by the Pearce Commission (1972), which Africans rejected, did not bring majority rule |

When peaceful protest, petitions and strikes were met with bans and detention, the nationalist movements decided that armed struggle was the only way left.

## The two armies

| | ZANLA | ZIPRA |
|---|---|---|
| Party | ZANU (Zimbabwe African National Union) | ZAPU (Zimbabwe African People's Union) |
| Main rear bases | Tanzania and Zambia at first; Mozambique after its independence in 1975 | Zambia, with transit through Botswana |
| Main areas of operation | The north-east, east and south-east | The west, north-west and Matabeleland |
| Main foreign support | China | The Soviet Union and Eastern European countries |
| Strategy | Mainly guerrilla war, politicising the rural people | Guerrilla war, and later the build-up of a conventional force |

Both armies fought for independence and both made great sacrifices. In 1976 ZANU and ZAPU formed the **Patriotic Front** to negotiate together.

## The course of the war in outline

The struggle passed through several phases.

1. **Early incursions (1966-1971).** On **28 April 1966** seven ZANLA fighters were killed in a battle near Chinhoyi (then Sinoia); the day is remembered as the start of the armed struggle. In 1967-68 ZIPRA, together with fighters of the South African ANC, fought the Wankie (Hwange) and Sipolilo campaigns. These early campaigns were costly because the fighters had little support inside the country.
2. **Guerrilla war (from 1972).** The movements learned that they needed the support of rural people. After careful political preparation in the north-east, ZANLA attacked **Altena Farm** in the Centenary area on **21 December 1972**, marking a new phase of the war.
3. **Expansion (1975-1979).** After Mozambique became independent in 1975, ZANLA operated along the whole eastern border; ZIPRA expanded from Zambia. Rhodesia called up more men and moved rural people into "protected villages" (keeps) to cut them off from the fighters. Rhodesian forces attacked refugee and guerrilla camps outside the country, including **Nyadzonia** (9 August 1976) and **Chimoio** (23 November 1977) in Mozambique and **Freedom Camp** near Lusaka in Zambia (19 October 1978). Many hundreds of people were killed in these attacks; estimates differ.

:::example{title="Why the movements changed their strategy"}
Compare the early clashes of 1966-68 with the war after 1972.

1. **Early campaigns:** groups crossed the border and met Rhodesian forces in open battle, often without local support.
2. **The lesson learned:** fighters could not survive without food, information and shelter from the people.
3. **The new approach:** political education of villagers (often at night meetings, called *pungwe*), and small groups that attacked and withdrew.
4. **Result:** the war spread across the country and became very costly for the Rhodesian government.
:::

## Attempts at a settlement

| Year | Attempt | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 1975 | Victoria Falls conference, held in a train on the bridge over the Zambezi | Failed |
| 1976 | Geneva conference | Failed |
| 1978 | Internal Settlement between the Smith government and some African leaders, leading to "Zimbabwe-Rhodesia" in 1979 | Not recognised internationally; the war continued because the Patriotic Front was excluded |
| 1979 | Lancaster House conference in London (September-December) | Agreement signed on 21 December 1979 |

The **Lancaster House Agreement** provided for a ceasefire, a new constitution and elections supervised by Britain. The constitution reserved 20 of the 100 seats in the House of Assembly for whites for seven years and protected property, so land could be bought only on a willing-buyer willing-seller basis for ten years. Elections were held in February 1980, and Zimbabwe became independent on **18 April 1980**.

:::example{title="Weighing why Lancaster House succeeded"}
Previous talks had failed. Why did these succeed?

1. **War pressure:** the Rhodesian economy and army were under great strain.
2. **Regional pressure:** the Frontline States urged the Patriotic Front to negotiate.
3. **International pressure:** sanctions and Britain's and the Commonwealth's involvement.
4. **Judgement:** no single factor explains it; the combined pressure of the war and diplomacy made both sides accept compromises.
:::

## Results of the war

- **Independence** on 18 April 1980 and majority rule.
- **Human cost:** tens of thousands of people died, many of them civilians; many more were wounded, displaced or became refugees.
- **Damage** to homes, schools, clinics, dip tanks and cattle; families were separated.
- **Violence by all sides** affected civilians; the memory of the war remains painful for many families.
- **A national heritage** of heroes, shrines and commemorations, and the policy of reconciliation after 1980.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Telling the story of one army only"}
ZANLA and ZIPRA both fought for independence. A good answer mentions both, and the role of the people who supported them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the dates"}
Chinhoyi 28 April 1966 (start of the armed struggle); Altena Farm 21 December 1972; Lancaster House 21 December 1979. The two 21 December dates are seven years apart.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing causes without explaining"}
"Land" earns little. "Land laws kept the best land for settlers, so Africans were crowded onto poor land and supported the struggle" earns full credit.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Causes and course"}
*Outline the causes* (Paper 2 Section A): one sentence per cause. *Explain why the nationalists turned to armed struggle*: link the banning of parties and failed talks to the decision. In Paper 1, expect dates and which army operated from which country.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Balanced essays"}
For *how far* or *discuss* questions about the war, treat ZANLA and ZIPRA fairly, credit the people and the Frontline States, and acknowledge the costs to civilians.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Causes: land, political exclusion, discrimination, banned parties, UDI (11 November 1965) and failed talks.
- The armed struggle began at Chinhoyi on 28 April 1966; the guerrilla war intensified after Altena Farm (21 December 1972).
- ZANLA (ZANU) operated mainly from Mozambique and ZIPRA (ZAPU) from Zambia; they formed the Patriotic Front in 1976.
- Camps were attacked at Nyadzonia (1976), Chimoio (1977) and Freedom Camp (1978).
- The Lancaster House Agreement (21 December 1979) led to independence on 18 April 1980; the war cost tens of thousands of lives.
:::
