## Introduction

A farmer in a communal area near Gokwe grows maize, groundnuts and sorghum on a few hectares, mostly to feed her family. A few hundred kilometres away, a lowveld estate grows sugarcane on thousands of hectares under irrigation and sells to a mill. Both are agriculture, but the systems differ in size, purpose, inputs and labour. Since the 1960s, new seeds, fertilisers and machinery, known as the Green Revolution, changed farming in many countries. This lesson classifies farming systems, then assesses the Green Revolution. You should already know the factors that affect farming, including Zimbabwe's agro-ecological regions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Subsistence farming"}
Farming in which most of the output is eaten by the farm family and only a surplus, if any, is sold.
:::

:::definition{term="Commercial farming"}
Farming in which most of the output is sold for profit, using hired labour, purchased inputs and often machinery.
:::

:::definition{term="Green Revolution"}
The rapid rise in crop yields, especially of wheat, rice and maize, from the 1960s, achieved by high-yielding varieties together with fertiliser, pesticides, irrigation and mechanisation.
:::

:::definition{term="High-yielding variety (HYV)"}
A crop variety bred to give a larger harvest per hectare than traditional varieties, usually when it is supplied with enough water and nutrients.
:::

## Subsistence systems

- **Shifting cultivation.** Farmers clear a patch of forest or woodland, often by cutting and burning, grow crops for a few years while the soil nutrients last, and then move to a new patch, leaving the old one to recover. It works where population density is low and fallow is long, as in parts of the Amazon and Congo basins and in the chitemene system of northern Zambia. With rising population, fallow shortens and the soil is exhausted.
- **Pastoral nomadism.** Herders move livestock (cattle, goats, camels) seasonally in search of grazing and water, in dry areas such as the Sahel and parts of East Africa. Herd size is wealth, and milk and meat are the main foods. Pressure on land, borders and drought make this system harder to follow.
- **Communal (mixed) farming.** In Zimbabwe's communal areas households hold land under customary tenure allocated through local leaders. They grow maize, small grains, groundnuts and vegetables and keep cattle and goats, which provide draught power, milk and savings. Plots are small, inputs are limited and yields depend on rainfall.

## Commercial systems

- **Dairy farming** supplies milk to processors, mostly near cities in Regions I and II where good pasture and fodder can be grown.
- **Horticulture** grows vegetables, fruit and flowers on intensively managed land, often under irrigation, for local supermarkets and for export. It needs labour, capital and fast transport.
- **Ranching** raises beef cattle (and sometimes game) on large areas in the drier Regions IV and V with little labour per hectare.
- **Plantations and estates** are large farms growing one cash crop and often processing it on site, such as tea and coffee in the Eastern Highlands and sugarcane in the south-east lowveld.

| Feature | Subsistence | Commercial |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | feed the family | sell for profit |
| Farm size | small | medium to very large |
| Labour | family | hired workers |
| Inputs | few; local seed, manure | purchased seed, fertiliser, machinery |
| Yield per hectare | low and variable | higher; irrigated systems highest |

## Major cereals

**Maize** is Zimbabwe's staple and most widely grown cereal; **wheat** is grown in winter, mainly under irrigation; **sorghum** and the millets (pearl and finger millet) are drought-tolerant small grains suited to Regions III to V; **rice** is a minor crop locally but a staple for much of Asia.

## The Green Revolution

The wheat breeding of Norman Borlaug in Mexico and rice breeding at the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines (IR8, released in the 1960s) produced short-strawed varieties that respond to fertiliser without falling over. They spread to India, Pakistan and many other countries, mainly on irrigated land.

**Biochemical changes:** HYV seed, chemical fertiliser, pesticides and herbicides. **Mechanical changes:** tractors, combine harvesters, pumps and irrigation canals and tubewells. **Socio-economic changes:** credit, extension advice, cooperatives, guaranteed prices, new markets and rural roads and electricity.

**Successes.** Yields rose sharply, countries such as India moved from food imports towards self-sufficiency in cereals by the 1970s and 1980s, and food prices fell relative to what they would otherwise have been. Fewer new fields had to be cleared for the same output.

**Problems.** HYVs need purchased inputs and reliable water, so richer farmers with irrigation and credit gained first and poorer farmers fell into debt, widening inequality. Heavy irrigation lowered water tables in places such as the Punjab, and caused salinisation and waterlogging. Fertiliser and pesticide runoff polluted rivers and groundwater, pests became resistant, and uniform varieties replaced diverse local ones, reducing genetic variety. Machines reduced work for labourers. Most gains came in Asia and Latin America; in much of sub-Saharan Africa less land is irrigated, input markets are weak and suitable varieties were fewer, so gains were smaller, although hybrid maize greatly raised yields on Zimbabwe's commercial farms from the 1960s.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Yield and production change]
In this exercise (illustrative data) an irrigated district has 800 ha of wheat. Average yield was 1.2 t/ha in 1965 and 3.0 t/ha in 1985.

1. Percentage increase in yield: $\dfrac{3.0 - 1.2}{1.2} \times 100 = 150\%$.
2. Production in 1965: $800 \times 1.2 = 960$ t. Production in 1985: $800 \times 3.0 = 2\,400$ t.
3. Increase in production: $2\,400 - 960 = 1\,440$ t, on the same area of land.
:::

:::worked-solution[Is the new variety worth growing?]
Per hectare, a traditional variety yields 1.8 t at US\$250 per tonne and costs US\$200 to grow. An HYV yields 4.0 t at the same price but costs US\$600 for seed, fertiliser and chemicals (illustrative data).

1. Traditional profit: $1.8 \times 250 - 200 = 450 - 200 = 250$, so US\$250 per hectare.
2. HYV profit: $4.0 \times 250 - 600 = 1\,000 - 600 = 400$, so US\$400 per hectare.
3. Gain: $400 - 250 = 150$, so US\$150 per hectare. A farmer without the US\$600 to spend cannot gain, and a drought that halves the HYV yield to 2.0 t gives $500 - 600 = -100$, a loss. This shows why credit and water matter.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing two Zimbabwean farm types"}
A communal farm of 3 ha growing maize with family labour uses saved seed and manure, and a good year gives a surplus to sell. A commercial horticulture farm of 40 ha uses hired workers, drip irrigation and packing sheds to export vegetables. The first aims to feed the household and cut risk by mixing crops and livestock; the second aims to maximise profit and invests heavily, making it more productive per hectare but more exposed to price changes and costs.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying subsistence farmers never sell anything"}
Many sell a surplus. The key difference is that the main purpose is feeding the family.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Green Revolution as only seeds"}
It was a package: HYVs, fertiliser, pesticides, water, machinery and supporting credit and markets. Without the package the yields do not rise.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided assessment"}
Give successes (yield, food supply) and failures (inequality, environmental damage), name who gained and who lost, and then make a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Subsistence systems include shifting cultivation, pastoral nomadism and communal farming; commercial systems include dairy, horticulture, ranching and plantations.
- Maize, wheat, sorghum, millets and rice are the main cereals; small grains suit drier regions.
- The Green Revolution combined HYVs, fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation and machines with credit and marketing support.
- It raised yields and improved food supply, especially in Asia.
- Costs included inequality, debt, water and chemical problems and loss of crop diversity.
- Assessment depends on who is considered and on whether inputs, water and credit are available.
:::
