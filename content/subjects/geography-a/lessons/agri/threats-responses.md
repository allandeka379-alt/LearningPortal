## Introduction

A farmer in Mwenezi plants maize on the first rains, but a long dry spell in January wilts the crop. In another season, floods wash away a field beside the river and armyworm larvae chew the leaves of the plants that remain. A household that depends on its own harvest can quickly face hunger. This lesson looks at the threats to food security, especially those linked to climate change, and at the responses that farmers, communities and the state use. You should already know the causes of climate change and droughts (see the earlier lessons) and that Zimbabwe is divided into agro-ecological regions (natural regions I to V), with the driest in regions IV and V.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Food security"}
A situation in which all people, at all times, have physical, social and economic access to enough safe and nutritious food to meet their needs for an active, healthy life. It has four dimensions: availability, access, utilisation and stability.
:::

:::definition{term="Food insecurity"}
The lack of reliable access to enough food. It may be temporary (after a drought) or chronic.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation farming"}
A set of practices that protect soil and water: minimum soil disturbance (planting basins or ripping), a permanent cover of mulch or crop residues, and crop rotation or intercropping.
:::

## Threats to food security

1. **Climate change and variability.** Higher temperatures increase evaporation and heat stress, especially at flowering; the rainy season starts later or ends earlier; dry spells inside the season become more frequent; and extreme events increase.
2. **Droughts.** Rain-fed crops fail and livestock die; the regions with the lowest rainfall are the most at risk. National maize production falls and grain has to be imported.
3. **Floods.** Heavy rain and cyclones wash away crops, erode soil, destroy stores, roads and bridges and cut off markets, as in Chimanimani and Chipinge in 2019.
4. **Pests and diseases.** Fall armyworm, which spread across Africa from 2016 and reached southern Africa in the 2016–17 season, attacks maize; quelea birds destroy small-grain crops; plant diseases such as maize streak virus reduce yields; livestock diseases such as theileriosis ("January disease") and foot-and-mouth disease cause deaths and restrict trade. Warmer, wetter conditions can favour some pests.
5. **Other pressures.** Soil degradation, high input costs, poor storage and weak markets add to the risk.

**Impacts on food security.**

- *Availability* falls when harvests fail. *Access* falls when prices rise and incomes fall, so poor households buy less. *Utilisation* suffers when diets become less varied and malnutrition increases. *Stability* is lost when poor seasons are frequent.
- Households sell livestock, reduce meals, borrow, withdraw children from school or migrate to find work. The country uses foreign currency to import grain and requires food aid.

## Responses

**Indigenous and drought-tolerant crops.** Small grains such as sorghum, pearl millet and finger millet, and legumes such as cowpeas and bambara groundnuts, tolerate heat and dry spells better than maize and need fewer inputs. Early-maturing and drought-tolerant maize varieties are also available. In regions IV and V, small grains and drought-tolerant crops are particularly suitable. The limits are lower market demand, bird damage and the time needed to process small grains.

**Conservation farming.** Planting basins or rip lines concentrate water and nutrients near the plant, mulch reduces evaporation and soil erosion and adds organic matter, and rotation breaks pest cycles and restores nitrogen. The Pfumvudza/Intwasa programme uses these ideas on a small plot. Limits include the labour needed to dig basins, the need for mulch, and the knowledge required, which can be a barrier to older or poorer farmers.

**Post-harvest techniques.** A large share of grain can be lost to pests, moulds and moisture after harvest. Drying grain properly, hermetic (airtight) bags and metal silos, protecting stores from rodents, and sorting and treating stored grain can reduce these losses and improve food safety. They are low-cost ways of increasing food availability without producing more.

**Improved water management.** Rainwater harvesting (tied ridges, infiltration pits, small dams and tanks), drip and other efficient irrigation, boreholes with solar pumps, scheduling irrigation to crop needs, and protecting catchments help crops survive dry spells. The limits are cost, the need for maintenance and the risk of conflict over water.

**Other responses.** Seasonal forecasts and advice from the Meteorological Services Department and agricultural extension officers, integrated pest management for armyworm, crop and income diversification, livestock vaccination and dipping, grain reserves, insurance and social protection.

## Evaluating responses

Judge each response by its **effectiveness** (does it stabilise yield?), **cost**, **labour and knowledge needed**, **suitability** to the region and the farmer, **speed** and **sustainability**. Responses work best in combination and with extension support. For example, drought-tolerant crops, conservation farming and improved storage together increase availability and reduce risk, while markets and credit help farmers to adopt them.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Is a district self-sufficient in cereals?]
A district has 120 000 people and needs about 150 kg of cereal per person per year (illustrative planning figure). The district produced 14 400 tonnes in a poor season.

1. Annual need = $120\,000 \times 150 \div 1\,000 = 18\,000$ tonnes.
2. Self-sufficiency = $14\,400 \div 18\,000 \times 100 = 80\%$.
3. Deficit = $18\,000 - 14\,400 = 3\,600$ tonnes, which must be met by purchases, stocks or food aid.
4. A district that produces only 80% of its need is food insecure unless it has stores, income to buy grain or aid.
:::

:::worked-solution[The value of reducing post-harvest losses]
A district harvests 4 000 tonnes of grain. Traditional storage loses 15% to pests and moulds; hermetic bags lose about 3% (illustrative values).

1. Traditional loss = $4\,000 \times 0.15 = 600$ tonnes.
2. Loss with hermetic bags = $4\,000 \times 0.03 = 120$ tonnes.
3. Grain saved = $600 - 120 = 480$ tonnes.
4. At 150 kg per person per year, this feeds $480\,000 \div 150 = 3\,200$ people for a year, without planting an extra hectare.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing crops in a dry season"}
Illustrative data for a farm in a region with low rainfall in a dry season: maize 0.8 t/ha, sorghum 1.5 t/ha. **Describe:** sorghum yields 0.7 t/ha more, which is $0.7 \div 0.8 \times 100 = 87.5\%$ higher. On 2 ha maize gives 1.6 t and sorghum 3.0 t. **Explain:** sorghum has deep roots, can wait out dry spells and survives heat, while maize is sensitive to water stress at flowering. **Evaluate:** sorghum is more reliable here, but the farmer also needs a market and a way to process it, so a mixed plan with early-maturing maize may be best.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating food security with food production"}
A household can be food insecure in a year of high national production if it cannot afford or reach food. Include access, utilisation and stability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing responses without matching them to the threat"}
Name the threat first, then the response that deals with it (for example drought and drought-tolerant crops, armyworm and integrated pest management).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a response is 'good' without evidence"}
State the benefit with a figure or example and then give its limits, such as cost, labour or adoption.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 10). *Explain* a threat with a chain from the climate change process to the effect on food. *Describe* impacts using the dimensions of food security. For *assess the effectiveness* of responses, choose at least two, give how each works, a strength, a limit and a judgement tied to the region and type of farmer. Include data when given (yields, losses, self-sufficiency) and calculate percentages carefully.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Food security means availability, access, utilisation and stability for all people at all times.
- Threats include climate change, droughts, floods, pests (such as fall armyworm) and diseases; they reduce yields and raise prices.
- Responses: indigenous and drought-tolerant crops, conservation farming, better post-harvest storage and improved water management.
- Cutting post-harvest losses can raise food availability without more land.
- Responses should be matched to the threat and region and judged on effectiveness, cost, labour and sustainability.
:::
