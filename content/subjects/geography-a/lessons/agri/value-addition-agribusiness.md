## Introduction

A farmer who sells groundnuts in the shell at the roadside earns far less per kilogram than a business that roasts them, grinds them, puts the peanut butter in a labelled jar and sells it in a supermarket. The difference is the **value added** by processing, packaging and marketing. Agriculture does not end at the farm gate: it is linked to suppliers of seed and fertiliser, to processors, transporters and shops. This lesson looks at agribusiness and at ways in which farmers and companies in Zimbabwe can add value to what they produce. You should already know the main crops and farming systems of Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Agribusiness"}
All the businesses involved in the chain from farm inputs to the consumer: input supply, farming, processing, storage, transport, marketing and retail.
:::

:::definition{term="Value addition"}
Increasing the value of a farm product by changing it or presenting it better, for example by processing, grading, packaging or branding, so that it sells for more than the raw product.
:::

:::definition{term="Contract farming"}
An arrangement in which a farmer grows a crop or raises animals for a buyer who agrees in advance to purchase the output at a set price or under set terms, and often supplies inputs or advice.
:::

## Forms of agribusiness

1. **Input suppliers:** seed houses, fertiliser and chemical companies, and sellers of machinery and veterinary products.
2. **Farm production:** smallholders, commercial farms and estates growing crops and raising livestock.
3. **Processing:** milling maize into mealie meal, crushing seed into cooking oil, making milk into cheese, yoghurt and powder, ginning cotton into lint, and refining sugarcane into sugar.
4. **Marketing and distribution:** auction floors, grain depots, wholesalers, transport firms and exporters.
5. **Retail:** supermarkets, open markets and informal traders.
6. **Support services:** banks and microfinance, insurers, research and extension, and agricultural shows.

Examples in Zimbabwe include sugar mills at Triangle and Hippo Valley that process cane from estates and smallholders, the dairy processors that collect milk from farms, and the Cotton Company of Zimbabwe (Cottco), which buys seed cotton from growers.

## Forms of value addition

- **Processing:** tomatoes into sauce or paste, groundnuts into peanut butter, sorghum into flour or malt, fruit into juice or dried fruit, maize into mealie meal, meat into sausages and biltong, cotton into yarn and cloth.
- **Grading and sorting:** separating produce by size and quality so that the best receives a higher price.
- **Packaging:** protects the product, lengthens shelf life and makes it easy to sell in different sizes.
- **Branding and labelling:** a name, logo and quality mark build trust, allow a price premium and help meet legal requirements.
- **Storage and timing:** keeping produce until prices are higher or preparing dried products that last through the year.
- **Certification:** meeting standards for hygiene, organic production or export markets.

## Contract farming and exhibitions

In **contract farming** a company such as a tobacco merchant, cotton buyer or sugar mill agrees to buy the output. It may supply seed, fertiliser and chemicals on credit, and deduct their cost from the payment at harvest. Benefits to the farmer include a guaranteed market, access to inputs and advice, and less price risk. Risks include being tied to one buyer, a low price fixed in advance, heavy deductions, and side-selling disputes. Contract farming grew rapidly for tobacco and cotton in the 2010s, giving smallholders access to finance that banks did not offer.

**Exhibitions and shows**, such as the Zimbabwe International Trade Fair in Bulawayo and the agricultural shows in Harare and other towns, let producers display products, meet buyers and processors, compare prices, learn about new technology and win recognition through competitions.

## Benefits and constraints

| Benefits of value addition | Constraints |
|---|---|
| Higher income per unit of raw produce | Needs capital for equipment, packaging and premises |
| More jobs in processing, packing and sales | Needs reliable electricity and clean water |
| Less waste: surplus produce is preserved | Quality and safety standards must be met and tested |
| Longer shelf life and wider markets | Competition from established brands and imports |
| Stronger links between farmers and industry | Poor roads and storage cause losses; skills for marketing may be lacking |

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Value added by processing groundnuts]
A small processor buys 1 000 kg of shelled groundnuts at US\$1.20 per kg. Processing yields 900 kg of peanut butter sold at US\$3.60 per kg. Processing costs (energy, labour, jars, labels) are US\$1 100 (illustrative figures).

1. Cost of raw groundnuts: $1\,000 \times 1.20 = 1\,200$, so US\$1 200.
2. Sales of peanut butter: $900 \times 3.60 = 3\,240$, so US\$3 240.
3. Value added: $3\,240 - 1\,200 = 2\,040$, so US\$2 040.
4. Profit after processing costs: $2\,040 - 1\,100 = 940$, so US\$940.
:::

:::worked-solution[A contract farming deduction]
A cotton farmer grows 2 ha under contract. The company supplies inputs worth US\$200 per hectare and buys seed cotton at US\$0.45 per kg. The farmer's yield is 1 200 kg per hectare (illustrative figures).

1. Output: $2 \times 1\,200 = 2\,400$ kg.
2. Payment before deductions: $2\,400 \times 0.45 = 1\,080$, so US\$1 080.
3. Input deduction: $2 \times 200 = 400$, so US\$400.
4. Net payment: $1\,080 - 400 = 680$, so US\$680.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating value addition in a village"}
A women's group in a rural district dries tomatoes and packs them in small bags. **Gains:** the dried tomatoes sell at a higher price per kilogram, fewer fresh tomatoes are wasted, and members earn income in the dry season. **Constraints:** drying equipment and packaging cost money, the group has no refrigerated transport, and it must meet health rules. **Judgement:** the project is worthwhile if the group can find a regular market and meet standards, so that training and a clear brand matter as much as machinery.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing value addition with higher production"}
Value addition raises the value of each unit by processing or presentation. Growing more crop on the same land is an increase in output, not value addition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling contract farming only a benefit"}
Give the benefits (market, inputs, advice) and also the risks (fixed price, deductions, dependence on one buyer).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the processing cost when calculating gain"}
Value added is sales minus the cost of raw materials; profit also subtracts processing costs such as energy, labour and packaging.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Agribusiness covers the whole chain from inputs through farming and processing to retail.
- Value addition includes processing, grading, packaging, branding, storage and certification.
- Value added = sales − cost of raw materials; profit also subtracts processing costs.
- Contract farming gives a market and inputs but carries risks of low fixed prices and dependence on one buyer.
- Exhibitions help producers find buyers, ideas and recognition.
- Success needs capital, power, skills, standards and a reliable market.
:::
