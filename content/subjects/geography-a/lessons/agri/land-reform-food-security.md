## Introduction

Land is the most important asset for most rural households in Zimbabwe, and the question of who holds it has shaped the country's history and economy. Land reform is also a topic on which people hold strong and different views. In geography the task is to describe what was done, use evidence about food production, and weigh benefits against limits without taking sides. You should already know Zimbabwe's agro-ecological regions and the difference between communal and commercial farming.

:::note{title="Neutral wording and labelled data"}
Positions are described as held by groups ("supporters argue …"). Figures in this lesson are **illustrative** unless a source is named. Real statistics should always be quoted with their source and year, for example from ZimStat, the Ministry of Lands, Agriculture, Fisheries, Water and Rural Development, or FAO.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
A deliberate change in the way land is held, shared or used, for example by redistributing farmland to people with little or none.
:::

:::definition{term="Food security"}
A situation in which all people at all times have physical and economic access to enough safe and nutritious food for an active, healthy life. It has four parts: availability, access, utilisation and stability.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-sufficiency ratio"}
National food production divided by national food requirement, multiplied by 100. A value below 100% means a shortfall, which must be met by imports or food aid.
:::

## The need for land reform

Before 1980 land in Zimbabwe was divided on racial lines. The **Land Apportionment Act of 1930** and the **Land Tenure Act of 1969** reserved large areas of the best-watered land, mostly in the higher-rainfall regions with good soils, for white commercial farms, while most African households lived in Tribal Trust Lands (later called communal areas) in the drier regions with poorer soils. The **Land Husbandry Act of 1951** tried to impose individual holdings and destocking in the reserves and was widely resented. By independence in 1980 a small number of commercial farms occupied a large share of the farmland, while many communal areas were overcrowded and suffered soil erosion and low yields.

Arguments for land reform included correcting these inequalities, giving land to the landless, relieving population pressure in communal areas, and enlarging the base for national food production. Others stressed that the reform had to protect production, investment and jobs.

## The Land Reform Programme (in outline)

1. **1980s: market-based resettlement.** The independence constitution agreed at Lancaster House in 1979 allowed land to be acquired only from willing sellers at market price for the first ten years. Resettlement schemes moved households onto land bought by the state, but the pace was slower than planned.
2. **1992: Land Acquisition Act.** It allowed compulsory acquisition of land with compensation.
3. **From 2000: the Fast Track Land Reform Programme.** A large share of white-owned commercial farmland was acquired and redistributed in two main models: **A1** (smallholder farms, either villagised or self-contained) and **A2** (small to medium commercial farms). Land was also taken in other ways, and the process was disputed.
4. **After 2000.** Later steps dealt with tenure and compensation, including offer letters and leases for beneficiaries, and agreements about compensation of former owners.

## Food production since 2000

Food production changed in a complicated way. Maize output is strongly affected by rainfall, so droughts (for example in 1991–92, 2015–16 and 2018–19 in southern Africa) lower it whatever the land system. After 2000, supporters of the programme argue that many more households now have land to grow food. Critics argue that disruption, loss of experienced farmers, reduced investment, shortage of inputs and an unstable economy lowered output in the early 2000s. Both groups agree that tobacco recovered strongly in the 2010s, with smallholder contract farming responsible for much of the output, while maize and wheat have varied from season to season.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Self-sufficiency and shortfall]
Use season 3 in the table.

1. Self-sufficiency: $\dfrac{1\,400}{1\,800} \times 100 = 77.8\%$.
2. Shortfall: $1\,800 - 1\,400 = 400$ thousand tonnes, which must be imported or met by aid.
3. Mean output over the six seasons: $(2\,200 + 1\,100 + 1\,400 + 800 + 1\,900 + 1\,900) \div 6 = 9\,300 \div 6 = 1\,550$ thousand tonnes, which is $1\,550 \div 1\,800 \times 100 = 86.1\%$ of requirement.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting the pattern"}
Output reaches or exceeds requirement only in seasons 1, 5 and 6, which have normal rainfall, and falls to 800 thousand tonnes in the drought of season 4. The table shows that **rainfall** is the major influence on output, so land reform alone cannot guarantee food security. Even a larger number of farmers cannot raise output without reliable inputs, irrigation and drought-tolerant crops. A fair conclusion is that the data cannot show whether the land system raised or lowered output without also comparing farms of different type in the same season.
:::

## Contribution and limits

**Contribution:** more households with direct access to land; a wider base of producers, including women and young people in some schemes; growth of smallholder crops such as tobacco and cotton under contract; and, in some areas, better food access for beneficiary households.

**Limits:** insecure tenure (many beneficiaries hold offer letters or leases, not title deeds, so they cannot easily use the land as collateral for loans); shortage of inputs, credit, irrigation, machinery and extension advice; drought and climate change; poor infrastructure and markets; and an unstable economy. Food security also depends on **access** (incomes and prices), **utilisation** (nutrition, safe water and health) and **stability** over time, not only on how much maize is harvested.

Programmes to improve food security include the Grain Marketing Board's role in buying and storing grain, input support schemes, conservation farming such as the Pfumvudza (Intwasa) approach from 2020, small grains promotion in dry regions, and irrigation schemes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Taking a political side"}
Write "supporters argue … while critics argue …". Do not use emotional words; support each point with evidence such as production data.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating food security as the same as maize output"}
Food security also needs access, nutrition and stability. A good harvest does not help a household that cannot afford or reach the food.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming or crediting land reform for every change"}
Rainfall, prices, inputs and the economy also change output. Explain which part of a trend the land system could cause and which part it could not.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Colonial land laws gave the best-watered land to commercial farms and left communal areas overcrowded, which created the need for reform.
- Reform moved from market-based resettlement in the 1980s to the Fast Track programme from 2000, with A1 and A2 models.
- Food production varies strongly with rainfall, so trends must be read with climate in mind.
- Land reform widened access to land but production is limited by tenure security, inputs, credit, irrigation and drought.
- Food security means availability, access, utilisation and stability, not only the size of the harvest.
- Use neutral wording, label data and end an assessment with a balanced judgement.
:::
