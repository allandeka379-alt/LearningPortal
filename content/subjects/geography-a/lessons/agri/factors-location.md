## Introduction

Drive from Mutare up into Chimanimani and the crops change from maize and cotton to tea, coffee and timber. Drive south from Masvingo towards Chiredzi and you meet cattle ranches and irrigated sugarcane. The land is the same country, yet farming differs because rainfall, temperature, soil, markets, roads and prices differ. This lesson explains why different farming is found in different places, and then tests one famous attempt to predict farm location, von Thünen's model. You should already know the difference between arable and pastoral farming and what is meant by inputs, processes and outputs of a farm.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Agricultural location"}
The place where a particular type of farming is carried out, decided by a mixture of physical conditions and human and economic choices.
:::

:::definition{term="Agro-ecological region (natural region)"}
One of five zones into which Zimbabwe is divided according to rainfall amount and reliability, temperature and soils, each suited to a range of farming systems.
:::

:::definition{term="Economic rent"}
The profit a farmer earns per unit of land after production costs and transport costs to the market have been paid: economic rent = market price − production cost − transport cost.
:::

:::definition{term="Margin of cultivation"}
The distance from the market at which economic rent falls to zero, so that farming a crop is no longer worthwhile.
:::

## Physical factors

**Climate.** Crops need enough water in the growing season. Rainfall amount, reliability and timing matter more than the annual total alone, because a mid-season dry spell can ruin a maize crop. Temperature sets the length of the growing season: tea and coffee need cool, moist highlands without frost, while cotton and sugarcane need warmth.

**Relief.** Steep slopes cause soil erosion and cannot be ploughed by tractor; flat valley floors are easy to mechanise and irrigate. Height above sea level lowers temperature.

**Soils.** Deep, well-drained loams hold water and nutrients. Sandy granite soils are easily leached and need fertiliser, and heavy clays are hard to work when wet. Soil depth and drainage affect root growth.

**Water supply and natural hazards.** Rivers and dams permit irrigation where rain is short, but drought, hail, frost and flooding are risks.

## Human and economic factors

- **Market and transport:** a nearby market and good roads allow perishable products such as milk and vegetables to reach buyers quickly.
- **Labour and skills:** labour-intensive crops such as tobacco or horticulture need many workers; ranching needs few.
- **Capital and technology:** irrigation, machinery, fertilisers and improved seed need money; many smallholders lack credit.
- **Land tenure and farm size:** security of tenure encourages long-term investment, and the size of the holding limits what can be grown.
- **Government policy and prices:** guaranteed prices, input schemes, subsidies, taxes and marketing rules influence what farmers plant. A change in the price of maize relative to cotton can change the hectares planted in a season.
- **Culture and tradition:** food preferences, inheritance customs and knowledge handed down affect crop choice.

## Zimbabwe's agro-ecological regions

The regions are numbered I to V. The rainfall values are approximate and are illustrative of the system rather than exact boundaries.

| Region | Rainfall (about) | Main character | Typical farming |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | over 1 000 mm, fairly well spread | cool, moist, mainly the Eastern Highlands | tea, coffee, fruit, forestry, dairy, intensive crops |
| II | 750–1 000 mm | reliable rain, good soils | intensive farming: maize, tobacco, cotton, soya beans, wheat under irrigation, dairy |
| III | 650–800 mm | rain with mid-season dry spells | semi-intensive: livestock with fodder crops, maize with risk, drought-tolerant cash crops such as cotton |
| IV | 450–650 mm | frequent seasonal droughts | semi-extensive: cattle ranching, sorghum, millets |
| V | below 450 mm | hot, dry, erratic rain, mostly the lowveld | extensive ranching and wildlife; crops only with irrigation, as with sugarcane in the south-east lowveld |

:::keyfact{title="Learning the regions"}
Rainfall falls and becomes less reliable from Region I to Region V, and farming moves from **intensive** to **extensive**. Region V crops (sugarcane, citrus) depend on irrigation water, not rain.
:::

## Von Thünen's model

Johann Heinrich von Thünen, a German landowner, published his theory in 1826. It explains how land use around a single market town is arranged by transport cost and economic rent.

**Assumptions:** an isolated state with one central market town; a flat plain with uniform soil and climate; farmers who aim to maximise profit and know prices; one means of transport, with transport costs rising in proportion to distance; and no government intervention.

**Prediction:** crops that are perishable, bulky or give high returns per hectare can pay high transport costs per kilometre, so they are grown close to the market. Crops with low transport costs relative to their value can be grown farther out. The model predicts concentric rings: market gardening and dairying, then forestry for fuelwood, then arable crops, then livestock ranching, then wilderness. Each ring is where that land use earns the highest economic rent.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Economic rent and the margin of cultivation]
In this exercise (illustrative figures) vegetables sell at US\$600 per tonne and cost US\$400 per tonne to produce, and transport costs US\$10 per tonne per kilometre. Maize sells at US\$320 and costs US\$180 per tonne, and transport costs US\$4 per tonne per kilometre.

1. Vegetables: rent at distance $d$ km is $600 - 400 - 10d = 200 - 10d$.
2. Maize: rent is $320 - 180 - 4d = 140 - 4d$.
3. Margin of cultivation: rent is zero when $200 - 10d = 0$, so $d = 20$ km for vegetables, and $140 - 4d = 0$, so $d = 35$ km for maize.
4. Crossover: $200 - 10d = 140 - 4d$ gives $60 = 6d$, so $d = 10$ km.

Within 10 km vegetables earn more rent (US\$150 per tonne at 5 km compared with US\$120 for maize), so vegetables are grown near the town. From 10 to 35 km maize earns more, and beyond 35 km neither pays, so cheaper land uses such as extensive grazing take over.
:::

:::example{title="Testing the model on a farming area"}
Around a provincial town in Zimbabwe a student maps land use and finds: vegetable and dairy farms within about 10 km, maize and tobacco farms from 10 to 40 km, and cattle ranching beyond 40 km. This matches the sequence of the model. But the student also notes that a belt of irrigated horticulture lies along a river 30 km away, and that soils are better to the north. These show that the assumptions of a flat plain with uniform soil do not hold. Roads running out of the town make some places cheaper to reach than others, so the rings are distorted into strips along the roads. The model therefore explains the **main pattern** but not every detail.
:::

## Applicability and limits

The model is useful because it shows that distance and transport cost matter, and it fits some modern patterns: milk and vegetable farms around Harare and Bulawayo, and ranching on the edge of the country. Its limits are that real land is not uniform, farmers do not have perfect knowledge, transport is faster, cheaper and more varied than in 1826, refrigeration allows perishables to travel, governments set prices and quotas, and there are many markets rather than one. In Zimbabwe, agro-ecological regions, irrigation water and land policy explain location at least as much as distance to the market.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without linking them to farming"}
Writing "rainfall" is not enough. Say how: "Region IV receives under 650 mm in an unreliable pattern, so drought-tolerant sorghum and cattle are preferred to maize."
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating von Thünen's rings as a rule"}
The model is a simplification. State the assumptions and then say where the real area departs from them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing economic rent with farm rent"}
Economic rent is the surplus profit per unit of land after production and transport costs, not the money a tenant pays to a landlord.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Farming depends on physical factors (climate, relief, soils, water) and human and economic factors (markets, transport, labour, capital, tenure, policy).
- Zimbabwe's Regions I to V move from high, reliable rainfall and intensive farming to low, erratic rainfall and extensive ranching.
- Economic rent = price − production cost − transport cost; the margin of cultivation is where rent is zero.
- Von Thünen predicts rings of land use around a market, with perishable and costly-to-transport products closest.
- The model rests on strict assumptions, so it explains the broad pattern but is distorted by relief, roads, irrigation and policy.
:::
