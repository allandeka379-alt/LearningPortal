## Introduction

Zambia earns most of its export income from one metal, copper, taken from the ground on the Copperbelt and sold to buyers on the other side of the world. South Korea, which in the early 1960s was one of the poorer countries in Asia, now sells cars, ships, microchips and phones to the world. Both trade, but their positions are very different. This lesson explains the factors that shape what countries trade and how much they gain from it, and then compares the two contrasting countries. You should already know the patterns of world trade, the balance of trade and the terms of trade.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Protectionism"}
Government policies that shield domestic producers from foreign competition, such as tariffs, quotas, subsidies and non-tariff barriers.
:::

:::definition{term="Transnational corporation (TNC)"}
A company that owns or controls production or services in more than one country.
:::

:::definition{term="Newly emerging economy"}
A country with fast-growing industry and trade, and rising incomes, that is moving from low or middle income towards high income, such as China, India, Brazil and South Korea in earlier decades.
:::

:::definition{term="Export-led industrialisation"}
A development strategy that builds industry mainly to sell manufactured goods abroad, using trade to gain income, technology and skills.
:::

## Factors influencing trade

| Group | Factors | Effect on trade |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | location (coast, ports, landlocked), mineral and farmland resources, climate | coastal countries with ports trade cheaply; landlocked countries pay more for transport; resources decide what is exported |
| **Historical** | colonialism and its legacy | colonies were organised to supply raw materials to the ruling country; railways ran from mines and farms to ports; borders and trade links still reflect those patterns |
| **Economic** | supply and demand, prices, **logistics and ICT** (containers, air freight, tracking, online ordering), finance and exchange rates, workforce skills and wages, level of development | efficient shipping and communications cut costs; skilled, lower-cost workers attract factories; developing countries often have little capital for processing |
| **Political** | trade blocs and **bilateral agreements** that reduce tariffs between partners; **protectionism** such as tariffs, quotas and subsidies; stability and law | members trade more with each other; protection can shield industries but raises prices and may start trade disputes |
| **Corporate** | **TNCs**, including those from emerging economies, such as Samsung (South Korea), Tata (India), Huawei (China) and Vale (Brazil) | they organise global supply chains, decide where to make and sell goods, and shape trade in manufactures |
| **Price setting** | many commodities, such as copper, are priced on world exchanges such as the London Metal Exchange | producers are "price takers": they cannot set the price, so earnings change when world prices change |

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Effect of a tariff]
A country imports a machine at US\$200. It then charges a tariff of 15% (illustrative data).

1. Tariff: $0.15 \times 200 = 30$, so US\$30.
2. Price to the buyer, before other costs: $200 + 30 = 230$, so US\$230.
3. If a local firm makes the same machine for US\$220, the tariff makes the local machine cheaper than the import and protects the local firm; but buyers who preferred the imported machine now pay more.
:::

:::worked-solution[How concentrated are a country's exports?]
Shares of export earnings (illustrative data). The concentration index is the sum of the squared shares, which is 10 000 for a country with one export and falls as exports are spread.

| Export | Country A (%) | Country B (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Copper (A) / vehicles (B) | 72 | 25 |
| Cobalt (A) / electronics (B) | 8 | 20 |
| Tobacco (A) / ships (B) | 6 | 15 |
| Maize (A) / chemicals (B) | 0 | 15 |
| Other | 14 | 25 |

1. Country A: $72^2 + 8^2 + 6^2 + 0^2 + 14^2 = 5\,184 + 64 + 36 + 0 + 196 = 5\,480$.
2. Country B: $25^2 + 20^2 + 15^2 + 15^2 + 25^2 = 625 + 400 + 225 + 225 + 625 = 2\,100$.
3. Country A's exports are far more concentrated, so a fall in one price harms it much more.
:::

## Contrasting countries

### Zambia: dependent on a primary commodity

Zambia is a landlocked country in southern Africa. **Resource and history:** copper on the Copperbelt was developed in the colonial period and sent by rail to ports; after independence in 1964 the mines were nationalised, and from the 1990s to 2000 state mining assets were privatised. **Pattern:** copper provides well over half of export earnings in most years, with cobalt and agricultural products also sold. **Effects:** a boom in copper prices in the 2000s brought growth and investment; the fall in prices from 2014 and 2015, together with high borrowing, weakened the currency, and in 2020 Zambia failed to make a payment on one of its foreign bonds and debt restructuring talks followed. **Constraints:** a landlocked position (goods go through ports in Tanzania, Mozambique and South Africa), few processing industries, and limited diversification.

### South Korea: an exporter of manufactures

South Korea has few natural resources but a coastal location with major ports such as Busan. **History and strategy:** from the 1960s governments promoted export-led industrialisation through five-year plans, credit for large family-owned firms (chaebol such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG), and heavy investment in education. **Pattern:** exports are mainly manufactured goods: semiconductors, vehicles, ships and petrochemicals, and the country has trade agreements with the United States and the European Union. **Effects:** incomes rose from low levels in the early 1960s to those of a high-income country, and South Korean firms are now TNCs. **Constraints:** dependence on imported energy and materials, on a few large firms, and on foreign demand that can fall in a global slowdown.

| Feature | Zambia | South Korea |
|---|---|---|
| Position | landlocked | coastal with major ports |
| Main exports | copper (primary commodity) | manufactures: vehicles, chips, ships |
| Value per tonne | low and price-taking | high and set by firms |
| Main risk | price falls and debt | global demand and import dependence |
| Role of TNCs | foreign mining companies | Korean TNCs expanding abroad |

:::example{title="Comparing the two countries"}
Both countries trade, but their gains differ. Zambia sells a raw material whose price is set on world markets, so earnings change sharply when the copper price changes. South Korea sells goods that carry higher value per tonne and that it designs and brands, so it captures more of the value. The difference is explained by history (raw-material colony versus state-led industrial strategy), location (landlocked versus coastal) and investment in skills.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
The factors are needed by both boards (ZIMSEC Form 6, Paper 2; Cambridge A Level Paper 4 option 13). **Cambridge** also requires **two contrasting countries** with dated detail, one dependent on primary commodity exports, as in this lesson. Check any figure before quoting it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without examples"}
Support each factor with a named example, such as landlocked Zambia or containerisation reducing shipping costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying colonialism no longer matters"}
Explain the legacy: patterns of raw-material exports, transport routes to ports and unequal trade ties can persist.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing countries without a judgement"}
After a comparison, state which factors best explain the difference and give a reasoned conclusion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4 (option 13). *Explain* factors using groups (physical, historical, economic, political); *compare* two countries using a table of features; *assess* how far a country's trade position is explained by resources or by policy; *evaluate* the effect of protectionism. In Cambridge essays give dated facts for both countries and reach a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Physical factors include location and resources; historical factors include the colonial legacy of raw-material export.
- Economic factors include prices, logistics, ICT, finance and workforce skills; political factors include trade blocs and protectionism.
- TNCs, including those from emerging economies, organise global supply chains.
- Zambia depends on copper, a price-taking primary commodity, and is landlocked.
- South Korea industrialised through exports of manufactures with state support and heavy investment in education.
- Differences are explained by history, location, policy and the value of what is traded.
:::
