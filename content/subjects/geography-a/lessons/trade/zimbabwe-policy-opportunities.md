## Introduction

Early on a weekday morning at Beitbridge, traders queue with bales, cartons and sacks. Some are importing goods from South Africa to sell in Harare, others are exporting produce, and large trucks wait in the freight lanes. At Mupedzanhamo in Harare, second-hand clothing is sold at prices that few shops can match. All of this is trade, shaped by government policy, by the border, and by what people can earn and afford. This lesson looks at the policies that govern trade, the traders who are affected, the inequalities in who gains, and how a community can design a local enterprise. You should already know the meaning of imports, exports and value addition.

:::note{title="Policies and published figures"}
Policies change often. Details below are in outline and should be checked against current rules. Any published figure you use should be quoted with its source and year; the figures in worked examples are illustrative.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trade policy"}
The rules and measures a government uses to control trade, such as tariffs, quotas, import bans, licences, export incentives and trade agreements.
:::

:::definition{term="Tariff"}
A tax charged on imported goods, which raises their price and may protect local producers.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal cross-border trade"}
Small-scale buying and selling of goods across a border by individuals, often in small quantities and with little or no formal registration.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-benefit analysis"}
A way of judging a project by comparing all its costs with all its benefits, in money where possible and in words where not.
:::

## Trade policies and their effects on traders

| Policy | Purpose | Possible effects on traders |
|---|---|---|
| Import duties (tariffs) | raise revenue and protect local firms | goods cost more; traders pay duty or avoid it by using informal routes |
| Import restrictions and bans (for example the measures of 2016 on a list of goods) | protect local industry and save foreign currency | local producers gain; traders lose stock and some goods become scarce or dearer |
| Import licences and export permits | control and record trade | delays and costs for small traders; fewer abuses |
| Export incentives | encourage foreign sales | exporters gain; smallholders may not qualify |
| Regional agreements (SADC, COMESA, the African Continental Free Trade Area) | lower barriers between members | cheaper regional trade, but competition from stronger producers; rules of origin |
| Simplified trade regimes for small consignments | help small traders clear goods quickly | lower costs and fewer delays, if traders know the rules |

## Cross-border and large-scale trade

**Cross-border trade** is carried out by traders, many of them women, who buy clothing, food, household goods and electronics in South Africa, Botswana, Zambia and Mozambique to sell in Zimbabwe, and who carry Zimbabwean products out. It supports many households and spreads goods to areas without shops, but traders face long queues, costs of transport and duty, risks to safety, and difficulty in obtaining finance. **Large-scale trade** involves companies exporting minerals, tobacco and other produce and importing fuel, machinery, vehicles and chemicals, using formal documents, banks and freight transport, mostly through the Beitbridge border post.

**Inequalities in trading patterns** include differences between large firms and small traders in capital, information and access to markets; between urban and rural areas; between men and women; and between Zimbabwe's trade in primary products and its imports of manufactured goods.

## Cheap imported used goods

Used clothing, shoes, vehicles and electronics are sold at low prices. **Advantages:** poor households can afford clothes and goods; many people earn a living as importers, sellers and repairers. **Disadvantages:** local clothing, textile and shoe factories cannot compete, so jobs and demand for cotton fall; money leaves the country to pay for imports; goods may be poor quality or unsuitable, and waste increases. Governments therefore sometimes restrict imports while supporting local manufacturing.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Profit of a cross-border trader]
A trader buys goods in Johannesburg for US\$600. Transport costs US\$80 and duty and fees cost US\$70. She sells the goods in Harare for US\$1 050 (illustrative data).

1. Total cost: $600 + 80 + 70 = 750$, so US\$750.
2. Profit: $1\,050 - 750 = 300$, so US\$300.
3. Return on cost: $300 \div 750 \times 100 = 40\%$.
4. Profit as a share of sales: $300 \div 1\,050 \times 100 = 28.6\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Cost-benefit analysis of a honey enterprise]
A village group plans to produce and pack honey. Equipment costs US\$2 700 once. Each year, sales are US\$4 200 and running costs (hives, jars, labels, transport) are US\$2 400 (illustrative data).

1. Annual net benefit: $4\,200 - 2\,400 = 1\,800$, so US\$1 800.
2. Payback period: $2\,700 \div 1\,800 = 1.5$ years.
3. Over 5 years, total net benefit is $5 \times 1\,800 = 9\,000$; after the equipment cost, $9\,000 - 2\,700 = 6\,300$, so US\$6 300.
4. Non-money benefits: three part-time jobs, use of local flowers and trees, and skills for members. Costs not in money: time spent and the risk of bad seasons.
:::

## Designing and defending a local trade enterprise

1. **Choose a product** that uses local resources and has a market: honey, peanut butter, baobab oil, dried fruit, clothing, crafts, or processed grain.
2. **Research the market:** who will buy, how much they will pay, who the competitors are, and the quality required.
3. **Plan production:** raw materials, equipment, premises, labour, packaging and a brand.
4. **Cost-benefit analysis:** list start-up and running costs and expected sales, and estimate profit and payback.
5. **Check policy and standards:** registration, health and labelling rules, and any export documents.
6. **Defend the plan:** argue that benefits (jobs, local value addition, income retained in the area) outweigh costs, and say how risks will be managed, such as by starting small, saving, and insuring against shocks.

:::example{title="Defending a local product"}
A group proposes to process baobab fruit into a powder for drinks. **Reasons:** baobab trees grow wild in the lowveld, so the raw material is cheap; processing adds value; the product has a market among health-conscious buyers. **Risks:** a small market, the need to meet food safety rules and the cost of packaging. **Defence:** begin with local sales, apply for food safety approval, and compare costs and revenues before expanding.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating all imports as harmful or all as beneficial"}
Weigh gains for consumers and traders against losses for local producers, and name who is affected.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting statistics without a source"}
Say where a figure comes from (for example ZimStat) and the year, or use "illustrative" if the numbers are invented.
:::

:::mistake{title="A cost-benefit analysis with only money costs"}
Include benefits such as jobs and skills and costs such as time and risk, even if they cannot be given a money value.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Trade policies include tariffs, bans, licences, incentives and regional agreements, each with winners and losers.
- Cross-border traders, many of them women, supply goods and income but face costs and risks.
- Large-scale trade is formal, mostly through Beitbridge, and is dominated by minerals, tobacco and imported fuel and machinery.
- Cheap used goods help consumers but harm local manufacturers.
- A cost-benefit analysis compares money and non-money costs and benefits, and payback time.
- A good local enterprise uses local resources, has a market and adds value.
:::
