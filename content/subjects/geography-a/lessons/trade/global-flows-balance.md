## Introduction

The phone in your pocket may have been designed in one country, assembled in a second from parts made in several others, shipped across the Indian Ocean in a container, and sold in Harare by a company that imports it. Zimbabwe sells gold, platinum group metals and tobacco and buys fuel, machinery and medicines. The movement of goods and services between countries is world trade, and it is uneven: some countries sell high-value manufactures and services, others depend on raw materials. This lesson describes global trade flows and patterns, the idea of the balance of trade, and Zimbabwe's trading position. You should already know the meaning of imports, exports and value addition.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Visible trade"}
The export and import of physical goods, such as minerals, crops and manufactured products.
:::

:::definition{term="Invisible trade"}
The export and import of services, such as tourism, banking, insurance, shipping and communications.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance of trade"}
The difference between the value of a country's visible exports and visible imports: exports − imports. A positive value is a surplus; a negative value is a deficit.
:::

:::definition{term="Terms of trade"}
The relationship between the prices a country receives for exports and the prices it pays for imports, usually shown as (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100.
:::

:::definition{term="Trade bloc"}
A group of countries that agree to reduce or remove trade barriers between members, such as the European Union (EU), ASEAN or SADC.
:::

## Flows, routes and patterns

- **Main flows.** Large flows join East Asia, Europe and North America, because these regions have large industries and markets. Manufactured goods flow mainly from East Asia (China, Japan, South Korea) to Europe and North America; energy flows from the Gulf, Russia and other producers to importing regions; raw materials and food flow from Africa, South America and Australia to manufacturing regions.
- **Major routes.** Most goods travel by sea in containers. Key routes cross the North Atlantic and the Pacific, and link Asia and Europe through the Strait of Malacca, the Red Sea and the Suez Canal, or around the Cape of Good Hope. The Panama Canal links the Atlantic and Pacific. Narrow passages such as Hormuz, Malacca, Suez and Bab-el-Mandeb are "choke points": if they are blocked, trade is delayed and costs rise.
- **Trade blocs.** Much world trade takes place within and between large blocs such as the EU, USMCA (United States, Mexico and Canada), ASEAN, SADC and COMESA. In Africa, the African Continental Free Trade Area began trading in 2021 to raise trade between African countries, which is a small share of their total trade.
- **Primary commodities and manufactures.** Many developing countries export primary commodities (minerals, crops) whose prices are low per tonne and change often. Developed and newly industrialised countries export manufactures and services with higher and steadier value. A country that mainly sells raw materials and buys manufactures can find that its **terms of trade** worsen when commodity prices fall.
- **Invisible trade.** Services such as tourism, finance and shipping are an increasing share of trade, especially for developed countries and small island states.

### Inequalities in volume and value

A small number of countries and regions account for most of the **volume** and **value** of trade. Africa contributes a small share of world exports, and most of it is raw materials. Because manufactured goods carry far more value per tonne, a country exporting 100 000 t of ore may earn less than a country exporting 1 000 t of machinery. Inequalities also arise from history, from protection of rich-country markets, from transport costs and from differences in capital, skills and technology.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Balance of trade]
In a year a country's visible exports were US\$5 200 million and visible imports were US\$6 000 million (illustrative data).

1. Balance of trade: $5\,200 - 6\,000 = -800$, so a **deficit** of US\$800 million.
2. Exports as a percentage of imports: $5\,200 \div 6\,000 \times 100 = 86.7\%$, so exports paid for 86.7% of imports.
3. To balance trade at the same import level, exports would need to rise by $800 \div 5\,200 \times 100 = 15.4\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Terms of trade]
A country's export price index is 120 and its import price index is 150, with the base year set at 100 (illustrative data).

1. Terms of trade: $120 \div 150 \times 100 = 80$.
2. A value below 100 means the terms have worsened since the base year.
3. To buy the same quantity of imports the country must export $100 \div 80 = 1.25$ times as much, which is 25% more.
:::

## Zimbabwe's trade

**Exports** are dominated by minerals (gold, platinum group metals, nickel, chrome and ferrochrome, and lithium) and tobacco, with sugar, cotton, and horticultural products. **Imports** include fuel, machinery, vehicles, chemicals, fertiliser, wheat and cooking oil, medicines and manufactured consumer goods. South Africa is Zimbabwe's most important single trading partner, and China, the United Arab Emirates and neighbouring countries such as Zambia and Mozambique are also important (check current figures with ZimStat and ZimTrade, quoting the year). In many years Zimbabwe has recorded a **visible trade deficit**, which means it must cover the gap with remittances, borrowing, investment and services income.

**Improving the balance of trade and adding value:** process minerals before export (beneficiation); turn tobacco, cotton, milk and grain into higher-value products; diversify exports; expand regional trade under SADC, COMESA and the AfCFTA; reduce imports by producing substitutes locally (import substitution) while keeping industry efficient; and encourage investment, reliable power and trade facilitation at borders.

:::example{title="Describing a trade graph"}
A table shows a country's exports: copper 70% and other products 30% of the total. **Describe:** the country relies on one commodity for most of its export earnings. **Explain:** copper prices change on world markets, so earnings are unstable. **Evaluate:** diversification and processing would reduce risk, but need investment, skills and reliable power.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing balance of trade with balance of payments"}
The balance of trade covers only visible exports and imports (goods). The balance of payments is wider and includes services, income and transfers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a trade deficit is always bad"}
A deficit may reflect imports of machinery that raise future output. Judge it by its size, its cause and how it is financed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unreliable statistics"}
Quote the source and year of any trade figure, or say the number is illustrative.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Visible trade is goods; invisible trade is services; both flow mostly between East Asia, Europe and North America.
- Most trade goes by sea along key routes and through choke points such as Suez and Malacca.
- Trade blocs reduce barriers between members and shape flows.
- Primary commodities have low, unstable value compared with manufactures, so terms of trade can worsen.
- Balance of trade = exports − imports; a deficit has to be financed.
- Zimbabwe exports minerals and tobacco and imports fuel and manufactures; value addition and diversification can improve its balance.
:::
