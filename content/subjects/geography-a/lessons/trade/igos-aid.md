## Introduction

When two countries disagree about a tariff, there are rules and a forum to settle it. When a flood or a famine strikes, money, food and workers arrive from abroad. Both are examples of international organisations at work in trade and development. The World Trade Organization (WTO) and the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) shape the rules and the terms on which countries trade, while aid transfers resources from richer to poorer countries. Whether these actions help or harm is a matter of judgement. This lesson, needed for Cambridge A Level Paper 4 (option 13), explains their roles and how to evaluate them. You should already know the patterns of world trade, trade blocs and protectionism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Intergovernmental organisation (IGO)"}
An organisation whose members are governments, formed by treaty to co-operate on a shared aim, such as the WTO, UNCTAD, the World Bank and the United Nations.
:::

:::definition{term="Aid"}
Resources (money, goods, services or expertise) given or lent on easy terms to another country or to people in need. Official development assistance (ODA) is aid from governments whose main aim is the economic development and welfare of developing countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Tied aid"}
Aid that must be spent on goods or services from the donor country (or from a named group of firms), rather than freely chosen by the recipient.
:::

:::definition{term="Aid for Trade"}
Support, begun as a WTO initiative in 2005, that helps developing countries build the roads, ports, skills and rules they need to take part in trade.
:::

## The World Trade Organization (WTO)

The WTO was set up in 1995 to replace the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947, and has its headquarters in Geneva. Its roles are to provide a forum for **negotiating** trade rules, to **administer** the agreements, to **settle disputes** between members, and to **monitor** national trade policies. Key principles are **non-discrimination** (a country should give all members the same treatment, and treat imports and domestic goods alike) and the gradual lowering of barriers to trade. Its agreements include one on **intellectual property** (TRIPS), which protects patents and copyright.

**Impacts and evaluation.** Supporters argue that rules lower tariffs, make trade more predictable and give small countries a way to challenge larger ones. They point out that the dispute system has allowed weaker members to bring cases. Critics argue that richer countries have more influence in negotiations, that rich-country farm subsidies and barriers to textiles and farm goods remain, that intellectual property rules can raise the cost of medicines and technology, and that talks launched in 2001 to favour developing countries (the Doha Round) did not conclude. In recent years the appeal stage of dispute settlement has not worked fully. The impact on a particular country depends on its size, products and ability to take part in negotiations.

## UNCTAD

UNCTAD was created in 1964 as a body of the United Nations, also based in Geneva. Its focus is the interests of developing countries in trade, investment and development. It carries out **research** and publishes reports on trade and investment, provides **technical help**, such as customs software and advice on trade policy, supports **negotiation** between developing and developed countries, and promoted the idea of preferential tariffs for developing countries through the **Generalised System of Preferences**. It has no power to enforce rules, so its influence comes from research, advice and the agreement of members.

## Types of aid

| Type | Meaning | Example of use |
|---|---|---|
| **Humanitarian (emergency)** | short-term relief after disasters and conflicts | food, tents and medicines after a cyclone |
| **Development** | long-term help for health, schools, water and agriculture | building clinics, training teachers |
| **Climate** | finance for mitigation and adaptation to climate change | solar schemes, flood defences |
| **Bilateral** | from one government directly to another | a donor government funds a road project |
| **Multilateral** | pooled through IGOs such as the World Bank or UN agencies | a loan for a dam from a development bank |
| **Tied** | must be spent on goods and services from the donor | a donor funds a bridge built by its own firms |
| **Untied** | the recipient chooses how to spend it | budget support |
| **NGO aid** | from non-governmental organisations funded by donations | Oxfam or Médecins Sans Frontières projects |

**Donors** include rich governments, IGOs, NGOs, private foundations and increasingly governments of emerging economies. The UN set a target in 1970 that rich countries give **0.7% of their gross national income (GNI)** as ODA; only a few have met it consistently.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[The 0.7% target]
A donor has a GNI of US\$2 000 billion and gives US\$9 billion in aid (illustrative data).

1. Aid as a percentage of GNI: $9 \div 2\,000 \times 100 = 0.45\%$.
2. Target amount: $0.007 \times 2\,000 = 14$, so US\$14 billion.
3. Shortfall: $14 - 9 = 5$, so US\$5 billion below the target.
:::

:::worked-solution[Tied aid and the amount that stays with the donor]
A donor gives US\$600 million of aid, of which 40% is tied to purchases from its own firms. The recipient has a population of 15 million (illustrative data).

1. Tied aid: $0.40 \times 600 = 240$, so US\$240 million must be spent on the donor's goods and services.
2. Untied aid: $600 - 240 = 360$, so US\$360 million can be freely spent.
3. Aid per person: $600\,000\,000 \div 15\,000\,000 = 40$, so US\$40 per person.
:::

## Evaluating aid

| | Benefits | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| **For the recipient** | saves lives in emergencies; pays for health, schools, water, roads; transfers skills and technology; supports poor people and government budgets | dependence on donors; corruption and weak planning; tied aid reduces its value; projects may not suit local needs; loan repayments; conditions that limit policy choices |
| **For the donor** | influence, security and trade links; moral and political goals; markets and contracts for its firms, especially with tied aid | cost to taxpayers; criticism if aid is misused or fails to reach those in need |

The effectiveness of aid depends on the type (emergency aid works quickly; large infrastructure takes years), how it is spent, how well it is managed and whether local people take part. **Judgement:** aid is most effective when it is untied, targeted at health, education and basic services, planned with recipients, and monitored for results.

:::example{title="Evaluating tied aid"}
A donor offers a loan to build a bridge, on condition that its own firm does the work and that materials come from the donor country. **Benefits:** the bridge is built and improves transport. **Costs:** much of the money goes back to the donor's firm, local firms and workers gain less, and the recipient must repay the loan. **Judgement:** the bridge helps, but untied aid would have kept more of the money in the recipient economy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing the WTO as a bank or a charity"}
The WTO is a rule-making and dispute-settling organisation for trade. It does not lend money. UNCTAD is a research and advice body, not a lender.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming all aid is a gift"}
Some aid is a loan that must be repaid, and tied aid obliges the recipient to buy from the donor.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation of aid"}
Give benefits and problems for both the recipient and the donor, and then a judgement on when aid works.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The WTO (1995, Geneva) negotiates trade rules, settles disputes and monitors policy; its results favour some members more than others.
- UNCTAD (1964, Geneva) researches, advises and speaks for developing countries but cannot enforce rules.
- Aid may be humanitarian, development or climate, bilateral or multilateral, tied or untied, and from governments, IGOs or NGOs.
- The UN target is 0.7% of GNI as official development assistance.
- Aid saves lives and builds services, but can create dependence, be tied, or be misused.
- Aid works best when it is untied, planned with recipients and monitored.
:::
