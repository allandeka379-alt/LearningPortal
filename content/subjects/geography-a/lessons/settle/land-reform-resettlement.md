## Introduction

Land is the most important resource for most Zimbabweans, and how it is shared shapes where people live, how they farm and what services they can reach. Land reform in Zimbabwe changed the pattern of rural settlement more than any other event since independence. This lesson explains why land reform took place, describes the resettlement models and the settlements they created, and assesses the effects on services and on the development of settlements. Land reform is a sensitive subject, on which people hold different views. In examinations you are expected to describe events and evidence accurately and to give balanced explanations, using the viewpoints of different groups without taking sides. The effects on food production are covered in the agriculture lesson.

You should already know the terms commercial farm, communal area and settlement pattern.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Land reform"}
A change in the ownership, control or use of land, usually to make its distribution more equal or more productive. In Zimbabwe it meant the acquisition of land, mainly from large-scale commercial farms, and its redistribution to other farmers.
:::

:::definition{term="Resettlement"}
The movement of households onto land that has been acquired and planned for settlement, with plots allocated and sometimes with planned villages and services.
:::

:::definition{term="A1 and A2 models"}
The two main models of the Fast Track Land Reform Programme that began in 2000. A1 provided small plots for family farming, in either villagised or self-contained layouts. A2 provided larger plots for small and medium commercial farming.
:::

## Why land reform took place

1. **Historical inequality:** Before independence in 1980, laws such as the Land Apportionment Act (1930) and the Land Tenure Act (1969) reserved the better land, with good soils and rainfall, mainly for white commercial farmers, while most black Zimbabweans lived in communal areas (formerly Tribal Trust Lands), where the land was generally poorer, the rainfall less reliable and the population dense.
2. **Pressure on communal land:** Population growth in communal areas led to overcrowding, soil degradation and landlessness.
3. **Political and social expectations:** The liberation struggle was fought partly over land, so people expected the new government to redress the imbalance.
4. **Limited early progress:** The Lancaster House agreement (1979) required, for the first ten years, that land be acquired on a "willing buyer, willing seller" basis. The first resettlement programme in the 1980s settled families on land purchased by the government, but the pace was slower than many expected, because of the cost and the amount of land offered.
5. **Acceleration from 2000:** From 2000, following land occupations and disagreement over the pace of reform, a programme of compulsory acquisition ("fast track") settled large numbers of households in a short time. Supporters described this as righting a historic wrong; critics pointed to disruption of production, loss of jobs for farm workers and insecure tenure.

## The resettlement models

| Feature | A1 model | A2 model |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Decongest communal areas; give land to many families for household farming | Create small to medium-scale commercial farms |
| Plot | Small arable plot plus grazing; either a **villagised** layout (homes in a village, fields separate, shared grazing) or a **self-contained** layout (an individual farm with house, fields and grazing together) | A larger plot, generally managed as a commercial farm |
| Settlement pattern | Clustered in villages (villagised) or dispersed (self-contained) | Dispersed homesteads on separate farms, often using existing farmhouses |
| Services | Planned village sites need schools, clinics and water; usually built after settlement | Existing farm infrastructure such as houses, dams, barns and roads |

**Settlement patterns:** Villagised A1 areas have a nucleated pattern that makes it easier to provide services; self-contained A1 and A2 farms are dispersed, so services are more costly to deliver per person.

## Services, infrastructure and effects on settlements

- **Services:** New resettlement areas often began with few schools, clinics, roads or boreholes, and these had to be provided over time by government, councils, churches and the settlers themselves. Many children walked long distances to school until new schools were built.
- **Infrastructure:** Roads and bridges built for commercial farming were often in poor condition; irrigation systems and farm buildings required maintenance, and the supply of electricity depended on existing lines.
- **Rural service centres:** New business centres and markets grew at junctions and schools, and some existing centres gained customers while others lost the demand of former commercial farms.
- **Farm worker settlements:** Compounds that housed workers on former commercial farms faced changes in employment; some workers stayed and worked for new farmers, and others moved to towns, communal areas or informal settlements. Their rights and housing were a concern for many.
- **Population distribution:** Population density increased on former commercial farmland, and pressure eased on some communal areas, though not all.
- **Environment:** Clearing of land for fields and for curing tobacco in some places increased deforestation and soil erosion.
- **Planning:** Planned layouts, land-use plans and tenure documents (offer letters, permits and leases) affect investment, because secure tenure makes it easier to borrow money and improve the land.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Change in population density]
A former commercial farming district covers 24 000 ha (240 km²). Before reform it had 12 farms and 1 800 people, including workers' families. After resettlement it has 400 A1 households, each with 6 people (illustrative data).

1. Density before = 1 800 ÷ 240 = 7.5 people per km².
2. People after = 400 × 6 = 2 400; density after = 2 400 ÷ 240 = 10 people per km².
3. Increase = (10 − 7.5) ÷ 7.5 × 100 = 33%.
4. The district supports more households on the land, but pressure on services rises. This must be matched by schools, clinics and water.
:::

:::worked-solution[Arable share of A1 plots]
In the same district each A1 household has 60 ha in total, including 6 ha of arable land.

1. Arable area in the district = 400 × 6 = 2 400 ha.
2. Share of the district = 2 400 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 10%.
3. The rest is grazing and woodland, so many households keep livestock and use woodland for fuel, which increases pressure on those resources.
:::

:::example{title="Assessing services in a new village"}
A villagised A1 area was settled in 2002 with 80 households. By 2022 it has a primary school, a borehole for each 15 households and a clinic 12 km away, but no secondary school and no electricity. A balanced assessment says that basic services have improved with time (school and water), but that distance to the clinic and the absence of a secondary school and power limit development, so people rely on the nearest growth point or town for these.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing opinions instead of evidence"}
Give causes, features and effects with examples, and present the views of different groups (for example supporters and critics) with reasons, without taking a side.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing A1 and A2"}
A1 is for small household farms (villagised or self-contained); A2 is for small to medium commercial farms.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the settlement effects"}
This topic is about settlements: patterns, services and infrastructure. Do not answer only about crop production.
:::

Keep wording neutral.

## Summary

:::summary
- Land reform responded to unequal land distribution at independence, pressure on communal areas and expectations after the liberation struggle.
- The first resettlement used willing buyer, willing seller; fast track acquisition began in 2000.
- A1: small plots, villagised or self-contained; A2: larger commercial plots.
- Villagised layouts cluster homes and make services easier; dispersed layouts make services costly.
- New settlements needed schools, clinics, roads and water; effects on farm workers, rural centres and the environment varied.
:::
