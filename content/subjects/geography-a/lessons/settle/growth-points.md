## Introduction

Large cities attract people because they offer jobs, schools, clinics and shops, while rural areas often lack them. Left alone, this pattern leads to crowded cities and emptying villages. Zimbabwe's response, adopted in the early 1980s, was the **growth point policy**: to choose rural centres and develop them with services, infrastructure and enterprises, so that they would become engines of local development. The idea draws on the growth pole theory, which holds that development concentrated in one place can spread to the region around it. This lesson explains the concept, the characteristics of growth points, how successful they have been, and what could speed their growth.

You should already know the settlement hierarchy and the ideas of threshold and range.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Growth point"}
A rural settlement designated by the government for planned development, with infrastructure, services and employment, so that it serves the surrounding rural area and encourages local development. Growth points sit between rural service centres and towns in the hierarchy.
:::

:::definition{term="Growth pole and spread effects"}
Perroux's growth pole idea (1950s) is that economic activity concentrates in a particular place, from which benefits spread to the surrounding area as demand for food, labour and services grows (spread effects). If benefits fail to spread, or resources are drawn into the centre, the effects are backwash effects.
:::

## Reasons for establishing growth points

1. To spread development to rural areas and reduce the gap between urban and rural living standards.
2. To create jobs outside the large cities and reduce rural-urban migration.
3. To provide services (education, health, banking, markets) near the people.
4. To create markets and processing centres for farm produce and other local resources.
5. To use resources more efficiently by concentrating investment in a few centres, rather than spreading it too thinly across hundreds of villages.
6. To correct the colonial pattern, in which investment concentrated in a few towns and commercial farming areas.

## Characteristics and services

Growth points usually have a planned layout with residential, commercial and industrial areas, serviced stands, and are administered by a rural district council or local board. Typical services and facilities:

- Social: primary and secondary schools, a clinic, a police station.
- Economic: shops, a produce market, grain-marketing depots, grinding mills, bank agency or mobile money outlets, a bus terminus, fuel outlets.
- Infrastructure: tarred or graded roads, piped water, electricity, telecommunications.
- Enterprises: small-scale manufacturing and processing, such as milling, carpentry and brickmaking.

Examples that you may use are well-known growth points such as Murewa, Mutoko, Mount Darwin and Gokwe. Quote only the facts you are sure of, and refer to them as examples of rural centres that have grown as service and market centres.

## Evaluation of the policy

| Successes | Problems |
|---|---|
| Improved access to schools, clinics, shops and markets for the surrounding population | Slow development in many growth points; services often incomplete |
| Some jobs in trade, services, transport and small industry | Limited employment because few industries were attracted to remote places |
| Markets for farm produce; fewer long journeys to town | Shortages of water, electricity, roads and housing |
| Planned layout and serviced land | Dependence on government funding, which was limited by the economic problems of the country |
| Some centres have grown into towns | Weak links with farming areas; migration to the large cities continued |
| Improved local administration | In some growth points serviced stands remain unsold or undeveloped; investors prefer the large cities where markets and infrastructure are better |

Overall judgement: the policy improved services in rural areas and gave some centres a start, but it did not create enough employment to stop migration. Its success depended on investment, infrastructure and links with the surrounding farming economy.

## Speeding up growth with local resources

**Projects:** agro-processing (milling of maize, groundnut and sunflower oil pressing, fruit drying, meat and dairy processing); cotton ginning and cotton products; leather and furniture from local materials; brick and cement products using local clay and stone; small-scale mining services; rural tourism and crafts; solar and biogas energy services; horticulture with irrigation.

**Measures for future growth:**

1. Provide reliable electricity, water, roads and internet access, because investors need them.
2. Offer incentives (tax breaks, cheaper serviced land, credit) and simple licensing for small businesses.
3. Link growth points with farmers through contract farming, produce markets and storage.
4. Train local people in skills and business management.
5. Give local authorities more funds and power (devolution) and encourage public-private partnerships.
6. Improve public transport so that people can reach services.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Growth of a growth point]
A growth point had 5 200 people in 2002 and 8 700 in 2022 (illustrative data).

1. Increase = 8 700 − 5 200 = 3 500; as a percentage of the start, 3 500 ÷ 5 200 × 100 = 67.3%.
2. Annual compound rate $r$: $(8\,700 / 5\,200)^{1/20} = 1.0261$, so $r = 2.6\%$ a year.
3. At 2.6% a year the population would double in about 70 ÷ 2.6 = 27 years. This is a steady rate but modest for a centre that was meant to grow quickly.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing services]
A checklist of 12 services was used (primary school, secondary school, clinic, bank agency, produce market, bus terminus, police station, grinding mill, fuel outlet, piped water, electricity, mobile network). Growth point A has 9 of them and Growth point B has 5.

1. A: 9 ÷ 12 × 100 = 75%.
2. B: 5 ÷ 12 × 100 = 41.7%.
3. A is better provided with services. Because every service counts equally, the index hides the quality and the size of the services, so field observation is needed in addition.
:::

:::example{title="Assessing a project"}
A farmers' group and the rural district council propose a groundnut and sunflower oil press at a growth point. Benefits: a market for local farmers, 15 to 20 jobs, value added to local crops, and a supply of oil and cake for livestock. Needs: three-phase electricity, a water supply, transport to take oil to cities, and credit for the machine. Risks: low supply in drought years, price changes and competition from imports. A good assessment states benefits, needs and risks and judges whether the local resources are enough to make the project work.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling any small town a growth point"}
A growth point is a centre designated by government policy for planned development, not simply a small town.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing only successes or only failures"}
A fair evaluation gives both, then a judgement supported by evidence such as services, jobs and population growth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Suggesting projects without local resources"}
Link every project to a resource available locally (crops, minerals, labour, tourism) and to what it needs to succeed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 9), often as a 25-mark question with a final *assess* or *evaluate* part. *Explain* the reasons for growth points; *describe* services from a table or map description; *evaluate* the policy with successes, problems and a judgement; *suggest* projects and measures that fit local resources. Name Zimbabwean examples only where you are sure of the facts.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Growth points are rural centres designated for planned development to spread services and jobs.
- They draw on the growth pole idea that benefits spread out from a centre.
- Typical features: schools, clinics, markets, banks, roads, water, electricity and small industry.
- The policy improved services but did not create enough employment or stop migration to the big cities.
- Growth can be speeded by reliable infrastructure, local processing, incentives, skills, devolved funds and links with farmers.
:::
