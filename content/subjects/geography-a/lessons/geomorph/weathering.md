## Introduction

The great granite domes (dwalas) of the Matobo Hills are bare and rounded, yet in many places in the Zimbabwean savanna the same rock lies beneath several metres of crumbly, red-brown soil. In the cool, misty Eastern Highlands, rocks are covered by deep weathered material, while in the hot lowveld they are often exposed and cracked. **Weathering**, the breakdown of rock where it lies, explains the difference, and it is the first step in the formation of soil, slopes and many landforms.

You should already know the three rock types and that water, temperature and living things all act on rock. This lesson sorts the processes, links them to climate and then looks at the factors besides climate that change how fast weathering happens. Landforms of weathered rock in the tropics, and the movement of weathered material downslope, are in other lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Weathering"}
The breakdown of rock in place, by physical, chemical or biological processes, at or near the surface. No transport by a moving agent is involved, which separates weathering from erosion.
:::

:::definition{term="Regolith"}
The layer of loose, weathered material (rock fragments, clay, soil) that covers solid rock. It can be several tens of metres deep in humid tropical areas.
:::

:::definition{term="Peltier diagram"}
A graph of mean annual temperature (horizontal axis) against mean annual rainfall (vertical axis) that shows which kinds of weathering are dominant in each climate. It is a ZIMSEC requirement and is useful for Cambridge too.
:::

## Physical weathering

Physical (mechanical) weathering breaks rock into smaller pieces without changing its chemistry.

1. **Freeze-thaw (frost shattering).** Water gets into joints and cracks and freezes when the temperature falls below 0 °C. Ice occupies about 9% more volume than water, so it pushes the crack wider. Repeated cycles of freezing and thawing break the rock into angular fragments. It needs water, jointed rock and temperatures that cross 0 °C often. It is important on high mountains and in cold regions and occurs in Zimbabwe only on the highest ground on winter nights.
2. **Pressure release (unloading).** When overlying rock is removed by erosion, the pressure on rock below is reduced and it expands. Cracks open parallel to the surface (sheeting joints), and slabs peel off like layers of an onion, a process called **exfoliation**. It is a good explanation of the curved sheets seen on granite domes.
3. **Thermal expansion and contraction.** Rock heated by day and cooled by night expands and contracts. Minerals expand by different amounts, so stresses build up and flakes or grains break off. Laboratory tests show that this works much more strongly when moisture is present, so the process is better seen as moisture-assisted.
4. **Salt crystallisation.** Salty water enters pores and cracks. As it evaporates, salt crystals grow and press on the walls of the pores, which loosens grains. It is common near the coast and in dry climates where evaporation is strong.

## Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering changes the minerals in rock, usually through reactions with water. It is faster where it is warm and wet.

- **Hydrolysis:** feldspar in granite reacts with slightly acidic water to form clay minerals (kaolin) and soluble ions. The granite crumbles to a sandy, clayey material. It is the main process behind deep weathering of granite in the tropics.
- **Carbonation:** rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes weak carbonic acid. It reacts with calcium carbonate in limestone and dissolves it: $\ce{CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 -> Ca(HCO3)2}$. The calcium hydrogencarbonate is soluble and is carried away.
- **Oxidation:** iron-bearing minerals react with oxygen to form iron oxides, which colour rock and soil red or brown and make the rock weak and crumbly.
- **Solution:** soluble minerals such as rock salt and gypsum dissolve directly in water.

## Biological weathering

Roots grow into cracks and widen them as they thicken; burrowing animals and termites mix and expose rock. Lichens and plant roots release organic acids. **Chelation** is the process by which organic compounds bind metal ions such as iron and aluminium and remove them from minerals, which weakens the rock. Decaying vegetation also raises soil carbon dioxide and acidity, which speeds up carbonation.

## Weathering and climate: the Peltier diagram

| Climate | Mean annual temperature | Mean annual rainfall | Dominant weathering |
|---|---|---|---|
| Humid tropical (rainforest) | High | High | Chemical very strong; deep regolith |
| Savanna (wet and dry seasons) | High | Moderate and seasonal | Chemical moderate; some physical |
| Hot desert | High | Very low | Weak overall; thermal and salt weathering |
| Cold and moist (mountain or periglacial) | Low, crossing 0 °C | Moderate | Freeze-thaw strong |
| Polar (cold and dry) | Very low | Very low | Very slow |

The idea behind the diagram: chemical weathering needs both heat and water, so it is strongest at high temperature and high rainfall. Freeze-thaw needs frequent freezing and thawing with water, so it peaks in cold, moist climates. Where it is dry, whether hot or cold, all weathering is slow. As a rule of thumb, the rate of a chemical reaction roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise in temperature.

## Non-climatic factors

- **Rock type and structure.** Limestone is dissolved by carbonation but granite resists it; the many joints, bedding planes and faults of a rock give water routes to enter. Rocks with more minerals that are unstable at the surface (feldspars) weather faster.
- **Relief.** On steep slopes, weathered material is removed quickly and fresh rock is exposed. On gentle slopes and flat ground, a thick regolith builds up and holds water against the rock. Aspect matters in mountains: sun-facing slopes heat up and shaded ones keep moisture.
- **Vegetation.** Roots and organic acids speed up weathering, but plant cover also shades and protects the surface from extremes of temperature.
- **Human influence.** Quarrying exposes fresh rock. Burning coal and fuel produces acid rain, which speeds up the weathering of buildings and statues. Clearing vegetation and ploughing expose the soil and the rock.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Measuring weathering with limestone tablets]
A class buried identical 50.0 g limestone tablets at three sites for 24 months and re-weighed them (illustrative data, invented for this exercise):

| Site | Start mass (g) | Mass after 24 months (g) | Loss (g) | Loss (%) | Loss (% per year) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: shaded stream bank (wet) | 50.0 | 47.8 | 2.2 | 4.4 | 2.2 |
| B: open grassland slope | 50.0 | 48.9 | 1.1 | 2.2 | 1.1 |
| C: dry rock outcrop | 50.0 | 49.6 | 0.4 | 0.8 | 0.4 |

1. Loss = start mass − final mass. For site A: $50.0 - 47.8 = 2.2$ g.
2. Percentage loss = loss ÷ start mass × 100 = $2.2 \div 50.0 \times 100 = 4.4\%$.
3. Per year: $4.4 \div 2 = 2.2\%$ a year.
4. Site A lost the most, site C the least. Site A is moist and its soil holds carbon dioxide, so carbonation is fast, while site C is dry, so little solution takes place.
:::

:::worked-solution[Applying the temperature rule of thumb]
Suppose a chemical weathering reaction proceeds at a relative rate of 1 unit at a mean temperature of 10 °C. Estimate its rate at 30 °C using the rule that the rate doubles for each 10 °C rise.

1. The temperature rise is $30 - 10 = 20$ °C, which is two steps of 10 °C.
2. The rate doubles twice: $1 \times 2 \times 2 = 4$ units.
3. The reaction is about **four times** as fast at 30 °C as at 10 °C. This explains in part why deep weathering is found in the warm, humid tropics. The rule is only approximate and the real rate also depends on moisture.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing the dominant weathering for a climate"}
A station on a high plateau has a mean annual temperature of 4 °C, cold winter nights that fall below 0 °C and 900 mm of rain a year (illustrative). **Identify:** freeze-thaw is likely to dominate. **Explain:** the temperature often crosses 0 °C and rainfall supplies water for the joints. Chemical weathering is limited by the low temperatures. A station in the hot lowveld with 450 mm a year and mean temperature of 25 °C has the opposite problem: it is warm but dry. Chemical weathering is slow, and thermal stress and salt weathering are relatively important.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect physical, chemical and biological weathering and their dependence on climate and rock. ZIMSEC (5.2) requires you to justify weathering types for climates with the Peltier diagram and to explain the non-climatic factors; Cambridge examines weathering in tropical environments (7.2.1, an A Level topic) with coastal and arid applications in those options.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using weathering and erosion as the same thing"}
Weathering breaks rock down in place. Erosion involves removal by a moving agent (water, wind, ice). Say which one you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Claiming freeze-thaw is the main process in all of Zimbabwe"}
Zimbabwe rarely has freezing temperatures. Chemical weathering (hydrolysis of granite, oxidation) and moisture-assisted thermal weathering are more important across most of the country.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing carbonation as 'acid rain'"}
Carbonation uses natural carbonic acid formed when rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide. Acid rain from pollution is a separate, human-made addition.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 5) and Cambridge Paper 3 (option 7, tropical environments). *Name* and *describe* each process with its key condition. *Explain* needs the mechanism in steps (what enters the rock, what changes, what breaks). *Compare* climates using temperature and rainfall in every sentence. Weathering-rate data should be described with figures, using percentages per year. For *assess the importance of climate*, weigh climate against rock type, relief and vegetation and reach a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Weathering is the breakdown of rock in place; erosion needs transport.
- Physical processes: freeze-thaw, pressure release, thermal and salt crystallisation.
- Chemical processes: hydrolysis, carbonation, oxidation, solution; biological: roots, burrowing, chelation.
- Chemical weathering is fastest where it is hot and wet; freeze-thaw where it is cold and moist; all slow in dry places.
- Rock type, joints, relief, vegetation and human activity change the rate.
- Percentage loss = (start − final) ÷ start × 100; a reaction rate roughly doubles per 10 °C.
:::
