## Introduction

Look at a hillside in the Zimbabwean Highveld: the crest is rounded, the middle is straight and steep, and the bottom curves out gently into a vlei or river valley. This shape is not an accident. It is the result of weathering, soil movement and running water acting for a very long time on rock of a particular kind, and it shows what has happened on that hill in the past. Geographers measure slopes in the field and compare them with models to explain how landscapes evolve.

You should know the processes of weathering from the previous lesson. This lesson describes slope profiles, shows how to survey one, and then compares the three classic models of slope development. Mass movements as hazards are in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Slope profile"}
A cross-section of the ground from the crest of a hill to the valley floor, showing how the angle changes along the slope.
:::

:::definition{term="Slope elements"}
The parts of a slope profile: the **waxing slope** (convex, angle increases downslope), the **free face** (a cliff or bare rock), the **constant slope** (straight, rectilinear) and the **waning slope** (concave, angle decreases downslope). A pediment is a gently sloping rock surface at the foot of a steep slope.
:::

:::definition{term="Base level"}
The lowest level to which a river can erode, normally sea level for the main river. Falling base level makes rivers cut down and steepens valley sides.
:::

## Slope elements and the processes that shape them

| Element | Shape | Dominant processes |
|---|---|---|
| Crest and waxing slope | Convex | Soil creep and slow weathering; material moves downslope more with distance |
| Free face | Steep to vertical | Rock falls and weathering of exposed rock |
| Constant slope | Straight | Transport of debris by mass movement and wash; angle controlled by rock strength |
| Waning slope | Concave | Wash and deposition; thin sheets of water and slopewash lay down fine material |

The **nine-unit model** of Dalrymple and others divides an ideal slope into nine units, from the interfluve at the top to the stream channel at the bottom, and links each to its processes. It is a useful framework but few real slopes have all nine.

**Slope processes** include weathering (which prepares loose material), mass movement (creep, slides, falls), surface wash (rainsplash and running water), throughflow and solution. In humid climates dense vegetation reduces wash and encourages weathering and creep, producing rounded, convex slopes. In semi-arid climates with sparse vegetation wash is more important, and slopes are often steeper and angular.

## Factors affecting slope form

1. **Rock type and structure.** Resistant rocks form steep slopes or cliffs; weak rocks form gentle slopes. Bedding that dips downslope encourages sliding.
2. **Climate.** Rainfall, temperature and vegetation control the rate and kind of weathering and transport.
3. **Processes.** The balance between weathering, creep and wash decides whether a slope is convex, straight or concave.
4. **Base level.** A fall in base level causes rivers to cut down and the lower slope to steepen. A rise leads to deposition at the foot.
5. **Time.** Slopes change slowly, so older landscapes tend to have gentler profiles unless uplift renews them.
6. **Human activity.** Clearing vegetation, ploughing downslope or building roads increases erosion and instability.

## Measuring a slope profile in the field

1. Mark the line of the profile from the crest to the foot with a tape.
2. Place ranging poles at **breaks of slope** or at equal intervals (for example 10 m measured along the ground).
3. At each section use a clinometer to read the angle between two poles of the same height (to avoid error).
4. Calculate vertical height $= L \sin\theta$ and horizontal distance $= L \cos\theta$ for each section ($L$ is the length along the slope).
5. Add the sections up and plot the profile.

Errors include different pole heights, vegetation that blocks the sight line, and slopes that are not typical of the area. Sampling several profiles reduces these problems.

## Models of slope development

| Model | Idea | Result | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Slope decline** (Davis) | The whole slope becomes gentler as the valley floor stops being cut down | Maximum angle falls with time; gently convex crest | Humid temperate lands with soil creep |
| **Parallel retreat** (King) | Slopes keep the same angle and retreat as a whole, leaving a gentle pediment at the foot | Pediment grows, scarp moves back | Semi-arid and savanna lands with resistant caprock |
| **Slope replacement** (Penck) | A gentler lower slope grows at the expense of a steeper upper slope | A convex-concave slope in which the steep part shrinks | Lands where the valley is cut down quickly, then slowly |

![Three models of slope development: in slope decline the whole slope becomes gentler and lower; in parallel retreat the scarp keeps the same angle while it moves back and a pediment widens; in slope replacement a gentle lower slope grows at the expense of the steep upper slope.](media:diagrams/slope-development-models.png "Figure: slope decline, parallel retreat and slope replacement compared (schematic profiles at three times)")

### Planation surfaces

- **Peneplanation** (Davis): long-continued downwearing lowers the whole land to a nearly flat, low plain (a peneplain), with the occasional residual hill.
- **Pediplanation** (King): scarps retreat and pediments widen until they join to form a **pediplain**. Isolated residual hills (inselbergs, such as the dwalas) are left standing. King applied the idea to the plateau of southern Africa.
- **Etchplanation:** deep chemical weathering in the humid tropics creates a thick weathered layer (regolith) beneath the surface. When this is later stripped away, the boundary between sound rock and regolith (the weathering front) becomes the new surface, an **etchplain**. It explains how some inselbergs are exposed.

The three models are ideas that geographers use to interpret real landscapes. Most real landscapes show a mixture and the debate is not settled.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Analysing a surveyed slope profile]
A group measured ten sections of 10 m (measured along the slope) from the crest to the foot of a granite hill (illustrative data, invented for this exercise):

![Surveyed slope profile from the worked example: angles rise from 3 to 24 degrees over the first five sections (convex, waxing slope), stay at 24 degrees over sections 5 to 7 (constant slope) and fall to 4 degrees over sections 8 to 10 (concave, waning slope); total height 24.1 m over 96.0 m, a mean gradient of 25 percent.](media:diagrams/slope-profile-survey.png "Figure: the surveyed slope profile plotted from the angles in the worked example (calculated from 10 sin and 10 cos of each angle; height scale slightly exaggerated)")

| Section | Angle (°) | Vertical = 10 sin θ (m) | Horizontal = 10 cos θ (m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 0.52 | 9.99 |
| 2 | 7 | 1.22 | 9.93 |
| 3 | 12 | 2.08 | 9.78 |
| 4 | 20 | 3.42 | 9.40 |
| 5 | 24 | 4.07 | 9.14 |
| 6 | 24 | 4.07 | 9.14 |
| 7 | 24 | 4.07 | 9.14 |
| 8 | 15 | 2.59 | 9.66 |
| 9 | 8 | 1.39 | 9.90 |
| 10 | 4 | 0.70 | 9.98 |

1. **Describe the shape.** Angles increase from 3° to 24° over sections 1-5 (convex, waxing), stay at 24° over sections 5-7 (straight, constant), then decrease to 4° over sections 8-10 (concave, waning).
2. **Total height.** The vertical components add to about 24.1 m.
3. **Total horizontal distance.** The horizontal components add to about 96.0 m.
4. **Mean gradient.** $24.1 \div 96.0 = 0.251$, so 25%, and the mean angle is $\tan^{-1}(0.251) = 14.1°$.
5. The steepest section is 24°. A constant slope that stays at the same angle over several sections suggests that angle is set by rock strength.
:::

:::worked-solution[How far can a scarp retreat?]
King's model assumes that a scarp retreats parallel to itself. If a scarp retreats at 1.2 mm a year (illustrative), how far does it retreat in 20 million years?

1. Distance $= 1.2 \text{ mm/yr} \times 20\,000\,000 \text{ yr} = 24\,000\,000$ mm.
2. Convert to kilometres: $24\,000\,000 \text{ mm} = 24\,000$ m $= 24$ km.
3. The scarp retreats about **24 km** (1.2 mm a year is 1.2 km per million years). Real rates vary with rock and climate, so this is an estimate.
:::

:::example{title="Matching data to a slope model"}
Imagine a long-term survey (illustrative) in which the maximum slope angle is 35° at the start and is then measured every 2 million years: 28°, 21°, 14°, 7°. **Describe:** the angle falls steadily. **Interpret:** this matches **slope decline**, in which the whole slope becomes gentler. Under **parallel retreat** the maximum angle would stay at about 35° while the slope moved back, and the pediment at the foot would grow. Under **replacement** the steep upper part would shrink while a gentler lower slope grew at its expense.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a concave slope as steepening downslope"}
On a waning (concave) slope the angle **decreases** downslope. A waxing (convex) slope steepens downslope.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming one model fits every landscape"}
Decline, parallel retreat and replacement are ideas that fit different climates and rocks. Support your choice with evidence, such as changing angles or a growing pediment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the angle between poles of different heights"}
If the sighting pole is not the same height as the clinometer, the angle is wrong. Use poles of equal height.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A slope profile can have convex (waxing), straight (constant) and concave (waning) elements, and sometimes a free face.
- Convex slopes are shaped by creep, straight slopes by rock strength, and concave slopes by wash and deposition.
- Rock, climate, processes, base level, time and human activity all affect slope form.
- Field survey: equal-height poles, clinometer angle, $L\sin\theta$ and $L\cos\theta$.
- Models: decline (Davis), parallel retreat (King) and replacement (Penck).
- Planation: peneplanation by downwearing, pediplanation by scarp retreat, etchplanation by stripping of deeply weathered regolith.
:::
