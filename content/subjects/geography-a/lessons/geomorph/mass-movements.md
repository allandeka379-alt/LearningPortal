## Introduction

In March 2019 Cyclone Idai brought several days of heavy rain to the Eastern Highlands of Zimbabwe. On the steep slopes of Chimanimani district, saturated soil and weathered rock gave way in landslides and debris flows that swept through homes, cut roads and bridges and left hundreds of people dead in Zimbabwe. A **mass movement** like this is the downslope movement of rock, soil or debris under gravity, and it is one of the natural hazards that most affects mountain communities in southern Africa.

You should know from the lessons on weathering and slopes that rock is broken down in place and that gravity pulls material downslope. This lesson classifies mass movements by how fast they go and how much water they contain, explains why they happen, and evaluates ways to predict and reduce the hazard.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mass movement"}
The downslope movement of rock, regolith and soil under the force of gravity, with no transporting agent such as a river, though water often helps by adding weight and reducing friction.
:::

:::definition{term="Shear strength"}
The resistance of slope material to sliding. A slope fails when the downslope force (driving stress) exceeds the shear strength.
:::

:::definition{term="Pore water pressure"}
The pressure of water in the spaces between soil particles. When it rises it pushes the particles apart, reduces friction and so reduces the shear strength.
:::

## Types of mass movement

| Type | Description | Speed | Water | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Heave** (creep, solifluction, frost heave) | Slow movement of soil particles, which expand and settle in turn | A few millimetres a year | Damp soil | Gentle to moderate slopes, all climates |
| **Flow** (mudflow, debris flow, earthflow) | Saturated material moves like a thick liquid; debris flows carry boulders along channels | Fast (debris flows) to slow (earthflows) | Very high | Steep, wet slopes with loose material |
| **Slide** (translational slide; rotational slide or slump) | A mass moves along a surface; rotational slides move on a curved surface and leave a scar | Moderate to rapid | Often high | Weak rock, clay, dipping layers, undercut slopes |
| **Fall** (rock fall, topple) | Rock detaches from a cliff and drops, bounces and rolls | Very rapid | Little | Steep faces with joints |

## Causes

Slopes fail when the downslope force grows or the shear strength falls.

**Physical causes**
- **Geology:** weak, weathered or clay-rich rock; joints and bedding that dip downslope.
- **Topography:** steep slopes; slopes undercut by a river.
- **Rainfall:** water adds weight, increases pore water pressure and reduces the strength of soil. Intense or prolonged rain, as with tropical cyclones, is the commonest trigger in southern Africa.
- **Earthquakes and volcanic activity:** shaking reduces the strength of the slope.
- **Climate change:** more intense rainfall and, in cold areas, melting of ice and permafrost.

**Human causes**
- **Construction:** cutting a slope for a road or building removes support at the toe, and buildings add weight.
- **Land-use change:** removing trees takes away roots that bind soil and reduces interception and transpiration; cultivation of steep slopes can leave soil bare.
- **Water management:** leaking pipes, poorly drained irrigation and blocked drains add water to the slope.
- **Quarrying, mining and dumping** of waste on slopes.

## Impacts

- **People:** death and injury, burial of homes, loss of livelihoods; psychological stress; displaced families.
- **Infrastructure:** blocked or destroyed roads, bridges, power lines and water pipes. This isolates communities and delays rescue.
- **Economy:** loss of farmland and livestock; cost of clearing and rebuilding.
- **Environment:** scarring of the hillside, loss of vegetation and soil, sediment entering rivers, and the damming of valleys that may later burst and flood.

Impacts are greater where population density is high, houses are built on or below steep slopes, poverty limits quality of construction and warnings are weak.

## Prediction, mapping and monitoring

- **Hazard mapping** combines slope angle, geology, vegetation and the record of past events to divide land into zones of risk. Local authorities can then restrict building in the highest-risk zones.
- **Monitoring** uses rain gauges (with rainfall thresholds for warnings), piezometers (water pressure), extensometers and tiltmeters (small movements), and satellite or drone images.
- **Warnings** based on rainfall forecasts can trigger evacuation, but fast flows with short warning times are hard to predict exactly.

## Slope stability measures

| Measure | Method | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rock pinning and bolting | Steel bolts anchor loose rock to stable rock | Effective on rock cuts | Costly |
| Netting and mesh | Steel mesh holds loose rock | Protects roads | Does not stop large falls |
| Grading and terracing | Reduces the angle and breaks the slope into steps | Reduces driving stress | Needs space and earthworks |
| Drainage | Surface drains and drilled drains remove water | Lowers pore water pressure | Needs maintenance |
| Retaining walls and gabions | Support the base of the slope | Good for roads and houses | Costly; drains must stay clear |
| Afforestation | Tree and grass roots bind soil and use water | Cheap, lasting, benefits environment | Slow to work; limited on deep slides |
| Planning and education | Restrict building; train communities; early warning | Saves lives at low cost | Needs enforcement |

:::keyfact{title="Learn the match"}
Fast, wet and loose material flows. Cohesive material on a surface slides. Joints on a cliff make rock falls. Slow, damp soil creeps. Water increases weight and pore pressure and reduces strength; vegetation and drainage reduce both.
:::

## Mini case study: Chimanimani, Cyclone Idai (March 2019)

**Where and when:** the mountainous Chimanimani district of the Eastern Highlands of Zimbabwe, 14-17 March 2019.
**Physical causes:** Cyclone Idai brought several days of very heavy rain onto steep slopes with deeply weathered soil, so the slopes became saturated and pore water pressure rose.
**Human factors:** settlement on and below steep slopes and in valleys, farming on slopes and clearance of vegetation in places increased the vulnerability.
**Impacts:** landslides and debris flows buried homes, and roads and bridges were destroyed, cutting off communities. Hundreds of people died in Zimbabwe, many more were displaced, and farmland and infrastructure were damaged.
**Responses:** search and rescue by local people, the army and air support, emergency food and shelter from the government and aid agencies; longer-term road repair and relocation of some households.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Applying a rainfall threshold]
An early-warning scheme (illustrative) says that landslides become likely on steep slopes when the total rainfall in any three consecutive days exceeds 300 mm. Daily rainfall at a station was (data invented for this exercise):

![Daily rainfall of 14, 31, 96, 188 and 142 mm: the three-day total for days 1 to 3 is 141 mm, below the 300 mm landslide threshold, but days 2 to 4 total 315 mm, so a warning is issued on day 4, and days 3 to 5 reach 426 mm, 126 mm over the threshold.](media:diagrams/landslide-rainfall-threshold.png "Figure: three-day rainfall totals against the 300 mm landslide-warning threshold (worked-example data)")

| Day | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 14 | 31 | 96 | 188 | 142 |

1. Days 1-3: $14 + 31 + 96 = 141$ mm. This is below the threshold.
2. Days 2-4: $31 + 96 + 188 = 315$ mm. This exceeds 300 mm, so a warning would be issued on day 4.
3. Days 3-5: $96 + 188 + 142 = 426$ mm. The threshold is exceeded by 126 mm, so the risk is very high.
4. The scheme warns the day the totals first pass 300 mm, which gives a short time to evacuate, and a fixed threshold ignores how wet the soil was before the rain.
:::

:::worked-solution[Scoring a hazard map]
A planner scores four cells of a map from 1 (low) to 3 (high) on four factors and multiplies each score by a weight (slope ×3, rainfall ×2, bare ground ×2, weak geology ×3). The maximum possible score is 30 and the minimum is 10. Illustrative classes: 10-16 low, 17-23 moderate, 24-30 high.

| Cell | Slope (×3) | Rainfall (×2) | Bare ground (×2) | Weak geology (×3) | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 27 |
| B | 2 | 3 | 1 | 2 | 20 |
| C | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 12 |
| D | 3 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 26 |

1. Cell A: $(3 \times 3) + (3 \times 2) + (3 \times 2) + (2 \times 3) = 9 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 27$.
2. Cells A and D are in the **high** class, B is **moderate** and C is **low**.
3. Building would be restricted in A and D, and slope drainage and tree planting suggested for B.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that rain only makes the slope heavier"}
Added weight matters, but the key effect of rain is that rising pore water pressure reduces friction and shear strength.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every movement a landslide"}
Use the correct term: a creep is slow, a flow is wet and fluid, a slide moves along a surface, and a fall drops from a cliff.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recommending only hard engineering"}
Walls and bolts are costly and not affordable for most communities. Strong answers also consider drainage, vegetation, planning and warnings, and judge them on cost and effectiveness.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 5) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 3). *Describe* a movement by the material, speed and shape of movement. *Explain* causes in two steps: the trigger (for example heavy rain), then the mechanism (pore water pressure rises, strength falls). *Evaluate* measures by comparing cost, effectiveness, speed and who benefits, then judge. For a Cambridge case, include a date, a place, two causes, two impacts, a response and your judgement. Do not quote figures that you are not sure of.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mass movements are heaves, flows, slides and falls, classified by speed, water content and movement.
- Slopes fail when the downslope force exceeds shear strength; rain raises pore water pressure.
- Physical causes: geology, steep slopes, rainfall, earthquakes. Human causes: cutting, clearing, water leaks, loading.
- Impacts: deaths, destroyed homes and infrastructure, isolation of communities, loss of land.
- Management: hazard mapping, monitoring, warnings, drainage, grading, netting and pinning, vegetation, planning.
- Idai in Chimanimani (March 2019) is a located example: heavy rain, steep saturated slopes, debris flows and landslides.
:::
