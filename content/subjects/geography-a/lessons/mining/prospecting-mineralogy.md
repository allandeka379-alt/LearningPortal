## Introduction

Long before geologists arrived, people in southern Africa mined gold, copper and iron. They knew that certain plants grew on copper-rich soils, that termites carry grains of rock up from below, and that some hills were rich in ore. Today prospectors combine this knowledge with ultraviolet lamps, soil sampling and instruments that detect hidden rocks. When a sample is found, simple tests of hardness, colour and density help to name the mineral. This lesson covers the main prospecting methods, how to test an ore sample safely, and the properties of Zimbabwe's main minerals. You should know what a rock and a mineral are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prospecting"}
Searching for mineral deposits that may be worth mining, using observation, sampling, instruments and tests.
:::

:::definition{term="Mineral"}
A naturally occurring, solid, inorganic substance with a definite chemical composition and a characteristic crystal structure and properties.
:::

:::definition{term="Ore and gangue"}
An ore is a rock containing enough of a valuable mineral to be mined at a profit. Gangue is the worthless rock and mineral mixed with it, such as quartz in a gold reef.
:::

:::definition{term="Specific gravity (SG)"}
The density of a mineral compared with the density of water, found by $SG = \frac{W_{air}}{W_{air} - W_{water}}$, where $W_{air}$ is the weight of the sample in air and $W_{water}$ its weight when suspended in water.
:::

## Prospecting methods

| Method | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Indigenous knowledge** | Indicator plants (for example the copper flower, which grows on copper-rich soils), termite mounds that bring up material from depth, soil and rock colours, place names and old workings | Local, and needs checking by tests |
| **Panning and trenching** | Washing stream sediment to find heavy gold; digging pits or trenches to expose rock | Only works close to the surface; labour intensive |
| **Ultraviolet (UV) lamp** | Some minerals, such as scheelite (a tungsten ore), glow under ultraviolet light in the dark | Only some minerals fluoresce; eye protection needed |
| **Geochemical** | Samples of soil, stream sediment, rock or water are analysed for traces of metals; a high value points to a nearby deposit | Needs a laboratory; results are affected by soil and rainfall |
| **Geophysical** | Instruments measure physical properties from the surface or the air: magnetism, gravity, electrical resistance, radioactivity and seismic waves | Shows anomalies, not minerals; needs interpretation |
| **Remote sensing and GIS** | Satellite and aerial images show rock types, faults and vegetation changes; GIS combines the layers | Gives clues only; ground checks are needed |
| **Drilling** | Boreholes bring up cores for analysis and show depth and thickness | Expensive; used after other methods have narrowed the search |

## Physical and chemical tests on a sample

- **Colour and streak.** The streak is the colour of the powder left when a mineral is rubbed on an unglazed tile. It is more reliable than surface colour.
- **Lustre.** Metallic, glassy, dull or silky.
- **Hardness** by Mohs scale (talc 1, gypsum 2, calcite 3, fluorite 4, apatite 5, orthoclase 6, quartz 7, topaz 8, corundum 9, diamond 10). A fingernail scratches up to about 2.5, a copper coin about 3 to 3.5, and a steel knife or glass about 5.5. If your sample scratches calcite but not fluorite, its hardness is between 3 and 4.
- **Cleavage and fracture.** Cleavage is breaking along flat planes (galena breaks into cubes); fracture is irregular breaking.
- **Specific gravity.** Weigh in air and in water and calculate.
- **Magnetism.** Magnetite is attracted to a magnet.
- **Acid test.** Dilute hydrochloric acid fizzes on calcite and other carbonates.

**Safety:** wear eye protection and gloves, never taste minerals, work in ventilated places, keep acids and chemicals labelled and away from children, do not enter unsupported pits or old shafts, and tell someone where you are. Avoid mercury and do not use UV lamps without eye protection.

## Zimbabwe's main minerals

| Mineral | Hardness | SG | Properties and uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gold** | 2.5–3 | 19.3 | Soft, yellow, malleable, golden streak; jewellery and reserves |
| **Pyrite** ("fool's gold") | 6–6.5 | about 5.0 | Brassy, brittle, greenish-black streak |
| **Chromite** | about 5.5 | 4.5–4.8 | Black, brown streak; source of chrome for steel |
| **Quartz** | 7 | 2.65 | Glassy, hard, common gangue in gold reefs |
| **Galena** | 2.5 | 7.4–7.6 | Lead-grey, cubic cleavage; lead ore |
| **Chalcopyrite** | 3.5–4 | 4.1–4.3 | Brassy, greenish-black streak; copper ore |
| **Calcite** | 3 | 2.7 | Fizzes in acid; limestone and cement |
| **Diamond** | 10 | 3.5 | The hardest mineral; gems and industrial cutting |

### Co-occurrence of minerals in Zimbabwe

- **Gold** occurs with quartz, pyrite and arsenopyrite in veins in the greenstone belts.
- **Platinum group metals, chromite, nickel and copper** occur together in layers of the Great Dyke.
- **Lithium minerals** (lepidolite, petalite) occur with other minerals in pegmatites, as at Bikita.
- **Coal** forms layers in sedimentary rocks at Hwange.

Knowing associations helps: finding pyrite and quartz veins suggests that gold might be present, and finding chromite layers suggests that platinum group metals may be nearby.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Finding specific gravity]
A yellow metallic sample weighs 38.6 g in air and 36.6 g when suspended in water (illustrative data).

1. Loss of weight in water: $38.6 - 36.6 = 2.0$ g.
2. Specific gravity $= 38.6 \div 2.0 = 19.3$.
3. Conclusion: 19.3 matches gold (SG 19.3), not pyrite (about 5.0). A second sample of 25.0 g in air and 20.0 g in water gives $25.0 \div 5.0 = 5.0$, which matches pyrite.
:::

:::worked-solution[Ore grade]
A 2 500 kg sample of ore contains 12.5 g of gold (illustrative).

1. Convert the mass of ore to tonnes: $2\,500 \div 1\,000 = 2.5$ t.
2. Grade $= 12.5 \div 2.5 = 5$ g per tonne.
3. From 40 t of this ore the gold contained is $40 \times 5 = 200$ g.
:::

:::worked-solution[Interpreting a geochemical survey]
Copper in soil (ppm) at seven stations along a line (illustrative data): 25, 30, 25, 310, 30, 140, 28.

1. The highest value, 310 ppm, is at station 4.
2. Background from stations 1, 2, 3 and 5: $(25 + 30 + 25 + 30) \div 4 = 27.5$ ppm.
3. Anomaly: $310 \div 27.5 = 11.3$ times the background.
4. Conclusion: station 4 and its neighbour (140 ppm at station 6) are anomalous; the next step is trenching or drilling near station 4.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using surface colour to identify a mineral"}
Surface colour varies with weathering and impurities. Use streak, hardness, cleavage and specific gravity together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing gold with pyrite"}
Pyrite is hard (6–6.5) and brittle and has SG of about 5.0; gold is soft (2.5–3), malleable and has SG of 19.3.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting safety"}
Questions on tests often ask for precautions. Mention eye protection, gloves, ventilation, labelled chemicals and avoiding unsupported pits.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Prospecting uses indigenous knowledge, panning, UV lamps, geochemical and geophysical surveys, remote sensing and drilling.
- Tests on samples include colour, streak, lustre, Mohs hardness, cleavage, specific gravity, magnetism and acid.
- Specific gravity = weight in air ÷ (weight in air − weight in water); ore grade is mass of metal per tonne of ore.
- Gold, pyrite, chromite, quartz, galena, chalcopyrite, calcite and diamond differ in hardness and SG.
- Minerals occur together, for example gold with quartz and pyrite and PGMs with chromite on the Great Dyke.
- Use safe methods when testing and sampling.
:::
