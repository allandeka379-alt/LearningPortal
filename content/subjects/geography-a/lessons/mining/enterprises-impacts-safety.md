## Introduction

Mining supports thousands of Zimbabwean families, from a team of panners on a river bank to the workforce of a large platinum mine on the Great Dyke. It also leaves scars: open pits and waste dumps, silted rivers, dust, and sometimes injury and illness for the workers themselves. A geographer asks whether a mine is worth opening, who gains and who loses, and how harm can be reduced. This lesson compares types of mining enterprise, shows how a proposal and a cost-benefit analysis are prepared, and examines impacts, mitigation, health hazards and safety measures. You should know the main laws and the idea of an Environmental Impact Assessment.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small-scale mining"}
Mining by individuals, families or small teams with simple tools and little capital, often alluvial or surface workings, such as gold panning or small underground workings. It may be registered or informal (artisanal).
:::

:::definition{term="Large-scale mining"}
Mining by companies with large capital, heavy machinery, processing plants and many employees, for example a deep underground or open-cast mine.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)"}
Comparing the total costs and benefits of a project, including environmental and social ones where possible, to decide whether it is worthwhile.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid mine drainage"}
Acidic water, often rich in dissolved metals, that forms when sulphide minerals in waste rock or tailings react with air and water.
:::

## Small- and large-scale enterprises

| Feature | Small-scale | Large-scale |
|---|---|---|
| Capital and technology | Low; hand tools, simple machines | High; heavy machinery, plants |
| Employment | Few workers, often family or informal | Hundreds or thousands, formal jobs |
| Output | Small and variable | Large and steady |
| Efficiency | Low recovery of minerals | High recovery; processing on site |
| Regulation | Often weak; many unregistered | Strict; EIAs, safety inspections |
| Environmental effect | Many scattered sites; mercury, siltation, unfilled pits | One large site; waste dumps, dust, water use, but easier to monitor |
| Importance | Livelihoods, source of local income and of gold sales | Exports, foreign currency, taxes |

## The mining proposal and cost-benefit analysis

A proposal should include: the mineral and its estimated quantity and grade; the claim and rights; the method of mining and processing; capital and running costs; markets and expected income; the workforce and training; environmental plans (EIA, mitigation, rehabilitation); safety and health measures; and a timetable. The **CBA** compares:

- **Costs:** capital, wages, power, equipment, royalties and taxes, rehabilitation, and the cost of damage to land, water and health.
- **Benefits:** income from sales, jobs, taxes and royalties, infrastructure, skills and spending in the local economy.

The project may be worthwhile if the benefits exceed the costs, if payback is acceptable, and if damage can be reduced. Successes and failures of past projects (for example due to falling prices, poor planning, power cuts or poor management) should be studied.

### Contribution of mining to the economy

Mining earns export income and foreign currency, pays taxes and royalties, creates jobs and demand for services, and supplies materials for industry. It may also lead to dependence on a few minerals and exposure to world price changes.

## EIA of a mine

The assessment follows the stages of an EIA: **scoping** identifies the main issues (water, land, dust, communities); **baseline studies** measure the existing environment; **impacts** are predicted for each phase (building, operation, closure); **public consultation** gives affected people a voice; **mitigation and a management plan** set out measures and responsibilities, including a plan for **closure and rehabilitation**; and monitoring checks the results.

## Impacts and mitigation

| Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| **Land degradation:** pits, waste dumps, removal of vegetation and topsoil | Plan the layout, store topsoil, backfill pits, level and re-vegetate dumps with local species |
| **Water pollution and acid mine drainage; mercury and cyanide** | Line and cover tailings dams, treat and recycle water, avoid mercury (use gravity methods and retorts), monitor |
| **Siltation of rivers** | Keep operations away from banks, use settling ponds, vegetate banks |
| **Dust and noise** | Water sprays, enclosed crushers, tree belts, working hours |
| **Deforestation and loss of habitat** | Limit clearing, replant, protect sensitive areas |
| **Social:** displacement, conflict over land, crowding and pressure on housing and services | Consultation, fair compensation, community development, housing |
| **Positive impacts:** jobs, income, roads, clinics, schools | Local hiring and procurement, community funds |

## Diseases and hazards

- **Dust diseases:** silicosis (from silica dust of quartz-bearing rock), pneumoconiosis and, with asbestos, lung damage and cancer. Silicosis increases the risk of tuberculosis.
- **Other illnesses:** hearing loss from noise, mercury poisoning in gold panning, heat stress, skin and eye problems, and the spread of infections such as sexually transmitted infections and HIV in crowded mining settlements, where prevention and health services are needed.
- **Accidents:** rock falls and roof collapse, flooding of workings, fire and explosion (including methane in coal mines), poisonous gases such as carbon monoxide, and machinery and transport accidents.

## Safety and health policy and design

Employers should have a written policy and plan with responsibilities, training, health checks, reporting of accidents and inspection by the authorities. Measures include:

- **Shaft and tunnel supports:** timber or steel props and sets, rock bolts, concrete lining (shotcrete) and pillars of unmined rock left to hold up the roof.
- **Ventilation** shafts and fans to supply fresh air and remove gas and dust; **dust suppression** by water sprays.
- **Protective equipment:** helmets, boots, goggles, respirators, ear protection and lamps.
- **Emergency preparation:** escape routes, refuge areas, rescue teams, first aid, gas detection.
- **Safe design:** stable pit slopes, safe spacing of pillars, drainage of water, and safe storage of explosives.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Payback for a small mine]
A proposal for a small gold mine (illustrative figures, not real data): capital cost US\$600 000. The mine processes 3 000 t of ore a year at an average grade of 5 g per tonne, with 80% of the gold recovered. Gold sells at US\$30 per gram in this exercise. Operating costs are US\$210 000 a year and a royalty of 5% of sales is paid.

1. Gold recovered: $3\,000 \times 5 \times 0.80 = 12\,000$ g.
2. Sales: $12\,000 \times 30 = 360\,000$, so US\$360 000.
3. Royalty: $5\% \times 360\,000 = 18\,000$.
4. Annual profit before tax: $360\,000 - 210\,000 - 18\,000 = 132\,000$.
5. Payback: $600\,000 \div 132\,000 = 4.5$ years.
6. Evaluation: if the gold price or grade falls, payback lengthens; environmental and rehabilitation costs must also be included.
:::

:::worked-solution[Health and safety figures]
Illustrative data: of 600 miners screened, 36 had signs of silicosis. In one year a mine recorded 18 injuries in 1.2 million hours worked.

1. Silicosis rate: $36 \div 600 \times 100 = 6\%$, or 60 per 1 000 miners.
2. Injury frequency rate per million hours: $18 \div 1.2 = 15$.
3. Interpretation: the figures show a need for dust control, protective equipment and regular medical checks; a falling injury rate over several years would show that safety measures are working.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining the effect of acid mine drainage"}
**Describe:** water draining from a waste dump has a pH of 3, compared with 7 in the river above it. **Explain:** sulphide minerals in the waste react with air and water to form sulphuric acid, which dissolves metals. The pH scale is logarithmic, so a pH of 3 is $10^4$ = 10 000 times more acidic than pH 7. The water kills fish and plants and is unsafe to drink. **Mitigation:** cover the waste, divert water, treat the drainage.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing only environmental damage"}
Mining brings real economic benefits as well. A balanced answer weighs costs and benefits for different groups.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing mitigation with rehabilitation"}
Mitigation reduces impacts while the mine operates (for example dust suppression); rehabilitation restores the land after mining (backfilling, re-vegetation).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to include environmental costs in a CBA"}
A CBA that only counts money income and wages ignores the cost of damage and clean-up.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Small-scale mining needs little capital and gives livelihoods but is hard to regulate; large-scale mining uses heavy capital, creates formal jobs and exports.
- A proposal and a cost-benefit analysis compare costs and benefits, including environmental and social effects.
- Mine impacts include land degradation, acid mine drainage, mercury and cyanide, siltation, dust, noise and social change; mitigation and rehabilitation reduce them.
- Dust diseases (silicosis), noise, mercury, heat and accidents are major health hazards.
- Safety depends on supports, ventilation, protective equipment, training, inspection and emergency plans.
- A good evaluation weighs benefits and costs for different groups and reaches a judgement.
:::
