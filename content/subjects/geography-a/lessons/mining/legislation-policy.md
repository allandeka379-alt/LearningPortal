## Introduction

Zimbabwe is rich in minerals: gold, platinum group metals and chrome on the Great Dyke, coal at Hwange, nickel, diamonds in Manicaland, lithium at Bikita and many more. Mining is one of the country's most important sources of export earnings and jobs. A mineral in the ground belongs to the state, so a person or company that wants to prospect or mine must follow the law. A farmer who finds gold on her land cannot simply start digging, and a company cannot export raw diamonds without the proper certificates. This lesson outlines how mining rights are obtained, what the main laws and bodies do, and how government policy affects mining. You should know the main minerals of Zimbabwe and the idea of a government policy.

:::note{title="Check current law"}
Laws, policies and fees change. The names and outlines below are for learning the structure of the system and should be verified against current legislation. Examination questions follow the syllabus wording.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mining right"}
The legal right to prospect for, or extract, minerals in a defined area, granted by the state under mining law.
:::

:::definition{term="Claim"}
A piece of ground, marked out by beacons and registered under the mining law, over which the holder has the right to mine a specific mineral.
:::

:::definition{term="Mining policy"}
The government's plans and rules for how minerals are explored, mined, processed, sold and taxed, and who benefits.
:::

## Steps to gain mining rights over a claim (in outline)

1. **Find out who holds the land and rights.** Check records of existing claims and ownership; on private or communal land, consult the landholder, the Rural District Council and local leaders, since mining affects farming and homes.
2. **Obtain a prospecting licence** (or permission to prospect) from the mining authority. A licence allows searching for a mineral in a specified area for a stated period.
3. **Peg the claim.** Mark the corners and boundaries with beacons, and display notices stating the holder and the mineral.
4. **Lodge an application** for registration with the Mining Commissioner, with the sketch plan, discovery details and fees, within the period set by law.
5. **Registration.** If the application is accepted, a certificate of registration is issued; fees and work requirements must be met each year or the claim can be lost.
6. **Environmental approval.** Before mining, an Environmental Impact Assessment is usually required and a certificate must be obtained from the Environmental Management Agency.
7. **Mining and reporting.** The holder works the claim, follows safety and environmental rules, keeps records, pays royalties and taxes and sells minerals through the approved channels.

Precious stones and large mining projects involve special grants and agreements, which are more complex.

## The main laws and bodies

| Law or body | Role (in outline) |
|---|---|
| **Mines and Minerals Act** | Main law on prospecting, claims, mining rights, the Mining Commissioner and Mining Affairs Board, royalties and safety |
| **Environmental Management Act** (Chapter 20:27) | Requires environmental impact assessments and controls pollution, land degradation and rehabilitation; EMA is the regulator |
| **Minerals Marketing Corporation of Zimbabwe (MMCZ)** | State body that markets and sells most minerals on behalf of producers and handles export documentation |
| **Gold Trade Act** and **Precious Stones Trade Act** | Control the buying, selling and export of gold and of diamonds and other gemstones, including licences |
| **Zimbabwe Mining Development Corporation (ZMDC)** | State-owned company that holds mining interests and partners with investors |
| **Fidelity Printers and Refiners** | Buys gold from small-scale miners and refines it |
| **Kimberley Process** | International certification scheme (started in 2003) to stop trade in conflict diamonds; members, including Zimbabwe, must certify shipments |

## Policies and their effects

Policies include **local participation requirements**, **royalties and taxes**, controls on the **export of unprocessed minerals** (for example, a ban on exports of raw lithium ore announced in December 2022 to encourage processing in Zimbabwe), formalisation of small-scale miners and gold-buying schemes.

| Possible benefits | Possible costs |
|---|---|
| More income for the state through royalties and taxes | Red tape, delays and uncertainty may discourage investors |
| Control of trade reduces smuggling and gives more reliable statistics | Miners may sell illegally if official prices or payment are poor |
| More processing in the country, creating jobs and value | Processing needs power, skills and capital which may be lacking |
| Environmental rules reduce damage | Costs of EIAs and rehabilitation are high for small miners |
| Supporters argue that local ownership keeps more benefit in Zimbabwe | Critics argue it can slow foreign investment; views differ |

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Royalty and net income]
A small-scale gold miner sells 500 g of gold at US\$65 per gram. In this exercise a royalty of 5% is paid on the sales value (illustrative rate, not the current legal rate).

1. Value of sales: $500 \times 65 = 32\,500$, so US\$32 500.
2. Royalty: $5\% \times 32\,500 = 1\,625$, so US\$1 625.
3. Income after royalty: $32\,500 - 1\,625 = 30\,875$, so US\$30 875.
4. Costs such as labour, tools, fuel and fees must still be deducted to find profit.
:::

:::worked-solution[Area of a claim]
In this exercise a claim is 150 m wide and 300 m long.

1. Area: $150 \times 300 = 45\,000$ m².
2. In hectares: $45\,000 \div 10\,000 = 4.5$ ha.
3. A block of 10 claims would cover $10 \times 4.5 = 45$ ha.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a ban on raw mineral exports"}
**For:** it encourages processing in Zimbabwe, which keeps more value, creates jobs and skills, and may raise export earnings per tonne. **Against:** processing plants need electricity, water, skills and finance; if they are not available, miners may be unable to sell and output falls; buyers may look elsewhere. **Judgement:** the policy can add value if it is supported by power, infrastructure and clear rules, otherwise it may reduce income in the short term.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a landowner owns the minerals under the land"}
Minerals belong to the state, not the landholder. A mining right is granted under the mining law, though landholders must be consulted and may be compensated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing Acts without saying what they do"}
Give each Act's role: for example, the Environmental Management Act requires an EIA before mining begins.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided evaluation of policies"}
Give benefits and costs for different groups (the state, large companies, small-scale miners, communities and the environment) and then a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Minerals belong to the state; mining rights are obtained through prospecting licences, pegging, registration and approvals.
- The Mines and Minerals Act governs claims and mining; the Environmental Management Act requires EIAs.
- MMCZ markets minerals, ZMDC holds state mining interests, and trade in gold and precious stones is controlled by their own Acts.
- The Kimberley Process certifies diamonds to prevent trade in conflict diamonds.
- Policies such as royalties, export controls and local participation have benefits and costs for different groups.
- Check details against current law; a good evaluation ends with a judgement.
:::
