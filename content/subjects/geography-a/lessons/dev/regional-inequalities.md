## Introduction

Development is never spread evenly. In Zimbabwe, a household in a Harare suburb is more likely to have piped water, electricity, a secondary school and a clinic nearby than a household in a remote lowveld district. The same pattern appears between regions of Africa and between countries of the world. Geographers call these differences **regional inequalities** and explain them with ideas such as the core-periphery model. This lesson describes the inequalities, explains their causes and impacts, shows how a survey of disparities can be done, and evaluates solutions.

You should already know the main indicators of development from the lesson on indicators.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Regional inequality"}
A difference between regions of a country, or between world regions, in income, employment, services, infrastructure and living standards.
:::

:::definition{term="Core and periphery"}
The core is the region with the greatest concentration of wealth, industry, services and political power. The periphery is the region of lower income and fewer opportunities, often remote, which supplies labour and raw materials to the core. Friedmann (1966) used the idea to explain how regions differ.
:::

:::definition{term="Cumulative causation and spread and backwash effects"}
Myrdal (1957): once a region gains an advantage, growth tends to feed on itself; investment, skilled workers and firms move to it. Backwash effects draw resources from the periphery into the core. Spread effects are the benefits that flow outwards from the core to the periphery, such as demand for farm produce. Backwash effects often outweigh spread effects in the early stages.
:::

## The pattern

- **In Zimbabwe:** the cities of Harare and Bulawayo and the Highveld commercial and mining districts have the highest incomes, best services and infrastructure, while remote and dry districts in the lowveld and along borders are generally poorer, with fewer schools, clinics, roads and jobs. Provinces differ in poverty and in access to water and electricity.
- **In Africa:** core regions include Gauteng in South Africa (roughly a third of the country's output), the Nile Delta around Cairo, and the coastal belts around Lagos and Nairobi; peripheries include the Sahel and remote interior regions.
- **In the world:** a divide between richer countries (largely in the north) and poorer countries (largely in the south), sometimes shown by the Brandt Line (1980), with large differences in income, health and education. Regional inequalities exist inside every country, rich or poor.

## Causes

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| Physical | Rainfall, soils and relief: dry, infertile or mountainous areas support less farming; natural hazards; remoteness from ports and markets |
| Economic | Concentration of industry, services and investment in cities (agglomeration); resources such as minerals located in few places; poor infrastructure in the periphery |
| Historical | Colonial investment in railways, ports and farms in some areas and labour reserves elsewhere |
| Political | Decisions about where to place government, services and infrastructure; centralised government; conflict |
| Social | Differences in education, health and skills; unequal access to land and credit |

Cumulative causation links these: a region with services and jobs attracts the best-educated workers, raising its income, while the periphery loses young people and falls further behind.

## Impacts

- **Economic:** low incomes and unemployment in the periphery; underused resources; congestion in the core.
- **Social:** poor access to schools, health and water; poverty; differences in life expectancy.
- **Demographic:** migration to the core, leaving the periphery with older people, women and children and a high dependency ratio.
- **Environmental:** pressure on land and fuelwood in poor areas; pollution and informal settlement in the core.
- **Political:** a sense of neglect, tension and calls for devolution of power and funds.

## Surveying regional disparities in Zimbabwe

1. **Aim and hypothesis:** for example, "Access to services falls with distance from the provincial capital."
2. **Choose indicators:** household income or poverty, access to piped water, electricity, school and clinic distance, road quality, employment by sector.
3. **Collect data:** secondary data from ZimStat census and surveys, ministries and councils; primary data by household questionnaire using a sample of wards or villages, and observation.
4. **Process and present:** calculate rates and indices (a composite index of standardised indicators), maps (choropleth), graphs, and test relationships with Spearman's rank correlation; the Gini coefficient measures inequality between districts.
5. **Interpret and evaluate:** describe patterns, give causes and note limits such as small samples, old data and averages that hide local differences.

## Solutions

| Policy | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Decentralisation and devolution | Moves power, funds and decisions to provinces and districts (the 2013 Constitution provides for devolution) | Needs capacity, funds and honest management |
| Growth points and rural service centres | Concentrate services and jobs in rural centres | Slow growth where investment is weak |
| Infrastructure | Roads, rail, electricity, water, telecommunications | Costly; benefits depend on use |
| Rural industries and agro-processing | Add value to local crops and minerals and create jobs | Need markets and power |
| Incentives to firms in peripheral areas | Tax breaks, cheap land | May not outweigh the advantages of the core |
| Social programmes | Education, health, social protection, microcredit | Funding and targeting |

Evaluation asks whether the policy reduces the gap, who benefits, what it costs and how long it takes. Most successful strategies combine several measures.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Measuring inequality between provinces]
Illustrative income per person (US\$ a year) in six provinces: A 1 800, B 1 200, C 900, D 700, E 500, F 400.

![Lorenz curve for incomes of 400, 500, 700, 900, 1200 and 1800 US dollars in six provinces: the poorest third of provinces receive about 16 percent of income and the poorest half about 30 percent, so the curve bows below the line of equality and the Gini coefficient is 0.28.](media:diagrams/lorenz-curve-provinces.png "Figure: Lorenz curve and Gini coefficient for six provinces (worked-example incomes, illustrative)")

1. Mean = (1 800 + 1 200 + 900 + 700 + 500 + 400) ÷ 6 = 5 500 ÷ 6 = US\$917.
2. Ratio of highest to lowest = 1 800 ÷ 400 = 4.5.
3. Only two provinces (A and B) are above the mean. The Gini coefficient for these six values is 0.28 (0 is equal, 1 is the most unequal), showing moderate inequality between provinces.
4. The ratio and the Gini coefficient describe the gap. They do not explain it, and averages for a province hide differences between districts.
:::

:::worked-solution[Spearman rank correlation]
Hypothesis: provinces with higher incomes have more households with piped water. Percentage of households with piped water: A 92, B 81, C 70, D 64, E 41, F 48 (illustrative data).

1. Rank incomes: A 6, B 5, C 4, D 3, E 2, F 1. Rank water: A 6, B 5, C 4, D 3, E 1, F 2.
2. Differences d: all 0 except E (+1) and F (−1), so $\sum d^2 = 2$.
3. $r_s = 1 - \dfrac{6 \sum d^2}{n^3 - n} = 1 - \dfrac{12}{210} = 0.943$.
4. The critical value for $n = 6$ at the 0.05 level is 0.886. Since 0.943 is greater, there is a significant positive relationship between income and access to piped water.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a core-periphery pattern"}
A choropleth map shows the percentage of households with electricity by district. Districts around the capital and along the main road to the second city have over 80% of households with electricity, while districts in the south and along the border have under 30%. A description says that electricity access is highest in the core districts and falls with distance from the main routes and cities, with a clear contrast between the core and the periphery. Possible explanations are investment in the grid near cities, the cost of extending lines to sparsely populated districts, and lower incomes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using only averages"}
An average for a province hides differences between districts. Quote a range or compare districts where data allow.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing without explaining"}
Say why the periphery is poor: remoteness, dry climate, few investors, lost workers. Link each cause to the pattern.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing solutions without evaluating them"}
For each policy say who benefits, what it costs, what stops it from working and how long it takes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Regional inequalities are differences in income, services and opportunities between regions.
- The core has wealth, industry and power; the periphery is poorer and supplies labour and resources.
- Causes: physical, economic, historical, political and social, reinforced by cumulative causation and backwash effects.
- Impacts include poverty, migration, pressure on the core and political tension.
- Solutions include devolution, growth points, infrastructure, rural industry and social programmes, each with limits.
:::
