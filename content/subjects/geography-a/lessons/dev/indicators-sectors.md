## Introduction

Development means improvement in people's lives: higher incomes, longer and healthier lives, education, security and choice. Geographers measure it with indicators so that places can be compared and progress tracked. No single number is enough. A country may have a high average income but a short life expectancy, or low incomes but good schools and clinics. This lesson explains the main indicators and their strengths and weaknesses, shows how to survey development in a local area, and explains how the different kinds of economic activity contribute to development.

You should already know the terms rural, urban, trade and the idea of unequal development between countries. Regional inequalities within Zimbabwe are covered in a separate lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Gross National Income (GNI)"}
GDP is the total value of goods and services produced inside a country in a year. GNI is GDP plus income received from abroad by the country's people and companies minus income sent abroad. Dividing by the number of people gives GDP or GNI per capita. Purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts values for differences in the cost of living between countries.
:::

:::definition{term="Human Development Index (HDI)"}
A composite index between 0 and 1 produced by the United Nations Development Programme. It combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy at birth), knowledge (mean and expected years of schooling) and a decent standard of living (GNI per capita, on a logarithmic scale). Countries are grouped as very high (0.800 or more), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699) and low (below 0.550).
:::

:::definition{term="Economic sectors"}
Primary activities extract or grow raw materials (farming, mining, forestry, fishing). Secondary activities process materials and make goods (manufacturing, construction, electricity generation). Tertiary activities provide services (retail, transport, health, education, banking, tourism). Quaternary activities deal with knowledge and information (research and development, information technology, consultancy).
:::

## Economic and social indicators

| Indicator | What it shows | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| GDP or GNI per capita | Average income | Easy to find and compare; PPP allows for prices | An average that hides inequality; ignores the informal economy, household work and environmental damage |
| Employment structure | Share of people working in each sector | Shows the stage of development | Does not show income or conditions |
| Life expectancy | Average length of life | Reflects health care, nutrition, water and safety | An average that hides inequality; affected by epidemics |
| Infant mortality rate | Deaths of babies under one per 1 000 live births | Sensitive to health and sanitation | Data may be unreliable where births and deaths are not registered |
| Literacy rate and years of schooling | Education level | Basic and widely collected | Definitions vary; years of schooling do not measure quality |
| Doctors per 1 000 people, access to safe water or electricity | Access to services | Direct measures of living conditions | National averages hide differences between rural and urban areas |
| Human Development Index | Combined health, education and income | Combines several dimensions; simple ranking | Hides inequality; leaves out the environment, freedom and security; the weights are fixed |
| Gini coefficient | Inequality of income (0 equal, 1 unequal) | Shows how evenly income is shared | Says nothing about the level of income |

A good analysis uses several indicators together, compares them with other places and over time, and questions the quality of the data.

## Surveying development in the local area

To investigate the level of development of a local area (a ward, a village, a town) students can: (1) set a question such as "How developed is our ward compared with a neighbouring one?"; (2) choose indicators that can be measured locally, such as housing materials, access to safe water, electricity, clinics, schools and shops, employment types, school attendance and ownership of goods; (3) collect **primary data** by questionnaires and interviews (with a sample of households chosen fairly), observation and mapping of land use and services; (4) use **secondary data** such as census results from ZimStat, council records and clinic registers; (5) present the results in tables, maps and graphs; and (6) evaluate the reliability: sample size, honesty of answers, seasonal effects and whether the indicators capture development. Ethical practice means asking permission and keeping people's answers confidential.

## The role of economic activities

- **Primary**: employ many people in Zimbabwe and other low-income countries (farming, mining) and earn export income (for example tobacco, gold and platinum). Advantages: uses local resources and gives employment. Disadvantages: low value added, price changes on world markets, climate risk and environmental damage.
- **Secondary**: processing and manufacturing add value to raw materials (for example turning cotton into cloth or milling maize), create skilled jobs and links with other businesses, and earn more from exports. They need electricity, capital, skills and markets.
- **Tertiary**: services support all other sectors and make up the largest share of output in most countries. Examples: trade, transport, banking and mobile money, health, education and tourism. In Zimbabwe, much service work is informal.
- **Quaternary**: research, information technology and advice. They require skilled workers and good communications, and bring innovation and high incomes.

**Change over time.** As economies develop, the share of workers in the primary sector usually falls, secondary rises and then levels off, and tertiary and quaternary grow (the Clark–Fisher model). Poorer countries have a large share of people in primary work with low output per worker; richer countries have a majority in services.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating the HDI]
For an illustrative Country X: life expectancy 62 years, expected years of schooling 11.5, mean years of schooling 6.5, GNI per capita US\$ 2 800 (PPP). Use goalposts of 20–85 years for life expectancy, 0–18 and 0–15 years for schooling, and US\$ 100–75 000 for income.
1. Life expectancy index $= (62 - 20) \div (85 - 20) = 0.646$.
2. Education index $= \left(\dfrac{11.5}{18} + \dfrac{6.5}{15}\right) \div 2 = (0.639 + 0.433) \div 2 = 0.536$.
3. Income index $= \dfrac{\ln 2800 - \ln 100}{\ln 75000 - \ln 100} = 0.503$.
4. $\text{HDI} = \sqrt[3]{0.646 \times 0.536 \times 0.503} = 0.559$, which is **medium** human development (0.550–0.699).
5. A country with the same income but lower life expectancy would have a lower HDI; because the three parts are multiplied, a very low score in one dimension cannot be fully offset by high scores in the others.
:::

:::worked-solution[Sector shares and output per worker]
Illustrative data for a country: the primary sector produces 20% of GDP and employs 55% of workers; the secondary sector produces 25% of GDP and employs 12%; the tertiary sector produces 55% of GDP and employs 33%.
1. Output per worker relative to the national average $=$ share of GDP $\div$ share of employment.
2. Primary: $20 \div 55 = 0.36$; secondary: $25 \div 12 = 2.08$; tertiary: $55 \div 33 = 1.67$.
3. A primary worker produces about a third of the average; so moving workers into secondary and tertiary activities, with training and capital, raises average income.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and evaluating a table of indicators"}
Illustrative table: Country P has GNI per capita US\$ 1 400, life expectancy 58 years and literacy 82%; Country Q has US\$ 9 500, 74 years and 97%. Description: "Q has a GNI per person about 6.8 times that of P, a life expectancy 16 years longer and literacy 15 percentage points higher." Evaluation: the figures are averages; they do not show inequality within each country, the informal economy or environmental cost, and PPP values differ from market exchange rates. Using several indicators gives a fuller picture than income alone.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating development with income alone"}
Income is only one indicator. Health, education, access to services and equality also matter, which is why composite indices such as HDI are used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a national average as typical"}
GDP per capita and life expectancy are averages. They can hide wide differences between rich and poor, rural and urban, men and women.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying primary activities are unimportant"}
They are vital in many countries as employment, food and export income. The problem is their low value added and price risk, not their existence.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (development) and in survey work for Paper 3 and school-based projects. *State* indicators precisely and give units. *Describe* tables by comparing figures. *Calculate* indices and shares carefully (show the formula). For *assess/evaluate* an indicator, give both advantages and disadvantages and conclude that several indicators together are better. For the sector question, link each sector to jobs, income, value added and linkages, using Zimbabwean examples.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Development is measured by economic indicators (GDP or GNI per capita, employment structure) and social indicators (life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, access to services).
- Composite indices such as HDI combine health, education and income, but still hide inequality and omit the environment.
- Each indicator has advantages and disadvantages; use several, with care about the quality of data.
- A local development survey combines primary data (questionnaires, observation) and secondary data (census, council records), and the method must be evaluated.
- Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary activities have different roles; processing and services raise value added and incomes.
- As countries develop, employment shifts from primary to secondary to tertiary and quaternary activities.
:::
