## Introduction

Zimbabwe has no coast, but the country depends on coasts all the same: much of its fuel, fertiliser and export cargo passes through the ports of Beira, Maputo and Durban. These harbours, and the beaches and cliffs around them, are shaped by waves, which are the main agent of change on any shoreline. Waves can build a beach in a season of calm weather and strip it away in one storm. To understand coastal landforms, and to manage a coast, you must first understand how waves form, how they erode, how they move sediment and where the sediment comes from.

You should already know the idea of a system with inputs, stores, transfers and outputs. This lesson covers waves and sediment movement; cliff weathering and landforms come next.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fetch"}
The distance of open water over which the wind blows in a constant direction. The longer the fetch, the bigger the waves can grow.
:::

:::definition{term="Swash and backwash"}
Swash is the movement of water up the beach after a wave breaks; backwash is the return of water down the beach under gravity.
:::

:::definition{term="Longshore drift"}
The movement of sediment along a coast in a zig-zag path, caused by waves that approach the shore at an angle: swash carries material up the beach in the direction of the wave, and backwash returns it straight down the slope.
:::

:::definition{term="Sediment cell"}
A length of coastline, usually bounded by headlands or other barriers, within which the movement of sediment is largely self-contained. It is a system with inputs, stores, transfers and outputs.
:::

:::definition{term="Abrasion (corrasion)"}
Erosion by the wearing away of the coast by rock fragments carried in waves and hurled against the cliff or dragged across a platform.
:::

## How waves form and break

Waves are formed by wind blowing across the sea and transferring energy by friction. Their size depends on **wind speed**, **how long the wind blows** and the **fetch**. Out at sea the water moves in circular (orbital) paths, and the wave form moves on while the water hardly travels forward. When a wave enters shallow water, friction with the sea bed slows the base, the wavelength shortens, the height increases, and the wave steepens until the top topples forward and it **breaks** (usually when the depth is about 1.3 times the wave height). Water then rushes up the beach as swash.

:::keyfact{title="Wave facts for deep water (approximate)"}
Wavelength $L \approx 1.56\,T^2$ metres and speed $c = L \div T \approx 1.56\,T$ metres per second, where $T$ is the wave period in seconds (the time between two crests). Wave frequency per minute $= 60 \div T$. Wave energy increases with the **square of the wave height**.
:::

| Feature | Constructive wave | Destructive wave |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Low | High |
| Frequency | About 6–8 per minute | About 10–14 per minute |
| Wave shape | Long, low, gentle | Short, high, steep |
| Swash and backwash | Strong swash, weak backwash | Weak swash, strong backwash |
| Effect | Builds up the beach | Removes sediment, steepens the beach |

**Refraction.** Where a coast has headlands and bays, the part of a wave that reaches shallow water off a headland slows first, while the part in deeper water continues faster, so the wave front bends and wraps around the headland. The energy is concentrated on the headland and spread out in the bay. Headlands are therefore eroded, and bays are low-energy places where sediment accumulates.

## Processes of marine erosion

- **Hydraulic action.** Waves force water and air into cracks. The air is compressed, pressure rises and falls with each wave and the rock is wedged apart.
- **Cavitation.** Air bubbles in the water collapse with great force when pressure changes. The shock waves weaken rock.
- **Abrasion (corrasion).** Sand, pebbles and boulders carried by waves scour and batter the cliff and platform. It is the most effective process where the material is coarse and waves are large.
- **Attrition.** Particles collide with each other and become smaller and more rounded. This is a process of the load, not of the cliff.
- **Solution (corrosion).** Soluble rock such as limestone and chalk is dissolved by sea water.

## Transport and deposition

Waves move material in four ways: **traction** (large pebbles rolled along the bed), **saltation** (smaller particles bounced), **suspension** (fine sand, silt and clay carried within the water) and **solution** (dissolved minerals). The main process of movement along the shore is **longshore drift**. When the prevailing wind drives waves at an angle, the sediment travels along the beach in the direction of the wave. The direction of drift can change with the season or the weather.

**Deposition** occurs when wave energy falls: in sheltered bays, behind obstacles, in shallow water, or when constructive waves drop more sediment than they remove. The largest particles are deposited first.

## Sediment: sources, cells and budgets

**Sources** of sediment include erosion of cliffs, rivers (the largest input in many places), material moved from the sea floor onshore, and glacial deposits. Particle **size, shape and sorting** change along a beach: sediment is often coarser at the top and finer towards the sea, and it becomes smaller and rounder in the direction of drift because of attrition and sorting.

A sediment cell is a system:

- **Inputs:** cliff erosion, river sediment, onshore movement from the sea floor, longshore drift entering the cell.
- **Stores:** beaches, spits, dunes, offshore bars.
- **Transfers:** longshore drift, onshore and offshore movement.
- **Outputs:** drift leaving the cell, offshore loss to deep water, dredging.

If inputs exceed outputs, the coast builds out (accretion); if outputs exceed inputs, it retreats. This is the **sediment budget**.

## Influences on coastal processes

- **Rock type and structure.** Hard, resistant rocks (granite, basalt) erode slowly and form headlands. Soft rocks (clay, sand) erode quickly and form bays. Joints, bedding planes and faults give weakness for waves to exploit, and the dip of the strata affects cliff form.
- **Climate.** Storm frequency and wave energy, rainfall (which supplies sediment by rivers and weakens cliffs), freeze–thaw in cold climates, and tropical cyclones, which bring exceptional waves and surges to coasts such as that of Mozambique.
- **Human activity.** Sea walls and groynes change the movement of sediment; dams trap river sediment so that beaches receive less; dredging and sand mining remove material; building on dunes removes a store.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Wave speed and wavelength]
Deep-water waves have a period of 8 seconds (illustrative data).

1. Wavelength $L \approx 1.56 \times 8^2 = 1.56 \times 64 = 99.8$ m.
2. Speed $c = L \div T = 99.8 \div 8 = 12.5$ m/s (or $1.56 \times 8 = 12.5$).
3. Frequency $= 60 \div 8 = 7.5$ waves per minute, which is within the constructive range of 6–8 per minute.
:::

:::worked-solution[Wave height and energy]
A destructive wave of height 3 m is compared with a wave of height 1 m on the same coast (illustrative data).

1. Energy is proportional to the square of the height, so the ratio is $(3 \div 1)^2 = 9$.
2. The 3 m wave has about nine times the energy of the 1 m wave, which is why storm waves cause so much erosion.
3. A tripling of height does not triple the energy, and this is why small increases in height have large effects.
:::

:::worked-solution[Sediment budget and drift rate]
A beach receives 40 000 m³ per year from cliff erosion, 15 000 m³ from a river and 10 000 m³ from the sea floor. Longshore drift removes 50 000 m³ and dredging 5 000 m³ (illustrative data). Tracer pebbles on this beach moved 150 m along the shore in 3 days.

![Sediment budget of a beach: inputs of 40 000 m3 from cliff erosion, 15 000 m3 from a river and 10 000 m3 from the sea floor total 65 000 m3 a year, outputs of 50 000 m3 by longshore drift and 5 000 m3 by dredging total 55 000 m3, so the net gain is 10 000 m3 a year and the beach builds out.](media:diagrams/sediment-budget-beach.png "Figure: inputs and outputs of a beach sediment budget (worked-example values; arrow thickness shows size)")


1. Inputs $= 40\,000 + 15\,000 + 10\,000 = 65\,000$ m³ per year.
2. Outputs $= 50\,000 + 5\,000 = 55\,000$ m³ per year.
3. Net change $= 65\,000 - 55\,000 = +10\,000$ m³ per year, so the beach is building (accretion).
4. Drift rate $= 150 \div 3 = 50$ m per day.
:::

:::example{title="Describing the effect of refraction from a description"}
A coast has a headland 2 km wide that projects into the sea, with a bay on each side. Waves approach from the south-east. **Describe:** the wave crests bend, so that they arrive almost parallel to the shore in both the bay and the headland. **Explain:** the sea is shallow off the headland, so the waves slow first, while those in deeper water continue; the front bends and energy is concentrated on the headland, where erosion is greatest; in the bay the energy is spread over a wide area, so sand is deposited.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing erosion with transport"}
Attrition is not erosion of the cliff: it reduces the size of the load. Describe each process in the right place: hydraulic action and abrasion erode the cliff; attrition acts on the particles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that waves travel water forwards"}
In deep water the wave form moves but the water moves in circles. Only in the surf zone does the water move forward as swash.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wave size depends on wind speed, duration and fetch; waves break when the depth is about 1.3 times the wave height.
- Constructive waves are low and build beaches (6–8 per minute); destructive waves are steep and erode (10–14 per minute).
- Refraction concentrates energy on headlands and spreads it in bays.
- Erosion processes are hydraulic action, cavitation, abrasion, attrition and solution; transport is by traction, saltation, suspension and solution, with longshore drift along the shore.
- A sediment cell is a system; the budget (inputs − outputs) decides if the coast builds or retreats.
- Rock type, climate and human activity all alter the rate of erosion and the supply of sediment.
:::
