## Introduction

Every sandy holiday beach, every sand-and-shingle ridge across a bay and every long, curved finger of land in an estuary mouth is a store of sediment that waves have delivered and left behind. These landforms look permanent, but they are in constant change: they grow when more sediment arrives than leaves, and shrink when the balance changes. In some places, such as Walvis Bay in Namibia, a long sandy spit encloses a harbour; in others, such as Spurn Head at the mouth of the Humber in England, a spit has been cut through by storm waves.

You should know how waves transport sediment (swash, backwash, longshore drift) and the idea of a sediment budget from the first lesson of this option. Dunes, saltmarsh and mangrove are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Beach"}
An accumulation of sand, shingle or pebbles between the lowest tides and the highest reach of the waves, shaped by swash and backwash.
:::

:::definition{term="Spit"}
A long, narrow ridge of sand or shingle, joined to the land at one end and ending in open water, formed by longshore drift where the coastline changes direction or at the mouth of an estuary.
:::

:::definition{term="Tombolo"}
A ridge of sand or shingle that links an island to the mainland (or to another island).
:::

:::definition{term="Barrier beach (bar, barrier island)"}
A long ridge of sand or shingle that lies roughly parallel to the coast. A **barrier beach** or bar is attached to the land at one or both ends; a **barrier island** is separate from the mainland and has a lagoon behind it.
:::

## Beaches

**Profile.** A beach usually has a **backshore** (above the normal high tide, often with a berm or storm ridge), a **foreshore** (between low and high tide, where swash and backwash act) and a **nearshore** zone with bars. The slope depends on sediment size and wave type:

| Feature | Sandy beach | Shingle beach |
|---|---|---|
| Gradient | Gentle, about 1–5° | Steeper, often 5–15° |
| Permeability | Low, so water stays on the surface | High, so swash sinks in and the backwash is weak |
| Waves | Often destructive waves flatten the beach | Constructive waves build steep ridges |
| Features | Ridges and runnels, dunes behind | Berms, storm ridges, cusps |

**Plan.** Where the waves arrive almost parallel to the coast, usually in a bay between headlands where refraction has turned them, the beach is **swash-aligned**: the crest of the beach is parallel to the wave crests and sediment moves up and down the beach, with little net movement along it. Where waves arrive at an angle, the beach is **drift-aligned**: longshore drift carries sediment along the shore and the beach line follows the direction of the drift. A drift-aligned beach is the starting point of a spit.

## Spits

1. **Sediment supply.** Longshore drift carries sand and shingle along the coast.
2. **Change of direction.** At a bend in the coast, or at an estuary, the drift continues in a straight line, but the coast turns away, so the sediment is deposited beyond the bend as a ridge in sheltered, shallow water.
3. **Growth.** The spit extends in the direction of drift as more sediment arrives. The river current or tidal flow at the estuary prevents the spit from closing the mouth.
4. **Recurved end.** Waves from a secondary direction, and wave refraction around the end, curve the tip into a **hook**.
5. **Stability.** In the sheltered water behind the spit, fine sediment accumulates and plants colonise it, forming **saltmarsh**. Dunes may develop on the top.

Spits can be breached by storms. **Spurn Head**, in the Humber estuary, was breached in the surge of December 2013. The **Walvis Bay** spit in Namibia, which grows north from the coast and encloses a lagoon, is a well-known sand spit on a coast with strong longshore drift.

## Tombolos and barrier beaches

**Tombolo.** An island close to the coast slows waves and causes them to refract around it, leaving a sheltered area (a wave shadow) behind it. Sediment carried by longshore drift is deposited in this calmer water and builds a ridge towards the island. If it reaches the island, a tombolo results. The best-known are the tombolos at Monte Argentario in Italy, which join a former island to the mainland.

**Barrier beaches and islands.** These are long ridges parallel to the coast, fronted by the open sea and backed by a lagoon or marsh. They are found on low-gradient coasts with large supplies of sediment. Theories of formation include:

- A **spit** that grows across a bay until it closes it, forming a bar and lagoon.
- **Offshore bars** built up by waves during storms until they emerge above the surface.
- **Rising sea level** after the last glaciation, which drove shingle ridges landward over the sea floor. **Chesil Beach** in Dorset, England, a shingle bar of roughly 29 km, is thought to have been moved in this way.

Barrier islands migrate landwards by **rollover**: waves overwash the island during storms and carry sediment from the front to the back.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Gradient of a beach (illustrative data)]
A shingle beach rises 2.7 m over a horizontal distance of 18 m. A sandy beach rises 1.4 m over 70 m.

1. Shingle: gradient $= 2.7 \div 18 = 0.15$, which is 15%, and the angle is $\tan^{-1}(0.15) = 8.5°$.
2. Sand: gradient $= 1.4 \div 70 = 0.02$, which is 2%, and the angle is $\tan^{-1}(0.02) = 1.1°$.
3. The shingle beach is steeper because the swash percolates into the coarse, permeable sediment, so the backwash is weak and cannot pull the shingle down. On sand, the backwash drags material down more easily.
:::

:::worked-solution[Spit growth and volume (illustrative data)]
A spit was 1.2 km long when it was mapped in one year and 1.8 km long 20 years later. Its mean width is 140 m and its mean height above the sea bed is 3.5 m.

1. Extension $= 1.8 - 1.2 = 0.6$ km $= 600$ m in 20 years.
2. Rate $= 600 \div 20 = 30$ m per year.
3. Volume $\approx 1\,800 \times 140 \times 3.5 = 882\,000$ m³ (treating the spit as a box).
4. This is a rough estimate, since a spit is not a box, but it shows how much sediment such a landform stores.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a spit from a map description"}
A map extract shows a ridge of sand that leaves a south-facing coast at the point where the coast turns sharply north. The ridge runs east for 2.4 km, is narrow (about 150 m), and ends in a curve towards the land. Behind it is a sheltered lagoon with a marsh. **Describe:** a narrow ridge attached at the western end, 2.4 km long, with a recurved eastern tip. **Explain:** longshore drift moves sediment eastwards and continues in a straight line when the coast turns; sediment is deposited in the sheltered water; waves from another direction curve the end into a hook; the marsh forms in the shelter of the spit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that the sea builds a spit by itself"}
A spit needs a supply of sediment and longshore drift, and a change of direction of the coast. Include all three.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing swash-aligned with drift-aligned"}
Swash-aligned beaches lie parallel to the wave crests and have little movement along the shore; drift-aligned beaches are oblique to the waves and are dominated by longshore drift.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining a hooked end without the second wind direction"}
The recurve is caused by refraction and by waves from a second direction, not by the main drift.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sandy beaches are gentle (about 1–5°); shingle beaches are steeper because their permeable sediment gives a weak backwash.
- Swash-aligned beaches lie parallel to the wave crests; drift-aligned beaches follow the direction of longshore drift.
- A spit forms where drift continues past a change in the direction of the coast; its end is curved by refraction and waves from a second direction.
- A tombolo forms in the wave shadow of an island, joining it to the land.
- Barrier beaches and islands may form from spits, offshore bars or from the landward movement of ridges as sea level rose; they migrate by rollover.
- Gradient = vertical ÷ horizontal; rate = distance ÷ time; volume = length × width × height.
:::
