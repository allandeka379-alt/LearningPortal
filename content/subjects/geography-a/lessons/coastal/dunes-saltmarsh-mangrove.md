## Introduction

Not every coast is rock and shingle. Where sediment is abundant and the water is calm, plants colonise the shore and begin to shape it. Sand dunes grow on exposed sandy coasts, saltmarshes in sheltered estuaries in cool and temperate climates, and mangrove swamps in the same sheltered places in the tropics. In each, the plants trap sediment, the sediment builds up the surface, and the changing surface allows new plants to take over. This feedback between vegetation and sediment is the key idea of the lesson. All three systems also protect coasts, support fisheries and store carbon, and all three are threatened.

You should know the idea of succession from biogeography, and from the previous lessons how waves and winds move sediment. Coral reefs are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Psammosere (dune succession)"}
The sequence of plant communities that colonise sand, from pioneer grasses on the beach to a stable community inland, with soil developing as the succession proceeds.
:::

:::definition{term="Halosere (saltmarsh succession)"}
The succession of plant communities on a tidal mud flat, controlled by salinity and by the time that the surface is covered by water.
:::

:::definition{term="Halophyte"}
A plant adapted to grow in salty conditions, for example by excreting salt through glands, storing water in succulent tissue or tolerating high salt levels in its cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Pneumatophore"}
A breathing root that grows upwards out of the waterlogged mud of a mangrove swamp to take in oxygen.
:::

## Sand dunes

**Conditions needed:** a large supply of sand, a wide beach that dries at low tide, an onshore wind strong enough to move sand (more than about 5 m/s), and a surface or plants that trap the grains.

**Succession (psammosere).**

1. **Embryo dunes** form at the top of the beach where sand collects around driftwood or **pioneer plants** such as sand couch grass and lyme grass. The plants tolerate salt spray, drought and burial. The dune is low (under 1 m) and the sand is dry, alkaline and has little humus.
2. **Foredunes (yellow dunes)** are taller (a few metres). **Marram grass** thrives on burial and grows upwards through the sand, trapping more. Its long roots bind the sand and its rolled leaves reduce water loss. The sand is still yellow because of the lack of humus.
3. **Fixed (grey) dunes** lie further inland, more sheltered, with more complete plant cover (grasses, herbs, mosses and lichens). Dead plants add humus, so the soil is darker and more acid, holds more water and nutrients.
4. **Dune slacks** are hollows between dunes where the water table is close to the surface, with damp-loving plants such as rushes and willows.
5. **Mature dune heath, scrub or woodland** is the climax community inland, where the soil is deepest and most acid.

As you move inland the dunes are older, plant diversity and cover increase, soil humus rises and pH falls. A **blowout** is a hollow where wind has removed the sand after the vegetation has been damaged.

## Saltmarshes

Saltmarshes form in sheltered places, such as behind spits or in estuaries, where the tidal currents are weak and fine silt and clay settle at **slack water**. Clay particles in salt water clump together (**flocculation**) and sink faster. Once a **pioneer** species such as glasswort (*Salicornia*) or cord grass (*Spartina*) colonises the mud, its stems slow the water and trap more sediment, and its roots bind it. The surface rises, tides cover it less often, and other species replace the pioneers:

- **Pioneer zone** (lowest, flooded twice daily): glasswort and cord grass.
- **Low and middle marsh:** sea purslane, thrift and other salt-tolerant plants; creeks drain the marsh.
- **Upper marsh** (flooded only by the highest tides): rushes and grasses, grading to scrub or woodland on land.

Zonation is controlled by the **frequency and duration of flooding** and by salinity. Halophytes have adaptations such as salt glands, succulent stems that store water, and air spaces in the roots for waterlogged mud.

## Mangrove swamps

Mangroves occur on sheltered, muddy tropical and subtropical coasts, usually between about 25° north and south of the equator, where the winter water temperature is above about 20 °C. In southern Africa they are best known on the coast of Mozambique, for example in the Zambezi delta and around Sofala Bay.

**Adaptations.** Prop or stilt roots (*Rhizophora*) support trees in soft mud; pneumatophores (*Avicennia*) supply oxygen; leaves may excrete salt or tissues may exclude it; and seeds germinate on the tree (**vivipary**), so that a seedling (**propagule**) drops with a root already formed and quickly anchors itself.

**Zonation.** The seaward edge, which is flooded for longest, is colonised by pioneers such as *Avicennia* and *Sonneratia*; behind them come *Rhizophora* in the middle zone, then other species (*Bruguiera*, *Ceriops*) where flooding is less frequent, and land plants where the ground is above the reach of most tides. Roots trap sediment, slow water and gradually raise the surface, so the zones shift seawards over time.

## Uses and threats

| System | Uses and benefits | Threats |
|---|---|---|
| Dunes | Defence against storms and flooding; habitat; recreation; water storage | Trampling and vehicles, building, sand mining, overgrazing, defences that stop sand supply |
| Saltmarsh | Wave attenuation, nursery for fish, bird habitat, carbon storage, grazing | Reclamation for farming and development, pollution, sea-level rise with no room to move (coastal squeeze), dredging |
| Mangrove | Protection from waves and storms, nursery for fish and prawns, timber, charcoal and fuelwood, carbon storage | Clearance for shrimp farms, agriculture and settlement, over-harvesting, pollution, sea-level rise, changes in river flow |

Management includes fencing and boardwalks, planting marram grass, protected areas, restoration of cleared mangrove by planting, and **managed realignment**, in which a sea wall is breached to let a saltmarsh develop.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Can a saltmarsh keep up with sea-level rise? (illustrative data)]
A saltmarsh surface is rising by 5 mm a year through the trapping of sediment. Sea level is rising by 3 mm a year.

1. Net gain in height relative to the sea $= 5 - 3 = 2$ mm a year.
2. The time to gain a further 100 mm relative to the sea is $100 \div 2 = 50$ years.
3. If sea-level rise increases to 6 mm a year, the net change is $5 - 6 = -1$ mm a year, and the marsh would slowly be drowned, particularly where a sea wall prevents it from moving inland.
:::

:::worked-solution[Loss of mangrove (illustrative data)]
A district had 12 400 ha of mangrove 30 years ago and 9 300 ha today.

1. Loss $= 12\,400 - 9\,300 = 3\,100$ ha.
2. Percentage lost $= 3\,100 \div 12\,400 \times 100 = 25.0\%$.
3. Mean annual loss $= 3\,100 \div 30 = 103$ ha per year (to the nearest hectare).
4. This is a loss of a quarter of the forest, which reduces storm protection and fish nurseries.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a dune transect (illustrative data)"}
A transect inland from the beach gives: embryo dune, 0.6 m high, pH 8.3, humus 0.2%, plant cover 8%; foredune, 4.0 m, pH 7.8, humus 0.6%, cover 45%; fixed dune, 3.0 m, pH 6.5, humus 2.4%, cover 95%. **Describe:** with distance inland pH falls from 8.3 to 6.5, humus rises from 0.2% to 2.4% and plant cover rises from 8% to 95%. **Explain:** pioneer plants trap sand and add organic matter; as the dunes become stable, decaying vegetation increases humus and acidity and shell fragments are leached, allowing more species to grow.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing the succession in the wrong direction"}
The oldest, most stable communities are furthest from the sea in dunes and saltmarsh. In mangroves, the oldest are landward of the pioneers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up dune plants and saltmarsh plants"}
Marram grass is a dune plant that is stimulated by burial and tolerates drought and salt spray; halophytes such as glasswort live on salty, waterlogged mud. Name the adaptation and the problem it solves.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating mangroves and saltmarsh as the same thing"}
They have similar functions, but mangroves are trees of tropical coasts, with specialised roots, and saltmarsh is dominated by herbs and grasses in cooler climates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Dunes form where sand is abundant and wind and plants trap it: embryo dunes, foredunes, fixed dunes, slacks and a stable community inland.
- Saltmarshes form in sheltered estuaries where fine sediment settles and halophytes trap it; zonation depends on flooding and salinity.
- Mangroves are tropical trees with prop roots, pneumatophores and viviparous seeds; they trap mud and zone from the sea to the land.
- In each system vegetation and sediment interact: plants trap sediment, the surface rises and new plants move in.
- Dunes, marshes and mangroves protect coasts, support wildlife and store carbon, but are threatened by development, clearance and sea-level rise.
- Net gain = accretion rate − sea-level rise; percentage change = change ÷ original × 100.
:::
