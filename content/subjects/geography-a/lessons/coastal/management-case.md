## Introduction

Where a coast is eroding and people, farmland or industry stand behind it, someone must decide whether to defend it, move away from it or let it go. Every choice costs money, and every defence changes the movement of sediment, so a decision at one place can affect another further along the coast. Cambridge requires a **detailed specific example** of a stretch of coast, with its physical and human challenges, the strategies used and an evaluation of them. This lesson sets out the strategies and then works through one example, the Holderness coast in eastern England, with a contrasting example of managed retreat.

You should know the processes in the first lesson of this option (waves, erosion, longshore drift, sediment cells) and the landforms from the next two. A detailed example is useful only if you quote **places, dates and what actually happened**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hard engineering"}
Building structures to resist waves and stop erosion, such as sea walls, groynes, rip-rap (rock armour) and revetments.
:::

:::definition{term="Soft engineering"}
Working with natural processes to reduce erosion and flood risk, such as beach nourishment, dune stabilisation and managed retreat.
:::

:::definition{term="Managed retreat (managed realignment)"}
Deliberately allowing the sea to flood or erode a stretch of land, often by breaching a defence, and moving the line of defence inland. It creates a buffer, such as saltmarsh.
:::

:::definition{term="Shoreline Management Plan (SMP)"}
A plan that sets out, for each stretch of coast in a sediment cell, a policy for the next century: hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment or no active intervention.
:::

## Strategies and their evaluation

| Strategy | What it is | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sea wall | Concrete or masonry wall, often curved to throw waves back | Strong, long-lasting, protects property directly | Very costly, reflects waves and can scour the beach at its foot |
| Groynes | Barriers built out across the beach | Trap sediment and build the beach, which protects the cliff | Starve the coast downdrift, may look unsightly |
| Rip-rap (rock armour) | Large rocks piled at the foot of the cliff or wall | Cheaper than a wall, absorbs wave energy, flexible | Rocks can be moved by storms, may be unattractive and need imports |
| Revetment | Sloping structure of timber, rock or concrete | Absorbs wave energy at lower cost | Needs maintenance, short life in severe storms |
| Beach nourishment | Sand or shingle added to the beach | Looks natural, protects and improves recreation | Must be repeated, as the material is lost; costly dredging |
| Dune stabilisation | Fencing, planting marram, controlling access | Cheap, natural protection and habitat | Slow, needs constant care, only for sandy coasts |
| Managed retreat | Defence removed or set back | Creates habitat, cheap in the long term | Land and property are lost; owners need compensation |

To judge a scheme, ask: Does it address the cause? What does it cost compared with what it protects (a **cost–benefit** comparison)? Does it affect the sediment cell and places downdrift? Is it sustainable in rising sea level? Who gains and who loses?

## Detailed specific example: the Holderness coast, East Riding of Yorkshire

**Location.** The Holderness coast runs for about 60 km from Flamborough Head south to Spurn Head, on the North Sea. It is often described as one of the fastest-eroding coasts in Europe.

**Physical challenges.**

- The cliffs are formed of **boulder clay (till)**, soft, unconsolidated material left by ice sheets, which is easily eroded by waves and saturated by rain, so that it fails by slumping and mudflow.
- A long **fetch** across the North Sea allows large waves to reach the coast, and storm surges raise the water level (a major surge in December 2013 breached Spurn Head).
- Average retreat is about 1.8 m per year (some places more), and the material eroded is carried south by longshore drift towards Spurn Head.
- Many villages mentioned in medieval records have been lost to the sea.

**Human challenges.** The coast has towns (Hornsea, Withernsea), villages, caravan parks, farmland, roads and the Easington gas terminal, so there is a strong economic reason to protect parts of it. Property owners ask for defences, but the value of the land to be protected is low in some places.

**Strategies.**

- **Hornsea and Withernsea:** sea walls, groynes and rock armour defend the towns and their seafronts.
- **Mappleton (1991):** two rock groynes and a rock revetment were built to protect the village and its road. They trapped sediment and the beach in front of Mappleton has grown.
- **Management policy:** the Shoreline Management Plan for the area sets different policies for different stretches: *hold the line* at the larger settlements and the gas terminal, and *no active intervention* on much of the rest (policies are reviewed, and should be checked against the current plan).

**Evaluation.**

- *Successes:* the towns and Mappleton have been protected, the beach at Mappleton has grown, and tourism and property values were protected.
- *Problems:* the groynes at Mappleton trapped sediment that would have moved south, so the coast further down the drift, near **Cowden**, is reported to have eroded faster. Defences are costly to build and maintain, are hard to justify where little is at stake, and can give a false sense of security. Rising sea level and storm surges will increase pressure on the defences.
- *Judgement:* hard engineering protected valuable places for a time, but it moved the problem along the coast and cannot be sustained everywhere, so a policy that is selective, with managed retreat in places, is more realistic.

**Contrasting example of soft engineering: Medmerry, West Sussex.** At Medmerry, a shingle bank that repeatedly flooded was replaced in 2013 by new embankments set back inland, so that the sea now floods farmland and creates intertidal habitat. It was described when it opened as the largest open-coast managed realignment in Europe. It protects homes and roads at a lower long-term cost than rebuilding the old defence, creates wildlife habitat, but farmland is lost (to be verified for current figures).

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Cost–benefit comparison (illustrative data)]
A sea wall 1 200 m long costs US\$5 000 per metre to build. The property it would protect is worth US\$9.0 million.

1. Cost $= 1\,200 \times 5\,000 = 6\,000\,000$, which is US\$6.0 million.
2. Benefit–cost ratio $= 9.0 \div 6.0 = 1.5$.
3. A ratio above 1 suggests that the wall is worth building; a ratio below 1 suggests that other options, such as moving the property, are better value. Environmental and social effects are not included in this simple ratio.
:::

:::worked-solution[Time before a building is reached (illustrative data)]
A farmhouse stands 45 m from the edge of a boulder-clay cliff that is retreating at an average of 1.8 m per year.

1. Time $= 45 \div 1.8 = 25$ years.
2. Retreat comes in bursts, so the actual date could be earlier or later, and an owner might decide to move the house, seek compensation or ask for a defence.
:::

:::worked-solution[Volume of a beach nourishment scheme (illustrative data)]
A beach 800 m long is to be widened by 30 m and raised by an average of 1.5 m. Sand costs US\$8 per m³ delivered.

1. Volume $= 800 \times 30 \times 1.5 = 36\,000$ m³.
2. Cost $= 36\,000 \times 8 = 288\,000$, which is US\$288 000.
3. The scheme must be repeated every few years if the sand is lost, so the long-term cost is higher.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a strategy from a description"}
A rock groyne field is built in front of a village on a drifting coast. In two years the beach in front of the village is 15 m wider; 2 km down the drift a farm has lost 12 m of land in a single winter. **Describe:** the beach is wider at the village and the coast is retreating faster down the drift. **Explain:** the groynes trap sediment, which protects the village, but starve the area downdrift, so erosion increases there. **Assess:** it works at the village but moves the problem, so the cost to others must be counted.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing management without a place or a date"}
A detailed specific example needs named places, dates and results. Quote the village, the scheme and the year, and say whether it worked.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a scheme a success or failure without criteria"}
Judge it against clear criteria: protection, cost, side effects on neighbouring coasts, sustainability and fairness.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the sediment cell"}
Any structure that traps sediment affects the coast further down the drift. Show that you know this, and name the place affected.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 8), often as a 20-mark essay that asks you to assess management. Organise your answer as: challenges (physical, human), strategies (hard, soft, managed retreat, with dates), evaluation (successes, problems, side effects, cost), and a judgement. Use the same example throughout and bring in a short contrasting example if it strengthens the argument.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hard engineering (sea walls, groynes, rip-rap, revetments) resists erosion; soft engineering (nourishment, dune stabilisation, managed retreat) works with natural processes.
- Judge a scheme by cost against benefit, effect on the sediment cell, sustainability and fairness.
- Holderness has soft boulder-clay cliffs, a long fetch and storm surges, retreating at about 1.8 m a year.
- Hornsea, Withernsea and Mappleton have hard defences; Mappleton's groynes are reported to have increased erosion downdrift near Cowden.
- Medmerry (2013) is an example of managed realignment that creates habitat and lowers long-term cost.
- Benefit–cost ratio = benefit ÷ cost; time = distance ÷ rate of retreat; volume = length × width × depth.
:::
