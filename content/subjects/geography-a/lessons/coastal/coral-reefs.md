## Introduction

Coral reefs cover a tiny fraction of the sea floor, yet they shelter an enormous variety of fish, shellfish and other animals and protect tropical coasts from waves. Millions of people, including communities along the coast of Mozambique and the western Indian Ocean, depend on reefs for fish, tourism and shore protection. Reefs are also among the first ecosystems to show the effect of climate change: in 1998 and in several later years, warm water caused mass bleaching around the world. This lesson explains what a reef is, the conditions that allow it to grow, what threatens it, and how it can be managed.

You should know the idea of an ecosystem from earlier topics, and from the atmosphere topic the link between carbon dioxide and ocean warming. The other coastal systems (dunes, saltmarsh and mangrove) are in the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Coral"}
A marine animal (a polyp) that lives in a colony and secretes a hard skeleton of calcium carbonate. Reef-building corals live in a partnership with algae. When many generations of coral and the skeletons of other organisms accumulate, they build a reef.
:::

:::definition{term="Zooxanthellae"}
Microscopic algae that live inside the tissues of reef-building corals. They photosynthesise and give the coral most of its food and its colour, and the coral gives them shelter and nutrients (a symbiotic relationship).
:::

:::definition{term="Coral bleaching"}
The loss of colour of coral that occurs when stress, particularly unusually warm water, causes the coral to expel its zooxanthellae. A bleached coral is not dead but is starved and vulnerable, and it may die if the stress continues.
:::

:::definition{term="Ocean acidification"}
A fall in the pH of sea water as the ocean absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, which makes carbonate ions less available for corals and other organisms to build skeletons and shells.
:::

## Where reefs grow and why

Reefs occur in the tropical and subtropical oceans, mainly between about 30° north and south of the equator, and best on the **western** sides of oceans where warm currents flow. The best-known regions are the Great Barrier Reef off north-east Australia, the Coral Triangle of Indonesia and the Philippines, the Red Sea, the Caribbean, and the East African coast and Indian Ocean islands.

| Condition | Requirement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | About 23–29 °C is ideal; rarely below about 18 °C | Enzymes and the zooxanthellae work best in warm water; too warm causes bleaching |
| Depth and light | Mostly shallower than about 30–50 m | Zooxanthellae need sunlight for photosynthesis |
| Water clarity | Clear water with little sediment | Sediment blocks light and smothers polyps |
| Salinity | About 32–36 parts per thousand | Heavy fresh water from rivers or rain kills coral, so reefs are absent at large river mouths |
| Oxygenation and movement | Moving, well-oxygenated water | Waves and currents supply oxygen and plankton and remove sediment |
| Substrate | Hard, stable surface | Larvae need rock or old reef to attach to |

**Types of reef.** A **fringing reef** grows close to the shore; a **barrier reef** is separated from the land by a lagoon; an **atoll** is a ring of reef surrounding a lagoon, formed as a volcanic island sinks (Darwin's theory).

**Growth.** Coral grows slowly: massive, boulder-shaped corals add only about 0.5–2 cm a year, branching corals faster, and a reef takes thousands of years to build. Damage can therefore take a very long time to repair.

## Threats

1. **Global warming and bleaching.** Warming of 1–2 °C above the usual summer maximum for several weeks can bleach corals. The Great Barrier Reef had mass bleaching in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022 and 2024. Reefs of the western Indian Ocean were severely bleached in 1998.
2. **Ocean acidification.** The ocean has absorbed a large share of the carbon dioxide emitted by people, and its pH has fallen by about 0.1 since the start of the industrial era. Corals then build skeletons more slowly.
3. **Storms.** Tropical cyclones break coral by waves. A healthy reef recovers, but repeated storms make recovery slower.
4. **Salinity change.** Floods bring fresh water and sediment into coastal waters and can kill corals near river mouths.
5. **Sea-level rise.** Healthy reefs can grow upward to keep pace with moderate rises, but reefs weakened by stress and acidification may not.
6. **Pollution and sediment.** Run-off from farms, mines and construction carries sediment, fertiliser and pesticides, which smother polyps, reduce light and encourage algae that overgrow corals. Outbreaks of the crown-of-thorns starfish, which eats coral, have been linked to nutrient-rich run-off.
7. **Physical damage and overuse.** Anchors, trampling, collection for souvenirs, and destructive fishing with dynamite or poison break the reef; overfishing removes fish that graze algae.

## Management strategies

- **Marine protected areas and zoning.** The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park was established in 1975, and in 2004 its zoning was changed so that about a third of the park became no-take (no fishing) zones.
- **Improving water quality.** Reducing fertiliser and sediment run-off from farms through Reef water quality plans.
- **Controlling pests and damage.** Culling crown-of-thorns starfish, using mooring buoys instead of anchors, banning destructive fishing and limiting visitor numbers.
- **Restoration.** Growing coral fragments in nurseries and planting them on damaged reefs, and protecting heat-tolerant colonies.
- **Reducing greenhouse gas emissions**, the only way to deal with warming and acidification at their source, which requires international agreement.

**Evaluation.** Protected areas increase fish stocks and can make a reef healthier and more resilient, and local actions on pollution and fishing are within the power of managers. But local measures **cannot stop warming or acidification**, restoration works only on small areas and is costly, and enforcement of protected areas in poor regions is hard. Success is greatest when local protection is combined with global cuts in emissions.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[How long does a coral head take to grow? (illustrative data)]
A boulder-shaped coral head is 1.2 m high and grows by 1.5 cm a year.

1. Convert the height: $1.2 \text{ m} = 120$ cm.
2. Age $= 120 \div 1.5 = 80$ years.
3. A head of this size is therefore decades old, so loss of such colonies cannot be quickly made good.
:::

:::worked-solution[Change in acidity as pH falls (illustrative data)]
The pH of sea water falls from 8.2 to 8.1. The pH scale is logarithmic: each fall of 1 unit is a tenfold rise in hydrogen ion concentration.

1. Ratio $= 10^{(8.2 - 8.1)} = 10^{0.1} = 1.26$.
2. The hydrogen ion concentration has increased by about 26%.
3. A fall of only 0.1 in pH is therefore a big change in chemistry, which shows why small changes in pH matter to organisms with shells and skeletons.
:::

:::worked-solution[Cumulative heat stress (illustrative data)]
Over six weeks the sea temperature exceeded the local bleaching threshold by 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 1.2, 0.8 and 0.6 °C.

1. Cumulative heat stress $= 0.5 + 1.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 + 0.8 + 0.6 = 5.6$ °C-weeks.
2. The longer and larger the excess, the greater the risk of mass bleaching; agencies use indices of this type (such as degree heating weeks) to give warnings.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting reef survey data (illustrative data)"}
A survey of a reef before and after a bleaching event found live coral cover of 46% before and 28% after. **Describe:** cover fell by 18 percentage points, which is a 39% fall in relative terms ($18 \div 46 \times 100 = 39.1\%$). **Explain:** unusually warm water caused the corals to expel their zooxanthellae, so they starved; some died and algae overgrew the dead skeletons. **Suggest** how recovery could be helped: limit run-off and fishing so that grazing fish keep the algae in check.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that coral is a plant"}
Corals are animals (polyps). The algae living inside them (zooxanthellae) are the plants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that bleached coral is always dead"}
Bleached coral has expelled its algae and is starved, but it can recover if conditions return to normal soon enough.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing threats without linking them"}
Link each threat to a reef condition: warming (temperature), run-off (light and salinity), acidification (skeleton building). That shows understanding.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Corals are animals that build calcium carbonate skeletons and live with zooxanthellae algae; they grow slowly (about 0.5–2 cm a year for massive corals).
- Reefs need warm (about 23–29 °C), clear, shallow, salty (32–36 parts per thousand), well-oxygenated water and a hard base.
- Threats are warming and bleaching, acidification, storms, salinity change, sea-level rise, pollution and sediment, and physical damage.
- Management includes protected areas and zoning, better water quality, pest control, restoration and cuts in greenhouse gas emissions.
- Local measures help reefs recover but cannot stop warming and acidification.
- Age = height ÷ growth rate; a fall in pH of 0.1 raises hydrogen ion concentration by about 26%.
:::
