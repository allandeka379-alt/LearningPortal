## Introduction

A cliff face may look as if it is changed only by the waves that break at its foot, but it is also attacked from above. Rain seeps into cracks, salt spray dries in the pores of the rock, frost splits it in cold climates and gravity pulls loosened blocks downwards. The waves then remove the debris and attack the foot of the cliff again. The coastal landforms in this lesson, from cliffs and shore platforms to arches and stacks, are the result of marine and sub-aerial processes working together.

You should already know the erosion processes of waves (hydraulic action, abrasion and others) and the weathering processes from earlier topics. Here they are applied to a coastline; depositional landforms are in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sub-aerial processes"}
Processes of weathering and mass movement that act on the land surface above the reach of the waves, such as freeze–thaw, salt crystal growth, rockfalls and slumping.
:::

:::definition{term="Shore platform (wave-cut platform)"}
A gently sloping rock surface, usually less than about 5°, at the foot of a cliff, between the high and low tide marks, formed by the retreat of the cliff and by weathering and abrasion of the rock.
:::

:::definition{term="Concordant and discordant coastline"}
On a **concordant** coastline the bands of rock lie parallel to the coast; on a **discordant** coastline they lie at right angles (or at a high angle) to it, so that hard and soft rock outcrop alternately.
:::

:::definition{term="Geo"}
A long, narrow, steep-sided inlet cut into a cliff along a line of weakness, often formed when the roof of a cave collapses.
:::

## Sub-aerial processes on cliffs

**Weathering** weakens the cliff and provides loose material:

- **Freeze–thaw:** water freezes in joints and expands by about 9%, wedging the rock apart. It is important only where temperatures regularly fall below 0 °C.
- **Salt crystal growth:** sea spray enters pores and cracks, evaporates and leaves salt crystals that grow and force the rock apart.
- **Carbonation and solution:** slightly acidic rain dissolves limestone and chalk.
- **Hydrolysis:** water reacts with feldspar in rocks such as granite, forming clay and weakening the rock.

**Mass movement** carries the material down the cliff:

- **Rockfall:** blocks fall from steep, jointed cliffs of hard rock, particularly after frost or heavy rain.
- **Landslide (slide, including rotational slumping):** a block of soft rock or clay moves along a curved slip surface after the clay is saturated or the foot of the cliff is undercut.
- **Mudflow:** saturated soil and clay flow downslope as a thick fluid.

Waves remove the debris at the foot of the cliff. Where there is little wave energy the debris accumulates and protects the cliff, so the processes depend on one another.

## Cliffs

A cliff retreats by a cycle: waves undercut the base by hydraulic action and abrasion, forming a **wave-cut notch**; the rock above is left unsupported and collapses; the debris is removed by the waves; and the cycle begins again. The **profile** reflects geology:

- **Steep or vertical cliffs** occur in hard, massive or horizontally bedded rock (chalk, limestone, granite), where collapse is by rockfall.
- **Gentler, often curved cliffs** occur in soft rock such as clay, or where the beds dip towards the sea, where slumping is common.

In **plan** a coast has headlands (hard rock, resistant) and bays (softer rock), and cliffs retreat fastest where the rock is weak, well jointed or poorly protected by a beach.

## Shore platforms

As the cliff retreats, a gently sloping platform is left at its foot. The platform is widened by **abrasion** (debris moved across its surface), by **weathering** of the rock when it is exposed to wetting and drying and salt, and by the retreat of the cliff. The platform slope is mostly about 1–3° and it usually shows small pools and runnels. A wide platform dissipates the energy of waves before they reach the cliff, so the rate of erosion slows, and the platform can limit its own growth. Platforms may be stranded above the present sea level if sea level has fallen relative to the land.

## Caves, geos, arches, stacks and stumps

1. Waves exploit a **weakness** (a joint, a fault or a band of softer rock) at the foot of a cliff, using hydraulic action and abrasion, and enlarge it to a **cave**.
2. If the cave roof collapses, or a **blowhole** forms where compressed air blasts a vertical opening through the roof, a **geo** is formed.
3. On a headland, caves cut from opposite sides, or one cave extending right through, form an **arch**.
4. Weathering and erosion weaken the roof until it collapses, leaving a **stack** isolated from the cliff.
5. Further erosion at the base and weathering of the top reduce the stack to a **stump**, which may be visible only at low tide.

Well-known examples include the chalk stacks of Old Harry Rocks in Dorset and the Needles of the Isle of Wight, the limestone arch of Durdle Door (Dorset), and the Hole in the Wall on the Wild Coast of South Africa's Eastern Cape.

## Concordant and discordant coasts

| Feature | Concordant coast | Discordant coast |
|---|---|---|
| Rock bands | Parallel to the coast | At right angles to the coast |
| Exposure | One rock type (the outer band) faces the sea | Hard and soft rock alternate along the coast |
| Landforms | A fairly straight coastline; coves where the outer band is breached | Headlands (hard rock) and bays (soft rock) |
| Example | Dalmatian coast of Croatia; Lulworth Cove in Dorset | Swanage Bay, Dorset, between the chalk of Ballard Point and the limestone of Peveril Point |

At **Lulworth Cove** the resistant limestone on the outside has been breached at a narrow gap, after which the waves widened the cove in the softer clays behind. On the **Dalmatian coast** long ridges and valleys lie parallel to the shore, and flooding by the sea has left the ridges as islands.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Rate of cliff retreat (illustrative data)]
Survey pegs show that a clay cliff retreated 36 m in 18 years.

1. Rate $= 36 \div 18 = 2$ m per year.
2. At that rate, the time to lose a further 100 m is $100 \div 2 = 50$ years.
3. Retreat is not steady in reality: it comes in bursts during storms, after rain saturates the clay, or when the beach is lowered, so the rate is an average.
:::

:::worked-solution[Volume and mass of cliff material lost (illustrative data)]
A cliff 800 m long and 15 m high retreats at 1.6 m per year. Assume a density of 2.0 tonnes per m³.

1. Volume lost per year $= 800 \times 15 \times 1.6 = 19\,200$ m³.
2. Mass $= 19\,200 \times 2.0 = 38\,400$ tonnes per year.
3. This material is a major input of sediment to beaches, so cliff protection reduces the sediment supply to the coast further along.
:::

:::worked-solution[Gradient of a shore platform (illustrative data)]
A platform is 160 m wide from the cliff foot to low tide and falls 2.8 m over that distance.

1. Gradient $= 2.8 \div 160 = 0.0175$, or 1 in 57.
2. Angle $= \tan^{-1}(0.0175) = 1.0°$.
3. The gentle slope is typical of a platform and lets wave energy dissipate through friction.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining a stack from a field description"}
A photograph taken from a cliff top looking east shows an isolated column of chalk, about 25 m high, standing 40 m from the cliff, with a narrow neck of rock linking it to a lower stump nearby. **Identify:** a stack, with a stump. **Explain:** waves eroded a cave in a joint, which was cut through the headland to form an arch; weathering and erosion weakened the roof, which collapsed and left the stack. The stump shows that it is eroding further.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing the sequence of arch, stack and stump in the wrong order"}
The order is cave, arch, stack, stump. A geo is a separate feature formed from a cave or blowhole roof collapse.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving freeze–thaw for every coast"}
It operates only where temperatures regularly fall below freezing. In tropical coasts use salt crystal growth, solution and hydrolysis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming that the platform is cut by waves alone"}
Weathering and abrasion both help to level the platform, and the retreat of the cliff gives it its width.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sub-aerial weathering (freeze–thaw, salt, carbonation, solution, hydrolysis) and mass movement (rockfall, landslide, mudflow) shape cliffs along with marine erosion.
- Cliffs retreat by undercutting, notch formation, collapse and removal of debris; the profile reflects rock type and dip.
- Shore platforms are gentle slopes (about 1–3°) formed by cliff retreat, abrasion and weathering.
- The order of landforms is cave, arch, stack, stump; a geo forms from a collapsed cave roof or blowhole.
- Concordant coasts have rock bands parallel to the shore; discordant coasts have bands at right angles, giving headlands and bays.
- Rate = distance ÷ time; volume = length × height × retreat.
:::
