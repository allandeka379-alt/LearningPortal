## Introduction

On 7 February 2009, a day of extreme heat and strong, dry winds in the Australian state of Victoria, a number of fires spread so fast that people had little time to leave. The fires, known as Black Saturday, killed 173 people and destroyed about 2 000 homes. In the dry season in Zimbabwe, too, veld fires burn grassland and woodland every year, often started by people clearing land or hunting. Fire is a natural part of many ecosystems, but it becomes a hazard when it threatens people, property and livelihoods.

This lesson covers wildfires as a hazard. You should know about climate zones and vegetation. Other hazards have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Wildfire"}
An uncontrolled fire in vegetation (forest, shrubland, grassland or peat) in the countryside or at the edge of towns. In Zimbabwe a fire in grassland or woodland is called a veld fire.
:::

:::definition{term="Fire triangle"}
The three things needed for a fire: fuel (vegetation), heat (a source of ignition and high temperatures) and oxygen. Removing any one stops the fire.
:::

:::definition{term="Wildland–urban interface"}
The zone where houses and other buildings meet or mix with natural vegetation. It is the area of greatest risk to people because fire can reach buildings directly or by flying embers.
:::

## Distribution and conditions

Fire-prone regions have a plant growth season followed by a long, hot, dry season that dries the fuel.

- **Mediterranean-climate regions**: California, the Mediterranean basin, central Chile, south-west and south-east Australia and the Western Cape. Wet winters grow plenty of vegetation, and hot dry summers dry it.
- **Boreal forests** of Canada and Russia: lightning, long dry spells and peat and conifer fuel.
- **Savannas** of Africa and northern Australia: tall grass dries in the dry season and burns almost every year.
- **Tropical forests and peat swamps** (Amazon and Indonesia) where forests are cleared by burning and drought dries the surface (the Indonesian fires of 2015 produced a smoke haze over South-East Asia).

Conditions that favour severe fires: a large and dry **fuel load**, high temperature, low humidity, strong wind, a recent drought and an ignition source. Wind speed, temperature and humidity are combined in fire danger indices.

## Causes

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Physical | Lightning strikes (the main natural cause in boreal forests); sparks from falling rocks; very rarely volcanic activity; spontaneous combustion of dry peat or compost |
| Human (accidental) | Discarded cigarettes, campfires, sparks from vehicles and machinery, fires set to clear land or cook that escape, faulty power lines |
| Human (deliberate) | Arson; burning to clear land or to flush game in the dry season, which sometimes escapes control |

## Spread

A fire may be a ground fire (burning peat or roots), a surface fire (burning grass, litter and shrubs) or a crown fire (jumping from tree top to tree top, the fastest and most intense). Spread depends on:

1. **Fuel**: dry, fine, continuous fuels such as grass burn quickly; moist or sparse fuel burns slowly.
2. **Wind**: pushes flames towards new fuel, supplies oxygen and carries burning embers ahead of the front (spotting) so that new fires start far away.
3. **Topography**: fires travel faster uphill because flames are closer to the fuel above and preheat it; steep valleys act as chimneys.
4. **Weather**: hot, dry air keeps fuel dry; a wind change can turn the flank of a fire into a new front.

## Impacts and vulnerability

**Short term**: deaths and injuries, burned homes, farms and infrastructure, loss of livestock and wildlife, smoke that damages health and closes airports and schools, evacuation and loss of power. **Long term**: rebuilding costs, mental health problems, lost jobs in farming, tourism and forestry, soil erosion and floods on burned slopes (ash and a water-repellent soil surface increase runoff), poorer water quality and changes in the ecosystem. Fire also releases carbon dioxide, and many fire-adapted plants (eucalypts, proteas, some pines) regenerate afterwards.

Vulnerability is greater where houses are built of flammable material and surrounded by vegetation, roads are few and narrow, warnings are weak, people do not know what to do, and where poverty limits protection. Cases: Black Saturday (2009, 173 deaths); the Camp Fire in California (November 2018, 85 deaths and about 18 000 buildings destroyed, with investigators linking it to electrical equipment); the Black Summer fires in Australia (2019–20, more than 17 million hectares burned and 33 people killed directly, with smoke linked to hundreds more deaths); Fort McMurray in Canada (May 2016, about 88 000 people evacuated and about 2 400 buildings destroyed, with no direct deaths).

## Prediction, monitoring and management

- **Prediction**: fire danger rating systems combine temperature, humidity, wind and the dryness of fuel; days are rated from low to catastrophic and warnings are issued. Seasonal forecasts show drought.
- **Monitoring**: satellites (MODIS and VIIRS) detect active fires and hot spots within hours, aircraft and lookouts report fires, and mapping tracks the burned area.
- **Reducing fuel**: prescribed (controlled) burning in the cool season, thinning, and firebreaks (fire guards). These work but can escape control, need skilled crews and are harder as weather becomes more extreme.
- **Building and planning**: cleared "defensible space" around homes, non-flammable roofing and walls, limits on building at the wildland–urban interface.
- **Firefighting and evacuation**: ground crews, aircraft dropping water, clear evacuation routes and "leave early" advice; refuges for the last resort.
- **Education and law**: awareness campaigns, bans on fires on dangerous days and penalties for arson or careless burning. In Zimbabwe the Forestry Commission and the Environmental Management Agency promote fire guards and awareness during the dry season.
- **Recovery**: emergency erosion control, replanting, rebuilding to safer standards.

**Climate change** is lengthening fire seasons and increasing the frequency of extreme fire weather (hotter, drier, windier days) in many regions, and drought and heat stress add to the fuel dryness. This increases the likelihood of large fires but does not alone cause them; ignition and land management matter too.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Area burned and time to reach a town]
A fire front advances 2 km in every direction from the point of ignition (assume a circular burn area).
1. Area $= \pi r^2 = 3.1416 \times 2^2 = 12.57$ km².
2. Convert to hectares: 1 km² = 100 ha, so $12.57 \times 100 = 1\,257$ ha.
3. A town lies 15 km away on level ground and the fire spreads at 3 km/h. Time $= 15 \div 3 = 5$ h. If the town lies uphill and the fire spreads twice as fast, time $= 15 \div 6 = 2.5$ h, so the warning time is halved.
:::

:::worked-solution[Percentage of fires started by people]
In one fire season a region has 400 fires: 280 are human-caused (accidental or deliberate), 100 are caused by lightning and 20 have an unknown cause (illustrative data).
1. Human-caused share $= 280 \div 400 \times 100 = 70\%$.
2. Lightning share $= 100 \div 400 \times 100 = 25\%$.
3. Most fires are started by people, so education, regulation and surveillance can reduce them, though lightning fires remain.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a pattern of spread"}
A map extract shows a fire that began in a valley and travelled 8 km north-east in three hours, then only 1 km in the next three hours when it reached a river and an irrigated farm. Description: "The fire spread quickly (about 2.7 km/h) across dry grass and up the slope, but slowed to about 0.3 km/h at the river." Explanation: dry fuel, strong wind and uphill slope increase spread; water and irrigated crops act as a natural firebreak because the fuel is moist and discontinuous.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying lightning causes most wildfires everywhere"}
Lightning is the main natural cause in boreal forests, but in many regions (including Zimbabwe, the Mediterranean and California) most fires are started by people, accidentally or deliberately.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all fire as damaging"}
Many ecosystems (savannas, eucalypt woodland, some pine forests) depend on regular fire. It is a hazard because of the threat to people and property, and because of fires that burn unusually hot or often.
:::

:::mistake{title="Crediting climate change with starting fires"}
Climate change makes fire weather more extreme and fuel drier, but a fire still needs an ignition source. Say "increases the risk and severity" and not "causes fires".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Wildfires need fuel, heat and oxygen; they are most likely where a wet growth season is followed by a long hot, dry season.
- Physical causes are lightning and sparks from falling rocks; most fires in many regions are started by people.
- Spread is faster in dry, continuous fuel, strong wind, uphill and in crown fires; embers start new fires ahead of the front.
- Impacts include deaths, lost homes, smoke, erosion and floods after fire, and changes to ecosystems.
- Management combines danger ratings and satellite monitoring, fuel reduction, planning at the wildland–urban interface, education, firefighting and evacuation.
- Climate change is lengthening fire seasons and making extreme fire weather more likely.
:::
