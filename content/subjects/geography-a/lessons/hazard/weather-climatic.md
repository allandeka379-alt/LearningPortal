## Introduction

In March 2019 Cyclone Idai brought days of heavy rain to the Eastern Highlands of Zimbabwe, and floods and landslides destroyed homes, roads and bridges in Chimanimani and Chipinge. A few years earlier, in the 2015–16 season, an El Niño drought cut harvests across southern Africa. Weather and climatic hazards are among the most frequent and costly natural hazards in the region. This lesson explains their causes, effects and management. Tropical cyclones are covered in detail in their own lesson; here they appear as one cause of flooding. You should already know about the ITCZ, air masses and El Niño from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hazard"}
A natural event or process that has the potential to harm people or property. It becomes a **disaster** when it affects a vulnerable community and overwhelms its ability to cope.
:::

:::definition{term="Drought"}
A prolonged period of below-normal rainfall that causes a shortage of water. *Meteorological* drought is low rainfall; *agricultural* drought is a soil-moisture deficit that damages crops; *hydrological* drought is low river, dam and groundwater levels.
:::

:::definition{term="Heatwave"}
A period of several days of unusually high temperatures for the place and time of year (countries define it differently, for example a stated number of days above a threshold).
:::

:::definition{term="Recurrence interval"}
The average time between floods of a given size or larger. A "1-in-20-year" flood has a 5% chance of occurring in any year.
:::

## Drought

**Causes.** Natural causes include El Niño seasons, a late start to the rains or long dry spells during the season (often when the ITCZ is weak or in the wrong place), and persistent high pressure over the region. Climate change adds higher temperatures and more evaporation. Human factors include over-use of water, deforestation and degraded catchments, which worsen the effects.

**Effects.** Crop failure, loss of livestock, food insecurity and malnutrition; lower dam and river levels (for example reduced hydropower at Kariba); water shortages in towns; more veld fires; conflict over water; rural–urban migration.

**Mitigation and adaptation.** Seasonal forecasts from the Meteorological Services Department and regional climate outlook forums; drought-tolerant crops (sorghum, millets); conservation agriculture; water harvesting, boreholes and small-scale irrigation; destocking and fodder banks; grain reserves and food aid; water-use efficiency and demand management in towns.

## Floods

**Causes.** Intense rainfall, rain from tropical cyclones, saturated soil that cannot absorb more water, bare or degraded catchments that increase runoff, siltation of channels, urban surfaces and poor drainage, and failure or overflow of dams.

**Examples.** In early 2014 heavy rain filled the Tokwe-Mukosi dam, which was still being completed in Masvingo Province, and flooded the basin; thousands of families had to be moved to temporary camps. In March 2019 Cyclone Idai caused floods and landslides in Chimanimani and Chipinge (hundreds of people died in Zimbabwe according to official Civil Protection reports, and many more were affected in Mozambique and Malawi).

**Effects.** Loss of life, damage to homes, roads, bridges, crops and livestock, outbreaks of disease from contaminated water, displacement and long recovery.

**Mitigation.** Early warning and evacuation plans; no building on floodplains; dams and embankments; drainage improvements; afforestation and soil conservation in catchments; flood-proof buildings; community disaster committees; relief and rebuilding supported by the Civil Protection Department.

## Tornadoes

A tornado is a narrow, violently rotating column of air extending from a thunderstorm to the ground. It forms from a rotating thunderstorm (supercell) when warm, moist, unstable air meets strong wind shear. Tornadoes are rare and small in Zimbabwe compared with the Great Plains of the United States; severe thunderstorms with hail and damaging winds are much more common and can destroy roofs, crops and electricity lines. Intensity is rated on the Enhanced Fujita scale (EF0 to EF5). Mitigation includes warnings from the weather service, strong shelters and securing roofs.

## Tropical storms (hurricanes and typhoons)

Hurricanes (Atlantic and eastern Pacific), typhoons (north-west Pacific) and cyclones (Indian Ocean and southern Pacific) are the same kind of storm: a tropical cyclone (see the separate lesson on tropical cyclones). Zimbabwe lies inland, but storms that make landfall in Mozambique can bring torrential rain, floods and strong wind to the Eastern Highlands, as Idai did.

## Heatwaves

**Causes.** A persistent anticyclone, with sinking air that warms by compression, clear skies and light winds, allows heat to build for days. Dry soils reduce cooling by evaporation. Cities make it worse through the urban heat island, and climate change is making heatwaves more frequent and intense.

**Effects.** Heat stress, illness and death (especially among older people, young children and outdoor workers), higher demand for electricity and water, damage to crops and livestock, and wildfires.

**Mitigation.** Heat warnings; adjusting working hours; shade, trees and cool roofs; access to water; public cooling spaces; building design; and care for vulnerable people.

## How effective are the measures?

| Level | Examples | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household | Drought-tolerant seed, rainwater tanks, raising houses, saving, storing grain | Quick, low cost, local knowledge | Limited by poverty and knowledge; cannot protect against a large flood |
| Community | Ward disaster committees, early-warning messages, shared water points, community gardens | Strong local ownership; fast response | Limited funds and equipment; depends on training and leadership |
| National | Forecasting, the Civil Protection Department, dams, relief food, laws on floodplain use | Large scale and technical capacity | Funding shortages, slow delivery to remote areas, poor maintenance |

The best results come from combining the levels: a forecast issued nationally has no effect unless it reaches the community in time and households know what to do.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Measuring a drought against the normal]
The long-term mean seasonal rainfall at a station is 650 mm. In a drought season the station records 500 mm.

1. Percentage of normal: $500 \div 650 \times 100 = 76.9\%$.
2. Deficit: $650 - 500 = 150$ mm, which is $150 \div 650 \times 100 = 23.1\%$ below normal.
3. With a rule of thumb that a season below 75% of the mean is a drought (a teaching rule), this season is just above the threshold, but because rainfall in dry spells at the critical growth stage may matter more than the total, crops may still fail.
:::

:::worked-solution[Recurrence interval of floods]
A river has 25 years of annual peak flood records. The floods are ranked from the largest (rank 1) downwards. Recurrence interval $= (n + 1) \div \text{rank}$.

1. Rank 1: $26 \div 1 = 26$ years.
2. Rank 2: $26 \div 2 = 13$ years.
3. Rank 3: $26 \div 3 = 8.7$ years.
4. The chance in any year of a flood the size of the rank-2 flood or greater is $1 \div 13 = 7.7\%$.
5. A "13-year flood" does not mean one flood every 13 years: two can occur in successive years. The estimate also depends on the length of the record.
:::

:::example{title="Identifying a heatwave from daily data"}
Illustrative maximum temperatures (°C) over ten days in a hot spell: 33, 34, 36, 37, 38, 36, 35, 34, 36, 37. Use the definition: at least three consecutive days with a maximum above 35 °C (a teaching definition). **Analysis:** days 3 to 6 (36, 37, 38 and 36 °C) are all above 35 °C, so this is a four-day heatwave. Day 7 (35 °C) is not above the threshold, so the run ends. Days 9 and 10 are above 35 °C but there are only two, so they do not qualify. **Explain:** a persistent anticyclone with clear skies and sinking air is the most likely cause.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing about hazards without the effect on people"}
A hazard becomes a disaster because of vulnerability. Always link the physical event to its effects on people, and to why some people suffer more.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing weather, climate and hazard"}
A heatwave or a storm is weather. A change in how often they occur is climate change. State which you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing measures without judging them"}
For each measure give who benefits, the cost, how fast it works and its limits. The question "assess the effectiveness" needs a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 4). *Explain causes* in a chain (for example El Niño, weaker rains, dry spell, crop failure). *Describe effects* in groups: people, economy, environment. For *assess the effectiveness of measures*, use levels (household, community, national), short-term and long-term effects, and a final judgement. Quote data (for example a rainfall percentage or a recurrence interval) and name examples (Idai 2019, Tokwe-Mukosi 2014, 2015–16 drought).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Droughts come from El Niño, late or interrupted rains and persistent high pressure; their effects are crop and livestock losses, hunger and low dams.
- Floods are caused by intense rainfall, cyclones, saturated soils, poor land use and dam failure, and are managed by warning, planning and defences.
- Tornadoes are rare in Zimbabwe; severe thunderstorms are more common. Hurricanes, typhoons and cyclones are the same type of storm.
- Heatwaves arise under persistent anticyclones and are worsened by dry soils and cities.
- Measures work at household, community and national levels, and are most effective when combined.
:::
