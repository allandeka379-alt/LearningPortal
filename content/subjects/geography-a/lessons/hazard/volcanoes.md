## Introduction

When Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines erupted on 15 June 1991, it sent ash about 35 km into the atmosphere and injected sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, which cooled the planet by about half a degree Celsius for the next two years. Yet around 800 people died, far fewer than the number at risk, because scientists had warned in time and tens of thousands were moved. Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia, a smaller eruption in November 1985, killed about 23 000 people in the town of Armero because mud flows reached it before the warning could be acted on. Volcanoes are a hazard that is partly predictable, and the outcome depends on the type of eruption, the hazard and how people respond.

This lesson covers volcano types, hazards and impacts. You should know the plate boundaries and hotspots from tectonics. Earthquakes and the management of both hazards are in separate lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Volcano and magma"}
A volcano is a vent in the crust through which magma (molten rock with dissolved gas) reaches the surface. Once it erupts, the molten rock is called lava, and fragments blown out are called tephra.
:::

:::definition{term="Effusive and explosive eruptions"}
An effusive eruption releases runny, low-viscosity lava that flows from the vent. An explosive eruption blasts gas-rich, viscous magma into fragments (ash, pumice, bombs) and may produce pyroclastic flows. Viscosity, which depends mainly on the silica content, and the amount of dissolved gas decide which occurs.
:::

:::definition{term="Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)"}
A scale from 0 to 8 that classifies eruptions mainly by the volume of material ejected and the height of the eruption column. It is roughly logarithmic: each step up is about ten times the volume of tephra.
:::

## Formation and types

Volcanoes form at **subduction zones** (the sinking plate releases water, which lowers the melting point of the mantle above it), at **divergent boundaries** (rising mantle melts as pressure falls) and at **hotspots** (plumes of hot mantle rock, such as under Hawaii and Iceland). Their shape depends on the magma.

| Type | Magma and eruption | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stratovolcano (composite) | Viscous, silica-rich (andesite) magma; explosive eruptions alternating with lava | Steep, symmetrical cone built of layers of lava and ash | Mount Pinatubo, Philippines; Mount Fuji, Japan |
| Shield volcano | Runny basalt lava; mostly effusive | Broad, gently sloping dome | Mauna Loa and Kilauea, Hawaii |
| Cinder cone | Gas-rich basalt thrown out as fragments (cinders) | Small, steep cone, often on the flank of a larger volcano | Paricutin, Mexico |
| Lava dome | Very viscous lava that piles up near the vent and may collapse | Rounded mound inside a crater | Soufrière Hills, Montserrat |
| Fissure eruption | Basalt lava from long cracks | Flood-like lava sheets, no cone | Iceland (Laki, 1783) |

## Hazards

1. **Lava flows** destroy property but are usually slow enough to outrun. Basalt lava from Nyiragongo (Democratic Republic of Congo) flowed fast down steep slopes into Goma in January 2002 and again in 2021, which shows that the fluidity and slope matter.
2. **Tephra and ash fall** (fragments from fine ash to blocks). Ash collapses roofs, contaminates water, damages crops, harms lungs and engines and closes airports: the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull (Iceland, VEI 4) closed much of northern European airspace for about a week.
3. **Pyroclastic flows**: avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock at hundreds of degrees Celsius travelling at speeds that can exceed 100 km/h. They are the most lethal hazard; on Montserrat 19 people died in June 1997.
4. **Lahars**: mud flows made when ash mixes with rainfall or melted snow. They can travel tens of kilometres down valleys. At Nevado del Ruiz in 1985 lahars from melted ice killed about 23 000 people.
5. **Volcanic landslides and sector collapse**, which can also cause tsunamis.
6. **Jökulhlaups**: sudden floods when an eruption melts a glacier or ice cap (Iceland).
7. **Gases**: carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and others. SO₂ forms acid rain and, high in the stratosphere, reflects sunlight and cools the climate (as after Pinatubo).

## The Volcanic Explosivity Index

| VEI | Minimum tephra volume | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 million m³ | Explosive |
| 3 | 10 million m³ | Severe |
| 4 | 100 million m³ | Cataclysmic |
| 5 | 1 billion m³ (1 km³) | Paroxysmal |
| 6 | 10 billion m³ (10 km³) | Colossal |

Because each step is about ten times larger, a VEI 5 eruption has at least 100 times the minimum volume of a VEI 3 eruption. VEI measures size, not deaths: the VEI 3 eruption at Nevado del Ruiz killed more than the VEI 6 eruption at Pinatubo.

## Impacts and vulnerability

**Short term**: deaths and injuries from pyroclastic flows, lahars and ash, evacuation, damaged homes, crops and livestock, disrupted transport and water. **Long term**: loss of land and settlement (Plymouth on Montserrat was abandoned from 1997), migration, loss of tourism and farming income, but also fertile volcanic soils and geothermal energy. Global effects include climate cooling.

Vulnerability is greater where many people live close to the volcano on fertile slopes, where hazard maps and warning systems are weak, where roads for evacuation are few and where people do not trust or understand warnings.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Comparing eruption sizes with the VEI]
Compare the minimum tephra volumes of a VEI 5 eruption (1 km³) and a VEI 3 eruption (0.01 km³).
1. VEI 5 minimum volume $= 1\,000\,000\,000$ m³; VEI 3 minimum volume $= 10\,000\,000$ m³.
2. Ratio $= 1\,000\,000\,000 \div 10\,000\,000 = 100$.
3. Each VEI step is about ten times, so two steps give $10^2 = 100$ times.
:::

:::worked-solution[Time for a hazard to reach a town]
A pyroclastic flow moves at 108 km/h down a valley. How long does it take to reach a village 9 km from the vent, and how does this compare with a lahar at 36 km/h travelling 30 km?
1. Pyroclastic flow: time $= 9 \div 108 = 0.0833$ h $= 5$ minutes.
2. Lahar: time $= 30 \div 36 = 0.833$ h $= 50$ minutes.
3. The pyroclastic flow arrives with almost no warning, so people must leave before the eruption; the lahar allows a short time to move to high ground if a warning is given.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a pattern of damage"}
A table of an eruption shows deaths: pyroclastic flows 24, lahars 95, ash fall 12, other 4 (illustrative data). Description: "Lahars caused the most deaths (95 out of 135, 70%), followed by pyroclastic flows (24, 18%)." Explanation: the lahars were triggered when rain mixed with ash on steep slopes and ran down river valleys where settlements were located, travelling beyond the exclusion zone drawn for the pyroclastic flows.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all volcanoes are explosive"}
Shield volcanoes erupt runny lava effusively. Explosiveness depends on the silica content (viscosity) and the amount of dissolved gas, not on whether a volcano is large.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equating VEI with the number of deaths"}
VEI measures the size of the eruption. Deaths depend on the hazard, the nearness and number of people and the warning, as Pinatubo and Nevado del Ruiz show.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling lava the main killer"}
Most deaths are caused by pyroclastic flows, lahars and the impacts of ash, not by slow-moving lava.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Volcanoes form at subduction zones, divergent boundaries and hotspots; types include stratovolcano, shield, cinder cone, lava dome and fissure.
- Viscous, gas-rich magma gives explosive eruptions; runny basalt gives effusive eruptions.
- Hazards: lava flows, tephra, pyroclastic flows, lahars, landslides, jökulhlaups and gases.
- The VEI (0–8) is roughly logarithmic: each step is about ten times the volume of tephra.
- Deaths depend on vulnerability and warning, not only size: Pinatubo (1991) and Nevado del Ruiz (1985) contrast.
- Long-term impacts include abandoned land, migration and lost income, but also fertile soils and geothermal energy.
:::
