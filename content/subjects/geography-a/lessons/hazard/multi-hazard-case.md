## Introduction

A multi-hazard environment is one where several different hazards threaten the same people. The Philippines, an archipelago of more than 7 000 islands in the western Pacific, is one of the clearest examples: it lies on the Pacific Ring of Fire, with many earthquakes and active volcanoes, and in the typhoon belt of the north-west Pacific. Cambridge expects a detailed specific example with real, dated facts, an explanation of the causes, the impacts, the management strategies and a judgement of how well they have worked. This lesson sets out one such example, chosen by the author; you may use another (for example Japan or Indonesia) provided your facts are secure.

This lesson assumes the single-hazard lessons on earthquakes, volcanoes, their management and tropical cyclones. It does not repeat the theory, but applies it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Multi-hazard environment"}
A place exposed to two or more different hazards, which may occur independently, interact, or follow one another. The risk is higher than for a single hazard because resources for response are shared and recovery from one event may not be complete before the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Cascading and compound hazards"}
A cascading hazard is one in which a first event triggers another (an earthquake triggering landslides or a tsunami; a volcanic eruption followed by lahars when rain falls on the ash). A compound hazard is one in which two hazards occur at the same time or close together and their effects combine (an eruption during a typhoon).
:::

## Setting and causes

The Philippines is part of the Philippine Mobile Belt, a zone of small plates between the Eurasian plate to the west and the Philippine Sea plate to the east. Subduction on both sides (the Manila Trench to the west and the Philippine Trench to the east) produces earthquakes, and melting above the subducting plates feeds about two dozen active volcanoes. The Philippine Fault, a long strike-slip fault running through Luzon, Leyte and Mindanao, adds shallow, damaging earthquakes. Tropical cyclones form over the warm waters of the western Pacific (above about 26.5 °C) and about twenty enter the Philippine area of responsibility in an average year, mostly between June and November, while heavy monsoon rain adds to the flooding. Many of the people live on low coasts and flood plains, on steep slopes and near volcanoes, because of fertile soils, fishing and the jobs of cities such as Manila.

## The hazards and their impacts

| Hazard | Event | Facts (rounded) |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake | Luzon earthquake, 16 July 1990 | Magnitude 7.7 on the Philippine Fault; about 1 600 deaths; landslides and liquefaction; damage in Baguio and Dagupan |
| Earthquake | Bohol earthquake, 15 October 2013 | Magnitude 7.2; more than 200 deaths and tens of thousands of damaged homes; many historic churches damaged |
| Volcano | Mount Pinatubo, 15 June 1991 | VEI 6; about 800 deaths; large-scale evacuation; lahars for years; global cooling of about 0.5 °C |
| Volcano | Taal Volcano, 12 January 2020 | Explosive eruption with an ash column reaching about 15 km; tens of thousands evacuated; Manila's airport temporarily closed |
| Tropical cyclone | Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda), 8 November 2013 | One of the strongest tropical cyclones ever to make landfall; storm surge of several metres, especially at Tacloban; more than 6 000 deaths; millions displaced |

**Interactions.** When Pinatubo erupted, Typhoon Yunya was passing nearby: its rain mixed with ash, making the ash fall heavy enough to collapse roofs, which caused many of the deaths, and it also began the lahars. Earthquakes and typhoon rain both trigger landslides on steep slopes, and a house that is cracked by an earthquake is weaker when the next typhoon arrives. Repeated hazards also mean that relief agencies and national budgets are stretched.

## Management

1. **Institutions and law.** The Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical and Astronomical Services Administration (PAGASA) forecasts weather and cyclones. The Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS) monitors earthquakes and volcanoes and uses a volcano alert level system from 0 to 5. The Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Act of 2010 (Republic Act 10121) shifted policy from relief towards risk reduction, set up the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Council and required local governments to prepare plans and set aside funds.
2. **Monitoring and warning.** Seismometers and gas and ground monitoring at active volcanoes; satellite and radar tracking of typhoons; public wind signals; hazard maps. After Haiyan, warnings were improved to explain the term "storm surge" and its likely height.
3. **Preparation.** Regular earthquake and fire drills in schools and offices; evacuation centres; pre-emptive evacuations before typhoons; community-based early warning.
4. **Planning and construction.** The National Building Code (1977) sets standards, but enforcement is uneven, and many informal homes are in hazard zones. Hazard maps guide land use around Taal and Mayon, such as permanent danger zones.
5. **Recovery.** Reconstruction after Haiyan and Bohol included rebuilding to higher standards and new housing sites, but progress was slow and many people lived in temporary shelters for years.

## Evaluating success

**Successes.** The Pinatubo evacuation saved thousands of lives; deaths from typhoons have fallen where pre-emptive evacuation is organised; legal and institutional frameworks are among the more advanced in the region; communities near volcanoes know the warnings.

**Limits.** Haiyan showed that warnings must be understood and acted on; poverty and informal housing in hazard zones keep vulnerability high; building codes are weakly enforced; local governments differ in resources; the hazards recur so that recovery is incomplete; and climate change is likely to increase rainfall and the proportion of intense typhoons.

**Judgement.** Strategies are most effective for hazards that can be monitored (volcanoes, typhoon tracks) and weakest where they depend on housing quality and poverty reduction (earthquakes, surges), so overall the country has improved but remains highly vulnerable.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Comparing the hazard record]
An illustrative table lists 30 damaging events in the Philippines over a long period: 10 earthquakes, 5 volcanic eruptions and 15 tropical cyclones, with total deaths of 900, 1 500 and 6 600 respectively (illustrative data, not a real record).
1. Share of events that are cyclones $= 15 \div 30 \times 100 = 50\%$.
2. Mean deaths per event: earthquakes $900 \div 10 = 90$; volcanoes $1\,500 \div 5 = 300$; cyclones $6\,600 \div 15 = 440$.
3. Cyclones are the most frequent and the most deadly per event, but earthquakes and eruptions can cause very high losses in a single event, so a risk assessment must consider frequency, scale and vulnerability.
:::

:::worked-solution[Percentage of a risk budget]
A province has US\$ 24 million for disaster risk reduction. It spends 9 million on typhoon shelters, 6 million on earthquake-resistant schools, 5 million on volcano monitoring and 4 million on training.
1. Total spent $= 9 + 6 + 5 + 4 = 24$ million, so the whole budget is used.
2. Share spent on typhoon shelters $= 9 \div 24 \times 100 = 37.5\%$.
3. Whether this is the right balance depends on the frequency and scale of each hazard: typhoons are the most frequent, so a large share is justified, though one eruption or earthquake can cause losses that this share would not cover.
:::

:::example{title="Structuring a detailed specific example answer"}
For a question such as "Evaluate the success of strategies for managing hazards in a multi-hazard environment": (1) name the place and the hazards, with the physical cause of each in one sentence; (2) give two dated events with rounded figures; (3) describe the management strategies, one paragraph each, giving the named organisation or law; (4) judge each: what worked, for whom, and what did not (Haiyan, building codes); (5) write a conclusion that weighs the strategies and states how far the environment is better protected than before. Keep each fact accurate and attributed (for example "about 800 deaths at Pinatubo"), and avoid listing facts without linking them to the question.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This lesson is Cambridge only (A Level, Paper 3 option 9, hazardous environments). Candidates may choose a different multi-hazard example, so long as the facts are real, dated and accurate. ZIMSEC students use Zimbabwean and southern African examples for weather hazards.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Telling a story about each hazard without comparison"}
The question is about the environment as a whole. Compare the hazards, show how they interact and explain why one environment has several.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unreliable figures"}
Do not quote deaths or costs you are unsure of. Give rounded values with the year ("more than 6 000 deaths in Haiyan, 2013") or describe the scale generally.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing management without judging it"}
List strategies, then say who benefits, what limits them (cost, enforcement, trust) and whether the example shows success or failure.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 9), often as a 20-mark essay needing a detailed specific example and a judgement. *Describe* causes with a clear physical chain. *Explain* impacts with located facts and dates. *Evaluate* management by weighing monitoring, planning, preparation and recovery, comparing hazards and ending with a supported conclusion. Plan the facts you know securely before writing.
:::
