## Introduction

On 11 March 2011 a plate boundary off north-eastern Japan slipped and released an earthquake of moment magnitude 9.0 to 9.1. The shaking lasted several minutes, yet most Japanese buildings stood. The tsunami that followed killed around twenty thousand people. A year earlier, in January 2010, a much smaller earthquake (magnitude 7.0) struck close to Port-au-Prince in Haiti, and estimates of the death toll run from over 100 000 to over 300 000. The hazard is the same type of event; the disaster is very different. This lesson explains where earthquakes happen, how they are measured, what hazards they cause and why vulnerability shapes the outcome.

You should already know the plate boundaries (convergent, divergent, conservative) from the tectonics lessons. Managing earthquakes and volcanoes is in a separate lesson, and volcanoes are in another.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Focus and epicentre"}
The focus (hypocentre) is the point underground where the rock breaks and energy is released. The epicentre is the point on the surface directly above it. A shallow focus (under about 70 km) usually causes more damage than a deep one of the same magnitude.
:::

:::definition{term="Seismic waves"}
Energy released at the focus travels as waves. Body waves pass through the Earth: P (primary, compressional) waves are the fastest and travel through solids and liquids; S (secondary, shear) waves are slower and travel only through solids. Surface waves travel along the ground: Love waves move the ground sideways and Rayleigh waves move it in a rolling motion. Surface waves are slower than body waves but cause most of the damage.
:::

:::definition{term="Magnitude"}
A measure of the energy released at the source. The Richter scale is logarithmic: each whole number is about ten times the wave amplitude and about thirty-two times the energy. The moment magnitude scale (Mw), based on the area of the fault that slipped, the amount of slip and the strength of the rock, is now the standard for large earthquakes.
:::

:::definition{term="Intensity"}
A measure of the effects of shaking at a particular place. The Modified Mercalli scale runs from I (not felt) to XII (total destruction) and is based on observed damage, so intensity changes with distance from the epicentre, local ground and building quality.
:::

## Distribution

Earthquakes cluster along plate boundaries. The Pacific Ring of Fire (subduction zones off Japan, the Philippines, Chile, Alaska and the Americas, and the San Andreas transform fault) has the greatest number and the largest. The Alpine–Himalayan belt (collision zones from the Mediterranean through Turkey and Iran to Nepal) is the second. Mid-ocean ridges have frequent but small earthquakes. Intraplate earthquakes also occur, usually small, along old faults, for example in the East African Rift, which runs close to Zimbabwe, and sometimes from mining and reservoir loading.

Earthquakes at subduction zones are the largest because a locked plate boundary can store strain over a long area, while earthquakes at transform boundaries are shallower and often more damaging to cities nearby.

## Hazards

| Hazard | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ground shaking | Buildings and bridges are shaken sideways and fail, especially unreinforced masonry and soft ground | Most deaths in Haiti, 2010 |
| Liquefaction | Water-saturated sandy soil loses strength and behaves like a liquid; buildings tilt and sink, pipes break | Christchurch, New Zealand, February 2011 |
| Landslides and rock falls | Shaking triggers slope failure, burying villages and blocking roads | Nepal, April 2015 |
| Tsunami | A sudden vertical movement of the sea floor displaces water; waves travel at jet speed in deep water and slow and build up near the coast | Indian Ocean, December 2004; Japan, 2011 |
| Fire | Broken gas pipes and electricity cables start fires, and broken water mains stop them being fought | San Francisco 1906; Kobe 1995 |
| Aftershocks | Smaller earthquakes continue as the crust readjusts and damage already weakened buildings | Christchurch, Nepal |

## Impacts and vulnerability

Short-term impacts are deaths and injuries, collapsed homes, broken roads, water, power and communications, and the strain on hospitals. Long-term impacts are homelessness, loss of livelihoods, disease where water supplies are damaged, trauma, cost of reconstruction, and the loss of heritage sites. Economic losses are larger in rich countries because they have more to lose, but deaths are far fewer.

Vulnerability is higher where:

1. **Building quality is poor**: unreinforced masonry, no building code or no enforcement.
2. **Population density is high** and many people live in informal settlements on steep slopes or made-up ground.
3. **Poverty** limits repair, insurance and the ability to move.
4. **Warning, education and emergency services are weak**: few people know what to do, roads are blocked, hospitals are few.
5. **Governance is weak**: corruption in construction and slow, poorly co-ordinated aid.

Comparing Haiti (2010, magnitude 7.0, shallow focus near a city of millions, little enforcement of building codes) with Chile (27 February 2010, magnitude 8.8, about 500 deaths, a strict code and well-prepared services) shows that magnitude alone does not predict the disaster.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[How much larger is one earthquake than another?]
Compare a magnitude 8.0 earthquake with a magnitude 6.0 earthquake on the logarithmic scale.
1. The difference is $8.0 - 6.0 = 2.0$ magnitude units.
2. Amplitude ratio $= 10^{2} = 100$.
3. Energy ratio $\approx 10^{1.5 \times 2} = 10^{3} = 1\,000$.
4. So the magnitude 8.0 earthquake has waves 100 times as large and releases about 1 000 times as much energy.
:::

:::worked-solution[Distance to the epicentre from the S–P time lag]
A seismometer records the first P wave and the first S wave 12 seconds apart. Take the speed of P waves as 6.0 km/s and of S waves as 3.5 km/s.
1. Each kilometre takes $\dfrac{1}{3.5} = 0.2857$ s for S and $\dfrac{1}{6.0} = 0.1667$ s for P.
2. The lag grows by $0.2857 - 0.1667 = 0.1190$ s for every kilometre.
3. Distance $= 12 \div 0.1190 = 100.8 \approx 101$ km. (Equivalent shortcut: 8.4 km for every second of lag, $12 \times 8.4 = 100.8$ km.)
4. One station gives only a distance, so a circle of this radius is drawn; three stations give the epicentre where the circles meet.

![Travel-time graph for seismic waves: P waves at 6.0 km/s and S waves at 3.5 km/s drift further apart with distance, and a lag of 12 seconds between the first P and S arrival corresponds to a distance of about 101 km from the epicentre.](media:diagrams/seismic-travel-time-lag.png "Figure: P and S wave travel times and the S–P lag method for finding distance to the epicentre (worked-example speeds)")

:::

:::example{title="Describing the effects of an earthquake from a table of Mercalli intensities"}
A table shows intensity VIII at 5 km from an epicentre, VI at 40 km and IV at 120 km (illustrative data). Description: "Intensity falls with distance from the epicentre, from VIII (heavy damage to ordinary buildings) at 5 km to IV (felt indoors, little damage) at 120 km, a fall of four units over 115 km." Explanation: seismic energy spreads out and is absorbed as it travels. A local exception would be soft sediment, which amplifies shaking and may give a higher intensity than expected.
:::

## Prediction and the nature of the hazard

Earthquakes cannot be predicted in the sense of giving date, place and magnitude in advance. Scientists can map the faults, estimate the probability of a large earthquake in a given period and detect seismic gaps, but foreshocks are not reliable signals. This is why preparation, rather than prediction, is the main defence; that is the subject of the management lesson.

:::keyfact{title="Values to learn"}
- Each whole magnitude step is about 10 times the amplitude and about 32 times the energy.
- P waves are faster than S waves; S waves cannot pass through liquid; surface waves do most damage.
- Intensity (Mercalli, I–XII) varies by place; magnitude is one value for the earthquake.
- The tsunami speed in the open ocean depends on depth: $v = \sqrt{g d}$, about 710 km/h in water 4 000 m deep.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing magnitude and intensity"}
Magnitude measures the energy released and has one value per earthquake. Intensity describes the effects at a place and has many values. Do not write "the earthquake had an intensity of 7.0".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying each Richter step is ten times the energy"}
Each step is ten times the amplitude but about thirty-two times the energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming the earthquake alone for the deaths"}
Most deaths come from collapsing buildings, tsunami and landslides. These depend on vulnerability and not only on magnitude, so a good answer uses both.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Earthquakes occur mainly at plate boundaries (Ring of Fire, Alpine–Himalayan belt, ridges); shallow-focus events cause the most damage.
- P waves are fastest, S waves cannot pass through liquid, and surface waves (Love and Rayleigh) do most of the damage.
- Magnitude (Richter, moment magnitude) is logarithmic: about 10 times the amplitude and 32 times the energy per step; intensity (Mercalli) varies by place.
- Hazards are shaking, liquefaction, landslides, tsunami, fire and aftershocks.
- Impacts depend on vulnerability: building quality, density, wealth, preparedness and governance, as the contrast between Haiti and Chile shows.
- Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely, so preparation is the main defence.
:::
