## Introduction

Nobody can stop an earthquake or an eruption, so management is about reducing losses. The aim is to lower **vulnerability** and **exposure** and to prepare for the response. When Mount Pinatubo erupted in 1991, monitoring by Philippine and American scientists allowed the evacuation of tens of thousands of people, and most of the 800 or so deaths came from roof collapse under wet ash during a typhoon, not from the main blast. In Haiti in 2010 almost nothing had been done in advance. This lesson asks which strategies work, how well, and why they work better in some places than others.

You should already know the hazards and their causes (earthquakes and volcanoes lessons). Tropical cyclones and wildfires have their own lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prediction, forecasting and early warning"}
Prediction states when, where and how big a hazard will be. Forecasting gives a probability over a period (for example a chance of a large earthquake on a fault in 30 years). Early warning detects an event that has started and sends an alert before the damaging waves arrive.
:::

:::definition{term="Precursor"}
A change that comes before an event and may signal it: for a volcano, swarms of small earthquakes, swelling of the ground, rising gas emission and increased heat; for an earthquake, foreshocks, although these are unreliable.
:::

:::definition{term="Perception of risk"}
How people judge the danger from a hazard. It depends on past experience, trust in authorities, education, and the benefits of living in the area, and so differs from the real risk.
:::

## Prediction and warning

**Earthquakes.** There is no reliable method of short-term prediction. Scientists map active faults and identify **seismic gaps**, parts of a fault that have been locked for longer than usual, and calculate probabilities. Foreshocks occur before only some earthquakes and are normally recognised only after the event, and reports of unusual animal behaviour are not reliable. **Early warning systems** are different: they detect the fast P waves near the epicentre and send an alert before the slower, more damaging S and surface waves arrive. Warning times are only seconds to tens of seconds, but they allow trains to stop, gas to be shut off and people to take cover. Japan's system is the best known.

**Volcanoes.** Eruptions are more predictable because most volcanoes show precursors. Monitoring uses seismometers (swarms and tremor), tiltmeters, GPS and satellite radar (ground deformation), gas sensors (sulfur dioxide) and thermal imaging. Alert-level systems (for example the Philippines' scale of 0 to 5) tell the public what to do. Even so, the timing, size and style of an eruption cannot be given exactly: warnings may be false alarms (which reduces trust) or too late.

## Protection and planning

| Strategy | How it works | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Earthquake-resistant design | Reinforced frames, cross-bracing, shear walls, base isolators and heavy damper masses allow buildings to sway without collapse | Costly; applies mainly to new buildings, and old ones need expensive retrofitting |
| Building codes and enforcement | Legal minimum standards for design and materials | Only work if inspected and enforced; corruption and cost undermine them |
| Land-use planning | Keeping housing and critical buildings off fault lines, soft ground, steep slopes, lahar channels and areas of pyroclastic flows | Hard to apply where land is scarce or people are already settled |
| Hazard maps and exclusion zones | Show areas at risk and restrict building and settlement | Maps need updating and people may ignore them |
| Engineering against volcanic hazards | Channels and sediment dams for lahars, diverting lava (seawater spraying at Heimaey, Iceland, in 1973), strong, steeply pitched roofs for ash | Limited against large eruptions |
| Education and drills | People know what to do (drop, cover and hold on; evacuate); regular school and community drills | Needs sustained funding, and knowledge fades |

## Emergency response and recovery

**Short term**: evacuation, search and rescue (the chances of finding survivors fall sharply after about three days), medical care, clean water, food and shelter. Roads and hospitals that are damaged or overloaded slow the response. **Long term**: rebuilding to safer standards ("build back better"), restoring livelihoods, mental health care and, after large eruptions, resettlement. Recovery depends on wealth, insurance, aid and governance; in Nepal after the April 2015 earthquake reconstruction of rural houses was slow, while in Japan and New Zealand rebuilding to modern codes was funded largely by the state and insurers.

## Risk perception

People stay in dangerous places because of fertile soils and jobs (volcano slopes), poverty and lack of alternatives, family ties, trust in scientists or religion, and the low frequency of events, which makes people underestimate the danger. Others do not move because earlier warnings proved false. A warning is effective only if people believe it, understand it and can act on it.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Warning time from an early warning system]
An earthquake occurs 84 km from a city. The system detects the P waves near the epicentre and issues an alert 9 seconds after the earthquake begins. S waves travel at 3.5 km/s.
1. S waves reach the city after $84 \div 3.5 = 24$ s.
2. Warning time $= 24 - 9 = 15$ s.
3. The warning time is short, but fifteen seconds is enough to stop trains and shut gas valves. For a city 28 km away the S waves arrive after 8 s, before the alert is issued: this is the "blind zone" close to the epicentre.
:::

:::worked-solution[Cost–benefit of strengthening buildings]
Retrofitting 200 schools in an earthquake zone costs US\$ 60 million. Without it, expected damage in a future earthquake is US\$ 150 million; with it, expected damage falls to US\$ 30 million (illustrative figures).
1. Damage avoided $= 150 - 30 = 120$ million US dollars.
2. Benefit-to-cost ratio $= 120 \div 60 = 2.0$.
3. The ratio exceeds 1 even before the lives saved are counted, so retrofitting is cost-effective; but the cost falls now and the benefit is uncertain and in the future, which is why governments often delay.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating the success of management: Pinatubo, 1991"}
Warning signs began in April 1991 with steam explosions, earthquakes and rising sulfur dioxide. Scientists raised the alert level step by step, drew hazard zones, and tens of thousands of people were evacuated before the eruption of 15 June. Around 800 people died, many when ash soaked by typhoon rain collapsed roofs. Success factors: early monitoring, clear communication, a trusted evacuation system and help from outside experts. Limits: the typhoon was not foreseen, lahars continued to destroy homes for years, and thousands of evacuees lived in poor camps for long periods.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying earthquakes can be predicted"}
No method gives the time, place and magnitude in advance. Say that forecasts give probabilities and that early warning gives seconds, not days.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing strategies without judging them"}
"Building codes reduce collapse" is only half an answer. Add when they work (enforced, affordable, applied to old buildings too) and where they fail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the hazard as the only cause of the disaster"}
Management mainly changes vulnerability and exposure. A well-prepared country can have a very large earthquake with few deaths (Chile, 2010).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 9). *Explain* how a technique works (precursors, P-wave detection, base isolation). *Assess* effectiveness by weighing success, cost, reliability, who can afford it and the example (Pinatubo, Japan, Chile, Haiti, Nepal). For *evaluate* essays include both earthquakes and volcanoes if the question asks about "hazards", compare countries with different incomes and end with a judgement on which strategies matter most.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Earthquakes cannot be predicted; forecasting gives probabilities and early warning gives seconds.
- Volcanic eruptions show precursors (earthquake swarms, ground deformation, gas), so warnings and evacuations can be effective, though timing and size remain uncertain.
- Design, codes, land-use planning, hazard maps and education reduce vulnerability, but only if they are enforced and affordable.
- Emergency response and recovery depend on wealth, preparation and governance.
- Perception of risk explains why people stay in danger zones or ignore warnings.
- Assess strategies by effectiveness, cost, reliability and equity, using contrasting examples.
:::
