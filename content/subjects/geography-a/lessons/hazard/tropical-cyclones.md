## Introduction

In March 2019 Cyclone Idai came ashore near Beira in Mozambique, flooded the Buzi and Pungwe river plains and then moved inland. Its rain triggered landslides and river floods in the Eastern Highlands of Zimbabwe, especially around Chimanimani, and in Malawi. More than a thousand deaths were recorded across the three countries, and thousands of homes, roads, bridges and crops were lost. Zimbabwe is a landlocked country, yet it is exposed to tropical cyclones because storms formed in the south-west Indian Ocean reach the Mozambique Channel and the coast of Mozambique.

This lesson explains why cyclones form, how they are measured and what they do. You should know the global circulation, the Coriolis effect and ocean currents.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tropical cyclone"}
A large, rotating, low-pressure storm system that forms over warm tropical oceans, with sustained winds of at least 119 km/h. It is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific and a tropical cyclone in the south-west Indian Ocean and around Australia.
:::

:::definition{term="Storm surge"}
A rise in sea level above the normal tide, caused by strong onshore winds piling water against the coast and by the very low pressure at the centre. It is usually the main cause of deaths on low-lying coasts.
:::

:::definition{term="Saffir-Simpson scale"}
A scale of categories 1 to 5 based on the maximum sustained wind speed. It measures wind only, not the surge or rainfall.
:::

## Conditions and formation

A tropical cyclone needs:

1. **A warm sea**: a surface temperature of at least about 26.5 °C through a depth of roughly 50 m, which supplies heat and moisture by evaporation.
2. **Distance from the equator**: usually between about 5° and 30° latitude, where the **Coriolis effect** is strong enough to make the air spin. It does not form on the equator.
3. **Low vertical wind shear**: winds of similar speed and direction at different heights, so the storm structure is not torn apart.
4. **Moist air** in the lower and middle troposphere, and an existing area of low pressure or disturbance to start the process.
5. **The right season**: late summer and autumn, when the sea is warmest. In the south-west Indian Ocean the season runs from about November to April.

Stages: (1) warm moist air rises over the ocean, forming low pressure at the surface; (2) air flows in from outside, picks up moisture and begins to rotate because of the Coriolis effect; (3) condensation of the rising air releases latent heat, which warms the air further and strengthens the updraught, so pressure falls and winds increase; (4) a calm **eye** forms with sinking air, surrounded by the **eyewall** of the strongest winds and heaviest rain; (5) the cyclone weakens when it moves over land (no moisture or heat supply, more friction) or over cool water.

## The Saffir-Simpson scale

| Category | Sustained wind (km/h) | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 119–153 | Damage to roofs, trees and power lines |
| 2 | 154–177 | Extensive roof and door damage; power cuts last days |
| 3 | 178–208 | Structural damage to small buildings; major hurricane |
| 4 | 209–251 | Severe damage; walls fail; areas uninhabitable for weeks |
| 5 | 252 or more | Catastrophic; many buildings destroyed |

## Hazards

- **High winds** destroy roofs and light buildings and carry debris.
- **Storm surge** floods low coasts and deltas; a surge of several metres on a gently sloping coast can reach far inland. Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines, November 2013) killed more than six thousand people, mostly by the surge, in a place where many people did not understand the warning word "surge".
- **Intense rainfall** causes river floods and flash floods, and after landfall it can fall as far as 300 km inland.
- **Landslides and mass movements** on saturated slopes (Chimanimani, 2019).
- **Coastal erosion** and damage to ecosystems such as mangroves and reefs.

## Impacts and vulnerability

Short-term impacts are deaths, injuries, destroyed homes, damaged roads, water and power supplies, disrupted communications, and disease risk after floods (cholera was reported in Beira within weeks of Idai). Long-term impacts are lost crops and livelihoods, debt, children out of school, displaced people, damaged ecosystems and the cost of rebuilding. Vulnerability is greater where settlements are on low coasts, flood plains and steep slopes; where housing is weak; where there is poverty, poor warning and weak roads; and where people have little experience of cyclones, as in inland areas.

## Prediction, warning and management

**Prediction.** Satellites, aircraft, radar and computer models forecast the track well (reasonably accurate to about two to three days) but are weaker in forecasting rapid changes in intensity. **Warnings** are issued as watches and warnings, with hazard maps and evacuation orders from national agencies (in Zimbabwe the Civil Protection Department works with the Meteorological Services Department). A warning only works if it reaches people, is understood and can be followed: radio, SMS, community leaders and flags are used.

**Management strategies:**

1. **Cyclone shelters and evacuation**: Bangladesh has thousands of raised multipurpose shelters and a Cyclone Preparedness Programme with trained volunteers. Deaths fell from about 300 000 in 1970 to about 3 400 in Cyclone Sidr (2007) and far fewer in later storms.
2. **Coastal defences**: embankments, sea walls and mangrove belts reduce the surge, though they can fail or be overtopped.
3. **Building design and land use**: strong roofs and raised foundations, and keeping settlements out of flood plains and surge zones.
4. **Education and drills**: community plans, and in Zimbabwe, disaster risk plans coordinated by the Civil Protection Department.
5. **Recovery**: aid, rebuilding, restoring livelihoods; recovery is slow and depends on funding.

**Climate change.** Warmer seas and a moister atmosphere are expected to raise rainfall rates (by roughly 7% per °C of warming) and to increase the proportion of the strongest storms (categories 4 and 5). Rising sea level raises surge heights. There is no clear evidence yet that the total number of storms is increasing.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Classifying cyclone strength and measuring its speed]
An illustrative track shows a cyclone at 14.5°S 51.0°E at 6 hours with winds of 140 km/h, and at 15.7°S 50.2°E at 18 hours with winds of 190 km/h.
1. At 6 hours: 140 km/h is between 119 and 153, so **category 1**. At 18 hours: 190 km/h is between 178 and 208, so **category 3**.
2. The storm intensified by two categories in 12 hours (rapid intensification), which is difficult to forecast.
3. Distance moved (great-circle distance between the two positions) $\approx 158.7$ km, so the speed of movement is $158.7 \div 12 = 13.2$ km/h.
:::

:::worked-solution[Effect of warming on rainfall rate]
A cyclone drops rain at 200 mm per day. Assume rainfall rates rise by 7% for each 1 °C of warming. What would the rate be after 2 °C of warming?
1. After 1 °C: $200 \times 1.07 = 214$ mm per day.
2. After 2 °C: $200 \times 1.07^2 = 229$ mm per day.
3. An increase of 29 mm per day, or 14.5%, which increases the risk of flash floods and landslides.
:::

:::example{title="Case study summary: Cyclone Idai (March 2019)"}
Idai re-intensified over the Mozambique Channel and made landfall near Beira on the night of 14 March. Hazards: wind, a surge in the Beira estuary, and very heavy rain that flooded the Buzi and Pungwe plains. In Zimbabwe, heavy rain on 15–16 March caused landslides and flash floods in Chimanimani and Chipinge districts. More than a thousand deaths (rounded) were recorded in Mozambique, Zimbabwe and Malawi together. Impacts: homes, roads and bridges washed away, crops lost, tens of thousands displaced and a cholera outbreak in Beira. Management: early warnings were issued but did not reach all people or convey the flood risk well; relief came from national and international agencies; reconstruction took years. Lesson: inland and upstream areas also need warnings.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cyclones form at the equator"}
The Coriolis effect is too weak close to the equator (within about 5°), so cyclones do not form there.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Saffir-Simpson scale as a measure of damage or deaths"}
It measures only the maximum sustained wind. A Category 1 storm can kill many people through surge or flooding.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that cyclones weaken inland"}
The storm loses its supply of warm moist air and has more friction, but the rain can still cause severe floods and landslides far inland, as in Zimbabwe.
:::

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (weather and climatic hazards) and Cambridge Paper 3 (option 9). *Explain* formation as a chain: warm sea, evaporation, rising air, Coriolis spin, latent heat, falling pressure. *Describe* a track or table with figures, direction and category. For *assess/evaluate* management, set strategies such as shelters, forecasts and defences against their cost, who benefits and whether people can act, and use named, dated examples (Idai 2019, Haiyan 2013, Bangladesh).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tropical cyclones need sea temperatures above about 26.5 °C, latitude of about 5° to 30°, low wind shear and moist air.
- They strengthen by latent heat from condensation and weaken over land or cold water.
- The Saffir-Simpson scale (categories 1 to 5, from 119 km/h) measures wind speed only.
- Hazards: wind, storm surge, intense rain, floods and landslides; storm surge and flooding cause most deaths.
- Warning, shelters, defences and education reduce deaths (Bangladesh), but vulnerable people may not be reached (Idai, Haiyan).
- Climate change is expected to increase rainfall rates, surge heights and the share of the strongest storms.
:::
