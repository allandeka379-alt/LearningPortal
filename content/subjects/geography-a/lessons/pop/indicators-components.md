## Introduction

A planner in the Ministry of Health must decide how many maternity wards, schools and clinics the next ten years will need. She cannot do it by looking at the total population alone: she needs to know how many babies are born, how many people die, how many arrive or leave, how many children each woman has and how many people depend on workers for support. Population indicators turn these questions into numbers that can be compared between countries and over time. In Zimbabwe they come from the census run by ZimStat every ten years (the latest in 2022) and from household surveys such as the Demographic and Health Survey.

You should already know that population changes by births, deaths and migration. This lesson defines each indicator, shows how to calculate it and explains why fertility and mortality change. Age structure and the demographic transition model are in the next lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Birth rate and death rate"}
The crude birth rate (CBR) is the number of live births per 1 000 people in a year. The crude death rate (CDR) is the number of deaths per 1 000 people in a year. They are called crude because they use the whole population, not just women of child-bearing age or a particular age group.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural increase and net migration"}
Natural increase (NI) is the difference between the birth rate and the death rate. Divide the per 1 000 figure by 10 to give a percentage. Net migration is immigrants minus emigrants; it can be given as a number or per 1 000 people. A negative figure means that more people leave than arrive.
:::

:::definition{term="Total fertility rate (TFR)"}
The average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime if she experienced the age-specific fertility rates of the year in question. About 2.1 children per woman is the replacement level in countries with low child mortality.
:::

:::definition{term="Infant mortality rate (IMR)"}
The number of deaths of children under one year old per 1 000 live births in a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Life expectancy"}
The average number of years a newborn would live if current death rates stayed the same throughout life. It is a measure of the whole population's health.
:::

:::definition{term="Dependency ratio"}
The number of dependants (children under 15 and adults aged 65 and over) for every 100 people of working age (15 to 64): $\dfrac{\text{under 15} + \text{65 and over}}{\text{15 to 64}} \times 100$.
:::

## Calculating the indicators

:::keyfact{title="Formulas to learn"}
- CBR = births ÷ total population × 1 000
- CDR = deaths ÷ total population × 1 000
- Natural increase (per 1 000) = CBR − CDR; as a percentage = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10
- IMR = infant deaths ÷ live births × 1 000
- TFR = sum of the age-specific fertility rates (per woman) over all age groups; with 5-year groups and rates per 1 000 women, TFR = 5 × the sum of the rates ÷ 1 000
- Total growth rate (%) = natural increase (%) + net migration (%)
:::

## Why fertility changes

Falling fertility is usually linked to these factors, while the opposite conditions keep fertility high.

1. **Education and work of women:** educated women marry later, choose smaller families and have jobs that compete with childcare.
2. **Access to contraception and family planning:** services and the freedom to use them lower the number of unplanned births.
3. **Infant mortality:** where many children die, parents have more to make sure some survive. When IMR falls, fertility follows later.
4. **Economic value of children:** in farming families children give labour and support in old age; in cities they are costly (school fees, housing).
5. **Urbanisation:** smaller homes, higher costs and more access to services reduce family size.
6. **Culture, religion and government policy:** beliefs about family size, early marriage and pro- or anti-natalist laws.
7. **HIV/AIDS:** can reduce births indirectly through illness and deaths of adults.

## Why mortality changes

Death rates fall with better health care and vaccination, safe water and sanitation, adequate food, education about hygiene, and peace. They rise with epidemics (cholera, HIV/AIDS, malaria), famine, war, poor health services and natural disasters. Death rates can also rise as a country ages, because a population with many older people has more deaths even when health is good. This is why the CDR of a rich ageing country can exceed that of a poorer young one. Life expectancy and IMR are better measures of health than the crude death rate because they are not affected by age structure in the same way.

In Zimbabwe, life expectancy fell sharply during the 1990s and early 2000s because of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, and then recovered as treatment and prevention expanded.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Rates for Country A]
Illustrative data for one year: population 1 200 000; live births 45 600; deaths 14 400; deaths under one year 2 280; immigrants 3 000; emigrants 7 800.

1. CBR = 45 600 ÷ 1 200 000 × 1 000 = 38 per 1 000.
2. CDR = 14 400 ÷ 1 200 000 × 1 000 = 12 per 1 000.
3. Natural increase = 38 − 12 = 26 per 1 000, which is 2.6%.
4. IMR = 2 280 ÷ 45 600 × 1 000 = 50 per 1 000 live births.
5. Net migration = 3 000 − 7 800 = −4 800, which is −4.0 per 1 000 (−0.4%).
6. Total growth rate = 2.6% − 0.4% = 2.2%. The country is growing because natural increase is much larger than the loss from migration.
:::

:::worked-solution[Dependency ratio and TFR]
Age groups in Country A (illustrative): under 15, 480 000; 15 to 64, 672 000; 65 and over, 48 000.

1. Dependants = 480 000 + 48 000 = 528 000.
2. Dependency ratio = 528 000 ÷ 672 000 × 100 = 78.6. For every 100 working-age people there are about 79 dependants, mostly children (a youth dependency ratio of about 71 compared with an old-age ratio of about 7).

Age-specific fertility rates (births per 1 000 women a year): 15–19: 60; 20–24: 170; 25–29: 190; 30–34: 150; 35–39: 90; 40–44: 30; 45–49: 10.

3. Sum of the rates = 700 per 1 000 women a year.
4. TFR = 5 × 700 ÷ 1 000 = 3.5 children per woman, above the replacement level of 2.1, so the population will keep growing unless fertility falls.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a comparison"}
Illustrative data: Country X has CBR 12, CDR 11, IMR 4 and life expectancy 82. Country Y has CBR 42, CDR 10, IMR 55 and life expectancy 58. Country Y's natural increase is 32 per 1 000 (3.2%) against 1 per 1 000 (0.1%) in Country X. Y's high birth rate and high IMR suggest limited health care and family planning, and its fast growth will put pressure on schools and jobs. X has an ageing population: its death rate is close to its birth rate even though health is excellent, because many people are old.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test these indicators, their calculation and the factors behind fertility and mortality, using published data. State the unit for every rate (per 1 000) and show the working. Published values for Zimbabwe come from ZimStat and must be quoted with their year.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to convert per 1 000 to per cent"}
A natural increase of 26 per 1 000 is 2.6%, not 26%. Divide by 10.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using total births for IMR"}
IMR is infant deaths per 1 000 live births, not per 1 000 people. Use the number of births as the base.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a high death rate always means poor health"}
The crude death rate also depends on age structure. An ageing population can have a higher CDR than a young one in a poorer country.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
These topics are tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 8) and in Cambridge Paper 2 (AS topic 4). *Calculate* with the formula, working and a unit; *describe* data by quoting the figures and the trend; *explain* changes in fertility or mortality with social, economic, environmental and political factors linked to the country; *compare* two countries using several indicators; *assess* how useful an indicator is by stating what it shows and what it hides.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- CBR, CDR and IMR are per 1 000; natural increase = CBR − CDR, divided by 10 for a percentage.
- Total growth = natural increase + net migration.
- TFR is the average number of children per woman; about 2.1 is replacement level.
- Dependency ratio = (under 15 + 65 and over) ÷ ages 15 to 64 × 100.
- Fertility falls with women's education, contraception, lower infant mortality and urbanisation; mortality falls with health care, water, food and peace.
:::
