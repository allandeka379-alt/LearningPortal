## Introduction

When Rudo leaves a village in Masvingo province to work as a nurse in another country, her decision has many consequences. Her family receives money, but the clinic she leaves is short of a trained nurse. The country she joins gains a skilled worker but may face questions about housing and services. Migration is therefore never only a movement of people: it moves skills, money, ideas and pressure between places. This lesson explains why people migrate, and then assesses the impacts on the places they leave, the places they go to and the migrants themselves. It ends with a detailed example of one country and the attempts to manage international migration.

You should already know the types of migration and the push-pull idea from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Push and pull factors"}
Push factors are conditions that drive people away from their home area (unemployment, drought, conflict). Pull factors are conditions that attract them to a destination (jobs, higher wages, schools, safety). Both are perceived by the migrant, who may have incomplete information.
:::

:::definition{term="Remittances"}
Money sent home by migrants to their families, usually through banks, money-transfer services or mobile money.
:::

:::definition{term="Obstacles (barriers) to migration"}
Factors that prevent or reduce movement, such as cost, distance, immigration laws, lack of skills or information, language and family ties.
:::

## Causes of migration

| Type of factor | Push (origin) | Pull (destination) |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Unemployment, low wages, small plots, crop failure | Jobs, higher wages, business opportunities |
| Environmental | Drought, floods and cyclones, soil degradation, land shortage | Reliable rainfall, fertile land, a safer environment |
| Social and cultural | Few schools and clinics, discrimination, family conflict | Better schools, health care, entertainment, relatives already there |
| Demographic | Rapid growth, a large youthful population, shortage of land | Labour shortages in an ageing country |
| Political | Conflict, persecution, unstable government, policies that restrict | Peace, safety, rights and welfare, or policies that attract workers |

Most decisions mix several factors. Push factors are strongest in forced migration; pull factors are stronger when people choose to move. Obstacles reduce movement: the cost of travel and documents, border controls, language, and attachment to family and land. This is why the poorest people may be unable to migrate, and why migrants often come from middle-income households.

## Impacts of migration

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Source area** | Remittances raise incomes and pay for food, school fees and housing; fewer people compete for jobs; skills and ideas return with migrants; reduced pressure on land and services | Loss of young, skilled and enterprising people (brain drain); an ageing population left behind; fewer workers to farm; family separation; dependence on remittances; lower tax base |
| **Destination area** | Workers fill labour shortages; migrants pay taxes and start businesses; cultural diversity; a younger population | Pressure on housing, schools, health services and transport; competition for jobs and lower wages in some sectors; informal settlements; social tension and, in some cases, xenophobia |
| **Migrants** | Higher income, better services, safety, education | Exploitation, poor working conditions, discrimination, loneliness, loss of status, risky journeys |

How big the impacts are depends on the number of migrants, their age, sex and skills, how long they stay, how much they send home, how quickly the destination can provide housing and jobs, and the policies of both governments. Where the migrants are mainly young men, the home area is left with women, children and older people.

## Detailed example: international migration to Qatar

Qatar, a small oil and gas producing state on the Arabian Peninsula, relies on migrant workers. Migrants make up the great majority of residents, roughly 85 to 90%, and most are men from South Asia and other parts of Asia and Africa who work in construction, services, hospitality and domestic work (rounded; to be verified against current official figures).

**Pull factors:** wealth from hydrocarbons, large building programmes (including for the 2022 FIFA World Cup), wages higher than at home, and a small national population that cannot supply the workforce.

**Impacts on Qatar:** a large labour force allowed rapid construction and economic growth; the population is strongly male and mainly young; the sponsorship (kafala) system tied workers to employers and was criticised for low pay, poor housing and limited freedom to change job; pressure on housing and services; limited citizenship rights and a social divide between nationals and migrants.

**Impacts on source countries:** remittances are important to the national income and household budgets of countries such as Nepal, the Philippines and Bangladesh.

**Management:** reforms in 2020 removed the need for an employer's permission to change jobs and to leave the country, and a non-discriminatory minimum wage was introduced in 2021. Critics say that enforcement is uneven, and some argue that reform came under international pressure around the World Cup. The case shows that migration can bring large economic gains but that benefits and costs are shared unequally, and that management depends on enforcing rules.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Remittances as a share of GDP]
Country E has 400 000 emigrants who each send home US\$1 500 a year on average; its GDP is US\$15 000 million (illustrative data).

1. Total remittances = 400 000 × US\$1 500 = US\$600 000 000 = US\$600 million.
2. Share of GDP = 600 ÷ 15 000 × 100 = 4%.
3. At 4% of GDP, remittances are a significant source of income, but they depend on migrants keeping their jobs and on the policies of the destination country.
:::

:::worked-solution[Emigration and the dependency ratio]
A district has 40 000 children, 10 000 people aged 65 and over and 50 000 adults of working age. Then 10 000 working-age adults emigrate (illustrative data).

1. Before: dependency ratio = (40 000 + 10 000) ÷ 50 000 × 100 = 100.
2. After: working-age adults = 40 000, so the ratio = 50 000 ÷ 40 000 × 100 = 125.
3. Emigration of working-age adults raises the dependency ratio, so the remaining workers support more dependants, unless remittances compensate.
:::

:::example{title="Assessing a source area"}
A village in a dry district loses many young men to work abroad. Positive: remittances pay school fees and build better houses, and fewer people compete for scarce land. Negative: fields are left uncultivated because of a shortage of labour, older people and women have more work, and children grow up with absent fathers. The overall effect depends on how much money is sent and whether it is invested in farming or business, not just spent on consumption.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect push and pull factors, obstacles and the impacts on source, destination and migrants. Cambridge also requires a detailed specific example of international migration on one country and an evaluation of attempts to manage it; any well-studied country with accurate facts is acceptable.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Listing push and pull factors without links"}
Write which factor acts at which place and how it affects the decision, for example "unemployment at home pushes young adults to move to a town with jobs".
:::

:::mistake{title="Seeing migration as all positive or all negative"}
Impacts differ between source, destination and migrants, and change over time. Give both sides and a judgement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring who gains and who loses"}
Sort impacts into economic, social, demographic and environmental, and say for whom they are good or bad.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 8) and Cambridge Paper 2 (AS topic 4), often as a 15-mark essay. *Explain* causes by linking factors to the decision; *describe* impacts on each group; *assess* or *to what extent* needs both sides, the size of the impact and a conclusion supported by a named example with dates and facts.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Push and pull factors may be economic, environmental, social, political and demographic; obstacles reduce movement.
- Source areas gain remittances and skills but lose young and skilled people.
- Destination areas gain workers but face pressure on housing and services and possible tension.
- Migrants gain income and services but may face exploitation and discrimination.
- Qatar illustrates large economic gains from migrant workers, unequal sharing of benefits, and reform of employment rules.
:::
