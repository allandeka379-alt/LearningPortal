## Introduction

Look at a night-time satellite picture of southern Africa and the lights cluster around Gauteng, the Zambezi and Limpopo valleys stay dark, and Zimbabwe shows a bright patch around Harare and a scatter of smaller points along the main roads. People are not spread evenly over the Earth. Where people live, how many there are and how fast their numbers change decide how much food, water, housing, energy and employment a place needs. This lesson explains the pattern (distribution and density), the trend (growth) and the argument about whether a place has too many, too few or just the right number of people for its resources.

You should already know the terms population, birth rate and death rate. The detailed rates are in the lesson on indicators, and age structure and the demographic transition model are in their own lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population distribution and density"}
Distribution is the pattern of where people live (spread out, clustered, along lines). Population density is the average number of people per unit area, usually people per km², found by dividing the population by the land area.
:::

:::definition{term="Population growth"}
The change in the number of people in a place, caused by natural change (births minus deaths) and by net migration (immigrants minus emigrants). Growth rate is expressed as a percentage per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Overpopulation, underpopulation and optimum population"}
Overpopulation: too many people for the resources and technology available, so living standards are lower than they could be. Underpopulation: too few people to make full use of the resources. Optimum population: the number that gives the highest standard of living with the resources and technology available. All three change when technology, trade or resources change.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The largest population that an area can support indefinitely without damaging the environment that sustains it.
:::

## Factors affecting distribution and density

People cluster where living is easy and productive and avoid places that are harsh. The influences are usually grouped as follows.

| Group | Dense where | Sparse where |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Fertile soils, reliable rainfall, flat or gently sloping land, fresh water, moderate temperatures | Deserts, steep mountains, thick forest, swamp, tsetse-fly infested valleys, very cold or very hot places |
| Economic | Mines, industry, ports and trade, commercial farming, many jobs | Few jobs, isolated, poor soils for farming |
| Social and cultural | Long-settled areas, family land, services such as schools and clinics | Places with few services; places avoided by tradition |
| Political and historical | Planned settlements, areas opened by land policy, safe areas | Conflict zones, areas cleared or restricted by law, such as national parks |

In Zimbabwe the 2022 census counted about 15.2 million people (ZimStat, rounded) on about 391 000 km², an average density of roughly 39 people per km². The average hides large differences. Density is highest in the cities (Harare, Bulawayo), in the communal lands of the Highveld and eastern districts, and around mining and farming centres, where rainfall is higher and more reliable (Natural Regions I to III). It is lowest in the Zambezi valley, the south-western and lowveld districts and the large protected areas such as Hwange, where rainfall is low and unreliable (Natural Regions IV and V), disease risk or wildlife rules limit settlement, and the soils are poorer for crops.

Globally, about half of all people live on a small fraction of the land: in East Asia, South Asia, Europe and the coasts and river valleys of Africa, with the largest sparse areas in the deserts, polar regions, high mountains and tropical forest. Density is not the same as pressure: the Netherlands is dense but wealthy, while a much lower density in a dry area can already be too high.

## Growth

The world's population passed 7 billion in 2011 and reached 8 billion in November 2022 (United Nations). It took all of human history to reach the first billion, and only about twelve years for each of the latest billions. The rate of growth peaked at about 2.1% a year in the 1960s and has fallen since, so the number still rises but more slowly. Growth is fastest in sub-Saharan Africa and slowest, or negative, in much of Europe and East Asia.

**Causes of rapid growth:** falling death rates (better medicine, vaccination, clean water, food supply) while birth rates remain high because of early marriage, low contraceptive use, the need for child labour, high infant mortality and cultural or religious values.

**Effects of rapid growth**

1. Pressure on food, water, land, energy and housing.
2. Pressure on schools, clinics and jobs, because a youthful population needs services before it can pay for them.
3. Environmental damage: deforestation, soil erosion, pollution.
4. Possible benefits: a large future workforce and market, a source of ideas and innovation if people are educated and employed.

ZIMSEC also asks how growth affects development. Where economic growth is faster than population growth, income per person rises; where it is slower, income per person falls and the country struggles to pay for services. Determinants of economic development include natural resources, capital, education and skills, technology, trade, political stability and good governance.

## Population and resources

Three ways of thinking about the balance:

- **Malthus (1798):** population grows geometrically and food arithmetically, so checks (famine, disease, war) must limit numbers.
- **Boserup (1965):** pressure of people stimulates innovation, so food production rises (intensive farming, new technology).
- **Optimum population** is a moving target: the same land can be overpopulated with simple tools and not overpopulated with irrigation, fertilisers and trade.

Signs of overpopulation: unemployment, hunger, shortage of water, pressure on land, environmental damage, migration out. Signs of underpopulation: unused resources, labour shortages, ageing workers, services that cannot reach their threshold. Carrying capacity is a similar idea for the environment, but it can be raised by technology or lowered by damage such as soil loss.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Population density of three districts]
The table gives illustrative data for three districts.

| District | Population | Area (km²) | Density (people per km²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 84 000 | 700 | 120 |
| Q | 18 200 | 1 400 | 13 |
| R | 260 000 | 650 | 400 |

1. Density = population ÷ area.
2. P: 84 000 ÷ 700 = 120 people per km².
3. Q: 18 200 ÷ 1 400 = 13 people per km².
4. R: 260 000 ÷ 650 = 400 people per km². R is more than thirty times as dense as Q; a likely reason is a town or mine with jobs and services, while Q may be dry, remote or partly a protected area.
:::

:::worked-solution[Growth, doubling time and projection]
A country has 14.0 million people and grows at 2.5% a year (illustrative data). Estimate the doubling time and the population after 10 years.

1. Doubling time by the rule of 70: 70 ÷ 2.5 = 28 years.
2. Population after 10 years = 14.0 × 1.025¹⁰ = 14.0 × 1.28 = 17.9 million (to 3 s.f.).
3. The rule of 70 is an estimate and assumes a constant rate; real rates change as fertility falls.
:::

:::example{title="Describing Zimbabwe's growth from census totals"}
ZimStat counted about 13.1 million people in 2012 and about 15.2 million in 2022 (rounded). The population rose by about 2.1 million, or 16%, in ten years, which is a compound rate of about 1.5% a year. This is slower than the 2.5% of the example above, so the doubling time would be nearly 50 years (70 ÷ 1.5). A description of data always quotes the figures with units and gives the trend, as here.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test distribution and density, growth trends, causes and effects of growth, and over- and underpopulation with optimum population and carrying capacity. ZIMSEC also names the effect of population growth on development and the determinants of economic development.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing density with distribution"}
Density is a number (people per km²); distribution is a pattern. Describe a pattern with places and relative size, and use a figure when density is given.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying overpopulation means a high density"}
Overpopulation is about the balance between people and resources and technology. A dense, rich country such as the Netherlands is not overpopulated, while a sparse dry area can be.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a falling growth rate as a falling population"}
A lower growth rate still adds people, just more slowly. The population falls only when the growth rate is below zero.
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 8) and Cambridge Paper 2 (AS topic 4) test this. *Describe* a distribution map or density table with places, a trend and figures; *explain* with physical, economic, social and political factors linked to the places named; *compare* growth between two countries with figures; *assess* overpopulation by weighing signs, technology and trade and giving a judgement. Show the formula and units for any density or growth calculation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Density is people per km²; distribution is the pattern of where people live.
- Physical, economic, social and political factors explain why people cluster in some places and avoid others.
- The world has about 8 billion people; growth peaked in the 1960s and is now slower, with the fastest growth in sub-Saharan Africa.
- Rapid growth is caused by falling death rates with high birth rates and creates pressure on resources and services.
- Optimum population and carrying capacity change with technology, trade and resource use, so over- and underpopulation are relative judgements.
:::
