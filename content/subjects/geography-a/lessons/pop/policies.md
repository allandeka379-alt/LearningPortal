## Introduction

Few decisions are more personal than how many children to have, yet governments have long tried to influence the answer. A country with fast growth may fear that it cannot feed, house or employ its people. A country with very low fertility may fear an ageing workforce and shrinking tax base. The policies they choose are called population policies. They show how geographical ideas about people and resources turn into laws, services and incentives, and they raise sensitive questions of rights and choice. This lesson explains the aims and types of policy, the ideas behind them, and how to judge their success using contrasting examples.

You should already know about birth rates, total fertility rate and the demographic transition model.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population policy"}
A set of measures by a government to influence the size, growth, structure or distribution of the population, usually by changing fertility, mortality or migration.
:::

:::definition{term="Anti-natalist and pro-natalist policies"}
Anti-natalist policies aim to reduce the birth rate (for example family planning programmes, limits on family size). Pro-natalist policies aim to increase the birth rate (for example cash payments, paid parental leave, tax relief and childcare).
:::

:::definition{term="Malthusian and Boserupian views"}
Malthus (1798) argued that population tends to outgrow food supply, so growth must be checked by famine, disease and war unless people limit their numbers. Boserup (1965) argued that growing numbers stimulate innovation and intensive farming, so food supply can increase with the population.
:::

## Why governments act

1. **Rapid growth:** pressure on food, water, land, schools, clinics and jobs, and on the environment, so governments try to slow growth.
2. **Low fertility and ageing:** too few young workers to support old people, falling school rolls, so governments try to raise births or encourage immigration.
3. **Health and human rights:** to improve the health of mothers and children through spacing of births.
4. **Economic and political goals:** to raise income per person, to maintain the size of a national group or military, or to reach development targets.

## Types of policy

| Aim | Examples of measures |
|---|---|
| Anti-natalist | Free or cheap family planning clinics and contraceptives; education of girls; raising the legal age of marriage; campaigns and slogans; tax or housing penalties for large families; direct limits on family size (China, 1979–2015) |
| Pro-natalist | Child benefit and baby bonuses; paid maternity and paternity leave; subsidised childcare; tax relief; flexible work; restrictions on contraception or abortion (some countries have used these, which are strongly criticised) |
| Migration policies | Skilled-worker visas and immigration targets to offset ageing; limits on emigration |
| Indirect | Health care for children (which reduces the need for large families); pensions; women's employment |

Policies succeed more easily when they work with changes already under way, such as rising education, falling infant mortality and urban living. Incentives usually work better than coercion and are seen as fairer.

## Contrasting examples

**China (anti-natalist, then relaxed).** In the 1970s campaigns to have children later, space them more widely and have fewer already lowered fertility. From about 1980 the one-child policy set strict limits in most urban areas, with some exceptions in rural areas and minority groups, and used incentives and penalties. The total fertility rate fell from about 2.7 in 1979 to below replacement level, and the government says that hundreds of millions of births were prevented, although part of the fall would have happened anyway as incomes and education rose. Consequences included rapid ageing, a shrinking workforce, a gender imbalance because of a traditional preference for sons, and many families with one child supporting two parents and four grandparents (the "4-2-1" problem). The policy was criticised for coercion in some places. It was replaced by a two-child policy in 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021, yet fertility has stayed low.

**Sweden (pro-natalist family policy).** Sweden supports parents with paid parental leave of about 16 months shared between both parents, subsidised childcare and child allowances, designed to combine work and family life. Fertility is higher than in many European countries, but it is still below replacement level and has fallen again in recent years, which shows that incentives are less powerful than other social changes.

**Zimbabwe (family planning).** After independence, the government supported family planning through the Zimbabwe National Family Planning Council, clinics and community-based distribution of contraceptives. Contraceptive use rose strongly and fertility fell from more than five children per woman in the 1980s to about four in the 2010s (ZimStat surveys, rounded), with the fall helped by education, urbanisation and the HIV epidemic. The policy relied on voluntary use and information, not on limits. Fertility remains above replacement level, so natural increase is still high.

## Evaluating success

Ask: Did fertility change in the intended direction? How much of the change was due to the policy? What were the social costs (ageing, gender imbalance, loss of rights)? Was the method acceptable? Could the policy be copied elsewhere? Judge success by comparing the aims with the results, using data from before and after, and remember that other factors act at the same time.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Births prevented]
Country S has 10 million people and a crude birth rate of 40 per 1 000 before a family planning programme and 28 per 1 000 afterwards (illustrative data; population assumed constant).

1. Births before = 10 000 000 × 40 ÷ 1 000 = 400 000 a year.
2. Births after = 10 000 000 × 28 ÷ 1 000 = 280 000 a year.
3. Fall = 400 000 − 280 000 = 120 000, which is 120 000 ÷ 400 000 × 100 = 30%.
4. This does not prove that the programme caused the fall; rising education or urbanisation may have contributed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Size of the next generation]
A country has a total fertility rate of 1.4 and replacement level is about 2.1 (migration ignored).

1. Each generation as a fraction of the last = 1.4 ÷ 2.1 = 0.667.
2. The next generation is about 67% of the size of the present one, a fall of about 33%.
3. After two generations it is 0.667 × 0.667 = 0.444, or about 44% of the starting size. A very low TFR therefore leads to a falling population unless immigration or higher fertility replaces it.
:::

:::example{title="Assessing a policy from data"}
Illustrative data for Country L: the total fertility rate fell from 2.9 in 1980 to 1.9 in 2000 and 1.3 in 2020; the share of people aged 65 and over rose from 5% to 8% and then 15%. The policy achieved its aim of lower fertility, since the TFR fell by 1.6 children per woman (55%) over forty years. However, the TFR is now well below replacement level and the share of older people has tripled, so the policy now has costs: pensions, health care and a shrinking workforce. A fair assessment states both the success and the cost, and notes that other factors also lowered fertility.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up pro-natalist and anti-natalist"}
Natalist refers to births. Pro-natalist raises births; anti-natalist lowers them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a policy caused all the change"}
Fertility also falls with education, urbanisation and lower infant mortality. Assess how much of the change a policy explains.
:::

:::mistake{title="Judging success only by the birth rate"}
A fair evaluation also considers ageing, gender balance, rights and costs.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Governments manage population to deal with rapid growth, low fertility or ageing.
- Anti-natalist policies reduce births; pro-natalist policies raise them; migration policies change numbers directly.
- Malthus feared growth outrunning food; Boserup saw pressure stimulating innovation.
- China's one-child policy lowered fertility but caused rapid ageing and a gender imbalance; Sweden's incentives have had limited effect on fertility; Zimbabwe relied on voluntary family planning.
- Success is judged against aims, other influences, costs and rights.
:::
