## Introduction

Two countries can have the same total population and face completely different futures. In one, four in every ten people are under 15 and schools, jobs and housing are the urgent problems. In the other, one person in four is over 65 and the pressure comes from pensions and hospitals. The difference lies in the **population structure**: how people are divided by age and sex. The **demographic transition model (DTM)** then links changes in structure to changes in birth and death rates as a country develops. This lesson shows how to read a population pyramid, how to describe each stage of the DTM, and how to judge how far the model helps.

You should already know how to calculate the birth rate, death rate and natural increase, from the lesson on indicators.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Age-sex (population) pyramid"}
A bar graph showing the percentage or number of males (left) and females (right) in each age group, with the youngest at the bottom. Its shape shows fertility, mortality and migration in the past.
:::

:::definition{term="Demographic transition model (DTM)"}
A model that describes how birth rates and death rates, and so natural increase and total population, change as a country develops. It has four stages, with a fifth stage often added.
:::

:::definition{term="Youthful and ageing population"}
A youthful population has a high share of children and young people (for example, over 40% under 15). An ageing population has a high and rising share of people aged 65 and over, and a falling share of children.
:::

## Reading a pyramid

| Feature | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Wide base, steep sides | High birth rate and high death rate at each age: a youthful, expanding population (typical of stage 2) |
| Base narrowing, sides less steep | Falling birth rate, falling death rate: stage 3 |
| Nearly straight sides, narrow base | Low birth and death rates: a stationary population (stage 4) |
| Wide top, narrow base | Very low birth rate and long life expectancy: an ageing and possibly shrinking population (stage 5) |
| A notch or bulge in one band | A past event: a baby boom, war, epidemic or migration |
| Fewer males than females at older ages | Women live longer; the gap between males and females grows with age |
| More males in the working-age bands | Male-dominated labour migration into the area |

When you describe a pyramid, give the shape, the share of young and old with figures, the dependency ratio, the sex balance and one sentence of interpretation.

## The demographic transition model

| Stage | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase | Total population | Main reasons |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 High stationary | High (about 35 to 45) | High and fluctuating | Low | Stable or very slow growth | No family planning, high infant mortality, famine and disease |
| 2 Early expanding | High | Falling rapidly | Rising to a peak | Rapid growth | Better food, clean water, vaccination and medicine; birth rates stay high |
| 3 Late expanding | Falling | Low and falling slowly | Falling | Growth slows | Contraception, women's education, urban living, lower infant mortality |
| 4 Low stationary | Low (about 10 to 15) | Low | Low or zero | Stable or slowly rising | Small families, good health care |
| 5 Declining (optional) | Below death rate | Low but rising with age | Negative | Falling without immigration | Very low fertility and an ageing population |

![Demographic transition model: birth and death rates are both high in stage 1; the death rate falls first in stage 2, so the gap (natural increase) widens; in stage 3 the birth rate falls and the gap narrows; in stage 4 both are low; in stage 5 the birth rate may fall below the death rate.](media:diagrams/demographic-transition-model.png "Figure: birth and death rates through the five stages of the demographic transition model (schematic)")

Stage 5 is not part of the original model and is sometimes called a "possible" stage. Many countries are in stage 4 or beyond (for example Germany and Japan), and low-income countries such as many in sub-Saharan Africa are in stage 2 or 3. Zimbabwe is often placed in the late part of stage 2 or the early part of stage 3, but the exact placement depends on which indicator is used.

**Usefulness of the DTM**

- It is simple and shows the link between development and population change.
- It helps planners expect changing needs: first schools, then jobs, then pensions.
- It allows countries to be compared and shows why growth is fastest in stage 2.

**Limitations**

- It was based on the history of Europe and may not fit other regions: death rates in the developing world fell much faster because medicine was imported, and the gap between death and birth rates became far larger than in Europe.
- It ignores migration, which can matter more than natural change in small countries and cities.
- It cannot account for events such as the HIV/AIDS epidemic, which raised death rates in southern Africa and delayed a country's progress.
- It assumes that all countries follow the same path, whereas government policies (such as China's) and war can change the timing.
- It does not predict what happens after stage 4: stage 5 is uncertain.

## Impacts of structure

**Youthful population:** pressure for schools, clinics and jobs, high youth dependency, strain on food and housing, and a possible "demographic dividend" if the large working-age group later finds work and the number of dependants falls. Environmental pressure may grow with population.

**Ageing population:** rising costs of pensions and health care, a shrinking workforce and tax base, labour shortages (often met by immigration), closure of schools, but also experience and skills, and savings and spending by older people (the "grey economy"). Politically, older voters may influence policy.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Dependency ratios from two pyramids]
Illustrative data, percentage of the population in each broad age group.

| Age group | Country M | Country N |
|---|---|---|
| Under 15 | 43 | 14 |
| 15 to 64 | 54 | 62 |
| 65 and over | 3 | 24 |

1. Country M: dependency ratio = (43 + 3) ÷ 54 × 100 = 85.2.
2. Country N: dependency ratio = (14 + 24) ÷ 62 × 100 = 61.3.
3. Both have a heavy burden on workers, but of different kinds: in M, children are 43% of the population, so schools are the main cost; in N, the old are 24%, so pensions and health care are.
4. The ratios alone do not show whether workers have jobs, so a high ratio does not by itself mean economic hardship.
:::

:::worked-solution[Placing a country on the DTM]
Illustrative birth and death rates (per 1 000) for Country Z.

| Year | Birth rate | Death rate | Natural increase (%) | Stage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1950 | 45 | 28 | 1.7 | Early stage 2 |
| 1980 | 44 | 14 | 3.0 | Stage 2 (peak growth) |
| 2010 | 30 | 8 | 2.2 | Stage 3 |

1. Natural increase = (birth rate − death rate) ÷ 10: 1950, (45 − 28) ÷ 10 = 1.7%; 1980, (44 − 14) ÷ 10 = 3.0%; 2010, (30 − 8) ÷ 10 = 2.2%.
2. The death rate fell from 28 to 14 between 1950 and 1980 while the birth rate hardly changed, so natural increase almost doubled. This is stage 2.
3. By 2010 the birth rate had fallen to 30 while the death rate was still falling, which is stage 3: growth is still fast but slowing.
4. At 3.0% the doubling time is about 70 ÷ 3.0 = 23 years.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a youthful pyramid"}
A country with 43% under 15, 54% aged 15 to 64 and 3% aged 65 and over has a very wide base and steep, concave sides that taper quickly. This is the shape of an expanding population with high fertility and high mortality at older ages. The dependency ratio is 85, mostly children. Few people reach old age, so the top is narrow, and women outnumber men slightly in the oldest group. Planners must provide schools and jobs for the large numbers entering the workforce.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a pyramid without figures"}
Write "43% are under 15" or "the 15 to 19 band is 9%" rather than "there are many young people".
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the DTM stage depends only on the birth rate"}
A stage is decided by both the birth rate and the death rate and the gap between them. Falling death rates start stage 2; falling birth rates start stage 3.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the DTM as a prediction"}
It is a generalised model. Countries differ in timing, and migration and policies can change the path.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A pyramid shows age and sex structure; its shape reflects past fertility, mortality and migration.
- Dependency ratio = (under 15 + 65 and over) ÷ ages 15 to 64 × 100.
- DTM stages: 1 high birth and death rates; 2 death rate falls; 3 birth rate falls; 4 both low; 5 (optional) birth rate below death rate.
- The DTM is simple and useful for planning but is based on Europe and ignores migration, disease and policy.
- Youthful populations strain schools and jobs; ageing ones strain pensions and health care and shrink the workforce.
:::
