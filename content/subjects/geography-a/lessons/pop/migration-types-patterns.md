## Introduction

Every morning in Harare, minibuses bring people in from Chitungwiza, Epworth and Ruwa; every few years families leave rural districts for towns; and every year thousands of Zimbabweans, Mozambicans, Malawians and Congolese cross borders to work, study, trade or find safety. Migration is the movement of people from one place to another to live, and it changes the size and structure of every population it touches. This lesson sets out the types of migration, the main trends and patterns (with southern Africa as the example), and the models geographers use to describe and predict flows. The causes and impacts follow in the next lesson.

You should already know the components of population change (births, deaths and migration).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Migration"}
A permanent or long-term change of residence. Immigration is entering a country or region; emigration is leaving it; net migration is immigrants minus emigrants.
:::

:::definition{term="Refugee, asylum seeker and internally displaced person"}
A refugee is a person outside their country of nationality who cannot or will not return because of a well-founded fear of persecution for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a social group or political opinion (1951 Refugee Convention). An asylum seeker has applied for protection and is waiting for a decision. An internally displaced person (IDP) has been forced to flee but remains inside their own country.
:::

:::definition{term="Voluntary and forced migration"}
Voluntary migrants choose to move, usually for work, education, family or a better life. Forced migrants are compelled to move by conflict, persecution, disaster or development projects such as dams.
:::

## Types of migration

| Basis | Types | Zimbabwean or southern African example |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary crossed | Internal (within a country) or international | Rural to urban movement to Harare, Bulawayo or Mutare; workers in South Africa |
| Choice | Voluntary or forced | Job seekers moving to Johannesburg; people displaced by Cyclone Idai in Chimanimani and Chipinge in 2019 |
| Time | Permanent, temporary, seasonal or circular | Seasonal farm workers; cross-border traders who move back and forth |
| Direction | Rural to urban (the commonest internal flow), urban to rural, urban to urban, rural to rural | Movement of families to new farms and resettlement areas |
| Skills | Skilled (professionals) and unskilled | Nurses and teachers moving abroad ("brain drain") |
| Legal status | Documented and undocumented (irregular) | Border crossings without permits |

## Trends and patterns

- Internal migration involves far more people than international migration, and the largest internal flow is rural to urban.
- About 280 million people, 3.6% of the world population, lived outside their country of birth in 2020 (United Nations, rounded). Most international migrants move to find work.
- Most movements are over short distances and between neighbouring countries, so intra-regional migration is large in Africa, Asia and Europe.
- Southern Africa has a long history of labour migration to South African mines and farms, drawing workers from Mozambique, Malawi, Lesotho and Zimbabwe. South Africa remains the main destination in the region, and Botswana, the United Kingdom and other countries are also destinations for Zimbabweans.
- Refugee movements follow conflict and persecution, with most refugees hosted by neighbouring countries. In Zimbabwe, Tongogara Refugee Camp in Chipinge district has hosted people from Mozambique and from the Great Lakes region.
- Climate and disaster displacement is rising, as with floods and cyclones.

## Models of migration

**Ravenstein's laws (1885)** in outline: most migrants move a short distance; migration proceeds step by step (village to town to city); every main flow produces a counter-flow; people from rural areas migrate more than urban people; long-distance migrants go to large centres; economic reasons dominate; and technology increases migration.

**Lee's push–pull model (1966):** at the origin there are push factors (-), at the destination pull factors (+), and many neutral ones. Between them lie **intervening obstacles** (distance, cost, immigration laws, borders, language) and **personal factors** (age, family, information, attitudes). A move happens only if the perceived advantage of the destination is large enough to overcome the obstacles.

**Gravity model:** the number of migrants between two places is proportional to the product of their populations and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them: $M \propto \dfrac{P_1 \times P_2}{d^2}$. Large and near places attract the most.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Gravity model]
A rural district R (population 50 000) sends migrants to three towns (illustrative data). Compare the relative attraction using population (in thousands) divided by distance squared.

| Town | Population (thousands) | Distance from R (km) | P ÷ d² | Share of attraction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 400 | 100 | 0.04 | 14.3% |
| B | 100 | 25 | 0.16 | 57.1% |
| C | 200 | 50 | 0.08 | 28.6% |

1. A: 400 ÷ 100² = 0.04. B: 100 ÷ 25² = 0.16. C: 200 ÷ 50² = 0.08.
2. Sum = 0.28. Share of B = 0.16 ÷ 0.28 = 57.1%.
3. Town B, the smallest, attracts the most because it is much nearer. Distance matters more than size because it is squared. In practice jobs, roads and networks also matter, so the model gives a first estimate only.
:::

:::worked-solution[Share of migrants in the world population]
About 280 million international migrants and a world population of about 7.8 billion in 2020 (United Nations, rounded).

1. 280 million ÷ 7 800 million = 0.0359.
2. 0.0359 × 100 = 3.6%, so about one person in 28 lived outside their country of birth.
3. This shows that most people stay in the country where they were born, and that internal migration involves many more people.
:::

:::example{title="Classifying five people"}
1. Tendai moves from a rural district to Gweru to work in a factory: internal, voluntary, rural to urban, permanent.
2. Nyasha works on a farm in South Africa each season and returns to Zimbabwe: international, voluntary, seasonal (circular).
3. A family flees fighting across a border and registers at a camp: forced, international, refugee.
4. A family loses its home to a cyclone and moves to a relative's village in the same province: forced, internal, an IDP.
5. A nurse accepts a job abroad: international, voluntary, skilled (brain drain).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing refugees with economic migrants"}
A refugee flees persecution or conflict. An economic migrant moves mainly for work or higher income. The reasons and legal protection differ.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying internal migration is not migration"}
Moving within a country, especially rural to urban, is migration and is far larger than international movement.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting obstacles in the push–pull model"}
Push and pull alone do not decide a move. Costs, distance, laws and personal factors act as intervening obstacles.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Migration can be internal or international, voluntary or forced, temporary or permanent.
- A refugee has fled persecution across a border; an asylum seeker awaits a decision; an IDP has fled within their country.
- Internal rural-urban migration is the largest flow; about 3.6% of the world's people live outside their birth country.
- Ravenstein's laws and the gravity model show that distance and size matter; Lee's model adds obstacles and personal factors.
- Southern Africa has long-established labour migration to South Africa and refugee movements from conflict areas.
:::
