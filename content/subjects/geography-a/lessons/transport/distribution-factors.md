## Introduction

Zimbabwe has no coastline, so almost everything it imports and exports travels by road or rail across a neighbour: fuel from the Mozambican port of Beira, goods from Durban through South Africa, and minerals to ports on both coasts. Inside the country, maize, tobacco, coal, minerals, passengers and fuel move along a network that follows the history, relief, population and economy of the country. This lesson describes the main transport systems, explains the patterns they form, and shows how transport affects development. You should already know the idea of a network, and the location of the main towns and mining areas of Zimbabwe.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transport network"}
A set of routes (links) joining places (nodes) such as towns, ports, mines and borders.
:::

:::definition{term="Radial pattern"}
A network in which routes spread out like the spokes of a wheel from a central place, such as a capital city.
:::

:::definition{term="Traffic count"}
A survey that records the number and type of vehicles passing a point in a period of time.
:::

:::definition{term="Corridor"}
A major transport route, with roads, rail and sometimes pipelines, joining an inland area to a port or to another region, such as the Beira Corridor.
:::

## Systems, distribution and purposes

| System | Distribution (in outline) | Main purposes |
|---|---|---|
| **Roads** | Main trunk roads join Harare to Bulawayo, Mutare, Masvingo and Beitbridge, Chirundu on the Zambian border, and Bulawayo to Victoria Falls; feeder and rural roads link growth points and communal areas | passenger buses and kombis, freight trucks, access to farms, mines and markets, regional trade |
| **Rail** (National Railways of Zimbabwe, NRZ) | Lines from Bulawayo to Victoria Falls, to Harare and on to Mutare and the port of Beira, to Beitbridge and South Africa, and to Plumtree and Botswana; branch lines to Hwange coal and the lowveld | bulk freight such as coal, minerals, fertiliser, grain and containers; some passenger services |
| **Air** | International airports at Harare, Bulawayo and Victoria Falls; smaller airports and airstrips at Kariba, Hwange, Buffalo Range and mines and lodges | tourists, business passengers, urgent and high-value freight, access to remote areas |
| **Water** | Lake Kariba and other large dams carry ferries, tour boats and fishing craft, for example between Kariba and Binga | passengers and goods to lakeside communities, tourism, fishing |
| **Pipeline** | A fuel pipeline from Beira to Feruka near Mutare | bulk movement of petroleum products |

Roads carry the largest share of freight and almost all passengers because they reach every town and farm and need little fixed infrastructure on the user's side. Rail is cheaper per tonne over long distances for heavy goods, but needs reliable locomotives and track. Air is fast but costly. Pipelines are efficient for fuel, but only between two fixed points.

## Factors governing network patterns

1. **Relief.** Rivers need bridges (such as at the Zambezi crossing at Chirundu), escarpments such as the Zambezi Escarpment and the Eastern Highlands need winding roads and are costly to build, and the flatter central watershed (the high plateau running from south-west to north-east) is easiest for routes.
2. **Population.** Routes are densest where people live: the Harare area, the Midlands, Bulawayo and the Mashonaland and Manicaland farming districts. Sparsely populated areas such as parts of the lowveld have few routes.
3. **Economic activity.** Routes follow mines (Hwange coal, Great Dyke minerals), commercial farms, the tobacco floors in Harare, tourism at Victoria Falls and industry in Harare and Bulawayo.
4. **History.** Rhodes's plan to link the Cape to the north led to a line from Mafeking (via Bulawayo) in 1897, a line from Beira reaching Mutare in 1898 and the Victoria Falls in 1904. They were built to serve mines, settler farms and trade with ports, so they are oriented to the coast rather than to rural communal areas.
5. **Politics and location.** Zimbabwe is landlocked, so access to ports depends on neighbours (Mozambique, South Africa, Botswana, Namibia, Zambia), on agreements and on regional corridors under SADC. Government policy decides investment in roads and rail, and past sanctions and economic problems reduced maintenance.

The result is a **radial** pattern from Harare and Bulawayo, with a main north–south spine from Beitbridge through Masvingo, Harare and on to Chirundu and with long links to the ports.

## Impact of transport on development

Good transport lowers the cost of inputs and goods, helps farmers sell produce, connects mines and industries to markets, supports tourism, and gives access to schools and clinics. Poor roads raise vehicle costs, delay deliveries, increase crop losses and accidents, and isolate rural areas. Improved routes can also bring pressure on land and increase imports, so the benefit depends on maintenance and on whether local people can use the route.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Analysing a traffic count]
A student counts vehicles passing a point on a trunk road for one hour at five times of the day (illustrative data).

| Hour | Cars | Buses and kombis | Trucks | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 06:00–07:00 | 120 | 80 | 60 | 260 |
| 08:00–09:00 | 210 | 140 | 90 | 440 |
| 12:00–13:00 | 180 | 100 | 120 | 400 |
| 16:00–17:00 | 240 | 160 | 100 | 500 |
| 19:00–20:00 | 100 | 60 | 60 | 220 |

1. Total counted: $260 + 440 + 400 + 500 + 220 = 1\,820$ vehicles in 5 hours.
2. Mean per hour: $1\,820 \div 5 = 364$ vehicles.
3. Peak hour: 16:00–17:00 with 500 vehicles.
4. Share of trucks: $(60 + 90 + 120 + 100 + 60) \div 1\,820 \times 100 = 430 \div 1\,820 \times 100 = 23.6\%$.
5. Rough estimate of traffic over 12 daytime hours: $364 \times 12 = 4\,368$ vehicles (an estimate, since only five hours were sampled).
:::

:::worked-solution[Cost saving of a rail link]
A mine sends 20 000 t of ore 400 km. Road costs US\$0.12 per tonne per km and rail costs US\$0.07 (illustrative data).

1. Road cost per tonne: $400 \times 0.12 = 48$, so US\$48.
2. Rail cost per tonne: $400 \times 0.07 = 28$, so US\$28.
3. Saving per tonne US\$20, and for the whole shipment $20 \times 20\,000 = 400\,000$, so US\$400 000.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a route map"}
A map extract shows a main road running north–south through a town, with a railway beside it, and a single tarred road branching east to a growth point. **Description:** one main linear route with a branch. **Explanation:** the main route connects the capital to the border and carries freight; the branch serves a smaller settlement. **Suggestion:** a count at the junction would show how much traffic uses the branch and whether it needs improvement.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing routes without place names"}
Name specific towns and routes, such as Harare–Beitbridge or Bulawayo–Victoria Falls, and give their purposes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that Zimbabwe is landlocked"}
Include the role of neighbouring countries and corridors when explaining freight routes and costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading one traffic count as the whole story"}
A count at one place and time is a sample. State that it must be repeated at different hours and days to estimate daily flow.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe's systems are road, rail, air, water and pipeline, each with a different distribution and purpose.
- Roads carry most freight and passengers; rail is best for heavy bulk goods; air is fast but costly.
- Patterns reflect relief, population, economic activity, colonial history and the country's landlocked position.
- The pattern is radial from Harare and Bulawayo with long links to ports.
- Transport affects development through cost, access to markets and services, but depends on maintenance.
- Traffic counts are samples that show flow, peak hours and the mix of vehicles.
:::
