## Introduction

Zimbabwe has no large natural lakes. The water that supplies Harare, Bulawayo and Mutare comes from rivers that have been dammed, from reservoirs such as Lake Chivero and Lake Kariba, and from underground water reached by boreholes. In a country with a long dry season and strongly seasonal rain, these stores are essential. Most countries rely on a mix of such resources, but the amount available, and how well people can reach and use it, differs widely around the world. This lesson describes where the water is, how people capture and use it, how they change the water cycle and what water stress and scarcity mean.

You should know the water cycle and the drainage basin as a system. The regional factors that decide supply, and the management of water, are in the next two lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water resource"}
A source of water that is useful, or could be useful, to people: a store of fresh water that can be reached, abstracted and used for drinking, farming, industry, energy or ecosystems.
:::

:::definition{term="Water stress and water scarcity"}
**Water stress** occurs when the supply of water per person is under about 1 700 m³ per year; **water scarcity** when it is under about 1 000 m³ per person per year; and absolute scarcity below about 500 m³. These thresholds are widely used (the Falkenmark indicator) and are only a guide, because they use national averages.
:::

:::definition{term="Physical and economic scarcity"}
**Physical scarcity** is a lack of water resources to meet demand, as in arid regions or where withdrawals are too high. **Economic scarcity** is a lack of the money, infrastructure and institutions to capture, treat and deliver water that is available; it occurs even where water is plentiful.
:::

## Types of water resource

| Resource | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Oceans** | About 97% of all water, but salt water; needs desalination, which is costly in energy |
| **Ice caps and glaciers** | About two thirds of fresh water, but mostly in Antarctica and Greenland, far from people; glacier melt feeds some rivers |
| **Groundwater (aquifers)** | Almost 30% of fresh water; stored in permeable rock; reached by wells and boreholes; some is renewed slowly (fossil water is not) |
| **Rivers** | A very small share of fresh water, but the main source for many cities; flow varies with the season |
| **Lakes and reservoirs** | Natural lakes and man-made reservoirs behind dams; reservoirs capture seasonal flow |
| **Precipitation and soil moisture** | Rainfall is the input to every other store; rainwater harvesting captures it directly |
| **Recycled water** | Treated waste water reused for industry, irrigation or drinking |

Only about 2.5% of the world's water is fresh, and only a very small part (rivers, lakes, shallow groundwater) is easy to reach. Supply is also uneven: countries and regions with high rainfall and large rivers, such as parts of the humid tropics and high latitudes, have more water per person than the arid regions, such as North Africa, the Middle East and much of Southern Africa. Many large populations live where the water per person is low.

## The human water cycle

People add a stage to the natural cycle: **capture, use, disposal and reuse**.

1. **Capture.** Abstraction from rivers and aquifers, storage in reservoirs and tanks, rainwater harvesting and desalination.
2. **Treatment and supply.** Purification and distribution through pipes, tankers or standpipes.
3. **Use.** Agriculture (irrigation, about 70% of global withdrawals), industry (cooling, processing, mining), households (drinking, cooking, washing, sanitation) and energy (hydropower, cooling).
4. **Disposal.** Waste water goes to sewers, septic tanks or directly to rivers; treated or untreated, it may pollute.
5. **Reuse.** Treated waste water or grey water is used again, for example for irrigation or industry.

Some uses are **consumptive** (the water evaporates or is incorporated, as in irrigation) and others **non-consumptive** (water returns to the river, as in hydropower or cooling), though its quality may change.

## How people modify the water cycle

| Activity | Effects on the cycle |
|---|---|
| **Agriculture and irrigation** | Large abstraction from rivers and aquifers; evaporation from fields and canals; return flow carries salts and fertilisers; drainage and compaction change runoff |
| **Afforestation** | More interception and transpiration, less runoff and sometimes lower river flow; more infiltration and steadier flow |
| **Deforestation** | Less interception and transpiration, more runoff and erosion, shorter lag times and a higher flood risk; lower infiltration and dry-season flow |
| **Urbanisation** | Impermeable surfaces and drains increase runoff and reduce infiltration and groundwater recharge; leaks may add recharge; pollution |
| **Industry** | Abstraction, use for cooling and processing, and disposal of waste water; thermal and chemical pollution |
| **Abstraction** | Over-pumping lowers water tables, reduces river flow and can cause saltwater intrusion and land subsidence |
| **Flood control and dams** | Reservoirs store water, reduce peaks and increase dry-season flow below the dam; evaporation from reservoirs and trapped sediment are costs |

:::keyfact{title="Learn the direction"}
Impermeable surfaces and deforestation: more runoff, less infiltration. Irrigation and abstraction: less water left in the river. Dams: lower peaks and a steadier flow. Afforestation: more interception and transpiration.
:::

## Water stress, scarcity and development

Consumption rises with income and development: richer households have more taps and appliances, and industrial and energy use grows. Poorer countries use less water per person but may have more people without safe water, because of economic scarcity: they lack dams, treatment works and pipes. Physical scarcity is greatest in arid regions and in places where population growth and irrigation have pushed withdrawals close to the renewable supply. Climate change is expected to alter rainfall and increase evaporation, which will add to stress in some regions. Remember that national averages hide inequality: a country may be above the threshold while one province, or one poor suburb, has little water.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Water available per person]
A country has renewable water resources of 14.25 billion m³ per year and a population of 9.5 million (illustrative data). Calculate the water available per person per year and say whether it is under stress.

1. $14.25 \times 10^{9} \div 9.5 \times 10^{6} = 1\,500$ m³ per person per year.
2. This is below 1 700 m³ (stress) but above 1 000 m³ (scarcity), so the country is **water-stressed**.
3. If the population grows to 12.5 million with the same resources: $14.25 \times 10^{9} \div 12.5 \times 10^{6} = 1\,140$ m³ per person. The country is still stressed and closer to scarcity.
:::

:::worked-solution[Shares of water use]
The table gives the annual water withdrawals of a country by sector (illustrative data). Calculate the percentage used by each sector.

| Sector | Withdrawal (billion m³ per year) | Share (%) |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | 5.6 | 70 |
| Industry | 1.6 | 20 |
| Domestic | 0.8 | 10 |
| **Total** | **8.0** | **100** |

1. Total $= 5.6 + 1.6 + 0.8 = 8.0$ billion m³.
2. Agriculture $= 5.6 \div 8.0 \times 100 = 70\%$; industry $= 20\%$; domestic $= 10\%$.
3. Irrigated farming dominates withdrawals, so improving irrigation efficiency would save most water.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a global pattern"}
A world map shows water availability per person by country (described in words). **Describe:** values are highest in the humid tropics and cold high latitudes with large rivers and low population density, and lowest across North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula and parts of Central Asia and Southern Africa. **Explain:** low rainfall and high evaporation limit supply in arid regions, while dense populations divide a given supply among more people. **Add economic scarcity:** several countries in sub-Saharan Africa have enough water on average but lack the money to store and distribute it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using water stress and scarcity as the same thing"}
Stress is below about 1 700 m³ per person per year, scarcity below about 1 000 m³, and economic scarcity is a lack of access even when supply exists.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that most fresh water is not accessible"}
Most fresh water is in ice caps and deep aquifers. Only a small share is easily abstracted.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying afforestation always increases river flow"}
Trees intercept and transpire water and reduce runoff, so river flow may fall; they also increase infiltration and slow runoff, so the effect depends on place.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- About 97% of water is salt water; about 2.5% is fresh, mostly in ice and groundwater; little is easily accessible.
- The human water cycle adds capture, treatment, use, disposal and reuse to the natural cycle.
- Agriculture, afforestation and deforestation, urbanisation, industry, abstraction and dams all modify the cycle.
- Water stress is below about 1 700 m³ per person per year and scarcity below about 1 000 m³.
- Physical scarcity is a lack of water; economic scarcity is a lack of money and infrastructure to use it.
- Use rises with development; national averages hide inequality within countries.
:::
