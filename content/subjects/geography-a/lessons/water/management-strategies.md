## Introduction

Lake Kariba, created when the Zambezi was dammed in the 1950s, supplies electricity to Zimbabwe and Zambia, supports fishing and tourism, and also showed the costs of large water schemes: tens of thousands of Tonga people had to move, and the lake's level now affects power supply in dry years. Managing water means balancing four goals: enough water, safe water, fair access and a healthy environment. Governments can either find more water, or use the water they have more wisely, and in practice they do both.

You should know the factors of supply and demand and the human water cycle from the earlier lessons. This lesson compares the strategies, the problems that arise and one detailed example.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water management"}
Planning and controlling the capture, treatment, distribution, use and disposal of water so that needs for people, industry, farming and the environment are met now and in the future.
:::

:::definition{term="Supply-side and demand-side management"}
**Supply-side** strategies raise the amount of water available or the number of people who can reach it (dams, transfers, desalination, harvesting). **Demand-side** strategies reduce the amount people use (pricing, rationing, efficient technology, education).
:::

:::definition{term="Transboundary river"}
A river that crosses or forms the boundary between two or more countries. Its water must be shared, which needs agreements and cooperation. The Zambezi and the Limpopo are examples in southern Africa.
:::

## Increasing supply and access

| Strategy | How it works | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** | Store wet-season flow; give supply, flood control, power and irrigation | Large, reliable volume; **multipurpose schemes** such as Kariba share the cost across several uses | Very costly; flood land and displace people; silting; evaporation; changes the river below |
| **Water transfers** | Pipes, tunnels and canals move water from a surplus to a deficit basin | Serve big cities far from sources | Very costly, energy use, impacts on the donor area, disputes |
| **Desalination** | Distillation or reverse osmosis turns sea or brackish water into fresh water | Almost unlimited source on coasts; independent of rainfall | High energy cost and carbon emissions; brine disposal; only for rich areas |
| **Rainwater harvesting** | Roofs and tanks, or small earth dams, capture rain | Cheap, local, easy to maintain | Only in the rains; small volumes; needs storage |
| **Grey-water and waste-water reuse** | Treated bath, wash and waste water is reused for gardens, industry or drinking | Reduces demand for new water | Treatment cost; health and acceptance concerns |
| **Low-technology supplies** | Protected springs, hand-dug wells, boreholes with hand pumps, sand dams | Cheap, local, easy to maintain by communities | Low yields; quality risks; breakdowns |

## Managing demand

- **Pricing and metering:** charging by volume (often with low prices for a basic amount and higher prices for heavy use) discourages waste.
- **Rationing and restrictions:** limits on garden watering and time-limited supplies; they work quickly but are unpopular.
- **Efficient technology:** repairing leaks, low-flow taps and toilets, drip irrigation and efficient industrial cooling.
- **Education and awareness:** campaigns to change behaviour.

Demand-side measures are usually cheaper than new supply and quicker, but they require enforcement and public cooperation.

## Problems of water management

- **Variability in volume and quality.** Rainfall and river flows vary between seasons and years, so planners must allow for drought; quality varies with pollution and floods.
- **Abstraction rates.** Taking more than the renewable supply lowers water tables and river flow.
- **Saltwater intrusion.** Pumping groundwater near the coast draws in sea water and makes wells salty.
- **Ground subsidence.** Heavy pumping from some aquifers makes the pore spaces collapse, so the land sinks, damaging buildings and pipes.
- **Conflicts and transboundary issues.** Upstream users or countries may take water that downstream users need; agreements on sharing, joint commissions and monitoring are needed.
- **Pollution.** Sewage, industry, mines and farm chemicals make water unsafe and raise the cost of treatment.

## A detailed example: the Lesotho Highlands Water Project (Lesotho and South Africa)

**What:** a transfer and multipurpose scheme that captures water from the highlands of Lesotho and carries it by tunnel to the Vaal river system, which supplies Gauteng, South Africa's main urban and industrial region. Katse Dam was completed in 1998 and Mohale Dam in 2004; a second phase, including the Polihali Dam, has been under construction since the 2020s. A hydropower station in Lesotho uses the water on its way.

**Why:** Gauteng has a large and growing demand but few large rivers of its own, while the Lesotho highlands have high rainfall and steep valleys suited to storage.

**Success:** it secures a large share of the water supply for South Africa's major economic region; Lesotho receives royalties and generates much of its own electricity; the scheme is governed by a bilateral treaty and a joint commission, an example of cooperation over a transboundary resource.

**Problems:** the cost was very high; villages and farmland were flooded or affected and compensation was criticised; changes in river flow harmed downstream ecosystems; the scheme did not at first bring clean water to many people in the Lesotho highlands; and the water price and royalties remain debated.

**Judgement:** a success in security of supply for a distant city and in showing cooperation, but with large social and environmental costs, and less benefit for people near the dams than for people in Gauteng.

A shorter example of demand management comes from Cape Town, South Africa, during the severe drought of 2015-2018, when the city's dam levels fell towards a level at which the taps would be turned off. Strict restrictions, higher tariffs, pressure management to cut leaks, and public campaigns cut the city's use by about half, and the worst was avoided as rain returned. This shows how quickly demand can fall when people are informed and charged.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Saving water with drip irrigation]
A farm irrigates 200 ha. Flood irrigation uses 10 000 m³ per hectare per year; drip irrigation uses 5 500 m³ per hectare per year (illustrative data). Calculate the annual saving, in m³ and as a percentage.

1. Saving per hectare $= 10\,000 - 5\,500 = 4\,500$ m³.
2. Total saving $= 4\,500 \times 200 = 900\,000$ m³ per year.
3. Percentage saving $= 4\,500 \div 10\,000 \times 100 = 45\%$.
4. The scheme saves almost half the irrigation water, which could supply other users, but the cost of the equipment must be set against it.
:::

:::worked-solution[Rainwater harvesting]
A school roof has an area of 120 m². The average rainfall is 600 mm per year, and 80% of the rain falling on the roof is collected (illustrative data). Calculate the volume collected per year.

1. Rainfall depth $= 0.600$ m.
2. Volume of rain on the roof $= 120 \times 0.600 = 72$ m³.
3. Volume collected $= 72 \times 0.80 = 57.6$ m³ per year, or 57 600 litres.
4. At 20 litres per person per day this is enough for only about 8 people for a year (57 600 ÷ 365 ÷ 20), so harvesting suits small needs and is best combined with other sources.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a water-transfer scheme"}
A pipeline brings water 200 km to a city (illustrative). **Describe benefits:** a secure supply for a million people and industries, and fewer shortages. **Describe costs:** high cost of construction and pumping energy; land crossed by the pipe; the donor river has less flow; and conflict with farmers in the donor region. **Judge:** success depends on whether the water price, the compensation and the environmental flow are fair and whether demand management has been tried first.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating dams as always positive"}
Dams also flood land, displace people, silt up, evaporate water and alter ecosystems. Give both sides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming an example without facts"}
A detailed example needs the place, the dates, the strategies, who gained and who lost, and a judgement. A general statement of "dams help" gains little.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting demand management"}
Supply-side schemes are often more costly than reducing waste. A balanced answer discusses both.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 2 (topic 5; Section A resources and the Section B essay). *Describe* a strategy from a diagram or table. *Explain* how it increases supply or reduces demand. *Assess* or *to what extent* a scheme was successful: give benefits and costs for different groups, use specific facts from your example and reach a clear, supported judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply-side strategies: dams and multipurpose schemes, transfers, desalination, rainwater harvesting, grey-water reuse and low-tech supplies.
- Demand-side strategies: pricing and metering, rationing, efficient technology and education.
- Problems include variable volume and quality, over-abstraction, saltwater intrusion, subsidence, conflict and pollution.
- Transboundary rivers need agreements and cooperation.
- The Lesotho Highlands Water Project shows a transfer that secures supply for Gauteng but has social and environmental costs.
- Judge a scheme by cost, effectiveness, who gains and loses, sustainability and the alternatives.
:::
