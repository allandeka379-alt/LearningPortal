## Introduction

When the taps run dry in a Zimbabwean town, the reasons are rarely simple. The rains may have failed and the dams may be low; but the pipes may leak, the treatment works may be old and the demand of a growing population may have outrun the supply. A household in a dry-season queue and a household with a metered tap in the same town experience the same drought very differently. Supply, demand and the ability to get a reliable, safe share together decide a community's **water security**.

You should know the water resource types and the human water cycle from the previous lesson. This lesson explains what controls supply and demand and what happens when they are out of balance. It does not cover management strategies, which come next.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water security"}
The reliable availability of enough water of acceptable quality, at an affordable cost, for health, livelihoods and ecosystems. **Water insecurity** is the lack of this, whether because of too little water, poor quality or unequal access.
:::

:::definition{term="Water supply and water demand"}
**Supply** is the amount of water that can be abstracted, stored and delivered. **Demand** is the amount people want or use for domestic, farming, industrial and environmental needs. A **deficit** results when demand exceeds supply.
:::

:::definition{term="Groundwater and aquifer"}
**Groundwater** is water stored below the water table in the pores and fractures of rock. An **aquifer** is a rock that stores enough water and allows it to flow to wells and springs.
:::

## Factors affecting supply

**Physical factors**

- **Climate.** The amount, seasonality and reliability of rainfall, and the rate of evaporation. Zimbabwe's rain falls mostly in about five months, with a high risk of drought and high evaporation from open water.
- **Geology.** Permeable rocks (sandstone, limestone, sands) store and yield more water than impermeable rocks. Much of Zimbabwe is underlain by crystalline rocks such as granite and gneiss; these store water only in the weathered zone and in fractures, so yields are often low and vary from place to place.
- **Drainage.** The size, density and flow of rivers and the presence of lakes, wetlands and flood-plains that store water. Streams on impermeable rocks have high runoff but dry up in the dry season.
- **Relief.** Highlands receive more rain and feed rivers; steep slopes give fast runoff and fewer chances for storage; flat basins allow reservoirs and wetlands.

**Human factors**

- **Storage.** Dams and tanks capture the seasonal flow and carry it into the dry season; reservoirs lose water by evaporation and silt up.
- **Transfers.** Pipelines and canals move water from a surplus basin to a deficit area, at a high cost and sometimes with conflict.
- **Abstraction.** Pumping from rivers and aquifers; over-abstraction lowers water tables and river flow.
- **Desalination and treatment.** Desalination converts sea or saline water into fresh water, but it uses much energy. Treatment makes polluted water safe.
- **Infrastructure.** Intake works, pipes, pumps and power. Leaking pipes can waste a large share of treated water, and power cuts stop pumping.
- **Pollution and land use** reduce the usable supply: sewage in Lake Chivero has raised the cost of treating Harare's water.

## Factors affecting demand

- **Population size and growth,** especially urban growth, which adds households that need piped water.
- **Income and standard of living.** Higher incomes mean more taps, appliances, gardens and swimming pools; use per person rises.
- **Economic structure.** Irrigated farming, mining and manufacturing need large volumes; a service economy needs less.
- **Seasonal demand.** Demand peaks in the hot dry months for garden watering, irrigation and livestock, just when supply is lowest, so shortages follow.
- **Price, metering and technology.** Cheap or free water encourages waste; efficient appliances and drip irrigation reduce demand.

## Water security and insecurity

Security has four parts: **availability** (enough water), **access** (people can reach and afford it), **quality** (safe to drink) and **reliability** (it flows when needed, including in drought). Globally, the pattern of insecurity follows physical scarcity in arid regions (North Africa, the Middle East, parts of southern Africa and South Asia) and economic scarcity in poorer regions where many people lack safe piped water. Within a country the poor, rural people and those in informal settlements are most insecure.

**Access issues:** long walking distances, which fall on women and girls and keep girls from school; queues and rationing; high prices from vendors; shared standpipes that break down; unsafe sources that cause diarrhoeal disease, including cholera outbreaks; and conflicts between users or between countries sharing a river.

**Impacts of drought:**

- *People:* crop failure and hunger, loss of livestock, higher food prices, loss of income, rationing, poor hygiene and disease, forced migration.
- *Environment:* falling river and lake levels, loss of wetlands and wildlife, die-back of vegetation, more soil erosion and bush fires, lower groundwater.
- *Economy:* lower hydropower output (Lake Kariba levels have affected electricity generation), reduced industrial output and irrigation, and government spending on relief.

## ZIMSEC detail: groundwater and local knowledge

**Occurrence.** Groundwater occurs in the weathered zone (regolith) above fresh crystalline rock and in fractures and faults; in sandstones and Kalahari sands in the west it is stored in pores, so yields can be larger. **Quantity** depends on rainfall recharge, the rock and the depth; some aquifers recharge only in wet years. **Quality** can be affected by high mineral content (hardness, iron, fluoride in some places), by pollution from pit latrines, farms and mines, and by saltwater intrusion near coasts. **Boreholes** are drilled and fitted with a hand pump or motor; shallow wells are dug by hand. Sustainable use means abstracting no more than recharge, spacing boreholes, protecting recharge areas, siting boreholes away from latrines, and monitoring water levels.

**Indigenous knowledge.** Many communities protect springs, wetlands and headwater forests with traditional rules and taboos, dig shallow wells in dry sand riverbeds and know which places hold water in a drought. This knowledge can be combined with scientific survey.

**Afforestation of catchments.** Planting and protecting trees on slopes and headwaters reduces erosion, improves infiltration and recharge and slows runoff, though trees also use water, so the species and sites must be chosen with care.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Supply deficit]
A town of 500 000 people uses 120 litres per person per day. The water works can deliver 52 000 m³ per day (illustrative data). Calculate the demand and the deficit as a percentage of demand.

1. Demand $= 500\,000 \times 120 \div 1\,000 = 60\,000$ m³ per day (1 000 litres = 1 m³).
2. Deficit $= 60\,000 - 52\,000 = 8\,000$ m³ per day.
3. Percentage deficit $= 8\,000 \div 60\,000 \times 100 = 13.3\%$.
4. The town needs to cut demand by about a seventh or find more supply; seasonal peaks will make the shortage worse.
:::

:::worked-solution[Sustainable use of a groundwater catchment]
A groundwater catchment of 40 km² receives an average recharge of 60 mm per year. Boreholes abstract 3.0 million m³ per year (illustrative data). Is the abstraction sustainable?

1. Recharge volume $= 40 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^2 \times 0.060\ \text{m} = 2.4 \times 10^{6}$ m³ per year.
2. Abstraction exceeds recharge by $3.0 - 2.4 = 0.6$ million m³, or $0.6 \div 2.4 = 25\%$ more.
3. The abstraction is **not sustainable**: the water table will fall, boreholes may run dry and quality may worsen, unless abstraction is cut or recharge increased.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining a town's shortage"}
A town's reservoirs are at 20% in October (illustrative). **Describe:** rationing is in force and households receive water on two days a week. **Explain supply:** the rains in the previous season were below average; evaporation is high in the hot months; the town depends on one river, and the pipes lose a large share of the water by leakage. **Explain demand:** the population has grown, and demand for gardens and cooling rises in the dry season. **Impacts:** poor households buy water from vendors, hygiene falls and the risk of disease rises. Link each point to supply or demand.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating drought as the only cause of shortage"}
Shortages also result from leaks, pollution, poor management, rising demand and lack of money. Separate physical causes from human causes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying groundwater is unlimited"}
Aquifers recharge slowly and some store fossil water. Abstraction beyond recharge lowers the water table.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing supply factors with demand factors"}
Population growth and income raise demand; geology, storage and infrastructure affect supply.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Supply depends on climate, geology, drainage and relief (physical) and on storage, transfers, abstraction, desalination, treatment and infrastructure (human).
- Demand rises with population, income, economic structure and seasonal peaks.
- Water security means reliable access to enough, safe, affordable water; insecurity affects the poor most.
- Drought affects people, the environment and the economy (food, health, hydropower).
- Groundwater occurs in the weathered zone and fractures of crystalline rock and in porous rocks; boreholes must not abstract more than recharge.
- Indigenous knowledge and afforestation of catchments support sustainable supply.
:::
