## Introduction

When the power goes off in Harare, people reach for candles, charcoal braziers and solar lamps. When the price of oil rises on the world market, the cost of fuel at a Beitbridge filling station rises within weeks. Energy lies behind lighting, cooking, transport, farming and industry, yet the world's supplies are unevenly spread and the sources people choose have very different costs to the environment. This lesson classifies energy sources, describes where the major fuels are found, and explains how production and consumption are changing. You should know what fossil fuels are and the difference between a renewable and a finite resource.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Renewable energy"}
Energy from sources that are replaced naturally on a human timescale, such as the Sun, wind, flowing and tidal water, the Earth's heat and plant material grown and used sustainably.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-renewable energy"}
Energy from finite stocks that are used far faster than they form: coal, oil, natural gas and nuclear fuel (uranium).
:::

:::definition{term="Energy mix"}
The proportion of a country's or the world's energy that comes from each source.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy security"}
Having reliable access to enough energy at an affordable price, now and in future.
:::

## Types of energy and their issues

| Source | Main use | Advantages | Issues |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coal** | Electricity, steel and cement | Abundant, cheap, can be stored | Largest carbon dioxide emitter per unit of energy; dust, sulphur dioxide, mining damage |
| **Oil** | Transport fuel, plastics, some electricity | Easy to move; high energy content | Price shocks, spills, emissions, uneven supply |
| **Natural gas** | Electricity, heating, cooking, industry | Burns more cleanly than coal or oil; flexible | Still emits carbon dioxide; leaks of methane; needs pipelines or liquefaction |
| **Nuclear** | Electricity | Very low carbon emissions; large output from little fuel | High cost, radioactive waste, accident risk, security concerns |
| **Hydroelectricity** | Electricity | Low emissions; stores water; cheap to run | Flooding and displacement, loss of habitat, sediment, dependence on rain (as drought showed at Kariba) |
| **Solar** | Electricity, heat | Free fuel; suitable for remote places | Intermittent; needs storage; large areas for big plants |
| **Wind** | Electricity | No fuel; fast to build | Intermittent; visual and noise effects; bird and bat harm |
| **Geothermal** | Electricity, heating | Reliable all day | Only near volcanic or rift areas; limited sites |
| **Tidal** | Electricity | Predictable | High cost; few sites; ecological impacts |
| **Biomass and biofuels** | Cooking, heat, electricity, fuel | Can be grown; uses waste | Needs land; competition with food; smoke if burnt inefficiently |

### Domestic fuels

Billions of people cook with **wood, charcoal and dung**. These are cheap and local, but burning them indoors causes smoke-related respiratory disease, collecting wood takes time (mainly of women and girls), and cutting trees causes deforestation. Cleaner options include improved stoves, biogas, LPG and electricity.

## Global distribution of the main fuels

- **Oil:** the largest proven reserves are in the Middle East (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates), with others in Venezuela, Canada, Russia and Nigeria.
- **Natural gas:** the largest reserves are in Russia, Iran and Qatar, with big producers also in the United States.
- **Coal:** very large reserves in the United States, Russia, Australia, China and India; China and India are also the biggest consumers. Zimbabwe's coal is at Hwange.
- **Uranium:** the biggest producer is Kazakhstan, followed by Canada, Namibia and Australia.

### Effects of unequal distribution

Producers can earn large export incomes, but dependence on one resource can leave economies exposed to price falls ("resource curse"). Importers face **energy insecurity**: price shocks, supply cut-offs and political pressure, as when conflict disrupts oil and gas trade. Narrow shipping routes such as the Strait of Hormuz are vulnerable. Countries respond by diversifying suppliers, building storage, developing renewable sources and signing long-term contracts. Competition for resources can also add to tensions between states.

## Global patterns and trends

- **Total demand has grown** with population, industrialisation and rising incomes, especially in Asia.
- **Fossil fuels still supply roughly four-fifths** of world primary energy, though their share is slowly falling.
- **Renewables are growing fastest**, notably solar and wind, as costs fall; hydropower remains the largest renewable source of electricity.
- **Consumption per person** is highest in high-income countries and oil-exporting states, and lowest in many low-income countries, where traditional biomass is a main fuel.
- **Production and consumption centres have shifted** towards Asia; China is now the largest consumer and also leads in installing renewable capacity.
- Factors: resource availability, price, technology, government policy, climate concerns and income.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Comparing electricity mixes]
Illustrative electricity generation (%) in three countries (data invented for this exercise).

| Source | Country P | Country Q | Country R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | 70 | 0 | 0 |
| Gas | 10 | 4 | 55 |
| Oil | 0 | 0 | 40 |
| Nuclear | 8 | 0 | 0 |
| Hydro | 6 | 88 | 0 |
| Wind, solar and biomass | 6 | 8 | 5 |

1. Fossil share, Country P: $70 + 10 = 80\%$. Country Q: $4\%$. Country R: $55 + 40 = 95\%$.
2. Renewable share (hydro, wind, solar and biomass): P $6 + 6 = 12\%$; Q $88 + 8 = 96\%$; R $5\%$.
3. Interpretation: P depends on coal, which is probably abundant at home; Q has rivers and relief suited to hydroelectricity; R is an oil and gas exporter that uses its own fuels. Choices reflect resources, not only policy.
:::

:::worked-solution[Describing a trend]
Illustrative world primary energy use rose from an index of 100 in 2000 to 150 in 2020, while the fossil share fell from 82% to 80%.

1. Percentage rise in demand: $(150 - 100) \div 100 \times 100 = 50\%$.
2. Change in fossil share: $80 - 82 = -2$ percentage points.
3. Interpretation: demand grew so fast that fossil fuel use still rose in absolute terms: fossil use in 2000 was $82\%$ of 100, or 82 units; in 2020 it was $80\%$ of 150, or 120 units, an increase of 38 units.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining the effect of unequal oil supply"}
**Describe:** most oil reserves lie in a few Middle Eastern countries, while the largest users are in Asia, North America and Europe. **Explain:** importers need long supply lines; if supply is disrupted, prices rise sharply, which raises transport and food costs. Exporters gain income but depend on one resource. Importing countries therefore diversify their suppliers and develop other sources such as nuclear and renewables.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling nuclear energy renewable"}
Uranium is a finite resource, so nuclear energy is non-renewable, even though it produces little carbon dioxide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying renewables are free of environmental impact"}
Every source has impacts: dams flood land, wind turbines affect birds, and biomass competes with food. Weigh the costs and benefits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a distribution without naming places"}
Say which countries or regions hold the resource, and add figures or relative terms (largest, second largest).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Energy sources are renewable (solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, tidal, biomass) or non-renewable (coal, oil, gas, uranium).
- Each source has advantages and issues, including emissions, cost, reliability and land use.
- Oil is concentrated in the Middle East, gas in Russia, Iran and Qatar, coal in the United States, Russia, Australia, China and India, and uranium in Kazakhstan, Canada, Namibia and Australia.
- Unequal supply creates export income for some and energy insecurity for others.
- Global demand is rising, fossil fuels supply roughly four-fifths of energy, and renewables are growing fastest.
- Domestic fuels such as wood, charcoal and dung bring health and deforestation problems.
:::
