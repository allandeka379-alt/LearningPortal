## Introduction

Zimbabwe's electricity comes mainly from two big stations: the hydroelectric power station at Kariba on the Zambezi, and the coal-fired thermal station at Hwange. When rainfall is low and Lake Kariba falls, or when old plant breaks down, the country has load-shedding, and households and businesses turn to solar panels, generators and firewood. Most rural households still cook with wood. At the same time, the country has plentiful sunshine and other untapped resources. This lesson covers the energy sources Zimbabwe uses now, the ones it could develop, and the gadgets and plans that bring clean energy to households. You should know the global energy types from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Actual energy source"}
A source that is already developed and used in a country, for example hydroelectricity at Kariba or coal at Hwange.
:::

:::definition{term="Potential energy source"}
A resource that exists but is little or not yet developed, because of cost, technology or lack of investment.
:::

:::definition{term="Clean (green) energy"}
Energy produced with low emissions of greenhouse gases and pollutants, such as solar, wind, small hydro and biogas.
:::

## Zimbabwe's actual energy sources

- **Hydroelectricity at Kariba.** The Kariba South power station, on the Zimbabwean side of the dam, has an installed capacity of about 1 050 MW after an extension completed in the 2010s. The dam and station are shared with Zambia through the Zambezi River Authority. Output depends on the water level in the lake, so droughts, such as the one in 2024, reduce generation.
- **Coal-fired thermal power.** Hwange Power Station (originally about 920 MW in six units, with newer units added in recent years) uses coal from the Hwange coalfield. Smaller thermal stations at Harare, Bulawayo and Munyati are older and less reliable. Coal gives reliable base-load electricity but emits carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide and needs maintenance.
- **Imports** of electricity from neighbours, such as Mozambique and Zambia, fill part of the gap.
- **Biomass.** Fuelwood and charcoal are the main cooking fuels for most rural households and many urban ones, with consequences for forests and health.
- **Petroleum.** Imported fuel for transport; **ethanol** from sugarcane (produced at Chisumbanje) is blended with petrol.
- Other: solar water heaters, small solar home systems, diesel generators and small hydro schemes in some highland areas.

## Potential energy sources

| Source | Why it has potential | Constraints |
|---|---|---|
| **Solar PV** | Strong sunshine and long dry seasons; suits remote places and rooftops | Cost of panels and batteries; theft; needs storage for evenings |
| **Small and mini-hydro** | Rivers draining the Eastern Highlands and the plateau | Seasonal flow; site surveys; local ownership |
| **Wind** | Some exposed highland ridges and open sites | Wind speeds are modest in much of the country; needs measurement |
| **Biogas** | Cattle dung and crop waste in rural households and institutions | Needs water, dung supply and maintenance |
| **Coal-bed methane** | Gas in coal seams, for example in Matabeleland North | Exploration and extraction costs; environmental effects |
| **Large hydro** | Planned schemes on the Zambezi (such as the Batoka Gorge project, shared with Zambia) | Very high cost, long construction, dependence on river flow |
| **Jatropha and other biofuels** | Can be grown on poor soils | Yields and profits often below expectation; land competition |

### Using GIS and remote sensing to find sites

A GIS overlays layers: solar radiation, wind speed, river flow, slope, land use, protected areas, roads, the distance to the grid and settlements. The best sites score high on the resource and low on constraints, for example a sunny site near a school with no grid. Remote sensing provides data on land cover, biomass, water bodies and surface temperature, and shows where forests are being cut for fuel.

## Clean energy gadgets

| Gadget | What it does | Advantages | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solar panel + regulator + battery + inverter** | Converts sunlight to electricity; the regulator protects the battery; the inverter gives mains-type power | Lights, TV, phones, small appliances | Initial cost; battery replacement |
| **Solar lantern or lamp** | Lighting and phone charging | Cheap, replaces candles and paraffin | Limited power |
| **Solar water heater** | Heats water on the roof | Cuts electricity or wood use | Needs sunny roof; initial cost |
| **Biogas digester** | Dung and water ferment to produce methane gas for cooking and lighting, and slurry for fertiliser | Reduces wood use and smoke | Needs daily feeding and water |
| **Improved cookstove** | Burns wood more efficiently | Cuts fuel use and smoke | Still uses wood |
| **Solar pump** | Pumps water for gardens and livestock | No fuel | Cost; depends on sunshine |

**Dirty energy** (coal, paraffin, diesel and open-fire wood) releases carbon dioxide and pollutants, causes deforestation, indoor smoke and health problems.

## Preparing a green energy proposal

1. State the need (for example a clinic with no power) and the evidence.
2. Assess the resource (sunshine, flow) and the site.
3. Choose and size the technology.
4. List costs, funding sources and who will maintain the system.
5. Assess environmental and social effects.
6. Give a timetable and a plan for monitoring and review.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Sizing a solar home system]
A household wants three LED bulbs (10 W each, 4 hours a day), phone charging (5 W for 3 hours) and a small television (40 W for 3 hours). The site gets an average of 5 peak sun hours a day and system losses mean only 75% of the panel's rating is usable (illustrative figures).

1. Energy per day: bulbs $3 \times 10 \times 4 = 120$ Wh; phone $5 \times 3 = 15$ Wh; television $40 \times 3 = 120$ Wh. Total $= 255$ Wh.
2. Panel size needed: $255 \div (5 \times 0.75) = 68$ W.
3. Choose the next standard size up, an 80 W panel.
4. Check: an 80 W panel supplies $80 \times 5 \times 0.75 = 300$ Wh a day, more than the 255 Wh needed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Payback period]
The system costs US\$250 and saves US\$10 a month on candles, paraffin and phone charging at a shop (illustrative).

1. Payback period $= 250 \div 10 = 25$ months.
2. As years: $25 \div 12 = 2.1$ years.
3. If panels last 10 years or more, the household saves well after the system has paid for itself, but the battery may need replacing sooner, which lengthens the payback.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining the effect of drought on Kariba power"}
**Describe:** when the lake level falls, less water can pass through the turbines and output is cut. **Explain:** hydroelectric output depends on the volume of water and the height (head) above the turbines; droughts reduce inflow from the Zambezi, and the shared dam limits how much each country can use. **Response:** diversify with solar and other sources, repair thermal plant and improve efficiency.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming only Kariba and Hwange"}
Include biomass, imports and petroleum as actual sources, and name the potential ones with the reason they are not yet developed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying clean energy has no cost"}
Clean energy has costs: panels, batteries, installation and maintenance. Present both the benefits and the costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to size the system"}
A proposal needs numbers: daily energy, panel size, cost and payback.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 13), often with a map or table of power stations and a proposal question. *Identify* sources; *describe* location with named places; *explain* why potential sources are undeveloped (cost, technology, investment); *calculate* energy use, panel size and payback; *suggest* how GIS and remote sensing help; *evaluate* proposals by cost, reliability and environmental effect.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zimbabwe's electricity comes mainly from Kariba hydro and Hwange coal, with imports; fuelwood is the main household fuel.
- Hydro output depends on the lake level, so drought can cause load-shedding.
- Potential sources include solar, small hydro, wind, biogas, coal-bed methane, large hydro and biofuels.
- GIS overlays resource and constraint layers; remote sensing maps land cover and biomass.
- Clean gadgets include solar panels with regulators and batteries, lanterns, water heaters, biogas digesters and improved stoves.
- A green energy proposal states the need, resource, size, cost, effects, timetable and monitoring.
:::
