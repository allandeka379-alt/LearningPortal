## Introduction

Norway produces nearly all its electricity from rivers and mountains, and most new cars sold there are electric. South Africa generates most of its electricity from coal and has suffered years of load-shedding while trying to add wind and solar. Both are middle- to high-income countries with large resources, yet their energy systems look very different. Moving a country's energy system from fossil fuels to sustainable sources is called the **energy transition**. It is a huge task, because energy is built into every part of the economy. This lesson explains why it is hard, what shapes each country's mix, how countries try to keep supplies secure, and how energy can be used more sustainably. You should know the energy sources and their issues.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Energy transition"}
The shift from an energy system based mainly on fossil fuels to one based mainly on low-carbon sources, with more efficient use of energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy efficiency"}
Getting the same service (light, heat, movement) from less energy, for example with LED lamps or insulated buildings.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy conservation"}
Using less energy by changing behaviour, for example by switching off appliances or walking instead of driving.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-sufficiency in energy"}
The proportion of a country's energy use that is met from its own production.
:::

## Challenges of the transition

1. **Scale.** Fossil fuels supply roughly four-fifths of the world's energy, and the infrastructure (power stations, pipelines, refineries, vehicles) is built to use them. Replacing it takes decades and huge investment.
2. **Intermittency and storage.** Wind and solar vary with weather and time of day, so batteries, pumped storage, flexible gas or links to other grids are needed.
3. **Grid connection.** Many good sites for solar and wind are far from cities; grids must be extended and modernised to carry variable power.
4. **Decarbonising hard-to-change sectors** such as steel, cement, shipping, aviation and heavy trucks, which need high-temperature heat or dense fuels.
5. **Cost and finance.** Low-income countries struggle to raise capital; fossil fuel workers and regions lose jobs and income and need support (a "just transition").
6. **Materials and technology.** Batteries and panels need minerals such as lithium, cobalt and copper, with mining impacts and supply risks.
7. **Politics and public acceptance.** Local opposition to wind farms or transmission lines, and short-term political cycles, slow action.

## National factors in the energy mix

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Resources** | Coal-rich countries tend to burn coal; countries with rivers and mountains use hydro; sunny countries can use solar |
| **Climate and geography** | Cold climates need heating; large countries need long transmission lines |
| **Income and investment** | Rich countries can pay for new technology; poor ones may choose the cheapest option or rely on traditional fuels |
| **Policy** | Subsidies, carbon prices, targets and bans change what is built |
| **Technology** | Falling costs of solar and wind speed their adoption |
| **Security and politics** | Fear of import dependence encourages domestic sources |

## Energy security, conservation and efficiency

Energy security means reliable supplies at affordable prices. Countries improve it by **diversifying** sources and suppliers, building storage, adding domestic renewables, and cutting demand. **Efficiency** (insulation, LED lighting, efficient motors, public transport) and **conservation** reduce the need for new supply and are often the cheapest ways to save energy. At home and community scale, sustainable use includes solar water heaters, efficient stoves, biogas, community woodlots and switching off unused appliances.

### International control

- **OPEC** (Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, founded in 1960, with members including Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait and the United Arab Emirates) coordinates oil production quotas to influence prices. It gives members influence and revenue, but can raise prices for importers, and its effect is limited by non-members and by members that exceed their quotas.
- **Nuclear materials** are monitored by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), part of the United Nations system, which inspects facilities under safeguards agreements, linked to the Non-Proliferation Treaty. This aims to prevent nuclear material being diverted to weapons, but depends on countries' co-operation.

### Cambridge detail: two contrasting countries

**Norway (high-income, mountainous, wet):** about nine-tenths of its electricity comes from hydropower, with growing wind generation. Cheap, low-carbon electricity supports heavy use of electric cars and heating. It exports surplus power to neighbours. Its security is high, but droughts reduce hydro output and it depends on interconnections for balance. (Reviewer: check figures.)

**South Africa (upper-middle income, coal-rich):** around four-fifths of its electricity comes from coal, produced by the state utility Eskom, with some nuclear (Koeberg) and a growing share of wind and solar after the renewable programme begun in 2011. Ageing coal plants have broken down, causing load-shedding since 2007–08 and especially in 2022–23. An international partnership announced in 2021 aims to help finance a shift away from coal, but the transition is slow because of the cost, jobs in the coal industry and grid constraints. (Reviewer: check dates and shares.)

**Comparison:** Norway's mix is explained by geography and wealth; South Africa's by abundant coal and past investment. Both face challenges: variable hydro in Norway; reliability, finance and employment in South Africa.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Energy self-sufficiency and import dependence]
Country X produces 240 units of energy and uses 300 units a year (illustrative).

1. Self-sufficiency: $240 \div 300 \times 100 = 80\%$.
2. Import dependence: $(300 - 240) \div 300 \times 100 = 20\%$.
3. Interpretation: the country must import one-fifth of its energy, so it is exposed to price and supply risks.
:::

:::worked-solution[Saving energy with LED lamps]
A household replaces a 60 W incandescent bulb with a 9 W LED bulb. Each is used for 4 hours a day for 365 days, and electricity costs US\$0.10 per kWh (illustrative).

1. Old bulb: $0.060 \times 4 \times 365 = 87.6$ kWh a year.
2. LED: $0.009 \times 4 \times 365 = 13.14$ kWh a year.
3. Saving: $87.6 - 13.14 = 74.46$ kWh, which is $74.5$ kWh to 1 decimal place.
4. Cost saved: $74.46 \times 0.10 = \text{about } 7.45$, so about US\$7.45 per bulb per year.
5. Evaluation: low-cost, but the saving depends on the hours of use and the price of electricity.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating the transition in a coal-dependent country"}
**For a faster transition:** cleaner air, lower carbon emissions, falling costs of solar and wind, reduced import risk and new jobs. **Against or limiting:** coal plants are already built, mining communities depend on jobs, the grid needs rebuilding, and storage is costly. **Judgement:** the transition is necessary but must be phased, with financial help and retraining for workers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing efficiency with conservation"}
Efficiency gets the same service from less energy through better technology. Conservation reduces the service by changing behaviour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the transition as only a technology problem"}
Costs, jobs, politics and grid limits are as important as technology. Always include social and economic challenges.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using examples without facts"}
A detailed specific example needs named sources, approximate shares, dates and reasons. "Norway uses a lot of hydro" is not enough.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The energy transition is hard because of scale, intermittency, storage, grids, hard-to-change sectors, finance, materials and politics.
- National energy mixes reflect resources, climate, income, investment, policy, technology and security.
- Energy security is improved by diversification, storage, domestic sources, efficiency and conservation.
- OPEC influences oil prices through quotas; the IAEA monitors nuclear material through safeguards.
- Norway (hydro) and South Africa (coal) show how geography, wealth and history shape electricity systems.
- Sustainable energy at home and community scale includes efficiency, solar, biogas and efficient stoves.
:::
