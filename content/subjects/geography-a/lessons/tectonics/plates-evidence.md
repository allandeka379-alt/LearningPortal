## Introduction

Zimbabwe is one of the quietest places on Earth for earthquakes and volcanoes, yet the rocks beneath it are part of a restless system. The ground under Harare sits on an ancient, stable block of continental crust that is moving, very slowly, together with the rest of the African plate. A few hundred kilometres to the north-east, the African plate is being pulled apart along the East African Rift. **Plate tectonics** explains both facts, and it explains where the world's earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges and ocean trenches are found.

You should already know that the Earth has a crust, mantle and core. This lesson explains what plates are, what evidence shows that they move, what drives them, and how their boundaries are classified. The landforms and hazards that result are met in the next lesson and in the hazards topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lithosphere and asthenosphere"}
The **lithosphere** is the rigid outer shell of the Earth: the crust plus the uppermost solid mantle, roughly 100 km thick on average. It rests on the **asthenosphere**, a hotter, weaker part of the upper mantle that is solid but can flow very slowly.
:::

:::definition{term="Tectonic plate"}
A large slab of lithosphere, bounded by zones of earthquake and volcanic activity, that moves as a unit over the asthenosphere. There are about seven major plates and a number of smaller ones.
:::

:::definition{term="Continental drift"}
The idea, proposed by Alfred Wegener from 1912, that the continents were once joined in a single land mass (**Pangaea**) and have since moved apart. It lacked a mechanism, so many scientists rejected it until the 1960s.
:::

:::definition{term="Sea-floor spreading"}
The creation of new oceanic crust at a mid-ocean ridge, where magma rises and cools, and the movement of that crust away from the ridge on both sides.
:::

## Continental and oceanic plates

| Feature | Continental | Oceanic |
|---|---|---|
| Typical thickness | About 35 km (up to about 70 km under mountains) | About 5-10 km |
| Main rock | Granite-type rocks (rich in silica) | Basalt (rich in iron and magnesium) |
| Density | Lower, about 2.7 g/cm³ | Higher, about 3.0 g/cm³ |
| Age | Very old: parts more than 3 billion years | Young: mostly less than about 200 million years |
| Fate at a convergent boundary | Too buoyant to subduct | Sinks (subducts) |

Oceanic crust is young because it is continually destroyed at subduction zones. The Zimbabwe Craton, by contrast, contains rocks more than 3 billion years old. Plates move at a few centimetres a year, about as fast as fingernails grow: roughly 2-3 cm a year across the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and over 10 cm a year on the fastest part of the East Pacific Rise.

## Evidence for continental drift and sea-floor spreading

1. **Jigsaw fit.** The edges of the continental shelves of South America and Africa fit together better than the coastlines, because the shelf edge is the true edge of the continent.
2. **Fossils.** Fossils of the freshwater reptile *Mesosaurus* are found only in southern Africa and eastern South America. Fossils of the seed fern *Glossopteris* occur in Africa, South America, India, Australia and Antarctica. These organisms could not have crossed an ocean.
3. **Rock sequences and mountain belts.** Rocks of the same type and age, and mountain belts that line up when continents are reassembled, occur on both sides of the Atlantic. The Karoo-age rocks of southern Africa, including the coal of the Hwange area, have counterparts in other southern continents.
4. **Past climates.** Glacial deposits about 300 million years old (the Dwyka tillite of South Africa is an example) occur in places now warm, while coal, formed in warm swamps, occurs in Antarctica. These places must have been at different latitudes.
5. **Palaeomagnetism.** As basalt cools, magnetic minerals line up with the Earth's magnetic field. The field has reversed many times, so the ocean floor carries **stripes** of normal and reversed polarity that are symmetrical on either side of a ridge. Fixed rocks record the direction of the pole when they formed, and the apparent position of the pole differs for each continent unless the continents are moved back together.
6. **Age of the ocean floor.** Drilling and dating show that basalt is youngest at the ridge and gets older with distance, and that a layer of sediment gets thicker away from the ridge. This is the direct evidence of spreading.

## Driving forces

The underlying energy comes from heat inside the Earth, from the cooling of the core and the decay of radioactive elements.

- **Convection currents** in the mantle: hot material rises, spreads, cools and sinks. The old view was that the currents carried the plates like conveyor belts; the modern view is that they are one part of a system that includes the forces below.
- **Slab pull:** a cold, dense, subducting slab sinks under its own weight and pulls the rest of the plate behind it. Most geologists regard slab pull as the strongest force.
- **Ridge push:** a ridge stands high above the ocean floor; lithosphere slides down the slope of the asthenosphere away from it under gravity, pushing the plate.

## Types of plate boundary

| ZIMSEC term | Cambridge term | Movement | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Constructive | Divergent | Plates move apart; new crust forms | Mid-Atlantic Ridge; East African Rift |
| Destructive | Convergent: oceanic-continental or oceanic-oceanic | Denser oceanic plate subducts | Nazca plate under South America; Pacific plate under the Philippine plate |
| Destructive (collision) | Collision (continental-continental) | Neither plate subducts; crust crumples and thickens | India and Eurasia (Himalaya) |
| Conservative | Conservative (transform) | Plates slide past each other; crust is neither made nor destroyed | San Andreas Fault |

![Four plate boundary types in cross-section and plan: at a constructive boundary plates move apart and magma forms new crust; at a destructive boundary oceanic crust subducts under continental crust forming a trench and volcanoes; at a collision boundary two continents crumple into fold mountains; at a conservative boundary plates slide past each other with earthquakes.](media:diagrams/plate-boundary-types.png "Figure: the four types of plate boundary (schematic)")

Most earthquakes and volcanoes lie along these boundaries. The **Pacific Ring of Fire**, around the edge of the Pacific plate, contains most of the world's active volcanoes. Zimbabwe lies in the interior of the African (Nubian) plate, far from a boundary, which is why its tremors are few and small.

:::keyfact{title="Learn these values"}
Continental crust: thick, light, old and not subducted. Oceanic crust: thin, dense, young and subducted. Plates move at about 1-10 cm a year. Sea-floor spreading rate = distance from the ridge ÷ age of the rock. One kilometre per million years equals one millimetre per year.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating a spreading rate]
Basalt dredged 540 km from a ridge axis is dated at 18 million years old. Calculate the rate of spreading on that side of the ridge.

1. Rate = distance ÷ time, so rate = 540 km ÷ 18 million years.
2. $540 \div 18 = 30$ km per million years.
3. 1 km per million years = 1 mm per year, so the rate is **30 mm per year** (3.0 cm per year).
4. If the other side of the ridge spreads at the same rate, the plates move apart at 60 mm per year.
:::

:::worked-solution[Reading ocean-floor ages and predicting how long an ocean takes to open]
Illustrative data for a line of drilling sites across a ridge (data invented for this exercise):

![Age of ocean-floor basalt rises in a straight line from 0 at the ridge axis to 36 million years at 900 km, a steady spreading rate of 25 km per million years, which is 25 mm a year on each side of the ridge.](media:diagrams/seafloor-age-distance.png "Figure: age of basalt against distance from a mid-ocean ridge (illustrative drilling data)")

| Distance from ridge axis (km) | Age of basalt (million years) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 100 | 4 |
| 250 | 10 |
| 400 | 16 |
| 650 | 26 |
| 900 | 36 |

1. **Describe.** Age increases steadily with distance, from 0 at the axis to 36 million years at 900 km.
2. **Rate.** $900 \div 36 = 25$ km per million years = 25 mm per year per side. Every row gives the same rate, so the spreading was steady.
3. **Predict.** If both sides spread at 25 mm per year, the plates separate at 50 mm per year. To open an ocean 4 500 km wide: $4\,500 \text{ km} \div 50 \text{ mm/yr} = 90$ million years. Real rates vary, so this is an estimate, not a fact about any real ocean.
:::

:::example{title="Describing palaeomagnetic stripes"}
Imagine a survey across a ridge. Moving away from the axis to the east, the polarity is normal for 12 km, reversed for 8 km, normal for 15 km and reversed for 10 km. To the west the same widths occur in the same order. **Describe:** the pattern is a symmetrical sequence of bands on either side of the ridge. **Explain:** new basalt forms at the axis and records the magnetic field at the time. When the field reverses, the next basalt records the opposite polarity. Each side carries the same record because both halves of the new crust move away at the same rate. Wider bands show longer periods without reversal or faster spreading.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect plate structure, evidence, driving forces and boundary types (ZIMSEC 5.1; Cambridge 3.1). Cambridge compares continental and oceanic plates and names collision boundaries separately; ZIMSEC uses the terms constructive, destructive and conservative and asks you to distinguish the features of constructive and destructive boundaries.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the continents plough through the ocean floor"}
The continents do not move through the oceans. They are part of plates, and the whole plate (continent and ocean floor) moves.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the crust with the plate"}
A plate is lithosphere: crust plus the rigid top of the mantle. Plates are not just the crust.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating that new crust forms at all boundaries"}
Crust is created only at divergent (constructive) boundaries and destroyed at convergent ones. At conservative boundaries it is neither created nor destroyed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 5) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 3). *State* and *identify* ask for a term, boundary type or value with units. *Describe* a distribution by naming features and locations. *Explain* evidence by linking each observation to what it shows: do not just list it. A calculation of a spreading rate needs the formula, the conversion and a unit. For *assess the evidence* (Cambridge) or *to what extent*, weigh the strength of different lines of evidence and reach a judgement: no single line proves drift on its own, but together they are very strong.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The lithosphere is broken into plates that move over the weaker asthenosphere at about 1-10 cm a year.
- Continental crust is thick, light and old; oceanic crust is thin, dense and young and is subducted.
- Evidence: jigsaw fit, fossils, matching rocks, past climates, magnetic stripes and the age of the ocean floor.
- Slab pull, ridge push and mantle convection drive the plates; slab pull is usually judged the strongest.
- Boundaries are divergent (constructive), convergent (destructive), collision or conservative (transform).
- Spreading rate = distance ÷ age; 1 km per million years = 1 mm per year.
:::
