## Introduction

Stand at the top of the Zambezi Escarpment above Lake Kariba and you look down a steep, fault-bounded slope into a broad valley. That valley is a rift basin: ancient, but a close relative of the active East African Rift further north. Landforms like this record what the crust has done under stress. This lesson explains the **processes** that stretch, squeeze, slide and melt the crust, and the **landforms** each one produces.

You should know the types of plate boundary from the previous lesson. Here we ask what happens at each of them and what we can see on the surface. The hazards that go with these landforms (earthquakes and volcanic eruptions) are covered in the hazards topics.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fault"}
A fracture in rock along which the two sides have moved. The surface the rocks slide on is the fault plane; the vertical displacement is the throw.
:::

:::definition{term="Rift valley"}
A long, steep-sided valley formed where a block of crust between two roughly parallel faults has dropped (a graben) as the crust is stretched.
:::

:::definition{term="Subduction"}
The sinking of one plate, usually dense oceanic lithosphere, beneath another into the mantle at a convergent boundary.
:::

:::definition{term="Fold"}
A bend in rock layers caused by compression. An **anticline** is an arch (upfold); a **syncline** is a trough (downfold).
:::

## Faulting and the stress behind it

Rocks react to stress by folding (when they bend) or by breaking (when they are brittle or the stress is sudden).

| Stress | Fault type | Movement | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tension (pulling apart) | Normal fault | Block above the fault plane moves down | Fault scarp; rift valley (graben) if two faults dip towards each other |
| Compression (squeezing) | Reverse and thrust faults | Block above the plane moves up and over; a thrust is a low-angle reverse fault | Crust shortened and thickened; older rock pushed over younger |
| Shear (sideways) | Strike-slip (transform) fault | Blocks slide horizontally past each other | Offset features, shutter ridges; no crust made or lost |

A **fault scarp** is the steep face left on the surface where the ground has moved. A **horst** is a block left standing between two faults; a **graben** is the block that has dropped.

![Fault types: under tension a normal fault drops the block above the fault plane (hanging wall), under compression a reverse or thrust fault pushes it up and over, and under shear a strike-slip fault moves the blocks horizontally past each other.](media:diagrams/fault-types-stress.png "Figure: normal, reverse and strike-slip faults and the stress that makes each")

## Divergent boundaries: rifts and ridges

**Continental rifting.** Where a plate is stretched, the crust thins and cracks along normal faults, and the central block drops to form a rift valley. Magma rises through the thinned crust, so volcanoes occur along the rift. The **East African Rift** runs for thousands of kilometres from the Afar region towards Mozambique. It has an eastern branch (through Kenya and Tanzania) and a western branch (holding deep lakes such as Tanganyika and Malawi). If stretching continues, the rift may widen until it floods and becomes a new ocean.

**Sea-floor spreading.** In an ocean, magma rises into the gap, cools into basalt and forms a **mid-ocean ridge**, a chain of underwater mountains standing about 2-3 km above the surrounding ocean floor. Transform faults offset the ridge into segments. Where the ridge rises above sea level, as in Iceland (where a hot spot adds extra magma), the landscape is volcanic with fissures and shield volcanoes.

## Convergent boundaries: subduction and fold mountains

1. **Oceanic plate meets continental plate.** The denser oceanic plate is subducted and bends down to form an **ocean trench**, such as the Peru-Chile Trench. Earthquakes occur along the descending slab and get deeper beneath the continent. At about 100 km depth the slab releases water that lowers the melting point of the mantle above it. The magma rises to form **composite volcanoes** and the continental margin is squeezed into **fold mountains**: the Andes.
2. **Oceanic plate meets oceanic plate.** One plate is subducted beneath the other. Volcanoes build up from the sea floor to form a curved chain, a **volcanic island arc**, with a trench on the convex side. The Mariana Islands and the trench beside them (nearly 11 km deep) are an example.
3. **Continental plate meets continental plate (collision).** Neither plate subducts. The crust is shortened by folding and thrust faulting and becomes thicker, forming high **fold mountains**. The Himalaya formed when India collided with Eurasia from about 50 million years ago, and they are still rising by a few millimetres a year. There are few volcanoes because little oceanic crust melts.

**Fold mountains** form as sediments that built up in an ocean basin are squeezed. Folds range from symmetrical, to asymmetrical, to overfolds, to recumbent folds (lying on their sides) and nappes (huge sheets thrust over others).

## Conservative boundaries: shearing

At a conservative boundary plates slide past each other. Friction locks the plates for decades and then they slip suddenly, causing earthquakes. Crust is neither made nor destroyed, so there are no volcanoes or mountains. The landscape shows offset streams, linear valleys and low ridges along the line of the fault, as along the San Andreas Fault.

## Volcanoes compared

| Feature | Composite volcano | Shield volcano |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | Subduction zones | Divergent boundaries and hot spots |
| Magma | Thick, silica-rich, sticky | Runny basalt |
| Shape | Steep cone, layers of ash and lava | Broad, gentle slopes |
| Eruption | Explosive | Mostly effusive |
| Example | Mount Pinatubo | Mauna Loa (Hawaii) |

:::keyfact{title="Learn the match"}
Tension gives normal faults and rift valleys. Compression gives folds, thrusts and fold mountains. Oceanic-continental convergence gives a trench, volcanoes and fold mountains. Oceanic-oceanic convergence gives a trench and an island arc. Collision gives fold mountains but few volcanoes.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Measuring a fault scarp on a profile (ZIMSEC detail: modelling with a digital elevation model)]
A digital elevation model (DEM) stores the height of the ground in a grid of cells. A profile through it across a fault gives these illustrative heights (data invented for this exercise):

![Height profile across a fault from a digital elevation model: the ground is flat at about 1250 m, drops 76 m over 200 m between 200 and 400 m (gradient 38 percent, angle 20.8 degrees) and is flat again near 1168 m, a throw of 82 m consistent with a normal fault.](media:diagrams/fault-scarp-dem-profile.png "Figure: DEM profile across a fault scarp (data from the worked example; height axis starts at 1150 m)")

| Distance along line (m) | Height (m) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1250 |
| 100 | 1250 |
| 200 | 1248 |
| 300 | 1210 |
| 400 | 1172 |
| 500 | 1168 |
| 600 | 1168 |

1. The ground is flat near 1 250 m on the high side and near 1 168 m on the low side. The vertical offset (throw) is about $1250 - 1168 = 82$ m.
2. The scarp lies between 200 m and 400 m. Its drop is $1248 - 1172 = 76$ m over a horizontal distance of 200 m.
3. Gradient $= 76 \div 200 = 0.38$, or **38%**.
4. Angle $= \tan^{-1}(0.38) = 20.8°$.
5. The low side is the downthrown block. This profile is consistent with a normal fault, which is found where the crust is stretched.
:::

:::worked-solution[How fast can a rift widen?]
A rift in Africa is widening by about 6 mm a year. How long would it take to widen by 1 km?

1. Convert 1 km to millimetres: $1 \text{ km} = 1\,000\,000$ mm.
2. Time $= 1\,000\,000 \div 6 = 166\,667$ years.
3. The answer is about **167 000 years**, tiny on a geological timescale. At this rate an ocean basin thousands of kilometres wide needs tens of millions of years to form. Rates vary and this is a simple estimate.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting earthquake foci at a subduction zone"}
Illustrative data: foci of earthquakes lie at depths of 70, 140, 210 and 280 km at distances of 100, 200, 300 and 400 km from a trench. **Describe:** depth increases steadily with distance from the trench. **Calculate the dip:** $\tan^{-1}(70 \div 100) = 35°$. **Explain:** the earthquakes mark the descending oceanic plate, which dips beneath the continent. The deepest earthquakes occur furthest from the trench. This zone of foci is the Benioff (Wadati-Benioff) zone, and it shows that plate material is being destroyed.

![Subduction zone cross-section: earthquake foci at depths of 70, 140, 210 and 280 km lie 100, 200, 300 and 400 km from the trench along a plate dipping at 35 degrees beneath the continent, marking the Benioff zone, with a composite volcano above the descending slab.](media:diagrams/subduction-benioff-zone.png "Figure: earthquake foci along a subducting plate (Benioff zone); depths and distances from the worked example")

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying volcanoes form at all plate boundaries"}
Volcanoes form at divergent boundaries, at subduction zones and over hot spots. Conservative boundaries have none, and collision boundaries have very few.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a rift valley as made by erosion"}
A rift valley is made by faulting (a dropped block). Rivers and glaciers modify it, but they do not create it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting why magma forms at a subduction zone"}
The slab does not simply melt from friction. Water released from the slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above it, and that magma rises.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tension makes normal faults, scarps and rift valleys; compression makes folds, reverse faults and thrusts; shear makes strike-slip faults.
- Divergent boundaries give rifts on land and mid-ocean ridges at sea, with basalt volcanoes.
- Subduction makes trenches, Benioff-zone earthquakes, composite volcanoes and island arcs.
- Collision shortens and thickens the crust to form fold mountains, with few volcanoes.
- Conservative boundaries have earthquakes but no volcanoes.
- The East African Rift is a continental divergent boundary; gradient = vertical ÷ horizontal.
:::
