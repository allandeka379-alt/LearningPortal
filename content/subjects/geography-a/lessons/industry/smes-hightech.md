## Introduction

Walk through Magaba in Harare or Siya-So in Gweru and you see welders, carpenters, metal fabricators and repairers working in small businesses that supply the neighbourhood and the region. Down the road a student repairs phones, and a young software developer works from a shared office. These are small and medium enterprises (SMEs), and many of them are the main source of income for households. At the other end of the scale are hi-tech industries, which make computers, phones and electrical gadgets using advanced knowledge and skilled workers. This lesson describes SMEs and hi-tech industries, explains why they are located where they are, and assesses what they contribute. You should already know the factors that influence industrial location.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Small and medium enterprise (SME)"}
A business that is small in terms of employees, turnover and assets. Exact limits are set by law and differ by sector and country, but SMEs typically range from one-person micro businesses to firms with a few dozen workers.
:::

:::definition{term="Informal sector"}
Economic activity that is not fully registered, taxed or regulated, such as street vending and backyard workshops; many SMEs belong to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Hi-tech industry"}
An industry that makes products with advanced technology, uses a large share of its income for research and development, and employs many highly skilled workers, such as computers, phones and electrical gadgets.
:::

## Characteristics of SMEs

- Few employees, often family members or workers hired informally.
- Small capital, usually from savings, family or small loans, with limited machinery.
- Owner-managed: the owner makes most decisions.
- Local market, local materials and simple technology.
- Often informal, with limited records and no formal accounting.
- Flexible and quick to respond to demand, but easily harmed by shocks.

## Contribution to growth and development

SMEs provide **jobs** for many people, especially young people and women, and create **income** where formal jobs are scarce. They use local skills and materials, spread **production** across towns and rural areas, produce goods that low-income households can afford, supply parts and services to large firms (linkages), and train people in business skills. Successful SMEs can grow into larger firms and pay taxes. Labour force surveys in Zimbabwe show that a large share of workers earn a living in the informal economy.

## Constraints and support

| Constraints | Support (measures that help) |
|---|---|
| Lack of finance and collateral for loans | Microfinance, SME loan funds and the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Corporation (SMEDCO) |
| Shortage of workspace and premises | Serviced industrial sites and incubators |
| Unreliable electricity and water | Shared solar power and decentralised utilities |
| Weak skills in management and marketing | Training programmes and business advice |
| Competition from imports and large firms | Procurement rules that favour local products; linkages with large buyers |
| Complex regulations and taxes | Simplified registration and tax schemes |
| Poor access to markets and information | Trade fairs and mobile phone business platforms |

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Break-even for a small workshop]
A carpenter's fixed costs (rent of a stall and tools) are US\$600 a month. Each chair sells for US\$5 and costs US\$3 in materials (illustrative data).

1. Contribution per chair: $5 - 3 = 2$, so US\$2.
2. Break-even output: $600 \div 2 = 300$ chairs a month.
3. Selling 400 chairs gives profit $400 \times 2 - 600 = 200$, so US\$200 a month.
:::

:::worked-solution[Survival of start-ups]
A support programme helps 200 new businesses. After three years 130 are still trading (illustrative data).

1. Survival rate: $130 \div 200 \times 100 = 65\%$.
2. Failure rate: $100 - 65 = 35\%$, which is 70 firms.
3. A weakness is that most failures occur through lack of finance and markets, so support must continue after start-up.
:::

## Hi-tech industries

Hi-tech industries make **computers, mobile phones and electrical gadgets**. Their features are: research and development, a high proportion of skilled scientists and technicians, products that are light and high in value per kilogram, rapid change in products, and a need for a clean environment and reliable power. Because transport costs are small relative to value, they are **footloose**: they can choose locations freely, guided by labour, links to universities, good airports and communications, a pleasant environment and government incentives. They form clusters, called science parks or technology parks, such as Silicon Valley in California, Bangalore in India and Shenzhen in China.

In Zimbabwe, most computers, phones and electrical goods are imported, but there is local assembly, repair and software work, and universities and technology institutes train people for the sector. Many hi-tech firms and start-ups cluster in Harare, where skills, universities and communications are best.

:::worked-solution[Choosing a hi-tech location with weighted scores]
Three sites are scored from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on four factors, and each factor has a weight (illustrative data).

| Factor (weight) | Site A: city near a university | Site B: town on the main road | Site C: rural land |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skilled labour (3) | 5 | 3 | 2 |
| University links (2) | 5 | 2 | 1 |
| Airport and communications (2) | 4 | 5 | 1 |
| Low land cost (1) | 2 | 4 | 5 |

1. Site A: $3 \times 5 + 2 \times 5 + 2 \times 4 + 1 \times 2 = 15 + 10 + 8 + 2 = 35$.
2. Site B: $9 + 4 + 10 + 4 = 27$.
3. Site C: $6 + 2 + 2 + 5 = 15$.
4. Site A scores highest because hi-tech firms value skills and links more than cheap land.
:::

**Importance:** hi-tech industries create well-paid jobs, earn export income, raise the skills of the workforce, support other sectors through software and communications, and spread new technology. Disadvantages include high capital costs, a need for imported components, electronic waste, and jobs that are only open to the highly skilled.

:::example{title="Assessing the contribution of SMEs"}
A town has 1 200 registered and unregistered small businesses that employ on average 3 people each, making 3 600 jobs. A large factory employs 400 people. **Description:** SMEs provide nine times as many jobs as the factory. **Explanation:** they need little capital and use family labour. **Evaluation:** the jobs are often low-paid and insecure, so support for finance, premises and skills is needed to make the sector more productive.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating SMEs with informal street trading only"}
SMEs include formal firms such as small manufacturers and service businesses, as well as informal ones.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hi-tech industries locate near raw materials"}
Hi-tech goods are light and valuable, so transport cost matters little. Skills, links with universities, communications and incentives matter more.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing constraints without suggesting support"}
Link each constraint to a measure that could reduce it, such as microfinance for lack of capital.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- SMEs are small, owner-managed businesses, often informal, with limited capital and local markets.
- They provide jobs and income, use local materials and supply goods to low-income households.
- Constraints include finance, premises, power, skills, regulations and competition; support includes microfinance, training and simpler rules.
- Hi-tech industries make computers and gadgets, are skill- and research-intensive, and are footloose.
- They cluster near universities, airports and skilled workers, and Zimbabwe mainly imports, assembles and services such goods.
- Both sectors contribute to growth, but each depends on skills, finance and reliable services.
:::
