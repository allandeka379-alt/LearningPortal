## Introduction

Why is a sugar mill built in the lowveld among the cane fields, while a bottling plant stands in an industrial area of Harare close to its customers? Why do clothing makers, printers and repairers cluster in the same streets of Bulawayo or Harare? Firms choose locations to keep costs low and income high, and they are also pulled together by the benefits of being near each other. This lesson uses Alfred Weber's least-cost theory to explain location, then looks at why firms move, how they are linked to each other, and how size and clustering change costs. You should already know the difference between primary, secondary and tertiary activity and the idea of transport costs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Industrial location"}
The place chosen for a factory or workshop, decided by costs of raw materials, transport, labour and land, by markets and by government policy.
:::

:::definition{term="Material index"}
The weight of the localised raw materials needed divided by the weight of the finished product. A value above 1 means the industry loses weight in production; below 1 means it gains weight.
:::

:::definition{term="Agglomeration"}
The clustering of firms in one place so that they share services, workers, suppliers and information, and reduce their costs.
:::

:::definition{term="Linkage"}
A relationship between firms in which one supplies the other (backward linkage to suppliers, forward linkage to users of its product).
:::

## Weber's least-cost theory

Weber (1909) assumed a single firm choosing the point at which total costs are lowest, with a uniform plain, fixed raw material sources, a known market, fixed wage rates at certain places, and perfect competition. He argued that three factors decide the location.

1. **Transport costs** come first. They depend on weight and distance. The firm chooses the place that minimises the cost of bringing materials in and sending the product out. Lines of equal transport cost around a point are called **isotims**, and the best site is usually at a corner or inside the **location triangle** formed by the market and the material sources.
2. **Labour costs.** Cheap labour can pull the firm away from the least-transport-cost site if the saving in wages is larger than the extra transport cost. Lines joining places where extra transport cost is the same are **isodapanes**; the **critical isodapane** is the line along which extra transport cost equals the labour saving.
3. **Agglomeration.** Savings from being close to other firms may also outweigh the extra transport cost of a move.

**Material index rule:** if the index is above 1 (for example ore that yields a small amount of metal), the plant is placed near the raw material, because transporting the product is cheaper. If the index is below 1 (the product is heavier than the localised materials, as in bottling where water is found everywhere), it is placed near the market.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Locating a plant using the material index]
A smelter needs 4 t of ore to make 1 t of metal, so the material index is $4 \div 1 = 4$. Transport costs US\$0.10 per tonne per kilometre. The ore deposit (R) and the market (M) are 100 km apart (illustrative data).

1. At R, 0 km from the ore: only the metal is carried 100 km: $1 \times 100 \times 0.10 = 10$, so US\$10 per tonne of metal.
2. Halfway, 50 km from each: ore $4 \times 50 \times 0.10 = 20$, metal $1 \times 50 \times 0.10 = 5$, total US\$25.
3. At M: ore $4 \times 100 \times 0.10 = 40$, so US\$40.
4. The least transport cost is at R. A weight-losing industry locates near its raw material.
:::

:::worked-solution[Labour saving and the critical isodapane]
Moving the smelter from R towards the market adds US\$0.30 to the transport cost per tonne of metal for each kilometre. A town 30 km away offers labour savings of US\$9 per tonne of metal (illustrative data).

1. Extra transport cost for 30 km: $30 \times 0.30 = 9$, so US\$9.
2. Labour saving US\$9 equals the extra cost, so 30 km is the critical isodapane.
3. Beyond 30 km extra transport cost exceeds the saving, so the firm would not move farther. At 20 km it would gain $9 - 6 = 3$, so US\$3 per tonne.
:::

## Economies and diseconomies of scale

**Economies of scale** are falling average costs as output rises, because fixed costs are spread over more units, bulk buying lowers input prices, and specialised machines and workers are used. **Diseconomies of scale** appear when a firm becomes too large and costs per unit rise because of poor communication, congestion, strain on supply of inputs and workers, and management problems.

:::worked-solution[Average cost]
A factory has fixed costs of US\$600 000 and variable costs of US\$4 per unit (illustrative data). Average cost = fixed cost ÷ output + variable cost per unit.

1. At 100 000 units: $600\,000 \div 100\,000 + 4 = 6 + 4 = 10$, so US\$10 per unit.
2. At 300 000 units: $600\,000 \div 300\,000 + 4 = 2 + 4 = 6$, so US\$6 per unit.
3. The fall from US\$10 to US\$6 is an economy of scale of 40% in average cost.
:::

## Linkages and agglomeration

**Backward linkages** connect a firm to its suppliers: a sugar mill buys cane from estates and smallholders; a clothing factory buys cloth from textile mills that buy yarn from spinners that buy cotton lint from ginneries. **Forward linkages** connect a firm to those who use its product: the same sugar mill sells to bakers and soft drink makers. Strong linkages reduce transport, storage and communication costs, and create jobs along the chain. Industrial estates in Harare (such as Msasa and Willowvale) and Bulawayo (such as Belmont) show **agglomeration**: firms share roads, power, skilled workers, repair services, banks and suppliers, and customers can compare many sellers in one place. Diseconomies of agglomeration include congestion, high land prices, pollution and competition for workers.

## Relocation

Firms relocate when the original site no longer serves them. Reasons include rising land rents and congestion, shortage of space to expand, a more profitable market elsewhere, cheaper or more reliable power and water, better transport, government incentives (such as Special Economic Zones), poor security or infrastructure, closure of a key supplier or customer, and environmental rules. Some firms have moved from Bulawayo to Harare to be closer to the largest market, and some factories have moved from city centres to industrial estates on the edge of town. Relocation causes job losses at the old site and gains at the new one.

## Applying Weber in Zimbabwe

Weber fits some cases: sugar mills and ginneries near their raw materials, cement works near limestone, and mineral processing near mines (weight-losing). It fits less well where policy, power supply, historic location, rail networks and the position of the largest markets shape decisions, or where labour is mobile. The industry of Harare is explained by market size, agglomeration and infrastructure, not by raw materials alone.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the material index rule"}
A high material index (a weight-losing process) means location **near the raw material**; a low index means location **near the market**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing factors without a theory"}
When asked to apply Weber, name transport, labour and agglomeration, then show how each affects the chosen site, and state the theory's limits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing economies of scale with agglomeration"}
Economies of scale occur inside one firm as output grows. Agglomeration economies come from being near other firms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Weber's theory seeks the least-cost site using transport costs, labour costs and agglomeration.
- Material index above 1 points to the raw material; below 1 points to the market.
- Labour savings can pull a plant away from the least-transport site up to the critical isodapane.
- Economies of scale reduce average cost as output rises; diseconomies appear when a firm grows too large.
- Linkages tie suppliers and users together; agglomeration brings shared services and customers.
- Relocation follows changes in costs, markets, policy and space, and Weber explains part but not all of Zimbabwe's pattern.
:::
