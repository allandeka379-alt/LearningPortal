## Introduction

Tourism can bring jobs, foreign currency and pride in local culture, and it can also crowd out residents, damage fragile environments and add to climate change through flights. The same visitors create both benefits and costs, and the balance depends on how tourism is planned and managed. This lesson covers the impacts of tourism, the idea of carrying capacity and sustainable tourism, and two contrasting destinations, Victoria Falls and the Maldives, which give detailed examples for Cambridge answers. It also summarises the importance, planning and marketing of tourism in Zimbabwe for ZIMSEC. You should already know the patterns and types of tourism.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sustainable tourism"}
Tourism that meets the needs of visitors and host communities today without harming the environment, culture or economy needed by future generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The maximum number of visitors a place can hold at one time, or in a period, without unacceptable damage to the environment, loss of quality of the visitor experience or harm to the host community.
:::

:::definition{term="Leakage"}
The part of tourist spending that leaves the host area, for example profits sent abroad by foreign owners and money spent on imported goods.
:::

## Impacts of tourism

| | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | jobs in hotels, guiding and transport; foreign exchange; taxes; support for local suppliers and crafts; investment in roads, airports and water | leakage of profits; seasonal and low-paid jobs; higher prices and land values for residents; over-dependence on one industry |
| **Social and cultural** | pride in heritage; revival of crafts and festivals; services shared with residents | crowding and loss of access for locals; commercialisation of culture; conflicts over land; crime and begging; changes in behaviour |
| **Environmental** | funds and reasons for protecting parks and wildlife; ecotourism | damage to paths and habitats; waste and sewage; water use; noise; coral and reef damage; pollution from boats and aircraft |

**The carbon cost of travel.** Air travel burns fuel and releases greenhouse gases high in the atmosphere. A single long-haul return flight can produce a large share of a person's yearly travel emissions. Responses include carbon offsetting (which has limited reliability), cleaner aircraft and fuel, taxes on flying, and encouraging longer stays so that one flight is spread over more days.

### Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Carrying capacity of a viewpoint]
A viewing area of 2 400 m² gives each visitor 4 m² at one time. A visit lasts 1 hour and the site opens for 8 hours (illustrative data).

1. Visitors at one time: $2\,400 \div 4 = 600$.
2. Visitors per day: $600 \times 8 = 4\,800$, because each hour can admit a new group of 600.
3. If 6 000 visitors arrive on a peak day, the site exceeds its capacity by $6\,000 - 4\,800 = 1\,200$, so timed tickets or other viewing points are needed.
:::

:::worked-solution[Leakage and carbon]
Tourists at a resort spend US\$100 a day and 40% of that leaves the area (illustrative data). The resort has 40 000 visitors who each fly a return journey causing 2.5 t of carbon dioxide (illustrative figure).

1. Retained per tourist day: $100 \times (1 - 0.40) = 60$, so US\$60 stays in the local economy.
2. Leakage per tourist day: $100 \times 0.40 = 40$, so US\$40.
3. Flight emissions: $40\,000 \times 2.5 = 100\,000$ t of carbon dioxide.
:::

## Detailed example 1: Victoria Falls (Zimbabwe and Zambia)

The Victoria Falls (Mosi-oa-Tunya, "the smoke that thunders") lie on the Zambezi on the border of Zimbabwe and Zambia and were made a World Heritage Site in 1989. **Development:** a rail bridge opened in 1905, and the town of Victoria Falls in Zimbabwe grew as a tourist centre with hotels, an airport that was expanded and modernised in the 2010s, and activities such as white-water rafting, bungee jumping from the bridge, helicopter flights and sunset cruises. **Impacts:** jobs and foreign currency for the town and the region; informal curio sellers earn an income; but pressure on water and waste services, wear on paths in the spray rainforest, noise from flights, seasonal employment, and leakage of profits where operators are foreign-owned. **Management:** entrance fees and controlled access to the park and rainforest, protected status, regulation of activities and environmental assessment of new developments, and regional co-operation under the Kavango-Zambezi Transfrontier Conservation Area (KAZA), whose joint visa makes travel between the two countries easier. **Evaluation:** management has protected the Falls themselves, but growth in numbers and flights needs strict limits, and benefit for nearby communities depends on local ownership and jobs.

## Detailed example 2: the Maldives

The Maldives is a chain of about 1 200 coral islands in 26 atolls in the Indian Ocean, with an average ground level of about 1.5 m above sea level. Tourism started in the early 1970s, with the first resorts opened in 1972. **Development:** a policy of "one island, one resort" kept resorts apart from the islands where Maldivians live; since about 2009, guesthouses on inhabited islands have been allowed. Tourism became the largest part of the economy, supplying a very large share of foreign exchange. **Impacts:** high earnings and jobs; but food and most materials are imported (leakage), reefs are damaged by anchors, dredging and snorkellers, sewage and waste are difficult to manage on small islands (waste has been dumped on the island of Thilafushi), and the country is threatened by sea-level rise. **Management:** limits on building density and height, marine protected areas, a tax on tourists' nights, and waste and sewage treatment rules. **Evaluation:** the model has produced income and kept tourists away from daily life, but it depends on flights, imports and healthy reefs, so long-term sustainability is uncertain.

**Contrast:** Victoria Falls is a single natural attraction on a land border with day-visit activities and a town; the Maldives is a whole island nation with beach resorts and a stay of several days, flown in by long-haul flights.

## Tourism in Zimbabwe (ZIMSEC)

**Importance:** tourism earns foreign exchange, provides jobs in hotels, safaris, crafts and transport, supports conservation through park fees and community schemes such as CAMPFIRE, and encourages investment in roads, airports and services. **Planning:** zoning of tourist areas, environmental impact assessment, community involvement, training, spreading visitors beyond Victoria Falls to other regions, and quality grading by the Zimbabwe Tourism Authority. **Marketing:** brochures, websites, trade fairs, travel agents, social media, and joint marketing with neighbours. **Improving local attractions:** better signs, roads, toilets and guides; clear entrance fees; heritage interpretation; safe accommodation; and craft markets that keep money in the community.

:::example{title="Balancing impacts at a heritage site"}
A district council wants to attract visitors to a rock art site. **Gains:** guides and craft sellers earn money, and fees pay for fencing. **Costs:** more footsteps and vehicles could damage the paintings and disturb sacred places. **Judgement:** limit group sizes, train local guides and use part of the fees for care of the site, so the benefits last.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Impacts, carrying capacity and sustainability are needed by both boards. **Cambridge** (A Level Paper 4 option 13) requires **two contrasting detailed examples** of tourist areas or resorts with evaluation of management. **ZIMSEC** (Form 5) requires the importance of tourism to Zimbabwe, planning, marketing and improving local attractions. Check any figure you quote.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving a list of impacts with no balance"}
Explain who gains and who loses, in the short and long term, and reach a judgement about sustainability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a place name without detail"}
For Cambridge, give dated specific facts about your examples (what was developed, what happened to the environment and communities, and what management was done).
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring leakage"}
A large amount of spending can leave a country. Explain the reasons (foreign ownership, imported food) and ways of reducing leakage (local suppliers, local ownership).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4 (option 13). *Explain* impacts with cause and effect; *calculate* carrying capacity, leakage or percentage change; *compare* two destinations in a table of features; *assess* or *evaluate* management by weighing success against remaining problems, and conclude whether tourism is sustainable. Cambridge essays of 20 marks need two or more detailed examples.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tourism has economic, social and environmental impacts, both positive and negative.
- Leakage, seasonality and over-dependence reduce the economic benefit.
- Carrying capacity sets the number of visitors a place can hold without damage; long-haul flights have a high carbon cost.
- Victoria Falls and the Maldives show contrasting destinations, with different problems and management.
- Management includes limits on access, fees, zoning, assessment, local ownership and conservation.
- ZIMSEC adds the importance, planning, marketing and improvement of attractions in Zimbabwe.
:::
