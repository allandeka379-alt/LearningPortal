## Introduction

Examination papers in both boards are full of resources: graphs, tables, maps, photographs and extracts. Scoring well means choosing the right way to show data, reading it accurately, describing it with figures, and judging how far it can be trusted. In Cambridge these skills are assessed through all four papers; in ZIMSEC they are part of Form 5 skills and appear on every paper. This lesson covers the main types of graph and map and the language used to describe them. Photographs and satellite images are described in words in this course.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Trend"}
The general direction of change in a data set over time or along a distance, for example "rising steadily" or "falling then levelling off".
:::

:::definition{term="Anomaly"}
A value that does not fit the general pattern. An anomaly may be a mistake, or a real result that needs explaining.
:::

:::definition{term="Choropleth map"}
A map in which areas are shaded in classes of colour or tone according to a value, such as population density by district.
:::

:::definition{term="Isoline"}
A line joining places of equal value, such as contours (height), isobars (pressure) and isohyets (rainfall).
:::

## Choosing the right graph

| Data | Suitable graph |
|---|---|
| Separate categories (crop types) | Bar chart; divided bar for parts of a whole |
| Continuous data in classes (stream width classes) | Histogram (bars touch; area or height shows frequency) |
| Change over time | Line graph (several lines for comparisons) |
| Relationship between two variables | Scatter graph with a line of best fit |
| Parts of a whole, a few categories | Pie chart (angle = share × 360°) or divided bar |
| Three parts that add to 100% | Triangular graph (soil texture, employment sectors) |
| Wide range of values | Logarithmic or log-log graph |
| Spread of values | Dispersion graph (each value as a point on a line) |
| Temperature and rainfall for a place | Climate graph: line for temperature, bars for rainfall |
| Age and sex structure | Population pyramid (age-sex pyramid) |

## Describing a graph

Use four steps: **overall trend** (say increasing, decreasing, fluctuating), **figures** with units (quote the highest, lowest and a range of values), **anomalies** (values that break the pattern), and **comparison** if there are several lines or bars. Never explain unless the question says *explain*: description says what, explanation says why.

## Thematic maps

- **Choropleth:** areas shaded in classes. Good for rates and densities; hides variation within each area.
- **Dot map:** each dot stands for a number of items (e.g. 500 people); shows distribution.
- **Proportional symbol map:** the size of a circle or bar shows the value; the area, not the radius, should be proportional to the value, so a value four times larger has a radius twice as large.
- **Isoline map:** lines of equal value (isohyets, contours); closer lines show faster change.
- **Flow and desire-line maps:** arrows or lines whose width shows the amount of movement (migration, trade) or the direction of journeys.
- **Sketch maps:** simple labelled maps drawn from observation; they show the main features and relationships, not every detail.

## Photographs, satellite images and other resources

Describe a photograph by **foreground, middle ground, background**, and name the features (landforms, land use, settlement) with their position and relative size. In a satellite image look at tone, texture, pattern and shape. For a newspaper extract, pick out the facts (what, where, when, how many), the sources and any opinions, and note whether it is balanced. For a data table, find the highest and lowest values, totals, percentages and unusual figures.

## Limitations and bias

Check the **source** (who produced it and why), the **date** (is it current?), the **scale and classes** (a choropleth with wide classes hides variation), the **axis** (a graph that does not start at zero exaggerates change), the **sample** (small or unrepresentative?) and whether the data are **real or estimated**.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Pie chart angles]
Illustrative data: land use in a sample of 200 plots in a district.

| Land use | Plots | Angle (share × 360°) | Percentage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cropland | 90 | 162 | 45 |
| Grazing | 60 | 108 | 30 |
| Woodland | 30 | 54 | 15 |
| Built-up | 20 | 36 | 10 |

1. Total plots $= 200$.
2. Each plot represents $360^\circ \div 200 = 1.8^\circ$.
3. Angle for cropland $= 90 \times 1.8 = 162^\circ$. The four angles add to $360^\circ$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Describing a line graph]
Illustrative data: national maize production ('000 tonnes) over six seasons.

| Season | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Production | 500 | 640 | 720 | 410 | 600 | 760 |

1. Trend: production rose from 500 to 720 between 2016 and 2018, an increase of 44%, then fell sharply to 410 in 2019 (the anomaly, perhaps a drought year), before recovering to a high of 760 in 2021.
2. Range: from 410 to 760, a difference of 350 thousand tonnes.
3. Overall: production fluctuated but the long-term trend is upward.
:::

:::worked-solution[Describing a climate graph]
Illustrative climate data for a station on the highveld:

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 21.5 | 21.3 | 20.8 | 19.2 | 16.4 | 13.6 | 13.2 | 15.4 | 18.5 | 20.8 | 21.4 | 21.5 |
| Rainfall (mm) | 190 | 160 | 100 | 30 | 10 | 3 | 2 | 3 | 8 | 35 | 100 | 170 |

1. Annual temperature range $= 21.5 - 13.2 = 8.3$ °C, a small range because the station is in the tropics.
2. Annual rainfall $= 811$ mm, with a wet season from November to March and a dry season from May to September (10 mm or less a month).
3. The coolest months, June and July, are in the dry season, which is typical of the southern African highveld.
:::

:::example{title="Proportional symbols"}
A map uses circles to show town populations. Town A has 100 000 people and Town B has 400 000. Area is proportional to population, so Town B's circle has 4 times the area and its radius is $\sqrt{4} = 2$ times as long. If Town A's radius is 5 mm, Town B's is 10 mm.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test the same resource skills. ZIMSEC includes triangular graphs, log-log graphs and dispersion diagrams in Form 5 skills and uses data in Paper 3; Cambridge examines resources in the Skills section and in the stimulus material of every paper.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing without figures"}
"It goes up and down" earns little. Quote values with units and say when the highest and lowest occur.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a line graph for categories"}
Lines imply continuity. Use bars for separate categories and lines for change over time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining when asked to describe"}
Reasons are credited only in answers to *explain*. In *describe* answers they waste time and earn no marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "describe the trend", make three points: the overall direction, a pair of figures with units, and one anomaly or fluctuation. For "compare", use words such as "whereas", "higher than" and "twice as". For "to what extent does the resource show …", say what it shows and what it does not (limits of source, date or sample).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Choose a graph to match the data: bars for categories, lines for time, scatter for relationships, pies and triangles for parts of a whole.
- Describe graphs with the trend, figures with units, anomalies and comparisons.
- Thematic maps (choropleth, dot, proportional symbol, isoline, flow) each suit particular data; choropleths hide variation inside areas.
- Proportional symbols use area, so radius scales with the square root of the value.
- Always judge source, date, scale, axis and sample before trusting a resource.
:::
