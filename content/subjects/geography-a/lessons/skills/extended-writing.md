## Introduction

Two candidates know the same geography. One writes a long list of facts about floods and scores 6 out of 15. The other answers the question that was set, supports each point with evidence, links the points and ends with a reasoned judgement, scoring 13. The difference is not knowledge but technique. At A Level, marks are given for knowledge, for the skills of applying and analysing, and for evaluation, and the last two are won by how you write. This lesson is neutral about topics: the examples come from several areas of the syllabus. You should already be able to write clear paragraphs and read tables and graphs.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Command word"}
The word or phrase in a question that tells you what kind of answer to give, such as *describe*, *explain* or *assess*. It decides how the marks are awarded.
:::

:::definition{term="Levels of response"}
A mark scheme for longer answers that rewards the overall quality of an answer: its depth of knowledge, use of examples and evidence, and evaluation. Answers are placed in a level and then given a mark within it.
:::

:::definition{term="Evaluation"}
Weighing strengths and limitations, or the importance of different factors, using evidence, and reaching a judgement that is supported and clearly stated.
:::

## What the command words ask for

| Command word | What to do | Common error |
|---|---|---|
| State, identify, name | Give a short fact, term or value | Adding explanation; no extra marks |
| Define | Give a precise meaning of the term | A loose example instead of a definition |
| Outline | Give the main points briefly | Too much detail |
| Describe | Say what something is like: a pattern, trend, feature or process, with figures if there are data | Giving reasons (that is *explain*) |
| Explain | Give reasons: how and why, linking cause, process and effect | Describing without a reason |
| Compare | Show similarities and differences, in the same sentence, using comparative words (greater, whereas) | Describing each in turn with no link |
| Suggest | Apply your knowledge to a new situation with a reasoned idea | Writing a general answer unrelated to the resource |
| Justify | Give reasons for a choice or decision | Stating the choice without reasons |
| Calculate | Show working and give the unit and the stated accuracy | Answer only; no unit |
| Discuss | Consider several arguments, perspectives or factors | One-sided answers |
| Assess, evaluate, to what extent, how far | Weigh the evidence or factors, reach a judgement, say how important or successful | Describing; no judgement |

## Building a paragraph: point, evidence, explanation, link

1. **Point**: a clear statement that answers the question.
2. **Evidence**: a fact, figure, example or data from the resource.
3. **Explanation**: why or how the evidence supports the point.
4. **Link**: back to the question or the next paragraph.

A weak sentence: *"Deforestation causes floods."* A developed paragraph: *"Deforestation in a catchment increases the risk of flooding (point). On bare, compacted slopes much more rainwater runs off over the surface than on forested slopes (evidence). Without tree roots and leaf litter there is less infiltration and trees no longer intercept rainfall, so water reaches the river faster and in greater volume (explanation). This is why flood peaks are higher after clearance, which is the key human cause in this catchment (link)."*

## Examples, located detail and data

- A **located example** names the place, the date and one or two specific facts (for example "Cyclone Idai in March 2019 caused landslides in Chimanimani"). Never invent a fact; choose examples you know.
- When using a **resource**, quote it with units (for example "rose from 12 °C to 25 °C"), compare values, calculate a change, and say what it means.
- Add **scale** and **time**: local, national or global; short-term or long-term.

## Planning and timing

Spend about 3–5 minutes planning a long answer: write the command word and the topic; list 3–4 points or factors; decide the order; choose an example or a piece of evidence for each; decide the judgement. Time yourself by marks. Cambridge Papers 1–4 each last 90 minutes for 60 marks, which is 1.5 minutes a mark. ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 last 3 hours for four 25-mark questions, which is 1.8 minutes a mark, or 45 minutes a question.

## Evaluative essays (15, 20 and 25 marks)

1. **Introduction.** Define the key terms and state your line of argument briefly.
2. **Body.** Several paragraphs, each with a point, evidence, explanation and link. Include a counter-argument or limitation.
3. **Conclusion.** An explicit judgement that answers the question ("To a large extent … because … although …"), not a repeat of the body.

For ZIMSEC 25-mark structured questions, the early parts test recall and skills (state, calculate, describe), and the final 8–10 mark part tests evaluation, so keep time for it.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Taking apart a question]
Question: "To what extent is urbanisation the most important cause of increased flood risk in a named city?"

1. **Command phrase**: "to what extent" means you must weigh and conclude.
2. **Topic**: increased flood risk in a city.
3. **Focus or constraint**: "the most important cause": compare urbanisation with other causes; "a named city": you need a located example.
4. **Plan**: (i) urbanisation (impermeable surfaces, drains, building on floodplains); (ii) climate change and heavy rain; (iii) deforestation upstream and silted channels; (iv) failure of defences and drainage maintenance; (v) judgement on which matters most, depending on the city.
5. **Judgement**: "Urbanisation is the main cause in the city centre, but climate change and upstream land use also matter, so it is the most important local cause, not the only cause."
:::

:::worked-solution[Dividing the time in a ZIMSEC question]
A 25-mark ZIMSEC question has parts worth 3, 4, 5, 5 and 8 marks and you have 45 minutes.

1. Time per mark = $45 \div 25 = 1.8$ minutes.
2. Part (a), 3 marks: $3 \times 1.8 = 5.4$ minutes.
3. Part (b), 4 marks: $4 \times 1.8 = 7.2$ minutes.
4. Parts (c) and (d), 5 marks each: $5 \times 1.8 = 9$ minutes each.
5. Part (e), 8 marks: $8 \times 1.8 = 14.4$ minutes.
6. The total is $5.4 + 7.2 + 9 + 9 + 14.4 = 45$ minutes. Do not spend 20 minutes on a 3-mark part.
:::

:::example{title="Turning description into explanation"}
Data: a table shows that the proportion of a city's population living in informal settlements rose from 20% to 35% in 15 years. **Describe:** the proportion rose by 15 percentage points. **Explain:** rural–urban migration is rapid and formal housing is too expensive and too slow to build, so newcomers build informal homes, often on risky land. **Evaluate:** upgrading services in place may be cheaper and less disruptive than relocation, but it can leave people exposed to flooding. The three levels show how a *describe* answer is extended to *explain* and *evaluate*.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards use the same command words. Cambridge Papers 3 and 4 reward detailed, located examples; ZIMSEC rewards examples from Zimbabwe and southern Africa and accurate use of data. Cambridge marks assess knowledge and understanding, skills and analysis, and evaluation. Multiple-choice questions are not set in either board's examinations; they appear here for practice only.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing everything you know"}
Knowledge not linked to the command word earns few marks. Select only what answers the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing when asked to explain"}
"Rainfall decreased" is description. "Rainfall decreased because El Niño weakens the rain-bearing systems" is explanation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ending without a judgement"}
A conclusion that repeats the points cannot reach the top level. Say which factor, strategy or view is most important or successful, and why.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Underline the command word and the topic before you write. Use the mark allocation: roughly one developed point per mark in short answers. In resource questions use figures with units in every part. In essays decide your judgement while planning, make sure every paragraph links back to it, and keep five minutes to write a conclusion. If you run short of time, write a brief but explicit judgement rather than leaving the answer unfinished.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The command word decides the type of answer: describe says what, explain says why, evaluate weighs and judges.
- Develop each point with evidence, explanation and a link (point, evidence, explanation, link).
- Use located examples and quote data with units.
- Plan for 3–5 minutes and allocate time by marks (1.5 minutes a mark in Cambridge; 1.8 minutes a mark in ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2).
- Essays need a line of argument, balanced body paragraphs and a judgement that answers the question.
:::
