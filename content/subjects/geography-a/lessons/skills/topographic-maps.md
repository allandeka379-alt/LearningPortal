## Introduction

A topographic map is a reference book for the landscape: it shows relief, rivers, vegetation, settlements, roads and boundaries, all at a fixed scale. ZIMSEC Paper 3 (Section B) asks questions on a 1:50 000 extract, and Cambridge sets map and resource skills across all papers. In your examination the extract is printed; here we describe extracts in words and tables, so practise by imagining the map as you read.

You should already know the compass points, how to use a ruler and how to read the key of a map. The skills below are grouped into location, distance, relief and interpretation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Scale"}
The relationship between a distance measured on a map and the same distance on the ground. A representative fraction of 1:50 000 means 1 cm on a map is 50 000 cm (500 m) on the ground.
:::

:::definition{term="Contour line"}
A line joining places of equal height above sea level. The contour interval is the vertical distance between neighbouring contours.
:::

:::definition{term="Gradient"}
The steepness of a slope, found by dividing the vertical interval by the horizontal distance. It is written as a ratio (1 in 24) or a percentage.
:::

:::definition{term="Intervisibility"}
Whether one point can be seen from another, without the view being blocked by higher ground between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Grid reference"}
A set of numbers that fixes a place using a map's grid lines. Eastings (vertical lines, numbered across the map) always come before northings (horizontal lines, numbered up the map).
:::

## Location and direction

**Four-figure reference:** read the easting of the left side of the grid square, then the northing of its lower side, for example 2143. It identifies a whole square (1 km by 1 km on most 1:50 000 maps; check your sheet's margin).

**Six-figure reference:** divide each side of the square into tenths by eye. A school 4 tenths of the way across from easting line 21 and 7 tenths up from northing line 43 is at 214437.

**Bearings** are measured clockwise from grid north, from 000° to 360°, using a protractor. The **back bearing** is the bearing plus or minus 180°. Cardinal points: north 000°, east 090°, south 180°, west 270°.

## Scale and measurement

- Ground distance (km) $= \dfrac{\text{map distance (cm)} \times \text{denominator}}{100\,000}$.
- **Straight lines** are measured with a ruler. **Curved lines** (rivers, roads) are measured by laying a thread or the edge of paper along the route and then laying it against the scale bar.
- **Area:** count whole grid squares and treat part squares as halves (an approximation). On a 1:50 000 map each full square usually represents 1 km².
- **Sinuosity** of a river $=$ channel length $\div$ straight-line length; 1.0 is perfectly straight, values from about 1.05 to 1.5 are sinuous and values above about 1.5 are meandering.

## Relief

| Contour pattern | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Close together | Steep slope |
| Widely spaced | Gentle slope |
| Evenly spaced | Uniform slope |
| V-shapes pointing uphill | River valley (the V points to the source) |
| V-shapes pointing downhill | Spur or ridge |
| Rings of contours with a spot height | Hill or summit; a cluster of tight rings with a steep outline is typical of a dwala (granite dome) |
| Contours with very wide spacing around a stream | Flat floodplain or a dambo (vlei) |

**Cross-sections:** lay a strip of paper along the line, mark where each contour crosses it with the height, then plot the heights against distance on graph paper. **Vertical exaggeration** is the vertical scale divided by the horizontal scale; state it if you use it.

## Interpreting the landscape

- **Drainage patterns:** dendritic (tree-like, on uniform rock), radial (outward from a hill), trellis (rectangular, on alternating hard and soft rocks), parallel (on a uniform slope), centripetal (inward to a basin).
- **Settlement patterns:** nucleated (clustered), linear (along a road or river), dispersed (scattered farms). Reasons include water sources, flat land, routes and the mine or market.
- **Land use and communication:** farmland, plantations, mine dumps, dams, railways, tarred and gravel roads, tracks and footpaths; use the key.
- **Evidence for human activity:** dams and canals suggest irrigation; straight boundaries and regular rectangles suggest commercial or resettlement fields.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Distance, sinuosity and area]
On a 1:50 000 extract a student measures the straight line from a bridge to a school as 7.4 cm and the river between them, measured with thread, as 9.6 cm.

1. Straight-line distance: $7.4 \times 50\,000 \div 100\,000 = 3.7$ km.
2. River length: $9.6 \times 50\,000 \div 100\,000 = 4.8$ km.
3. Sinuosity $= 4.8 \div 3.7 = 1.30$, so the river is sinuous (not strongly meandering).
4. Area of a forest block covering 14 whole grid squares and 8 part squares: $14 + 8 \div 2 = 18$ squares, about 18 km².
:::

:::worked-solution[Gradient and bearing]
Point A at the foot of a slope is at 1 250 m and point B on the hillside is at 1 350 m. They are 4.8 cm apart on a 1:50 000 map.

1. Vertical interval: $1350 - 1250 = 100$ m.
2. Horizontal distance: $4.8 \times 50\,000 \div 100 = 2400$ m.
3. Gradient $= 100 \div 2400 = 1/24$, so **1 in 24**, which is 4.2% or about 2.4°.

Bearing: a borehole at easting 21, northing 45 and a clinic at easting 26, northing 49 (grid-line values).

4. Eastward difference $= 5$, northward difference $= 4$.
5. $\tan\theta = 5 \div 4$, so $\theta = 51.3^\circ$, measured from north towards east. The bearing of the clinic from the borehole is 051°.
6. The back bearing (clinic to borehole) is $51 + 180 = 231^\circ$.
:::

:::example{title="Intervisibility along a line"}
Station A (height 1120 m) and station B (height 1100 m) are 6 km apart. A ridge crest of 1170 m lies 3 km from A.

A straight line of sight falls by 20 m over 6 km, so at 3 km it is at 1120 − 10 = 1110 m. The ridge (1170 m) is higher than the line of sight (1110 m), so it blocks the view: B is not visible from A. (In practice the tops of trees and masts would also be considered.)
:::

## Describing a map extract

Use this routine for a "describe the" question: say **where** (grid squares or compass directions), **what** (the feature), **quote evidence** (heights, distances, grid references) and **compare** (more/less, higher/lower). For *explain* add a reason, such as "settlements are on the flat land near the river because of water and farmland".

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards test grid references, scale, gradient, cross-sections and description of relief, drainage and settlement. ZIMSEC Paper 3 sets a long 1:50 000 question with area and cross-section work; Cambridge uses maps at various scales within Papers 1-4 and a Skills section.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Northings before eastings"}
"Along the corridor, then up the stairs": eastings first. 214437 is easting 214, northing 437. Reversing them gives a different place.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to convert units in gradient"}
The vertical interval is in metres and the map distance is in centimetres or kilometres. Convert both to metres before dividing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring a curved route with a ruler"}
Rivers and roads must be measured with thread or a paper strip. A ruler gives only the straight-line distance, which underestimates length.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Start by reading the scale, key and contour interval. Show working for every calculation (scale, gradient, area) with units. A cross-section needs a labelled axis, a suitable vertical scale and labels of features. *State* a grid reference to six figures; *describe* relief with heights and slope words (steep, gentle, valley, spur); *suggest reasons* for a settlement site using water, relief, routes and land.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Give eastings then northings; six figures locate a place to 100 m on a 1 km grid.
- Ground distance (km) = map cm × denominator ÷ 100 000; measure curves with thread.
- Close contours mean steep slopes; V-shapes point uphill for valleys.
- Gradient = vertical interval ÷ horizontal distance, in the same unit.
- Cross-sections and intervisibility depend on heights between two points.
- Describe features with location, evidence and comparison.
:::
