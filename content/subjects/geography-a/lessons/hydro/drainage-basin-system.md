## Introduction

Harare lies near the top of the Manyame catchment. Rain that falls on the city's roofs, roads and lawns does not all follow the same path: some runs off at once into drains and streams that flow towards Lake Chivero, some soaks into the ground to feed wells and boreholes, and some is evaporated or taken up by plants. Understanding where the water goes, how long it takes and how much is stored is the basis of flood forecasting, water supply and catchment management. Geographers describe this using a **systems** approach.

You should already know the water cycle in outline. This lesson applies it to a single **drainage basin**, the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Hydrographs and morphometry, river processes and floods are in the next lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Drainage basin (catchment)"}
The area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. Its boundary is the **watershed**, the high ground that separates it from neighbouring basins. The river leaves the basin at the **mouth** or outlet, and a **confluence** is a place where two streams join.
:::

:::definition{term="Open system"}
A system in which matter and energy cross the boundary. A drainage basin is an open system: water enters as precipitation and leaves as evapotranspiration and channel flow. The global water cycle, by contrast, is a closed system.
:::

:::definition{term="Water table and aquifer"}
The **water table** is the upper surface of the saturated zone underground. An **aquifer** is permeable rock that stores and transmits useful quantities of water.
:::

:::definition{term="Evapotranspiration"}
The combined loss of water to the atmosphere by evaporation from surfaces and transpiration from plants.
:::

## Inputs, outputs, stores and transfers

| Part of the system | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Input** | Precipitation (rain, hail, snow, and mist or dew where they are significant) |
| **Outputs** | Evaporation, transpiration (together evapotranspiration), and channel flow (river discharge) to the sea or a lake |
| **Stores** | Interception (water held on leaves and stems); surface storage (puddles, dams, wetlands); soil moisture; groundwater; water in the channel |
| **Transfers (flows)** | Stemflow, throughfall, infiltration, overland flow, throughflow, percolation, groundwater flow |

### How water moves through the basin

1. **Interception.** Leaves and stems catch some rain, and part of it evaporates without reaching the ground. Forests intercept more than crops or bare soil.
2. **Stemflow and throughfall.** Water runs down the stems and trunks (stemflow) or drips off and falls between the leaves (throughfall) to reach the ground.
3. **Infiltration.** Water soaks into the soil. The **infiltration capacity** is the maximum rate at which the soil can absorb water.
4. **Overland flow.** If rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it (infiltration-excess flow), or if the soil is already saturated (saturation flow), water runs over the surface. It reaches the channel quickly.
5. **Throughflow.** Water moves downslope through the soil, usually faster along root channels and cracks than through the soil itself.
6. **Percolation.** Water moves down into the underlying rock.
7. **Groundwater flow.** Water moves very slowly through the saturated rock below the water table. It feeds the river as **baseflow**, which keeps rivers flowing in the dry season.

Surface flows are fast (minutes to hours), throughflow is moderate (hours to days), and groundwater flow is slow (weeks to years).

## What affects the movement of water

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Basin size** | Larger basins have bigger discharge and a longer journey for water to reach the outlet |
| **Basin shape** | A circular basin delivers water from all parts to the outlet at about the same time (high, sharp peak); an elongated basin spreads arrival times (lower, later peak) |
| **Drainage density** | The total length of channels per unit area. A high density means water reaches a channel quickly |
| **Relief and slope** | Steep slopes increase overland flow and speed |
| **Soil** | Sandy soils have high infiltration capacity; clay or compacted soils have low capacity and produce more overland flow; saturated soil cannot absorb more |
| **Rock** | Permeable rock stores water and has high baseflow; impermeable rock produces fast runoff. The granites and gneisses of much of Zimbabwe store water mainly in weathered and fractured zones |
| **Vegetation** | Increases interception and transpiration and slows overland flow; roots help infiltration |
| **Land use** | Paved and built surfaces are impermeable and send water quickly into drains; ploughed or overgrazed land is more easily compacted |
| **Climate** | Rainfall amount, intensity and season. In Zimbabwe most rain falls between November and March, so many streams are seasonal and rely on baseflow in the dry season |

## The water balance

Over a year, the inputs, outputs and change in storage balance:

$$P = Q + E \pm \Delta S$$

where $P$ is precipitation, $Q$ is runoff (channel flow), $E$ is evapotranspiration and $\Delta S$ is the change in storage. Over many years $\Delta S$ is close to zero, so $Q \approx P - E$. In a wet season $\Delta S$ is positive (stores fill) and in a dry season it is negative (stores are used). Values are given as depths of water (mm) over the whole basin so they can be compared.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating a water balance]
A catchment receives 840 mm of precipitation in a year. Evapotranspiration is 640 mm and runoff is 150 mm (illustrative data, invented for this exercise). Calculate the change in storage.

1. Write the balance: $P = Q + E \pm \Delta S$, so $\Delta S = P - Q - E$.
2. Substitute: $\Delta S = 840 - 150 - 640 = 50$ mm.
3. The positive value means that **50 mm** was added to storage (soil moisture and groundwater increased), so the stores filled during the year.
4. Runoff is $150 \div 840 \times 100 = 17.9\%$ of precipitation. Evapotranspiration takes $76.2\%$, which is typical of a warm climate.
:::

:::worked-solution[Converting discharge into depth of runoff]
A river drains a basin of 1 800 km². Its mean flow over a year is 3.0 m³/s (illustrative). Calculate the runoff depth in millimetres and as a percentage of 800 mm of rainfall.

1. Seconds in a year $= 365 \times 24 \times 3\,600 = 31\,536\,000$.
2. Volume of water $= 3.0 \times 31\,536\,000 = 94\,608\,000$ m³.
3. Area $= 1\,800 \text{ km}^2 = 1\,800 \times 1\,000\,000 = 1.8 \times 10^9$ m².
4. Depth $= 94\,608\,000 \div 1.8 \times 10^9 = 0.05256$ m $= 52.6$ mm.
5. As a percentage of rainfall: $52.56 \div 800 \times 100 = 6.6\%$. Only a small share of rain becomes river flow, which is common in dry tropical basins with high evapotranspiration.
:::

:::example{title="Why do two basins respond differently to the same storm?"}
Illustrative data: a storm delivers rain at 30 mm per hour to two basins.

| Basin | Land and soil | Infiltration capacity (mm/h) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Woodland on deep sandy soil | 60 |
| B | Paved and compacted clay | 8 |

**Compare:** in basin A the infiltration capacity (60 mm/h) is greater than the rainfall rate (30 mm/h), so all the rain can soak in and there is no overland flow. In basin B the capacity (8 mm/h) is much lower, so the excess, $30 - 8 = 22$ mm per hour, runs over the surface as infiltration-excess overland flow. **Explain:** water in B reaches the river quickly, so peak discharge is higher and the response is faster, with greater flood risk; water in A takes the slow routes of throughflow and groundwater, so the river responds slowly and has more baseflow.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a drainage basin a closed system"}
A drainage basin has inputs (precipitation) and outputs (evapotranspiration and channel flow), so it is an open system. The global water cycle is the closed one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing stemflow, throughfall and throughflow"}
Stemflow and throughfall happen on the plants, before water reaches the ground. Throughflow is movement through the soil, downslope.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the units in a water balance"}
Precipitation, evapotranspiration and runoff must all be in the same units (mm over the basin) before they are combined.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 6) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 1). *State* the inputs, outputs or stores using exact terms. *Describe* transfers in order, from interception to channel flow, and say how fast each is. *Explain* a factor in the form 'cause → process → effect on runoff'. *Compare* two basins by using the same factor in each sentence. A water-balance calculation needs the equation, the substitution and the unit (mm), and a comment on whether storage rose or fell.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A drainage basin is an open system with precipitation as the input and evapotranspiration and channel flow as outputs.
- Stores: interception, surface, soil moisture, groundwater, channel. Transfers: stemflow, throughfall, infiltration, overland flow, throughflow, percolation, groundwater flow.
- Surface flow is fast, throughflow moderate, groundwater flow slow and it sustains baseflow.
- Size, shape, drainage density, relief, soil, rock, vegetation, land use and climate affect the speed of response.
- Water balance: $P = Q + E \pm \Delta S$.
- Runoff depth (mm) = volume ÷ basin area; convert discharge using seconds in a year.
:::
