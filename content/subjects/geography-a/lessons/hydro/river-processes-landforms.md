## Introduction

At Victoria Falls, the Zambezi plunges about 100 m into a narrow gorge, and in the lower Zambezi valley the same river flows slowly across a wide, flat floodplain and splits into channels before reaching the sea. The same river does very different work in different places. Where it is steep it cuts down and carries coarse boulders; where it is gentle it swings from side to side and drops its load. Reading these changes is the key to understanding river landforms.

You should know how water reaches a river (the drainage basin lesson) and what discharge is. This lesson explains how rivers erode, transport and deposit, how flow behaves, which landforms result in each course, and how the channel changes downstream. Floods are in the next lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Long profile"}
A graph of the height of a river bed against distance from the source. It is typically concave: steep near the source and gentle near the mouth. The lowest level to which the river can cut is its base level.
:::

:::definition{term="Thalweg"}
The line of greatest depth and fastest flow along a river channel. It swings from side to side in a meandering river, close to the outer bank of each bend.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydraulic radius (channel efficiency)"}
The cross-sectional area of the channel divided by its wetted perimeter (the length of bed and banks in contact with water). A larger hydraulic radius means less friction for the amount of water, so the channel is more efficient.
:::

## Erosion, transport and deposition

**Erosion** (wearing away of the bed and banks):

- **Hydraulic action:** the force of water pushes air into cracks and compresses it; the pressure breaks the rock apart.
- **Abrasion (corrasion):** the river's load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
- **Attrition:** pieces of load collide and break; they become smaller and rounder downstream.
- **Solution (corrosion):** soluble rocks such as limestone dissolve.
- **Cavitation:** bubbles collapse in fast, turbulent water and send shock waves that weaken rock.

**Transport** (load moved by the river):

- **Traction:** large boulders roll along the bed in floods.
- **Saltation:** sand and small pebbles hop along the bed.
- **Suspension:** fine silt and clay are carried within the water.
- **Solution:** dissolved minerals move in the water.

**Deposition** occurs when the river loses energy: when velocity falls (inside of a bend, after a flood, where the gradient flattens, at the mouth) or discharge falls.

### The Hjulström curve

The Hjulström curve links the velocity of water to the size of particles. Three ideas to know: (1) **medium sand** is eroded at the lowest velocity; (2) very fine clay and silt need a *higher* velocity to erode than sand because the particles stick together, and gravel needs more because it is heavy; (3) once a particle is moving it can be carried at lower velocities than were needed to lift it, until it drops below the velocity at which it settles.

## Flow in the channel

- **Velocity** is lowest near the bed and banks, where friction is greatest, and highest near the middle just below the surface. It increases with gradient and with channel efficiency.
- **Laminar flow:** water moves in smooth parallel layers. It is rare in natural rivers.
- **Turbulent flow:** water moves in swirls and eddies. It is normal in rivers and helps to lift sediment.
- **Helicoidal flow:** a corkscrew motion in a bend. Surface water flows towards the outer bank and returns along the bed towards the inner bank, carrying sediment to the inner bank.

## Landforms along the long profile

| Course | Features of the channel | Landforms |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper** | Steep gradient, narrow shallow channel, coarse angular load, vertical erosion dominant, low efficiency | V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, rapids, waterfalls, gorges, potholes |
| **Middle** | Gentler gradient, wider and deeper channel, lateral erosion and deposition, load smaller | Meanders, river cliffs, point bars, riffles and pools |
| **Lower** | Very gentle gradient, wide deep channel, large discharge, high efficiency, deposition dominant | Floodplains, levees, bluffs, oxbow lakes, deltas |

### How landforms form

1. **Waterfall and gorge.** Where a river crosses a hard rock over softer rock, the soft rock is eroded faster. The hard rock is undercut and collapses, and the waterfall retreats upstream, leaving a deep gorge downstream. A plunge pool forms at the base. At Victoria Falls the Zambezi flows over basalt and falls into a zigzag gorge cut along cracks in the basalt, and the falls have retreated upstream over time.
2. **Meander, river cliff and point bar.** Water flows fastest on the outside of a bend (the thalweg), which erodes the bank and forms a steep **river cliff**. On the inside the water is slow, so sediment is deposited as a gently sloping **point bar**. The bend migrates sideways and downstream. Meander wavelength is roughly 10-14 times the channel width.
3. **Riffles and pools.** Along a stream, shallow **riffles** of coarse material alternate with deeper **pools** of finer material, about every 5-7 channel widths. Flow is fast and shallow over riffles and slow and deep in pools.
4. **Oxbow lake.** As two bends migrate towards each other, the neck of land between them narrows. In a flood the river cuts through the neck. Deposition seals the ends of the old bend and leaves a crescent-shaped lake.
5. **Floodplain.** Over time lateral migration of meanders and deposition of silt in floods build a wide, flat valley floor of alluvium.
6. **Levee.** In a flood, water overflowing the bank slows suddenly and drops the coarsest part of its load next to the channel. Repeated floods build natural raised banks, **levees**.
7. **Bluffs.** The steep valley sides at the edge of the floodplain where the river has cut into the valley wall.

## Changes downstream

Width, depth, discharge, velocity and channel efficiency usually increase downstream, while gradient and the size of the load decrease. The velocity increases downstream even though the gradient falls, because the channel becomes more efficient (larger hydraulic radius) and the bed less rough.

:::keyfact{title="Learn the match"}
Outer bank of a bend: fastest flow, erosion, river cliff. Inner bank: slow flow, deposition, point bar. Hard rock over soft rock: waterfall and gorge. Flood overflow: levee. Cut-off meander: oxbow lake. Upper course: vertical erosion. Lower course: deposition.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating discharge]
A cross-section of a river channel is 12 m wide, with a mean depth of 1.4 m. The mean velocity is 0.9 m/s (illustrative data). Calculate the discharge.

1. Cross-sectional area $= 12 \times 1.4 = 16.8$ m².
2. Discharge $Q = \text{area} \times \text{velocity} = 16.8 \times 0.9 = 15.12$ m³/s.
3. The discharge is about **15.1 m³/s**. If the river floods and the velocity doubles to 1.8 m/s the discharge doubles too, to 30.2 m³/s (if the area stays the same).
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing channel efficiency]
Two rectangular channels (illustrative data): the upper course is 3 m wide and 0.5 m deep; the lower course is 20 m wide and 3 m deep.

![Two channels drawn to the same scale: the upper-course channel (3 m wide, 0.5 m deep) has a hydraulic radius of 0.375 m, while the lower-course channel (20 m wide, 3 m deep) has 2.31 m, about six times more efficient because less of the water touches the bed and banks; the wetted perimeter is the thick line.](media:diagrams/channel-efficiency-hydraulic-radius.png "Figure: wetted perimeter and hydraulic radius of an upper and a lower channel (worked-example values)")

1. **Upper:** area $= 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$ m². Wetted perimeter $= 3 + 2(0.5) = 4$ m. Hydraulic radius $= 1.5 \div 4 = 0.375$ m.
2. **Lower:** area $= 20 \times 3 = 60$ m². Wetted perimeter $= 20 + 2(3) = 26$ m. Hydraulic radius $= 60 \div 26 = 2.31$ m.
3. The lower channel has a hydraulic radius about six times larger ($2.31 \div 0.375 = 6.2$). It loses less energy to friction, so it is much more efficient, and water moves faster for a given gradient.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a meander"}
A reach of river (illustrative) has a channel length of 6.9 km between two points that are 4.0 km apart in a straight line. **Calculate the sinuosity:** channel length ÷ straight distance $= 6.9 \div 4.0 = 1.73$, which is greater than 1.5, so the river is meandering. **Describe:** the channel swings from side to side across a wide floodplain. **Explain:** helicoidal flow takes water to the outer bank, where erosion forms a river cliff, and the sediment is carried across the bed to the inner bank, where it is deposited as a point bar. Over time the bends migrate downstream.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying deposition happens on the outside of a bend"}
Erosion occurs on the outside, where flow is fastest. Deposition occurs on the inside, where it is slowest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Believing that velocity decreases downstream because the gradient is lower"}
Velocity generally **increases** downstream because the channel is more efficient and has less friction, even though the gradient is gentler.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up abrasion and attrition"}
Abrasion is the load wearing away the bed and banks. Attrition is the load wearing itself down.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 6) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 1). *Name* a process or landform exactly. *Describe* a landform by its shape, position and size. *Explain* formation in numbered stages starting with the flow and the rock, and always say where erosion and deposition happen. Calculate discharge as area × velocity, and hydraulic radius as area ÷ wetted perimeter, with units. A diagram is worth marks only if it is labelled with the processes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Erosion processes: hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, solution, cavitation. Transport: traction, saltation, suspension, solution.
- Deposition occurs when velocity or discharge falls; the Hjulström curve links velocity and particle size.
- Flow is turbulent in most rivers; helicoidal flow in bends carries sediment to the inside; the thalweg is the fastest, deepest line.
- Upper course: V-valleys, waterfalls, gorges. Middle: meanders, riffles, pools. Lower: floodplains, levees, oxbow lakes.
- Downstream, discharge, width, depth, velocity and efficiency increase; gradient and load size decrease.
- Q = area × velocity; hydraulic radius = area ÷ wetted perimeter; sinuosity = channel length ÷ straight distance.
:::
