## Introduction

Floods cannot be stopped altogether, but their harm can be reduced. A farmer on the Zambezi floodplain who hears a warning on the radio can drive cattle to higher ground; a town council that refuses to allow new houses on the lowest land has cut the risk before the rain falls; an engineer who builds a dam holds back the peak of a flood. Each choice costs something and works only under certain conditions. Managing floods means choosing, combining and judging such responses.

You should know the causes and impacts of floods from the previous lesson. This lesson shows how floods are predicted and mapped, which strategies are used to reduce them, and how to judge their effectiveness. It does not repeat the causes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Recurrence interval"}
The average number of years between floods of a given size or larger, calculated from a record of annual maximum discharges. A 1-in-50-year flood has a 2% chance of being equalled or exceeded in any one year. It is a statistical average, not a timetable.
:::

:::definition{term="Hard and soft engineering"}
**Hard engineering** uses structures built in or beside the river (dams, embankments, straightened channels, spillways) to control the water. **Soft engineering** works with natural processes and land use (afforestation, wetlands, floodplain zoning, river restoration) and uses warnings and planning to reduce the harm.
:::

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Actions taken before and during a hazard to reduce its impacts (defences, planning, warnings, preparedness), as opposed to the emergency response after the event and long-term recovery.
:::

## Predicting floods

1. **Recurrence intervals.** Rank the annual maximum discharges from largest (rank $m = 1$) to smallest, with $n$ years of record. The Weibull formula is $T = (n + 1) \div m$, and the annual probability is $1 \div T$. Records of 20 to 30 years or more are needed; a short record is unreliable for rare events, and change in land use or climate may alter the pattern.
2. **Hazard maps.** Maps show areas likely to flood at stated return periods (for example the 1-in-10 and 1-in-100 flood zone), the depth of water and the location of homes, schools, clinics and roads. Planners use them to restrict building and plan evacuation routes. They depend on survey data, models and past floods, and need updating when land use changes.
3. **Gauging stations and forecasts.** Stations on rivers record stage (height), converted to discharge with a rating curve. Rainfall gauges, radar and satellites measure rain upstream. A forecast uses the rain falling and the lag time to estimate the time and height of the peak downstream. Warnings can then be sent by radio, SMS, sirens and local leaders. In southern Africa, regional agencies and national services share river and weather data because rivers cross borders. Warnings are less useful if they arrive late, are not understood or are not trusted, or if people have nowhere safe to go.

## Management strategies

### Hard engineering

| Strategy | How it works | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** | Store water in a flood and release it slowly; may also supply water and power | Reduce the peak a long way downstream; multipurpose | Very expensive; flood land and displace people; trap sediment; failure or a sudden release is dangerous; a full reservoir gives no protection |
| **Channel straightening (channelisation) and enlargement** | A shorter, smoother or larger channel carries water faster | Lowers the water level locally | Sends the flood faster downstream and may increase risk there; destroys habitats; needs maintenance |
| **Embankments and artificial levees** | Raised banks keep the water in the channel | Protect a town or farm land directly; cheaper than a dam | The river bed may rise with silt; a breach can be disastrous; water levels downstream rise; give a false sense of safety |
| **Diversion spillways and relief channels** | Channel excess water around a town or into a storage area | Protect a town at the peak | Need land and money; only for a flood size designed for |

### Soft engineering

| Strategy | How it works | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Afforestation and soil conservation** | Trees and ground cover increase interception and infiltration | Cheap, long-term and gives other benefits (wood, carbon, habitats) | Takes years to work; less effect in the largest storms; needs land and community agreement |
| **Wetland and bank conservation** | Vleis and river-bank vegetation store water and slow flow | Natural, cheap, good for wildlife and water quality | Need land; wetlands may be under pressure from farming |
| **Floodplain zoning and land-use planning** | Keep the floodplain for farms, parks and grazing; ban or limit building | Reduces exposure and damage permanently; cheap | Hard to enforce where there is a housing shortage; people may already live there |
| **Flood warnings and preparedness** | Forecasts, evacuation plans, flood-proofing, education | Save lives at low cost | Cannot protect buildings; depend on communication and trust |
| **River restoration** | Re-meandering channels and reconnecting floodplains | Slows flow and increases storage | Needs space; local results |

### Emergency response and recovery

During a flood: evacuation, rescue, shelter, clean water, food and medical care, and control of disease. Afterwards: repairing roads, bridges, power and water; rebuilding homes (preferably on safer ground, and to a higher standard); financial support, insurance and livelihood programmes; and a review of what failed. Communities that practise evacuation and have trained local volunteers respond faster. In Zimbabwe the Civil Protection Department coordinates the response, with local authorities, the army and non-government organisations; Cyclone Idai (March 2019) showed how difficult access, damaged roads and bridges and the scale of need can be.

## Judging effectiveness

Judge any strategy against these questions: Does it reduce the *hazard* (peak flow), the *exposure* (people and property in the way), or the *vulnerability* (ability to cope)? What does it cost, and who pays? Who benefits and who loses (upstream or downstream)? Is it sustainable and can it be maintained? What happens when the flood is bigger than the design? A **cost-benefit analysis** divides the value of damage avoided by the cost; a ratio above 1 shows a benefit. In practice a mix of hard structures for dense settlements, soft measures across the basin and warnings for everyone is most effective.

## A recent flood: Pakistan, 2022

In 2022 an unusually heavy monsoon season in Pakistan, preceded by a severe heatwave that increased glacier and snow melt in the north, caused very large floods along the Indus and its tributaries, especially in Sindh and Balochistan. Figures reported by the government and United Nations agencies (rounded) include more than 1,700 deaths and some 33 million people affected, with a large area of land under water for weeks. Impacts: homes, roads, bridges and crops (including cotton and rice) were destroyed; many families lived in temporary shelters; disease risks rose because of standing water. Responses: rescue and relief by the national disaster authority, the army and aid agencies, an international appeal, and later reconstruction; embankments were breached in several places. Questions about drainage, floodplain building and the effects of climate change on the monsoon followed.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating a recurrence interval]
The annual maximum discharges of a river were recorded for 19 years (illustrative data). The six largest, with their ranks, are shown below. Calculate the recurrence interval for each, using $T = (n + 1) \div m$ with $n = 19$.

| Rank $m$ | Year of record | Peak discharge (m³/s) | $T$ (years) | Annual probability (%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 530 | 20 | 5 |
| 2 | 15 | 481 | 10 | 10 |
| 3 | 7 | 455 | 6.7 | 15 |
| 4 | 1 | 412 | 5 | 20 |
| 5 | 11 | 389 | 4 | 25 |
| 6 | 5 | 367 | 3.3 | 30 |

1. For rank 1: $T = 20 \div 1 = 20$ years, so a flow of 530 m³/s or more has a 5% chance in any year.
2. For rank 3: $T = 20 \div 3 = 6.7$ years.
3. A flood of 455 m³/s therefore occurs on average about once in 6.7 years. This does *not* mean it will come exactly every 6.7 years.
:::

:::worked-solution[Chance over a period]
What is the chance of at least one 1-in-50-year flood in 10 years?

1. Annual probability $= 1 \div 50 = 0.02$, so the chance of no flood in a year is $0.98$.
2. No flood in 10 years: $0.98^{10} = 0.817$.
3. At least one flood: $1 - 0.817 = 0.183$, about **18%**.
4. The same calculation over 25 years gives about 40%. Rare floods are not as rare as the name suggests, which is why planners should not treat a 1-in-100-year event as impossible.
:::

:::worked-solution[A simple cost-benefit ratio]
A town considers an embankment costing US$12 million. It is expected to prevent flood damage worth US$18 million over its lifetime (illustrative data). The benefit-cost ratio is $18 \div 12 = 1.5$, so benefits are 1.5 times costs. A ratio above 1 supports the scheme, but the ratio ignores people displaced, habitats lost and the effect downstream, so it is only one part of the judgement.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards want prediction (recurrence intervals, hazard maps, forecasts and warnings), hard and soft engineering, and emergency response and recovery, with their effectiveness. Cambridge requires a detailed specific example of one recent flood: causes, impacts and responses (use a real, dated event and the facts you are sure of). ZIMSEC expects local mitigation measures and applies hypothesis testing to flood data, for example a Spearman test of rainfall and peak discharge.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a 1-in-100-year flood happens once every hundred years"}
It is a 1% chance in any year; two such floods can occur in successive years.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a strategy without evaluating it"}
Each strategy has a cost, a limit and an effect elsewhere on the river. Always give a strength and a weakness, and judge them for a particular place.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing mitigation with adaptation or with emergency response"}
Mitigation reduces the impacts before and during the hazard; emergency response and recovery are the actions during and after it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Recurrence interval $T = (n + 1) \div m$; annual probability is $1 \div T$; long records are needed.
- Hazard maps, gauging stations, rainfall data and lag time allow forecasts and warnings.
- Hard engineering (dams, straightening, embankments, spillways) is costly and can move the problem downstream.
- Soft engineering (afforestation, wetlands, zoning, restoration) is cheaper and sustainable but slower and partial.
- Emergency response and recovery reduce deaths and speed return to normal; preparation and warnings are low-cost.
- Judge each strategy by cost, effectiveness, who benefits, sustainability and what happens in an extreme flood.
:::
