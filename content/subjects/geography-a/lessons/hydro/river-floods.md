## Introduction

In February 2014 the Tokwe-Mukosi dam in Masvingo province was still being built when exceptionally heavy rain filled its basin faster than the planners had expected. The water rose across low-lying land and families had to be moved to higher ground. Five years later, in March 2019, Cyclone Idai brought intense rain to the Eastern Highlands, and flooding and landslides there caused serious loss of life and damage to homes, roads and bridges. These events show that a flood is not only a natural event; it becomes a hazard when it meets people, property and livelihoods.

You should know how a storm hydrograph is drawn and how channels carry water (the earlier hydrology lessons). This lesson explains why floods happen, what they do and why some places and people suffer more than others. Predicting and managing floods are covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="River flood"}
A rise in river level and discharge so that the water overflows the banks of the channel and spreads onto the floodplain. It begins when discharge exceeds the bankfull discharge, the greatest flow the channel can carry within its banks.
:::

:::definition{term="Flood hazard and vulnerability"}
A flood becomes a **hazard** when it threatens people and property. **Vulnerability** is how easily people, property and the environment are harmed by it, and how little they are able to cope and recover. Risk rises with both the size of the hazard and the vulnerability of the exposed population.
:::

:::definition{term="Flash flood"}
A rapid, short-lived flood following intense rain, with a very short lag time. It leaves little time for warning and is typical of steep, small or urban basins.
:::

## Physical causes

1. **Duration and intensity of precipitation.** Very heavy rain exceeds the infiltration capacity of the soil, so overland flow reaches the river quickly. Long periods of rain fill soil stores until the ground is saturated and every extra millimetre runs off. In Zimbabwe the main risk is the rainy season, November to March, and especially tropical cyclones and their remnants that reach the east and the Zambezi and Save basins.
2. **Antecedent conditions.** Soil already saturated by earlier rain, or baked hard in a long dry spell, produces more runoff.
3. **Snowmelt.** Rapid melting of snow floods many rivers in cold regions. It is not a cause in Zimbabwe, but you may meet it in resources and in Cambridge examples from other continents.
4. **Basin characteristics.** Steep slopes, impermeable rock and clay soils, a circular shape, a high drainage density and sparse vegetation all give a short lag time and a high peak. Basins with flat, permeable, wooded land respond more slowly.
5. **Coastal and estuary conditions.** Storm surges and high tides can prevent a river from draining to the sea, which raises levels upstream.

## Human causes

- **Deforestation.** Fewer trees means less interception and less infiltration, so more overland flow, and more soil erosion; silt from cleared slopes raises the beds of rivers and dams, reducing channel capacity.
- **Farming and irrigation.** Ploughing up and down slopes, overgrazing and compaction by cattle reduce infiltration. Bare fields after harvest are exposed to storms. Irrigation can saturate soils and canals can add water to rivers.
- **Urbanisation.** Roofs, roads and compacted ground are impermeable. Gutters and storm drains pass water quickly to rivers, so urban basins have a short lag time and a high peak. Building on floodplains also increases the amount of property at risk.
- **Wetland drainage and channel changes.** Draining vleis and wetlands removes natural storage; straightening or narrowing channels sends water downstream faster.
- **Dam failure and poor operation.** Sudden releases from a full reservoir can create a flood below the dam.
- **Climate change.** Warmer air holds more moisture, and many regions expect heavier rainfall events, which increases flood risk.

## Impacts

| | Short-term impacts | Long-term impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **People and society** | Loss of life and injury; homes and schools destroyed; people displaced; water supply and sanitation fail, so disease risk rises (for example cholera); roads and bridges cut | Rebuilding and resettlement take years; loss of savings and income; psychological stress; disrupted schooling; inequality may increase |
| **Economy** | Crops and livestock lost; shops, factories and power lines damaged; transport and trade stop | Lost productivity; high cost of repairs and insurance or aid; reduced investment in risky areas; food shortages and higher prices |
| **Environment** | Water polluted by sewage, fuel and chemicals; vegetation uprooted; erosion of banks and fields | Deposition of fertile silt on floodplains (a benefit for farming); changed channels and new landforms; soil loss; damaged ecosystems; wetlands replenished |

Floods have some benefits: silt renews soil fertility on floodplains, wetlands and fish stocks are replenished, and groundwater is recharged. These benefits support farming systems along the Zambezi and the Limpopo, where people plant on the retreating floodwater.

## Vulnerability

The same flood can cause a disaster in one place and little harm in another. Factors that increase vulnerability:

- **Scale and duration** of the flood: deeper, faster and longer floods do more damage, and flash floods leave less time to react.
- **Population density** in the floodplain: more people and property are exposed.
- **Infrastructure and building quality:** weak materials, poor drainage, low bridges and few roads out of the area increase damage and slow rescue.
- **Level of development and wealth:** poorer households have fewer resources to prepare, insure, move or rebuild; governments with limited budgets may lack forecasting, warnings and emergency services.
- **Knowledge, warning and preparation:** people who know the risk and receive timely warning can move livestock, possessions and themselves.
- **Health and age:** children, older people and people with disabilities are less able to escape and more at risk afterwards.

:::keyfact{title="Risk"}
Risk grows with hazard size and vulnerability, and falls with capacity to cope. A rich city with defences can suffer high property losses but fewer deaths; a poor settlement in a floodplain may lose everything.
:::

## Rivers and floods in Zimbabwe and southern Africa

Zimbabwe has a summer rainfall regime. River discharge is highest between about December and March and falls to a minimum in the late dry season, when many small streams stop flowing. Floods are most likely when a tropical cyclone or depression brings heavy rain to the eastern border and the Mozambique channel, or when storms fill rivers such as the Save, Runde, Mazowe and Zambezi tributaries. Low-lying land in the Zambezi valley and the lowveld of the Limpopo and Save basins floods when rivers overflow, and the flatness of the land means water spreads wide and drains slowly. In the Eastern Highlands, steep slopes and heavy rain also lead to landslides and flash floods.

The Limpopo basin shows that floods cross boundaries: heavy rain in several countries in early 2000 caused major flooding in Mozambique, and in some years flooding in the same basin affects South Africa, Botswana, Zimbabwe and Mozambique at the same time.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards want the physical and human causes, impacts, vulnerability and regime. Cambridge expects a detailed specific example of one recent flood (cause, impacts and response) in the following lesson; ZIMSEC expects local, southern African examples and the link between floods and local land use.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Runoff coefficient and urbanisation]
A basin of 25 km² receives 80 mm of rain in a storm. Before urbanisation, 0.4 million m³ of water ran off. After part of the basin was built on, 0.9 million m³ ran off in a similar storm (illustrative data). Calculate the runoff coefficient before and after.

1. Rainfall volume $= 25 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^2 \times 0.080\ \text{m} = 2.0 \times 10^{6}$ m³.
2. Before: $0.4 \div 2.0 = 0.20$, or **20%** of rainfall became runoff.
3. After: $0.9 \div 2.0 = 0.45$, or **45%**.
4. Urbanisation more than doubled the runoff coefficient, so more water reaches the river and the flood risk rises.
:::

:::worked-solution[Change in peak discharge]
The peak discharge of a river in a storm was 38 m³/s before a catchment was cleared of trees and 61 m³/s in a similar storm afterwards (illustrative data). Calculate the percentage increase.

![Flood hydrograph: before the catchment was cleared of trees the peak of 38 m3/s stayed below the bankfull discharge of 45 m3/s, but afterwards the peak of 61 m3/s, 60.5 percent higher, exceeds bankfull by 16 m3/s and the river overflows.](media:diagrams/flood-hydrograph-bankfull.png "Figure: peak discharge before and after clearance against bankfull discharge (peaks and bankfull from the worked example; curve shapes illustrative)")

1. Increase $= 61 - 38 = 23$ m³/s.
2. Percentage increase $= 23 \div 38 \times 100 = 60.5\%$.
3. The peak is about 60% higher, so a flood is more likely: if the channel can carry 45 m³/s within its banks (bankfull discharge), the earlier peak stayed in the channel but the new peak exceeds it by 16 m³/s.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining a flood in a Zimbabwean basin"}
A town on the lower reaches of a river floods in February (illustrative). **Describe:** the river overflowed its banks and water covered low-lying streets for several days. **Explain:** physical causes: 150 mm of rain fell in three days on already saturated soils after a wet January, so almost all of it ran off. Human causes: stream banks were cleared for fields upstream, and housing had spread across the floodplain. **Vulnerability:** many houses were built of weak materials, the only bridge was submerged, and warnings reached few people. Link each cause to its effect on the lag time and the peak.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating a flood as only a natural event"}
A flood is a hazard because of what is exposed to it. Include the human causes (urbanisation, deforestation) and the vulnerability of people, not just rainfall.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that rainfall is the only cause"}
Rain interacts with soil moisture, slope, rock, land use and basin shape. Weigh the importance of each cause in your answer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing impacts without linking them"}
Group impacts as short-term and long-term and as social, economic and environmental, and give a reason for each ("water supply fails, so disease risk rises").
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 6) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 1, Section A and Section B essays). *State* a cause precisely. *Describe* a flood hydrograph with figures (peak, lag time). *Explain* by linking a cause to the process (impermeable surface, so less infiltration, so more overland flow). *Assess* the relative importance of physical and human causes with a judgement; use a named example. *Suggest* reasons why impacts differ using the vulnerability factors.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A flood occurs when discharge exceeds bankfull discharge; it is a hazard when people and property are exposed.
- Physical causes: intense or prolonged rain, saturated soil, snowmelt (elsewhere) and steep, impermeable, circular basins.
- Human causes: deforestation, poor farming, urbanisation, wetland drainage and channel changes.
- Impacts are short- and long-term and social, economic and environmental; floods also bring some benefits.
- Vulnerability depends on scale, density, infrastructure, wealth, warning and knowledge.
- Zimbabwe's floods follow the summer rains and tropical cyclones, with low-lying valleys and the Eastern Highlands most at risk.
:::
