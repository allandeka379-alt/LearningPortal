## Introduction

Walk across the Namib and the first impression is of sand: great ridges of it, red in the evening light, stretching to the horizon. But the most dramatic changes come from water, not wind. A storm in the mountains can send a wall of muddy water down a dry valley within minutes, carrying boulders, cutting banks and spreading a fan of gravel across the plain, before it sinks into the ground. Over thousands of years, such rare floods have shaped the wadis, fans and pans of many deserts, while the wind has shaped the dunes and the polished ridges.

You should know the processes of weathering and wind erosion from the previous lesson, and the basic processes of rivers (erosion, transport, deposition) from the hydrology topic. This lesson applies them to arid landforms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Flash flood"}
A sudden, rapid rise of water in a normally dry channel after intense rain, which carries a heavy load of sediment and then falls quickly.
:::

:::definition{term="Sheet flood"}
A wide, shallow sheet of water, not confined to a channel, that spreads across a gently sloping surface such as a pediment after heavy rain.
:::

:::definition{term="Ephemeral stream"}
A stream that flows only for a short time after rain. Inland basins in deserts often have internal drainage (endoreic): the streams end in a basin and do not reach the sea.
:::

:::definition{term="Pediment"}
A gently sloping bedrock surface, often covered by a thin layer of debris, that lies at the foot of a mountain or escarpment, with a sharp break of slope at the junction.
:::

## Water in arid environments

**Episodic rainfall.** Rain falls rarely and unpredictably, often in intense convectional storms. The ground is dry and may be baked hard or crusted with salt, and there is little vegetation to slow the flow, so **infiltration is low and runoff is rapid**. Water collects quickly in valleys.

**Flash floods.** A narrow channel (a wadi) fills in minutes with fast, turbulent, muddy water that can carry boulders. The peak discharge is high and the flood lasts only hours. Water is lost by infiltration into the dry channel bed (**transmission losses**) and by evaporation, so the flow dies out before it reaches the sea.

**Sheet floods.** Where a flow leaves a canyon onto an open slope, it spreads out as a shallow sheet and moves material across the surface, abrading and levelling it.

**Rivers.** An **exotic river** (such as the Nile or the Colorado) rises in a wet region and flows through the desert, losing water by evaporation. Most desert streams are ephemeral.

**Processes.** Floods erode the bed and banks by abrasion and hydraulic action and transport large amounts of sediment as bedload and suspended load. Deposition is rapid when the flow loses energy and water: where a canyon opens onto a plain, where it infiltrates, or when it reaches a basin.

## Wind landforms

| Landform | Description | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Deflation hollow | A shallow depression in loose sediment | Wind removes fine material until it reaches a hard layer or the water table (a large example is the Qattara Depression in Egypt, whose floor lies well below sea level) |
| Ventifact | A stone with smooth, faceted, polished faces and sharp edges | Abrasion by sand grains from one or more directions |
| Rock pedestal | A rock with a narrow base and a broad top ("mushroom rock") | Abrasion concentrated in the lowest metre undercuts the base |
| Yardang | A streamlined ridge, tens of metres high and up to kilometres long, aligned with the wind | Abrasion and deflation carve soft rock into ridges between grooves |
| Zeugen | Table-like blocks of resistant rock on softer rock | Abrasion and weathering cut grooves through the hard cap, leaving a tabular mass |
| Barchan dune | A crescent-shaped dune with its horns pointing downwind | Limited sand supply and a single wind direction; migrates, with a gentle windward slope and a steep slip face |
| Seif (linear) dune | A long, narrow ridge, often sinuous, parallel to the wind | Winds from two directions in a narrow range, with plentiful sand |
| Star dune | A pyramid-shaped dune with several arms | Winds from many directions in turn; builds upwards, and can be very high, as in the Namib sand sea |

## Water landforms

| Landform | Description | Formation |
|---|---|---|
| Wadi (arroyo) | Steep-sided, flat-floored dry channel | Cut by flash floods; dry between rare flows |
| Pediment | A gently sloping bedrock surface at the foot of a mountain front | Sheet floods, lateral erosion by streams and weathering cause the mountain front to retreat parallel to itself |
| Mesa and butte | Flat-topped hills with steep sides; a mesa is broader than it is high, a butte is narrower | Scarp retreat in horizontal strata with a resistant cap rock; weathering and erosion shrink the mesa to a butte (Monument Valley in the USA is a well-known example) |
| Inselberg | An isolated, steep-sided residual hill (for example Uluru in Australia) | Left standing as the surrounding land is lowered; resistant rock |
| Alluvial fan | Cone-shaped deposit of gravel and sand | Where a stream leaves a mountain front, the velocity falls, water infiltrates and the load is dropped |
| Bajada | A broad apron of overlapping alluvial fans | Fans along a mountain front grow and merge |
| Playa and salt lake | A flat bed of silt and salt in a basin of internal drainage; a playa is dry most of the time | Temporary lakes form after floods and evaporate, leaving fine sediments and salts (Etosha Pan in Namibia and the Makgadikgadi Pans in Botswana are large salt pans) |

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Migration of a barchan (illustrative data)]
A barchan dune is moving downwind at 22 m per year and is now 1.1 km from a village.

1. Convert the distance: $1.1 \text{ km} = 1\,100$ m.
2. Time $= 1\,100 \div 22 = 50$ years.
3. Barchan migration is not constant, because it depends on wind strength and sand supply, so the dune could arrive sooner or later, and a village would have to plan to move or to stabilise the dune.
:::

:::worked-solution[Discharge of a flash flood (illustrative data)]
A flash flood in a wadi is 18 m wide, with a mean depth of 1.4 m and a mean velocity of 3.5 m/s.

1. Discharge $Q = \text{width} \times \text{depth} \times \text{velocity} = 18 \times 1.4 \times 3.5 = 88.2$ m³/s.
2. If this flow lasted 2 hours (7 200 s), the volume would be $88.2 \times 7\,200 = 635\,040$ m³.
3. A normal discharge of 0 m³/s means that the flood changes the channel from dry to destructive in minutes, which is why flash floods are so dangerous.
:::

:::worked-solution[Slope of a pediment (illustrative data)]
A pediment falls 14 m over a horizontal distance of 700 m.

1. Gradient $= 14 \div 700 = 0.02$, which is 2%.
2. Angle $= \tan^{-1}(0.02) = 1.1°$.
3. This gentle slope, with a sharp break of slope where it meets the mountain, is typical of a pediment.
:::

:::example{title="Identifying landforms from a field description"}
A traverse across a desert basin finds: (1) a steep mountain front; (2) a gently sloping bare rock surface with a thin cover of gravel, 1° towards the basin; (3) a fan of gravel and sand 3 km across, spreading from the mouth of a canyon; (4) a flat white surface of dried mud and salt in the lowest part. **Identify:** (2) a pediment, (3) an alluvial fan, (4) a playa. **Explain (3):** a flash flood leaves the canyon, spreads out, loses velocity and water by infiltration, and drops its load, building the fan.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that deserts are shaped only by wind"}
Floods are usually the main agent of erosion and transport of coarse material, and wind is more important for sand and dust. Give both, with examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the shape of a barchan"}
The horns of a barchan point **downwind**, and the steep slip face is on the lee side. A star dune has arms in several directions, and a seif is a long ridge.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a pediment as a pile of sediment"}
A pediment is mainly an erosional surface of bedrock with a thin cover of debris. An alluvial fan, by contrast, is a depositional landform.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Episodic rain on dry, crusted, sparsely vegetated ground produces rapid runoff, flash floods and sheet floods; flows die out by infiltration and evaporation.
- Wind landforms: deflation hollows, ventifacts, rock pedestals, yardangs and zeugen (erosional); barchan, seif and star dunes (depositional).
- Water landforms: wadis, pediments, mesas, buttes and inselbergs (erosional); alluvial fans, bajadas and playas or salt lakes (depositional).
- Barchans have horns downwind, seifs are linear, and stars have arms in many directions.
- Discharge Q = width × depth × velocity; gradient = vertical ÷ horizontal; time = distance ÷ rate.
:::
