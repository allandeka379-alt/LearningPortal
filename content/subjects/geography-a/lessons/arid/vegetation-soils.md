## Introduction

In the Namib, a plant called *Welwitschia* grows only two long strap-shaped leaves, which split into ribbons over the years, yet it can live for hundreds of years by absorbing the fog that rolls in from the Atlantic. After a rare rainstorm in the same area the gravel plains turn green for a few weeks with annual grasses and flowers that then disappear. These are two answers to the same problem: how to live where water is scarce, the soil is poor and the temperature is extreme. Plants and soils in deserts look sparse, but they are exactly adapted to their conditions.

You should know the climate causes of aridity from the first lesson of this option, and from biogeography the ideas of biomass, productivity and nutrient cycling (the Gersmehl diagram). Desertification is in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Xerophyte"}
A plant adapted to survive in dry conditions, by storing water, reducing water loss or obtaining water from deep or wide-ranging roots.
:::

:::definition{term="Physical drought and physiological drought"}
**Physical drought** is a real shortage of water in the soil. **Physiological drought** is when water is present but plants cannot take it up, for example because the soil is so salty that the roots cannot absorb water.
:::

:::definition{term="Solonchak"}
A saline soil with a high concentration of soluble salts, often with a white salt crust at the surface. The pH is usually neutral to slightly alkaline.
:::

:::definition{term="Solonetz"}
A sodic soil in which sodium ions are a high proportion of the exchangeable ions. The soil is strongly alkaline, the clay is dispersed and the B horizon is dense, with a columnar structure.
:::

## Vegetation

**Low biomass and productivity.** Water is the limit on growth. Typical textbook estimates of net primary productivity (rounded) are about 2 200 g/m² per year for a tropical rainforest and about 90 g/m² per year for a desert scrub. Plants are small and widely spaced, the ground is mostly bare, and there is little litter. Biodiversity per unit area is low, but the plants and animals that survive are specialised, and some deserts (for example the Namib) have many species found nowhere else.

**Nutrient cycling.** On a Gersmehl diagram all the stores are small, and the flows are slow. Litter decomposes slowly because it is dry, but there is little leaching, so the soil keeps its nutrients and the stores in the soil are the largest. Growth comes in short pulses after rain.

**Adaptations to drought.**

| Strategy | Adaptation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Storing water | Fleshy stems or leaves (succulents); stomata open at night (CAM photosynthesis) | Cacti, aloes, *Euphorbia* |
| Reaching water | Very deep tap roots to groundwater; wide, shallow roots that catch light rain | Acacia (camelthorn); cacti |
| Reducing water loss | Small or no leaves, spines, a thick waxy cuticle, hairs, leaves that fold, leaf loss in the dry season | Acacias, many shrubs |
| Escaping drought | Ephemerals: annuals that germinate, flower and seed within weeks of rain, and whose seeds lie dormant for years | Desert annuals |
| Using fog and dew | Leaves and roots that absorb moisture | *Welwitschia* in the Namib |
| Tolerating salt | Halophytes with salt glands or tolerance of high salt | Saltbush |

**Adaptations to extreme temperatures.** Pale or hairy surfaces reflect radiation; small leaves lose heat quickly; shade from spines and arrangement of leaves reduces heating; at night, tissues tolerate cool conditions.

**Effects of climate and people.** The cover of plants rises and falls with rainfall, so the vegetation is sparse in arid areas and denser on the semi-arid margins. People change the vegetation by overgrazing, by cutting trees for fuelwood, by trampling, by irrigation, which causes salinisation, and by introducing invasive plants such as mesquite (*Prosopis*) in parts of southern Africa, which uses large amounts of groundwater.

## Soils

Desert soils (aridisols) are **thin, pale, stony and low in organic matter**, because there is little vegetation and decay is slow. Weathering is slow, so the soils are immature. Water movement is mainly **upwards**, by capillary action as water evaporates, so salts and calcium carbonate accumulate near the surface (calcification and salinisation) rather than being leached away.

### Solonchak (saline soil)

1. Salts are present in groundwater or are released by weathering.
2. Evaporation exceeds rainfall, so water is drawn upward by capillary rise to the surface.
3. The water evaporates and leaves salts as a white crust or as accumulations near the surface.
4. The soil develops in depressions and on low plains where the water table is shallow, or under irrigation with poor drainage.

Plants find it hard to grow because of the physiological drought. A solonchak has a distinct salt layer, a pH that is usually neutral to slightly alkaline and a granular or loose surface.

### Solonetz (sodic soil)

1. Sodium is the dominant ion held on the clay particles, often after the other salts are leached or removed.
2. The sodium makes the clay particles disperse (they repel each other), and the clay moves downwards.
3. A dense, hard B horizon builds up with a **columnar or prismatic structure** and rounded tops, while the A horizon above is pale and thin.
4. The pH is strongly alkaline (above about 8.5). The soil is hard when dry and sticky and impermeable when wet, so infiltration is poor and roots struggle.

| Feature | Solonchak | Solonetz |
|---|---|---|
| Main problem | Too much soluble salt | Too much sodium on the clay |
| Surface | White salt crust | Pale, thin A horizon, often bare |
| Structure | Granular or loose | Dense columnar B horizon |
| pH | Neutral to slightly alkaline | Strongly alkaline |
| Where | Basins, low plains, irrigated land | Margins of saline areas, after leaching |

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Comparing productivity (illustrative, rounded textbook values)]
Net primary productivity: rainforest about 2 200 g/m² per year; desert scrub about 90 g/m² per year.

1. Ratio $= 2\,200 \div 90 = 24.4$.
2. A rainforest produces about 24 times as much new plant material per square metre as a desert scrub does.
3. Each year, 100 m² of desert scrub produces $90 \times 100 = 9\,000$ g = 9 kg of new biomass, which shows how little food there is for animals.
:::

:::worked-solution[Salt added by irrigation (illustrative data)]
Irrigation water contains 0.8 kg of salt per m³ (0.8 g per litre). A farmer applies 7 500 m³ per hectare each year, and drainage removes 2 000 kg of salt per hectare each year.

1. Salt added $= 0.8 \times 7\,500 = 6\,000$ kg per hectare per year.
2. Net gain $= 6\,000 - 2\,000 = 4\,000$ kg per hectare per year, which is 4 t per hectare.
3. In 10 years the soil gains $4 \times 10 = 40$ t per hectare, so salinisation (and a solonchak) will develop unless the drainage is improved.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a soil description (illustrative data)"}
A soil pit in a dry basin shows: a white crust 2 cm thick; pH 8.0; grey-brown loose silt to 30 cm; below it, a layer with white veins of gypsum; the water table at 1.5 m. **Identify:** a solonchak. **Explain:** the shallow water table supplies saline water, which rises by capillary action and evaporates at the surface, leaving the crust. **Suggest** an effect on plants: only halophytes will survive, and others will suffer physiological drought.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that desert plants never need water"}
They need water, but they have ways of getting it, storing it or avoiding drought. Describe the adaptation and its function.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing solonchak and solonetz"}
A solonchak is salty (salt crust, neutral to slightly alkaline), and a solonetz is sodic (high sodium, strongly alkaline, columnar B horizon). Name the problem for each.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing water movement in desert soils as downwards"}
Most movement is upwards by capillary rise and evaporation, which is why salts accumulate near the surface.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Productivity and biomass are low because of lack of water; stores and flows of nutrients are small, and growth comes in pulses after rain.
- Plants cope by storing water, reaching deep or wide for it, reducing loss, escaping drought as ephemerals, using fog, and tolerating salt.
- Physical drought is a lack of water; physiological drought is water that plants cannot absorb, for example in salty soil.
- Desert soils are thin, pale and low in humus; capillary rise brings salts and calcium carbonate to the surface.
- A solonchak is saline, with a salt crust; a solonetz is sodic and alkaline, with a columnar B horizon.
- Ratio = value ÷ value; salt added = concentration × volume of water.
:::
