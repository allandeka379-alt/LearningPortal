## Introduction

Southern Zimbabwe, around Beitbridge and the Limpopo valley, receives so little rain that cattle and goats are more reliable than crops. A few hundred kilometres to the west the Kalahari is drier still, and on the Atlantic coast the Namib is one of the driest deserts on Earth, even though the cold sea lies just beside it. About a third of the world's land is arid or semi-arid, and many millions of people live in these areas. The reasons for aridity are not the same everywhere, and examiners expect you to separate them.

You should already know the global circulation: the ITCZ, the Hadley cell and the subtropical high-pressure belts, and how clouds form when air rises and cools. This lesson describes the climate and explains its causes. Weathering, landforms, plants and soils, and the challenges of arid environments follow in the next four lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Arid and semi-arid"}
An **arid** area receives very little rain, typically less than about 250 mm a year, and a **semi-arid** area typically about 250–500 mm. Rainfall is low compared with the potential loss of water by evaporation and plant use (potential evapotranspiration). The boundaries vary between authors.
:::

:::definition{term="Aridity index"}
The ratio of annual precipitation (P) to potential evapotranspiration (PET). Low values mean dry conditions: a common scheme gives hyper-arid below 0.05, arid 0.05–0.20 and semi-arid 0.20–0.50.
:::

:::definition{term="Continentality"}
The degree to which a place is remote from the sea, so that it has a large temperature range and little moisture from marine air.
:::

:::definition{term="Rain shadow"}
A dry area on the sheltered (leeward) side of a mountain range, where descending air warms and holds its moisture.
:::

## Distribution

Hot arid and semi-arid environments lie mainly between about 15° and 35° north and south of the equator, beneath the subtropical high-pressure belts. The largest are the **Sahara** and the **Arabian Desert**, the deserts of Iran and the **Thar** in Asia, the interior of **Australia**, the Mojave, Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts in North America, the **Namib** and **Kalahari** in southern Africa, and the **Atacama** in South America. Semi-arid belts, such as the **Sahel** south of the Sahara and the lowveld of south-western Zimbabwe, lie on the margins and have a short wet season. Many deserts lie on the western sides of continents, where cold ocean currents flow.

## Climate

| Feature | Hot arid and semi-arid environments |
|---|---|
| Rainfall | Low (under about 250 mm in arid and about 250–500 mm in semi-arid areas), very variable from year to year, and often in short, intense convectional storms |
| Evaporation | Potential evaporation far exceeds rainfall, so there is a permanent water deficit |
| Temperature | Very high in the day in summer, often above 40 °C; the highest reliably recorded air temperature, 56.7 °C, was at Death Valley in California in 1913 |
| Diurnal range | Large, often 20–30 °C, because clear skies and dry air allow strong heating by day and rapid heat loss at night |
| Seasonal range | Large in the subtropical interior; smaller near cold coasts such as the Namib |
| Cloud and humidity | Little cloud and low humidity, so sunshine hours are high |
| Wind | Strong, gusty winds in daytime when the heated surface sets up convection and mixes in winds from aloft; a lack of vegetation means that there is little friction, so winds have high energy |

## Causes of aridity

1. **Subtropical high pressure.** Air that rises at the ITCZ moves poleward, cools and sinks at about 30° as part of the Hadley cell. Sinking air is compressed and warms, so its relative humidity falls and clouds dissolve. Winds blow out of the high-pressure centre, so moist air does not enter. This is the main cause of the Sahara, Arabian and Australian deserts.
2. **Continentality.** In the middle of a large continent, air masses lose moisture on the way as rain falls near the coast. By the time they arrive they are dry. The central Sahara and Central Asia (the Gobi) are examples.
3. **Cold ocean currents.** A cold current, such as the **Benguela** current along the coast of Namibia, the **Humboldt (Peru)** current off Chile and Peru, the **Canary** current off north-west Africa and the **West Australian** current, cools the air above it. The cool air is dense and stable, so it does not rise, and so there is no rain, although coastal fog forms. The Namib and the Atacama are coastal deserts for this reason.
4. **Rain shadow.** Moist air forced over a mountain range loses its moisture on the windward side as rain; on the leeward side it descends, warms and gives little rain. Patagonia lies in the rain shadow of the Andes, and the Great Basin of North America in that of the Sierra Nevada.
5. **Offshore winds.** Where the prevailing winds, such as the trade winds on western coasts, blow from the land, they bring dry air.

Most deserts are the result of **more than one cause**: for example, the Namib lies beneath the subtropical high and beside the cold Benguela current, and the Atacama has in addition a rain shadow from the Andes.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Aridity index (illustrative data)]
Station A has an annual rainfall of 180 mm and a potential evapotranspiration of 2 100 mm. Station B has 420 mm and 1 500 mm.

1. Station A: $P \div PET = 180 \div 2\,100 = 0.086$, which is 0.09 to two decimal places and falls in the **arid** class (0.05–0.20).
2. Station B: $420 \div 1\,500 = 0.28$, which is **semi-arid** (0.20–0.50).
3. The water deficit at A is $2\,100 - 180 = 1\,920$ mm a year, which shows how much more water could be lost than is received.
:::

:::worked-solution[Variability of annual rainfall (illustrative data)]
Annual rainfall (mm) at a semi-arid station over ten years was: 142, 96, 215, 63, 188, 121, 270, 77, 154, 134.

1. Mean $= 1\,460 \div 10 = 146$ mm.
2. Sample standard deviation $s = 64.0$ mm (using $n - 1$).
3. Coefficient of variation $= s \div \text{mean} \times 100 = 64.0 \div 146 \times 100 = 43.9\%$.
4. The range is $270 - 63 = 207$ mm. A coefficient of variation this high means that rainfall is unreliable: a year can bring less than half or nearly twice the mean, which makes farming without irrigation risky.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a desert temperature record"}
A weather station in a hot desert records, on one day in July, a maximum of 41.5 °C and a minimum of 14.0 °C. **Calculate:** the diurnal range is $41.5 - 14.0 = 27.5$ °C. **Explain:** the sky is clear and the air is dry, so insolation heats the ground strongly by day, and almost no cloud or water vapour traps heat at night, so long-wave radiation escapes rapidly.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that deserts are always hot and sandy"}
Many are rocky or stony, some are cool (the Atacama and Namib coasts), and some have cold winters (the Gobi). Only about a fifth of the Sahara is sand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving one cause for every desert"}
Subtropical high pressure is not the cause of the Atacama alone, nor of Patagonia. Choose the causes that fit the place and use more than one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining rain shadow as a lack of wind"}
It is caused by moisture loss on the windward side and warming of descending air on the leeward side.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 10). *Describe* a climate graph with figures, the range and a total. *Explain* the causes with a chain of reasoning (air sinks, warms by compression, relative humidity falls, cloud does not form). For *assess the importance of different causes*, name a desert, give two or more causes and say which is most important there, with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hot arid areas lie at about 15°–35° latitude: arid receives under about 250 mm, semi-arid about 250–500 mm.
- Rainfall is low, variable and intense; evaporation exceeds rainfall; the diurnal range is large (often 20–30 °C).
- Causes: subtropical high pressure (sinking air), continentality, cold ocean currents, rain shadow and offshore winds.
- Most deserts have more than one cause, for example the Namib (subtropical high plus the Benguela current).
- Aridity index = P ÷ PET; coefficient of variation = standard deviation ÷ mean × 100.
- Strong winds with high energy occur because the surface is hot and has little vegetation to slow the air.
:::
