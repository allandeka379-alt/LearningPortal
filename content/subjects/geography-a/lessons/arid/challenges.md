## Introduction

In the arid and semi-arid parts of Africa, farmers and herders have always faced drought, and over centuries they developed ways of living with it: moving livestock between pastures, planting drought-resistant crops and keeping trees in their fields. When populations grow, land is divided, herds increase, rainfall fails for several years and money is short, the old balance can break down. Drylands cover roughly two-fifths of the world's land (UNCCD, rounded) and are home to well over two billion people. In southern Zimbabwe, around Beitbridge and Mwenezi, families depend on cattle and goats and suffer when rain fails, as it did in the El Niño seasons of 2015–16 and 2023–24.

You should know the causes of aridity, the soils and the vegetation of arid areas. This lesson looks at how and why these environments are changing and at the success of attempts to manage them. It requires a **detailed specific example**, and here we use the Sahel.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Desertification"}
Land degradation in arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid areas, resulting from many factors, including climatic variations and human activities, so that the land loses its biological and economic productivity. It does not mean the spread of an existing desert.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil salinisation"}
The build-up of soluble salts in the soil, most often after irrigation of poorly drained land in a dry climate, so that crops cannot grow.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The number of people or livestock that an area can support indefinitely without damage to the land.
:::

:::definition{term="Overgrazing"}
Grazing by more animals, or for longer, than the vegetation can sustain, so that the plant cover is reduced and not allowed to recover.
:::

## Causes and challenges

**Population pressure and land-use change.** More people need more food, fuel and land. Cultivation spreads onto marginal land that receives little and unreliable rainfall; fallow periods shorten; herds grow; and trees are cut for fuel and building. In many dryland countries poverty and weak land rights discourage people from investing in the long term.

**Desertification.** Physical and human factors work together:

| Physical factors | Human factors |
|---|---|
| Drought and variable rainfall | Overgrazing by livestock |
| Intense storms on bare soil (water erosion) | Overcultivation and ploughing of marginal land |
| Strong winds (wind erosion) | Removal of trees and shrubs for fuelwood and charcoal |
| High temperatures and evaporation | Poor irrigation, leading to salinisation |
| Soils that are thin and easily eroded | Population growth, poverty, conflict and weak land tenure |

The result is a **cycle**: less cover means more erosion, so that soil is lost, so that less grows, so there is less cover. The role of vegetation loss in reducing rainfall by increasing the reflection of sunlight is debated.

**Salinisation.** In irrigated dry areas, water is applied to the soil, some evaporates, and the salt is left behind. If drainage is poor and the water table rises, capillary rise brings more salt to the surface. The Aral Sea region of Central Asia shows the scale of the problem: from the 1960s, rivers feeding the sea were diverted to irrigate cotton, the sea shrank to a fraction of its former area, and the exposed salty bed produced harmful dust.

**Vegetation degradation in semi-arid areas.** Grasses are replaced by bush and by plants that animals avoid, trees are lost, and the cover that remains cannot protect the soil.

**Climate change.** Warming increases evaporation and heat stress; rainfall patterns may change and become more variable (projections for Africa vary by region); droughts may become more frequent or severe; shorter and less reliable growing seasons threaten **crops and livestock**; water becomes scarcer. People with little capital are the most vulnerable.

## Detailed specific example: the Sahel

**Location.** The Sahel is the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara, from Senegal and Mauritania in the west to Sudan and Eritrea in the east, with annual rainfall of roughly 200–600 mm, falling in one wet season. The ITCZ migration governs the rains.

**Issues.**

- Severe **droughts in 1968–73 and 1983–85** killed livestock, caused famine and forced people to move.
- Rapid **population growth** increased demand for fuelwood, cropland and grazing.
- Clearing of trees, overgrazing and cultivation of marginal land exposed the soil to wind and water erosion.

**Management strategies.**

- **Farmer-managed natural regeneration (FMNR)** in Niger: from the 1980s, farmers protected and pruned the shoots that grow from tree stumps and roots in their fields instead of clearing them. Tree cover increased over several million hectares (estimates differ; to be verified), giving fuelwood, fodder and shade, and raising yields.
- **Zaï pits and stone lines** in Burkina Faso: small planting pits filled with manure, and lines of stones along the contours, which trap water and soil. These were revived by farmers in the 1980s.
- **The Great Green Wall**, launched in 2007 by the African Union: first imagined as a belt of trees across Africa, it is now a programme of land restoration of many kinds. Progress has been slower than planned and funding uneven.

**Evaluation.** The most successful measures have been **low-cost and farmer-led** (FMNR, zaï, stone lines). They are cheap, use local knowledge, and give farmers a direct benefit and the incentive to continue, but they need secure rights to land and trees. Large top-down programmes need more money and organisation, and tree planting alone has often failed where seedlings died. Satellite images show that much of the Sahel has become greener since the mid-1980s, partly because rainfall recovered and partly because of these management efforts, so credit is hard to assign. The Sahel is **still vulnerable** to drought, rapid population growth and conflict.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Overstocking (illustrative data)]
A rangeland of 400 km² has a carrying capacity of one livestock unit (LU) per 8 ha. It carries 7 500 LU.

1. Area $= 400 \times 100 = 40\,000$ ha.
2. Carrying capacity $= 40\,000 \div 8 = 5\,000$ LU.
3. Actual stocking as a percentage of capacity $= 7\,500 \div 5\,000 \times 100 = 150\%$, so the range is overstocked by 50%.
4. Overgrazing at this level reduces cover, so that the carrying capacity falls further.
:::

:::worked-solution[Land loss by degradation (illustrative data)]
A district of 12 000 km² is losing 180 km² a year to severe degradation.

1. Loss in 25 years $= 180 \times 25 = 4\,500$ km².
2. Share of the district $= 4\,500 \div 12\,000 \times 100 = 37.5\%$.
3. This assumes a constant rate; if the rate increases as the cover falls, the loss would be greater.
:::

:::worked-solution[Effect of a management technique on yield (illustrative data)]
Sorghum yields on a plot with zaï pits average 0.9 t/ha, compared with 0.35 t/ha on a similar plot without pits.

1. Increase $= 0.9 - 0.35 = 0.55$ t/ha.
2. Percentage increase $= 0.55 \div 0.35 \times 100 = 157\%$.
3. Such results explain why farmers adopt the technique, although pits need labour and manure.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a strategy from a description"}
A project plants trees in a line across a village's land, but fencing is poor and goats eat the seedlings; only 3 in 10 trees survive after two years. In a neighbouring village, farmers protect natural regrowth in their fields and 7 in 10 fields now have trees. **Assess:** natural regeneration is more successful than planting because it is cheaper, uses roots that are already established, and farmers have a direct interest in protecting the trees. The planting scheme could be improved by involving the community and protecting seedlings.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that deserts are spreading like a tide"}
Desertification is land degradation in dry areas, caused by climate and people together. It does not mean that the Sahara is marching south in a line.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming only local people"}
Population growth, poverty, weak land rights, markets, conflict and climate all contribute. Include physical and human causes, and larger-scale causes such as government policy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a strategy as a success or failure without evidence"}
Give dates, places and results, and weigh cost, who benefits and whether the benefit lasts.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 10), often as a 20-mark essay needing a detailed specific example. Organise the answer as issues (physical and human, with dates), strategies (named, with where and when), evaluation (successes, limits, who benefits, sustainability) and a judgement. Use a table or short points in planning, but write in connected prose. For climate change, link a change in temperature or rainfall to a specific effect on crops or livestock.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Population pressure, overgrazing, cultivation of marginal land, fuelwood cutting and poor irrigation cause degradation, salinisation and loss of vegetation in drylands.
- Desertification is land degradation caused by climate and people together, in a cycle of loss of cover and erosion.
- Climate change may increase evaporation, heat and drought, and threaten crops and livestock.
- The Sahel suffered droughts in 1968–73 and 1983–85; strategies include farmer-managed natural regeneration, zaï pits and stone lines, and the Great Green Wall.
- Low-cost, farmer-led measures have been most successful; large projects need funds, rights and community involvement.
- Stocking rate = animals ÷ capacity × 100; loss = rate × time; percentage change = change ÷ original × 100.
:::
