## Introduction

In the early morning in the Namib the rock surface can be cold and damp with dew, and by midday it is too hot to touch. By night it cools again. Salt carried up from the ground dries in the rock pores. Over thousands of years, such small changes can split boulders, break granite into grains and cover the rock in a honeycomb of hollows. Once the surface is loosened, the wind finds it: fine grains are lifted and carried away, and sand is blasted against exposed rocks. Weathering prepares the material and the wind moves it, so the two are best studied together.

You should know the general types of weathering (physical, chemical, biological) and the effect of wind on the surface. This lesson covers the processes only; the landforms that result come in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Thermal fracture (insolation weathering)"}
The cracking of rock caused by repeated expansion and contraction as it heats by day and cools at night. Different minerals expand at different rates, which adds stress.
:::

:::definition{term="Exfoliation"}
The peeling of curved sheets from the surface of a rock, like the skin of an onion, caused by thermal stress, pressure release and chemical weathering.
:::

:::definition{term="Deflation"}
The removal of loose, fine particles from a dry surface by the wind, which lowers the surface and leaves coarser material behind.
:::

:::definition{term="Abrasion (wind)"}
The wearing of rock surfaces by sand grains carried by the wind, in the way that sandpaper wears wood.
:::

## Weathering in arid environments

| Process | How it works | Where and when it is important |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal fracture | Daily heating and cooling cause expansion and contraction of minerals; stress builds up and cracks form | Bare rock with a large diurnal range; laboratory tests in the 1930s suggested that it works best when the rock is also damp, for example from dew |
| Exfoliation | Outer layers expand and peel off in sheets; helped by pressure release as overlying rock is removed and by chemical weathering | Granite domes and boulders |
| Freeze–thaw | Water in cracks freezes at night and expands by about 9%, wedging the rock apart | High deserts and cold deserts (Gobi, Atacama and Andean highlands) and in cold winter nights |
| Salt crystal growth (haloclasty) | Salty water enters pores and evaporates, leaving crystals that grow and exert pressure; some salts also expand when they take up water | Probably the most effective process in many deserts, especially near coasts and in basins with salty groundwater |
| Hydrolysis | Water reacts with silicate minerals such as feldspar to form clay, weakening the rock | Slow, because water is scarce, but takes place wherever dew or rain wet the rock |
| Carbonation | Rain or dew containing dissolved carbon dioxide dissolves limestone | Limited, but occurs in the rare periods of wetness |

**Dew and fog** are an important source of moisture in coastal deserts such as the Namib and make chemical and salt weathering possible. Weathering in deserts is slow compared with humid areas, but it is not absent.

The surface is often reshaped: the weathered **fine material is removed** and the surface left is a layer of coarse stones (a stony **desert pavement**). Minerals in dew can form a thin dark coating on the rock (**desert varnish**). Salt weathering can cut hollows (**honeycomb weathering** or tafoni) in cliffs and in building stone.

## Wind processes

**Conditions.** Wind is a powerful agent where the surface is dry, loose and has little vegetation. Wind must exceed a **threshold speed** to move grains (about 4–5 m/s for fine sand, but varying with grain size, moisture and surface roughness). Very fine particles (silt and clay) are cohesive, so they resist movement until the wind is strong, but once lifted they stay in the air.

**Erosion.**

1. **Deflation** removes loose grains and dust from the surface. Over time, this lowers the surface and leaves a pavement of stones (a *lag deposit*), and in places hollows called deflation hollows form. Dust from the Bodélé Depression in Chad is blown across the Atlantic.
2. **Abrasion** is the sandblasting of rock surfaces. Because sand grains bounce close to the ground, abrasion is greatest in the lowest metre or so, and it undercuts rocks and polishes and grooves them.

**Transport.** Material of different sizes moves in different ways:

| Process | Particle size | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Traction (surface creep) | Coarse sand and fine gravel | Grains roll or slide along the surface when they are hit by saltating grains |
| Saltation | Sand (about 0.06–2 mm), mainly fine and medium sand | Grains bounce along the surface in short hops, normally a few centimetres to about a metre high; it carries most of the sand |
| Suspension | Silt and clay (finer than about 0.06 mm, dust) | Fine particles carried high in the air, over hundreds or thousands of kilometres |

The amount of sand moved increases rapidly with wind speed. According to Bagnold's relationship, the rate of transport is proportional to the **cube of the wind (shear) speed**, so a doubling of speed gives eight times as much sand movement.

**Deposition.** Wind drops its load when speed falls, when it meets an obstacle (a rock, a plant, a slope), when the surface becomes wet, or when it enters a sheltered place. Sand is deposited as sheets and dunes, and fine dust may be deposited far away (for example, Saharan dust falls on the Caribbean and the Amazon basin) and builds up as loess.

## Human effects

Overgrazing, ploughing and removal of vegetation expose the soil and increase deflation. In the 1930s, a drought and ploughing of the Great Plains in the United States produced the "Dust Bowl", in which topsoil was blown away in huge dust storms. In semi-arid Africa, loss of ground cover has the same effect.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Effect of wind speed on sand transport (illustrative data)]
The rate of sand transport is proportional to the cube of the shear velocity $u_*$. A wind has $u_* = 0.3$ m/s and then increases to $u_* = 0.6$ m/s.

1. Ratio of speeds $= 0.6 \div 0.3 = 2$.
2. Ratio of transport $= 2^3 = 8$.
3. A doubling of the wind speed gives **eight times** as much sand movement, which is why strong storms move most of the sand.
:::

:::worked-solution[Rate of lowering by deflation (illustrative data)]
A deflation hollow is 2.4 m deeper than the surrounding surface and has taken 30 years to form.

1. Rate $= 2.4 \div 30 = 0.08$ m per year, which is 8 cm per year.
2. At that rate, it would take $5 \div 0.08 = 62.5$ years to deepen a further 5 m. In practice the rate would slow as the hollow reaches a water table or a hard layer, so a constant rate is a simplification.
:::

:::worked-solution[Salt weathering and rock retreat (illustrative data)]
Salt weathering lowers a cliff face by an average of 0.8 mm per year.

1. In 250 years the face retreats by $0.8 \times 250 = 200$ mm, which is 0.2 m.
2. Such rates are small compared with those on rivers or coasts, but they act on every surface, so salt weathering is important in arid areas.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining honeycomb weathering from a description"}
A photograph taken in a coastal desert shows a sandstone cliff face covered in rounded hollows, each 5–15 cm across, separated by thin ridges. White crusts of salt line the hollows. **Identify:** honeycomb weathering (tafoni). **Explain:** salty water, from sea spray or fog, enters the rock and evaporates in the heat; crystals grow in the pores and force grains apart; the hollows are enlarged because weathering is faster in the damp, sheltered hollow than on the ridge.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that only thermal fracture happens in deserts"}
Salt crystal growth is probably more effective, and chemical processes occur wherever dew or rain wets the rock. Give several processes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing deflation with abrasion"}
Deflation is the removal of loose material. Abrasion is erosion of rock by grains carried in the wind. Name the process, then the result.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing saltation as grains floating in the air"}
Saltating grains bounce close to the surface. Only dust is carried in suspension.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Arid weathering includes thermal fracture, exfoliation, freeze–thaw, salt crystal growth, hydrolysis and carbonation; salt and moisture from dew are important.
- Deflation removes loose fine material and leaves a stony pavement; abrasion sandblasts rock mainly in the lowest metre.
- Wind moves material by traction (coarse), saltation (sand, most of the load) and suspension (dust).
- Sand transport rises with the cube of wind speed: doubling the speed gives eight times the movement.
- Deposition occurs when wind speed falls, at obstacles or on damp surfaces.
- Rate = distance ÷ time; ratio of transport = (ratio of speeds)³.
:::
