## Introduction

Cambridge asks for two things in this part of the disease option: a comparison of how two contrasting countries responded to one pandemic since 2000, and a critical evaluation of a global programme to eradicate a disease. This lesson offers one pair of countries (New Zealand and Zimbabwe, responding to COVID-19) and one programme (the Global Polio Eradication Initiative). You may use other examples if your facts are secure and dated. In each case the question is not just what happened, but why the outcomes were different, and how far success was due to policy, resources, geography or luck.

You should know the general ideas of monitoring and response (previous lesson). The lesson does not discuss the biology of the virus. Where numbers are uncertain they are described generally.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Elimination, eradication and containment"}
Elimination is the reduction of cases of a disease to zero in a defined area (a country or region), with continued action to prevent its return. Eradication is the permanent reduction to zero of the worldwide incidence of the disease, so that control measures are no longer needed. Containment aims to limit spread without necessarily reaching zero (mitigation or suppression).
:::

:::definition{term="Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs)"}
Measures other than vaccines and drugs that reduce transmission: border controls, quarantine, testing and tracing, lockdowns, school closures, masks, hand-washing and physical distancing.
:::

## Two contrasting responses to COVID-19, 2020–22

COVID-19, caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, was declared a pandemic by the WHO in March 2020. New Zealand is a high-income island country with a small population and a compact health system. Zimbabwe is a lower-middle-income, landlocked country with a large informal economy and a health system under strain.

| Feature | New Zealand | Zimbabwe |
|---|---|---|
| First case | 28 February 2020 | 20 March 2020 (Victoria Falls) |
| Early border measures | Entry barred to most non-residents from 19 March 2020; from April all returning travellers had to spend 14 days in managed isolation | Borders closed to most travellers in March 2020; returnees quarantined |
| Lockdown | Level 4 national lockdown from 25 March 2020; eased in stages in April–June | National lockdown from 30 March 2020, with schools closed; eased in stages |
| Strategy | Elimination: stamp out cases quickly and keep out new ones; community transmission was declared over in June 2020 | Suppression of spread with restrictions, public messaging and later vaccination, with limited testing and tracing capacity |
| Economic support | Government wage subsidy scheme and support for businesses | Limited state support for informal workers, so many people had to work during restrictions |
| Vaccination | Began in February 2021 with border workers; wider public rollout from mid-2021 | Began in February 2021 with doses donated by China; further supplies through donations and COVAX, in phases starting with health workers |
| Outcome (general) | Very few deaths in 2020–21; elimination strategy ended in late 2021 as vaccination rose and the Delta and Omicron variants arrived | Several waves; over 5 000 reported deaths by the end of the pandemic phase (WHO, rounded), probably an underestimate because of limited testing |

**Similarities**: both closed borders and imposed national lockdowns within a few weeks of the first case, closed schools, used public messages on hygiene and masks, and began vaccination in the same month (February 2021).

**Differences and the factors behind them**
1. **Geography**: New Zealand's island position made border control easier; Zimbabwe has long land borders with returning workers and traders.
2. **Wealth and health systems**: New Zealand had more testing, tracing and intensive care capacity and could pay to support incomes; Zimbabwe had limited funds, staff and equipment.
3. **Economy and society**: most workers in Zimbabwe depend on daily informal income, so staying at home was costly; many households lack running water for hand-washing.
4. **Governance and trust**: both used the law to enforce measures; New Zealand's high trust in public messaging helped compliance, while in Zimbabwe, economic hardship and uneven enforcement affected compliance.
5. **Vaccine access**: New Zealand could buy doses; Zimbabwe relied on donations and COVAX, so supplies arrived in smaller quantities.

**Assessment.** New Zealand's strategy was effective at saving lives until vaccination, but depended on isolation and wealth and could not be kept forever. Zimbabwe's response was constrained by resources, and measures that worked elsewhere were costly for poor households. Success is not simply a matter of policy, but of resources, geography and context.

## Disease eradication: the polio programme

Polio is a viral disease that mainly affects children; most infections cause no symptoms, but about one in 200 infections causes irreversible paralysis. The **Global Polio Eradication Initiative** was launched in 1988 by the WHO, Rotary International, the US Centers for Disease Control, UNICEF and later partners such as the Gates Foundation. Methods: routine immunisation and mass campaigns using oral polio vaccine, surveillance of children with sudden paralysis and sampling of sewage, and "mop-up" vaccination around cases.

**Achievements.** Cases fell by more than 99% from an estimated 350 000 in 125 countries in 1988. Wild poliovirus type 2 was declared eradicated in 2015 and type 3 in 2019. The WHO African region, including Zimbabwe, was certified free of wild polio in August 2020. Wild poliovirus type 1 remains in Afghanistan and Pakistan.

**Why eradication has been difficult.**
- Most infections are silent, so many carriers are not noticed until a child is paralysed.
- Conflict and insecurity limit access (attacks on vaccinators), and distrust of vaccination campaigns leads some families to refuse.
- Weak routine immunisation, poverty and population movement leave gaps.
- **Circulating vaccine-derived polioviruses**: the oral vaccine contains a weakened live virus that can, rarely, mutate and spread in under-vaccinated communities. Outbreaks have occurred in Africa and Asia, so a new type of vaccine has been introduced.
- Funding and attention are not constant, and the target date has been repeatedly delayed.

**Why it could re-emerge.** If immunity falls (children missing vaccination because of conflict, displacement or disruption such as the pandemic), the virus can spread again from remaining reservoirs or from vaccine-derived strains. Imported cases can reach polio-free countries. The gains are fragile until global eradication is certified.

**Comparison with smallpox**, eradicated in 1980: smallpox had no animal reservoir, produced clear symptoms that made cases easy to find, and the vaccine was effective, stable and gave lasting immunity. Polio's silent infections make it harder.

**Evaluation.** Strengths: huge reduction in cases, five of the WHO's six regions (including Africa) certified free of wild polio, better surveillance used for other diseases. Limits: cost over decades, remaining pockets in conflict areas, and vaccine-derived outbreaks. The programme has been a major success but the final step is the hardest.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Percentage reduction and the vaccination threshold]
Suppose that annual polio cases fall from 350 000 to 700 (illustrative figure for the calculation).
1. Fall $= 350\,000 - 700 = 349\,300$.
2. Percentage reduction $= 349\,300 \div 350\,000 \times 100 = 99.8\%$.
3. The herd immunity threshold for a disease with $R_0 = 6$ (illustrative) is $1 - 1/6 = 0.833$, or 83.3%. A district with vaccine coverage of 70% is below the threshold and at risk of an outbreak.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing the pace of two responses]
Country A recorded 200 cases in the first week of an outbreak and closed borders and schools after 7 days. Country B had 200 cases in the first week and acted after 21 days. In both countries cases double every 7 days without action (illustrative).
1. After 7 days Country A has 200 cases and acts. Country B continues for 14 more days: $200 \times 2^{14/7} = 200 \times 2^2 = 800$ cases.
2. Country B had 600 more cases at the time of action. An earlier response matters because growth is exponential.
3. In practice other differences (testing, compliance, resources) also matter.
:::

:::example{title="Structuring a comparison answer"}
For a question such as "Compare the responses of two countries to a pandemic and explain the differences": (1) name the countries, the disease and the period; (2) give two similarities with dates (borders and lockdown within weeks of the first case); (3) give differences in strategy, speed, support for households, testing and vaccination, with figures; (4) explain each difference with a type of factor (geography, wealth, health system, economy, governance, trust); (5) conclude on which factors mattered most. Keep to facts you are sure of and use the language of "reported" for uncertain figures.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing each country in turn without comparing"}
Compare directly. Use "whereas", "similarly" and "in contrast" and explain the factors that made the responses different.
:::

:::mistake{title="Judging the responses only by the number of deaths"}
Reported deaths depend on testing and reporting, on the age of the population and on the timing of waves. Also consider economic cost, freedom and long-term effects.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing elimination and eradication"}
Elimination is zero cases in an area with continuing prevention; eradication is zero worldwide. Smallpox is the only human disease eradicated so far, and wild polio has been eliminated from the African region but not eradicated globally.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 4 (option 14), often as a 20-mark essay. Plan the facts first: two countries, dates, measures and outcomes. *Compare* by theme, *explain* by type of factor, and *evaluate* by weighing which factors were most important, with a judgement. For the eradication programme, give achievements, obstacles and reasons for possible re-emergence, then conclude on how far the programme has succeeded. Quote rounded figures with the year and source.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- New Zealand pursued elimination with border control and lockdowns, supported by wealth, geography and a compact health system; Zimbabwe pursued suppression with limited resources and a large informal economy.
- Similarities included early border closure, lockdowns, school closures, public messaging and a vaccination start in February 2021.
- Differences came from geography, income, health systems, the economy, trust and access to vaccines.
- The polio programme (from 1988) has cut cases by over 99% and eliminated wild polio from Africa (2020), but wild virus remains in Afghanistan and Pakistan.
- Silent infections, conflict, distrust, weak immunisation and vaccine-derived strains explain the difficulty of eradication and the risk of re-emergence.
- Evaluate programmes by achievements, obstacles and the fragility of progress.
:::
