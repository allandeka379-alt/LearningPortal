## Introduction

Where a person is born influences how long they are likely to live and what is likely to cause their death. A child born in one country may face repeated risk from diarrhoeal disease and malaria; a child born in another is more likely to live to old age and die of heart disease or cancer. The study of the geography of disease asks where diseases occur, how common they are, how they spread and why the patterns differ between places and over time. It uses data, so the ability to calculate and interpret rates is part of the topic.

This lesson sets out the general ideas and measures. Individual diseases (influenza, cholera, malaria), monitoring and responses, and pandemics are in the following lessons. You should already know the demographic transition and the idea of the development gap.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Communicable and non-communicable disease"}
A communicable (infectious) disease is caused by a pathogen that can spread from one host to another: a virus (influenza, HIV), a bacterium (cholera, tuberculosis) or a parasite (malaria). A non-communicable disease does not spread from person to person; examples are heart disease, most cancers, diabetes and chronic lung disease. A contagious disease is one that spreads easily by contact.
:::

:::definition{term="Outbreak, epidemic, pandemic and endemic"}
An outbreak is a sudden rise in cases of a disease in a place. An epidemic is a rapid rise in cases across a region or population above the expected level. A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several continents. An endemic disease is constantly present at a fairly stable level in a particular area, as malaria is in much of tropical Africa.
:::

:::definition{term="Incidence and prevalence"}
Incidence is the number of new cases in a population during a period, often per 1 000 or 100 000 people. Prevalence is the share of the population that has the disease at a given time, new and old cases together.
:::

:::definition{term="Morbidity and mortality"}
Morbidity is illness (how many people are sick, and how seriously). Mortality is death. The death rate is the number of deaths per 1 000 people per year. The infant mortality rate (IMR) is the number of deaths of children under one year old per 1 000 live births.
:::

## How diseases spread

The route of transmission affects where a disease spreads. **Airborne** (droplets and aerosols: influenza, tuberculosis, COVID-19) spread fastest in crowded places and in air travel. **Waterborne and food-borne** diseases (cholera, typhoid) spread where water supplies and sanitation are poor. **Vector-borne** diseases (malaria, dengue) need an insect or other carrier, so they are limited by the vector's habitat and climate. **Contact, blood and sexual** transmission (HIV, hepatitis B) depends on behaviour, health services and screening. **Zoonoses** pass from animals to people (Ebola, some influenza strains).

## Measures and patterns

Health and wealth are linked, and geographers use indicators together.

| Indicator | What it shows | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Death rate | Overall mortality | Affected by age structure: an older population has a higher death rate even if healthcare is good |
| Infant mortality rate | Child health, nutrition, sanitation and access to care | Often more sensitive than the overall death rate |
| Life expectancy at birth | Average years a newborn is expected to live | Reflects mortality at all ages |
| Age–sex structure | Share of children, adults and older people | Wide base indicates high birth and high child mortality |
| GDP per person, health spending per person | Resources available | Ignore how fairly resources are shared |
| Doctors or nurses per 1 000 people | Availability of care | Rural–urban differences hidden in national averages |

**Causes of death.** In low-income countries, communicable, maternal, neonatal and nutritional conditions (lower respiratory infections, diarrhoeal diseases, malaria, HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, complications of birth) cause a large share of deaths, around half or more by World Health Organization estimates, and many victims are children. In middle-income countries the share of communicable disease falls and non-communicable diseases (heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cancer) grow. In high-income countries the large majority of deaths (over 80% by the same estimates) are from non-communicable disease, and most deaths occur at older ages. This change is called the **epidemiological transition**. It is not a fixed sequence: many middle- and low-income countries carry a "double burden" of infectious and non-communicable disease.

## Why disease varies from place to place

| Type of factor | Examples |
|---|---|
| Environmental | Climate and rainfall (vectors need warmth and standing water); altitude; floods and droughts; access to safe water; animal reservoirs |
| Social | Education and knowledge; crowded housing and informal settlements; cultural practices; gender; stigma that prevents testing; diet and lifestyle |
| Economic | Poverty and malnutrition; spending on health care; number of clinics, staff and medicines; ability to pay |
| Political | Governance and priority given to health; conflict that displaces people and destroys clinics; policies on vaccination and testing; corruption |
| Global | Air travel and trade; migration; international aid and organisations such as WHO; climate change; global supply of vaccines and medicines |

Within a country the pattern also varies: urban slums and rural areas far from clinics have more disease than affluent suburbs; and groups such as young children, older people and people with HIV are more vulnerable.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Death rate, infant mortality rate and prevalence]
In a district of 160 000 people there were 1 280 deaths in a year, and 24 000 live births of which 960 infants died before their first birthday. A survey of 60 000 residents found 4 800 people with a disease, of whom 900 were new cases in the past year.
1. Death rate $= 1\,280 \div 160\,000 \times 1\,000 = 8.0$ per 1 000.
2. IMR $= 960 \div 24\,000 \times 1\,000 = 40$ per 1 000 live births.
3. Prevalence $= 4\,800 \div 60\,000 \times 100 = 8\%$.
4. Incidence $= 900 \div 60\,000 \times 1\,000 = 15$ new cases per 1 000 people per year.
5. Prevalence is higher than annual incidence because it includes all existing cases, not only the new ones.
:::

:::worked-solution[Case fatality rate]
An outbreak has 1 500 confirmed cases and 45 deaths among them.
1. Case fatality rate $= 45 \div 1\,500 \times 100 = 3\%$.
2. This is not the death rate of the population: it describes the proportion of cases that die. It tends to be lower where treatment is early and many mild cases are counted, and higher where care is poor or only severe cases are tested.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a table of three countries"}
Illustrative data: Country A has life expectancy 58 years, IMR 54 per 1 000, 0.1 doctors per 1 000 people and 62% of deaths from communicable disease; Country C has life expectancy 82 years, IMR 4, 3.4 doctors and 7% communicable. Description: "Country A has the lowest life expectancy and the highest IMR, which is 13.5 times that of Country C." Explanation: low spending on health, few doctors, poor sanitation and malnutrition raise the risk from infection and child deaths in A; in C, health care and sanitation are good, and the older population dies mainly of non-communicable disease. Caution: national averages hide differences between rich and poor and between town and countryside.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using epidemic, endemic and pandemic as if they meant the same"}
Endemic means constant presence in an area; epidemic is a rise above the expected level; pandemic is an epidemic across continents. The words describe spread, not severity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing prevalence with incidence"}
Incidence counts new cases in a period. Prevalence counts all existing cases at a time. A long-lasting disease can have high prevalence with low incidence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining health differences by one factor only"}
Disease patterns come from several factors acting together (environment, social conditions, economic resources, politics and global links). A good answer uses at least two types and links them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 4 (option 14). *Calculate* rates carefully with units (per 1 000, per 100 000, %). *Describe* a table or map by comparing places with figures, then *explain* using named types of factor. For *assess/to what extent* questions, weigh factors (for example, poverty against climate) and conclude which matters most in a given case, using data and examples. Remember that national averages can hide inequality.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diseases are communicable (pathogen-caused, spread between hosts) or non-communicable; outbreaks, epidemics and pandemics describe spread; endemic diseases persist in an area.
- Incidence counts new cases; prevalence counts all cases; death rate and IMR are per 1 000.
- Low-income countries have a large share of deaths from communicable, maternal and neonatal causes, and high-income countries mainly from non-communicable disease (epidemiological transition).
- Spatial variation is explained by environmental, social, economic, political and global factors.
- Wealth, health spending, sanitation, education and governance strongly influence health outcomes.
- National averages hide differences within countries.
:::
