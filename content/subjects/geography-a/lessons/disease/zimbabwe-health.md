## Introduction

Health shapes population and development. In the 1990s and early 2000s the HIV/AIDS epidemic reduced life expectancy in Zimbabwe to around 40–45 years, left many children without parents and removed many adults from the workforce. Since then, treatment and prevention have brought new infections and deaths down, and life expectancy has risen again to about 60 years (rounded). Other diseases remain important: tuberculosis (TB), often linked to HIV; cancers, especially cervical cancer; and Ebola, which Zimbabwe has not experienced but prepares for. This lesson looks at the effects of these diseases on the people and economy of Zimbabwe and at ways to prevent and treat them, including traditional methods. Cholera and malaria are in their own lessons.

These are real illnesses that affect real people and families. Facts are given factually and without blame, and no part of this lesson is medical advice.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="HIV and AIDS"}
HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is a virus that attacks cells of the immune system. Without treatment it leads to AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome), when the body can no longer fight infections such as tuberculosis. HIV is transmitted through unprotected sex, contaminated blood or needles, and from mother to child in pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding. It is not spread by hugging, sharing food or casual contact.
:::

:::definition{term="Antiretroviral therapy (ART)"}
Daily medicines that reduce the amount of HIV in the blood to very low levels. People on effective ART stay healthy and, when the virus is undetectable, do not pass HIV on through sex.
:::

:::definition{term="Tuberculosis (TB)"}
A bacterial disease (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*) that mainly affects the lungs and spreads in the air when an infected person coughs. It is curable with a six-month course of several antibiotics.
:::

:::definition{term="Ebola virus disease"}
A severe viral disease spread by direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a sick or dead person (and by contact with infected animals). Fruit bats are thought to be the natural reservoir.
:::

## HIV/AIDS

**Effects on population.** Zimbabwe's epidemic spread widely in the 1990s, reaching about a quarter of adults at its peak (UNAIDS estimates, rounded). Death rates in adults aged 20–49 rose, life expectancy fell, and the population pyramid shows narrowing in the middle-aged groups. Today about one adult in eight is living with HIV in surveys (rounded), and most are on treatment. **Effects on development.** Loss of workers in farming, mining, teaching and health; many orphans, who are cared for by grandparents or relatives, and child-headed households; high spending on health care by households and government; lower school attendance; and stigma that leads some people to avoid testing.

**Prevention and care.** Public information and condoms; voluntary testing and counselling; treatment of pregnant women to prevent mother-to-child transmission; voluntary medical male circumcision, which lowers the risk of infection in men; pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for people at high risk; safe blood supply; and ART for all who need it. Funding comes from the national AIDS levy (a tax on incomes, collected since 1999) and donors, through the National AIDS Council.

## Tuberculosis

TB is spread through the air, so crowding, poor ventilation and malnutrition raise the risk, and HIV is the strongest risk factor because it weakens immunity. Miners and people in overcrowded housing are at higher risk. Signs: a cough lasting more than two weeks, fever, night sweats and weight loss. **Control:** early testing (sputum tests and rapid tests), a six-month course of drugs taken regularly with support from health workers (directly observed treatment), screening of people living with HIV, BCG vaccine for babies, and good ventilation. If treatment is stopped early the bacteria can become drug-resistant (MDR-TB), which is harder and longer to treat.

## Ebola

Ebola outbreaks occur in central and west Africa. The 2014–16 epidemic in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone had about 28 600 cases and 11 300 deaths (rounded). It spread in towns and across borders, where health systems were weak and burial practices involving contact with the dead spread the virus. Zimbabwe has not recorded a confirmed case (check current WHO reports), but air travel makes preparation necessary: screening at airports and border posts, isolation wards, trained rapid response teams, and public information. **Control** relies on quickly finding and isolating cases, tracing and monitoring contacts, protective equipment for health workers, safe and dignified burials, and, since 2019, a vaccine and effective treatments.

## Cancer

Cancer is a non-communicable disease in which cells grow out of control. Common cancers in Zimbabwe include cervical and breast cancers in women, prostate cancer in men, Kaposi's sarcoma (linked to HIV) and cancers of the liver and oesophagus. **Cervical cancer** is caused by long-lasting infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), and risk is higher in women living with HIV. Many cancers are found late because screening is limited, people have to travel far, and treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) is costly and available in few centres. **Prevention:** HPV vaccination of girls; screening of women (for example by visual inspection of the cervix with acetic acid, followed by treatment of early changes); not smoking, less alcohol and a healthy diet; vaccination against hepatitis B; and early investigation of lumps or other symptoms.

## Indigenous ways of preventing and treating disease

Many people in Zimbabwe use traditional medicine and traditional healers (*n'anga*), faith healers and traditional birth attendants, alongside or instead of clinics. They are respected and nearby, they offer personal attention and community support, and traditional leaders can help to share health messages. Herbal remedies have been the source of some modern medicines. But many remedies have not been tested for safety or effectiveness; some claim to cure HIV or cancer without evidence; some may harm the liver or interact with ART; and visiting a healer first can delay testing and treatment, so people arrive late. **Assess** an indigenous practice by asking: is there evidence that it is safe and effective? Does it delay proper care? Is it affordable and accessible? Does the healer work with clinics, refer patients and follow guidelines (the Traditional Medical Practitioners Council is the registering body)? A balanced view is that traditional and scientific approaches can complement each other when practices are tested, safe and linked to the health system.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[The HIV treatment cascade]
In a district an estimated 10 000 people are living with HIV (illustrative data). Of these, 9 000 know their status; 8 100 of those who know are on ART; and 7 290 of those on ART have a suppressed viral load.
1. Percentage who know their status $= 9\,000 \div 10\,000 \times 100 = 90\%$.
2. Percentage of those who know who are on ART $= 8\,100 \div 9\,000 \times 100 = 90\%$.
3. Percentage of those on ART who are suppressed $= 7\,290 \div 8\,100 \times 100 = 90\%$.
4. Overall share of all people with HIV who are suppressed $= 7\,290 \div 10\,000 \times 100 = 72.9\%$. The gaps at each step (testing, treatment, adherence) show where services need to improve.
:::

:::worked-solution[Case fatality rate of Ebola]
Rounded figures for West Africa, 2014–16: about 28 600 cases and about 11 300 deaths.
1. CFR $= 11\,300 \div 28\,600 \times 100 = 39.5\%$.
2. The rate varied between places and outbreaks (roughly a quarter to nine-tenths of cases have died), and was affected by access to care.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a change in the population pyramid"}
Illustrative pyramids for a country with a severe HIV epidemic show a wide base, a narrow section for ages 25–45 and a small older group. Description: "The pyramid has a wide base of children, but the 25–45 age groups are narrower than expected, with relatively small numbers of men and women." Explanation: deaths from AIDS among young adults; fewer births because of deaths of potential parents; fewer older people because of lower life expectancy. After ART is widely available, the narrowing shrinks as adults survive.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using stigmatising language"}
Write "people living with HIV", not "AIDS victims", and do not suggest that any group is to blame. HIV is a virus that can infect anyone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing HIV and AIDS"}
HIV is the virus; AIDS is the late stage of untreated infection. With treatment people can live long, healthy lives without developing AIDS.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dismissing indigenous practices without assessment, or accepting them uncritically"}
Judge each practice on evidence of safety and effect, access and cultural acceptance, and whether it delays proper care. Use "some" and "many" and not "all".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- HIV/AIDS reduced life expectancy and the working-age population in Zimbabwe; treatment and prevention have since lowered new infections and deaths.
- Prevention and care include testing, ART, prevention of mother-to-child transmission, male circumcision, PrEP and public education.
- TB is airborne and strongly linked to HIV; it is curable with a full six-month course of drugs.
- Ebola spreads by contact with body fluids; control relies on isolation, contact tracing, safe burial and vaccine; Zimbabwe prepares but has not recorded a case.
- Cervical cancer is the most common cancer in women; HPV vaccination, screening and early treatment can reduce it.
- Indigenous medicine is accessible and respected but needs testing and links to clinics; assess it using evidence, safety, access and risk of delay.
:::
