## Introduction

Malaria is among the most important diseases in the tropics. The World Health Organization reports roughly 250 million cases and about 600 000 deaths a year in recent years (rounded), and around 95% of the cases and deaths are in Africa, with children under five most at risk. In Zimbabwe, malaria is common in the hot, low-lying areas of the Zambezi valley and the eastern and south-eastern lowveld, and hardly occurs on the cool high plateau around Harare. This pattern shows how a single organism, a mosquito, ties a disease to climate, relief and human conditions. Malaria is preventable and treatable, and some countries have eliminated it, so it also shows what money, organisation and technology can do.

You should know the general terms (incidence, prevalence, endemic) from the geography of disease lesson. The detailed life cycle of the parasite is not required.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Malaria"}
A disease caused by single-celled parasites of the genus *Plasmodium* (the most dangerous in Africa is *Plasmodium falciparum*), carried between people by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. Symptoms include fever, chills, headache and vomiting; untreated, severe malaria can damage the brain, kidneys and blood, and kill.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without usually being harmed. The *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector of malaria; the parasite is the pathogen.
:::

:::definition{term="Stable and unstable malaria"}
Stable (endemic) malaria occurs where transmission is regular all or most of the year and adults have acquired partial immunity, so most severe disease is in children. Unstable (epidemic) malaria occurs where transmission is irregular, and people have little immunity, so epidemics affect all ages.
:::

## Transmission and the factors that influence spread

1. A female *Anopheles* mosquito bites an infected person and takes up the parasite with the blood.
2. The parasite develops in the mosquito for about one to two weeks, depending on temperature.
3. When the mosquito bites another person, usually between dusk and dawn, it injects the parasites, which multiply in the liver and then in red blood cells.

| Factor | How it affects malaria |
|---|---|
| Temperature | The parasite develops in the mosquito only above about 18 °C, and faster in warmer conditions; above about 35 °C mosquitoes survive poorly. So transmission is limited to the warm tropics and subtropics, and by altitude |
| Rainfall and water | Mosquitoes breed in standing water. Transmission peaks 1–2 months after the start of the rains; irrigation schemes, dams, ponds and drains extend breeding |
| Altitude | Temperature falls with altitude, so above about 1 500 m malaria is rare or seasonal; warming allows it to reach higher places ("highland malaria") |
| Housing and land use | Open eaves, no screens and living near breeding sites raise bites; deforestation and farming can create breeding sites |
| Poverty and health systems | Poor households cannot afford nets or treatment; clinics far away delay diagnosis. Malnutrition raises the risk of severe disease |
| Population movement | Migrants and refugees from low-risk areas lack immunity; travellers carry the parasite to new areas |

## Distribution

Globally, malaria is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, with smaller burdens in South Asia, South-East Asia, the Amazon basin and Papua New Guinea. It has been eliminated from Europe, North America and many other countries through drainage, spraying, housing improvements and health care; Sri Lanka was certified malaria-free by the WHO in 2016.

In Zimbabwe, transmission is **seasonal**, mostly from November to May and peaking in March to May, and varies with altitude and rainfall:
- **High risk**: low-lying areas in the north (Zambezi valley, Mashonaland), the east and south-east lowveld along the Mozambique border (Manicaland districts such as Chipinge and Mutare), and parts of Matabeleland North, such as around Hwange and Victoria Falls.
- **Low or no risk**: the cool, high plateau (Harare, Bulawayo, Gweru and the higher parts of the Eastern Highlands), although epidemics can occur after warm, wet seasons or travel.

## Impacts

**Social**: illness and death, especially children under five and pregnant women; anaemia and low birth weight; missed school and work. **Economic**: the cost of treatment and prevention for households; lost income from sick days; lower farm output at peak seasons; discouraged tourism and investment; and public health spending that cannot be used for other purposes. Poorer families are hit hardest and malaria in turn deepens poverty.

**Changing conditions.** Warming may allow transmission at higher altitudes; irrigation and dams can extend breeding seasons; more mosquitoes in cities near open water (the invasive *Anopheles stephensi* in parts of eastern Africa); resistance of mosquitoes to insecticides and of parasites to drugs; conflict and displacement; and in some places funding cuts. Effects differ from place to place, and can be reversed by control programmes.

## Detailed specific example: prevention and control in Zimbabwe

1. **Nets.** Long-lasting insecticide-treated nets are distributed free to households in risk areas (including to pregnant women and children).
2. **Indoor residual spraying (IRS)** of walls with insecticide in high-burden districts before the rainy season.
3. **Diagnosis and treatment.** Rapid diagnostic tests and artemisinin-based combination therapy at clinics and through village health workers, so that people are treated quickly.
4. **Environmental management and education.** Draining or filling breeding sites, clearing vegetation around homes, and community campaigns about sleeping under nets and seeking care early.
5. **Surveillance and plans.** Case reporting through clinics, and a national malaria programme that aims for much lower transmission and, in the longer term, elimination (check the current national plan).
6. **New tools.** The WHO recommended malaria vaccines for children in high-risk areas in 2021 (RTS,S) and 2023 (R21), and roll-out began in several African countries in 2024.

**Evaluation.** Strengths: nets, spraying and early treatment save lives and are cost-effective; the programme can target the districts at highest risk. Limits: funding depends partly on donors; nets wear out and are not always used; insecticide and drug resistance threaten gains; roads and clinics in remote areas are poor; and climate and human movement can bring outbreaks. Sustained funding and community participation are essential.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Incidence and net coverage]
A district of 80 000 people at risk recorded 12 400 confirmed cases in a year. A programme aims for one net for every two people, and 28 000 nets were distributed.
1. Incidence $= 12\,400 \div 80\,000 \times 1\,000 = 155$ cases per 1 000 people.
2. Nets needed $= 80\,000 \div 2 = 40\,000$.
3. Coverage $= 28\,000 \div 40\,000 \times 100 = 70\%$, so 12 000 more nets are needed to meet the target.
:::

:::worked-solution[Altitude and malaria: correlation and regression]
Eight sites in an eastern province (illustrative data, invented for this exercise) have the following annual incidence (cases per 1 000 people).

| Altitude (m) | 450 | 600 | 800 | 1 000 | 1 200 | 1 400 | 1 500 | 1 650 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Incidence per 1 000 | 262 | 300 | 195 | 190 | 140 | 60 | 75 | 10 |

1. Pearson's correlation coefficient is $r = -0.968$. The critical value for 8 pairs at the 0.05 level is 0.707, and $|-0.968| > 0.707$, so there is a significant strong negative relationship: incidence falls as altitude rises.
2. The regression line is $y = 396.1 - 0.225x$, where $y$ is incidence per 1 000 and $x$ is altitude in metres.
3. At 1 100 m: $y = 396.08 - 0.22519 \times 1\,100 = 148.4$, so about 148 cases per 1 000 are predicted (using the unrounded gradient; the rounded line gives a similar answer).
4. Do not extrapolate: the line gives a negative incidence at 1 760 m and above, which is impossible. Altitude is also linked to temperature, rainfall and wealth, so it is not the only cause.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a seasonal pattern"}
A table of monthly rainfall and malaria cases (illustrative) shows rainfall peaking at 190 mm in January and cases peaking at 640 in March. Description: "Rainfall peaks in January at 190 mm; cases peak two months later in March at 640." Explanation: after rain, pools form and mosquitoes breed; the mosquitoes need days to mature, and the parasite needs one to two weeks to develop inside them, so human cases rise one to two months after the rain.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying malaria is caused by mosquitoes"}
It is caused by a parasite (*Plasmodium*). The mosquito is the vector that carries it. Only female *Anopheles* mosquitoes carry malaria.
:::

:::mistake{title="Linking malaria to rainfall in the same month"}
There is a delay of weeks between rain, mosquito breeding, parasite development and cases. Describe this time lag.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating nets as a complete solution"}
Nets work only when used correctly and replaced, and they must be combined with spraying, diagnosis, treatment and environmental measures. Resistance and funding are limits.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium* parasites, carried by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes that bite mainly between dusk and dawn.
- Temperature (above about 18 °C), standing water and low altitude control the distribution; Africa has about 95% of cases and deaths.
- In Zimbabwe transmission is seasonal and highest in the low-lying north, east and south-east.
- Impacts include child deaths, anaemia, lost school and work, and household costs that deepen poverty.
- Control combines nets, indoor spraying, rapid diagnosis and treatment, environmental management and education; vaccines are new tools.
- Resistance, funding, climate change and movement are risks to progress.
:::
