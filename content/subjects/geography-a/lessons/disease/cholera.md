## Introduction

Cholera is an old disease that can be prevented. It spreads where drinking water is contaminated with human waste, so it is a disease of poor water supply, poor sanitation and crowding. Between August 2008 and mid-2009 Zimbabwe experienced one of the worst cholera epidemics in Africa in recent decades: the World Health Organization recorded nearly 100 000 cases and over 4 000 deaths (rounded). The epidemic began in the high-density suburbs of Harare and Chitungwiza and spread to every province and into neighbouring countries. Geographers study cholera because its pattern shows how the environment, poverty, infrastructure and government action combine to decide who gets ill.

You should know the general ideas in the geography of disease lesson (epidemic, incidence, case fatality rate). The medical details of treatment are outside this lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cholera"}
An acute diarrhoeal disease caused by the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*. It is transmitted by the faecal–oral route, usually through drinking water or eating food contaminated with faeces from an infected person. Many infected people have mild or no symptoms, but some develop severe watery diarrhoea and vomiting, which cause rapid dehydration and can kill within hours if untreated.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral rehydration solution (ORS)"}
A mixture of clean water, salts and sugar given by mouth to replace the fluid and salts lost in diarrhoea. Most cholera patients can be treated successfully with ORS alone; severe cases need fluids by drip (intravenous) and sometimes antibiotics.
:::

:::definition{term="Case fatality rate (CFR)"}
The percentage of people with a disease who die from it. For cholera with proper treatment, the CFR should be under 1%.
:::

## Transmission and risk factors

1. An infected person excretes the bacteria in faeces. Where there is no toilet or the sewage system fails, the bacteria enter rivers, wells and shallow boreholes, or reach food by flies and unwashed hands.
2. Other people drink the contaminated water, or eat contaminated food (raw shellfish, vegetables washed in unsafe water), and the bacteria multiply in the intestine. The incubation period is from a few hours to about five days.
3. Spread is fastest in **crowded areas** (informal settlements, camps for displaced people, markets, funerals where food is shared) where many people share a water source.

Risk factors: lack of safe water and sanitation, poverty, crowding, poor hygiene, living near a contaminated water source, and delay in reaching treatment. Floods spread sewage into water sources; droughts force people to use unsafe sources. Cholera is not usually spread by casual contact, so washing hands, boiling or chlorinating water and cooking food well are effective.

## Distribution

Cholera is **endemic** in parts of South Asia (the Ganges delta), sub-Saharan Africa and some other places, and it breaks out as epidemics in countries where infrastructure fails or after disasters (Yemen since 2016, Haiti after the earthquake of 2010). The World Health Organization estimates that there are about 1.3 million to 4 million cases and 21 000 to 143 000 deaths every year, and the range is wide because many cases are not reported. It has largely disappeared from high-income countries, which have safe water and sewage treatment.

In Zimbabwe, outbreaks occur mainly in the rainy season (November to March), in high-density urban areas, in places along the main transport routes and borders (Beitbridge, Mutare), and in rural districts with few safe water points. Repeat outbreaks have occurred in 2018 (Harare) and 2023–24.

**Links to disasters.** Floods, cyclones and earthquakes damage water pipes and sewers and displace people into crowded shelters. Cholera cases were confirmed in Beira, Mozambique, within weeks of Cyclone Idai (March 2019); cholera spread widely in Haiti after the earthquake of January 2010.

## Impacts

**Social**: deaths (especially of children and the poor), fear, funerals and burials affected, schools and clinics overwhelmed, and stigma towards those affected. **Economic**: costs of treatment, lost work, closed markets and borders, loss of tourism and trade, and the cost of repairing water and sewage systems. Poor households are hit hardest because they have the least access to clean water and care.

## Detailed specific example: Zimbabwe, 2008–09 and the response

**Causes.** After years of falling public funding, water treatment plants lacked chemicals, pumps and electricity, sewers burst, refuse was not collected and many boreholes and standpipes failed in towns such as Harare and Chitungwiza. People turned to unprotected wells and streams. The health system was weakened by a shortage of staff, medicines and equipment. The first rains of the season, and gatherings such as funerals, helped the spread. People travelling and working across borders took the disease to South Africa, Mozambique, Zambia and Botswana.

**Response.** The government declared the outbreak an emergency in December 2008; UNICEF, WHO, the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies, Médecins Sans Frontières and other agencies set up cholera treatment centres, oral rehydration points, supplied chlorine and water purification tablets, trucked clean water, and carried out hygiene education. The case fatality rate, which was high in the early months, fell as treatment centres opened and supplies arrived, and the outbreak declined during 2009 as water treatment was restored.

**Longer-term prevention.** Repair and rehabilitation of water and sewage systems and boreholes; community health clubs and village health workers promoting handwashing and latrines; oral cholera vaccination in outbreak areas (used in Harare in 2018 and in later outbreaks); surveillance and early reporting; and rapid chlorination of wells.

**Evaluation.** Strengths: treatment saved many lives and the outbreak was contained; vaccine and rapid response are now available. Limits: the underlying causes (ageing water and sewer systems, low municipal funding, poverty) take years to fix, so outbreaks recur; vaccine protection lasts only a few years and doses are limited. Prevention through infrastructure is more effective than treatment, but it is slower and more expensive.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Case fatality rate]
In a district outbreak (illustrative data) there were 2 500 cases and 125 deaths.
1. CFR $= 125 \div 2\,500 \times 100 = 5.0\%$.
2. This is well above the target of 1%, because many people could not reach treatment early and treatment centres were short of supplies. The national rate for Zimbabwe in 2008–09 was also above the target, at more than 4%.
:::

:::worked-solution[Safe water and cholera: Spearman's rank correlation]
The table shows illustrative data for eight districts (invented for this exercise): the percentage of households with safe water and the cholera cases per 10 000 people in one season.

| District | Safe water (%) | Cases per 10 000 | Rank x | Rank y | d | d² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 92 | 3 | 8 | 1 | 7 | 49 |
| B | 85 | 6 | 7 | 3 | 4 | 16 |
| C | 78 | 5 | 6 | 2 | 4 | 16 |
| D | 70 | 14 | 5 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| E | 61 | 18 | 4 | 5 | −1 | 1 |
| F | 55 | 21 | 3 | 6 | −3 | 9 |
| G | 40 | 35 | 2 | 7 | −5 | 25 |
| H | 32 | 41 | 1 | 8 | −7 | 49 |

1. $\sum d^2 = 49+16+16+1+1+9+25+49 = 166$.
2. $r_s = 1 - \dfrac{6 \times 166}{8^3 - 8} = 1 - \dfrac{996}{504} = 1 - 1.976 = -0.976$.
3. The critical value for 8 pairs at the 0.05 level is 0.738. Since $|-0.976| > 0.738$, there is a significant strong negative relationship: districts with more safe water have fewer cholera cases. This shows association, not proof of cause, but it supports the explanation from transmission.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cholera is caused by dirty water itself"}
Cholera is caused by a bacterium. Water is dangerous when it is contaminated with faeces that contain it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating cholera as a disease of poor people's behaviour"}
Outbreaks are driven mainly by infrastructure and access to safe water and sanitation, which individuals cannot easily change. Do not blame communities.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that most cases can be treated easily"}
Prompt oral rehydration saves most lives. Deaths occur when people cannot reach treatment, which is why access to care is part of the explanation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cholera is caused by *Vibrio cholerae* and spreads by the faecal–oral route through contaminated water and food.
- Risk is highest with poor sanitation, no safe water, crowding and poverty, and after floods and cyclones.
- Cholera is endemic in parts of South Asia and Africa; the WHO estimates 1.3–4 million cases a year.
- Zimbabwe's 2008–09 epidemic (nearly 100 000 cases, over 4 000 deaths, rounded) followed the failure of water and sewage systems.
- Treatment with oral rehydration saves lives; prevention needs safe water, sanitation, hygiene and, in outbreaks, vaccine.
- Long-term infrastructure is the most effective but slowest and costliest solution.
:::
