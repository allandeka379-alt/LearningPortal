## Introduction

Influenza ("flu") is a common viral disease of the respiratory tract. Most people recover within a week or two, but flu can be dangerous for infants, older people and people with other illnesses, and every so often a new strain spreads around the world and kills millions. The pandemic of 1918–19 is generally estimated to have killed at least 50 million people. Because the virus changes so quickly, the disease has to be monitored and the vaccine updated every year. For geographers influenza shows how a disease moves through space (by air travel and everyday contact), how it varies over time (seasonal peaks) and how rich and poor places differ in preparedness.

You should know the general terms from the geography of disease lesson (epidemic, pandemic, incidence). The biology of the virus is outside this lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Influenza virus types"}
Influenza A, B and C are the three types. Type A infects people and animals (especially birds and pigs) and causes pandemics; type B mainly infects people and causes seasonal epidemics. Type A viruses are named by two surface proteins, haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N), for example H1N1 and H3N2.
:::

:::definition{term="Antigenic drift and shift"}
Drift is the gradual change in the virus from small mutations, which is why people can catch flu again and why the vaccine has to be updated each year. Shift is a sudden major change, often when viruses from different species mix, producing a new subtype to which few people have immunity: the cause of pandemics.
:::

:::definition{term="Zoonosis and reservoir"}
A zoonosis is a disease passed from animals to people. A reservoir is the host in which a pathogen normally lives. Wild aquatic birds are the natural reservoir of influenza A.
:::

## Transmission and risk factors

Influenza spreads in **droplets and aerosols** produced when an infected person coughs, sneezes or talks, and by touching surfaces and then the face. The incubation period is short (about one to four days), and a person can pass the virus on a day before symptoms appear, which makes it hard to control. Crowded places (schools, care homes, public transport, aeroplanes) speed the spread.

Higher risk of serious illness: children under five, adults over 65, pregnant women, people with chronic conditions (lung and heart disease, diabetes) or a weak immune system (including people living with HIV), and health workers who are exposed to patients.

## Distribution in space and time

- **Space**: seasonal influenza occurs everywhere. The World Health Organization estimates about 3 to 5 million cases of severe illness and 290 000 to 650 000 respiratory deaths a year worldwide. Death rates are higher in places with poor health care and malnutrition, although high-income countries have many deaths among older people.
- **Season in temperate zones**: epidemics peak in the cool season, from about November to March in the northern hemisphere and from about May to September in southern Africa and Australasia, because people spend more time indoors and the virus survives better in cold, dry air. In tropical countries, flu occurs throughout the year, often with peaks in the rainy season.
- **Spread**: air travel carries the virus quickly between continents. A new strain may appear first in one region and reach others within weeks.
- **Time**: pandemics occurred in 1918 (H1N1), 1957 (H2N2), 1968 (H3N2) and 2009 (H1N1, first detected in North America in April 2009, declared a pandemic by the WHO in June 2009). Each is a new strain.

## Impacts and new strains

**Social**: illness and death, absence from school and work, strain on hospitals during winter peaks, and in a pandemic school closures and disrupted services. **Economic**: lost working days, health care costs, and in pandemics, reduced trade and travel. The costs fall unevenly: poor households have little income to lose time and few health services.

**New strains and animal reservoirs.** Some avian influenza viruses (such as H5N1, first seen in people in Hong Kong in 1997) cause severe disease in people who have close contact with infected poultry, but do not yet spread easily from person to person. Pigs can be infected with both bird and human viruses, so they can become "mixing vessels" in which new combinations arise. A strain that spreads easily between people and for which there is no immunity would cause a pandemic. Watching animal outbreaks is therefore part of control.

## Detailed specific example: prevention and control in the United Kingdom

1. **Surveillance.** National agencies (the UK Health Security Agency in England) collect reports from sample general practices (sentinel networks), hospitals and laboratories, and share virus samples with the WHO global network that advises each year on the vaccine strains.
2. **Vaccination.** The National Health Service offers free annual vaccination to people aged 65 and over, pregnant women, people with long-term conditions, frontline health and social care staff, and children (a nasal spray, given in primary school and to some other age groups). Uptake among older people has been about three-quarters in recent seasons (rounded), and lower in some groups of younger adults.
3. **Antivirals and preparedness.** Stocks of antiviral drugs (such as oseltamivir) were built up after the avian flu scare of the mid-2000s, and national pandemic plans cover vaccine supply, hospital capacity and communication.
4. **Public information.** Campaigns on hygiene ("Catch it, Bin it, Kill it" in 2009) and advice to stay at home when ill.

**Evaluation.** Strengths: universal health care, strong surveillance, free vaccines for those at highest risk, and good communication. Limits: vaccine effectiveness varies from year to year (often about 40–60% in a well-matched season, less if the strain changes), some people do not take the offer, winter flu still puts pressure on hospitals, and the vaccine has to be made months before the season starts. Overall the United Kingdom has reduced severe illness among those at highest risk, but cannot prevent epidemics.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Vaccine effectiveness]
In a season, 10% of unvaccinated people in a study caught influenza and 4% of vaccinated people did.
1. Vaccine effectiveness $= \dfrac{10 - 4}{10} \times 100 = 60\%$.
2. This means the vaccinated group had 60% fewer cases than the unvaccinated group, not that 60% of vaccinated people were protected for certain.
3. The effectiveness differs by year and age group, so a figure from one season cannot be applied to every year.
:::

:::worked-solution[Uptake and unvaccinated population]
A health authority has 240 000 residents aged 65 and over. 180 000 had the vaccine.
1. Uptake $= 180\,000 \div 240\,000 \times 100 = 75\%$.
2. Unvaccinated $= 240\,000 - 180\,000 = 60\,000$ people (25%).
3. These 60 000 people are at higher risk of serious illness, so targeted reminders, home visits or visits to care homes would help.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining seasonal patterns"}
A table shows weekly flu cases per 100 000 in Country N (northern hemisphere, illustrative) peaking at 95 in week 4 and falling to under 5 by week 16, and in Country S (southern hemisphere) peaking at 82 in week 30. Description: "Country N peaks early in the year (week 4, at 95 cases per 100 000) while Country S peaks in mid-year (week 30, at 82); the peaks are about 26 weeks apart." Explanation: both peaks are in the local winter, when people crowd indoors and the virus survives better in cold, dry air; the opposite seasons in the two hemispheres explain the six-month difference.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that flu vaccines give full protection for life"}
The virus changes, so protection is partial and the vaccine is updated every year. Say that it reduces the risk and severity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing drift and shift"}
Drift is the small, steady change that causes seasonal epidemics. Shift is the sudden major change that can cause a pandemic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating influenza as a disease only of cold countries"}
Flu occurs in the tropics all year, and in Africa and Asia is often poorly measured because of weak surveillance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Influenza is a viral respiratory disease that spreads by droplets, aerosols and contaminated surfaces; people at highest risk are the very young, the old, pregnant women and those with chronic illness.
- Seasonal epidemics peak in the local winter in temperate zones; in the tropics flu occurs all year; air travel spreads strains quickly.
- Drift causes seasonal epidemics and yearly vaccine updates; shift can cause pandemics (1918, 1957, 1968, 2009).
- Animal reservoirs (birds and pigs) are sources of new strains, so surveillance includes animal outbreaks.
- The UK combines surveillance, free vaccination for risk groups, antivirals, plans and information, but cannot prevent epidemics.
- Vaccine effectiveness is partial and varies by year.
:::
