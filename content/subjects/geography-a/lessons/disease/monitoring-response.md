## Introduction

An outbreak of an infectious disease is a race between the spread of the disease and the speed of the response. In August 2014 the World Health Organization declared the Ebola outbreak in West Africa an international emergency, many months after the first cases had occurred; by then the virus was spreading in the capital cities of three countries. In Nigeria, in the same year, a rapid response including finding and monitoring contacts limited the outbreak to a small number of cases. The difference illustrates the lesson: effective monitoring finds disease early, and an effective response depends on resources, trust, leadership and the setting.

You should know the terms from the geography of disease lesson and the individual diseases (influenza, cholera, malaria). Examples of pandemics are in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Disease surveillance (monitoring)"}
The continuous collection, analysis and sharing of health data to detect outbreaks, track trends and guide action. Types include passive surveillance (clinics and laboratories report cases), active surveillance (health workers search for cases), sentinel surveillance (selected sites report regularly) and syndromic surveillance (reports of symptoms such as fever or diarrhoea before tests confirm the cause).
:::

:::definition{term="Contact tracing"}
Identifying, informing and monitoring people who have been in contact with a case, so that new cases are found early and can be isolated before they pass on the disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Herd immunity"}
The protection of a population when enough people are immune (through vaccination or past infection) that the disease cannot spread easily. The proportion needed depends on the basic reproduction number $R_0$, the average number of people one case infects in a population with no immunity.
:::

## What makes monitoring effective

| Factor | Why it matters | Problems |
|---|---|---|
| Testing: cost, access and speed | Early diagnosis shows where the disease is and allows treatment and isolation | Tests and laboratories are costly; rural people may be far from them; slow results delay action |
| Data accuracy and completeness | Good data show real trends | Under-reporting, poor records, different definitions of a case, delays in sending reports |
| Confidentiality and trust | People test and report only if they trust that their data are safe and they will not be blamed | Stigma, fear of losing work, misuse of data |
| Skilled staff and communication | Staff must recognise, report and respond; information must reach the public | Shortage of trained staff, weak phone or internet links |
| International collaboration | Diseases cross borders: sharing data, samples and alerts is faster and cheaper than working alone | Political distrust, delays in reporting for fear of trade or travel restrictions |

At the international level the WHO coordinates alerts under the International Health Regulations (2005), which require countries to detect and report certain events; global networks share virus samples (influenza), and regional bodies such as the Africa Centres for Disease Control support member states. New tools include genetic sequencing of pathogens, mobile phone reporting, and testing of wastewater.

## Controls

- **Vaccines**: prevent disease in individuals and, if coverage is high, protect whole communities. Limits: cost, cold-chain storage, supply, access in remote or conflict areas, and hesitancy caused by rumours.
- **Education**: teaches hand-washing, safe water, how to recognise symptoms and when to seek care. It is cheap and flexible, but depends on trust and does not work alone.
- **Sanitation and safe water**: the long-term solution for waterborne disease; expensive and slow.
- **Low-technology solutions**: insecticide-treated nets, face masks, hand-washing stations, oral rehydration salts and chlorine tablets. They are cheap, easy to produce locally and effective when used correctly, but depend on use and supply.
- **Isolation, quarantine and travel measures**: separating cases and contacts; closing borders or schools. They can slow spread but cost income and freedom.

## What decides whether a response succeeds

| Type of factor | Examples |
|---|---|
| Environmental | Terrain and floods that block roads; remote areas; climate affecting mosquitoes; urban slums where disease spreads quickly |
| Social | Trust in health workers; cultural practices (burial customs); stigma; education; rumours and misinformation; community participation |
| Economic | Funding for staff, tests, drugs and vaccines; ability of households to lose work time; national wealth; donor support |
| Political | Leadership and transparency; willingness to report early; conflict and displacement; coordination between agencies and countries; priorities in spending |

**Case contrast: Ebola in West Africa and Nigeria, 2014.** In Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone, where health systems were weak after conflict, there were few doctors and laboratories, cases were found late, some communities distrusted health workers and followed traditional burial practices that spread the virus, and the epidemic reached about 28 600 cases and 11 300 deaths (rounded). In Nigeria, a single case reached Lagos by air in July 2014. A national emergency centre, built on the country's polio programme, traced hundreds of contacts, isolated cases quickly and informed the public; there were 20 cases and 8 deaths and Nigeria was declared free of the virus in October 2014. In the later outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (2018–20), vaccination of contacts ("ring vaccination") helped, but conflict and community mistrust slowed the response. The lessons: speed, trust and capacity matter more than wealth alone.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Herd immunity threshold]
A disease has $R_0 = 2.5$. A vaccine is 90% effective.
1. Herd immunity threshold $= 1 - \dfrac{1}{R_0} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{2.5} = 0.6$, or 60% of the population must be immune.
2. Vaccination coverage needed $= 60 \div 90 \times 100 = 66.7\%$, because the vaccine does not protect everyone.
3. If coverage is only 50%, the disease can still spread, which is why uptake and rumours matter.
:::

:::worked-solution[The effect of a delay in response]
An outbreak starts with 25 cases. Without control, cases double every 4 days. With control measures starting on day 12 the doubling time becomes 8 days.
1. Without control on day 28: $25 \times 2^{28/4} = 25 \times 2^7 = 3\,200$ cases.
2. With control: by day 12 there are $25 \times 2^3 = 200$ cases; from day 12 to day 28 (16 days) the number doubles twice (16 ÷ 8), so $200 \times 2^2 = 800$ cases.
3. Cases avoided $= 3\,200 - 800 = 2\,400$. Starting the control earlier would avoid even more, which shows why early detection matters. In practice growth is not exactly exponential, so these are estimates.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating the data from a surveillance system"}
A table shows that Region A reported 40 cholera cases and Region B 400, but Region A has one clinic and no laboratory, and Region B has six clinics and a laboratory. Evaluation: "The higher figure for Region B may reflect better testing and reporting, not more disease. Region A may have under-reported its cases, so the data cannot be compared without information about testing and population size." Good answers question the reliability of the data.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Assuming rich countries always respond best"}
Wealth helps, but speed, trust, leadership and communication also decide success. Compare Nigeria's quick control of Ebola in 2014 with the larger outbreaks in neighbouring countries.
:::

:::mistake{title="Taking reported cases as the true number"}
Reported cases depend on testing, access to clinics and reporting systems. Places with more testing report more cases. Say so when comparing places.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing only about technology"}
Low-technology measures and trust in communities are often as important as vaccines and drugs. Include social and political factors in your answer.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Surveillance (passive, active, sentinel, syndromic) detects outbreaks; effectiveness depends on testing, accurate data, confidentiality, trained staff and international collaboration.
- Controls include vaccines, education, sanitation and low-technology measures such as nets, masks and oral rehydration; each has limits.
- Herd immunity needs coverage of at least $1 - 1/R_0$ of the population, adjusted for vaccine effectiveness.
- Early action reduces cases sharply because outbreaks grow exponentially.
- Responses depend on environmental, social, economic and political factors, as the contrast between Nigeria and neighbouring countries in 2014 shows.
- Reported case numbers reflect testing and reporting as well as the real spread.
:::
