## Introduction

A thunderstorm builds over Harare on a hot January afternoon, while on the same day the sky stays clear over a dry Kalahari plain. In July the Eastern Highlands are wrapped in cloud while the lowveld is dry. These differences depend on **atmospheric moisture** and on whether air will rise. This lesson explains how water vapour changes state, how a rising parcel of air cools, how stability decides whether air keeps rising, and how the different kinds of precipitation form. You should already know what latent heat is from the energy budget lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Dew point"}
The temperature at which air becomes saturated (relative humidity 100%) and condensation begins. Cooling air below the dew point produces cloud, dew or fog.
:::

:::definition{term="Adiabatic process"}
A change in the temperature of a parcel of air caused only by a change in pressure, with no heat exchanged with the surroundings. Rising air expands and cools; sinking air is compressed and warms.
:::

:::definition{term="Lapse rate"}
The rate at which temperature falls with height. The **environmental lapse rate (ELR)** is the actual temperature change in the surrounding air; the **dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR)** is the cooling of unsaturated rising air; the **saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR)** is the cooling of saturated rising air.
:::

:::definition{term="Atmospheric stability"}
A stable atmosphere resists vertical movement: a displaced parcel returns to its starting level. In an unstable atmosphere a parcel that is lifted becomes warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising.
:::

## Changes of state

Water changes state by taking up or giving out latent heat. **Evaporation** (liquid to vapour) and **melting** (solid to liquid) absorb energy. **Condensation** (vapour to liquid) and **freezing** (liquid to solid) release it. **Sublimation** is the direct change between solid and vapour (ice to vapour; the reverse change is also called deposition, for example frost forming). Condensation needs cooling to the dew point and tiny particles called condensation nuclei (dust, salt, smoke) on which droplets form.

## Lapse rates

| Lapse rate | Value (approximate) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ELR | average about 6.5 °C per 1 000 m | Varies with time and place; may be negative in an inversion |
| DALR | about 10 °C per 1 000 m (9.8) | Fixed; unsaturated air |
| SALR | about 5–9 °C per 1 000 m | Slower than the DALR because condensation releases latent heat; about 5 in warm moist air, nearer 9 in very cold air |

## Stability and instability

Compare the rising parcel with the surrounding air (the ELR):

1. **Absolutely unstable**: ELR greater than the DALR. A lifted parcel stays warmer than its surroundings at every height and keeps rising.
2. **Absolutely stable**: ELR smaller than the SALR. A lifted parcel becomes colder than the surroundings and sinks back. A **temperature inversion** (temperature rising with height) is the extreme case and traps smoke and fog under it.
3. **Conditionally unstable**: ELR between the SALR and DALR. The air is stable while unsaturated but unstable if lifted enough for condensation to start.

Heating from below, as over hot land in summer, steepens the ELR and makes the air unstable; air sinking in a high-pressure system warms and makes it stable.

## Causes of precipitation

Air must cool to its dew point and have enough ice or large droplets for rain, snow or hail to fall. Cooling comes from:

- **Convection.** Strong surface heating makes moist air rise. It cools, condenses, releases latent heat and often rises to great heights as cumulonimbus cloud, giving heavy local rain, often with thunder and sometimes hail. It is the main cause of summer rain in Zimbabwe.
- **Orographic (relief) rainfall.** Air forced to rise over high ground cools, condenses and rains on the windward side. The leeward side is a **rain shadow** because the air has lost its moisture and warms as it sinks. The Eastern Highlands, such as Nyanga and Chimanimani, receive far more rain than the lowveld to the west or south.
- **Frontal rainfall.** Where a warm air mass meets a cold one, the warm air is forced to rise over the cold. Cold fronts produce intense, narrow bands of rain; warm fronts produce longer, lighter rain. Frontal rain is typical of mid-latitudes, and cold fronts passing south of Zimbabwe affect it mainly in winter by bringing cold weather.
- **Radiation cooling.** On clear, calm nights the ground and the air touching it cool below the dew point, giving dew, mist, radiation fog or frost. This produces little or no rain.

## How raindrops form

A cloud droplet is about 0.02 mm across and a raindrop 1–5 mm, so droplets must grow about a million times in volume. Two theories explain this.

- **Collision–coalescence** (warm clouds, above 0 °C). Larger droplets fall faster than smaller ones, collide with them and merge, growing until they are heavy enough to fall. It is important in tropical clouds. *(ZIMSEC detail)*
- **Bergeron–Findeisen process** (cold clouds). Where the air is below freezing, supercooled water droplets and ice crystals exist together. Because the saturation vapour pressure is lower over ice than over water, vapour moves to the ice crystals, which grow at the expense of the droplets. Heavy crystals fall and melt into rain if the air below is warm. *(ZIMSEC detail)*

## Forms of moisture

- **Cloud**: a mass of water droplets or ice crystals. Cumulus and cumulonimbus (heaped) form in rising air, stratus (layered) in gentle uplift, and cirrus (high, wispy) is made of ice.
- **Rain**: liquid drops that reach the ground.
- **Hail**: balls of layered ice formed in cumulonimbus clouds where strong up-currents carry ice pellets repeatedly above the freezing level.
- **Snow**: ice crystals that reach the ground without melting.
- **Dew**: water that condenses on cold surfaces at night.
- **Fog**: cloud at ground level. **Radiation fog** forms on clear, calm nights in valleys; **advection fog** forms when warm, moist air moves over a cold surface, as along the Namib coast beside the Benguela Current.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[How high does the cloud base form, and is the air stable?]
A parcel of air at the surface has a temperature of 30 °C and a dew point of 18 °C. Use the approximation that the condensation level is 125 m for each degree between the temperature and the dew point.

1. Dew point depression = $30 - 18 = 12$ °C.
2. Condensation level (cloud base) = $12 \times 125 = 1\,500$ m.
3. Temperature at the cloud base, cooling at the DALR (10 °C per 1 000 m): $30 - 1.5 \times 10 = 15$ °C.
4. Above the cloud base the parcel cools at the SALR, taken as 5 °C per 1 000 m. At 3 000 m: $15 - 1.5 \times 5 = 7.5$ °C.
5. Compare with the surrounding air at 3 000 m. If the ELR is 6.5 °C per 1 000 m, the air is $30 - 3 \times 6.5 = 10.5$ °C. The parcel (7.5 °C) is colder, so it would sink back: the air is stable.
6. If the ELR is 8 °C per 1 000 m, the surroundings are $30 - 3 \times 8 = 6$ °C. The parcel (7.5 °C) is warmer, so it keeps rising: the air is unstable.
:::

:::worked-solution[Classifying stability from temperature readings]
Temperatures from a balloon sounding: 28 °C at ground level, 21 °C at 1 000 m, 13 °C at 2 000 m.

1. ELR for 0–1 000 m: $28 - 21 = 7$ °C per 1 000 m.
2. ELR for 1 000–2 000 m: $21 - 13 = 8$ °C per 1 000 m.
3. Both values lie between the SALR (about 5) and the DALR (10), so the layer is conditionally unstable: stable if the rising air is unsaturated, unstable if it is lifted until saturated.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a rain shadow"}
Illustrative data: Station W lies on the windward slope of a range at 1 700 m and receives 1 350 mm of rain a year; Station L lies in a valley on the leeward side at 900 m and receives 620 mm. **Describe:** the windward station receives 730 mm more than the leeward station (1 350 − 620), more than twice as much. **Explain:** moist air from the ocean is forced to rise over the range, cools to its dew point, condenses and rains on the windward side. On the leeward side the dry air descends, warms by compression and can hold more water vapour, so little rain falls.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect changes of state, lapse rates, stability, the four causes of precipitation and the types of moisture. The Bergeron–Findeisen and collision–coalescence theories of raindrop formation are named by ZIMSEC only (4.3.2); Cambridge 2.2 does not require them.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the lapse rates"}
The ELR is the real temperature of the surrounding air; the DALR and SALR describe a rising parcel. Compare the parcel with the surroundings, never one lapse rate with another without saying which is which.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying warm air rises because it is lighter without explaining the cooling"}
After uplift, the air expands and cools adiabatically. Rain forms only if it cools to the dew point; mention condensation and latent heat release.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all mountain rainfall relief rainfall"}
Relief rainfall needs moist air forced to rise over high ground. Convectional storms over hills are triggered by heating, not by forced uplift.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 4) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 2). *State* a lapse rate with its unit. *Calculate* temperature at height by multiplying the lapse rate by the height in thousands of metres, and say whether the air is stable. *Explain* a type of precipitation as a chain: cause of uplift, cooling, dew point, condensation, latent heat, growth of drops. *Compare* the types by their cause, location and intensity. In ZIMSEC *explain how raindrops form* requires both theories and the conditions each needs.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Evaporation and melting absorb latent heat; condensation and freezing release it.
- Typical lapse rates: ELR about 6.5, DALR about 10 and SALR about 5–9 °C per 1 000 m.
- Air is stable when the ELR is below the SALR, unstable when above the DALR, and conditionally unstable in between.
- Precipitation forms by convection, orographic uplift, frontal uplift and radiation cooling; the last gives dew, fog and frost rather than rain.
- Raindrops grow by collision–coalescence (warm clouds) or by the Bergeron–Findeisen process (cold clouds), a ZIMSEC detail.
- Hail, snow, dew and fog have particular conditions: strong up-currents, ice crystals, ground cooling and saturated air near the ground.
:::
