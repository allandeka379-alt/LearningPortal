## Introduction

Zimbabwe's weather is not the same every day or in every season. A cool, misty drizzle (known in Shona-speaking areas as *guti*) can settle over Harare for days in the cool season, then give way to clear skies and cold nights. In October the heat is dry and strong, while in January the air feels thick and thunderclouds build in the afternoon. These changes occur because different **air masses** reach the country, and because pressure systems move across it. You should already know how pressure and wind belts shift with the seasons (see the lesson on global energy transfers).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Air mass"}
A very large body of air, hundreds or thousands of kilometres across, in which temperature and humidity are fairly uniform horizontally. It takes on these properties from the **source region** where it stays for some time.
:::

:::definition{term="Source region"}
A large, uniform surface (such as a desert, ocean or snow field), usually under a high-pressure system with light winds, where an air mass acquires its temperature and moisture.
:::

:::definition{term="Cyclone (depression)"}
A low-pressure system in which air converges and rises, producing cloud, rain and wind. In this topic a "cyclone" means a depression; tropical cyclones are a separate topic.
:::

:::definition{term="Anticyclone"}
A high-pressure system in which air descends and spreads out at the surface. It brings clear skies, light winds and stable conditions.
:::

## Classifying air masses

Air masses are named by temperature (from their latitude) and by moisture (continental = dry, maritime = moist):

| Air mass | Symbol | Typical properties |
|---|---|---|
| Equatorial maritime | mE | Hot, very humid, unstable |
| Tropical continental | cT | Hot, dry, clear skies when stable |
| Tropical maritime | mT | Warm, moist, becomes cloudy and drizzly when lifted |
| Polar continental | cP | Cold, dry, stable |
| Polar maritime | mP | Cool, moist, unstable when moving over warmer surfaces |

## Modification

An air mass changes as it moves away from its source region.

1. **Heating from below.** Cold air moving over a warm surface is warmed from below, becomes unstable, and gives showers and cumulus cloud.
2. **Cooling from below.** Warm air moving over a cold surface is cooled from below, becomes stable, and can produce fog and low stratus cloud.
3. **Gaining moisture.** Dry air crossing a warm sea picks up water vapour and becomes humid.
4. **Losing moisture.** Moist air forced over mountains loses moisture as rain.

## Air masses and Zimbabwe

Zimbabwe lies inland, between the Indian Ocean and the Kalahari, and several air masses reach it:

- **Tropical maritime air from the Indian Ocean.** Brought by south-easterly winds around the South Indian Ocean high-pressure system, it is moist. Where it is forced to rise on the Eastern Highlands it brings cloud and drizzle and, spreading inland, the cool misty weather called guti. It also contributes to summer rainfall.
- **Equatorial and Congo air.** Moist air from the north-west and the Congo basin reaches the country in the rainy season and meets the south-easterly air along the Congo Air Boundary. The convergence and rising air give heavy showers and thunderstorms.
- **Tropical continental air.** Dry air from the interior of southern Africa, hot in the dry season. It is associated with clear skies, low humidity and high daytime temperatures, especially before the rains in September and October.
- **Cold air from higher latitudes.** Behind cold fronts that pass south of the continent in the cool season, cold dry air spreads northward. It produces cold snaps, clear nights and frost in the highveld.

## Cyclones (depressions)

A depression has low pressure at the centre. Air converges, rises, cools and condenses, so cloud and rain are typical. In the southern hemisphere the winds spiral **clockwise** and inwards, and in the northern hemisphere anticlockwise. Mid-latitude depressions form along the polar front, where warm air and cold air meet, and have a **warm front** and a **cold front**:

![Cross-section of a depression: a cold front undercuts the warm air steeply, lifting it into tall cloud with heavy showers, while at the warm front warm air rises gently over cold air ahead, giving thickening cloud and steady rain.](media:diagrams/fronts-cross-section.png "Figure: a cold front and a warm front in cross-section (schematic)")

- **Warm front.** Warm air rises gently over the cold air: cloud thickens and lowers, with steady rain, and the temperature rises after it passes.
- **Cold front.** Cold air undercuts the warm air, which is lifted steeply: tall cumulonimbus clouds, heavy rain or showers, then a sharp drop in temperature, a rise in pressure and clearer skies.

Frontal depressions affect southern Africa chiefly in the cooler months, mostly south of Zimbabwe, but the cold fronts can still bring cooler weather.

## Anticyclones

An anticyclone has high pressure at the centre, with air descending (subsiding) and spreading outwards. The sinking air warms, which prevents cloud from forming. In the southern hemisphere the winds spiral **anticlockwise** and outwards. Weather is dry with light winds. In summer, anticyclones can bring heat; in winter, clear skies allow strong night-time cooling, so frost and radiation fog are common. The subtropical high-pressure belt over southern Africa is the reason for Zimbabwe's dry season.

## Reading weather maps

- **Isobars** join places of equal pressure (usually in hPa). The closer together they are, the greater the pressure gradient and the stronger the wind.
- Wind blows almost parallel to the isobars, turning slightly towards low pressure at the surface because of friction.
- **Fronts** are shown by lines with triangles (cold) or semicircles (warm).
- On satellite images a depression shows as a comma- or spiral-shaped band of bright cloud; an anticyclone shows as a large area of cloud-free or patchy low cloud.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Pressure gradient and wind strength]
On a weather map the 1 008 hPa isobar is 600 km from the 1 020 hPa isobar over the sea. Over the land the 1 012 hPa and 1 016 hPa isobars are 800 km apart.

1. Sea: pressure difference $= 1020 - 1008 = 12$ hPa. Gradient $= 12 \div 600 = 0.02$ hPa/km $= 2$ hPa per 100 km.
2. Land: difference $= 1016 - 1012 = 4$ hPa. Gradient $= 4 \div 800 = 0.005$ hPa/km $= 0.5$ hPa per 100 km.
3. The gradient over the sea is four times as steep, so winds there will be stronger.
:::

:::worked-solution[Passage of a cold front]
Illustrative readings at a station in the southern hemisphere:

![Passage of a cold front at a station: pressure falls to a minimum of 1004 hPa at 12:00 then rises 10 hPa to 1014 hPa by 18:00, while temperature falls 12 degrees C from 27 at 12:00 to 15 at 18:00, most of it between 12:00 and 15:00 when the front passes.](media:diagrams/cold-front-passage-graph.png "Figure: pressure and temperature readings during the passage of a cold front (data from the worked example)")

| Time | Pressure (hPa) | Temperature (°C) | Wind direction | Weather |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 06:00 | 1010 | 24 | North-west | Warm, hazy |
| 09:00 | 1007 | 26 | North-west | Cloud thickening |
| 12:00 | 1004 | 27 | West | Heavy rain begins |
| 15:00 | 1009 | 17 | South-west | Showers |
| 18:00 | 1014 | 15 | South-west | Clearing, cold |

1. Pressure fell to a minimum of 1 004 hPa at 12:00 and then rose by $1014 - 1004 = 10$ hPa by 18:00.
2. Temperature fell by $27 - 15 = 12$ °C between 12:00 and 18:00, with most of the fall (10 °C) between 12:00 and 15:00.
3. The wind changed from north-west to south-west as warm air was replaced by cold air.
4. These changes show the cold front passed between 12:00 and 15:00. The heavy rain at 12:00 shows the steeply rising air along the front.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a satellite image"}
A satellite image in July shows a large cloud-free area over Zimbabwe and Botswana, a comma-shaped band of cloud south of Cape Town, and thin cloud along the Mozambique coast. **Interpretation:** the clear area is a subtropical anticyclone with sinking air, giving dry weather and cold nights in the cool season. The comma of cloud is a depression with a cold front, moving east south of the continent; its trailing cold air may bring a cold snap to the highveld a day or two later. The coastal cloud suggests moist tropical maritime air from the Indian Ocean.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing the rotation"}
In the southern hemisphere the air spirals clockwise around a low and anticlockwise around a high. The directions reverse north of the equator.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing a cyclone with a tropical cyclone"}
In this topic a cyclone means a mid-latitude depression. A tropical cyclone is a separate, intense storm that forms over warm oceans and has its own lesson.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the air mass 'causes' weather without naming the process"}
Always link the air mass properties to the weather: moist and lifted gives cloud and drizzle; dry and sinking gives clear skies.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An air mass takes its temperature and moisture from its source region; the symbols combine continental or maritime with equatorial, tropical, polar.
- Air masses are modified by heating or cooling from below and by gaining or losing moisture.
- Moist tropical maritime air, Congo air, dry tropical continental air and cold air behind fronts all affect Zimbabwe.
- Depressions have rising air, cloud and rain, with warm and cold fronts; anticyclones have sinking air and clear weather.
- In the southern hemisphere winds turn clockwise round lows and anticlockwise round highs.
- Close isobars mean a steep pressure gradient and strong winds.
:::
