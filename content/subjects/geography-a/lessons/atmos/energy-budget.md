## Introduction

Why does a clear winter night on the Zimbabwean highveld bring frost, while a cloudy night stays mild? Why is a tarred car park painful to stand on at noon but a maize field is not? The answer lies in the **energy budget**: the balance between the energy a place receives and the energy it loses. Nearly all the energy that drives weather and climate comes from the Sun, so understanding the budget is the starting point for the whole atmosphere topic, for climate change and for microclimates.

You should already know that the Sun heats the Earth unevenly and that the atmosphere is a mixture of gases including water vapour and carbon dioxide. This lesson follows the energy through the atmosphere, at the global scale and then over a single day.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Insolation (incoming solar radiation)"}
The shortwave energy from the Sun that reaches the top of the atmosphere or a surface, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). The energy arriving at the top of the atmosphere, facing the Sun, is about 1 360 W/m² (the solar constant); the average over the whole spinning, spherical Earth is about a quarter of that.
:::

:::definition{term="Albedo"}
The fraction of incoming shortwave radiation that a surface or the planet reflects, written as a decimal from 0 to 1 (or as a percentage). $\text{albedo} = \dfrac{\text{reflected radiation}}{\text{incoming radiation}}$. The Earth as a whole has an albedo of about 0.3.
:::

:::definition{term="Energy budget"}
An account of the inputs, outputs and stores of energy of a system. When inputs equal outputs the system is in balance and its average temperature stays steady.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural greenhouse effect"}
The warming of the lower atmosphere and surface caused by naturally occurring gases (mainly water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone), which absorb outgoing longwave radiation and radiate part of it back downwards.
:::

## Shortwave in, longwave out

The Sun is very hot, so it emits mainly **shortwave radiation** (ultraviolet, visible light and some near-infrared). The Earth is much cooler, so it emits **longwave (infrared) radiation**. This difference is the key to the greenhouse effect: the atmosphere is mostly transparent to incoming shortwave radiation but absorbs a large part of outgoing longwave radiation.

## What happens to incoming radiation

Of every 100 units of incoming solar energy, a simplified and rounded budget shows:

| Fate of 100 units of insolation | Units |
|---|---|
| Reflected by clouds and scattered by the atmosphere back to space | 26 |
| Reflected by the Earth's surface (snow, ice, sand, water) | 4 |
| Absorbed by the atmosphere (ozone, water vapour, clouds, dust) | 20 |
| Absorbed by the land and oceans | 50 |

The reflected parts add up to 30 units, which gives the planetary albedo of about 0.3. Sources differ by a few units; treat these as approximate.

1. **Reflection.** Bright surfaces and cloud tops send radiation back unchanged. Thick cloud reflects a great deal.
2. **Scattering.** Gas molecules and dust change the direction of some radiation; some of it goes back to space and some reaches the ground as diffuse light. Scattering of the shorter blue wavelengths gives the daytime sky its colour.
3. **Absorption.** Ozone absorbs most ultraviolet, and water vapour, clouds and dust absorb some of the rest. Absorbed energy heats the atmosphere directly.
4. **Surface absorption.** The energy that reaches the ground warms the soil, rock, vegetation and water.

## Transfers from the surface

A warm surface loses energy in four ways:

- **Longwave radiation** upwards, most of which is absorbed by greenhouse gases and clouds.
- **Conduction**: direct transfer of heat from the ground to the thin layer of air touching it. Air conducts poorly, so this matters only close to the surface.
- **Sensible heat**: heat carried upwards by rising, warmed air (convection) that you can feel and measure with a thermometer.
- **Latent heat**: energy used to evaporate water (including transpiration from plants). It is hidden in the water vapour and released as heat when the vapour condenses into cloud, perhaps hundreds of kilometres away.

The atmosphere in turn radiates longwave energy both upwards to space and downwards to the surface. The downward part is **back radiation**.

## The natural greenhouse effect

Without greenhouse gases, the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C. With them it is about +15 °C, a difference of about 33 °C. Back radiation is the reason that the surface is warmer than it would be if it simply balanced the energy it absorbs from the Sun. The natural greenhouse effect is therefore essential for life. Do not confuse it with the **enhanced** greenhouse effect, which is the extra warming caused by human emissions (see the lesson on evidence and causes of climate change).

## Day and night, clear and cloudy

By day the surface receives more shortwave energy than it loses, so it warms. At night there is no insolation, so the surface cools by emitting longwave radiation. **Clear skies** let more of that radiation escape to space, so nights are cold, and in cold seasons frost can form. **Cloud** absorbs longwave radiation and sends much of it back (strong back radiation), so cloudy nights are warmer. By day, cloud does the opposite: it reflects insolation and keeps maximum temperatures lower. Cloud cover therefore reduces the **diurnal range** (the difference between the highest and lowest temperatures in 24 hours). Dry air has the same effect as a clear sky: deserts have large diurnal ranges.

:::keyfact{title="Budget rule"}
A surface warms when absorbed energy exceeds energy lost, and cools when losses exceed gains. Low albedo means more absorption. Clear skies mean more heat loss at night.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Absorption and albedo]
Illustrative values: at noon a surface receives 800 W/m² of shortwave radiation. The table shows how much each surface reflects and absorbs, using typical albedos (approximate; real values vary with sun angle, moisture and condition).

| Surface | Albedo | Reflected (W/m²) | Absorbed (W/m²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open water (calm, high sun) | 0.06 | 48 | 752 |
| Dense forest | 0.12 | 96 | 704 |
| Grassland (savanna) | 0.20 | 160 | 640 |
| Bare dry sand | 0.35 | 280 | 520 |
| Fresh snow | 0.85 | 680 | 120 |

1. Reflected = insolation × albedo. For grassland: $800 \times 0.20 = 160$ W/m².
2. Absorbed = insolation − reflected = $800 - 160 = 640$ W/m² (or $800 \times (1 - 0.20)$).
3. Fresh snow absorbs only 120 W/m², which is why snow stays frozen under strong sun. Water and forest absorb the most.
4. Surfaces that absorb more warm faster, but water has a high heat capacity and uses much of its energy for evaporation, so it warms slowly.
:::

:::worked-solution[Is the global budget balanced?]
Using the 100-unit budget above:

1. Units returned to space by reflection and scattering: $26 + 4 = 30$. Albedo = $30 \div 100 = 0.30$.
2. Units absorbed by the Earth and atmosphere: $20 + 50 = 70$.
3. For a steady average temperature the planet must lose the same 70 units as longwave radiation to space. Inputs ($100$) = outputs ($30 + 70$), so the system is in balance.
4. If greenhouse gases increase, less longwave radiation escapes at first, outputs fall below inputs and the planet warms until it again emits 70 units.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining a day and night comparison"}
Illustrative data for a highveld station on a clear day and on a cloudy day (temperatures in °C every three hours):

| Time | Clear sky (°C) | Cloudy (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| 00:00 | 11 | 15 |
| 03:00 | 9 | 14 |
| 06:00 | 8 | 14 |
| 09:00 | 16 | 17 |
| 12:00 | 25 | 21 |
| 15:00 | 28 | 22 |
| 18:00 | 22 | 20 |
| 21:00 | 14 | 17 |

**Describe:** on the clear day the temperature ranges from 8 °C (06:00) to 28 °C (15:00), a diurnal range of 20 °C. On the cloudy day it ranges from 14 °C to 22 °C, a range of only 8 °C. **Explain:** by day the cloud reflects and absorbs insolation, so the afternoon peak is 6 °C lower; at night the cloud sends longwave radiation back to the surface, so the minimum is 6 °C higher.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing the greenhouse effect with global warming"}
The natural greenhouse effect keeps the Earth habitable. Global warming is the extra warming from the enhanced greenhouse effect. Say which one you mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the atmosphere is heated mainly by the Sun directly"}
Most shortwave radiation passes through the atmosphere. The lower atmosphere is heated mostly from below, by longwave radiation, conduction, convection and latent heat from the warm surface.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the radiation types"}
Insolation is shortwave; terrestrial radiation is longwave. Back radiation is longwave radiation sent down by the atmosphere, not reflected sunlight.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 4) and Cambridge Paper 1 (topic 2). *State* and *identify* need a term or a value with units. *Describe* a graph or table with figures (for example the diurnal range) and a trend. *Explain* needs the process in order: shortwave in, absorbed or reflected, longwave out, back radiation. *Compare* day and night or clear and cloudy using both sides in each sentence. A calculation of absorbed radiation needs the formula, the substitution and W/m².
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Sun emits shortwave radiation; the Earth emits longwave radiation, and greenhouse gases absorb much of it.
- Insolation is reflected, scattered or absorbed; about 30% is returned to space (planetary albedo about 0.3).
- Surface losses are by longwave radiation, conduction, sensible heat and latent heat; the atmosphere returns back radiation.
- The natural greenhouse effect raises average surface temperature from about −18 °C to about +15 °C.
- Clear skies give high diurnal ranges and cold nights; cloud reduces the range.
- Albedo: reflected ÷ incoming; absorbed = insolation × (1 − albedo).
:::
