## Introduction

On a still, clear night in Harare the air in the city centre can feel several degrees warmer than the air in a farming area only a few kilometres away. Farmers in a highveld valley lose crops to frost while neighbours on the slope above are untouched. Hikers in a forest on the Eastern Highlands feel the air cool and damp as they step off a sunlit path. These are **microclimates**: local climates, from a few metres to tens of kilometres across, that differ from the general climate of the surrounding region. This lesson uses the energy budget and the behaviour of air to explain several of them. You should be comfortable with albedo, latent heat and the idea that air moves from high to low pressure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Microclimate"}
The climate of a small area, such as a city, a valley, a forest or a lake shore, which differs from the climate of the surrounding area because of its surface, shape or position.
:::

:::definition{term="Urban heat island (UHI)"}
The higher temperature of a built-up area compared with the surrounding countryside. The UHI intensity is the temperature difference between the urban and rural values at the same time.
:::

:::definition{term="Katabatic and anabatic winds"}
Katabatic winds are cold, dense air draining downslope, mainly at night. Anabatic winds flow upslope by day when the slope is warmed.
:::

## Urban heat islands

Several features of cities combine to raise temperatures:

1. **Surfaces.** Tar, concrete, brick and corrugated metal have low albedo (they absorb much solar radiation) and store a lot of heat, which they release slowly at night.
2. **Less evapotranspiration.** Fewer plants and rapid drainage mean less energy goes into latent heat, so more goes into heating the air.
3. **Urban geometry.** Tall buildings and narrow streets trap radiation by multiple reflection and reduce loss of longwave radiation to the sky.
4. **Waste heat.** Vehicles, industry, air-conditioners and cooking add heat.
5. **Air pollution.** Smog and dust can absorb and re-emit longwave radiation, and slow cooling at night.

The UHI is usually strongest at night, after clear, calm weather, because the countryside cools quickly while the city releases its stored heat. The difference is typically a few degrees Celsius and sometimes more than 5 °C. Cities are also often less humid and, because of rising warm air and particles that act as condensation nuclei, may receive slightly more convectional rain downwind. Effects include higher energy demand for cooling, heat stress for people (especially children, the elderly and outdoor workers), changed plant and insect behaviour in parks, and worse air quality.

## Land and sea breezes

By day, land warms faster than water because it has a lower heat capacity. Air above the land warms and rises, pressure falls, and cooler air flows in from the sea: the **sea breeze**, strongest in the afternoon. At night the land cools faster, so the pattern reverses: air flows from the land to the sea in the **land breeze**. Large lakes, such as Kariba, produce similar but weaker winds. These breezes moderate coastal temperatures and can trigger afternoon cloud and showers inland.

## Valley and slope winds

By day, slopes facing the Sun heat the air touching them, which rises along the slope as an **anabatic** wind. At night the slope surface cools by radiation and chills the air above it. This cold, dense air flows downslope as a **katabatic** wind and collects in valley bottoms and hollows. The result is a pool of cold air, called a **frost hollow**, where frost is more likely than higher up the slope. The area just above the cold pool (the "thermal belt") is warmer, so frost-sensitive crops are better planted on the middle of a slope than on the valley floor. On highveld vleis (shallow wet hollows) frost is common on clear winter nights for the same reason.

## Forest and water body microclimates

- **Forests.** The canopy intercepts radiation and shades the ground, so by day the air below is cooler than in a clearing. Transpiration cools the air, humidity is higher, and wind speeds are lower. At night the canopy slows loss of longwave radiation, so the forest is warmer than open ground. The result is a smaller temperature range.
- **Water bodies.** Water has a high heat capacity, so lakes and dams change temperature slowly. Nearby land is cooler by day and warmer at night than places far from the water. Evaporation raises humidity and the air close to the water may be cooler by day.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Urban heat island intensity]
Illustrative air temperatures (°C) at an urban and a rural site in calm, clear weather:

| Time | 00:00 | 03:00 | 06:00 | 09:00 | 12:00 | 15:00 | 18:00 | 21:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Urban | 21 | 20 | 19 | 24 | 29 | 32 | 28 | 24 |
| Rural | 17 | 15 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 30 | 25 | 20 |
| Difference | 4 | 5 | 5 | 3 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |

1. UHI intensity at each time = urban − rural, shown in the last row.
2. The largest difference is 5 °C, at 03:00 and 06:00. This is the maximum UHI intensity.
3. Mean difference $= 27 \div 8 = 3.4$ °C (to 1 decimal place).
4. The difference is smallest (1 °C) at 12:00 because the rural surface is also strongly heated by day.
5. Explanation: after sunset the countryside loses longwave radiation freely, but the city's surfaces release stored heat and the buildings reduce radiation loss.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing rainfall records]
Illustrative total seasonal rainfall (mm) over five seasons for a city and a rural station 30 km away:

| Season | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| City | 910 | 840 | 1010 | 770 | 880 |
| Rural | 860 | 800 | 940 | 760 | 820 |

1. City mean $= 4410 \div 5 = 882$ mm. Rural mean $= 4180 \div 5 = 836$ mm.
2. Difference $= 882 - 836 = 46$ mm.
3. As a percentage of the rural mean: $46 \div 836 \times 100 = 5.5\%$.
4. The city was wetter in every season, but the difference is small relative to year-to-year variation, so five seasons are not enough to prove an urban effect. More years and more stations are needed.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a forest microclimate"}
Illustrative midday readings in the Eastern Highlands: a clearing has an air temperature of 27 °C and relative humidity of 48%; under the forest canopy the temperature is 22 °C and humidity 78%. **Describe:** the forest is 5 °C cooler and 30 percentage points more humid. **Explain:** the canopy absorbs and reflects sunlight and shades the ground; trees transpire, cooling the air and adding water vapour; and the dense vegetation reduces wind so that moist air stays in place.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cities are warmer only because of pollution"}
The main causes are surface materials, lack of evapotranspiration, geometry and waste heat. Pollution is one factor among several.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing day and night breezes"}
A sea breeze blows from the sea to the land by day; a land breeze blows from the land to the sea at night.
:::

:::mistake{title="Placing frost-sensitive crops in the valley"}
Cold air flows downslope and collects in the lowest ground, so valley floors are frost hollows. Hilltops and mid-slopes are usually warmer on clear nights.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Microclimates are local climates that differ from the surrounding area; surface, shape and position control them.
- Urban heat islands result from low-albedo surfaces, heat storage, little evapotranspiration, geometry, waste heat and pollution, and are strongest on calm, clear nights.
- Sea breezes blow onshore by day and land breezes offshore at night because land heats and cools faster than water.
- Anabatic winds rise by day; katabatic winds drain downslope at night and form frost hollows in valleys.
- Forests and water bodies reduce temperature extremes and raise humidity.
- Compare microclimates with nearby areas using differences and means, but allow for the length of record.
:::
