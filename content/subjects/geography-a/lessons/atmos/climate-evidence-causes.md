## Introduction

Southern Africa has recorded some of its hottest seasons and most severe droughts in recent decades, and the Zambezi's Kariba reservoir has fallen to alarmingly low levels in several dry years. Are these events part of a lasting change in the climate, and if so, what is causing it? Scientists answer by gathering evidence from the distant past (proxy records), from thermometers (instrumental records) and from changes in ice, sea level and the composition of the atmosphere. This lesson sets out that evidence and the natural and human causes of climate change. You should already know the natural greenhouse effect (see the lesson on energy budgets).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate change"}
A lasting change in the average state of the climate (mean values and variability), over decades or longer, whether caused by natural processes or by human activity. **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere over a short time; **climate** is the average pattern over about 30 years.
:::

:::definition{term="Proxy record"}
Indirect evidence of past climate preserved in natural archives, such as ice cores, tree rings, pollen, ocean sediments and fossils, used for the time before instruments existed.
:::

:::definition{term="Global warming potential (GWP)"}
How much heat a gas traps over a stated period (usually 100 years) compared with the same mass of carbon dioxide, which has a GWP of 1. **Carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e)** is the emission of a gas multiplied by its GWP.
:::

:::definition{term="Enhanced greenhouse effect"}
The extra warming caused by increases in greenhouse gases from human activity on top of the natural greenhouse effect.
:::

## Evidence for climate change

**Proxy evidence**

- **Ice cores.** Layers of snow compressed into ice trap bubbles of ancient air in Antarctica and Greenland. Cores from Antarctica reach back about 800 000 years. Bubble chemistry gives past carbon dioxide and methane concentrations, and ratios of oxygen isotopes in the ice give past temperatures.
- **Tree rings.** A tree usually adds one ring a year; the width and density of the rings reflect growing conditions, such as warmth or rainfall, in each year.
- **Pollen, sediments and fossils.** Pollen preserved in lake or peat deposits shows which plants grew in the past; shells of tiny marine organisms in ocean sediments, and fossils, show past temperatures and sea levels.

**Instrumental and observational evidence**

- **Temperature records.** Thermometer records since about 1850, from land, ships and later satellites, show that global average surface temperature has risen. The IPCC reports that the period 2011–2020 was about 1.1 °C warmer than 1850–1900.
- **Carbon dioxide.** Concentration was about 280 parts per million (ppm) before the industrial era and has risen above 420 ppm in the 2020s; the rise matches the temperature rise and the burning of fossil fuels.
- **Sea level.** Global mean sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018 (IPCC), through expansion of warming water and melting of land ice, and the rate has increased.
- **Ice.** The extent of Arctic sea ice in September has fallen sharply since satellite records began in 1979, mountain glaciers are retreating worldwide, and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass.

**Evaluating the evidence.** No single record proves climate change, but independent records agree. Proxy records have uncertainty and limited coverage; early instrumental records have few stations in the oceans and the southern hemisphere; local weather varies from year to year, so trends must be judged over decades.

## Greenhouse gases

| Gas | Main sources | GWP over 100 years (rounded) | Lifetime in the atmosphere (approximate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (CO2) | Burning fossil fuels, deforestation, cement | 1 | Hundreds of years and longer (a share stays for thousands) |
| Methane (CH4) | Livestock, rice fields, landfill, gas leaks, wetlands | About 28 | About 12 years |
| Nitrous oxide (N2O) | Fertilisers, burning biomass | About 265 | About 110 years |
| F-gases (HFCs, PFCs, SF6) | Refrigeration, air conditioning, industry | Hundreds to over 20 000 | Decades to thousands of years |

Values are rounded and differ slightly between IPCC reports. Carbon dioxide has the greatest total effect because so much is emitted and it persists, even though other gases trap more heat per tonne. Water vapour is the strongest natural greenhouse gas, but its amount responds to temperature, so it amplifies warming rather than starting it.

## Natural causes

- **Solar output.** The Sun's output varies by about 0.1% over the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle; this has only a small effect on global temperature. Over tens of thousands of years, changes in the Earth's orbit and tilt change how much sunlight reaches each latitude and are the main pacemaker of ice ages.
- **Volcanic eruptions.** Large eruptions inject sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it forms aerosols that reflect sunlight. After the eruption of Mount Pinatubo (1991) global temperatures fell by about 0.5 °C for a year or two. Volcanoes also emit CO2, but far less than human activity.
- **ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation).** In normal conditions trade winds blow warm surface water westwards across the Pacific, and cold water rises off South America. In an **El Niño** the trades weaken, warm water spreads east and global temperatures rise temporarily; in southern Africa El Niño seasons tend to be hotter and drier, as in the droughts of 1991–92 and 2015–16. In a **La Niña** the trades strengthen and the eastern Pacific cools; southern Africa tends to be wetter, with a greater risk of floods. ENSO changes climate from year to year but does not explain the long-term warming. *(ZIMSEC detail: El Niño and La Niña.)*

## Human causes

1. **Burning fossil fuels** (coal, oil, gas) for electricity, transport and industry releases CO2 and is the largest source of the increase.
2. **Deforestation and land-use change** release stored carbon and reduce the carbon absorbed by growing trees.
3. **Agriculture** releases methane from livestock and rice fields and nitrous oxide from fertilisers.
4. **Industry and cooling** release F-gases.
5. **Albedo changes** such as replacing forest by farmland or cities, and melting snow, change the energy absorbed. Human air pollution also adds aerosols, which cool the planet in places and temporarily mask some warming.

The scientific assessment of the IPCC concludes that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Carbon dioxide equivalent]
Illustrative annual emissions of a country: carbon dioxide 20 million tonnes (Mt), methane 0.6 Mt and nitrous oxide 0.02 Mt. Use GWPs of 1, 28 and 265.

1. CO2e of CO2 = $20 \times 1 = 20$ Mt.
2. CO2e of methane = $0.6 \times 28 = 16.8$ Mt.
3. CO2e of nitrous oxide = $0.02 \times 265 = 5.3$ Mt.
4. Total = $20 + 16.8 + 5.3 = 42.1$ Mt CO2e.
5. Share from CO2 = $20 \div 42.1 \times 100 = 47.5\%$, so methane (39.9%) and nitrous oxide contribute more than half of the warming effect of this country's emissions, even though their masses are small.
:::

:::worked-solution[Percentage change in carbon dioxide]
CO2 concentration rose from about 280 ppm before the industrial era to about 420 ppm.

1. Increase = $420 - 280 = 140$ ppm.
2. Percentage change = $140 \div 280 \times 100 = 50\%$.
3. The concentration is therefore about half as high again as it was before industrialisation.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a temperature record"}
Illustrative global temperature anomalies (°C above the 1850–1900 average) by decade:

| Decade | 1900s | 1930s | 1960s | 1990s | 2010s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anomaly (°C) | 0.0 | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.6 | 1.1 |

**Describe:** the anomaly rises overall from 0.0 °C to 1.1 °C, with a small fall between the 1930s (0.2 °C) and the 1960s (0.1 °C), and the largest increase is between the 1960s and 2010s (+1.0 °C). **Evaluate:** the overall trend is clear, but the short pause shows that natural variability and aerosols also affect temperatures, so trends must be judged over several decades and with several lines of evidence.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing weather and climate"}
One hot summer or one drought is weather. Climate change is judged from averages over about 30 years.
:::

:::mistake{title="Blaming the ozone layer"}
Ozone depletion (caused by CFCs) is a separate problem from global warming, although some CFC substitutes are also greenhouse gases.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying volcanoes or the Sun explain the recent warming"}
Their effects are real but small or temporary, and they do not match the pattern of warming since 1850. The best match is the rise in greenhouse gases.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Proxy records (ice cores, tree rings, pollen, sediments) extend the record; thermometers, sea level, ice and CO2 measurements show recent change.
- Global temperature is about 1.1 °C above 1850–1900; CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm to over 420 ppm.
- GWP compares gases with CO2 over 100 years; CO2e = mass × GWP. Methane (about 28) and nitrous oxide (about 265) are more potent per tonne; CO2 lasts longest.
- Natural causes (solar output, volcanoes, ENSO) explain variability but not the long-term warming.
- Human causes: fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture, industry and albedo change, giving an enhanced greenhouse effect.
:::
