## Introduction

Zimbabwe lies in the tropics but is not as hot as the lowlands of the equator, and Harare in July is cool and dry while Harare in January is warm and wet. London, at a far higher latitude, is milder than places at the same latitude in northern Canada. These patterns follow from how energy is shared out across the globe by winds and ocean currents. This lesson explains the global scale of the energy budget. You should already know about insolation, albedo and the greenhouse effect from the lesson on energy budgets.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Radiation surplus and deficit"}
A latitude has a surplus when it absorbs more shortwave radiation than it loses as longwave radiation, and a deficit when it loses more than it absorbs. Low latitudes have a surplus; high latitudes have a deficit.
:::

:::definition{term="Tri-cellular model"}
A simplified model of the circulation in each hemisphere with three cells: the Hadley cell (equator to about 30°), the Ferrel cell (about 30° to 60°) and the polar cell (about 60° to the pole).
:::

:::definition{term="Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)"}
The belt near the thermal equator where trade winds from both hemispheres meet, air rises strongly, and heavy cloud and rainfall result. It moves north and south with the overhead Sun, with a time lag.
:::

:::definition{term="Jet stream"}
A narrow, fast-flowing current of air in the upper troposphere, near the boundaries between air masses of different temperature.
:::

## Why there is a surplus and a deficit

Near the equator the Sun is high, so insolation is concentrated on a small area and passes through a thin layer of atmosphere. Near the poles the same energy is spread over a larger area, passes through more atmosphere, and snow and ice reflect much of it (high albedo). Longwave loss varies less with latitude than insolation does. The result is a surplus in the low latitudes and a deficit at high latitudes, with the balance point at about 35° to 40°.

If nothing moved energy poleward, the tropics would get steadily hotter and the poles colder. Instead heat is transferred by the atmosphere and by the oceans, both **vertically** (rising warm air and sinking cold water) and **laterally** (winds and currents moving across the Earth). Both are important; their shares differ with latitude.

## The tri-cellular model

1. **Hadley cell.** Warm air rises at the ITCZ, flows poleward high in the troposphere, cools, and sinks at about 30°, forming the subtropical high-pressure belt. At the surface it returns equatorward as the **trade winds** (from the north-east in the northern hemisphere and the south-east in the southern hemisphere, because of the Coriolis effect).
2. **Polar cell.** Cold air sinks at the poles (high pressure), flows out at the surface as the **polar easterlies**, and rises at about 60° where it meets warmer air.
3. **Ferrel cell.** Between the two, surface air flows poleward from the subtropical high as the **westerlies**. It is driven by the other two cells and is less regular.

![Tri-cellular model for one hemisphere: in the Hadley cell air rises at the equatorial low and sinks at about 30 degrees, returning as the trade winds; in the polar cell air sinks at the pole and rises at 60 degrees, flowing out as polar easterlies; the Ferrel cell between them has westerlies at the surface.](media:diagrams/tricellular-circulation.png "Figure: the three cells of the global atmospheric circulation, with pressure belts and surface winds")

Areas of rising air have low pressure and cloud; areas of sinking air have high pressure and clear, dry conditions. Many of the world's hot deserts, including the Kalahari and Namib, lie under the subtropical highs.

## The ITCZ and the seasons

The thermal equator, the belt of highest temperatures, follows the overhead Sun, which reaches 23.5°N at the June solstice and 23.5°S at the December solstice. The ITCZ, the pressure belts and the wind belts follow with a lag of some weeks. Over land the ITCZ moves much further from the equator than over oceans, because land heats faster. In the southern African summer the ITCZ and associated low pressure lie over the interior, bringing the rains to Zimbabwe between about November and March; in winter the subtropical high dominates and the weather is dry. The belts move roughly 10–15° of latitude between seasons.

## Jet streams

Jet streams occur where there is a strong temperature contrast. The **polar front jet** lies at about 9–12 km near the boundary of polar and temperate air; the **subtropical jet** lies near 30° at a greater height. They flow from west to east at speeds that often exceed 100 km/h and can reach over 300 km/h in winter. They steer depressions and fronts and shift with the seasons.

## Ocean currents and gyres

Winds drive the surface currents, which are deflected by continents into large circular **gyres**: clockwise in the northern hemisphere and anticlockwise in the southern. On the western side of an ocean, currents carry warm water poleward; on the eastern side, cold water flows towards the equator.

- The **Agulhas** and Mozambique currents carry warm water south along the east coast of southern Africa. They help to give the Indian Ocean coast a warm, humid climate.
- The **Benguela Current** carries cold water north along the west coast. It cools the air, reduces evaporation and rainfall, and is one reason for the Namib Desert and its coastal fog.
- The **North Atlantic Drift** (an extension of the Gulf Stream) carries warm water towards north-west Europe and keeps it milder than other places at the same latitude.

Deep ocean water also circulates: cold, salty water sinks in high latitudes and flows slowly towards the tropics. The oceans transfer heat because water has a high heat capacity, so a current carries a very large amount of energy.

:::keyfact{title="Atmosphere and ocean work together"}
Winds drive surface currents; currents warm or cool the air above them and change pressure and rainfall. El Niño and La Niña are examples of the interaction (see the lesson on evidence and causes of climate change).
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Finding the latitude of balance]
Use the net radiation table above. The balance latitude is where net radiation is zero.

1. The values change from +10 W/m² (30–40°, mid-point 35°) to −30 W/m² (40–50°, mid-point 45°).
2. The total change is $10 - (-30) = 40$ W/m² over $10°$ of latitude.
3. Zero is reached after $10 \div 40 = 0.25$ of the way: $35° + 0.25 \times 10° = 37.5°$.
4. The balance point is therefore at about 37–38° in each hemisphere (the real value depends on the data used). Places poleward of it receive heat from lower latitudes.
:::

:::worked-solution[How concentrated is the rainfall season?]
Illustrative monthly rainfall (mm) for a station at about 18° S in the interior:

| Month | J | F | M | A | M | J | J | A | S | O | N | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 190 | 160 | 100 | 30 | 8 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 8 | 35 | 105 | 170 |

1. Annual total: $190 + 160 + 100 + 30 + 8 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 8 + 35 + 105 + 170 = 811$ mm.
2. November to March: $105 + 170 + 190 + 160 + 100 = 725$ mm.
3. Share: $725 \div 811 \times 100 = 89.4\%$.
4. Explanation: the ITCZ and the low-pressure zone move over the region from about November, bringing moist, rising air. From April the subtropical high takes over and almost no rain falls.
:::

:::example{title="Two coasts, two climates"}
Durban and Walvis Bay both lie on the coast of southern Africa, at latitudes only about seven degrees apart, but Durban is humid and well watered while Walvis Bay is cool, foggy and extremely dry. Durban lies on the east coast, beside the warm Agulhas Current and in the path of moist air from the Indian Ocean. Walvis Bay lies on the west coast, beside the cold Benguela Current. Air cooled by the current is stable, so little rain falls, but it produces fog when it meets the warmer land. Ocean currents therefore help to explain differences of climate along the same latitude.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the poles get no energy transfer"}
The poles receive energy by transfer from the tropics. They have a deficit in their own radiation budget, but they are not isolated.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up wind names and directions"}
Winds are named by the direction they blow **from**. The trades blow from the south-east in the southern hemisphere, and the westerlies from the west.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the ITCZ as fixed"}
The ITCZ moves with the seasons and further over land. Explain Zimbabwe's rainy season by its position, not by saying "the equator moves".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Low latitudes have a radiation surplus and high latitudes a deficit; the balance point is at about 35–40°.
- Heat is moved poleward by the atmosphere (cells, winds, jet streams) and by the oceans (currents and gyres).
- The tri-cellular model has Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells; the ITCZ is the rising limb of the Hadley cells.
- Pressure and wind belts follow the overhead Sun with a lag, which explains the seasons of the tropics, including Zimbabwe's rains.
- Warm currents warm coasts; cold currents cool them and can cause aridity and fog.
:::
