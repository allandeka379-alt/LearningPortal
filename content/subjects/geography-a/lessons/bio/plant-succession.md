## Introduction

Walk through a maize field that was abandoned a few years ago and you find it covered with weedy annuals and young grass. Walk through one left for forty years and you may be in woodland. Climb a granite dwala and the bare rock carries only grey-green lichens, while a few metres lower there are mosses, grasses and shrubs. These are snapshots of **plant succession**: the gradual, predictable change in the plant community of a place over time. Succession shows how plants change their own environment and how soil and ecosystems develop. You should already know what photosynthesis, weathering and humus are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plant succession"}
The sequence of changes in the species composition and structure of a plant community over time, as one community modifies the habitat and is replaced by another.
:::

:::definition{term="Sere and seral stage"}
A sere is the whole sequence of communities from the first colonisers to the climax. Each community in the sequence is a seral stage.
:::

:::definition{term="Pioneer species"}
The first species to colonise an area. They tolerate harsh conditions, spread easily and prepare the habitat for later species.
:::

:::definition{term="Climax community"}
The relatively stable final community of a succession, in balance with the climate and soil of the area, which does not change unless the environment changes.
:::

:::definition{term="Plagioclimax"}
A community held at an earlier stage than the climatic climax by continuing human activity such as burning, grazing or mowing.
:::

## Primary succession

Primary succession begins where **no soil and no previous community** exist: bare rock, new lava, sand, or open water. It is slow, often taking hundreds of years.

### Lithosere (starting on bare rock)

On a granite outcrop in Zimbabwe the sequence is:

1. **Crustose lichens and algae** colonise the bare rock. They tolerate drought and release weak acids that weather the surface.
2. **Foliose lichens and mosses** trap dust and dead remains. Weathering and decay begin to make a thin, gritty soil with a little humus.
3. **Annual herbs, ferns and small grasses** root in pockets of soil. Their roots widen cracks, and their dead remains add organic matter.
4. **Perennial grasses and herbs** and then **shrubs** follow as the soil deepens and holds more water and nutrients.
5. **Trees** shade out the light-demanding species and form a woodland or forest, which is the climax.

At each step the plants change the habitat in ways that let the next stage take over. This is **facilitation**.

### Hydrosere (starting in open fresh water)

In a shallow dam or pond, silt and plant remains slowly fill the basin. The sequence is open water with floating algae, submerged plants, floating-leaved plants, reedswamp (reeds, bulrushes), marsh or sedge meadow, shrubs on drier ground and finally woodland. Each stage raises the bed with sediment and dead matter and so leads to drier conditions.

## Secondary succession

Secondary succession begins **after an existing community is destroyed but the soil remains**: after fire, flood, felling or abandonment of a field. Because soil, seeds and roots remain, it is faster, often 20 to 100 years or more to reach woodland. An abandoned maize field may pass through:

1. Fast-growing annual weeds in the first year.
2. Perennial grasses and herbs.
3. Pioneer shrubs and small trees, which are often thorny or fire-tolerant.
4. A woodland of longer-lived trees, such as miombo on suitable plateau soils, in the final stage.

## How the ecosystem changes during succession

| Feature | Early stages | Later stages |
|---|---|---|
| Species diversity | Low | Rises, then may level off or fall slightly at the climax |
| Biomass | Small | Large |
| Plant size and layers | Small, one layer | Tall, several layers |
| Soil depth and organic matter | Thin, little humus | Deep, rich in humus |
| Nutrient store | Small | Larger and more tightly cycled |
| Food webs | Simple | Complex, stable |
| Net productivity | Rises quickly | Highest in the middle stages, then low compared with biomass |

Pioneers are usually small, fast-growing, produce many seeds and need full light. Later species grow slowly, live long and tolerate shade.

## Climax and human influence

Ecologists describe a **climatic climax** that matches the regional climate. In many parts of southern Africa the observed community is held back by repeated fire, grazing, cutting and cultivation. This gives a **plagioclimax** or fire-maintained savanna. If burning stops, grassland may be replaced slowly by shrubs and woodland.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Interpreting a succession data set]
Illustrative data from four plots on an abandoned maize field, sampled with quadrats (data invented for this exercise):

| Years since abandonment | 1 | 5 | 15 | 40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant cover (%) | 35 | 70 | 85 | 95 |
| Species per 100 m² | 9 | 16 | 22 | 28 |
| Soil organic matter (%) | 1.1 | 1.6 | 2.4 | 3.5 |
| Mean plant height (m) | 0.4 | 1.2 | 3.5 | 9.0 |

1. Increase in species from year 1 to year 40: $28 - 9 = 19$ species.
2. As a percentage of the year 1 value: $19 \div 9 \times 100 = 211\%$.
3. Rate of increase in organic matter between years 5 and 15: $(2.4 - 1.6) \div 10 = 0.08$ percentage points per year.
4. Between years 15 and 40: $(3.5 - 2.4) \div 25 = 0.044$ percentage points per year.
5. Description: every variable rises with time, but organic matter increases more slowly in the later period, which suggests that the community is approaching a stable stage.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining the lithosere on a granite dwala"}
**Describe:** the bare rock carries crustose lichens, a few metres lower there are mosses and ferns in cracks, then grasses and shrubs where soil has collected. **Explain:** lichens release acids and trap dust, so weathering and decay create thin soil; mosses and ferns add humus and hold moisture; deeper soil lets grasses and shrubs root; each stage changes the habitat so that the next can replace it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Plant succession (lithosere, hydrosere, secondary succession, sere diagrams described in words, plagioclimax) is **ZIMSEC only** (topic 7.2, Form 5, Paper 1). Cambridge uses climax community ideas in the tropical vegetation topic.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Primary or secondary?"}
Ask one question: is there soil? If **no** (bare rock, sand, open water), it is primary. If **yes** (burnt, cleared or abandoned land), it is secondary and faster.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that succession ends when the soil is deep"}
The climax is reached when the community is stable and balanced with the climate, not simply when soil is deep. Species composition, biomass and nutrient cycling must be stable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing primary with secondary succession"}
Succession after a veld fire or on an abandoned field is secondary because soil is present. Only bare rock, new surfaces and open water start primary succession.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the stages without explaining the change"}
A list of plants earns few marks. Explain how each stage changes the habitat (soil, shade, moisture) so that the next stage can establish.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 7), often as a sere described in words, a data table of cover or species through time, or a "sketch" you must describe. *State* a stage or the climax; *describe* a sere in order with named plants; *explain* with "which modifies the habitat by …, allowing …"; *compare* primary and secondary succession in speed, starting point and soil; *assess* the effect of fire or grazing by contrasting the climatic climax with a plagioclimax.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Succession is the orderly change of plant communities through time, leading to a relatively stable climax.
- Primary succession starts without soil (lithosere on rock, hydrosere in water) and is slow; secondary succession starts with soil after disturbance and is faster.
- Pioneer species modify the habitat by weathering rock, adding humus and trapping sediment, which allows later species to replace them.
- Over time species diversity, biomass, soil depth and nutrient stores increase and food webs become more complex.
- Fire, grazing and cultivation can hold a community at a plagioclimax short of the climatic climax.
- Describe a sere in order and explain the change at each stage.
:::
