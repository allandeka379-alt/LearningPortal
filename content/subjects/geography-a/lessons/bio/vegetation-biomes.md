## Introduction

Drive from the Eastern Highlands to the Zambezi valley and the vegetation changes again and again: dense evergreen forest on the wet mountain slopes, miombo woodland on the high plateau, mopane and baobab in the hot lowveld. Travel on to the Congo basin and you meet the giant trees of the equatorial rainforest, and in the Kalahari and Namib you meet sparse desert plants. Geographers explain these patterns with a small number of controls and group the results into **biomes**. This lesson gives you the controls, then four biomes, and ends with adaptations. You should already know the basic ideas of climate (temperature, rainfall, seasons) and of photosynthesis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biome"}
A large-scale ecosystem, recognised by its dominant vegetation and its climate, that covers extensive areas of the Earth, for example tropical rainforest, savanna or hot desert.
:::

:::definition{term="Biomass"}
The total mass of living organic material in an area, usually given in kg per m² or tonnes per hectare. It is a **store**.
:::

:::definition{term="Net primary productivity (NPP)"}
The rate at which plants build new organic matter after they have used some for respiration, usually in g per m² per year. It is a **flow**.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
A structural, behavioural or physiological feature that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
:::

## Factors affecting the distribution of vegetation

1. **Climatic factors** are the most important at the global scale. Temperature controls the growing season and the rate of photosynthesis; rainfall and its seasonal pattern control water supply; light controls photosynthesis; wind increases water loss.
2. **Edaphic (soil) factors.** Soil depth, texture, drainage, pH and nutrient content decide which plants can root. Waterlogged vlei soils carry grass and sedges, while deep well-drained soils can carry woodland.
3. **Relief (topography).** Altitude lowers temperature by about 6.5 °C per 1 000 m on average; aspect (the direction a slope faces) changes the sunshine received; steep slopes have thin, dry soils; valley floors collect water and cold air.
4. **Biotic factors.** Competition for light and water, grazing and browsing (elephants break trees, cattle graze grass), disease, pollination and, above all, people. Fire, deforestation, cultivation and planting have changed most of the world's vegetation.

At local scale these factors interact. On a granite hill (a dwala) in Zimbabwe the thin soil and bare rock carry lichens and resurrection plants, the footslope soils carry woodland, and the wet valley floor carries a grassy dambo.

## Biomass and productivity

Productivity depends on warmth, water and light. It is highest where all three are plentiful all year, and lowest where one is missing (cold in the tundra, water in the desert). The table gives typical textbook values, rounded. They vary a great deal from place to place.

| Biome | Typical biomass (kg per m²) | Typical NPP (g per m² per year) |
|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | about 45 | about 2 200 |
| Temperate deciduous forest | about 30 | about 1 200 |
| Savanna | about 4 | about 900 |
| Hot desert | less than 1 | about 90 |

Note that a biome can have a high productivity but a low biomass: savanna grasses grow fast but are grazed or burnt every year, so little accumulates.

## The four biomes

### Tropical rainforest

Found within about 10° of the equator (Amazon, Congo basin, South-East Asia). Temperatures are 25–28 °C all year with a small range, and rainfall is above about 2 000 mm, spread through the year. The forest has several layers: tall **emergent** trees above 40 m, a continuous **canopy**, an understorey, a shrub layer and a dark ground layer. Species diversity is very high. Litter decays within weeks in the heat and damp, so almost all nutrients are held in the living biomass and the soil is often poor.

### Savanna (tropical grassland)

Found on both sides of the rainforest (southern Africa, East Africa, Brazil's cerrado, northern Australia). It is warm all year and has a **wet season and a dry season**, with rainfall from about 500 to 1 300 mm. Grasses dominate, with scattered trees such as acacia, baobab and mopane. Fire in the dry season is natural and common. Herds of grazers support large predators.

### Hot desert

Found around 15–30° from the equator (Sahara, Kalahari, Namib, Australian deserts), where air sinks and rainfall is below about 250 mm and unreliable. Days are very hot, nights can be cold, so the daily range is large. Plants are widely spaced, soils are thin and rich in minerals but low in humus. Productivity and biomass are low.

### Temperate deciduous forest

Found in mid-latitudes with four seasons (western Europe, eastern North America, parts of China). Rainfall is moderate (about 500–1 500 mm), summers are warm and winters cool. Trees such as oak and beech lose their leaves in winter. There are fewer species and fewer layers than in the rainforest, and leaf litter builds up on the forest floor.

## Adaptations

| Biome | Plant adaptations | Animal adaptations |
|---|---|---|
| Rainforest | Drip tips shed water; buttress roots support tall trees on shallow soil; lianas and epiphytes reach light; large dark leaves in the shade | Many live in the canopy (climbing, gliding); camouflage; fruit eaters spread seeds |
| Savanna | Thick bark resists fire; deep taproots reach water; many trees drop leaves in the dry season; grasses regrow from buds at or below ground level; baobabs store water in the trunk | Migration of grazers to follow rain and grass; burrowing and termite mounds; fast running and herd behaviour |
| Hot desert | Succulents store water; spines reduce loss and defend; small or no leaves, waxy cuticle; wide shallow roots catch rare rain; seeds that germinate only after rain (ephemerals) | Nocturnal activity; burrowing; large ears that lose heat; concentrated urine and dry droppings; camels store fat |
| Temperate deciduous | Leaves are shed in autumn to avoid frost damage and water loss; thick bark; buds protected by scales | Hibernation, migration, thick winter coats, food storage |

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Analysing a savanna climate record]
Illustrative monthly data for a station on the Zimbabwean plateau (data invented for this exercise).

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall (mm) | 190 | 170 | 100 | 35 | 8 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 12 | 45 | 110 | 175 |
| Mean temperature (°C) | 24 | 24 | 23 | 21 | 18 | 15 | 15 | 17 | 21 | 24 | 24 | 24 |

1. Annual rainfall: $190 + 170 + 100 + 35 + 8 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 12 + 45 + 110 + 175 = 850$ mm.
2. Rainfall from November to March: $110 + 175 + 190 + 170 + 100 = 745$ mm, so $745 \div 850 \times 100 = 88\%$ of the year's rain.
3. Months with less than 25 mm: May, June, July, August and September, so **5 dry months**.
4. Temperature range: $24 - 15 = 9$ °C.
5. Interpretation: a clear wet season (November–March) and a long dry season, with mild temperatures, is typical of savanna. Plants must survive five dry months, which explains deciduous trees, thick bark and deep roots.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining why savanna trees survive fire"}
**Describe:** many savanna trees have thick, corky bark and resprout from roots after a fire. **Explain:** grass fires burn quickly at ground level, so thick bark insulates the living tissue (the cambium) underneath; stored food in deep roots allows new shoots to grow once the rains return; and fire clears dead material, so nutrients are released in the ash. Fire therefore acts as a biotic and climatic control that favours fire-resistant species and keeps the savanna open.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing productivity]
Using the table: NPP of tropical rainforest is about 2 200 g per m² per year and of hot desert about 90.

1. Ratio: $2\,200 \div 90 = 24.4$, so rainforest productivity is about **24 times** that of desert.
2. Savanna as a percentage of rainforest: $900 \div 2\,200 \times 100 = 41\%$.
3. Explanation: rainforest has warmth, light and water all year; savanna loses water for months; desert has almost none.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating biomass and productivity as the same thing"}
Biomass is the amount of living material stored; productivity is the rate at which new material is made. Rainforest is high in both, but savanna has moderate productivity and low biomass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing adaptations without the reason"}
"Thick bark" earns little. Link the feature to the problem it solves: thick bark insulates the tissue from grass fires, so the tree survives.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming only climate as a control"}
Climate sets the pattern at the global scale, but soil, relief and living things (including people and fire) decide the pattern locally. Include at least one non-climatic factor.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 7), in structured questions that often give a climate table or a biome description. *State* and *identify* ask for the biome or factor; *describe* needs figures quoted from the table (peak month, total, range); *explain* needs a link such as "high rainfall and temperature, therefore rapid growth and decay"; *compare* needs both biomes in each sentence; *assess* the importance of climate against other factors needs a judgement. Use a Zimbabwean or southern African example (miombo, mopane, dambo) where it fits.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Vegetation distribution is controlled by climate, soils (edaphic), relief and biotic factors, including fire and people.
- Biomass is a store (kg per m²); net primary productivity is a flow (g per m² per year).
- Tropical rainforest has the greatest biomass and productivity; hot desert the least; savanna has high productivity but low biomass.
- Rainforest, savanna, hot desert and temperate deciduous forest differ in climate, structure and species diversity.
- Adaptations solve specific problems: drip tips shed water, thick bark resists fire, succulents store water and deciduous trees avoid winter.
- Always link a feature to the problem it solves when you explain an adaptation.
:::
