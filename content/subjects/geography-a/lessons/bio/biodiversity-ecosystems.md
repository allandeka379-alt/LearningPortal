## Introduction

A school in Masvingo has two patches of woodland near its grounds. One is a protected woodlot where pupils have planted a mixture of indigenous trees. The other is a stretch of communal grazing land where trees have been cut for firewood. A pupil says, "Both have trees, so they are the same." A geographer asks better questions: how many species are there, how evenly are they spread, and can we put a number on the difference? This lesson shows how to measure biodiversity in the field, calculate a diversity index and judge the effectiveness of conservation. You should know what a quadrat is and be able to calculate a percentage.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biodiversity"}
The variety of life in an area, including the variety of species, of genes within species and of habitats or ecosystems.
:::

:::definition{term="Species richness"}
The number of different species in an area. It ignores how many individuals there are of each.
:::

:::definition{term="Species evenness"}
How equally the individuals are shared between the species present. A community dominated by one species has low evenness.
:::

:::definition{term="Simpson's diversity index (D)"}
A measure that combines richness and evenness: $D = 1 - \sum \left(\frac{n}{N}\right)^2$, where $n$ is the number of individuals of one species and $N$ is the total number of individuals. $D$ lies between 0 (no diversity) and 1 (very high diversity).
:::

## Measuring plant diversity with quadrats

A **quadrat** is a square frame, often 1 m × 1 m for herbs and grasses and larger (for example 10 m × 10 m) for trees and shrubs, that marks out a sample area.

1. **Choose the sites** and decide what to compare (for example a protected woodlot and a grazed area).
2. **Sample randomly.** Use random numbers for coordinates along two tapes to avoid choosing "nice" spots. Stratified or systematic sampling can be used where the habitat varies.
3. **Use enough quadrats.** Add quadrats until the cumulative number of species stops rising.
4. **Record** in each quadrat: species names, number of individuals (count), height, crown width or trunk girth, and percentage cover.
5. **Calculate:** density (individuals per m²), frequency (percentage of quadrats in which a species occurs), mean height and Simpson's index.
6. **Present** with bar charts of abundance, kite diagrams along a transect and a table of results.

Sources of error include small samples, unidentified species, trampling, edge effects and recording different seasons.

## Interpreting diversity

A high Simpson's index shows many species with no single species dominating. A low value shows few species or dominance by one. Two sites with the same richness can have different diversity if one is uneven. High biodiversity usually makes an ecosystem more stable and productive, because there are more food sources, pollinators and soil organisms. It also gives people timber, fuel, medicine, thatching grass, wild fruits and tourism income.

## Threats to tropical ecosystems

| Threat | How it reduces biodiversity |
|---|---|
| **Commercial logging** | Removes large trees, fragments habitat, opens the canopy and soil to erosion |
| **Veld fires** | Late dry-season fires burn hot, kill seedlings and young trees and destroy habitats and grazing |
| **Poaching** | Illegal killing of animals (for meat, ivory, horn or the pangolin trade) reduces populations and disturbs food webs |
| **Encroachment** | Settlement and cultivation spreading into forests, wetlands and protected areas remove natural habitat |

## Responses and their evaluation

- **School woodlots and community tree planting** restore indigenous species and teach pupils, but survival depends on watering, protection from livestock and fire.
- **Fire committees and firebreaks** (under the Environmental Management Act) reduce uncontrolled burning; they work when communities are trained and rules are enforced.
- **Protected areas** managed by the Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (ZimParks) safeguard habitats; the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park, created by a treaty signed in 2002 between Mozambique, South Africa and Zimbabwe, links parks across borders so animals can move. Enforcement needs funds and rangers.
- **Community-based conservation** such as CAMPFIRE, started in the late 1980s, gives rural communities income from wildlife, so that they have a reason to protect it; benefit sharing is a challenge.
- **Anti-poaching patrols, tougher penalties and community scouts** reduce killing but are costly.
- **Law and monitoring** by EMA, the Forestry Commission and ZimParks depend on funding and local support.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Calculating Simpson's index for two sites]
Illustrative counts of woody plants in quadrats at two sites (data invented for this exercise).

| Species | Site A (protected woodlot) | Site B (grazed, cut area) |
|---|---|---|
| Msasa | 30 | 15 |
| Mutondo | 25 | 8 |
| Bushwillow (Combretum) | 20 | 5 |
| Acacia | 15 | 2 |
| Sickle bush | 10 | 70 |
| **Total (N)** | **100** | **100** |

1. Site A, $\sum (n/N)^2 = 0.30^2 + 0.25^2 + 0.20^2 + 0.15^2 + 0.10^2 = 0.0900 + 0.0625 + 0.0400 + 0.0225 + 0.0100 = 0.225$.
2. $D_A = 1 - 0.225 = 0.775$.
3. Site B, $\sum (n/N)^2 = 0.15^2 + 0.08^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.02^2 + 0.70^2 = 0.0225 + 0.0064 + 0.0025 + 0.0004 + 0.4900 = 0.5218$.
4. $D_B = 1 - 0.5218 = 0.478$.
5. Both sites have five species, but site A is much more diverse (0.775 compared with 0.478) because sickle bush, an invader that thrives after cutting and grazing, makes up 70% of the individuals at site B.
:::

:::worked-solution[Plant density from quadrats]
A student places ten 1 m × 1 m quadrats at random in a grassland and counts the plants of one grass species: 3, 5, 2, 4, 6, 0, 3, 4, 5, 3.

1. Total count: $3 + 5 + 2 + 4 + 6 + 0 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 3 = 35$.
2. Total area sampled: $10 \times 1 = 10$ m².
3. Density: $35 \div 10 = 3.5$ plants per m².
4. Frequency: the species occurs in 9 of 10 quadrats, so the frequency is 90%.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a fire committee"}
A ward sets up a fire committee that cuts firebreaks and fines people who start fires. **Strengths:** local people know the area, so early detection is quick, and firebreaks stop fires spreading. **Weaknesses:** firebreaks must be cut every year; fines are hard to enforce; people may light fires for hunting or to renew grass. **Judgement:** the committee works best when it is trained, equipped and backed by local leaders and when communities see a benefit such as better grazing.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Equating biodiversity with the number of species"}
Richness is only one part. A site with the same number of species but one dominant species has a lower Simpson's index.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to subtract from 1"}
Adding the squared proportions gives Σ(n/N)², which is high when diversity is low. Simpson's index is 1 minus this sum.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a conservation measure without evaluating it"}
For "evaluate", give strengths and weaknesses (cost, enforcement, community support) and reach a judgement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 7) and in Paper 3 research-technique questions. *Calculate* Simpson's index in steps (square each proportion, add, subtract from 1) and interpret the answer. *Describe* a sampling method in order, including randomisation and enough quadrats. *Suggest* improvements to a field enquiry. *Evaluate* a conservation response by cost, local support and effectiveness, and end with a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Biodiversity is the variety of life; richness counts species and evenness shows how equally individuals are shared.
- Quadrats sampled at random give counts, density, frequency and cover, which can be used to calculate Simpson's index.
- Simpson's D = 1 − Σ(n/N)²; values near 1 mean high diversity and values near 0 mean low diversity.
- Logging, veld fires, poaching and encroachment are major threats to tropical ecosystems.
- Responses include woodlots, fire committees, protected areas, community conservation and anti-poaching; each has strengths and limits.
- Judge a response by its effect, cost, enforcement and community support.
:::
