## Introduction

If climate change were a steady slope, each extra degree of warming would add the same amount of damage. The real climate may be less simple. Parts of the Earth system respond to warming in ways that speed it up, slow it down, or change it suddenly and permanently. A loose rock on a hillside shows the idea: push it a little and it rocks back; push it past a threshold and it rolls away. This lesson explains feedback loops and climate **tipping points**. You should already know the projected changes in cold and marine environments (see the lesson on climate projections).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Positive feedback"}
A process that amplifies an initial change. In climate, a positive feedback makes warming greater than the original cause would produce on its own.
:::

:::definition{term="Negative feedback"}
A process that reduces an initial change and tends to keep the system stable.
:::

:::definition{term="Climate tipping point"}
A critical threshold beyond which a part of the climate system changes to a new state, often abruptly, and the change becomes self-sustaining and difficult or impossible to reverse on a human timescale.
:::

## Feedback loops

**Positive feedbacks** (amplify warming):

1. **Ice–albedo.** Warming melts snow and sea ice, exposing darker ocean or land that absorbs more radiation, which causes more warming and more melting.
2. **Water vapour.** A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas, so warming increases.
3. **Permafrost carbon.** Warming thaws permafrost, which releases carbon dioxide and methane, causing more warming.
4. **Reduced carbon uptake.** Warmer oceans absorb less carbon dioxide, and drought and fire can turn forests from carbon sinks into sources.

**Negative feedbacks** (reduce change):

1. **Longwave emission.** A warmer Earth radiates more energy to space, which limits warming (this is a strong, basic stabilising effect).
2. **Plant growth.** More carbon dioxide can encourage plant growth where water and nutrients allow, so plants take up more carbon; this effect is limited.
3. **Ocean uptake.** The oceans absorb part of the extra CO2.
4. **Clouds.** Some low clouds reflect sunlight and cool the surface, although the overall cloud effect is uncertain and may be positive.

Positive feedbacks can speed up change and, if strong enough, push a system past a tipping point.

## Tipping points

Beyond a threshold, a small additional change in the climate can produce a disproportionately large and lasting change. Three features define them: a **threshold**, **self-sustaining** change by feedback, and **irreversibility** over human timescales. The projected tipping points in the Cambridge syllabus are:

- **Sea ice.** Summer Arctic sea ice may disappear in some years once warming continues, which greatly reduces the albedo of the Arctic. Sea ice can regrow if the climate cools, so it is often described as a reversible but important part of the system.
- **Ice sheets.** The Greenland ice sheet (about 7 m of sea-level equivalent) and the West Antarctic ice sheet (about 3 m) could pass thresholds beyond which loss continues for centuries even if warming stops. As Greenland's surface melts and lowers, it sits in warmer air, so melting accelerates (a positive feedback). Parts of West Antarctica sit on land below sea level, so warm ocean water can reach and undercut the ice.
- **Permafrost.** Widespread thaw of permafrost could release large amounts of carbon dioxide and methane, a positive feedback that adds to warming for decades to centuries.
- **Ocean circulation.** In the North Atlantic, the overturning circulation that carries warm surface water north depends on cold, salty water sinking. Melting ice and increased rain add fresh water, which makes surface water less dense and can slow the sinking. A weakening is likely during this century; a collapse would change heat transport, shift the tropical rain belts and cool north-west Europe. Scientists are uncertain about when or whether a collapse will occur.

Assessments suggest that some thresholds could be crossed at warming of about 1.5–2 °C, with large uncertainty; the precise values are debated.

## Effects on the speed and size of change

Tipping points make climate change **non-linear**: the response is not proportional to the cause. Some are abrupt (a shift in ocean circulation could occur within decades) and some are slow but committed (ice sheet loss could take centuries to millennia). Once triggered, the outcome may be locked in. The uncertainty about thresholds is a reason for caution in planning.

## Interactions between environments

The cold, marine and atmospheric environments are linked.

1. Atmospheric warming melts sea ice and ice sheets (cold environments).
2. Meltwater flows into the North Atlantic (marine environment), freshening the surface and slowing the overturning circulation.
3. Less sea ice lets the ocean absorb more radiation, so the ocean warms, which melts more ice from below.
4. Thawing permafrost releases greenhouse gases to the atmosphere, warming it further.
5. Changes in ocean circulation alter heat transport and rainfall patterns in the atmosphere.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Size of the ice–albedo feedback]
In the Arctic summer a surface receives 250 W/m² of solar radiation. Sea ice has an albedo of about 0.70 and open ocean about 0.07 (approximate values).

1. Absorbed by ice: $250 \times (1 - 0.70) = 75$ W/m².
2. Absorbed by open ocean: $250 \times (1 - 0.07) = 232.5$ W/m².
3. Extra energy absorbed when ice is replaced by water: $232.5 - 75 = 157.5$ W/m².
4. The water absorbs $232.5 \div 75 = 3.1$ times as much radiation, so a small loss of ice produces a large increase in absorbed energy: positive feedback.
:::

:::worked-solution[Writing out a feedback loop]
Permafrost carbon feedback, in order:

1. Global temperature rises.
2. Permafrost warms and thaws.
3. Microbes decompose frozen organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane.
4. Greenhouse gas concentration rises, increasing the greenhouse effect.
5. Temperature rises further, returning to stage 2.

The loop reinforces the original change, so it is a positive feedback. Always state the direction (positive or negative) and why.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a data table"}
Illustrative data: mass lost by an ice sheet each year (gigatonnes per year, invented for this exercise):

| Period | 1990s | 2000s | 2010s |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass loss (Gt per year) | 40 | 150 | 250 |

**Describe:** the loss increases in each period, from 40 to 150 Gt per year (an increase of 110) and then to 250 Gt per year (an increase of 100). The loss in the 2010s is $250 \div 40 = 6.25$ times as great as in the 1990s. **Link:** the accelerating loss is consistent with positive feedbacks, such as the melt-elevation and albedo feedbacks, but the record is too short to say whether a tipping point has been passed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using positive to mean good"}
In feedback, positive means the change is amplified and negative means it is reduced. A positive feedback in climate is harmful.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a tipping point has definitely been passed"}
Most thresholds are uncertain. Write "may be crossed at about" or "could", and cite the uncertainty.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the time scale"}
Some tipping points act abruptly and some over centuries. Say which, and note that "irreversible" means on human timescales.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Positive feedbacks amplify change (ice–albedo, water vapour, permafrost carbon); negative feedbacks reduce it (extra longwave emission, some plant growth, ocean uptake).
- A tipping point is a threshold beyond which change becomes self-sustaining and hard to reverse.
- Projected tipping points involve sea ice, the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, permafrost and ocean circulation.
- Tipping points make change non-linear, sometimes abrupt and sometimes slow but committed.
- Cold, marine and atmospheric environments interact, so a change in one can trigger changes in the others.
:::
