## Introduction

Climate change is a global problem: a tonne of carbon dioxide released in Harare, Beijing or Chicago has the same effect on the atmosphere. No single country can solve it, and countries that have done least to cause it are often hit hardest. This raises two questions. How do countries agree on what to do (**governance**)? And what can actually be done, either to reduce the change (**mitigation**) or to live with it (**adaptation**)? This lesson covers both. You should already know the causes of climate change and who is vulnerable (see the previous lessons).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mitigation"}
Action to reduce the amount of climate change by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or increasing the removal of greenhouse gases from the atmosphere. The long-term aim is **net zero**.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
Action to adjust to the actual or expected effects of climate change in order to reduce harm or use opportunities.
:::

:::definition{term="Nationally determined contribution (NDC)"}
A country's own plan, submitted under the Paris Agreement, setting out the emissions reductions and adaptation actions it will take.
:::

## Global governance

- **UNFCCC.** The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change was agreed at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992 and came into force in 1994. Almost all countries are parties.
- **COP.** The Conference of the Parties, the decision-making body of the UNFCCC, meets every year (COP followed by a number). COP decisions are made by consensus.
- **IPCC.** The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, set up in 1988 by the World Meteorological Organization and the UN Environment Programme, reviews scientific research and publishes assessment reports (the Sixth Assessment Report appeared in 2021–2023). It does not carry out research or make policy; it informs the COPs.
- **Kyoto Protocol (1997).** Set binding emission targets for developed countries.
- **Paris Agreement (COP21, December 2015).** Countries agreed to hold the rise in global average temperature to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. Every country submits an NDC and updates it every five years with greater ambition. A global stocktake checks progress. Developed countries are to support developing countries with finance. The agreement relies on national plans and public reporting, not on penalties.
- **Later agreements.** COP26 (Glasgow, 2021): first COP decision to refer to reducing coal use ("phasing down" unabated coal). COP27 (Sharm el-Sheikh, 2022): agreement to set up a fund for loss and damage in vulnerable countries. COP28 (Dubai, 2023): the first global stocktake, a call to transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems and to triple renewable energy capacity by 2030. COP29 (Baku, 2024): a new climate finance goal of at least US\$300 billion a year by 2035 for developing countries.

## Mitigation strategies

1. **Sustainable energy targets.** Shifting from coal, oil and gas to solar, wind, hydropower and other low-carbon sources, and improving energy efficiency.
2. **Carbon offsetting and credits.** An organisation pays for emissions to be cut or removed elsewhere (for example a tree-planting or clean-cooking project) to compensate for its own emissions. One credit represents one tonne of CO2e. Critics point to problems of whether the reduction would have happened anyway, whether it lasts, and whether it is counted twice.
3. **Climate engineering.** *Carbon capture and storage (CCS)* captures CO2 from power stations or industry and stores it underground; it is expensive and operates on a small scale (for example the Sleipner project in Norway stores about one million tonnes a year). *Solar radiation management* aims to reflect sunlight, for example by injecting aerosols into the stratosphere; it could cool the planet but does nothing about carbon dioxide or ocean acidification, could change rainfall patterns and has not been used.
4. **Nature-based solutions.** Protecting forests, restoring wetlands and mangroves and improving farming (agroforestry, conservation agriculture) store carbon and increase resilience, with benefits for biodiversity and people.

## Adaptation strategies

1. **Withdrawal (retreat).** Moving people and assets away from risky areas, such as managed relocation of coastal villages.
2. **Compromise (accommodation).** Living with the hazard: houses raised on stilts, flood-resistant buildings, drought-tolerant crops, early warning.
3. **Protection.** Defending places with sea walls, flood embankments, dams, or natural defences such as mangroves.

## Limits and challenges

- **Cost and finance.** Poorer countries lack funds; the finance promised has often been late or insufficient.
- **Technology and capacity.** CCS and engineering are unproven at scale; many countries lack skills and institutions.
- **Politics.** Governments face short election cycles; fossil fuel industries and jobs are affected; the benefits of cutting emissions are shared by all while the costs fall on those who act (the free-rider problem).
- **Time lags.** Greenhouse gases persist, so the effect of cuts is slow.
- **Trade-offs.** Countries need energy for development, and some measures (large dams or biofuels) harm people and ecosystems.
- **Justice.** The wealthiest countries have emitted most in the past, while the poorest countries are most vulnerable. Debates cover who should pay for mitigation, adaptation and loss and damage, and whether people on the front line have a voice.

## Two countries with contrasting strategies (Cambridge detailed specific examples)

Choose two real countries and learn dated facts. Two possible examples (check and update before use):

| | United Kingdom | Bangladesh |
|---|---|---|
| Income and emissions | High income; large historical emissions | Lower-middle income; very low emissions per person |
| Main emphasis | Mitigation: Climate Change Act 2008, legally binding net-zero target for 2050 (from 2019), growth of offshore wind, the last coal-fired power station closed in 2024 | Adaptation: cyclone early warning and shelters, coastal embankments, flood- and salt-tolerant crops, the Delta Plan 2100 |
| Reasons for choice | Able to afford the transition; responsible for large historical emissions | Very high exposure (low-lying delta, cyclones, floods) and little capacity; relies partly on international finance |
| Challenges | Cost of the transition, heating and transport emissions, public acceptance | Funding, a growing population, land loss and salt-water intrusion |

Why do the two countries differ? The United Kingdom has the wealth, technology and institutions to fund a rapid shift in its energy system, and its lower exposure to climate hazards leaves it free to put mitigation first. Bangladesh has contributed very little to global emissions but faces cyclones, floods and rising salinity, so adaptation is its first priority, and its larger mitigation targets depend on international finance.
## Zimbabwe (ZIMSEC detail)

Local and national measures include the Climate Change Response Strategy (2014) and National Climate Policy (2017), and conservation agriculture programmes such as Pfumvudza/Intwasa, which aim to protect soil moisture and yields. The Environmental Management Agency regulates environmental matters. Energy measures include expanded solar generation and the extension of Kariba South hydropower (completed 2018). Early warning is provided by the Meteorological Services Department, and national tree planting aims to increase forest cover. Constraints include shortage of finance, debt and dependence on rain-fed agriculture and hydropower. (Verify current policies against official sources.)

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Costing a carbon offset]
A company emits 12 000 tonnes of CO2e a year and plans to offset it at US\$12 per tonne.

1. Annual cost = $12\,000 \times 12 = 144\,000$, that is US\$144 000.
2. If a plantation absorbs 10 tonnes of CO2 per hectare per year (illustrative value), the area needed is $12\,000 \div 10 = 1\,200$ ha.
3. Evaluation: offsetting is cheap compared with cutting emissions in some cases, but the planted trees may take years to absorb that much, may burn or be cut down, and the company's own emissions continue. Offsets should supplement, not replace, cuts.
:::

:::worked-solution[Meeting a national target]
A country's baseline emissions were 80 Mt CO2e. Its target is a 35% cut by 2030, and emissions now stand at 70 Mt.

1. Target level = $80 \times (1 - 0.35) = 52$ Mt.
2. Further reduction needed = $70 - 52 = 18$ Mt.
3. As a share of current emissions: $18 \div 70 \times 100 = 25.7\%$.
4. The country has cut 10 Mt so far ($80 - 70$), which is $10 \div 80 \times 100 = 12.5\%$ of the baseline, only about a third of the way to the target.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating adaptation options with a scoring matrix"}
A coastal village compares three options. Scores are from 1 (poor) to 5 (good) and are illustrative.

| Option | Cost-effectiveness | Durability | Local acceptance | Co-benefits | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sea wall | 2 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 11 |
| Mangrove restoration | 4 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 16 |
| Relocation | 3 | 5 | 2 | 3 | 13 |

Mangrove restoration scores highest (16) because it is cheap and also gives fish nurseries, wood and carbon storage, but it is less durable against very large storms. Relocation is durable but is not accepted locally. The sea wall protects but has few other benefits. A combination is likely to be best, and the weights given to each criterion would change the result.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up mitigation and adaptation"}
Mitigation reduces the cause (emissions); adaptation deals with the effects. Building a sea wall is adaptation; planting trees to absorb CO2 is mitigation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Paris Agreement is legally enforced with penalties"}
The agreement requires countries to submit and report national plans; there is no penalty for missing targets. Its strength is transparency and regular review.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing strategies without evaluation"}
Always give a strength, a limit and a judgement for each strategy, and link the choice to a country's income, exposure and politics.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The UNFCCC (1992) and annual COPs are supported by IPCC science; the Paris Agreement (2015) uses national plans (NDCs), five-year updates and a stocktake.
- Mitigation: sustainable energy targets, offsets and credits, CCS and solar radiation management, nature-based solutions.
- Adaptation: withdrawal (retreat), compromise (accommodation) and protection.
- Limits: finance, technology, politics, free riding, time lags, trade-offs and climate justice.
- Countries choose different strategies depending on their income, exposure and politics, for example mitigation-led (UK) and adaptation-led (Bangladesh).
- Zimbabwe uses conservation agriculture, early warning, renewable energy and tree planting, but is limited by finance.
:::
