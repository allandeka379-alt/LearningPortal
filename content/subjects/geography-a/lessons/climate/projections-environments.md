## Introduction

How hot will the planet be in 2100? There is no single answer, because the future climate depends on the choices people make about emissions. Scientists therefore run climate models for a range of possible futures and report **projections**, not predictions. This lesson shows how to read the five categories of projected change used in the Cambridge syllabus, what each means for warming and emissions, and how a warmer climate would affect cold environments (the Arctic, Antarctic and high mountains) and marine environments. You should already know the causes of climate change (see the lesson on evidence and causes of climate change). The numbers below come from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021), rounded, and are approximate.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climate projection"}
A model-based estimate of future climate for a stated scenario of emissions. Projections show what would happen under a given pathway; they are not forecasts of which pathway will occur.
:::

:::definition{term="Net zero"}
The point at which the greenhouse gases added to the atmosphere by human activity are balanced by the amounts removed, such as by forests, soils or technology. Reaching net zero of carbon dioxide is needed to stop global warming from increasing.
:::

:::definition{term="Permafrost"}
Ground (soil, rock or ice) that stays at or below 0 °C for at least two consecutive years. It holds large amounts of frozen organic carbon.
:::

## The five categories of projected change

The categories are named from **very high** (the largest warming) to **very low** (the smallest warming). Warming is the best estimate for 2081–2100 compared with 1850–1900; sea-level rise is the median rise by 2100 compared with 1995–2014.

| Category | Emissions pathway | Peak year of emissions | Net-zero CO2 | Warming by 2081–2100 (°C) | Sea-level rise by 2100 (m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Very low | Fall quickly from now | About 2020 | About 2050 | 1.4 | 0.38 |
| Low | Fall steadily | About 2020 | About 2070 | 1.8 | 0.44 |
| Intermediate | Stay near current levels to mid-century, then fall | Levels off until mid-century | Not by 2100 | 2.7 | 0.56 |
| High | Keep rising | None before 2100 | None | 3.6 | 0.68 |
| Very high | Rise strongly (roughly double by 2050) | None before 2100 | None | 4.4 | 0.77 |

Carbon dioxide emissions would roughly double by 2100 in the high category. Observed warming to the 2010s was about 1.1 °C, so even the very low category involves some further warming. Changes are smallest in the very low category and largest in the very high category.

## Effects on the global climate

- **Sea level.** Rise continues through thermal expansion of seawater and melting of glaciers and ice sheets. Ranges widen at the high end because ice-sheet behaviour is uncertain; a rise of about 2 m by 2100 cannot be ruled out in the highest category, although it is unlikely.
- **Precipitation.** Generally, wet regions become wetter and dry regions drier. Warmer air holds about 7% more water vapour for each 1 °C of warming, so heavy rainfall events become more intense.
- **Extreme weather.** Heatwaves become more frequent and more intense; heavy rainfall and flood risk increase in many places; droughts become stronger in some regions; the proportion of intense tropical cyclones is expected to rise.
- **After 2100.** Projections end in 2100, but change continues afterwards. Carbon dioxide stays in the atmosphere for centuries, and the deep ocean and ice sheets respond slowly, so sea level continues to rise for centuries even if temperature stabilises.

## Cold environments

Cold environments are high-latitude regions (the Arctic and Antarctic) and high mountains. The Arctic is warming at least twice as fast as the global average.

1. **Snow and ice loss.** Sea ice, glaciers and ice sheets shrink. Sea ice is lost mostly in summer in the Arctic. Mountain glaciers retreat, affecting rivers fed by meltwater.
2. **Albedo effect.** Bright ice and snow are replaced by dark sea or land, which absorb more solar radiation, adding to warming.
3. **Permafrost thaw.** The ground warms and thaws, buildings and roads can subside, and microbes decompose the frozen carbon and release carbon dioxide and methane to the atmosphere.
4. **Ice sheets.** Melting of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets adds water to the oceans and contributes to sea-level rise.

## Marine environments

1. **Sea surface temperature** rises; the oceans absorb more than 90% of the extra heat in the climate system. Marine heatwaves cause coral bleaching and fish to move to cooler waters.
2. **Sea-level rise and coastal flooding.** Low-lying coasts, deltas and small islands are flooded more often, especially when storm surges from tropical cyclones are added to higher sea level. Erosion, salt water entering freshwater and farmland, and loss of wetlands result.
3. **Ocean acidification.** The ocean absorbs part of the extra carbon dioxide, which makes seawater more acidic and harms shell-forming organisms and corals.
4. **Ocean circulation.** Melting ice adds fresh water that lowers the density of surface water in the North Atlantic. This is expected to weaken the Atlantic overturning circulation, which carries heat northwards, with effects on regional climates and rainfall.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Comparing categories]
Use the table above.

1. Extra warming in the very high category compared with the very low category: $4.4 - 1.4 = 3.0$ °C.
2. Warming avoided by following the low category rather than the very high category: $4.4 - 1.8 = 2.6$ °C, which is $2.6 \div 4.4 \times 100 = 59.1\%$ of the very high warming.
3. Sea-level rise in the very high category exceeds that in the very low category by $0.77 - 0.38 = 0.39$ m (39 cm). As a percentage of the very low rise this is $0.39 \div 0.38 \times 100 = 103\%$: the rise is about twice as large.
4. Conclusion: the difference between categories is large, which shows that the choice of emissions pathway matters.
:::

:::worked-solution[Reading peak and net-zero years]
A country plans for its emissions to peak in 2030 and reach net zero in 2060.

1. Time from peak to net zero: $2060 - 2030 = 30$ years.
2. If emissions fall by the same amount each year from a peak of 600 million tonnes, the fall is $600 \div 30 = 20$ million tonnes per year.
3. A later peak or a later net-zero year means more greenhouse gas is added, so the total warming is higher. This is why both years appear in the descriptions of the categories.
:::

:::example{title="Describing and explaining Arctic sea ice change"}
Illustrative data: Arctic sea ice in September covered 7.0 million km² in 1980 and 4.6 million km² in 2020 (invented data for this exercise). **Describe:** the area fell by 2.4 million km², a decrease of $2.4 \div 7.0 \times 100 = 34\%$. **Explain:** warmer air and water melt more ice in summer. The dark ocean uncovered absorbs more radiation than the white ice it replaced (a positive feedback), so warming and melting increase. Less ice also means wave action and storms can break up the remaining ice.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating projections as predictions"}
A projection depends on a pathway of emissions. State "under the very high category the projected warming is about 4.4 °C", not "temperatures will be 4.4 °C higher".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying warming stops in 2100"}
The projections end in 2100 but the changes continue, particularly sea level, because greenhouse gases last for a long time and the ocean and ice respond slowly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up net zero with zero emissions"}
Net zero means remaining emissions are balanced by removals. Actual emissions need not reach zero.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 4 (topic 11). *Describe* a table or graph of the categories using values with units and a comparison. *Explain* why the categories differ (emissions, peak year, net-zero year). *Explain impacts* on cold and marine environments with a chain: warming, process, effect, consequence. *Assess* questions need both the confidence of the projections and their limits (uncertainty about ice sheets and feedbacks). Use specific figures and one or two named regions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Five categories (very high to very low) show different futures for warming by 2100: about 4.4 °C down to about 1.4 °C.
- The differences depend on how fast emissions peak and fall and when net zero is reached.
- Projected effects include sea-level rise, wetter wet areas and drier dry areas, and more frequent extremes; change continues after 2100.
- Cold environments suffer ice and snow loss, albedo feedback and permafrost thaw that releases carbon dioxide and methane.
- Marine environments face warming, acidification, sea-level rise, coastal flooding and weaker overturning circulation.
:::
