## Introduction

In 2007 the world passed a milestone: for the first time more than half of all people lived in towns and cities. Cities now produce most of the world's wealth and also face its greatest pressures: crowded housing, traffic, pollution and shortages of water and jobs. Zimbabwe is less urbanised than the world average, with roughly one third of its people in urban areas (ZimStat census 2022, rounded), but its cities grow quickly and spill beyond their boundaries into areas such as Epworth, Ruwa and Chitungwiza near Harare. This lesson explains the processes of urban change, their causes and consequences, and possible solutions.

You should already know the terms urban, rural, migration and natural increase. The internal layout of cities is covered in the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urbanisation and urban growth"}
Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas. Urban growth is the increase in the number of people living in towns and cities, or in the area they cover. A country can have urban growth without urbanisation if rural and urban populations grow at the same rate.
:::

:::definition{term="Suburbanisation and urban sprawl"}
Suburbanisation is the outward movement of people and activities from the centre to the edges of a city. Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled spread of low-density development over rural land around a city, often relying on cars.
:::

:::definition{term="Counter-urbanisation and re-urbanisation"}
Counter-urbanisation is the movement of people from large urban areas to smaller towns and villages. Re-urbanisation (urban regeneration) is the return of people and investment to inner cities, often after redevelopment.
:::

## Global and city-scale patterns

- The world's urban population passed 50% in 2007 (United Nations) and is projected to reach about two-thirds by 2050. Most future growth will be in Africa and Asia.
- High-income countries are about 80% urban and growth is slow; Africa is less than half urban, but its cities are among the fastest growing in the world.
- The number of megacities (over 10 million people) has risen and most are in the developing world; Lagos and Cairo are examples in Africa.
- City growth has two parts: **population growth** and **spatial growth** (the built-up area). Area often grows faster than population because of low-density sprawl.

## Causes of urbanisation

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| Economic | Jobs in industry and services; higher wages; informal business opportunities; rural poverty and falling farm incomes |
| Social | Schools, clinics, entertainment and family already in town; changing aspirations |
| Demographic | Natural increase in cities (young populations of migrants); rural population growth that outstrips farmland |
| Environmental | Drought and land degradation push people from farming |
| Political and historical | Colonial laws that restricted African urban residence in Zimbabwe until independence; their removal in 1980 was followed by rapid urban growth; government investment in cities; reclassification of areas as urban |

Suburbanisation is caused by rising incomes and car or minibus use, cheaper land at the edge, a wish for space and a safer environment, and congestion in the centre. Counter-urbanisation is caused by better transport and communications, remote working, a wish for a quieter life, and high costs in cities. Re-urbanisation follows government and private investment in inner-city housing, jobs and culture.

## Consequences

**For urban areas:** pressure on housing, which leads to informal settlements such as Epworth near Harare; strain on water, sanitation, waste collection and transport; unemployment and a large informal sector; pollution and congestion; disease outbreaks when water and sanitation fail, as in the 2008–09 cholera epidemic in Zimbabwe; crime and inequality. Benefits include economies of scale, a larger market and more services.

**For rural areas:** loss of young people and labour, an ageing rural population, but also remittances and reduced pressure on land. In counter-urbanisation, rural villages near cities gain commuters, house prices rise, and local services come under pressure.

## Solutions

*To rapid urbanisation:* develop rural areas (growth points, rural jobs, services and markets); decentralise industry; provide low-cost housing and serviced stands; upgrade informal settlements; improve public transport; plan growth with land-use plans and greenbelts; support the informal economy with licences and facilities.

*To counter-urbanisation problems:* planning controls on rural development, protect farmland, extend public transport and services to commuter villages.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Urbanisation compared with urban growth]
Illustrative data for Country X.

| Year | Total population (millions) | Urban population (millions) | Urban share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 8.0 | 2.0 | 25% |
| 2020 | 14.0 | 5.6 | 40% |

1. Urban share = urban ÷ total × 100: 1990, 2.0 ÷ 8.0 = 25%; 2020, 5.6 ÷ 14.0 = 40%. The country has urbanised by 15 percentage points.
2. Urban population multiplied by 5.6 ÷ 2.0 = 2.8; total population multiplied by 14.0 ÷ 8.0 = 1.75.
3. Annual urban growth rate = (2.8)^(1/30) − 1 = 3.5% a year; total growth = (1.75)^(1/30) − 1 = 1.9% a year.
4. Rural population rose from 6.0 million to 8.4 million, a 40% increase. The urban population grows faster than the rural population, so the country is urbanising, but rural numbers also rise.
:::

:::worked-solution[Population and area growth]
A city had 1.1 million people in 1990 and 2.0 million in 2020; its built-up area rose from 120 km² to 210 km² (illustrative data).

1. Population density 1990 = 1 100 000 ÷ 120 = 9 167 per km²; 2020 = 2 000 000 ÷ 210 = 9 524 per km².
2. Area increased by (210 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 75%; population increased by (2.0 − 1.1) ÷ 1.1 × 100 = 82%.
3. Density has hardly changed, so the city has grown outwards (urban sprawl) in step with population. Planners need to extend roads, water and sewers across a larger area.
:::

:::example{title="Epworth near Harare"}
Epworth is a large residential area to the south-east of Harare that has grown with a mix of formal and informal housing. It illustrates how a city's growth spills beyond its boundary: people unable to afford housing in the city settle on cheaper land at the edge, where water, sanitation and roads are often inadequate. The consequences include health risks and long journeys to work, and the responses include planning, upgrading of services and regularisation of tenure. Quote only facts you are sure of when using this example.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing urbanisation with urban growth"}
Urbanisation is the rising share of people living in towns; urban growth is an increase in numbers. Both can happen at once, but they are different.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing counter-urbanisation with suburbanisation"}
Suburbanisation is movement to the edges of the same city. Counter-urbanisation is movement out of the urban area to smaller towns and villages.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing problems without causes or solutions"}
Link every consequence to a cause (for example the lack of housing leads to informal settlements) and propose a solution that deals with it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Urbanisation is the rising share of people living in towns; urban growth is the rise in the number of urban people or the built-up area.
- More than half the world lives in urban areas; Africa is urbanising fastest.
- Causes: rural-urban migration, natural increase, economic opportunities, colonial history and policy; suburbanisation and counter-urbanisation follow rising incomes and transport.
- Consequences include housing shortage, informal settlement, pressure on services and loss of rural labour.
- Solutions include rural development, serviced housing, upgrading, planning and public transport.
:::
