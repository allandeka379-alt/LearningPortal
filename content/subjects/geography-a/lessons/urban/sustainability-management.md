## Introduction

Cities occupy a small part of the Earth's surface but use most of its energy and produce most of its waste. By 2050 about two-thirds of the world's people are expected to live in them. The question for planners is not whether cities will grow, but how to make them places where people can live well without damaging the environment or the opportunities of future generations. That is the aim of **sustainable urban development**. This lesson explains the idea and why it is hard to measure, how conflicting priorities are balanced, how strategies for waste, transport, housing and green space work, and how land-use planning uses maps and GIS.

You should already know the problems of urban growth and the structure of cities from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sustainable urban development"}
Growth and change in cities that meets the needs of the present population without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their needs. It balances environmental protection, social well-being and economic prosperity.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecological footprint"}
A measure of the land and water area needed to supply the resources a city's people use and to absorb the waste they produce. A city with a footprint larger than its own area is drawing on other places.
:::

:::definition{term="Land-use planning"}
The process by which authorities decide how land will be used (housing, industry, services, green space) and what rules apply, using surveys, maps, zoning and consultation.
:::

## Why sustainability is hard to measure

- It has **many dimensions** (environment, society, economy, governance) that cannot be added up simply.
- **Indicators** such as carbon emissions per person, air quality, share of trips by public transport, recycling rate, green space per person, access to water and sanitation and housing quality use different units and each hides detail.
- Results depend on **scale** (neighbourhood, city, region) and on **time** (today's gain may cause tomorrow's harm).
- Cities depend on **resources and waste sites elsewhere**, so a clean city may export its pollution.
- Judgements differ: what counts as "sustainable" depends on values and priorities.

## Balancing priorities and viewpoints

| Group | Typical priority |
|---|---|
| Residents | Affordable housing, safe water, clean air, transport, jobs |
| Property owners and developers | Profit, freedom to build, stable land values |
| Businesses | Low costs, good access, skilled workers, reliable services |
| Local government | Revenue, service delivery, meeting regulations and public pressure |
| National government and international bodies | Economic growth, national targets, climate and development commitments |

The priorities clash. A park may be popular but costs the council potential rates from building on the land; a bus lane helps commuters but reduces road space for cars; cheap housing may be built on a floodplain. Planners therefore use consultation, cost-benefit analysis and compromise.

## Strategies

**Solid waste management:** reduce, reuse, recycle, compost organic waste, and dispose of the remainder safely in engineered landfills (sometimes with energy recovery). Informal waste pickers recover materials in many cities. Constraints include poor collection, limited funds and open dumping. Harare's main dumpsite at Pomona illustrates the pressure on waste sites in a growing city.

**Transport:** public transport, bus rapid transit, cycling and walking routes, restrictions on cars, and mixed-use planning to shorten journeys. Constraints include cost, informal minibus operations and weak enforcement.

**Housing quality and density:** compact, mixed-use neighbourhoods, serviced stands, upgrading of informal settlements, social housing, and building standards. Higher density saves land and energy but needs good services.

**Green space:** parks, urban forests, wetlands and urban farming cool the city, manage floods, support biodiversity and improve health. Constraints include land cost, pressure to build and lack of maintenance.

**Incentives and constraints:** taxes and fees, subsidies, building codes, zoning, fines and education campaigns.

## Detailed example: Curitiba, Brazil

Curitiba, in southern Brazil, grew quickly in the twentieth century. A master plan in the 1960s guided growth along main transport corridors. Its main features:

- An integrated bus network with dedicated lanes, high-capacity articulated buses and boarding tubes, introduced from 1974, which became a model of bus rapid transit.
- Parks, including those created on floodplains, which store water in heavy rain and provide recreation.
- Recycling programmes, such as exchanging collected recyclables for bus tokens or food, which engage low-income households.

Successes: lower cost than rail, high public transport use and well-known environmental programmes. Limits: car ownership has grown, informal settlements exist at the edge, and the model depends on strong planning and funding. A good answer states these facts with dates and weighs success against limits.

## Land-use planning with maps and GIS (ZIMSEC)

1. **Survey** the existing land use, topography, soils, roads and services using maps, aerial or satellite images and field visits.
2. **Analyse** the data: overlay layers in a GIS to find suitable sites (flat, away from flood zones, near roads); use buffers (for example 400 m around bus stops) to find who has access; use network analysis to plan routes.
3. **Plan:** zone land for housing, industry, services and green space, and decide standards.
4. **Consult** residents and stakeholders.
5. **Implement and monitor** through approvals and enforcement. In Zimbabwe planning follows the Regional, Town and Country Planning Act, with master and local plans prepared by local authorities.

Rural land-use planning follows the same steps for farms, grazing, villages and conservation, using land capability maps and agro-ecological regions.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Life of a landfill site]
A city's landfill has 1.5 million m³ of space left and receives 75 000 m³ of waste a year. A recycling and composting programme will cut the waste sent to landfill by 40% (illustrative data).

1. Years left now = 1 500 000 ÷ 75 000 = 20 years.
2. Waste sent to landfill after the programme = 75 000 × (1 − 0.40) = 45 000 m³ a year.
3. Years left = 1 500 000 ÷ 45 000 = 33.3 years, which is 13.3 years longer.
4. The programme delays the need for a new site, but it requires collection systems and markets for recycled materials.
:::

:::worked-solution[A simple sustainability score]
Illustrative data for City P: public transport share of trips 45% (target 60%); green space 10 m² per person (target 20); recycling 28% (target 40%).

1. Score for each indicator = actual ÷ target × 100: 45 ÷ 60 = 75; 10 ÷ 20 = 50; 28 ÷ 40 = 70.
2. Mean score = (75 + 50 + 70) ÷ 3 = 65.
3. Green space is the weakest indicator (50), so it should be a priority. The mean hides this: a single score can mask a serious weakness, which is why indicators should also be read separately.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating a bus rapid transit scheme"}
A council proposes a dedicated bus lane on the busiest road. Supporters (commuters, environmental groups) point to faster journeys and lower emissions. Opponents (some drivers, shop owners worried about parking) point to the loss of road space and parking. A balanced evaluation compares the number of people carried per hour, costs, effects on traffic and air quality, and fairness for low-income commuters, and concludes with a recommendation, for example a phased scheme with a trial period.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the meaning and measurement of sustainability, strategies for waste, transport, housing and green space, and the balancing of viewpoints. Cambridge requires a detailed specific example of one urban area. ZIMSEC adds techniques of rural and urban land-use planning with GIS and maps.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Describing a strategy without constraints"}
Every strategy has costs and limits. State how well it works, who gains and loses, and what stops it from working better.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating sustainability as only an environmental matter"}
Sustainability also includes social well-being and economic viability. An answer that ignores jobs, housing or fairness is incomplete.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using an example without facts"}
Name the city, the strategy, a date or a feature, and an outcome. Do not invent figures.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 9) and Cambridge Paper 2 (AS topic 6), often as a 15-mark essay. *Explain* why sustainability is difficult to measure; *assess* a strategy by weighing success against constraints and the interests of different groups; *evaluate* with a conclusion that is supported by a detailed example. In ZIMSEC data questions show each step of a GIS or planning method.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sustainable urban development meets present needs without harming future generations, balancing environment, society and economy.
- It is hard to measure because of many dimensions, different indicators, scale, time and values.
- Strategies cover waste, transport, housing and green space; each faces constraints and conflicting interests.
- Curitiba's bus rapid transit, parks and recycling show a planned approach with limits.
- Land-use planning uses surveys, GIS overlays, buffers, zoning and consultation.
:::
