## Introduction

Walk out of the centre of Harare along any main road and you pass tall office blocks, then older shops and workshops, then neighbourhoods of different kinds, until the city gives way to farmland. The pattern of where activities and people are located in a city is its **urban structure**. It looks haphazard, but it follows reasons that can be explained: land costs, accessibility, planning, history and income. The structure is not fixed: shops move to malls, factories to the edge, offices to the suburbs, and housing changes with population and income. This lesson explains the zones of a city, the models that describe them, and the forces that change them.

You should already know the CBD and its measures from the lesson on spheres of influence and the processes of urbanisation (suburbanisation and sprawl) from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Urban structure and land-use zones"}
Urban structure is the arrangement of land uses and people in a city. Land-use zones are areas of the city dominated by one type of use, such as the central business district, industry, high-density housing or low-density housing.
:::

:::definition{term="Land value gradient and bid-rent"}
Land value is highest at the most accessible point in the city (the peak land value intersection) and falls with distance. In bid-rent theory, different users bid for land according to how much they gain from accessibility: shops and offices can pay most, so they occupy the centre, while housing and farming can pay least and occupy the edges.
:::

:::definition{term="Urban morphology"}
The physical form of a city: its street pattern, plot sizes, building types and heights, and how they have developed over time (ZIMSEC).
:::

:::definition{term="Place identity and perception"}
The character that people give to a place and the way they see it. Residents, visitors and planners may perceive the same area differently: as safe or dangerous, historic or run-down, vibrant or noisy.
:::

## Horizontal and vertical structure

**Horizontal structure** is the pattern of land use across the city map. A typical city has:

1. a **CBD** at the centre (shops, offices, banks);
2. an **inner city** around it, with older housing, small workshops and warehouses, and often decline or regeneration;
3. **suburbs** of residential areas, with density and income varying by zone;
4. **industrial areas** near railways and main roads (Harare has estates such as Southerton, Willowvale and Msasa);
5. a **rural-urban fringe** at the edge, mixing farms, new estates and informal settlements.

**Vertical structure:** buildings are tallest and use space most intensively in the CBD, where land is costliest, with shops at ground level, offices above and sometimes basements for parking. Heights fall with distance from the centre, and in suburbs most buildings have one or two storeys.

## Urban models

| Model | Idea | Fit and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Burgess concentric zones (1925) | Rings around the CBD: transition zone, working-class homes, better housing, commuter zone | Simple, based on 1920s Chicago; ignores transport routes and planning |
| Hoyt sector model (1939) | Zones spread outwards in wedges along main transport routes and by income | Explains why high-income and industrial sectors extend along roads or railways |
| Harris and Ullman multiple nuclei (1945) | Several centres (CBD, industrial estate, suburban shopping centre) develop separately | Fits large cities with decentralised retail and offices |
| Cities in developing countries | Often a mix of formal and informal areas; informal settlements at the edge or on marginal land; planned low-density suburbs beside crowded high-density suburbs | Models from Europe and the USA do not fit exactly |

Models are simplifications. Use them as a starting point, then compare a real city with the model.

## Structure of a Zimbabwean city

Many Zimbabwean cities were planned in the colonial period, with separate areas for different groups, and the pattern persists. A typical city has a CBD, **low-density suburbs** (large stands, such as Borrowdale and Avondale in Harare), **medium-density** areas, and **high-density suburbs** (small stands, crowded housing, such as Mbare, Highfield and Mufakose in Harare and Makokoba in Bulawayo), plus industrial estates. Satellite towns such as Chitungwiza were developed some distance from Harare. Since independence, planning has allowed higher density in some low-density areas, and informal settlements have grown at the edge.

## Changes in the location of activities

| Activity | Change | Reasons |
|---|---|---|
| Retail | Shopping centres and malls in the suburbs and edge, while parts of the CBD decline | Car and minibus use; cheaper land; space for parking; customers moved; competition between centres; investment by property companies |
| Offices | Some offices move to suburban business parks; the CBD remains the main centre | Lower rents, space, easier access for staff; the CBD remains attractive for banks and head offices |
| Manufacturing | From inner city to industrial estates and the edge | High land costs and congestion in the centre; need for large single-storey buildings; good road access |
| Services | Hospitals, schools and clinics spread to suburbs | Population moved outwards; demand in new suburbs |
| Housing | Growth of suburbs and sprawl; inner-city areas decline or are regenerated; flats and densification | Population growth, rising incomes and transport; planning and land supply; regeneration schemes |

Forces behind change: population change, land cost, transport, competition, investment and planning decisions.

## Residential zonation

Zones of housing form because of: **income** (what people can pay for housing and for transport); **supply** (type and cost of land and houses available); **ethnicity and culture** (a preference to live near a community, which in some cities was enforced by law); **planning** (zoning rules, minimum stand sizes, council housing); and **history** (the colonial planning of Zimbabwean cities, or the order in which areas were built). Zones change with time, as areas become older and cheaper or are regenerated.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Land value and building height]
A transect from the CBD along a main road (illustrative data).

| Distance from CBD (km) | Land value (US\$ per m²) | Average building height (storeys) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 200 | 14 |
| 1 | 640 | 8 |
| 2 | 380 | 4 |
| 4 | 150 | 2 |
| 8 | 70 | 1.5 |

1. Fall between 0 and 2 km = (1 200 − 380) ÷ 1 200 × 100 = 68.3%.
2. Land value at the centre is 1 200 ÷ 70 = 17.1 times that at 8 km.
3. Both land value and height fall with distance. High land values force users to build upwards and to use space intensively, so heights fall as costs fall. Shops and offices, which gain most from accessibility, can pay the highest rents.
:::

:::worked-solution[Share of households by zone]
A survey of 500 households in a city (illustrative data): 60 in the inner city, 275 in high-density suburbs, 120 in medium-density suburbs and 45 in low-density suburbs.

1. Check: 60 + 275 + 120 + 45 = 500.
2. Percentages: inner city 12%, high-density 55%, medium-density 24%, low-density 9%.
3. A majority of households live in high-density suburbs, and only 9% in low-density suburbs, which take a far larger area per household. The pattern shows that land use per person varies sharply between zones.
:::

:::example{title="Describing decentralisation of retail"}
A description of a city says: In 1990 most shops were in the CBD. By 2020 several shopping centres had opened in the northern suburbs, offering parking and a mixture of supermarkets and restaurants. Some CBD shops had closed or been converted to offices or informal trading. Reasons: customers, especially those with cars and higher incomes, moved to suburbs; land for parking was available at lower cost; developers invested in malls; and the CBD suffered from congestion. The result is a city with several retail centres, closer to a multiple nuclei structure.
:::

Both boards require zones, models and changing activities. ZIMSEC adds urban morphology and settlement development. Cambridge adds place identity and perception and expects located examples with dated facts.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Presenting models as fact"}
Models simplify. Say where a real city fits the model and where it differs, and why.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a zone without a reason"}
For each zone give land use, land value, density and height, and explain with accessibility, cost, history or planning.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that structure changes"}
Cities are changing: retail and offices decentralise, inner areas decline or regenerate, and informal settlements grow.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 2 (topic 9) and Cambridge Paper 2 (AS topic 6), often with a map or table of zones. *Describe* the pattern of land use, density and height using figures; *explain* with land value, accessibility, history and planning; *compare* a city with a model; *assess* the extent to which a model fits a city and give reasons for changes in location with a judgement.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cities have a CBD, inner city, suburbs, industrial areas and a rural-urban fringe; land value and building height fall with distance from the centre.
- Burgess, Hoyt and Harris-Ullman models are starting points: rings, sectors and multiple nuclei.
- Zimbabwean cities have low-, medium- and high-density suburbs, industrial estates and informal settlements, shaped by colonial planning.
- Retail, offices, manufacturing and housing decentralise because of land cost, transport, competition, investment and planning.
- Residential zones form from income, supply, culture, planning and history; perception and place identity shape how people see areas.
:::
