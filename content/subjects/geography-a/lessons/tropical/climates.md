## Introduction

Zimbabwe lies in the seasonally humid tropics. The rains arrive between about November and March, when moist air moves in and the sky builds up into afternoon thunderstorms; then comes a long dry season. Only a few thousand kilometres to the north, in the Congo basin, it rains in almost every month. The same part of the globe has two very different climates, and the difference is explained by the movement of the **Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** and the pressure systems around it.

This lesson covers the climate only. The plants and soils that go with it are in the next lesson, and the challenges of using these environments are in the third. You should already know the global circulation (Hadley cell, trade winds) and the difference between convectional and relief rainfall.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Humid tropical climate (tropical rainforest)"}
A climate within about 10° of the equator with high temperatures all year, a very small annual temperature range, and heavy rain in every month, usually more than 2 000 mm a year.
:::

:::definition{term="Seasonally humid tropical climate (savanna)"}
A climate between about 5° and 20° (sometimes 25°) latitude with high temperatures all year and a marked wet and dry season. Annual rainfall is usually between about 600 mm and 1 500 mm and falls mostly in the high-sun season.
:::

:::definition{term="Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)"}
The zone near the thermal equator where the trade winds from the two hemispheres meet. Air converges, is heated and rises, giving cloud and heavy rain. It moves north and south with the overhead Sun, lagging by several weeks.
:::

:::definition{term="Monsoon"}
A seasonal reversal of the prevailing wind, caused by the differential heating of land and sea and by the seasonal shift of the ITCZ. The summer monsoon brings moist air from the ocean and heavy rain; the winter monsoon brings dry air from the land.
:::

:::definition{term="ENSO (El Niño–Southern Oscillation)"}
A natural variation in the tropical Pacific in which sea-surface temperature and air pressure change together. The warm phase (El Niño) and the cool phase (La Niña) alter rainfall in many tropical regions every few years.
:::

## Distribution

Rainforest climates lie close to the equator in three main areas: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin of central Africa, and the islands and peninsulas of South-East Asia (for example Indonesia and Malaysia). Savanna climates form a broken belt on the poleward side, across much of Africa south of the Sahara (including Zimbabwe, Zambia and Tanzania), in the Brazilian highlands and Venezuelan llanos, in northern Australia and in India. Highlands and ocean currents shift the edges: rainforest does not extend into the Kalahari side of Africa, where the climate is much drier.

## Temperature and rainfall

| Feature | Rainforest | Savanna |
|---|---|---|
| Mean monthly temperature | About 25–28 °C in every month | About 20–30 °C, cooler in the dry season |
| Annual temperature range | Very small, usually under 3 °C | Larger, about 5–10 °C |
| Diurnal range | About 5–10 °C, larger than the annual range | Larger still in the dry season (clear nights) |
| Annual rainfall | More than about 2 000 mm | About 600–1 500 mm |
| Seasons | No dry season; one or two slight maxima | One wet season and one dry season of several months |
| Rainfall type | Convectional, usually afternoon storms | Convectional and, in places, ITCZ or monsoon rain |

Because the Sun is high all year, there is no cold season in the rainforest and **the temperature in a day varies more than it does through the year**. In the savanna the temperature peaks just before the rains begin, because cloud then cuts insolation, and falls to its lowest in the dry season, when clear nights lose heat quickly.

## Explaining the climate

1. **The ITCZ.** At the thermal equator the surface is strongly heated, air converges and rises, cools and condenses, and heavy rain follows. Near the equator the ITCZ passes overhead twice a year (around the equinoxes), so some places have two rainfall maxima. Further from the equator the ITCZ is overhead once, giving **one wet season**, and the rest of the year is dry.
2. **Subtropical anticyclones.** At about 20°–30° latitude, air that rose at the ITCZ sinks as part of the Hadley cell. Sinking air warms and is stable, which prevents cloud. As the ITCZ moves away in the low-Sun season, the high-pressure belt moves over the savanna and brings the dry season.
3. **Monsoons.** In South Asia the land heats rapidly in summer and the ITCZ moves far north, pulling in moist south-westerly air from the Indian Ocean (about June to September). In winter the flow reverses. West Africa has a similar summer monsoon from the Gulf of Guinea, with a hot, dry north-easterly wind (the Harmattan) in the dry season. In southern Africa the rains are linked to the southward shift of the ITCZ and to moist air from the Indian Ocean in the summer half of the year.
4. **ENSO.** In normal conditions strong trade winds push warm surface water towards the western Pacific, where air rises and rain is heavy. In an **El Niño** the trade winds weaken, the warm water spreads east, and the area of rising air moves with it. Indonesia, northern Australia and often the Amazon and southern Africa become drier; the Peruvian coast becomes wetter. The 2015–16 El Niño and the 2023–24 El Niño were each followed by serious drought in southern Africa, including Zimbabwe. A **La Niña** strengthens the trade winds and tends to bring above-average rain to southern Africa and to Indonesia.

## Worked examples

The tables below give illustrative climate data for two stations, invented for this exercise.

**Station R (near the equator)**

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 26.8 | 27.0 | 27.2 | 27.1 | 26.9 | 26.4 | 26.2 | 26.6 | 27.0 | 27.2 | 27.1 | 26.9 |
| Rainfall (mm) | 280 | 270 | 310 | 320 | 290 | 200 | 150 | 120 | 140 | 190 | 250 | 290 |

**Station S (about 15° south)**

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | 24.0 | 23.8 | 23.2 | 21.5 | 18.6 | 16.4 | 16.0 | 18.2 | 21.4 | 23.8 | 24.4 | 24.2 |
| Rainfall (mm) | 190 | 165 | 90 | 30 | 8 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 6 | 38 | 105 | 170 |

:::worked-solution[Annual temperature range and total rainfall]
1. Station R: warmest month 27.2 °C, coolest 26.2 °C, so the range is $27.2 - 26.2 = 1.0$ °C.
2. Station S: warmest month 24.4 °C (November), coolest 16.0 °C (July), so the range is $24.4 - 16.0 = 8.4$ °C.
3. Total rainfall for R: $280+270+310+320+290+200+150+120+140+190+250+290 = 2\,810$ mm.
4. Total for S: $190+165+90+30+8+2+1+2+6+38+105+170 = 807$ mm.
5. R has a small range and more than 2 000 mm in every season (rainforest); S has a large range, under 1 000 mm and a long dry season (savanna).
:::

:::worked-solution[Share of rain that falls in the wet season (Station S)]
1. The wet season is November to March: $105+170+190+165+90 = 720$ mm.
2. Share $= 720 \div 807 \times 100 = 89.2\%$.
3. The dry months May to September receive only $8+2+1+2+6 = 19$ mm. Rain is concentrated in the months when the ITCZ is overhead, and for the rest of the year the subtropical anticyclone dominates.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a climate graph"}
For Station R: "Temperature is high and almost constant, between 26.2 °C and 27.2 °C (a range of 1.0 °C). Rainfall is above 100 mm in every month, with a maximum of 320 mm in April and a minimum of 120 mm in August, and a total of 2 810 mm. There are two weak rainfall peaks (March–April and November–December), which fit the ITCZ passing overhead twice. There is no dry season." Quote figures with units, and describe the shape before explaining.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying rainforests have no seasons at all"}
They have no cold or dry season, but rainfall is not equal in every month and there may be two maxima. The temperature varies by only a degree or two through the year, while the day-to-night range is larger.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the ITCZ as a line where the Sun is hottest"}
The ITCZ is the zone where the trade winds meet and air rises. It follows the overhead Sun with a lag and sits over land and sea in different places.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating the monsoon as a type of rain"}
A monsoon is a seasonal wind system. It brings rain in the summer because the wind carries moist air onto the land.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 7, tropical environments). *Describe* a climate graph by quoting the highest and lowest values with units, calculating a range or total, and naming the shape. *Explain* with a chain: Sun overhead, heating, convergence, rising air, condensation, rain; for the dry season, sinking air in the subtropical anticyclone. For *assess how far ENSO explains variation*, say that it is one of several controls (with the ITCZ, ocean temperatures and local factors) and support this with a dated event.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rainforest climates lie within about 10° of the equator: about 25–28 °C all year, a range under 3 °C, more than 2 000 mm of rain and no dry season.
- Savanna climates lie at about 5°–20° latitude: one wet and one dry season, 600–1 500 mm of rain and a larger temperature range.
- The ITCZ gives rain when overhead; subtropical anticyclones with sinking air give the dry season.
- Monsoons are seasonal wind reversals, driven by land and sea heating and the shift of the ITCZ.
- El Niño usually brings drought to Indonesia, northern Australia and southern Africa; La Niña tends to bring more rain.
- Describe a climate graph with figures, a range and a total; explain using the ITCZ and pressure belts.
:::
