## Introduction

A farmer in the Zambezi lowveld clears mopane woodland for a cotton field. The first harvests are good, but a few seasons later the yield falls. A farmer in the Amazon who clears rainforest sees the same pattern even more quickly. In both cases the plants and the soil work as one system, and the removal of the vegetation breaks it. In this lesson you will see how climate shapes the plant communities of the humid and seasonally humid tropics, how the soils form beneath them, and why people change both.

You should already know the climates described in the previous lesson, and from the biogeography topic the idea of succession and climax and the way nutrients cycle between biomass, litter and soil (shown on a Gersmehl diagram). This lesson does not repeat the full nutrient-cycle theory.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Climatic climax"}
The stable plant community in equilibrium with the regional climate, which is the end point of succession when climate is the main control. Tropical rainforest is the climatic climax of the humid tropics.
:::

:::definition{term="Subclimax"}
A community that is held short of the climatic climax by a natural, non-climatic factor, such as repeated fire, grazing animals or poor soil drainage. Some savannas are fire subclimax communities.
:::

:::definition{term="Plagioclimax"}
A community whose development is arrested or altered by human activity, such as regular burning, grazing, clearing and cultivation, so that it differs from the climatic climax.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxisol (latosol)"}
A deep, red or yellow, strongly weathered soil of the humid tropics. It is dominated by clays such as kaolinite and by oxides of iron and aluminium, and has low natural fertility. Latosol is the older name and ferralsol a related international term.
:::

:::definition{term="Laterisation"}
Intense chemical weathering in hot, wet conditions, in which silica and bases are leached out and iron and aluminium oxides remain.
:::

## Rainforest vegetation

The rainforest is evergreen, species-rich and layered. The layers are:

1. **Emergent trees**, 40 m or more, which stand above the canopy and receive full light and wind.
2. **Canopy**, roughly 25–40 m, a continuous roof of leaves that intercepts most of the light and the rain.
3. **Understorey and shrub layer**, shorter trees and saplings that survive in shade.
4. **Forest floor**, dark and humid, where only a few per cent of the sunlight arrives, so few plants grow and litter decays quickly.

Plants are adapted to heat, rain and shade. **Drip-tip leaves** shed water quickly; **buttress roots** support tall trees in shallow soil; **lianas and epiphytes** reach light without making a thick trunk; many trees are **evergreen** because there is no dry season, and flowering and leaf loss are spread through the year. Species diversity is very high.

## Savanna vegetation

Savanna is a mix of grass and scattered trees, with a continuous cover of grass in the wet season. The grass is tall (more than a metre) in the wetter areas and shorter in the drier parts. Adaptations to the dry season, fire and grazing include:

- **Deciduous or semi-deciduous trees** that shed leaves in the dry season to reduce water loss.
- **Deep tap roots** and, in the grasses, dense fibrous roots near the surface that exploit light rain.
- **Thick, fire-resistant bark**, and buds protected below ground or low on the plant.
- **Small leaves, thorns and waxy surfaces** (for example acacias) to reduce transpiration and browsing, and trunks that store water (baobabs).
- Grasses that grow from the base, so that fire or grazing removes the top but not the growing point.

In Zimbabwe the dominant tree communities of the plateau are **miombo woodland** (species of *Brachystegia* and *Julbernardia*), while hotter, lower areas such as the Zambezi valley and lowveld have large stands of **mopane** (*Colophospermum mopane*). Both are seasonally humid woodlands rather than grass-dominated savannas.

## Climax, subclimax and plagioclimax

Where rainfall is high and there is no dry season, succession ends in rainforest, the climatic climax. Where rainfall is lower and seasonal, woodland is the likely climax, but **fire, grazing and soil conditions** often keep the vegetation as open savanna, a subclimax. Where people burn the grass each year to encourage new growth, graze livestock heavily or clear and cultivate land, the result is a **plagioclimax**. In the rainforest, clearing is followed by *secondary forest*: pioneer species grow fast, but the community has fewer species and a different structure from the original forest, and it may need centuries to recover (if at all).

## Tropical soils

### Oxisols (latosols) under rainforest

1. Heat and heavy rain cause rapid **chemical weathering** (especially hydrolysis), which breaks the parent rock down to a depth that can exceed 10 m.
2. Water percolating through the soil **leaches** silica and soluble bases (calcium, magnesium, potassium), leaving iron and aluminium oxides, which colour the soil red or yellow, and the clay mineral kaolinite.
3. Dead leaves decompose within weeks because the climate is warm and moist, and nutrients are taken up by roots almost at once, so the **humus layer is thin**.
4. The result is a deep profile, with indistinct horizons, a low cation-exchange capacity, an acid reaction and low fertility. The fertility is held in the living biomass, not in the soil.

### Tropical red and brown earths under savanna

With a long dry season leaching is less intense. The profile is shallower and has clearer horizons. The A horizon has more humus from the grass roots, and the **colour is red or brown** because of iron oxides. Soluble substances move upwards by capillary rise in the dry season, and clay moves downwards in the wet season, so the B horizon is richer in clay. Where iron oxides concentrate and harden after wetting and drying, a **laterite crust** can form. These soils are generally more fertile than oxisols but are low in nitrogen and phosphorus, and are easily eroded when the grass is removed.

| Feature | Oxisol (latosol) | Tropical red or brown earth |
|---|---|---|
| Climate | Humid, no dry season | Seasonal, wet and dry seasons |
| Depth | Very deep, often over 10 m of weathered material | Shallower, clearer horizons |
| Leaching | Very intense | Moderate, seasonal |
| Humus layer | Thin | Thicker, from grass roots |
| Colour | Red or yellow | Red or brown |
| Fertility | Low in the soil, held in the biomass | Moderate, low in nitrogen and phosphorus |

## Effects of people

Clearing for logging, farming and ranching removes the nutrient store, which in a rainforest is mostly in the biomass. Exposed soil is struck by heavy rain, so erosion and leaching increase and fertility can fall within a few years. In the savanna, overgrazing and burning reduce the cover and increase erosion and bush encroachment.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Where is the nutrient store? (illustrative data)]
Nitrogen stores in a rainforest are: biomass 1 700 kg/ha, litter 70 kg/ha, soil 530 kg/ha. For a savanna they are: biomass 320, litter 40, soil 1 240 kg/ha.

1. Rainforest total $= 1\,700 + 70 + 530 = 2\,300$ kg/ha. Biomass share $= 1\,700 \div 2\,300 \times 100 = 73.9\%$; litter 3.0%; soil 23.0%.
2. Savanna total $= 320 + 40 + 1\,240 = 1\,600$ kg/ha. Biomass share $= 20.0\%$; litter 2.5%; soil 77.5%.
3. In the rainforest most nutrients are in the living biomass, so clearing the forest removes most of the store. In the savanna, most are in the soil, so the loss on clearing is smaller at first.
:::

:::worked-solution[How fast does litter decompose? (illustrative data)]
Annual litter fall in a rainforest is 10 t/ha and the litter store on the ground is 0.9 t/ha. In a savanna woodland the input is 4 t/ha and the store is 3.0 t/ha.

1. Turnover time $=$ store $\div$ annual input.
2. Rainforest: $0.9 \div 10 = 0.09$ years, about 33 days.
3. Savanna: $3.0 \div 4 = 0.75$ years, about 274 days.
4. Litter breaks down about eight times faster in the rainforest, because warmth and moisture all year favour decomposers, which explains the thin humus layer.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a soil profile from a table"}
Illustrative profile under rainforest: 0–5 cm dark brown, organic matter 6%, pH 4.8; 5–200 cm red clay, organic matter 1%, pH 4.5; more than 200 cm deeply weathered rock. **Describe:** the humus layer is thin (5 cm) and the soil is red, deep and acid. **Explain:** rapid decomposition and uptake leave little humus; leaching removes bases, so the pH is low; iron oxides give the red colour. **Suggest** why farming fails here: the soil store is small, so yields fall after the biomass is removed.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying rainforest soils are fertile because the forest is lush"}
The forest is lush because nutrients are held and recycled in the biomass. The soil itself is leached and infertile.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing subclimax with plagioclimax"}
A subclimax is held back by a natural factor such as fire or grazing animals. A plagioclimax is held back by people. State the cause.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rainforest is a layered, evergreen, species-rich climatic climax; savanna is grass with scattered fire- and drought-adapted trees.
- A climatic climax is controlled by climate, a subclimax by natural factors such as fire, and a plagioclimax by people.
- Oxisols (latosols) are deep, red, leached and acid, with a thin humus layer; most nutrients are held in the biomass.
- Tropical red and brown earths are shallower, with more humus, clearer horizons and moderate fertility.
- Clearing vegetation breaks the nutrient cycle, increases erosion and leaching, and quickly reduces fertility.
- Turnover time = store ÷ annual input; shares of stores = part ÷ total × 100.
:::
