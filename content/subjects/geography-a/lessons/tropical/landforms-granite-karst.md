## Introduction

The granite domes of the Matobo Hills rise abruptly from the surrounding bush, and piles of rounded boulders perch on top of each other so delicately that they are known as balancing rocks. These are some of the best-known landforms of Zimbabwe, and similar forms are found across the tropics. In very different places, such as southern China or Jamaica, thick limestone has been dissolved into forests of conical hills and towers. Both sets of landforms are produced by weathering acting deep and for a long time in warm climates.

You should know the weathering processes (particularly hydrolysis and carbonation) from earlier in this topic. This lesson looks at the landforms they leave on granite and on limestone, and at the hard crusts that form in some tropical soils.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Bornhardt (dwala, ruware)"}
A large, steep-sided, dome-shaped hill of massive, widely jointed rock (usually granite or gneiss) that rises abruptly from a surrounding plain. It is named after the German geologist Bornhardt. In Zimbabwe the Shona word **ruware** and the Ndebele word **dwala** are used.
:::

:::definition{term="Kopje (koppie) and tor"}
A **kopje** is a small hill capped or made of masses of rounded blocks and boulders. A **tor** is a free-standing pile or stack of rounded blocks on a hilltop or slope. Both are smaller than a bornhardt.
:::

:::definition{term="Inselberg"}
A general term for an isolated residual hill rising from a plain. Bornhardts, kopjes and tors are all inselbergs.
:::

:::definition{term="Karst"}
A landscape formed by the solution of soluble rock, mainly limestone, with caves, sinkholes, underground drainage and distinctive hills.
:::

## Granite landforms

Granite is made of crystals of quartz, feldspar and mica and is resistant, but not equally so everywhere. Where the rock is **closely jointed**, water enters easily and the rock is weathered deeply. Where it is **massive** (few or widely spaced joints), it resists weathering. This difference in joint spacing is the key to every theory of how granite landforms develop.

| Landform | Size and shape | Typical features |
|---|---|---|
| Bornhardt (dwala) | Tens to hundreds of metres high; dome or whaleback | Bare rock, rounded or steep flanks, curved sheets (exfoliation), sharp break of slope at the base |
| Kopje | Smaller hill made of piled boulders | Rounded blocks split by joints, often castle-like |
| Tor | Stack of blocks on a hilltop or slope | Rounded boulders, sometimes balancing, with weathered rock around |

### Theories of formation

1. **Two-stage deep weathering and stripping.** In **stage 1**, chemical weathering (mainly hydrolysis) works down along joints, below the ground surface and under a cover of soil. Closely jointed rock is rotted to a great depth, and unjointed rock remains as a fresh core. In **stage 2**, the weathered regolith is stripped away (after uplift, a fall in base level or a change in climate), and the unweathered cores are exposed as bornhardts, kopjes and tors. The rounded shape arises because weathering attacks the blocks from the edges inwards. The same idea is used in **etchplanation**.
2. **Scarp retreat (pediplanation).** King proposed that scarps retreat parallel to themselves and leave isolated residual hills standing on a widening pediplain. The inselbergs survive because they are made of more massive rock.
3. **Pressure release.** As overlying rock is removed, a dome expands and cracks open parallel to its surface. Sheets of rock peel away (exfoliation), which helps to maintain and smooth the dome shape.

![Two-stage formation of a granite dwala: in stage 1 chemical weathering rots closely jointed rock deeply beneath the surface while massive unjointed rock remains as a fresh core; in stage 2 the weathered cover is stripped away, exposing a steep dome (a flank of 180 m in 300 m is a 60 percent gradient, 31 degrees) with a sharp break of slope at its foot.](media:diagrams/dwala-two-stage-formation.png "Figure: two-stage deep weathering and stripping explains a dwala (schematic cross-sections)")

Most geomorphologists accept that a combination applies and that the spacing of joints is the underlying control. **Evidence** for the two-stage theory includes deeply weathered granite found beneath the plain next to a dome, and boulders called corestones within the regolith.

## Duricrust (ZIMSEC detail)

A **duricrust** is a hard layer that forms at or near the surface when minerals are concentrated and cemented in soil or regolith.

- **Laterite (ferricrete):** iron and aluminium oxides concentrate in tropical soils with alternating wet and dry seasons. Heavy rain leaches away silica and bases, leaving the oxides behind. A soft, clay-rich layer (plinthite) hardens irreversibly when exposed to air and to repeated wetting and drying. The word comes from the Latin *later*, brick.
- Other types: **calcrete** (calcium carbonate), **silcrete** (silica) and **alcrete** (aluminium, in bauxite).

Duricrust resists erosion, so it can form a cap on a plateau and protect the layers below. When streams cut through the cap and remove the weaker rock beneath, scarps and flat-topped hills (mesas) remain. Hard crusts also limit root growth and infiltration, and they are quarried for building material.

## Tropical karst

**Karst** forms on thick, jointed limestone. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes weak carbonic acid, which dissolves calcium carbonate (carbonation). In the humid tropics:

![Limestone karst in cross-section: rain made slightly acid by carbon dioxide from soil and plants dissolves limestone along joints, widening them into shafts, caves and sinkholes, and water drains underground in caves and streams instead of on the surface.](media:diagrams/karst-solution-features.png "Figure: solution features of a limestone (karst) landscape (schematic cross-section)")

- Heavy rainfall supplies a large volume of water.
- Dense vegetation and warm, wet soil supply high concentrations of carbon dioxide from respiration and decay.
- Solution is concentrated along joints, which widen to caves and shafts.

The result is a landscape with **sinkholes** (depressions), caves and underground streams, and three characteristic hill landscapes.

| Type | Shape | Notes and examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Cockpit karst** | Closed star-shaped depressions (cockpits) surrounded by many rounded or conical hills | Jamaica's Cockpit Country |
| **Cone karst** | Many rounded, conical hills with cockpits between them | Parts of southern China |
| **Tower karst** | Isolated steep-sided towers on a flat plain, often with undercut bases | Guilin in China; Ha Long Bay in Vietnam, where the sea has drowned the towers |

Tower karst is often seen as a later stage, in which the hills are reduced and the plain between them is covered by sediment, but the sequence is still debated. Zimbabwe has no large area of tropical karst. The **Chinhoyi Caves**, in dolomitic limestone, show some solution features and a deep pool, but they are small.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Gradient of a dwala flank]
A surveyed profile on a dwala shows that the flank rises 180 m over a horizontal distance of 300 m (illustrative data).

1. Gradient $= 180 \div 300 = 0.60$, which is **60%**.
2. Angle $= \tan^{-1}(0.60) = 31.0°$.
3. This is much steeper than the gentle plain at its foot (for example 1-2°), so there is a sharp break of slope at the base, which is typical of a bornhardt.
:::

:::worked-solution[How long does solution take to lower a limestone surface?]
A limestone surface in a humid tropical area is lowered by solution at about 40 mm per 1 000 years (illustrative rate). How long would it take to lower the surface by 10 m?

1. Convert 10 m to millimetres: $10 \text{ m} = 10\,000$ mm.
2. Number of 1 000-year periods $= 10\,000 \div 40 = 250$.
3. Time $= 250 \times 1\,000 = 250\,000$ years.
4. Such a landscape needs hundreds of thousands to millions of years to develop, which explains why karst is most dramatic where the climate has been warm and wet for a very long time.
:::

:::example{title="Describing a karst landscape from a data table"}
Illustrative survey of 12 km² of a tropical limestone area: 48 closed depressions, hill height 60-120 m, hills conical and close together, a few caves, little surface drainage. **Calculate** the density of depressions: $48 \div 12 = 4$ per km². **Identify:** many closed star-shaped depressions among conical hills is **cockpit (cone) karst**. **Explain:** dissolution along joints by acid water, helped by high rainfall and carbon dioxide from vegetation, has enlarged the depressions and left the hills between them. Surface drainage is lacking because water flows underground through the joints and caves.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect bornhardts (dwala, ruware), kopjes and tors, with theories of their formation (ZIMSEC 5.4, Form 6; Cambridge 7.2.2-7.2.3, an A Level Paper 3 option). Duricrust (laterite) is ZIMSEC detail. Cambridge also asks you to describe and explain cone, tower and cockpit karst.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving a single cause for dwalas"}
There is more than one theory. Name at least two, show what evidence supports each, and state that joint spacing is the control common to them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining karst as caused by erosion by rivers"}
Karst is made mainly by solution (carbonation), not by river erosion. The main water movement is underground.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating all hard-crusted soils as laterite"}
Laterite is a duricrust of iron and aluminium oxides. Others (calcrete, silcrete) consist of other minerals. Name the crust's composition.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in ZIMSEC Paper 1 (topic 5) and Cambridge Paper 3 (option 7). *Describe* a landform by size, shape and surface features, with figures. *Explain* formation with numbered stages (what weathers, where, then what is removed). For *discuss theories* or *assess*, set out two theories, give evidence for each and a limitation, and reach a judgement. Name real, established examples (for example the Matobo Hills) without quoting figures that you are not sure of. For karst, always mention carbonic acid, joints and the role of climate and vegetation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bornhardts (dwalas, ruware), kopjes and tors are inselbergs of massive, widely jointed granite or gneiss.
- Theories: two-stage deep weathering and stripping; scarp retreat; pressure release. Joint spacing controls them all.
- Duricrust (laterite) is a hard crust of iron and aluminium oxides formed in tropical soils by leaching and wet-dry cycles.
- Karst forms by carbonation of limestone; in the tropics heavy rain and soil carbon dioxide make it rapid.
- Cockpit and cone karst have depressions and conical hills; tower karst has isolated steep towers.
- Gradient = vertical ÷ horizontal; time = thickness ÷ rate of lowering.
:::
