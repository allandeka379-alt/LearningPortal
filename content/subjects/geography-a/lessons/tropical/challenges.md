## Introduction

Rainforests cover only a small part of the land surface, yet they hold a very large share of the world's plant and animal species and store large amounts of carbon. Savannas support millions of people and much of Africa's wildlife and livestock. Both are changing quickly: forest is cleared for farms, ranches and mines, savanna woodland is cut for fuel and fields, and the climate is changing. Understanding these pressures, and judging whether the responses work, is a central part of this option.

You should know the climates, vegetation and soils from the previous two lessons. Fragile soils, nutrient stores held in biomass, and a climate dependent on the ITCZ explain why the environments react so strongly to change.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The permanent removal of forest cover and its conversion to another land use, such as farmland, pasture, mines or settlements.
:::

:::definition{term="Monoculture"}
The large-scale cultivation of a single crop, such as oil palm, soya or sugar cane, over a wide area.
:::

:::definition{term="Selective logging"}
Removing only chosen trees, usually the valuable ones, as opposed to clear-felling a whole area. The roads, skid trails and damage to neighbouring trees can still harm the forest.
:::

:::definition{term="Sustainable management"}
Use of a resource at a rate that allows it to renew and continue to meet needs without lasting damage to the environment or to future generations.
:::

## Causes of change

1. **Population pressure and land-use change.** Shifting cultivation, with long fallow periods, is sustainable at low densities. When the population increases, fallow periods shorten, the soil loses its nutrients and more forest is cleared. Roads built for other reasons open the forest to settlers. In savanna regions, rising numbers of people and livestock lead to overgrazing, clearing of woodland for fields and heavy use of wood for fuel. In Zimbabwe, for example, the cutting of woodland for fuelwood, including the curing of tobacco, has been identified as a pressure on miombo woodland.
2. **Monoculture and large-scale agriculture.** Oil palm in Indonesia and Malaysia, soya and cattle in Brazil, and sugar cane in tropical lowlands replace diverse forest with a single crop. Biodiversity falls, soil is exposed, agrochemicals pollute streams, and the carbon stored in the forest is released. The Brazilian Cerrado, a savanna, has been extensively cleared for soya since the 1970s.
3. **Logging.** Timber is exported or used locally. Selective logging damages many other trees and, above all, builds roads that allow farmers, hunters and illegal loggers to enter. Illegal logging is difficult to control over large, remote areas.
4. **Mining.** Large open-cast mines, such as iron ore at Carajás in Brazil, clear forest and need roads, railways and power. Small-scale gold mining pollutes rivers with sediment and with mercury.
5. **Climate change.** Warming raises evaporation and heat stress. Models and observations show more frequent droughts in parts of the Amazon (for example in 2005, 2010 and 2015–16), and drought makes forest dry and more likely to burn. In savannas the length and reliability of the rainy season may change and, with more fire, tree cover may be reduced. If much of the Amazon dried and turned to savanna, some scientists argue that a **tipping point** would have been passed, with large releases of carbon and effects on regional rainfall.

:::worked-solution[Rate and percentage of forest loss (illustrative data)]
Forest cover in a tropical region was: 1990, 420 thousand km²; 2000, 386; 2010, 361; 2020, 335.

1. Total loss, 1990–2020 $= 420 - 335 = 85$ thousand km².
2. Percentage loss $= 85 \div 420 \times 100 = 20.2\%$.
3. Mean annual loss $= 85 \div 30 = 2.8$ thousand km² a year (2 800 km²).
4. By decade the loss fell from $420 - 386 = 34$ thousand km² (3.4 a year) in 1990–2000 to $361 - 335 = 26$ (2.6 a year) in 2010–2020, so the rate of loss has slowed, although the forest is still shrinking.
:::

:::worked-solution[Carbon released by clearing forest (illustrative data)]
A forest of 5 000 km² stores 150 tonnes of carbon per hectare in its biomass. How much carbon is released if it is cleared and burned?

1. Convert area: $5\,000 \text{ km}^2 \times 100 = 500\,000$ ha.
2. Carbon $= 500\,000 \times 150 = 75\,000\,000$ tonnes, or 75 million tonnes of carbon.
3. As carbon dioxide (multiply by $44 \div 12$): $75 \times 44 \div 12 = 275$ million tonnes of CO$_2$.
4. This is why forest loss is a major source of greenhouse gases and why protecting forest is a form of climate mitigation.
:::

## Detailed specific example: the Brazilian Amazon (rainforest)

**Location and issues.** The Amazon basin of Brazil holds the largest area of tropical rainforest. From the 1970s, roads (such as the Trans-Amazonian highway), government settlement schemes, cattle ranching, soya, logging and mining brought rapid clearance. Cleared pasture and fields are frequently abandoned after a few years because the soils are infertile. Annual clearance in the Legal Amazon, measured by satellite (INPE, PRODES, rounded), was about 27 800 km² in 2004.

**Management strategies.**

- **Satellite monitoring and enforcement** from 2004 under the federal plan to prevent and control deforestation (fines, seizure of equipment and restrictions on credit for illegal clearing).
- **Protected areas and Indigenous territories**, which cover a very large part of the Brazilian Amazon, and where clearance rates are lower than outside.
- **The Soy Moratorium** (from 2006), in which major traders agreed not to buy soya grown on land cleared after an agreed date.
- **The Amazon Fund** (from 2008), supported by donors such as Norway and Germany, which pays for protection and sustainable projects.
- **Extractive reserves** and sustainable use of forest products (nuts, rubber, fruit), and certification of timber.

**Success.** Annual clearance fell to about 4 600 km² in 2012 (INPE, rounded), a fall of more than 80% from 2004, which shows what enforcement and demand-side agreements can achieve. However, clearance rose again after 2012 and exceeded 10 000 km² a year from 2019 as enforcement was weakened, and fell in 2023 after the policies were restored. Illegal clearance and fires, weak governance in remote areas, high prices for beef and soya, and drought are continuing problems. A fair judgement is that the strategies **work when enforcement and market pressure are strong and continuous**, but gains can be lost quickly when political support changes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Blaming only local farmers"}
Clearance is driven by national policy, roads, markets, and demand from other countries for beef, soya, palm oil and timber as well as by local people. Mention several causes and their links.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a case study in general terms"}
A *detailed specific example* needs the place, dates, names of policies and the results. Quote a date and a rounded figure and say whether the strategy worked.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that management has either worked or failed"}
Evaluation means judging how well, where and for how long. In the Amazon the same measures worked in the 2000s and weakened later.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Tested in Cambridge Paper 3 (option 7); the essay often requires a detailed specific example. *Explain* causes by linking the human action to the environmental effect (road, access, clearance, soil loss). *Assess the success of management* by naming each strategy, giving evidence of its effect (with a date and a figure), then a limitation and a judgement. Link climate change to fire risk, drought and the possible loss of the forest's role as a carbon store.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Population pressure, monoculture, logging, mining and climate change are changing rainforest and savanna.
- Roads give access to the forest, and cleared land is often abandoned when the soil is exhausted.
- Clearing releases stored carbon (area in ha × carbon per hectare), reduces biodiversity and may change regional rainfall.
- Amazon strategies include monitoring and enforcement, protected areas, the Soy Moratorium, the Amazon Fund and extractive reserves.
- Clearance fell sharply after 2004 and rose again after 2012, so success depends on enforcement and demand.
- A detailed example needs a place, dates, strategies, results and a judgement.
:::
