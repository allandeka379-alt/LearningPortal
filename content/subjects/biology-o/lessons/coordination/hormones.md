## Introduction

Sipho is herding cattle near the boundary of Hwange National Park when a lion roars close by. Within seconds his heart is pounding, he is breathing fast, his eyes are wide open and his legs are ready to run. His nervous system detected the danger, but the changes that spread through his whole body were caused by a **hormone**: **adrenaline**.

The body has two coordination systems. The **nervous system** sends fast electrical impulses along neurones. The **endocrine system** sends chemical messages, hormones, in the blood. In this lesson you will learn what hormones are, which glands make them, what adrenaline does, and how the two systems compare.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Both boards: the definition of a hormone, the adrenal glands, pancreas, testes and ovaries and their hormones, adrenaline in "fight or flight" situations, and nervous and hormonal control compared. **Cambridge Supplement**: the pancreas also secretes glucagon, and adrenaline raises the blood glucose concentration. The control of blood glucose is in the next lesson.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hormone"}
A chemical substance, produced by a gland and carried by the blood, which alters the activity of one or more specific target organs.
:::

:::definition{term="Endocrine gland"}
A gland that secretes hormones directly into the blood. Endocrine glands have no ducts (tubes).
:::

:::definition{term="Target organ"}
An organ whose activity is changed by a particular hormone. A hormone reaches every part of the body in the blood, but only its target organs respond to it.
:::

## The main endocrine glands

| Gland | Where it is | Hormone | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Adrenal glands | On top of each kidney | Adrenaline | Prepares the body for vigorous action ("fight or flight") |
| Pancreas | Just below the stomach | Insulin; glucagon (Supplement) | Insulin lowers the blood glucose concentration; glucagon raises it |
| Testes | In the scrotum | Testosterone | Male secondary sexual characteristics at puberty |
| Ovaries | In the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus | Oestrogen (and progesterone) | Female secondary sexual characteristics; control of the menstrual cycle |

You may also meet the **pituitary gland** below the brain, whose hormones control several other glands, and the **thyroid gland** in the neck, whose hormone thyroxine controls the rate of metabolism.

The pancreas has two jobs. It makes **digestive enzymes**, which pass along a duct to the small intestine, and it makes **hormones**, which pass straight into the blood.

:::keyfact
Hormones travel in the blood plasma to every part of the body, but only the cells of their target organs respond to them.
:::

## Adrenaline: "fight or flight"

When you are frightened, excited or under stress, the nervous system stimulates the **adrenal glands** to secrete adrenaline into the blood. Adrenaline prepares the body to fight or to run away.

| Effect of adrenaline | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Heart beats faster and more strongly | More blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, reaches the muscles |
| Breathing becomes faster and deeper | More oxygen enters the blood and more carbon dioxide is removed |
| Pupils dilate (widen) | More light enters the eyes, so the person can see the danger better |
| Blood glucose concentration rises (Supplement) | More glucose is available to the muscles for respiration |
| Blood is diverted from the gut and skin to the muscles | The muscles get the blood they need for action |

:::example{title="Example 1: Sipho and the lion"}
1. **Stimulus**: the lion's roar is detected by receptors in Sipho's ears.
2. Nerve impulses reach his brain, which stimulates his adrenal glands.
3. The adrenal glands secrete **adrenaline** into his blood.
4. Within seconds his heart rate and breathing rate rise, his pupils widen and his liver releases glucose into the blood.
5. His leg muscles receive more **oxygen and glucose**, so they can respire faster, release more energy and carry him quickly to safety.
:::

### How adrenaline raises blood glucose (Cambridge Supplement)

Adrenaline stimulates the **liver** to break down its store of **glycogen** into **glucose**, which passes into the blood. With more glucose and more oxygen arriving, the muscles can respire faster. Adrenaline also increases the **heart rate**, as you saw above. These are examples of a hormone controlling **metabolic activity**.

:::example{title="Example 2: a heart rate that is slow to settle"}
A learner's resting heart rate is 72 beats per minute. A sudden loud bang in the corridor makes it jump to about 110 within a few seconds. It then takes several minutes to return to 72. The quick jump shows how fast the response starts; the slow return shows that adrenaline stays in the blood for some time before it is broken down, so hormonal effects last longer than a single nervous response.
:::

Everyday "fight or flight" moments include taking a penalty in a cup final, being chased by a dog, a near miss on a busy road and waiting to sit an examination.

## Nervous and hormonal control compared

| Feature | Nervous control | Hormonal control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type of message | Electrical impulses (chemical only across synapses) | Chemical (hormones) |
| Carried by | Neurones | The blood (plasma) |
| Speed | Very fast | Slower |
| How long the effect lasts | Short-lived | Longer-lasting |
| Where the effect is | Precise: particular muscles or glands | Often widespread: several target organs |
| Examples | Reflexes, moving a hand | Fight or flight, control of blood glucose, puberty |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hormones travel along nerves"}
Hormones are carried in the **blood**. Nerves carry electrical impulses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a hormone affects every organ"}
A hormone reaches every organ in the blood, but only its **target organs** respond.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying adrenaline makes the pupils smaller"}
Adrenaline makes the pupils **dilate** (widen), letting in more light.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying insulin is made by the liver"}
Insulin is made by the **pancreas**. The liver is one of insulin's target organs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying adrenaline gives the muscles energy"}
Adrenaline contains no useful energy. It increases the supply of **oxygen and glucose** to the muscles, so they can release more energy by respiration.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
A full definition of a hormone has four parts: a **chemical**, made by a **gland**, carried by the **blood**, that alters the activity of **target organs**.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "explain how adrenaline helps a person escape danger", link each effect to its advantage: faster heart rate, so more oxygen and glucose reach the muscles, so they respire faster and release more energy.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Compare" questions need matched pairs: electrical **and** chemical, fast **and** slow, short-lived **and** long-lasting.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A hormone is a chemical made by a gland, carried by the blood, that alters the activity of target organs.
- Adrenal glands secrete adrenaline; the pancreas secretes insulin (and glucagon); the testes secrete testosterone; the ovaries secrete oestrogen.
- Adrenaline (fight or flight) increases heart rate and breathing rate and dilates the pupils, so the muscles get more oxygen and glucose.
- Supplement: adrenaline raises blood glucose by stimulating the liver to break down glycogen.
- Nervous control is electrical, fast, short-lived and precise; hormonal control is chemical, slower, longer-lasting and often widespread.
:::
