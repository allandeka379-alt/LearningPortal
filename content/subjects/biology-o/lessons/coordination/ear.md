## Introduction

At a grinding mill the engine roars all day. On a kombi the music may be very loud. On the steep road down from Nyanga, your ears suddenly feel blocked and then "pop". After spinning round and round in a playground game you feel dizzy and wobble when you stop. All of these involve the **ear**, which has two jobs: **hearing** and **balance**.

In this lesson you will learn the parts of the ear and their functions, how sound is turned into nerve impulses, how the ear helps you keep your balance, and how to protect your hearing.

You should already know what receptors and sensory neurones are (see the lesson on the nervous system).

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
This lesson covers ZIMSEC topic 8.16.3 (coordination and response): the structure of the ear and the functions of its parts in hearing and balance.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sound wave"}
Vibrations that travel through the air (or another material) from a source of sound.
:::

:::definition{term="Ossicles"}
The three tiny bones of the middle ear: the hammer, the anvil and the stirrup. They pass vibrations from the eardrum to the oval window and make them stronger.
:::

:::definition{term="Cochlea"}
A coiled, fluid-filled tube in the inner ear containing the receptor hair cells for hearing.
:::

:::definition{term="Semicircular canals"}
Three fluid-filled canals in the inner ear, set at right angles to each other, which detect movements of the head and help with balance.
:::

:::definition{term="Eustachian tube"}
A narrow tube linking the middle ear to the back of the throat. It lets air in or out so that the pressure is the same on both sides of the eardrum.
:::

## The parts of the ear

The ear has three regions. Here they are described part by part, from the outside in.

**Outer ear** (contains air)

1. **Pinna**: the flap of skin and cartilage on the side of the head. It collects sound waves and directs them into the ear canal.
2. **Ear canal**: a tube that carries sound waves to the eardrum. Hairs and **wax** in the canal trap dust and microorganisms.
3. **Eardrum**: a thin membrane stretched across the inner end of the canal. It vibrates when sound waves hit it.

**Middle ear** (contains air)

4. **Ossicles**: the **hammer**, **anvil** and **stirrup**, linked in a chain. The hammer touches the eardrum and the stirrup touches the oval window. They pass the vibrations on and **amplify** them (increase their force).
5. **Eustachian tube**: links the middle ear to the throat and equalises air pressure.

**Inner ear** (contains fluid)

6. **Oval window**: a membrane between the middle and inner ear. The stirrup pushes it in and out.
7. **Cochlea**: the coiled tube whose fluid is set vibrating by the oval window. Its **hair cells** are the receptors for sound.
8. **Round window**: a second, smaller membrane that bulges outwards so that the fluid in the cochlea can move.
9. **Semicircular canals**: the three canals for balance.
10. **Auditory nerve**: carries impulses from the cochlea (and the balance organs) to the brain.

:::keyfact
The stirrup is the smallest bone in the human body.
:::

## How we hear

:::example{title="Example 1: hearing the mbira"}
Tendai's grandfather plays the mbira at a family gathering.

1. Sound waves from the mbira are collected by the **pinna** and travel along the **ear canal**.
2. The waves make the **eardrum** vibrate.
3. The **hammer**, **anvil** and **stirrup** pass the vibrations on, making them stronger.
4. The stirrup moves the **oval window** in and out.
5. This makes the **fluid in the cochlea** vibrate.
6. The vibrations stimulate the **hair cells** (receptors) in the cochlea, which start nerve impulses.
7. The **auditory nerve** carries the impulses to the **brain** (cerebrum), which interprets them as the sound of the mbira.
:::

Louder sounds make bigger vibrations. Different parts of the cochlea respond to sounds of different pitch, which is how the brain can tell a high note from a low one.

## How the ear helps with balance

Each of the three **semicircular canals** lies in a different plane, at right angles to the other two, so a movement of the head in **any direction** is detected. Each canal is filled with fluid and has a swelling containing **sensory hair cells**.

1. When the head moves, the canal moves with it, but the fluid inside lags behind.
2. The moving fluid bends the hair cells.
3. The hair cells start impulses, which travel along the auditory nerve to the brain.
4. The **cerebellum** uses this information, together with information from the eyes and muscles, to adjust muscle contractions and keep the body balanced.

:::example{title="Example 2: dizzy after spinning"}
Rudo spins round quickly in a game and then stops suddenly. The fluid in her semicircular canals keeps moving for a few seconds, so her hair cells go on sending impulses that tell her brain she is still turning. Her eyes say she is still. The mixed messages make her feel dizzy until the fluid stops moving.
:::

## The Eustachian tube and air pressure

The eardrum vibrates freely only when the air pressure is the **same on both sides**. If the pressure outside changes quickly, the eardrum bulges, sounds become muffled and the ear may hurt.

:::example{title="Example 3: ears popping"}
A bus travels down the steep road from Nyanga towards Mutare. As it goes down, the air pressure outside rises. When a passenger swallows or yawns, the Eustachian tube opens, air moves into the middle ear and the pressure becomes equal on both sides of the eardrum: the ear "pops" and hearing returns to normal. The same thing happens when an aeroplane lands.
:::

The Eustachian tube has a disadvantage: when someone has a cold or sore throat, bacteria or viruses can travel up it into the middle ear and cause a painful **middle ear infection**, especially in young children.

## Looking after your ears

| Good practice | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Never push sticks, hairpins or other objects into the ear canal | They can scratch the canal or tear the eardrum; wax normally moves out on its own |
| Keep music and earphones at a moderate volume | Very loud sound damages the hair cells of the cochlea, and damaged hair cells do not grow back |
| Wear ear protectors near loud machinery (grinding mills, mines, factories) | They reduce the loudness of sound reaching the cochlea |
| Get ear pain, discharge or an ear infection treated at a clinic | An untreated infection can damage the eardrum or the ossicles |
| Never slap or hit someone on the ear | A sudden change in pressure can burst the eardrum |

:::example{title="Example 4: a grinding mill operator"}
Tatenda has worked at a grinding mill for many years without ear protection. He now struggles to follow conversations, especially when there is background noise. The loud noise has slowly damaged the **hair cells in his cochlea**. This damage is permanent, which is why ear protectors should be worn from the start.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the eardrum detects sound"}
The eardrum only vibrates. The **receptors** for sound are the hair cells in the **cochlea**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the cochlea and the semicircular canals"}
The cochlea is for **hearing**; the semicircular canals are for **balance**. Both are in the inner ear and both contain fluid and hair cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the ossicles in the wrong order"}
From the eardrum inwards: **hammer, anvil, stirrup**. The stirrup touches the oval window.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the Eustachian tube carries sound"}
It carries air, so that the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum stays equal. Sound reaches the eardrum through the ear canal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking ear wax is dirt that must be dug out"}
Wax traps dust and microorganisms and protects the ear canal. Digging it out can push it deeper or damage the eardrum.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The outer ear (pinna, ear canal, eardrum) collects sound; the middle ear has the ossicles and the Eustachian tube; the inner ear has the cochlea and the semicircular canals.
- Hearing: eardrum, hammer, anvil, stirrup, oval window, cochlear fluid, hair cells, auditory nerve, brain.
- The ossicles transmit and amplify vibrations; the hair cells of the cochlea are the receptors.
- Three semicircular canals at right angles detect head movement: moving fluid bends hair cells, and the brain (cerebellum) keeps the body balanced.
- The Eustachian tube equalises pressure on both sides of the eardrum.
- Protect hearing: avoid loud noise or wear ear protectors, keep objects out of the ear canal and get infections treated.
:::
