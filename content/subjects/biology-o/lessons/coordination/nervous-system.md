## Introduction

Rudo is cooking sadza in Masvingo. She grabs the handle of the pot, finds it is very hot, and her hand jerks away before she has even felt the pain. On the same afternoon, a goalkeeper at Barbourfields Stadium in Bulawayo dives the right way to save a penalty. Both actions depend on the **nervous system**, which detects changes and makes the body respond to them within a fraction of a second.

The body has two coordination systems: the **nervous system**, which uses electrical impulses, and the **endocrine system**, which uses hormones (the next lesson). Here you will learn how the nervous system is organised, the three types of neurone, how a reflex works and how impulses cross the gaps between neurones.

You should already know that cells can be specialised for a job and that respiration releases energy in cells.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Both boards: the organisation of the nervous system, neurones, the reflex arc and the idea of a synapse. **Cambridge Supplement**: the structure of a synapse and the events that pass an impulse across it. **ZIMSEC**: the functions of the cerebrum, cerebellum, pituitary gland, hypothalamus and medulla oblongata.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stimulus"}
A change in the surroundings or inside the body that can be detected by a receptor, for example heat, light, sound or pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Receptor"}
A cell, or group of cells, that detects a particular stimulus and starts nerve impulses. Receptors are found in sense organs such as the skin, eyes and ears.
:::

:::definition{term="Effector"}
A muscle or a gland that carries out a response. Muscles respond by contracting; glands respond by secreting.
:::

:::definition{term="Neurone"}
A nerve cell, specialised to carry electrical impulses quickly from one part of the body to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Reflex action"}
A rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that does not involve a conscious decision. Reflexes link stimuli to the responses of effectors (muscles and glands) and often protect the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Synapse"}
A junction between two neurones. The neurones do not quite touch: there is a tiny gap between them.
:::

## How the nervous system is organised

The nervous system has two parts:

- The **central nervous system (CNS)**: the **brain** and the **spinal cord**. It receives impulses, processes the information and sends impulses out.
- The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)**: all the **nerves** that connect the CNS to receptors and effectors in the rest of the body.

A **nerve** is a bundle of many neurones wrapped together, like the separate wires inside one cable. The nervous system **coordinates** the body (makes its parts work together) and **regulates** body functions such as breathing and heart rate.

:::keyfact
Information always flows in the same order: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector → response.
:::

## Three types of neurone

| Neurone | Carries impulses | Where its cell body is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensory neurone | From a **receptor** to the **CNS** | In a small swelling just outside the spinal cord, part of the way along the fibre |
| Relay neurone | From sensory neurones to motor neurones, **inside the CNS** | In the CNS; the whole neurone is short and branched |
| Motor neurone | From the **CNS** to an **effector** | In the CNS, at the start of a long axon |

A motor neurone, described part by part:

1. **Dendrites**: short branches on the cell body that pick up impulses from other neurones.
2. **Cell body**: contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm; it lies in the CNS.
3. **Axon**: a very long, thin fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body, sometimes more than a metre long.
4. **Myelin sheath**: a fatty layer around the axon that insulates it and speeds up the impulses.
5. **Motor end plates**: branched endings on a muscle that pass the impulse to the muscle fibres.

## Reflex actions and the reflex arc

The pathway of impulses in a reflex is called a **reflex arc**.

:::example{title="Example 1: stepping on a thorn"}
Chipo is walking barefoot to the river and steps on an acacia thorn.

1. **Stimulus**: the thorn presses into the skin of her foot.
2. **Receptor**: pain receptors in the skin start nerve impulses.
3. **Sensory neurone**: carries the impulses to the spinal cord.
4. **Relay neurone**: in the spinal cord, passes the impulses on to a motor neurone (across synapses).
5. **Motor neurone**: carries the impulses to muscles in her leg.
6. **Effector**: the leg muscles contract.
7. **Response**: her foot is lifted off the thorn.

At the same time, other relay neurones carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so Chipo feels the pain a moment **after** her foot has moved. The brain is told about the reflex but is not needed to carry it out.
:::

Other reflexes include blinking when dust flies towards the eye, coughing and sneezing, and the pupil getting smaller in bright light. You were born with these reflexes; you did not have to learn them.

:::example{title="Example 2: reflex or voluntary?"}
| Feature | Reflex action | Voluntary action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conscious decision? | No: automatic | Yes: decided in the brain (cerebrum) |
| Speed | Very fast: few neurones and synapses | Slower: more neurones and synapses |
| Response | The same every time | Can vary: you choose what to do |
| Learning | Inborn | Often learned, such as writing or riding a bicycle |
| Purpose | Often protective | Many purposes |

Pulling a hand off a hot pot is a reflex. Catching a ball, answering a question or kicking a penalty are voluntary actions, even when practice makes them quick.
:::

## Synapses

Where two neurones meet there is a **synapse**. The electrical impulse cannot jump the gap, so a **chemical** carries the message across.

### Structure and events at a synapse (Cambridge Supplement)

The end of the first neurone contains many small **vesicles** filled with **neurotransmitter** molecules. Between the two neurones is the **synaptic gap**. The membrane of the second neurone carries **receptor proteins** whose shape is complementary to the neurotransmitter.

:::example{title="Example 3: an impulse crossing a synapse"}
1. An impulse arrives at the end of the first neurone.
2. Vesicles move to the membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic gap.
3. The neurotransmitter **diffuses** across the gap.
4. It **binds** to the receptor proteins on the membrane of the second neurone.
5. This starts a **new impulse** in the second neurone.
:::

Vesicles of neurotransmitter are found only on one side of the gap and receptor proteins only on the other, so a synapse lets impulses pass in **one direction only**. This keeps the messages in a reflex arc travelling from receptor to effector.

## The main regions of the brain (ZIMSEC)

| Region | Where it is | Main functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cerebrum | The large, folded upper part, in two halves | Conscious thought, memory, learning, speech and voluntary actions; making sense of information from the eyes, ears and skin |
| Cerebellum | At the back, below the cerebrum | Balance, posture and coordination of smooth, precise movements |
| Medulla oblongata | In the brain stem, where the brain joins the spinal cord | Automatic control of breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure; reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing |
| Hypothalamus | A small region at the base of the brain | Controls body temperature, water balance (thirst) and hunger; controls the pituitary gland |
| Pituitary gland | A pea-sized gland just below the hypothalamus | Secretes hormones that control other endocrine glands, so it is often called the "master gland" |

:::example{title="Example 4: matching an injury to a brain region"}
After a hard tackle in a rugby match a player can think and speak clearly, but he walks unsteadily and cannot run in a straight line. Thinking and speech are cerebrum functions, so the cerebrum is working. Balance and coordinated movement are controlled by the **cerebellum**, so that is the region most likely to be affected.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up sensory and motor neurones"}
Sensory neurones carry impulses **to** the CNS from receptors; motor neurones carry impulses **from** the CNS to effectors. A memory aid: **m**otor neurones go to **m**uscles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the brain controls every reflex"}
Many reflexes, such as pulling the foot off a thorn, are coordinated by relay neurones in the **spinal cord**. The brain receives impulses too, which is why you feel the pain, but only after the response.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using nerve and neurone as if they mean the same"}
A neurone is one cell. A nerve is a bundle of many neurones.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the impulse jumps across the synapse"}
The electrical impulse stops at the end of the first neurone. A chemical, the neurotransmitter, diffuses across the gap and starts a new impulse.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "describe the pathway of a reflex", name every structure in order (receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector) and name the actual receptor and effector in the question's context, for example "pain receptors in the skin of the finger" and "muscles in the arm".
:::

:::exam-tip
Synapse answers earn marks for the key words: vesicles, neurotransmitter, diffuses, synaptic gap, receptor proteins, new impulse. To explain one-way transmission, give **both** halves: neurotransmitter is released only by the first neurone, and receptors are only on the second.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Compare" questions need both sides of each point: "a reflex is automatic, **whereas** a voluntary action needs a conscious decision".
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC brain questions: be specific. "Controls breathing rate and heart rate" earns the mark; "controls the body" does not.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The CNS is the brain and spinal cord; the PNS is the nerves. Neurones carry electrical impulses.
- Sensory neurones carry impulses from receptors to the CNS, relay neurones pass them on inside the CNS, and motor neurones carry them to effectors (muscles and glands).
- A reflex arc runs receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector; reflexes are automatic, fast and often protective.
- A synapse is a junction between neurones. Supplement: neurotransmitter released from vesicles diffuses across the gap and binds to receptor proteins, so impulses pass one way only.
- ZIMSEC: cerebrum (thought, voluntary actions), cerebellum (balance, coordination), medulla oblongata (breathing and heart rate), hypothalamus (temperature, water balance), pituitary gland (the "master gland").
:::
