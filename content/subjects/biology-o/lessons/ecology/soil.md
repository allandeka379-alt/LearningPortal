## Introduction

A maize crop in a communal area and a patch of msasa woodland a few kilometres away may get exactly the same rain, yet one field yields well and another poorly. Much of the difference lies underfoot. Many communal farming areas are on light, sandy soils formed from granite, which drain fast and lose their nutrients easily, while other farms sit on heavy clays that stay waterlogged after a storm.

Soil is not just "dirt". It is a living part of an **ecosystem**, full of organisms that recycle dead material into nutrients for plants. In this lesson you will learn what an ecosystem is, what soil contains, what soil organisms do, and how to compare sandy, clay and loam soils by experiment.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ecosystem"}
A self-contained unit made up of a community of interdependent organisms and the physical environment they interact with, for example a lake, a vlei or an area of msasa woodland.
:::

:::definition{term="Physical and biological components"}
The **physical** (abiotic, non-living) components are things such as air, water, soil, light and temperature. The **biological** (biotic) components are the living organisms: producers, consumers and decomposers.
:::

:::definition{term="Humus"}
Dark, partly decomposed organic matter in soil, formed from dead plants, animals and dung.
:::

:::definition{term="Leaching"}
The washing of dissolved mineral ions down through the soil, out of reach of plant roots, by water draining through it.
:::

## Ecosystems

Lake Kariba is an ecosystem: algae and water plants (producers) trap light energy, kapenta and other fish (consumers) feed on them directly or through other animals, and bacteria and fungi (decomposers) break down the dead. All of them depend on the physical components: the water, its temperature and dissolved oxygen, the light reaching it and the mud on the bottom.

The organisms are **interdependent**. In msasa woodland, the trees provide food and shelter for insects, birds and mammals; termites and fungi break down fallen wood and leaves; the nutrients released are taken up again by the trees. A maize field is an ecosystem too, but an artificial one, with far fewer species.

## What soil is made of

Soil has five parts:

- **mineral particles** from weathered rock: sand, silt and clay;
- **humus**;
- **water**, with mineral ions dissolved in it (the soil solution);
- **air**, in the spaces between particles, supplying oxygen for the respiration of roots and soil organisms;
- **living organisms**.

In a good soil, roughly half the volume is solid particles and humus, and the rest is pore space shared between water and air.

## The biological components of soil

| Component | Role in the soil |
| --- | --- |
| Litter (dead leaves, twigs, dung) | Food for decomposers; covers the soil, protecting it from heavy rain and drying out; decays to form humus |
| Earthworms | Burrows let in air and let water drain; they pull leaves underground, mix the soil layers and break litter into small pieces; their mucus helps bind particles into crumbs |
| Termites | Break down dead wood, grass and litter; tunnels aerate the soil and let rain soak in; some damage crops and timber |
| Nematodes (tiny roundworms) | Many feed on bacteria, fungi and decaying matter, releasing nutrients; some are parasites of roots, such as the root-knot nematodes of tobacco and tomatoes |
| Fungi and bacteria | **Decomposers**: they secrete enzymes onto dead matter and absorb the products, releasing carbon dioxide and mineral ions such as nitrate; some bacteria fix nitrogen, including those in the root nodules of groundnuts and soya beans |
| Humus | Holds water like a sponge; binds particles into crumbs, which improves aeration and drainage; releases mineral ions slowly as it decays; feeds soil organisms |

Termite mounds are a familiar sight on Zimbabwean farms. Mound soil is often richer in clay and nutrients than the soil around it, and some farmers spread it on their fields.

## Comparing sandy, clay and loam soils

Soil particles are grouped by size. **Sand** particles are the largest, up to about 2 mm across; **clay** particles are the smallest, less than 0.002 mm; silt lies in between. **Loam** is a mixture of sand, silt and clay with plenty of humus.

| Property | Sandy soil | Clay soil | Loam |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spaces between particles | Large | Very small | Medium, with crumbs |
| Aeration | Good | Poor, especially when wet | Good |
| Drainage | Fast | Slow; waterlogs easily | Moderate |
| Water-holding capacity | Low; dries out quickly | High | Good |
| Leaching | Rapid, so often infertile | Slow; clay particles hold mineral ions | Moderate |
| Digging | Light and easy | Heavy; sticky when wet, hard and cracked when dry | Easy |

Particle size explains most of the table. Large particles leave large pores, so water drains through quickly and air fills the spaces. Tiny clay particles leave tiny pores that hold water tightly and let it through slowly. Loam combines good drainage and aeration with good water and mineral retention, so it suits most crops.

## Experiments on soil

### Particle sizes: sedimentation

Shake a soil sample with water in a tall jar or measuring cylinder and leave it to settle. The largest particles settle first, so the layers from the bottom up are gravel and coarse sand, fine sand, silt and then clay, with the finest clay making the water cloudy for a long time. Humus floats on the surface.

### Drainage and water-holding capacity

Put equal masses of dry sandy, loam and clay soil into identical funnels plugged with cotton wool, each over a measuring cylinder. Pour $100\ \text{cm}^3$ of water onto each and record the volume collected after 10 minutes.

:::example{title="Example 1: interpreting drainage results"}
| Soil | Water collected after 10 min / cm³ | Water retained / cm³ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sandy | 78 | $100 - 78 = 22$ |
| Loam | 52 | $100 - 52 = 48$ |
| Clay | 21 | $100 - 21 = 79$ |

Water drained fastest through the sandy soil, so it holds the least water: crops on it suffer quickly in a dry spell. The clay soil held the most water but drained so slowly that it would become waterlogged in heavy rain. The loam was between the two.
:::

### Water and humus content

Weigh a sample of fresh soil, heat it in an oven at about $105^\circ\text{C}$ and reweigh it, repeating until the mass stops changing (**constant mass**). The loss is water. Then heat the dry soil strongly in a crucible until the organic matter has burned away, cool it and weigh it again: the further loss is humus. The first heating must be gentle, or some humus would burn and be counted as water.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: water and humus content of a garden soil]
A fresh soil sample has a mass of $62.5\ \text{g}$. After drying to constant mass it is $50.0\ \text{g}$. After strong heating it is $47.0\ \text{g}$.

1. Water $= 62.5 - 50.0 = 12.5\ \text{g}$, so water content $= \dfrac{12.5}{62.5} \times 100 = 20\%$ of the fresh mass.
2. Humus $= 50.0 - 47.0 = 3.0\ \text{g}$, so humus content $= \dfrac{3.0}{50.0} \times 100 = 6\%$ of the dry mass.
:::

### Air content, leaching and pH

- **Air**: press an empty tin of known volume upside down into the soil to collect a sample, then tip the soil into a measuring cylinder holding a known volume of water and stir out the bubbles. Air volume $=$ volume of water $+$ volume of soil $-$ final reading. Moist sandy soil usually contains more air than moist clay, because water drains out of its large pores and air takes its place.
- **Leaching**: add the same mass of soluble fertiliser to equal masses of each soil in funnels, pour the same volume of water through, and evaporate the water collected to dryness. More residue means more mineral salts were washed out: usually most from sand and least from clay. The same soils without fertiliser make a suitable control.
- **pH**: shake a little soil with distilled water, let it settle, add universal indicator to the clear liquid and compare the colour with the chart. Many sandy soils formed from granite are acidic; farmers raise the pH by adding **lime**.

### Improving soils

Sandy soils are improved by adding compost, manure and crop residues, which add humus to hold water and mineral ions, and by mulching. Conservation farming, such as planting basins with a mulch cover, uses the same ideas. Clay soils are improved by adding humus and lime, which help the particles form crumbs, and by draining waterlogged land.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Small particles mean fast drainage"}
It is the reverse. Small clay particles leave **tiny pores**, so water drains slowly. Large sand particles leave large pores and drain fast.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heating soil strongly to find its water content"}
Strong heating burns the humus too. Dry soil at about $105^\circ\text{C}$ to **constant mass** for water; heat strongly only to find the humus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Humus is a mineral"}
Humus is **organic**: decayed remains of dead plants, animals and dung. Sand, silt and clay are the mineral part.
:::

:::mistake{title="Earthworms and termites only harm crops"}
Their tunnels **aerate** the soil and help water soak in, and they break down dead material. Only some termites and nematodes damage crops.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Explain soil properties as a chain: **particle size → size of pores → drainage, aeration and water-holding capacity → effect on plants**.
:::

:::exam-tip
In soil experiments, name the controlled variables: same mass of dry soil, same volume of water, identical funnels, same time.
:::

:::exam-tip
Check what a percentage is of: water content is usually given as a percentage of the **fresh** mass, humus as a percentage of the **dry** mass.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An ecosystem is a community of interdependent organisms together with its physical environment (air, water, soil, light, temperature).
- Soil contains mineral particles, humus, water, air and living organisms.
- Earthworms and termites aerate and mix soil; bacteria and fungi decompose dead matter and recycle mineral ions; humus holds water and binds soil into crumbs.
- Sandy soil drains fast, holds little water and is easily leached; clay holds water and ions but drains poorly; loam combines the best of both.
- Soils are compared by sedimentation, drainage through funnels, heating to find water and humus, displacing air with water, leaching and testing pH with universal indicator.
:::
