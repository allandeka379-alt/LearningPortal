## Introduction

Put a pinch of yeast into warm sugar solution and, for a while, almost nothing seems to happen. Then the mixture froths as the yeast cells multiply rapidly, until after a few days the bubbling slows and stops. In Hwange National Park, the number of elephants has grown over many decades, and in a dry year the animals crowd around the few waterholes, stripping the trees nearby.

Both are examples of **population growth** and its limits. In this lesson you will learn the words used to describe groups of organisms, the factors that control population size, and how to read a population growth curve. You should already know that yeast and bacteria reproduce rapidly, and the meaning of a food chain.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Population"}
A group of organisms of one species, living in the same area, at the same time. For example, all the impala in Hwange National Park this year.
:::

:::definition{term="Community"}
All of the populations of different species in an ecosystem. For example, all the plants, animals and microorganisms in a vlei.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecosystem"}
A unit containing the community of organisms and their environment, interacting together. For example, Lake Kariba, a vlei or a rotting log.
:::

:::definition{term="Limiting factor"}
A factor that restricts the growth of a population when it is in short supply, such as food, water or space.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity (ZIMSEC)"}
The largest population of a species that an environment can support over a long period, given the food, water, shelter and other resources available.
:::

## What affects the rate of population growth?

A population grows when more individuals are born than die. The main factors that affect this are:

- **Food supply**: more food allows more young to be produced and to survive.
- **Competition**: individuals compete for food, water, space, shelter and mates, both within their own species and with other species.
- **Predation**: predators kill prey, so a large predator population keeps prey numbers down.
- **Disease**: infections spread quickly in crowded populations and can kill many individuals.

## The sigmoid growth curve

When a few organisms are put into a new environment with plenty of resources, such as yeast in sugar solution or bacteria in a flask of nutrient broth, the population grows in a typical way. Plotted against time, it makes an S-shaped (**sigmoid**) curve with four phases.

| Phase | What the graph shows | Explanation (Supplement) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lag phase | Numbers increase slowly | Few individuals are present to reproduce; they are adjusting to the new conditions, for example making the enzymes they need, or growing to the size or age at which they can breed |
| Exponential (log) phase | Numbers increase rapidly, doubling at regular intervals | Plenty of food and space, little competition and few toxic wastes: the birth rate is much greater than the death rate |
| Stationary phase | Numbers stay roughly constant | Limiting factors, such as a shortage of food, competition, a build-up of toxic waste, predation or disease, slow reproduction and increase deaths: the birth rate equals the death rate |
| Death phase | Numbers fall | Food runs out or toxic waste (such as ethanol made by yeast) reaches harmful levels: the death rate is greater than the birth rate |

In a closed container nothing new is added, so a death phase follows. In natural ecosystems, populations usually rise and fall around the stationary level instead, as the food supply changes with the seasons.

![Yeast growth curve from Example 2: numbers rise slowly from 2 to 3 thousand per cubic millimetre in the lag phase to 2 hours, then steeply through 8, 24 and 52 thousand in the exponential phase, and level off near 74 thousand in the stationary phase after about 10 hours.](media:diagrams/yeast-growth-curve.png "Figure: the sigmoid growth curve of yeast in sugar solution")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how fast can bacteria multiply?]
A culture starts with 40 bacteria, and each bacterium divides into two every 30 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 3 hours, if none die?
1. Number of divisions in 3 hours $= \dfrac{180\ \text{min}}{30\ \text{min}} = 6$.
2. The population doubles each time: $40 \times 2^6 = 40 \times 64 = 2560$ bacteria.
3. This is exponential growth. It cannot continue for long, because food runs out and wastes build up.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: reading a yeast growth curve]
The number of yeast cells (thousands per mm³) in a flask of sugar solution was counted every 2 hours: 0 h, 2; 2 h, 3; 4 h, 8; 6 h, 24; 8 h, 52; 10 h, 70; 12 h, 74.
1. Lag phase: 0 to about 2 h, where numbers rise slowly (2 to 3).
2. Exponential phase: about 2 to 8 h, where numbers rise steeply.
3. Stationary phase begins after about 10 h, as numbers level off near 74.
4. Mean rate of increase from 4 h to 8 h $= \dfrac{52 - 8}{8 - 4} = 11$ thousand cells per mm³ per hour.

:::

## Carrying capacity and limiting factors (ZIMSEC)

The level at which a population stops growing is the **carrying capacity** of its environment. It is set by the limiting factors that are in shortest supply:

- **water**: for land animals in the dry season;
- **food**: grazing and browse for herbivores, prey for predators;
- **oxygen**: for fish and other aquatic organisms;
- **space**: for territories, nesting and feeding;
- **shelter**: from predators and the weather.

Carrying capacity is not fixed. After a season of good rains, the grassland can support more grazers; in a drought it supports far fewer.

:::example{title="Example 3: elephants in Hwange"}
Hwange has very little permanent surface water, so in the dry season most of its water comes from waterholes filled by pumping water from boreholes. Elephants crowd around these waterholes. When the population is larger than the area around the water can support, the animals strip bark and branches, push over trees and trample the ground bare. Other species lose food and shelter, the soil erodes, and in drought years many animals die of hunger. This shows what happens when a population exceeds the carrying capacity of its environment: degradation of the habitat, overgrazing, disease and, eventually, deaths.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing population and community"}
A **population** is one species; a **community** is all the species in an area. An **ecosystem** includes the community **and** its physical environment.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying nothing is born in the stationary phase"}
In the stationary phase individuals are still being born and dying, but the **birth rate equals the death rate**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the exponential phase as steady"}
In the exponential phase the population **doubles** at regular intervals, so the curve gets steeper and steeper. It is not a straight line.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking carrying capacity never changes"}
Carrying capacity changes when resources change, for example between wet and dry years.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **describe** a curve, quote values with units and times ("the population rose from 8 to 52 thousand cells per mm³ between 4 and 8 hours"). When asked to **explain**, use births, deaths and named limiting factors.
:::

:::exam-tip
Name the phases exactly: lag, exponential (log), stationary and death.
:::

:::exam-tip
To calculate a rate from a graph or table, divide the change in numbers by the change in time, and give units such as "cells per hour".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A population is one species in one area at one time; a community is all the populations in an ecosystem; an ecosystem is the community and its environment interacting.
- Population growth depends on food supply, competition, predation and disease.
- A sigmoid curve has lag, exponential (log), stationary and death phases.
- Supplement: the phases are explained by the balance of births and deaths and by limiting factors such as food shortage and toxic waste.
- ZIMSEC: carrying capacity is the largest population an environment can support, set by limiting factors (water, food, oxygen, space, shelter); exceeding it causes degradation, overgrazing, disease and deaths.
:::
