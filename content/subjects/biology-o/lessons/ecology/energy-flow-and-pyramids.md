## Introduction

A hectare of grassland in Hwange supports many zebra and impala, but only a handful of lions. A hectare of maize near Chegutu can feed far more people as maize meal than it could as beef from cattle fed on that maize. Both facts have the same cause: **energy is lost at every step of a food chain**.

In this lesson you will follow energy through an ecosystem, draw and read ecological pyramids, and (Supplement) calculate how efficiently energy passes from one trophic level to the next. You should already know how to read food chains, and that photosynthesis and respiration involve energy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pyramid of numbers"}
A diagram in which the width of each bar shows the number of organisms at each trophic level.
:::

:::definition{term="Pyramid of biomass"}
A diagram in which the width of each bar shows the total dry mass of the organisms at each trophic level, at one time, usually per unit area.
:::

:::definition{term="Pyramid of energy (Supplement)"}
A diagram in which the width of each bar shows the energy passing through each trophic level, per unit area per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Efficiency of energy transfer (Supplement)"}
The percentage of the energy at one trophic level that is passed on to the next.
:::

:::formula{name="Efficiency of energy transfer (Supplement)"}
$$
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{energy transferred to the next trophic level}}{\text{energy at the previous trophic level}} \times 100\%
$$
:::

## How energy flows through an ecosystem

1. The **Sun** is the principal source of energy for biological systems.
2. **Producers** absorb light energy and convert it into **chemical energy** in glucose and other organic compounds (photosynthesis). Only a small part of the light reaching a field ends up stored in the plants.
3. The chemical energy passes from one organism to the next by **feeding**.
4. At every trophic level, much of the energy is **not** passed on:
   - organisms use energy in **respiration** for movement, growth and keeping warm, and this energy is eventually **transferred to the surroundings as heat**;
   - energy remains in **faeces** (undigested food) and in **urine**;
   - some parts are **not eaten**, such as roots, bark, bones and hooves.
5. The energy in dead organisms, faeces and uneaten parts passes to **decomposers**, which also release it as heat.
6. In the end, **all** the energy is transferred to the environment as heat.

:::keyfact{title="Energy flows; nutrients cycle"}
Chemical elements such as carbon and nitrogen are recycled again and again. Energy is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so ecosystems need a constant input of energy from the Sun.
:::

## Pyramids of numbers and biomass

A **pyramid of numbers** is usually wide at the bottom and narrow at the top: many grass plants feed fewer impala, which feed even fewer lions. But it can have an odd shape when organisms differ greatly in size. One large tree can support thousands of caterpillars, so the producer bar is narrower than the bar above it. A single cow can carry hundreds of ticks.

A **pyramid of biomass** uses the total **dry mass** of the organisms at each level, so it takes their size into account. One tree has a far greater mass than all its caterpillars, so the pyramid of biomass is almost always the expected shape: largest at the base. This is its main advantage over a pyramid of numbers. Its disadvantages are that organisms must be killed and dried to find their dry mass, and that it shows the mass present at just one time.

## Pyramids of energy and efficiency (Supplement)

A **pyramid of energy** shows the energy passing through each level per square metre per year. Because energy is lost at every transfer, each bar is **always** narrower than the one below it: a pyramid of energy is never inverted.

![Pyramid of energy for the grassland example, drawn to scale: grass 24 000 kJ per square metre per year, impala 1800 and lion 216; only 7.5% of the energy passes from grass to impala and 12% from impala to lion, so each bar is narrower than the one below.](media:diagrams/energy-pyramid.png "Figure: a pyramid of energy with bars drawn to scale (kJ per square metre per year)")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: efficiency in a grassland food chain]
In a grassland, the producers (grass) contain 24 000 kJ of energy per m² per year. The impala receive 1800 kJ per m² per year from the grass, and the lions receive 216 kJ per m² per year from the impala. Calculate the efficiency of each transfer.
1. Grass → impala: $\dfrac{1800}{24\,000} \times 100 = 7.5\%$.
2. Impala → lion: $\dfrac{216}{1800} \times 100 = 12\%$.
3. So 92.5% of the energy in the grass and 88% of the energy in the impala did not reach the next level.
4. Transfers are often quoted as "about 10%", but this is only a rough rule: real values vary, as here.

:::

### Why food chains are short

Because most of the energy is lost at each transfer, the energy remaining after four or five trophic levels is too little to support another population of animals. This is why food chains usually have fewer than five trophic levels, and why top carnivores such as lions are few and need large areas.

### Why eating crops feeds more people

Every extra trophic level means more energy lost. When people eat maize, they are primary consumers. When they eat beef from cattle fed on maize, there is one more trophic level, and most of the maize's energy has already been used by the cattle.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: maize meal or beef?]
A field produces maize grain containing 75 000 MJ of energy. Assume a person needs about 10 MJ of energy a day, and that about 10% of the energy in the maize would end up in beef if the maize were fed to cattle.
1. Eaten directly: $75\,000 \div 10 = 7500$ days of food for one person.
2. Fed to cattle first: energy in beef $= 0.10 \times 75\,000 = 7500\ \text{MJ}$.
3. As beef: $7500 \div 10 = 750$ days of food for one person.
4. Eating the crop directly feeds about **ten times** as many people, because one fewer trophic level means less energy is lost.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying energy is destroyed or used up"}
Energy is never destroyed. It is **transferred** to the surroundings, mostly as heat, where living things can no longer use it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying energy is recycled"}
Nutrients such as carbon are recycled; energy flows through an ecosystem in one direction and must be constantly supplied by the Sun.
:::

:::mistake{title="Turning the efficiency calculation upside down"}
Divide the energy at the **higher** level by the energy at the **lower** level. An efficiency can never be more than 100%.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking every pyramid has a pyramid shape"}
A pyramid of numbers can have a narrow base (one tree, many caterpillars). A pyramid of energy, however, is always the expected shape.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked how energy is lost, name specific processes: **respiration** (heat), **movement**, **egestion** (faeces), **excretion** (urine), and **parts not eaten**. "Energy is lost as waste" is too vague.
:::

:::exam-tip
Draw pyramids with the producer at the bottom, label each bar with the organism or trophic level, and make bar widths proportional to the values if a scale is asked for.
:::

:::exam-tip
For efficiency questions, show the fraction, multiply by 100 and give the % sign. Read the units carefully (kJ per m² per year).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The Sun is the principal source of energy; producers convert light energy into chemical energy, which passes along food chains by feeding and finally leaves as heat.
- At each trophic level, energy is lost in respiration (as heat), in faeces and urine, and in parts not eaten.
- A pyramid of numbers can be inverted when organisms differ greatly in size; a pyramid of biomass takes size into account and is almost always upright.
- Supplement: a pyramid of energy is never inverted; efficiency = energy passed on ÷ energy at the previous level × 100, often roughly 10%.
- Supplement: because so much energy is lost, food chains are short, and eating crops feeds more people than eating livestock fed on crops.
:::
