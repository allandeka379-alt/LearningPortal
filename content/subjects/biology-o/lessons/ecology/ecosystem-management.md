## Introduction

Drive east from Harare towards Mutare and the land changes. Patches of msasa woodland glow red with new leaves in September. Then come long fields of maize and tobacco, and in the Eastern Highlands, straight rows of pine and eucalyptus planted for timber. Some rivers along the way run brown with silt, and some hillsides are cut by gullies.

Some of these ecosystems formed naturally; others were made by people. This lesson compares them, explains why ecosystems with few species have problems, and looks at how human activities damage the land and how the damage can be prevented or repaired. You should already know what an ecosystem and a food web are, and the roles of decomposers and nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural ecosystem"}
An ecosystem that has developed without being planted or managed by people, such as msasa woodland, mopane woodland or a vlei.
:::

:::definition{term="Artificial ecosystem"}
An ecosystem created and maintained by people, such as a maize field, a pine or eucalyptus plantation, an orchard or a fish pond.
:::

:::definition{term="Species diversity"}
The number of different species in an ecosystem (and how evenly the individuals are shared among them).
:::

:::definition{term="Ground cover"}
Plants, or a layer of dead plant material (mulch), covering the surface of the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil erosion"}
The removal of topsoil by water or wind, usually where the soil has been left bare.
:::

## Natural and artificial ecosystems compared

| Feature | Natural ecosystem (msasa woodland) | Artificial ecosystem (maize field) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Species diversity | High: many kinds of trees, grasses, insects, birds, mammals and soil organisms | Low: mostly one crop, kept free of other plants |
| Food webs | Complex, with many links | Simple, with few links |
| Stability | Stable; a pest is held back by its many natural enemies | Unstable; a pest can multiply quickly on so much of one food |
| Nutrient cycling | Dead leaves and animals decompose where they fall, returning mineral ions | The crop is harvested and removed, taking its mineral ions with it |
| Human inputs | None needed | Ploughing, fertiliser, pesticides, weeding, often irrigation |

### Problems of low diversity

- **Falling soil fertility.** The same crop removes the same mineral ions every season, and harvesting takes them away instead of letting them return to the soil. Without legumes, little nitrogen is fixed. Fertiliser must be bought.
- **Pest and disease outbreaks.** Large areas of one host plant let pests and diseases spread quickly; a plantation of one pine species can lose many trees to one insect pest. There are few natural predators, so farmers depend on pesticides.
- **Fewer habitats.** Wildlife that needed the woodland loses food and shelter, so biodiversity falls.
- **Bare soil.** After harvest or ploughing the soil is unprotected and easily eroded.

## The advantages of ground cover

A layer of plants or mulch over the soil:

- **holds the topsoil**: roots bind the soil, and leaves and mulch break the force of raindrops and slow the wind, so less soil is washed or blown away;
- **retains water**: rain soaks in slowly instead of running off the surface;
- **reduces evaporation**: the soil surface is shaded and kept cool;
- **adds organic matter**: decaying plant material forms humus, improving soil structure and fertility.

Conservation agriculture, such as the Pfumvudza method promoted in Zimbabwe since 2020, uses these ideas: planting holes instead of full ploughing, and a mulch of crop residues kept on the soil surface.

## Harmful activities and corrective measures

| Activity | Harmful effects | Corrective measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overgrazing (too many livestock on the land) | Grass cover lost, bare soil, erosion, gullies, weak and hungry animals | Reduce stock numbers (destocking); rotational grazing in paddocks so grass can recover |
| Ploughing up and down slopes; cultivating stream banks | Water runs down the furrows, carrying soil; banks collapse and rivers silt up | Contour ploughing and contour ridges; leave a strip of natural vegetation along rivers |
| Monoculture and overuse of chemicals | Falling fertility, pests, pesticide resistance, fertiliser runoff | Crop rotation with legumes, intercropping, correct amounts of fertiliser |
| Veld fires (to clear land or hunt) | Ground cover, young trees and animals destroyed; soil left bare; carbon dioxide released | Fireguards (firebreaks), controlled early burning, education |
| Gold panning in rivers | Riverbanks dug away, rivers and dams silted, fish habitats destroyed, mercury pollution | Regulation of mining, refilling pits, replanting banks, safer methods without mercury |
| Open-pit and other mining | Land stripped bare, pits left open, dumps of waste rock | Rehabilitation: refill pits, replace topsoil, replant |
| Industry discharging untreated effluent | Rivers and lakes polluted, fish killed | Treat effluent before release; enforce laws (the Environmental Management Agency) |
| Cutting trees for firewood and settlement | Deforestation, erosion, loss of habitats | Woodlots, tree planting, efficient stoves, other energy sources |

:::example{title="Example 1: a gully on communal land"}
A cattle path runs straight down a hillside near Chivi. The grass along it has been grazed and trampled away, and each rainy season water rushes down the bare path, deepening it into a gully. Corrective measures: fence the area to keep animals out while grass regrows; build small stone walls (check dams) across the gully to slow the water and trap soil; plant grass and trees on the sides; dig contour ridges above it to lead water away safely; and reduce or rotate the grazing.
:::

## Carrying capacity of grazing land

Grazing land can support only a certain number of animals for a long period: its **carrying capacity**. Keeping more animals than this is **overstocking**, and the result is overgrazing and degradation of the land.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: is the grazing land overstocked?]
A communal grazing area is 600 ha. Advisers estimate that, in an average year, each cow needs 5 ha of this grazing. The village has 180 cattle.
1. Carrying capacity $= \dfrac{600\ \text{ha}}{5\ \text{ha per cow}} = 120$ cattle.
2. Excess cattle $= 180 - 120 = 60$.
3. Percentage over the carrying capacity $= \dfrac{60}{120} \times 100 = 50\%$.
4. The land is overstocked: expect overgrazing, bare soil and erosion. In a drought the carrying capacity falls further, so the problem gets worse. Destocking, rotational grazing or growing fodder would help.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking artificial ecosystems have no problems because people manage them"}
They need constant inputs (fertiliser, pesticides, labour), and without them yields fall. Low diversity makes them unstable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ground cover 'stops rain'"}
Ground cover does not stop rain; it slows the water and lets it **soak in**, protecting the soil and keeping water in it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague corrective measures"}
"Stop pollution" or "look after the land" earn nothing. Name a specific measure for each problem: contour ridges for erosion on slopes, rotational grazing for overgrazing, refilling pits for mined land.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Compare" questions need both sides of each point: "natural woodland has many species, but a maize field has few".
:::

:::exam-tip
For "suggest corrective measures", match each measure to a named harmful activity and say how it helps.
:::

:::exam-tip
In carrying-capacity calculations, show the division (area ÷ area needed per animal) and compare the answer with the actual number of animals.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Natural ecosystems (msasa or mopane woodland, vleis) have high species diversity and complex, stable food webs; artificial ecosystems (maize fields, plantations, fish ponds) have low diversity and need human inputs.
- Low diversity leads to falling soil fertility, pest and disease outbreaks, loss of habitats and bare, erodible soil.
- Ground cover holds the topsoil, retains water, reduces evaporation and adds organic matter.
- Harmful activities include overgrazing, poor ploughing, veld fires, gold panning, mining, untreated effluent and deforestation; corrective measures include rotational grazing, contour ridges, fireguards, rehabilitation and tree planting.
- Land stocked beyond its carrying capacity becomes overgrazed and degraded.
:::
