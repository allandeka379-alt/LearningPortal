## Introduction

Every maize plant needs nitrogen to make its proteins, and a crop short of nitrogen grows slowly, with pale yellow leaves. That is why many Zimbabwean farmers apply ammonium nitrate fertiliser as a top dressing a few weeks after their maize comes up. Yet the air around the plants is about 78% nitrogen gas. Plants cannot use this gas directly: it must first be changed into compounds such as nitrate ions.

Bacteria, lightning and decomposers keep nitrogen moving between the air, the soil and living things. This is the **nitrogen cycle**. For Cambridge candidates it is **Supplement** content. You should already know that proteins are made of amino acids, that plants absorb mineral ions such as nitrate through their roots, and that the liver makes urea.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nitrogen fixation"}
The conversion of nitrogen gas from the air into nitrogen compounds, such as ammonium ions, that living things can use. It is done by nitrogen-fixing bacteria and by lightning.
:::

:::definition{term="Nitrification"}
The conversion of ammonium ions into nitrate ions by nitrifying bacteria in the soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Denitrification"}
The conversion of nitrate ions back into nitrogen gas by denitrifying bacteria, especially in waterlogged soils.
:::

:::definition{term="Deamination"}
The removal of the nitrogen-containing part of amino acids that are not needed, forming urea; in mammals it takes place in the liver.
:::

:::definition{term="Root nodules"}
Swellings on the roots of legumes, such as groundnuts and soya beans, that contain nitrogen-fixing bacteria.
:::

## Why living things need nitrogen

Nitrogen is part of every **amino acid**, and so of every **protein**, including enzymes, and it is also found in DNA. Plants absorb **nitrate ions** (and some ammonium ions) from the soil, by active transport through their root hair cells, and use them to make amino acids. Animals cannot use nitrate: they obtain nitrogen by **eating** proteins in plants or other animals.

## The processes of the nitrogen cycle

| Step | Process | What happens | Carried out by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Absorption | Nitrate ions in the soil are absorbed and used to make amino acids and proteins | Plant roots |
| 2 | Feeding and digestion | Proteins in food are digested to amino acids, absorbed and built into new proteins | Animals |
| 3 | Deamination | Excess amino acids are broken down; the nitrogen leaves the body in urea, in urine | The liver of mammals |
| 4 | Decomposition | Proteins in dead organisms, and urea and faeces, are broken down to ammonium ions | Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) |
| 5 | Nitrification | Ammonium ions are converted to nitrite and then to nitrate ions; this needs oxygen | Nitrifying bacteria |
| 6 | Nitrogen fixation | Nitrogen gas is converted into ammonium compounds | Nitrogen-fixing bacteria, free-living in soil or in root nodules |
| 7 | Fixation by lightning | The energy of lightning makes nitrogen and oxygen combine; the oxides dissolve in rain and reach the soil as nitrates | Lightning (not living) |
| 8 | Denitrification | Nitrate ions are converted to nitrogen gas, which returns to the air | Denitrifying bacteria, in soils short of oxygen |

:::keyfact{title="Four groups of microorganisms"}
**Decomposers** turn protein into ammonium. **Nitrifying bacteria** turn ammonium into nitrate. **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria** turn nitrogen gas into ammonium compounds. **Denitrifying bacteria** turn nitrate into nitrogen gas.

:::

![Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen-fixing bacteria and lightning turn nitrogen gas into usable compounds, decomposers turn the proteins and urea of dead organisms into ammonium ions, nitrifying bacteria turn ammonium into nitrate, plants absorb nitrate to make protein, animals eat the plants, and denitrifying bacteria return nitrate to nitrogen gas.](media:diagrams/nitrogen-cycle.png "Figure: the nitrogen cycle with the four groups of bacteria and decomposers")

:::example{title="Example 1: following one nitrogen atom"}
1. A nitrogen molecule in the air enters the soil near the roots of a groundnut plant.
2. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria in a root nodule convert it into an ammonium compound, which the plant uses to make amino acids and then proteins in its seeds.
3. A family eats the groundnuts. The proteins are digested to amino acids and absorbed into the blood.
4. Amino acids that are not needed are deaminated in the liver, and the nitrogen leaves the body in urea, in urine.
5. In the soil, decomposers convert the urea to ammonium ions, and nitrifying bacteria convert these to nitrate ions.
6. A maize plant absorbs the nitrate through its root hairs and builds it into protein. Or, if the soil becomes waterlogged, denitrifying bacteria may return the nitrogen to the air as nitrogen gas.
:::

## Legumes and their nodules

**Legumes** are plants of the pea and bean family, including groundnuts, soya beans, cowpeas, sugar beans and round nuts (nyimo). Their roots have **nodules** containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria. The bacteria supply the plant with nitrogen compounds; the plant supplies the bacteria with sugars made by photosynthesis. Because of this partnership, legumes grow well in soils poor in nitrate, and when their roots and leftover stems decay, the nitrogen compounds they contain enrich the soil.

## Using the nitrogen cycle on the farm

:::example{title="Example 2: managing soil nitrogen"}
- **Crop rotation**: growing groundnuts or soya beans one season and maize the next. The maize benefits from the nitrogen compounds left by the legume's nodules and residues, so less fertiliser is needed.
- **Ploughing and drainage**: letting air into the soil helps nitrifying bacteria, which need oxygen, and reduces denitrification, which is fastest in waterlogged soil. A flooded field can lose much of its nitrate.
- **Manure and compost**: decomposers break down the proteins in them to ammonium, which nitrifying bacteria convert to nitrate.
- **Fertilisers** such as ammonium nitrate supply ammonium and nitrate ions directly, but too much can wash into rivers and cause pollution.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying plants absorb nitrogen gas"}
Plants cannot use nitrogen gas. They absorb **nitrate ions** (and some ammonium ions) from the soil. Only nitrogen-fixing bacteria can use nitrogen gas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up nitrogen fixation and nitrification"}
**Fixation** takes nitrogen **gas** out of the air. **Nitrification** changes **ammonium** into **nitrate** in the soil. Different bacteria do each job.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying decomposers make nitrate"}
Decomposers produce **ammonium** ions. Nitrifying bacteria then convert ammonium to nitrate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking all soil bacteria help crops"}
Denitrifying bacteria remove nitrate from the soil, returning nitrogen to the air. They are most active where there is little oxygen, as in waterlogged soils.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "describe the roles of microorganisms" questions, give each group and its conversion: decomposers (protein → ammonium), nitrifying (ammonium → nitrate), nitrogen-fixing (nitrogen gas → ammonium compounds), denitrifying (nitrate → nitrogen gas).
:::

:::exam-tip
Use "ions" correctly: ammonium ions and nitrate ions. Plants absorb nitrate **ions**, not "nitrogen".
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain why crop rotation with legumes helps, link nodules → nitrogen-fixing bacteria → nitrogen compounds left in the soil → more nitrate for the next crop → more protein and growth.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nitrogen is needed for amino acids and proteins; plants absorb nitrate ions, and animals obtain nitrogen by eating proteins.
- Decomposers turn proteins and urea into ammonium ions; nitrifying bacteria turn ammonium into nitrate.
- Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living or in the root nodules of legumes) and lightning convert nitrogen gas into usable compounds.
- In animals, excess amino acids are deaminated and the nitrogen is excreted as urea.
- Denitrifying bacteria turn nitrate into nitrogen gas, especially in waterlogged soils.
- Crop rotation with legumes, ploughing and drainage help keep soil nitrate high.
:::
