## Introduction

At a waterhole in Hwange National Park, impala graze on the short grass while a lion watches from the shade. Later, vultures and jackals will feed on what the lion leaves, and beetles, fungi and bacteria will break down the rest. Out on Lake Kariba at night, the lights of kapenta fishing rigs attract shoals of these small fish, which feed on tiny animals in the water and are hunted in turn by tigerfish.

Every organism needs energy, and all of it can be traced back to the Sun through **who eats whom**. Food chains and food webs show these feeding relationships. You should already know that green plants make glucose by photosynthesis and that organisms release energy from food by respiration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Food chain"}
A diagram showing the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer.
:::

:::definition{term="Food web"}
A network of interconnected food chains.
:::

:::definition{term="Producer"}
An organism that makes its own organic nutrients, usually using energy from sunlight, through photosynthesis.
:::

:::definition{term="Consumer"}
An organism that gets its energy by feeding on other organisms. A **primary** consumer eats producers; a **secondary** consumer eats primary consumers; then come **tertiary** and **quaternary** consumers.
:::

:::definition{term="Herbivore and carnivore"}
A **herbivore** is an animal that gets its energy by eating plants. A **carnivore** is an animal that gets its energy by eating other animals.
:::

:::definition{term="Decomposer"}
An organism that gets its energy from dead or waste organic material, such as many bacteria and fungi.
:::

:::definition{term="Trophic level"}
The position of an organism in a food chain or food web.
:::

## Food chains

In a food chain, each **arrow points from the organism that is eaten to the organism that eats it**. The arrow shows the direction in which energy flows.

grass → impala → lion

The grass is the **producer** (first trophic level). The impala is the **primary consumer** and a herbivore (second trophic level). The lion is the **secondary consumer** and a carnivore (third trophic level).

:::example{title="Example 1: a food chain from Lake Kariba"}
Microscopic algae called **phytoplankton** float near the surface of the lake and photosynthesise. Tiny animals called **zooplankton** feed on them. **Kapenta** feed mainly on zooplankton; **tigerfish** hunt kapenta; and an **African fish eagle** can snatch a tigerfish from near the surface.

phytoplankton → zooplankton → kapenta → tigerfish → fish eagle

| Organism | Trophic level | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phytoplankton | 1 | Producer |
| Zooplankton | 2 | Primary consumer (herbivore) |
| Kapenta | 3 | Secondary consumer (carnivore) |
| Tigerfish | 4 | Tertiary consumer (carnivore) |
| Fish eagle | 5 | Quaternary consumer (carnivore) |

The Sun is the source of the energy, but it is **not** a trophic level: the chain starts with the producer.
:::

## Food webs

Real animals rarely eat only one kind of food, and most are eaten by more than one predator. A food web shows all these links together. It can be drawn as a diagram of arrows, or described as a table.

:::example{title="Example 2: part of a food web in Hwange"}
| Organism | Eats | Is eaten by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grass | (producer) | zebra, impala, termites |
| Leaves of trees and shrubs | (producer) | elephant, giraffe, kudu, impala |
| Impala | grass and leaves | lion, leopard, wild dog |
| Kudu | leaves | lion, wild dog |
| Zebra | grass | lion |
| Termites | grass and dead plant material | aardvark |

Reading the web:
- One food chain in it is: leaves → kudu → wild dog.
- The impala, kudu, zebra, giraffe, elephant and termites are all **primary consumers**, because they eat producers.
- The lion, leopard, wild dog and aardvark are **secondary consumers**.
- An organism can be at more than one trophic level. A person who eats maize meal is a primary consumer; when the same person eats beef from cattle fed on grass, they are a secondary consumer.
:::

**Decomposers** are not usually drawn in food webs, but every web depends on them. Bacteria and fungi feed on dead organisms, dung and fallen leaves, releasing carbon dioxide and mineral ions that producers use again. Termites and dung beetles help by breaking the material into smaller pieces.

## When a food web changes

Because the organisms in a web are linked, a change in one population affects others, often in more than one direction.

:::example{title="Example 3: removing a predator, overharvesting and introducing a species"}
**Removing a predator.** If wild dogs in an area were killed, the kudu and impala they hunted would at first increase. More browsers would eat more leaves and young trees, damaging the woodland, and the kudu might later decline as their food ran short.

**Overharvesting.** Kapenta are caught in large numbers for food. If too many were caught, tigerfish would have less food and their numbers would fall, and so would the fish eagles that depend on them. With fewer kapenta eating them, zooplankton could increase.

**Introducing a species.** Kapenta are not native to Lake Kariba. They were brought from Lake Tanganyika in 1967 and 1968 to make use of the open water of the new lake, where few native fish lived. They spread quickly and now support a large fishery. Tigerfish gained a plentiful new food, while the zooplankton that kapenta eat came under heavy pressure. **Water hyacinth**, a floating plant from South America, has done harm instead: its thick mats on lakes such as Chivero block light from the producers beneath them.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing the arrows the wrong way"}
The arrow goes **from** the food **to** the feeder: grass → impala, not impala → grass. Read it as "is eaten by", following the energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Making the Sun the first trophic level"}
Food chains start with the **producer**. The Sun provides the energy but is not an organism.
:::

:::mistake{title="Predicting only one effect"}
When one population changes, think about what it eats **and** what eats it. Removing lions affects impala, grass and the other predators that compete for impala.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling decomposers the top of the chain"}
Decomposers feed on dead material from **every** trophic level, not only on top carnivores.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **construct** a food chain, start with a producer, use arrows in the direction of energy flow, and use only feeding links given in the question.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "explain the effect" questions, give a chain of reasons: "fewer wild dogs → fewer kudu eaten → more kudu → more leaves eaten → fewer young trees". State the direction of change for each organism.
:::

:::exam-tip
Name trophic levels with the correct words: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer, quaternary consumer.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A food chain shows the transfer of energy from one organism to the next, beginning with a producer; arrows point from the food to the feeder.
- Producers make their own organic nutrients by photosynthesis; consumers feed on other organisms; decomposers feed on dead and waste material.
- Trophic levels: producer, then primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary consumers.
- A food web is a network of interconnected food chains; an organism may feed at more than one trophic level.
- Removing a species, overharvesting (such as overfishing kapenta) or introducing a species (kapenta, water hyacinth) changes other populations in the web.
:::
