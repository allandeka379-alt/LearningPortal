## Introduction

A footballer sprinting down the wing, a maize seed pushing up through the soil and a bacterium dividing in a pot of sour milk all need energy. They get it in the same way: by **respiration**, the chemical reactions that release energy from food inside every living cell.

In this lesson you will learn what the energy is used for, how aerobic respiration works and how to investigate it in the laboratory. You should already know that glucose is made in photosynthesis or absorbed from digested food, and that enzymes control the chemical reactions in cells.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Respiration"}
The chemical reactions in cells that break down nutrient molecules, such as glucose, and release energy for the cell's activities (metabolism).
:::

:::definition{term="Aerobic respiration"}
Respiration that uses oxygen. It breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water and releases a relatively large amount of energy.
:::

:::definition{term="ATP (ZIMSEC)"}
Adenosine triphosphate, the molecule that carries energy from respiration to the processes in the cell that need it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Respiration is not breathing"}
Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs. Respiration is a set of chemical reactions that happens in **every living cell**, in plants and microorganisms as well as animals, day and night.
:::

## What the energy is used for

Living things use the energy released by respiration for:

- **muscle contraction**, for movement and for the beating of the heart;
- **protein synthesis**, building proteins such as enzymes from amino acids;
- **cell division**, for growth and for repairing tissues;
- **active transport**, moving substances into or out of cells against a concentration gradient;
- **growth**;
- the **passage of nerve impulses**;
- **keeping a constant body temperature** in mammals and birds.

## Aerobic respiration

Aerobic respiration takes place mainly in the **mitochondria** of cells and is controlled by enzymes.

:::formula{name="Aerobic respiration: word equation"}
$$
\text{glucose} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{carbon dioxide} + \text{water}
$$
:::

:::formula{name="Aerobic respiration: balanced equation (Supplement)"}
$$
\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}
$$
:::

Energy is released by the reaction, but it is not a substance, so it is not written as a product in the balanced equation. Notice that the equation is the reverse of the overall equation for photosynthesis.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: using the balanced equation (Supplement)]
How many molecules of oxygen are needed to respire three molecules of glucose completely?
1. From the equation, 1 molecule of glucose needs 6 molecules of oxygen.
2. So 3 molecules of glucose need $3 \times 6 = 18$ molecules of oxygen.
3. They would also produce 18 molecules of carbon dioxide and 18 of water.
:::

## ATP: the energy currency (ZIMSEC)

The energy released in respiration is not used straight away. It is used to make **ATP** from **ADP** (adenosine diphosphate) and **phosphate**. ATP moves to wherever energy is needed. There it is broken down to ADP and phosphate, releasing energy in small, usable amounts. Because it is made in one place and "spent" in another, ATP is called the **energy currency** of the cell.

$$
\text{ADP} + \text{phosphate} + \text{energy from respiration} \rightarrow \text{ATP}
$$

## Investigating respiration

### Temperature and respiration in yeast

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. Put the same mass of yeast in the same volume and concentration of sugar solution in several test tubes, and stand them in water baths at different temperatures. Leave them for a few minutes to reach the temperature, then measure the rate of respiration by counting the bubbles of carbon dioxide given off per minute, or by collecting the gas in a syringe. Bubbling the gas through limewater shows that it is carbon dioxide.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: describing yeast results]
Rudo counted 93 bubbles in 3 minutes from yeast at 40 °C. At other temperatures her rates were 4 (10 °C), 11 (20 °C), 24 (30 °C), 9 (50 °C) and 0 (60 °C) bubbles per minute.
1. Rate at 40 °C $= \dfrac{93}{3} = 31$ bubbles per minute, the highest rate.
2. From 10 °C to 40 °C the rate rises: molecules move faster, so enzyme and substrate collide more often.
3. Above 40 °C the rate falls steeply, reaching zero at 60 °C: the respiratory enzymes are being denatured.
4. The optimum lies between 30 °C and 50 °C; testing every 2 °C in that range would locate it more precisely.
:::

### Germinating seeds release heat

Two vacuum flasks are set up, each with a thermometer and a cotton wool plug:

- **Flask A:** soaked, germinating bean seeds.
- **Flask B (control):** the same number of seeds, boiled to kill them and rinsed in disinfectant, so that no microorganisms can respire on them.

After two or three days the temperature in flask A has risen (for example from 21.0 °C to 24.5 °C, a rise of 3.5 °C), while flask B stays at the starting temperature. Respiration in the living seeds releases some of its energy as **heat**; the vacuum flask stops the heat escaping.

### Detecting carbon dioxide

Put germinating seeds or small animals such as maggots on a gauze platform above **hydrogencarbonate indicator** in a sealed tube. As they respire, the indicator changes from red (or orange) to **yellow**, showing that carbon dioxide has been produced. A control tube with glass beads, the same size as the living material, stays red.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling breathing respiration"}
Respiration happens inside cells. Breathing (ventilation) is the movement of air in and out of the lungs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying respiration makes energy"}
Energy cannot be made. Respiration **releases** energy stored in glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying plants photosynthesise instead of respiring"}
Plants respire all the time, day and night. In bright light photosynthesis is faster, so it hides the respiration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the disinfectant in the seed control"}
Boiled seeds can be decomposed by bacteria and fungi, which respire and release heat. The disinfectant kills them, so any temperature rise in flask A is due to the seeds.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the word equation exactly: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. (Supplement) In the balanced equation, use the numbers 6, 6, 6 and 6 and write $\ce{O2}$, not O.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for **uses of energy**, give specific processes ("muscle contraction", "active transport"), not just "for life" or "for energy".
:::

:::exam-tip
In investigations, name the **control** (boiled, disinfected seeds or glass beads) and say what it shows: that the change is caused by living, respiring organisms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Respiration is the chemical reactions in all living cells that break down nutrient molecules and release energy.
- The energy is used for muscle contraction, protein synthesis, cell division, active transport, growth, nerve impulses and keeping body temperature constant.
- Aerobic respiration: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water; (Supplement) $\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}$.
- ZIMSEC: respiration makes ATP from ADP and phosphate; ATP is the energy currency of the cell.
- Respiration can be shown by heat from germinating seeds, carbon dioxide (limewater or hydrogencarbonate indicator) and gas from yeast, whose rate rises with temperature up to an optimum.
:::
