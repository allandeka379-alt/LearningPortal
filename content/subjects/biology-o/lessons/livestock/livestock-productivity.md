## Introduction

In Zimbabwe, cattle provide meat, milk, draught power for ploughing, manure for the fields and a store of wealth, and many families also raise broiler chickens to sell. Whether a farmer makes a profit depends on two questions: how much of the feed becomes meat, and how many animals stay healthy.

In the rainy season, when ticks are most numerous, herders across the communal areas regularly drive their cattle to the local dip tank, often every week. That simple routine protects the herd from diseases spread by ticks. This lesson explains how efficiently livestock turn feed into body mass, what affects their productivity, and how four important diseases are prevented and controlled. You should already know about food chains, respiration and how pathogens cause disease.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
The amount of useful product (meat, milk, eggs) an animal or herd produces from the food and other resources it uses.
:::

:::definition{term="Feed conversion ratio (FCR)"}
The mass of feed an animal eats divided by the gain in its body mass over the same period. A **lower** FCR is better.
:::

:::definition{term="Food conversion efficiency"}
The gain in body mass as a percentage of the mass of feed eaten. A **higher** efficiency is better.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism, such as a tick, that carries a pathogen from one host to another.
:::

## Food conversion

:::formula{name="Feed conversion ratio"}
$$
\text{FCR} = \frac{\text{mass of feed eaten}}{\text{gain in body mass}}
$$
:::

:::formula{name="Food conversion efficiency"}
$$
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{gain in body mass}}{\text{mass of feed eaten}} \times 100\%
$$
:::

Both describe the same thing: an FCR of 2 means 2 kg of feed for each kilogram of gain, which is an efficiency of 50%.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a broiler and a feedlot steer]
A broiler chicken ate 3.5 kg of feed and gained 2.0 kg. A steer in a feedlot ate 1200 kg of feed and gained 150 kg. Compare them.
1. Broiler: $\text{FCR} = \dfrac{3.5}{2.0} = 1.75$; efficiency $= \dfrac{2.0}{3.5} \times 100 = 57.1\%$.
2. Steer: $\text{FCR} = \dfrac{1200}{150} = 8.0$; efficiency $= \dfrac{150}{1200} \times 100 = 12.5\%$.
3. The broiler needs far less feed per kilogram of gain, so it converts feed much more efficiently.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the cost of the feed]
Broiler feed costs US\$0.60 per kg. Using the broiler's FCR of 1.75, what is the cost of feed for each kilogram of body mass gained?
1. Each kilogram of gain needs 1.75 kg of feed.
2. Cost $= 1.75 \times 0.60 = 1.05$, so US\$1.05 per kilogram gained.
3. Any change that lowers the FCR lowers this cost and raises the farmer's profit.
:::

### Why most of the feed does not become body mass

- **Faeces:** some food is not digested. Cattle eating grass lose much of it, because cellulose is hard to digest.
- **Urine:** excess amino acids are turned into urea and excreted.
- **Respiration:** most of the energy absorbed is released in respiration and used for movement, breathing, digestion and other life processes, and much of it ends up as **heat**. Mammals and birds also use energy to keep their body temperature constant.

Broilers convert feed well because they are young and fast-growing, have been bred for rapid growth, eat a concentrated, easily digested feed and are kept in warm houses where they move little. Cattle grazing on veld walk long distances, eat fibrous grass and are often older, so their FCR is much higher.

## Factors that affect productivity

- **Breed:** some breeds grow faster or give more milk; indigenous breeds such as the Mashona, Tuli and Nkone cope better with heat, ticks and poor grazing.
- **Feed:** enough food, with the right balance of energy, protein (for growth) and minerals, plus clean water.
- **Health:** pests, parasites and diseases reduce growth and milk yield and can kill.
- **Housing and management:** shelter from cold and heat, clean pens, the right number of animals for the grazing available, and good records.
- **Age and activity:** young animals grow fastest; animals that walk far use more energy.

## Four important diseases

| Disease | Cause | How it spreads | Main signs and effects | Prevention and control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anthrax | A **bacterium**, which forms spores that survive in soil for many years | Animals swallow or breathe in spores while grazing | Sudden death; fever; dark blood that does not clot oozing from the nose, mouth and anus | Yearly **vaccination**; do not open carcasses (exposed bacteria form more spores); burn or bury carcasses deeply; quarantine; report to a veterinary officer |
| Foot-and-mouth disease | A **virus**, highly infectious | Direct contact, contaminated vehicles, boots and animal products, the air; African buffalo can carry it | Fever; blisters on the mouth, tongue, feet and teats; drooling; lameness; less eating, weight loss and lower milk yield | **Quarantine** and **movement control** (permits); **vaccination**; fencing to keep cattle away from buffalo; disinfecting vehicles and boots. Antibiotics do not work on viruses |
| Liver fluke | A parasitic **flatworm** living in the bile ducts of the liver | Eggs pass out in dung; larvae develop inside a **water snail**, then form cysts on wet grass, which animals eat | Liver damage, weight loss, anaemia, lower milk yield, swelling under the jaw; damaged livers are condemned at the abattoir | **Dosing** (drenching) with drugs that kill flukes; draining or fencing off wet, marshy areas; controlling snails |
| Redwater | A **protoctist** that lives in and destroys red blood cells | Carried by **ticks** (the vector) | Fever; red-brown urine (haemoglobin from burst red cells); anaemia; weakness; death | **Dipping** or spraying to kill ticks; vaccination; treating sick animals with drugs |

Anthrax can also infect people, so meat from an animal that died suddenly must **never** be eaten. Ticks also spread other diseases, such as heartwater, which is another reason regular dipping matters.

## How disease reduces productivity

Sick animals eat less and grow more slowly, give less milk, may abort calves or fail to breed, and may die. Farmers pay for drugs and veterinary care, carcasses or livers may be condemned, oxen too weak to plough delay planting, and outbreaks of foot-and-mouth disease lead to bans on moving or selling cattle and beef.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a higher FCR is better"}
FCR is feed per kilogram of gain, so a **lower** FCR means less feed is needed: better conversion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ticks cause redwater"}
A **protoctist** causes redwater; ticks are the **vector** that carries it from animal to animal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating foot-and-mouth disease with antibiotics"}
Foot-and-mouth disease is caused by a virus; antibiotics only kill bacteria. Control relies on vaccination, quarantine and movement control.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the snail in the liver fluke life cycle"}
Cattle cannot catch liver fluke directly from each other: the larvae must pass through a water snail first.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In FCR questions, write the formula, put the feed on **top**, show the division and say whether the result is good (low) or poor (high).
:::

:::exam-tip
For each disease, learn four things: the **type** of pathogen, how it **spreads**, its **effects** on productivity, and its **control**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Feed conversion ratio = mass of feed eaten ÷ gain in body mass (lower is better); efficiency = gain ÷ feed × 100% (higher is better).
- Most feed is not turned into body mass: some is lost in faeces and urine, and much of the energy is released in respiration and lost as heat.
- Productivity depends on breed, feed, health, housing and management, age and activity.
- Anthrax (bacterium) and foot-and-mouth disease (virus) are controlled by vaccination, quarantine and movement control; carcasses with anthrax are burned or buried unopened.
- Liver fluke (flatworm, with a snail host) is controlled by dosing and draining wet pasture; redwater (protoctist spread by ticks) is controlled by dipping.
:::
