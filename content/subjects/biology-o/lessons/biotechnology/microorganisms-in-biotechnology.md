## Introduction

A loaf from a Bulawayo bakery, the ethanol blended into petrol at a Harare filling station, a pot of traditional sorghum beer brewing for a family gathering, a tub of yoghurt, a course of penicillin and a diabetic's insulin injection have one thing in common: microorganisms made them.

Using living things to make products for people is **biotechnology**, and it is far older than the word: people were brewing beer and souring milk thousands of years before anyone knew that yeast and bacteria existed. In this lesson you will learn why microorganisms are so useful, how yeast makes bread and fuel, how traditional fermented foods are made, and how industry grows microorganisms on a huge scale in **fermenters**. You should already know that yeast can respire anaerobically and that enzymes are affected by temperature and pH.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biotechnology"}
The use of living organisms, especially microorganisms, or their enzymes, to make useful products or carry out useful processes.
:::

:::definition{term="Fermentation"}
In yeast, anaerobic respiration that turns sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide. In industry, the word is used more widely for growing microorganisms in large vessels to make a product.
:::

:::definition{term="Fermenter"}
A large, sterile vessel in which microorganisms are grown under controlled conditions to make a product.
:::

## Why bacteria are so useful

- They **reproduce very quickly** by binary fission, some as often as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so a huge population grows in a short time.
- They can **make complex molecules**, such as proteins (including enzymes) and antibiotics.
- *Cambridge Supplement:* there are **few ethical concerns** about growing and modifying bacteria in vast numbers, unlike using animals.
- *Cambridge Supplement:* they contain **plasmids**, small rings of DNA that can be taken out, given a new gene and put back, which makes bacteria easy to genetically modify.

They also grow on cheap nutrients, and they read genes using the same genetic code as all other organisms, so a human gene put into a bacterium still produces the human protein.

## Yeast: bread and fuel

Yeast is a single-celled fungus. Without oxygen it respires **anaerobically**:

$$
\text{glucose} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{carbon dioxide}
$$

*Cambridge Supplement:* the balanced equation is $\ce{C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}$.

### Making bread

1. Flour, water, a little sugar, salt and yeast are mixed into a dough.
2. **Kneading** mixes in the yeast and stretches the gluten (a protein in the flour) into an elastic network.
3. The dough is left in a **warm** place, about $25$ to $35^\circ\text{C}$, to rise. Enzymes break some starch into sugars, and the yeast respires them, soon anaerobically. The **carbon dioxide** forms bubbles that are trapped by the stretchy dough, so it rises.
4. **Baking** in a hot oven kills the yeast, makes the gas bubbles expand so the bread becomes lighter, **evaporates the ethanol** and forms a crust.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how fast does dough rise?]
A ball of dough with a volume of $80\ \text{cm}^3$ is put in a measuring cylinder at $30^\circ\text{C}$. After 50 minutes its volume is $200\ \text{cm}^3$.

1. Increase in volume $= 200 - 80 = 120\ \text{cm}^3$.
2. Percentage increase $= \dfrac{120}{80} \times 100 = 150\%$.
3. Mean rate of rising $= \dfrac{120\ \text{cm}^3}{50\ \text{min}} = 2.4\ \text{cm}^3$ per minute.
:::

### Ethanol for fuel

Yeast ferments sugar from crops, such as sugar-cane juice or maize starch broken down into sugar, in large tanks without oxygen. The ethanol is separated by **distillation** and used as a fuel, often mixed with petrol. Since 2013, petrol sold in Zimbabwe has by law been blended with ethanol made from sugar cane at Chisumbanje in the south-east, although the proportion has varied over the years. Ethanol from crops is renewable, because the crops can be grown again every season, but the land used for fuel crops cannot grow food.

## Fermented foods and drinks (ZIMSEC; useful examples for everyone)

| Product | Microorganism | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sour milk (amasi, mukaka wakakora) | Lactic acid bacteria already in the milk | Lactose (milk sugar) is turned into **lactic acid**; the acid makes the milk proteins clot, so it thickens and tastes sour |
| Yoghurt | A starter culture of lactic acid bacteria | Milk is heated to kill other microorganisms, cooled to about $40$ to $45^\circ\text{C}$, given the starter and kept warm for several hours |
| Cheese | Lactic acid bacteria, plus an enzyme (rennet) | The milk is soured and clotted into solid curds and liquid whey; the curds are pressed, salted and left to ripen |
| Mahewu | Lactic acid bacteria (from a little malted grain or flour added to thin maize porridge) | Kept warm for a day or so; lactic acid makes a sour, refreshing drink with little or no alcohol |
| Traditional opaque (sorghum) beer | Yeast, with some lactic acid bacteria | Sorghum or millet grain is soaked and allowed to sprout (**malting**), so its enzymes turn starch into sugars; yeast then ferments the sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide |

In every case the acid or alcohol produced also slows the growth of harmful microorganisms, which is why fermented foods keep longer than fresh milk or porridge.

## Fermenters (Cambridge Supplement)

An industrial fermenter is a large stainless-steel tank with:

- an **inlet for sterile nutrients**, such as glucose and a source of nitrogen (for example ammonium salts);
- an **inlet for sterile air**, bubbled in at the bottom;
- a **motor-driven stirrer** with paddles;
- a **water jacket** through which cold water flows;
- **probes** that monitor temperature, pH and oxygen;
- an **outlet** for waste gases and a tap to drain off the product.

| Condition | How it is controlled | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Probe and cooling water jacket | Respiration releases heat; the temperature is kept near the optimum for the microorganism's enzymes, which would be denatured if it got too hot |
| pH | Probe; acid or alkali added | Enzymes work best at their optimum pH, and waste products can change the pH |
| Oxygen | Sterile air bubbled in | For aerobic respiration, which releases the energy for growth (for aerobic organisms such as *Penicillium*) |
| Nutrients | Added as needed | Glucose for respiration; nitrogen for making amino acids and proteins |
| Mixing | Stirrer | Keeps microorganisms, nutrients, oxygen and heat evenly spread |
| Waste products | Gases vented; product drained | A build-up of waste would slow growth |
| Sterility (aseptic conditions) | Steam-cleaned before use; all inputs sterile | Other microorganisms would compete for nutrients, lower the yield or make harmful products |

:::example{title="Example 2: three products from fermenters"}
**Insulin**: genetically modified bacteria carrying the human insulin gene are grown in a fermenter; the insulin is then extracted and purified.

**Penicillin**: the mould *Penicillium* is grown aerobically. It makes most of its penicillin once its growth slows, so nutrients are fed in gradually; the liquid is then filtered and the penicillin extracted and purified.

**Mycoprotein**: the fungus *Fusarium* is grown on glucose with a nitrogen source and plenty of oxygen. Its hyphae are harvested, heat-treated and pressed into a high-protein meat substitute.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Yeast makes oxygen that raises the dough"}
The gas is **carbon dioxide**, from respiration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Bread contains the alcohol made by the yeast"}
The ethanol **evaporates** during baking.
:::

:::mistake{title="Yoghurt bacteria make alcohol"}
Lactic acid bacteria turn lactose into **lactic acid**, which thickens and sours the milk.
:::

:::mistake{title="Fermenters must be heated"}
Respiring microorganisms release heat, so a fermenter usually needs **cooling** by its water jacket to stay at the optimum temperature.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For fermenter questions, give **what** is controlled, **how**, and **why**, and link the why to enzymes, respiration or contamination.
:::

:::exam-tip
In bread questions, explain the warm place (enzymes work faster, so more carbon dioxide), kneading (mixing, elastic dough) and baking (kills yeast, expands bubbles, evaporates ethanol).
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bacteria reproduce quickly and can make complex molecules; they also have plasmids and raise few ethical concerns (Cambridge Supplement).
- Yeast respires anaerobically: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.
- In bread the carbon dioxide raises the dough and the ethanol evaporates during baking; in biofuel production the ethanol is the product.
- Lactic acid bacteria make sour milk, yoghurt, cheese and mahewu; malted grain and yeast make traditional sorghum beer.
- Fermenters control temperature, pH, oxygen, nutrients, mixing, waste and sterility to grow microorganisms that make insulin, penicillin and mycoprotein.
:::
