## Introduction

For much of the twentieth century, people with type 1 diabetes injected insulin taken from the pancreases of slaughtered pigs and cattle. Today almost all insulin is made by microorganisms that carry a copy of the **human** insulin gene. Moving a gene from one organism into another is called **genetic modification**.

It is a powerful technology, and a debated one. Zimbabwe has chosen a cautious approach: farmers are not allowed to grow genetically modified (GM) crops, and imported GM maize has been milled before use so that it cannot be planted. Neighbouring South Africa, by contrast, grows large areas of GM maize, soya beans and cotton. In this lesson you will learn what genetic modification is, how it is done and how to weigh up its benefits and hazards. You should already know that genes are lengths of DNA that code for proteins, and that bacteria contain plasmids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Genetic modification"}
Changing the genetic material of an organism by removing, changing or inserting individual genes.
:::

:::definition{term="Recombinant DNA"}
DNA made by joining DNA from two different sources, usually two different species, such as a human gene inserted into a bacterial plasmid.
:::

:::definition{term="Restriction enzyme"}
An enzyme that cuts DNA at a specific sequence of bases.
:::

:::definition{term="DNA ligase"}
An enzyme that joins pieces of DNA together.
:::

:::definition{term="Plasmid"}
A small ring of DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main loop of DNA.
:::

## Examples of genetic modification

- **Bacteria making human proteins**: human insulin for people with diabetes, and human growth hormone. GM yeast is also used to make a vaccine against hepatitis B.
- **Herbicide-resistant crops**: soya and maize given a gene that lets them survive a particular herbicide, so a farmer can spray the whole field and kill the weeds without harming the crop.
- **Insect-resistant crops**: **Bt** maize and Bt cotton carry a gene from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*. The plants make a protein that is toxic to caterpillars such as maize stalk borers and cotton bollworms, but not to people or most other animals.
- **Crops with improved nutritional qualities**: "Golden Rice" has been given genes that let it make **beta-carotene** in its grains. The body turns beta-carotene into vitamin A, whose deficiency causes blindness in young children in places where rice is the main food.

## Making a human protein in bacteria (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

The steps for human insulin:

1. The human **insulin gene** is cut out of human DNA with a **restriction enzyme**. The enzyme cuts the two strands at slightly different places, leaving short single-stranded ends of unpaired bases called **sticky ends**.
2. A **plasmid** from a bacterium is cut open with the **same** restriction enzyme, so its sticky ends have bases complementary to those on the gene.
3. The genes and cut plasmids are mixed. Complementary sticky ends pair up: A with T and C with G.
4. **DNA ligase** joins the DNA permanently, sealing the gene into the plasmid. This is a **recombinant plasmid**.
5. The recombinant plasmids are taken up by bacteria, which are now **genetically modified**.
6. The GM bacteria are grown in a fermenter. They multiply rapidly, copying the plasmid every time they divide.
7. The bacteria **express** the gene, making human insulin, which is extracted and purified.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: why the same enzyme is used]
A restriction enzyme cuts a plasmid and leaves a sticky end with the unpaired bases G, A, T, C. Which unpaired bases must the gene's sticky end have to pair with it?

1. Bases pair A with T and C with G.
2. Pair each base in turn: G → C, A → T, T → A, C → G.
3. The gene's sticky end must carry **C, T, A, G**. Cutting the gene with the same enzyme produces exactly these complementary ends, so the gene and plasmid fit together.
:::

## Benefits and hazards

### Genetically modified microorganisms

Human insulin made by bacteria is identical to the body's own insulin, so it causes fewer allergic reactions than animal insulin. It can be made in huge, reliable amounts at a lower cost, and no animals are needed, which matters to people with religious or ethical objections to pig or cattle products.

### Genetically modified crops (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

| Possible advantages | Possible disadvantages and concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Higher yields, because less of the crop is lost to pests and weeds | GM seed is expensive and often patented, so farmers may have to buy new seed every season from a few large companies |
| Less insecticide is sprayed on insect-resistant crops, protecting useful insects and farm workers and saving money | Genes can spread in pollen to wild relatives or to non-GM crops, for example producing herbicide-resistant weeds |
| Weeds are controlled easily in herbicide-resistant crops | Pests may evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and weeds to the herbicide, if they are used heavily |
| Better nutrition, as in Golden Rice | More herbicide use can reduce the weeds and insects that other wildlife depends on, reducing biodiversity |
| Crops could be made tolerant of drought or resistant to disease | Some people worry about unknown long-term effects on health, such as new allergies, although approved GM foods are tested before sale; some object to moving genes between species |

Some countries also refuse to import GM crops, which can affect a farming country's exports.

:::example{title="Example 2: Bt cotton in a farming area"}
In countries where Bt cotton is grown, farmers have often been able to spray insecticide against bollworms far less often, because the plants produce their own toxin. But the Bt toxin does not kill sap-sucking pests such as aphids, so some spraying is still needed, and farmers are advised to plant strips of non-Bt cotton as "refuges" so that bollworms susceptible to the toxin survive and slow down the spread of resistance.
:::

### Genetic modification compared with selective breeding

| Genetic modification | Selective breeding |
| --- | --- |
| Genes can come from a different species | Uses only genes already present in the species |
| One or a few chosen genes are moved | Many genes are mixed, so unwanted features can come too |
| Fast: the change is made in one generation | Slow: needs many generations |
| Needs a laboratory | Can be done on a farm |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Restriction enzymes join DNA"}
Restriction enzymes **cut** DNA at specific base sequences. **DNA ligase** joins pieces together.
:::

:::mistake{title="Any enzyme can cut the plasmid"}
The plasmid must be cut with the **same restriction enzyme** as the gene, so that the sticky ends are complementary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sticky ends are a kind of glue"}
Sticky ends are **unpaired bases** that pair with complementary bases on another sticky end.
:::

:::mistake{title="Eating GM food changes your genes"}
Genes in food are digested like any other DNA; they do not enter your cells' chromosomes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the order: **cut gene (restriction enzyme) → cut plasmid (same enzyme) → sticky ends pair → DNA ligase → recombinant plasmid → into bacteria → bacteria multiply → gene expressed → protein extracted**.
:::

:::exam-tip
In **discuss** questions about GM crops, give specific advantages and disadvantages, then a balanced conclusion. "It is unnatural" earns little credit; "genes may spread to wild relatives in pollen" earns a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
Base pairing questions: A pairs with T, C pairs with G. Pair one base at a time.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Genetic modification changes an organism's genetic material by removing, changing or inserting individual genes; recombinant DNA combines DNA from different sources.
- Examples: bacteria making human insulin; herbicide-resistant and insect-resistant (Bt) crops; Golden Rice with beta-carotene.
- A restriction enzyme cuts out the gene and opens a plasmid, leaving complementary sticky ends; DNA ligase joins them into a recombinant plasmid.
- GM bacteria multiply in fermenters and express the gene, making the human protein.
- GM crops can raise yields and cut pesticide use, but raise concerns about seed costs, gene spread, resistance, biodiversity and safety; Zimbabwe does not allow GM crops to be grown.
:::
