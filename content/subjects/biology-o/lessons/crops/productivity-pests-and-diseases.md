## Introduction

In 2016 a new caterpillar was found eating maize in Zimbabwe: the **fall armyworm**, a pest from the Americas that had just spread across Africa. Within a season it was reported in maize fields across the country. Farmers who depend on a single maize crop to feed their families suddenly faced a new threat to their harvest.

A farmer's harvest depends on how fast the crop can photosynthesise and grow, and on how much of that growth is lost to pests and diseases. In this lesson you will learn how productivity is measured, what limits it, how pests and diseases damage maize, cotton and tobacco, and how farmers control them. You should already know about photosynthesis, limiting factors and the xylem and phloem.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biomass"}
The mass of living material in an area, usually measured as dry mass so that water content does not affect it.
:::

:::definition{term="Productivity"}
The rate at which a crop produces new biomass, measured per unit area per unit time, for example tonnes per hectare per year. The part we harvest is the **yield**.
:::

:::definition{term="Pest"}
An organism, usually an insect or a mite, that damages crops and so reduces their yield or quality.
:::

:::definition{term="Plant disease"}
A condition caused by a pathogen (a bacterium, fungus or virus) that harms the growth or functioning of a plant.
:::

## What limits productivity?

A crop's yield depends on how much photosynthesis it can do and how much of its product survives to harvest.

- **Light**: intensity and day length; shading by weeds or by planting too closely reduces it.
- **Water**: drought closes the stomata and stops growth; water is also a raw material.
- **Temperature**: enzymes work slowly when it is cold and are damaged when it is too hot.
- **Mineral salts**: nitrate, phosphate and potassium in the soil (see mineral nutrition).
- **Carbon dioxide**: a raw material, usually about 0.04% of the air.
- **Pests, diseases and weeds**: they eat, infect or compete with the crop.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: yield per hectare]
Chipo harvests 2.8 tonnes of maize grain from a field of 0.8 hectares. What is her yield per hectare?

1. Yield per hectare $=$ mass harvested $\div$ area.
2. $2.8 \div 0.8 = 3.5$ tonnes per hectare.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: yield lost to a pest]
In a trial, maize protected from stalk borer yielded 4.0 t/ha; unprotected maize beside it yielded 3.2 t/ha. What percentage of the protected yield was lost?

1. Loss $= 4.0 - 3.2 = 0.8$ t/ha.
2. Percentage loss $= 0.8 \div 4.0 \times 100 = 20\%$.
:::

## Pests

Pests are grouped by how they feed.

### Chewing (tissue-eating) pests

These have biting mouthparts and eat leaves, stems, roots or fruits.

- **Maize stalk borer**: caterpillars feed in the funnel of young maize, leaving rows of holes in the leaves, then tunnel down into the stem. The growing point may die (**dead heart**), stems break and cobs are damaged.
- **Fall armyworm**: caterpillars eat the leaves in the funnel, leaving ragged holes and sawdust-like droppings, and can bore into cobs.
- **Bollworms**: caterpillars that eat the bolls (fruits) of **cotton**; the African bollworm also attacks tomatoes and maize cobs.
- **Cutworms**: caterpillars that hide in the soil by day and cut young seedlings of maize, tobacco and vegetables off at ground level at night.
- **Locusts**: swarms of grasshopper-like insects that strip the leaves of maize, sorghum and grasses.

### Sap-sucking pests

These pierce the plant with needle-like mouthparts and suck out sap or cell contents.

- **Aphids**: small soft insects on **cotton**, **tobacco** and vegetables. They suck phloem sap, causing curled, weak leaves, excrete sticky honeydew on which black sooty mould grows, and can spread plant viruses.
- **Red spider mites**: tiny eight-legged arachnids (not insects) on **cotton**, tomatoes and beans. They suck the contents of leaf cells, leaving speckled, bronze leaves and fine webbing.
- **Whiteflies**: on cotton, tobacco and tomatoes; they suck sap, make cotton lint sticky with honeydew and spread viruses.

:::keyfact{title="How pests reduce yield"}
Less leaf area means less photosynthesis; tunnels in stems damage the xylem and phloem and make stems break; sucking removes sugars and amino acids; damaged cobs, bolls and leaves are lost or lower in quality; and some pests spread diseases.
:::

## Diseases

- **Bacterial wilt** affects **tobacco**, tomatoes and potatoes. The bacteria live in the soil, enter through the roots and multiply in the **xylem**, blocking it. The plant wilts even when the soil is moist, and the xylem shows brown streaks. There is no cure: farmers rely on rotation with non-host crops such as maize and grasses, resistant varieties, disease-free seedlings and removing infected plants.
- **Rust** is caused by fungi. **Common rust of maize** forms small, powdery, reddish-brown pustules on the leaves. Wind carries the spores from plant to plant. The pustules reduce the leaf area that can photosynthesise. Resistant varieties and fungicides are used to control it.
- **Viruses** also cause crop diseases, for example **maize streak virus**, spread by tiny leafhoppers, which gives yellow streaks along the leaves.

## Controlling pests and diseases

| Method | Examples | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cultural | Crop rotation; early planting; destroying crop residues; resistant varieties; weeding | Cheap; no chemicals; long-lasting | Slower; extra labour; may not stop a severe outbreak; works best if neighbours do it too |
| Chemical | Insecticides, fungicides, miticides | Fast and effective over large areas | Costly; kills useful insects such as pollinators and natural enemies; pests become resistant; residues on food; pollutes water; can poison farm workers |
| Biological | Ladybirds eat aphids; predatory mites eat red spider mites; parasitic wasps; *Bt* bacteria that kill caterpillars | Specific; no residues; can keep working on its own | Slow; reduces the pest but does not wipe it out; the control organism may harm other species |

**Integrated pest management (IPM)** combines these methods. Farmers **scout** their fields regularly, count the pests and spray only when the numbers pass a threshold, so that natural enemies survive and fewer chemicals are used.

:::example{title="Example 3: why each cultural method works"}
- **Destroying maize stalks** after harvest (ploughing them in, feeding them to cattle or burning them) kills stalk borer caterpillars that survive the dry season inside the stalks.
- **Early planting** with the first good rains lets maize grow tall and strong before the pest population builds up.
- **Rotating tobacco with maize or grass** deprives the bacterial wilt bacteria in the soil of a host, because maize and grasses are not infected, so their numbers fall over a few seasons.
- **Resistant varieties** carry alleles that allow them to survive a pest or disease, for example rust-resistant maize.
:::

:::example{title="Example 4: safe use of pesticides"}
Before spraying cotton, Sipho reads the label, wears gloves, a mask, overalls and boots, and sprays when there is little wind, never towards people or water. He does not eat, drink or smoke while spraying, washes afterwards, stores the chemicals locked away from children and food, and never uses an empty pesticide container to carry water.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pests and diseases are the same"}
A pest is an animal that feeds on the crop; a disease is caused by a pathogen such as a bacterium, fungus or virus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Insects become immune to insecticide"}
Individual insects do not change. A few already have resistant alleles; they survive spraying and breed, so the next generation contains more resistant insects. This is **natural selection**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Spider mites are insects"}
Red spider mites have **eight legs**: they are arachnids, related to spiders.
:::

:::mistake{title="Biological control wipes out the pest"}
Biological control keeps pest numbers **low**; if the pest disappeared, its natural enemy would starve too.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Name the pest or disease **and** the crop it attacks (maize stalk borer on maize, red spider mite on cotton, bacterial wilt in tobacco). Vague answers such as "insects" earn little.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining how damage reduces yield, link it to a process: less leaf area, so **less photosynthesis**; blocked xylem, so **water cannot reach the leaves**.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Discuss" or "evaluate" a control method: give advantages **and** disadvantages, then a conclusion.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Productivity is the rate of biomass production per unit area; it is limited by light, water, temperature, mineral salts, carbon dioxide, pests, diseases and weeds.
- Chewing pests (stalk borer, fall armyworm, bollworms, cutworms, locusts) eat plant tissues; sap-sucking pests (aphids, red spider mites, whiteflies) remove sap and spread viruses.
- Bacterial wilt blocks the xylem of tobacco and tomatoes; rust fungi reduce the photosynthetic area of maize leaves.
- Cultural control is cheap and safe but slow; chemical control is fast but causes resistance, pollution and harm to useful species; biological control is specific but slow.
- Integrated pest management combines methods and sprays only when scouting shows it is needed.
:::
