## Introduction

On an October afternoon in the Zambezi Valley the air can be hotter than 40 °C; on a winter night in Nyanga it can fall close to freezing. Through all of this, the temperature deep inside your body stays very close to **37 °C**. Your enzymes work best near this temperature, so keeping it steady is a matter of survival.

Keeping conditions inside the body constant is called **homeostasis**. This lesson shows how the skin and the brain control body temperature. For Cambridge candidates this is **Supplement** content; for ZIMSEC it is part of the homeostasis topic. You should already know how enzymes are affected by temperature and how respiration releases heat.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Homeostasis"}
The maintenance of a constant internal environment in the body, within narrow limits.
:::

:::definition{term="Negative feedback"}
A control mechanism in which a change away from the set point is detected and responses are made that bring the condition back towards the set point.
:::

:::definition{term="Vasodilation"}
The widening of the arterioles that supply the skin capillaries, so more blood flows near the skin surface.
:::

:::definition{term="Vasoconstriction"}
The narrowing of the arterioles that supply the skin capillaries, so less blood flows near the skin surface.
:::

## The structure of the skin

A section through the skin shows these labelled parts, from the surface inwards:

- **Epidermis**: the outer layer. Its surface cells are dead and form a tough, waterproof, protective layer.
- **Hairs**, each growing from a hair follicle in the dermis.
- **Hair erector muscles**: tiny muscles attached to each hair; when they contract, the hair stands up.
- **Sweat glands**: coiled tubes in the dermis that make sweat, which reaches the surface through a sweat duct and pore.
- **Receptors**: nerve endings that detect temperature (and touch, pressure and pain).
- **Sensory neurones**: carry impulses from the receptors to the brain and spinal cord.
- **Blood vessels**: arterioles supplying networks of capillaries near the surface.
- **Fatty tissue**: a layer beneath the dermis that insulates the body and stores energy.

## How the body detects and controls its temperature

1. **Temperature receptors in the skin** detect changes in the temperature of the surroundings and send impulses along sensory neurones to the brain.
2. A part of the brain called the **hypothalamus** monitors the temperature of the **blood** flowing through it (the core temperature) and compares it with the **set point**, about 37 °C.
3. The brain then sends nerve impulses to **effectors** (sweat glands, the muscles in arteriole walls, hair erector muscles and skeletal muscles) to correct any change.

This is **negative feedback**: a rise in temperature triggers responses that lower it, and a fall triggers responses that raise it.

### When the body is too hot

- **Vasodilation:** the muscles in the walls of the arterioles supplying the skin capillaries **relax**, so the arterioles widen. More blood flows through the capillaries close to the skin surface, and more heat is lost to the surroundings by radiation. The skin looks flushed.
- **Sweating:** sweat glands produce more sweat. As the water in sweat **evaporates** from the skin, it takes heat from the skin, cooling it.
- **Hairs lie flat:** the hair erector muscles relax, so little air is trapped next to the skin.
- **Behaviour:** finding shade, removing clothes, drinking cool water.

### When the body is too cold

- **Vasoconstriction:** the muscles in the walls of the arterioles **contract**, so the arterioles narrow. Less blood flows through the skin capillaries, so less heat is lost. The skin looks pale.
- **Shivering:** skeletal muscles contract rapidly and involuntarily; the extra respiration in the muscles releases heat.
- **Hairs stand up:** the hair erector muscles contract, trapping a layer of still air, which is a good insulator. (In humans this gives "goose pimples" but does little; in furry mammals it matters a lot.)
- **Less sweat** is produced.
- **Insulation:** the fatty tissue reduces heat loss.
- **Behaviour:** putting on warm clothes, moving about, sitting by a fire.

:::keyfact{title="Capillaries do not move"}
In vasodilation and vasoconstriction it is the **arterioles** that widen or narrow, changing how much blood flows into the skin capillaries. The capillaries themselves do not move up or down in the skin.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how much heat does sweating remove?]
Evaporating 1 g of water from the skin removes about 2.4 kJ of heat. A farm worker harvesting tobacco on a hot day loses 1.5 dm³ (1500 g) of sweat, all of which evaporates. How much heat does this remove?
1. Heat removed $= 1500 \times 2.4$.
2. $= 3600\ \text{kJ}$.
3. That is a large amount of heat, which is why sweating is so effective, and why the lost water must be replaced by drinking.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: blood flow to the skin in the cold]
During a cold shower, the blood flow through a patch of skin fell from 250 to 60 cm³ per minute. Calculate the percentage decrease and explain it.
1. Decrease $= 250 - 60 = 190$ cm³ per minute.
2. Percentage decrease $= \dfrac{190}{250} \times 100 = 76\%$.
3. Cold receptors sent impulses to the brain, which caused **vasoconstriction**: the arterioles supplying the skin narrowed, so less warm blood reached the surface and less heat was lost.
:::

:::example{title="Elephants in Hwange"}
African elephants have huge ears with a dense network of blood vessels. On a hot day the vessels in the ears widen (vasodilation) and the elephant flaps its ears. Air moving over the warm blood carries heat away, cooling the blood before it returns to the body.
:::

Sweating works less well in **humid** weather: when the air already contains a lot of water vapour, sweat evaporates more slowly, so less heat is removed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying capillaries move towards the skin surface"}
Capillaries stay where they are. Arterioles widen (vasodilation) or narrow (vasoconstriction), changing the blood flow through the capillaries.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sweat cools because it is cold"}
Sweat leaves the body warm. It cools the skin because **evaporation** of the water takes heat from the skin.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying shivering warms you because you move about"}
Shivering is involuntary muscle contraction; the **respiration** in the contracting muscles releases heat.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the skin controls body temperature on its own"}
Receptors in the skin detect changes, but the **brain** coordinates the responses.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the precise terms: **vasodilation** and **vasoconstriction** of **arterioles**; sweat **evaporates**; heat is lost by **radiation**.
:::

:::exam-tip
For negative feedback, give the sequence: change detected (receptors) → brain compares with the set point → effectors respond → temperature returns towards 37 °C.
:::

:::exam-tip
In data questions, link each response to its effect on **heat loss** or **heat production**, and quote numbers.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Homeostasis keeps the internal environment constant; body temperature is held close to 37 °C by negative feedback.
- Skin structures involved: hairs and hair erector muscles, sweat glands, temperature receptors, sensory neurones, blood vessels and fatty tissue.
- The brain (hypothalamus) monitors blood temperature, receives impulses from skin receptors and coordinates the responses.
- Too hot: vasodilation of skin arterioles, more sweating (cooling by evaporation), hairs flat.
- Too cold: vasoconstriction, shivering (heat from respiration), hairs raised, less sweating, insulation by fat.
:::
