## Introduction

Tatenda eats a large plate of sadza for lunch. The starch is digested to glucose, which is absorbed into his blood. That afternoon he runs the 1500 m at the school athletics day, and his muscles use glucose fast. Yet a few hours later the concentration of glucose in his blood is almost exactly where it was before lunch. Keeping it there is one example of **homeostasis**.

In this lesson you will learn why the blood glucose concentration must be kept steady, how the **pancreas** and the **liver** control it, and what happens in **diabetes**, a condition that affects many families in Zimbabwe and around the world.

You should already know that glucose is used in respiration and that hormones are carried in the blood (see the lesson on hormones).

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Both boards: homeostasis and the role of insulin. **Cambridge Supplement** and ZIMSEC: glucagon and the liver; Type 1 diabetes and its treatment. **Cambridge Supplement**: negative feedback and set points. **ZIMSEC**: the signs and symptoms of diabetes and how it is managed.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Homeostasis"}
The maintenance of a constant internal environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Internal environment"}
The conditions inside the body that surround the cells: the blood and tissue fluid, with their glucose concentration, water content and temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Set point (Supplement)"}
The normal level of a condition that homeostasis keeps the body close to, such as a body temperature of about 37 °C.
:::

:::definition{term="Negative feedback (Supplement)"}
A control process in which a change away from the set point is detected and causes responses that reverse the change, bringing the condition back to the set point.
:::

:::definition{term="Glycogen"}
A large, insoluble storage carbohydrate made from many glucose molecules, stored in the liver and muscles.
:::

## Why blood glucose must be kept steady

- Every cell needs a **steady supply of glucose** for respiration. Brain cells depend on it almost completely: if the concentration falls too low, a person becomes shaky and confused and may lose consciousness.
- If the concentration stays **too high**, the blood becomes more concentrated, so water tends to leave cells by **osmosis**; over many years, high glucose damages blood vessels, the eyes, the kidneys and the nerves.

## Insulin and glucagon

Both hormones are made by the **pancreas**. Their main target organ is the **liver**.

| Blood glucose is... | The pancreas secretes... | Effect on the liver | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Too high (after a meal) | **Insulin** | Takes up glucose and converts it to **glycogen** for storage; insulin also helps other cells take up glucose | Blood glucose **falls** |
| Too low (during exercise or fasting) | **Glucagon** | Breaks down **glycogen** to glucose and releases it into the blood | Blood glucose **rises** |

:::keyfact
Glucose is the sugar. Glycogen is the store. Glucagon is the hormone that releases the store. Insulin is the hormone that fills it.
:::

## Negative feedback (Cambridge Supplement)

Blood glucose is controlled by **negative feedback** around a **set point**:

1. The blood glucose concentration rises above the set point.
2. The change is detected by cells in the pancreas.
3. The pancreas secretes more insulin (and less glucagon).
4. The liver converts glucose to glycogen; body cells take up more glucose.
5. The concentration falls back to the set point, so the pancreas secretes less insulin.

If the concentration falls below the set point, the opposite happens: more glucagon is secreted, the liver breaks down glycogen, and the concentration rises again. The response always works **against** the change, which is why it is called negative feedback.

:::example{title="Example 1: after lunch"}
After Tatenda's sadza, glucose is absorbed from his small intestine and his blood glucose concentration rises. His pancreas secretes **insulin**. His liver cells take up glucose and store it as **glycogen**, and his muscle cells take up more glucose. Within two to three hours his blood glucose is back to normal.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: running the 1500 m"}
During the race Tatenda's muscles respire fast and use up glucose, so his blood glucose concentration starts to fall. His pancreas secretes **glucagon** (and less insulin). His liver breaks down glycogen to glucose and releases it into the blood, so his muscles and brain keep receiving glucose.
:::

## Diabetes

**Diabetes mellitus** is a condition in which the blood glucose concentration is not controlled properly and stays too high.

### Type 1 diabetes

In **Type 1 diabetes** the pancreas makes little or **no insulin**, because the cells that secrete it have been destroyed by the body's own immune system. It usually starts in childhood or young adulthood. Without insulin, glucose is not stored as glycogen after a meal, so the blood glucose concentration rises much too high.

### Signs and symptoms (ZIMSEC)

- High blood glucose concentration, and **glucose in the urine**, because the kidneys cannot reabsorb all of it
- **Frequent urination** and great **thirst**
- **Tiredness** and lack of energy, and weight loss
- Blurred vision and slow healing of cuts

### Treatment and management

Type 1 diabetes is treated with **insulin injections** (or an insulin pump), usually several times a day. Insulin is a protein, so it would be **digested** if it were swallowed. People with Type 1 diabetes also:

- **monitor** their blood glucose regularly with a finger-prick test and a meter;
- eat **regular meals** and control the amount of carbohydrate, avoiding large amounts of sugary food and drink;
- take regular **exercise**, adjusting food or insulin when they are more active.

Too much insulin, a missed meal or extra exercise can make the blood glucose concentration fall too low, causing shakiness, sweating and confusion. People with diabetes learn to recognise these signs and take something sugary straight away, which is why they often carry glucose sweets.

### Type 2 diabetes (ZIMSEC)

In **Type 2 diabetes** the body's cells stop responding properly to insulin, and the pancreas may not make enough. It is more likely in people who are overweight, take little exercise or have a family history of diabetes, and it usually develops in adults. It is managed with a healthy diet, weight loss, regular exercise and medicines; some people also need insulin.

:::example{title="Example 3: reading a blood glucose table"}
Two adults drank the same glucose drink after fasting overnight. Their blood glucose concentrations, in $\text{mmol/dm}^3$, were measured:

| Time after the drink / min | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Person A | 5.0 | 7.6 | 6.4 | 5.5 | 5.0 |
| Person B | 8.5 | 13.0 | 15.5 | 16.0 | 15.2 |

Person A's concentration rises and then returns to 5.0 within two hours: insulin is working. Person B starts higher, rises much further and stays high: B's blood glucose is not being controlled, which fits **diabetes**. The data alone cannot show the type: in Type 1 diabetes too little insulin is made, and in Type 2 the cells respond poorly to it. B's rise between 0 and 90 minutes is $16.0 - 8.5 = 7.5\ \text{mmol/dm}^3$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up glycogen and glucagon"}
**Glycogen** is a storage carbohydrate in the liver and muscles. **Glucagon** is a hormone from the pancreas. Check the spelling: glucag**on** is the hormone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying insulin turns glycogen into glucose"}
Insulin makes the liver turn glucose **into** glycogen, lowering blood glucose. Glucagon makes the liver turn glycogen back into glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the liver makes insulin"}
The **pancreas** makes insulin and glucagon. The liver is the target organ that stores or releases glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying insulin tablets treat Type 1 diabetes"}
Insulin is a protein, so it would be digested in the stomach and small intestine. It has to be **injected**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment; blood glucose must be kept steady for respiration, especially in the brain.
- High blood glucose: the pancreas secretes insulin, and the liver converts glucose to glycogen. Low blood glucose: the pancreas secretes glucagon, and the liver converts glycogen to glucose.
- Supplement: this is negative feedback around a set point.
- Type 1 diabetes: little or no insulin is made; it is treated with insulin injections, blood glucose monitoring, a controlled diet and exercise.
- ZIMSEC: signs include thirst, frequent urination, glucose in the urine and tiredness; Type 2 diabetes is managed mainly through diet, exercise and medicines.
:::
