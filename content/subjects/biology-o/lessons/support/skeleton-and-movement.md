## Introduction

Watch a footballer at the National Sports Stadium in Harare: as she sprints, her hips swing her legs forwards, her knees bend and straighten, and her arms pump back and forth at the shoulders and elbows. Every one of those movements needs bones, joints and muscles working together.

An adult human skeleton has about 206 bones. This lesson explains what the skeleton does, names the bones of the limbs, and shows how joints and muscles produce movement. You should already know that muscles contract using energy released by respiration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Joint"}
A place where two or more bones meet.
:::

:::definition{term="Ligament"}
A tough, slightly elastic band of tissue that joins bone to bone at a joint.
:::

:::definition{term="Tendon"}
A tough, inelastic cord of tissue that joins a muscle to a bone.
:::

:::definition{term="Antagonistic muscles"}
A pair of muscles that produce opposite movements at a joint: when one contracts, the other relaxes.
:::

## Functions of the skeleton

| Function | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Support** | Gives the body its shape, holds the organs in place and provides places for muscles to attach |
| **Protection** | The skull protects the brain; the ribcage protects the heart and lungs; the vertebral column protects the spinal cord |
| **Movement (locomotion)** | Muscles pull on bones, which act as levers and move at the joints |

The skeleton also makes blood cells (in the bone marrow) and stores calcium, although these are not the functions examiners usually ask for.

## The bones of the limbs

**Forelimb (arm), from the shoulder down:**

- **Scapula**: the flat, triangular shoulder blade, part of the shoulder girdle.
- **Humerus**: the single bone of the upper arm.
- **Radius**: the forearm bone on the thumb side.
- **Ulna**: the forearm bone on the little-finger side; its end forms the point of the elbow.
- Then the wrist bones (carpals), palm bones (metacarpals) and finger bones (phalanges).

**Hindlimb (leg), from the hip down:**

- **Pelvic girdle**: the hip bones, which join the legs to the vertebral column.
- **Femur**: the single bone of the thigh, the longest bone in the body.
- **Tibia**: the larger bone of the lower leg (the shin bone).
- **Fibula**: the thinner bone beside the tibia, on the outer side of the leg.
- Then the kneecap (patella), the ankle bones (tarsals), foot bones (metatarsals) and toe bones (phalanges).

:::keyfact{title="The same plan in arm and leg"}
Girdle → one bone (humerus or femur) → two bones (radius and ulna, or tibia and fibula) → many small bones of the wrist and hand, or ankle and foot.
:::

## Joints

- **Fixed joints**, such as those between the bones of the skull, allow no movement.
- **Hinge joints**, such as the **elbow** and the **knee**, allow movement in **one plane** only: bending and straightening, like the hinge of a door.
- **Ball-and-socket joints**, such as the **shoulder** and the **hip**, allow movement in **all directions**, including rotation. At the shoulder the rounded head of the humerus fits into a socket in the scapula; at the hip the head of the femur fits into a socket in the pelvic girdle.

### Inside a movable joint

Labelled parts of a movable (synovial) joint such as the knee:

- **Cartilage**: a smooth, slippery layer covering the ends of the bones; it reduces friction and absorbs shocks.
- **Synovial membrane**: lines the joint and makes synovial fluid.
- **Synovial fluid**: lubricates the joint so the bones move smoothly.
- **Ligaments**: hold the bones together while allowing them to move.
- **Capsule**: a tough covering around the joint.

## How muscles move bones

A muscle can only **pull**: it contracts (shortens) and pulls on the bone it is attached to by a tendon. It cannot push. When it relaxes, it must be stretched back to its original length by another muscle. That is why skeletal muscles work in **antagonistic pairs**.

At the elbow, the antagonistic pair is the **biceps** and the **triceps**:

| Movement | Biceps (flexor) | Triceps (extensor) | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bending the arm (flexion) | Contracts | Relaxes | Pulls the radius up, so the forearm rises |
| Straightening the arm (extension) | Relaxes | Contracts | Pulls the ulna, so the forearm is lowered |

The biceps lies on the front of the upper arm. Its tendons attach it to the scapula at the top and to the **radius** just below the elbow. The triceps lies on the back of the upper arm and is attached to the scapula and humerus and, by a tendon, to the **ulna**. The elbow acts as the pivot of a lever.

A **flexor** bends a joint; an **extensor** straightens it. At the knee, the hamstring muscles at the back of the thigh are the flexors and the quadriceps at the front are the extensors.

:::example{title="Carrying a bucket of water"}
Tatenda lifts a bucket of water from the borehole up to his chest.
- His **biceps contracts**, shortening and pulling on the radius through its tendon; the forearm rises about the elbow joint.
- At the same time his **triceps relaxes** and is stretched.
- To lower the bucket gently, the biceps relaxes slowly, so the weight of the bucket lowers the forearm in a controlled way. The triceps contracts when the arm is straightened against a resistance, for example when he pushes the bucket away from his body.
- The **tendons**, being inelastic, pass the full pull of the muscles to the bones; the **ligaments** of the elbow hold the humerus, radius and ulna together.
:::

:::example{title="Which joints does a goalkeeper use?"}
- Diving sideways and swinging the arm up to stop a high ball: **ball-and-socket** joints at the shoulder and hip allow movement in many directions.
- Bending the knees to crouch and straightening them to jump: **hinge** joints at the knees, moved by the hamstrings (flexors) and quadriceps (extensors).
- Catching the ball close to the chest: bending at the **elbow**, a hinge joint, by contraction of the biceps.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying muscles push"}
Muscles can only pull. To return a bone, the antagonistic muscle must contract.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up tendons and ligaments"}
**T**endons join muscle **t**o bone; ligaments join bone to bone.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the knee a ball-and-socket joint"}
The knee and the elbow are hinge joints. The shoulder and the hip are ball-and-socket joints.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a relaxed muscle pushes the bone back"}
A relaxing muscle does nothing to the bone; it is stretched by the contraction of its antagonist.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When describing a movement, name **both** muscles and say what **each** does: "the triceps **contracts** and the biceps **relaxes**, so the arm straightens".
:::

:::exam-tip
Name bones exactly: scapula, humerus, radius, ulna; pelvic girdle, femur, tibia, fibula. Learn which is on the thumb side (radius) and which is the larger lower-leg bone (tibia).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The skeleton supports the body, protects organs (skull, ribcage, vertebral column) and allows movement.
- Forelimb: scapula, humerus, radius and ulna. Hindlimb: pelvic girdle, femur, tibia and fibula.
- Hinge joints (elbow, knee) move in one plane; ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) move in all directions.
- Cartilage and synovial fluid reduce friction; ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone.
- Muscles only pull, so they work in antagonistic pairs: the biceps (flexor) bends the elbow and the triceps (extensor) straightens it.
:::
