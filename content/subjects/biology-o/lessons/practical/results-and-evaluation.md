## Introduction

Collecting results is only half of an investigation. You must also record them clearly, process them, present them in a graph, decide what they show and judge how far they can be trusted. In the practical papers (Cambridge Paper 5 or 6, ZIMSEC Paper 3) many marks are for exactly these skills, and they are easy marks once you know the rules.

This lesson covers results tables, graphs, the common calculations (mean, percentage change, rate), reading values from graphs, anomalous results, conclusions, evaluation, sampling and biological drawings. You should already know how to plan an investigation and identify its variables.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anomalous result"}
A result that does not fit the pattern of the other results, usually because of a mistake in measuring or in carrying out the method.
:::

:::definition{term="Mean"}
The sum of the repeat readings divided by the number of readings. Anomalous results are left out.
:::

:::definition{term="Reliable results"}
Results that are similar when the investigation is repeated.
:::

:::definition{term="Accurate result"}
A result that is close to the true value.
:::

:::definition{term="Interpolation"}
Reading a value from a graph between the points that were actually plotted.
:::

## Results tables

A good results table:

- has ruled lines and a column (or row) for each quantity;
- has the **independent variable in the first column**;
- gives each heading as the **quantity and its unit**, for example "time / s" or "mass / g";
- has **no units in the body** of the table, only numbers;
- records readings to a **consistent number of decimal places** that matches the instrument. A balance that reads to 0.01 g gives 2.50 g, not 2.5 g.

| 30 | 96 | 99 | 93 | 96 |

## Choosing a graph

| Type of data | Graph | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Both variables continuous (numbers on a scale) | Line graph | Rate of an enzyme reaction at different temperatures |
| Independent variable in separate categories | Bar chart, with gaps between bars | Mean protein content of four different foods |
| Continuous data grouped into classes, showing how many fall in each | Histogram, with bars touching | Number of leaves in each length class: 20–29 mm, 30–39 mm, 40–49 mm |

### Drawing a line graph

1. Put the independent variable on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis.
2. Label each axis with the quantity and unit, for example "temperature / °C".
3. Choose an even (linear) scale that uses more than half of the grid in each direction. Avoid awkward scales such as 3 or 7 units per square.
4. Plot each point exactly, as a small cross or a dot in a circle.
5. Join the points with ruled straight lines from point to point, or draw a smooth line of best fit if asked. Do not extend the line beyond your data unless the question asks you to.

## Calculations

:::worked-solution[Percentage change in mass]
A strip of sweet potato has a mass of 2.50 g at the start and 2.15 g after an hour in a sucrose solution.
1. Change in mass $= 2.15 - 2.50 = -0.35\ \text{g}$ (negative because mass was lost).
2. Percentage change $= \frac{\text{change}}{\text{starting value}} \times 100 = \frac{-0.35}{2.50} \times 100$.
3. Percentage change $= -14.0\%$. Always divide by the **starting** value, and keep the minus sign for a decrease.
:::

:::worked-solution[A mean with an anomalous result]
Four repeat times for starch to disappear at 30 °C were 42 s, 45 s, 71 s and 43 s.
1. 71 s is far from the other three readings, so it is anomalous and is left out.
2. Mean $= \frac{42 + 45 + 43}{3} = \frac{130}{3} = 43.3\ \text{s}$.
3. The readings were whole seconds, so give the mean as $43\ \text{s}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Turning times into rates]
In many enzyme experiments you measure the **time** taken for something to happen. A shorter time means a faster reaction, so the rate is calculated as $\frac{1}{\text{time}}$.
1. For a time of 125 s: rate $= \frac{1}{125} = 0.008\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
2. These numbers are small, so the rate is often given as $\frac{1000}{\text{time}}$: $\frac{1000}{125} = 8.0$.
3. Times of 250 s, 125 s, 80 s and 50 s give rates (×1000) of 4.0, 8.0, 12.5 and 20.0. Halving the time doubles the rate.
:::

Other calculations you may meet:

- **Percentage:** 18 seeds germinated out of 20, so $\frac{18}{20} \times 100 = 90\%$ germinated.
- **Magnification of a drawing:** length of the drawing ÷ actual length of the specimen, measured in the same units. A leaf drawn 96 mm long that is really 48 mm long is drawn at ×2.

## Reading values from a graph

To find a value between your points, draw a ruled vertical line up from the x-axis to your plotted line, then a horizontal line across to the y-axis, and read the value. Leave these construction lines on the graph to show your method.

:::example{title="Interpolation"}
On a graph of mean seedling height against time, the points for day 4 (12 mm) and day 6 (28 mm) are joined by a ruled line. Reading up from day 5 to the line and across gives a height of 20 mm, halfway between the two points.
:::

## Anomalous results

Circle or mark an anomalous result, leave it out when you calculate the mean, and repeat that test if you can. If asked, suggest a reason, such as a timing error, a wrongly measured volume or a change in temperature.

## Conclusions

A conclusion says what the results show, using the data:

- Describe the trend: "As the temperature increased from 10 °C to 40 °C, the time for the starch to disappear decreased from 480 s to 60 s."
- Quote values, including any peak or change in pattern.
- Explain it using biology if asked, and say whether it supports the prediction.
- Do not claim more than the data show: one investigation on one enzyme cannot prove what happens to all enzymes.

## Evaluating an investigation

Identify **sources of error** and suggest a **specific improvement** for each.

| Source of error | Improvement |
| --- | --- |
| Hard to judge exactly when the iodine stops turning blue-black | Compare each test with a colour standard, or use a colorimeter |
| The temperature of the tubes changed during the test | Use a thermostatically controlled water bath |
| Volumes measured roughly with a beaker | Use a syringe or pipette |
| Only one reading at each value | Repeat at least three times and calculate a mean |
| Values too far apart to find the optimum | Add more values close to the peak |

## Sampling

When you cannot count every organism, you take samples. To make them **representative**, place quadrats at **random**, for example at coordinates chosen with a random number generator on a grid marked out with tape measures, so that you do not choose places that look interesting. Take many samples; the more you take, the more reliable the mean.

## Biological drawings

Use a sharp pencil and clear, continuous lines; do not shade. Make the drawing large, with the parts in the right proportions. Rule label lines that touch the structure, do not cross each other and end outside the drawing. Add a title and, if asked, the magnification.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Units in the body of the table"}
Write "mass / g" in the heading and only numbers in the cells. "2.50 g" in every cell loses the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dividing by the wrong value"}
Percentage change is divided by the **starting** value, not the final one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including anomalous results in the mean"}
An anomalous result drags the mean away from the true value. Leave it out and say so.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague evaluation"}
"Human error" or "do it more carefully" earns nothing. Name the error and a specific improvement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a "describe the results" answer, always quote numbers from the table or graph, with units. Describe any change in the pattern, such as a peak.
:::

:::exam-tip
Check that your graph uses more than half the grid, that both axes are labelled with units, and that every point is plotted exactly. These are separate marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
Give calculated values to a sensible precision, usually the same number of decimal places as the raw data or one more, unless the question says otherwise.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tables: independent variable first, headings with quantity and unit, numbers only in the body, consistent decimal places.
- Line graphs for continuous data, bar charts for categories, histograms for grouped continuous data.
- Percentage change = change ÷ starting value × 100; rate = 1 ÷ time; leave anomalies out of means.
- Conclusions describe the trend with data; evaluations name a source of error and a specific improvement.
- Sample at random for representative results, and draw specimens with clear lines, ruled labels and no shading.
:::
