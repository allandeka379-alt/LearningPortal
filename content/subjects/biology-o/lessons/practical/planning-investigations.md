## Introduction

Biology is an experimental science: every fact in your textbook was once a question that somebody answered by investigation. Your practical examination (Cambridge Paper 5 or Paper 6, or ZIMSEC Paper 3) tests whether you can do the same. A common question gives you a situation you may never have seen, such as maize grains germinating at different temperatures or strips of sweet potato in sugar solutions, and asks you to **plan** an investigation.

A good plan is a fair test. It changes one thing, measures its effect, keeps everything else the same, and is repeated so that the results can be trusted. You should already know how to use common apparatus such as measuring cylinders, syringes, thermometers, balances, water baths and stop-clocks.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Independent variable"}
The factor that you deliberately change, for example temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Dependent variable"}
The factor that you measure to see the effect of the change, for example the number of grains that germinate.
:::

:::definition{term="Controlled variables"}
All the other factors that could affect the dependent variable. They are kept the same so that the test is fair.
:::

:::definition{term="Control"}
A set-up that is the same as the test except that the factor being investigated is absent or made inactive (for example boiled enzyme). It shows that any change in the test is caused by that factor.
:::

:::definition{term="Repeats"}
Carrying out the test more than once (usually at least three times) for each value of the independent variable, so that anomalous results can be spotted and a mean calculated. Results that agree closely when repeated are reliable.
:::

:::definition{term="Prediction"}
A statement of what you expect to happen, with a reason, such as "as the temperature rises from 10 °C to 30 °C, more grains will germinate, because the enzymes in the grains work faster".
:::

## Identifying the variables

:::example{title="Maize grains at different temperatures"}
Question: how does temperature affect the germination of maize grains?

- **Independent variable:** temperature, for example 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35 °C, each set in an incubator.
- **Dependent variable:** the number of grains (out of 20) with a radicle showing after 5 days. Converting this to a percentage makes dishes easy to compare.
- **Controlled variables:** the number of grains in each dish, the variety and age of the grains, the volume of water added to the cotton wool, light or darkness, and the time allowed.

Here each temperature is compared with the others. A temperature cannot be "absent", so this investigation does not need a separate control.
:::

## Choosing the range and the values

Use **at least five** values of the independent variable, spread evenly over a range wide enough to show the pattern. If you expect an optimum, choose values on both sides of it. Temperatures of 20, 21 and 22 °C are far too close together to show a trend; 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C would show how an enzyme behaves below, at and above its optimum.

Solutions of different concentrations are usually made by diluting a **stock solution** with distilled water. To make 20 cm³ of each sucrose solution from a 1.0 mol/dm³ stock:

| Concentration / mol per dm³ | Volume of stock / cm³ | Volume of distilled water / cm³ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 | 0 | 20 |
| 0.2 | 4 | 16 |
| 0.4 | 8 | 12 |
| 0.6 | 12 | 8 |
| 0.8 | 16 | 4 |
| 1.0 | 20 | 0 |

The volume of stock is the required concentration divided by the stock concentration, multiplied by the total volume: for 0.6 mol/dm³, $\frac{0.6}{1.0} \times 20 = 12\ \text{cm}^3$.

## Keeping variables constant

Saying "keep the temperature the same" is not enough: say **how**.

| Variable | How to keep it constant |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | A thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator; check it with a thermometer |
| Volume of a liquid | Measure it with a syringe, pipette or measuring cylinder |
| Size of plant tissue | Cut it with the same cork borer and trim it to the same length with a scalpel and ruler |
| Mass | Weigh it on a balance |
| pH | Add a buffer solution |
| Light intensity | Use the same lamp at the same distance |
| Time | Time each test with a stop-clock |
| Type of organism | Use the same species and variety, from the same batch |

## Controls

Some investigations need a control to prove that the factor you are testing causes the result.

- Enzyme investigations: repeat with **boiled** enzyme. If nothing happens, the change in the test was caused by the active enzyme.
- Yeast respiration: use boiled (dead) yeast to show that living yeast produces the carbon dioxide.
- Food tests: test **distilled water** with the reagent to show the colour of a negative result.

## Measuring the dependent variable

Measure, count or time whenever you can, and choose an instrument with suitable precision. A gas syringe that reads the volume of oxygen to 1 cm³ gives better results than counting bubbles of different sizes. State exactly what you will measure and when: "the time taken until a drop of the mixture no longer turns iodine solution blue-black, testing every 30 seconds".

## Writing the method

:::example{title="A plan: sweet potato strips in sucrose solutions"}
Aim: to find how the concentration of sucrose solution affects the mass of sweet potato tissue.

1. Make 20 cm³ of each sucrose solution (0.0 to 1.0 mol/dm³) as in the table above, in labelled boiling tubes. Set up three tubes of each concentration.
2. Use a cork borer to cut 18 cylinders from one sweet potato. On a tile, trim each to 40 mm with a scalpel, cutting away from your fingers.
3. Blot each cylinder gently with paper towel, weigh it on a balance to 0.01 g and record its mass.
4. Put one cylinder into each tube and start the stop-clock. Keep all the tubes at the same temperature, for example in a water bath at 25 °C.
5. After 60 minutes, remove each cylinder, blot it in the same way and reweigh it.
6. Calculate the percentage change in mass of each cylinder, then the mean for each concentration (leaving out any anomalous result).
7. Plot the mean percentage change in mass against the concentration of sucrose.

The **percentage** change is used because the cylinders do not all start with exactly the same mass, so their changes must be compared fairly. Blotting removes surface solution, which would add to the mass without having entered the cells.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Swapping the independent and dependent variables"}
The independent variable is the one you choose and set. The dependent variable is the one you measure. In "the effect of temperature on enzyme activity", temperature is independent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague controlled variables"}
"Use the same amount" earns little. Write "add 5 cm³ of starch solution, measured with a syringe".
:::

:::mistake{title="Too few values"}
Two or three values of the independent variable cannot show a trend or an optimum. Use at least five.
:::

:::mistake{title="Changing two things at once"}
If two tubes differ in both temperature and volume, you cannot tell which caused the difference. Change only the independent variable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying repeats make results accurate"}
Repeats let you spot anomalies and calculate a mean, which makes results more reliable. Accuracy (closeness to the true value) depends on good technique and suitable instruments.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Plan answers are marked point by point. Cover each heading: what you change (with actual values), what you measure and how, at least two controlled variables and how you keep them constant, a control if needed, repeats, a safety precaution, and how you will process the results.
:::

:::exam-tip
Quote numbers and apparatus: "at 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C in water baths", "10 cm³ measured with a syringe". Specific details earn marks; general statements do not.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question asks you to "suggest why" a step was done, link it to fairness ("so that all tubes start at the same temperature") or accuracy ("to remove surface liquid that would add to the mass").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Change one independent variable, measure the dependent variable and keep the controlled variables constant, saying how.
- Use at least five values of the independent variable over a sensible range; make concentrations by diluting a stock solution.
- Include a control (for example boiled enzyme) when you must show that the factor being tested causes the result.
- Repeat each test at least three times, leave out anomalies and calculate a mean.
- Write a numbered method with quantities, apparatus, timings, safety precautions and how the results will be recorded and processed.
:::
