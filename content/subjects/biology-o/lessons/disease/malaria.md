## Introduction

Nyasha's family from Harare spends the April school holiday with relatives at Kariba. They do not sleep under mosquito nets. Two weeks after they get home, Nyasha has a fever, chills and a bad headache. At the clinic, a finger-prick test shows **malaria**. Because there is very little malaria in Harare, the nurse's first question is: "Where have you travelled recently?"

Malaria is one of the most important diseases in Africa, and in Zimbabwe it is common in hot, low-lying parts of the country. In this lesson you will learn what causes it, how it is passed on, how it is treated and how it can be prevented.

You should already know the meaning of pathogen and vector (see the lesson on pathogens).

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
This lesson covers malaria for ZIMSEC topic 8.11.2: cause, transmission, symptoms, prevention and control. Chloroquine, which older textbooks name as the treatment, is no longer recommended for the main malaria parasite because of drug resistance.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Malaria"}
An infectious disease caused by the protoctist parasite *Plasmodium*, passed between people by the bites of female *Anopheles* mosquitoes.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another. The female *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector of malaria.
:::

:::definition{term="Drug resistance"}
The ability of a pathogen to survive a medicine that used to kill it.
:::

## Cause and vector

Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium*, a single-celled **protoctist** with a nucleus. In Zimbabwe, most malaria is caused by *Plasmodium falciparum*, the species that causes the most dangerous form of the disease.

Only **female** *Anopheles* mosquitoes feed on blood: they need its protein to produce their eggs. Males feed on plant juices such as nectar. *Anopheles* mosquitoes bite mainly between dusk and dawn. You cannot catch malaria by touching or sitting near a sick person; the parasite must be carried by a mosquito (or, rarely, passed in blood, for example from a mother to her baby).

## The life cycle in outline

:::example{title="Example 1: from mosquito to person and back"}
1. An infected female *Anopheles* mosquito bites a person and injects saliva containing *Plasmodium*.
2. The parasites travel in the blood to the **liver**, where they multiply.
3. They return to the blood and invade **red blood cells**, where they multiply again.
4. The red blood cells **burst**, releasing more parasites, which invade more red blood cells. Each round of bursting brings on a bout of fever.
5. Some parasites develop into sexual forms that stay in the blood.
6. Another female mosquito bites the infected person and sucks up these forms with the blood.
7. The parasites reproduce inside the mosquito and move to its **salivary glands**. The mosquito can now infect the next person it bites.
:::

## Symptoms and diagnosis

Symptoms usually start about **10 to 15 days** after the infective bite:

- **Fever** with **chills and shivering**, then heavy sweating, often coming in bouts
- Headache, muscle and joint pains, tiredness and vomiting
- **Anaemia**, because so many red blood cells are destroyed

Severe malaria can cause confusion, fits, breathing problems and coma, and it can kill. **Young children** and **pregnant women** are at greatest risk. Anyone with a fever in a malaria area, or after visiting one, should be **tested** quickly. A **rapid diagnostic test** uses a drop of blood from a finger prick; malaria parasites can also be seen in blood under a microscope.

## Treatment and drug resistance

Uncomplicated malaria is treated with **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)**: two drugs that work in different ways, such as artemether combined with lumefantrine, given by a health worker after a positive test. Severe malaria is treated in hospital with injections. Early treatment cures the patient and also leaves fewer parasites in human blood for mosquitoes to pick up.

**Chloroquine** was once the main malaria drug, but *Plasmodium falciparum* has become **resistant** to it, so it is no longer recommended for this parasite.

:::example{title="Example 2: how resistance develops"}
1. In a population of *Plasmodium* there is **variation**. By chance mutation, a few parasites are resistant to a drug.
2. When the drug is used, the non-resistant parasites are killed, but the resistant ones **survive**.
3. The survivors **reproduce** and pass on resistance; mosquitoes carry them to new people.
4. Over many years, most parasites in the area become resistant and the drug stops working.

This is **natural selection**. Combining two drugs makes resistance much less likely, because a parasite would need to resist both at once. Taking incomplete courses or fake medicines from unlicensed sellers speeds resistance up.
:::

## Prevention and control

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Insecticide-treated nets | Sleeping under a net every night stops mosquitoes biting; the insecticide kills mosquitoes that land on it |
| Indoor residual spraying | Long-lasting insecticide is sprayed on inside walls before the rainy season; mosquitoes resting there after feeding are killed |
| Removing breeding sites | Draining puddles, clearing old tins and tyres and covering water containers removes the still water where larvae develop |
| Killing larvae | Treating ponds and dams, or stocking them with fish that eat larvae |
| Personal protection | Repellents, long sleeves and trousers after dark, and screens on windows and doors |
| Preventive medicines | Prescribed by health workers for some travellers and for pregnant women in malaria areas |
| Early testing and treatment | Cures patients and reduces the number of parasites available to infect mosquitoes |

## Where and when the risk is highest

Malaria is most common in **hot, low-lying, wet** areas: the **Zambezi Valley** (around Kariba, Binga and Mbire), the **Lowveld** (around Chiredzi and Beitbridge) and the **eastern border districts** such as Mutasa and Chipinge. High, cooler towns such as Harare and Bulawayo have very little.

- **Warmth**: mosquitoes breed faster, and *Plasmodium* develops faster inside the mosquito, when it is warm. In cool highlands the parasite develops too slowly to be passed on easily.
- **Rain**: the rainy season, roughly November to April, leaves pools of still water where mosquitoes lay eggs. Cases usually rise to a peak a few weeks **after** the heaviest rain, because the larvae must develop into adults and the parasite needs time to develop in the mosquito and then in the person.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying malaria is caused by mosquitoes"}
Malaria is caused by the protoctist ***Plasmodium***. The mosquito is the **vector** that carries it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all mosquitoes spread malaria"}
Only **female *Anopheles*** mosquitoes carry the parasite, because only females feed on blood.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying malaria is caused by a bacterium or treated with antibiotics"}
*Plasmodium* is a protoctist. Malaria is treated with **antimalarial** drugs such as ACT.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying resistance develops because the parasites 'get used to' the drug"}
Individual parasites do not change. Resistant parasites that already existed **survive and reproduce**, so their numbers increase.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In life-cycle questions, name the stages in order: bite and injection, liver, red blood cells, bursting and fever, uptake by another mosquito, development in the mosquito.
:::

:::exam-tip
For control measures, always say **how** each one works: "insecticide-treated nets stop mosquitoes biting at night and kill mosquitoes that land on them".
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain drug resistance, use the words variation, survive, reproduce and pass on.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Malaria is caused by the protoctist *Plasmodium* (mainly *P. falciparum* in Zimbabwe) and carried by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes, the vector.
- Parasites multiply in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst, causing bouts of fever, chills and anaemia; severe malaria can kill.
- Diagnosis is by a rapid blood test; treatment is with ACT. Chloroquine is no longer recommended because the parasite has become resistant.
- Prevention: insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, removing breeding sites, repellents, preventive medicines and early treatment.
- Risk is highest in hot, low-lying areas (Zambezi Valley, Lowveld, eastern border districts) during and after the rainy season.
:::
