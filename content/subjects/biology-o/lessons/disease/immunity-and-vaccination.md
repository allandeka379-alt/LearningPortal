## Introduction

At a clinic in Chitungwiza, a nurse gives a baby an injection and records it on the child's health card. A few months later the same baby is exposed to a pathogen at a crowded bus terminus and never becomes ill. In Binga, a boy bitten by a puff adder is rushed to hospital, where he is given **antivenom**. Both are protected by **antibodies**, but in very different ways.

In this lesson you will learn how the body recognises pathogens, how it remembers them, why vaccines work, and how ready-made antibodies can protect someone immediately. You should already know the body's first lines of defence (see the lesson on pathogens).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antigen"}
A molecule, usually a protein, on the surface of a pathogen (or other cell) that the body recognises as foreign. Each type of pathogen has antigens with specific shapes.
:::

:::definition{term="Antibody"}
A protein made by lymphocytes that has a shape complementary to one specific antigen and binds to it.
:::

:::definition{term="Active immunity"}
Defence against a pathogen by antibody production in the body itself. It follows infection or vaccination and is long-term, because memory cells are made.
:::

:::definition{term="Passive immunity"}
Short-term defence against a pathogen by antibodies acquired from another individual, for example across the placenta, in breast milk or by injection. No memory cells are made.
:::

:::definition{term="Vaccine"}
A preparation containing weakened or dead pathogens, or just their antigens, that stimulates the body to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease.
:::

## The body's defences (ZIMSEC)

- **Skin**: a tough barrier; pathogens enter only through cuts and other damage.
- **Tears**: wash the eyes and contain an enzyme (lysozyme) that kills bacteria.
- **Blood clotting**: seals cuts quickly, so pathogens cannot enter.
- **White blood cells**: **phagocytes** engulf and digest pathogens; **lymphocytes** make antibodies.

## Antigens and antibodies

Each lymphocyte can make one kind of antibody. When a pathogen enters the body, only the lymphocytes whose antibodies are **complementary** in shape to its antigens respond. They divide many times and release large numbers of antibodies. The antibodies:

- bind to the antigens and destroy the pathogen directly, or
- clump pathogens together and mark them, so that phagocytes can find, engulf and digest them;
- some (antitoxins) bind to and neutralise the toxins that pathogens release.

Because the shapes must match, antibodies against the measles virus are useless against the polio virus. Each response is **specific**.

## Active immunity and memory cells

During the first infection with a pathogen, the response is slow: it takes days for enough antibodies to be made, so the person becomes ill. As well as making antibodies, some of the lymphocytes become **memory cells**, which stay in the blood for years.

If the same pathogen enters again, the memory cells recognise it at once. Antibodies are made **faster** and in **much larger amounts**, so the pathogen is destroyed before it can cause symptoms. The person is now **immune**.

:::example{title="Example 1: first and second responses"}
A graph of antibody concentration against time for two infections with the same pathogen shows two peaks.

1. After the **first** infection, the concentration rises slowly over about two weeks to a low peak, then falls. The person is ill for several days.
2. After the **second** infection, the concentration rises within a few days to a peak many times higher, and stays high for longer. The person does not become ill.
3. The difference is caused by the **memory cells** made during the first response.
:::

### How vaccination works

1. The vaccine introduces **antigens**, on weakened or dead pathogens or on their harmless parts. Most vaccines are injected; a few, such as oral polio vaccine, are swallowed as drops.
2. Lymphocytes with complementary antibodies are stimulated; they divide and make **antibodies**.
3. **Memory cells** are produced and remain in the body.
4. If the real pathogen enters later, the memory cells make antibodies so quickly that the pathogen is destroyed before the person becomes ill.

Some vaccines need **booster** doses, which produce more memory cells and keep immunity strong. Gaining immunity by catching a disease is **natural active immunity**; gaining it from a vaccine is **artificial active immunity**.

## How vaccination controls the spread of disease

When most people in a community are vaccinated, a pathogen meets very few people it can infect. Each infected person passes it on to fewer others, so the chains of transmission break and outbreaks die out. This also protects people who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborn babies, which is sometimes called **herd immunity**. Vaccination programmes made it possible to **eradicate smallpox** from the whole world.

## Passive immunity

| Source of the antibodies | Type (ZIMSEC term) |
| --- | --- |
| From the mother across the **placenta** before birth | Natural passive |
| In **breast milk**, especially the first milk (colostrum) | Natural passive |
| Injection of an **antiserum** containing ready-made antibodies | Artificial passive |

Passive immunity acts **immediately**, but it is **short-lived**: the antibodies are gradually broken down, and because the person's own lymphocytes were not stimulated, **no memory cells** are made. Breastfeeding helps to protect babies during the first months, before their own immune systems are fully working.

:::example{title="Example 2: antivenom for a snake bite"}
Antivenom is made by injecting small, harmless doses of snake venom into animals such as horses and collecting the antibodies they produce. A person bitten by a puff adder needs protection **now**: a vaccine would take days or weeks to produce antibodies. Antivenom supplies ready-made antibodies that bind to the venom and neutralise it at once. The protection fades within weeks, but by then the venom is gone.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a bite from a dog that may have rabies"}
After a bite from a dog suspected of having rabies, the wound is washed thoroughly and the person is started on a course of **rabies vaccine** (artificial active immunity). For serious bites, an injection of rabies antibodies (**immunoglobulin**) is given as well. The antibodies protect immediately (artificial passive immunity) while the vaccine builds long-lasting memory cells.
:::

| Feature | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who makes the antibodies? | The person's own lymphocytes | Another individual (or an animal) |
| Memory cells? | Yes | No |
| Speed of protection | Takes days to weeks | Immediate |
| How long it lasts | Long-term, often years | Short-term, weeks to months |

## The Expanded Programme on Immunisation (ZIMSEC)

The **Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI)** gives babies and young children free vaccines at clinics and through outreach visits, following a schedule recorded on the child health card. Among the diseases it protects against are **tuberculosis** (the BCG vaccine, given soon after birth), **polio**, **diphtheria**, **whooping cough**, **tetanus** and **measles**. Immunisation protects each child, builds community protection, prevents death and disability (such as paralysis from polio) and costs far less than treating the diseases.

## HIV and the immune system (ZIMSEC)

HIV infects and destroys a type of **lymphocyte**. As the number of these lymphocytes falls, the body makes fewer antibodies and cannot fight other pathogens. People with untreated HIV therefore suffer from **opportunistic infections** such as tuberculosis, pneumonia and thrush, and may develop AIDS. Antiretroviral treatment keeps the virus under control, so the lymphocyte numbers recover.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling antibodies cells"}
Antibodies are **proteins**. They are made by lymphocytes, which are cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying vaccines contain antibodies"}
Vaccines contain **antigens** (weakened or dead pathogens, or their parts). Ready-made antibodies are given in an antiserum, which gives passive immunity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying passive immunity makes memory cells"}
In passive immunity the person's own lymphocytes are not stimulated, so **no memory cells** are made and the protection is short-term.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up antibodies and antibiotics"}
Antibodies are proteins made by your body. Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria.
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Antigens on pathogens have specific shapes; lymphocytes make antibodies with complementary shapes that bind to them and lead to the pathogens' destruction.
- Active immunity follows infection or vaccination: the body makes its own antibodies and memory cells, giving long-term protection.
- Vaccines contain weakened or dead pathogens or their antigens; vaccinating most people breaks chains of transmission and protects others too.
- Passive immunity uses antibodies from another individual (placenta, breast milk, antiserum): immediate but short-term, with no memory cells.
- ZIMSEC: defences include skin, tears, clotting and white cells; the EPI protects children against diseases such as TB, polio and measles; HIV destroys lymphocytes, weakening the immune system.
:::
