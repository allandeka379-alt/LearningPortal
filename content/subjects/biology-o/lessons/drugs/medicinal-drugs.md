## Introduction

Tendai has a blocked nose, a sore throat and a cough. He asks the nurse at the clinic for antibiotics "to make it go away faster". She explains that his cold is caused by a **virus**, that antibiotics will not help, and that taking them when they are not needed helps to create **resistant bacteria**. In this lesson you will learn why she is right.

You will learn what drugs are, how antibiotics work and why they fail against viruses, how bacteria become resistant, and how medicines should be used safely.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Both boards: what a drug is, antibiotics and bacterial infections, why antibiotics do not affect viruses, and antibiotic resistance. **Cambridge Supplement**: MRSA and how using antibiotics only when essential limits resistance. **ZIMSEC**: analgesics, antimalarials and the safe use of medicines.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Drug"}
Any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Medicinal drug"}
A drug used to prevent, treat or cure a disease, or to relieve its symptoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Antibiotic"}
A medicinal drug that kills bacteria or stops them growing, without harming the patient's own cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Antibiotic resistance"}
The ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them.
:::

## Antibiotics

The first antibiotic, **penicillin**, comes from the mould *Penicillium*. In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that bacteria did not grow on the part of a culture plate around a patch of this mould. Penicillin was developed into a medicine in the 1940s and has saved millions of lives.

Antibiotics work by damaging structures or processes that **bacterial cells** have but human cells do not. Penicillin, for example, stops bacteria building their **cell walls**, so they burst.

Antibiotics are used to treat bacterial infections such as tuberculosis, typhoid, bacterial pneumonia, infected wounds and some sexually transmitted infections, such as syphilis and gonorrhoea.

### Why antibiotics do not work against viruses

Viruses are not cells. They have **no cell wall** and none of the processes that antibiotics attack, and they reproduce **inside the host's own cells**. So antibiotics have no effect on viral infections such as colds, influenza, measles, HIV or COVID-19. Different drugs, called **antivirals**, are used against some viruses; for example, antiretroviral drugs control HIV.

:::example{title="Example 1: two patients at a clinic"}
Rudo has a cold: a runny nose, sneezing and a mild sore throat. It is caused by a virus, so the nurse advises rest and fluids, and no antibiotics. Chipo has a deep cut on her foot that has become hot, swollen and full of pus: a **bacterial** infection. The nurse cleans the wound, and a course of antibiotics is prescribed.
:::

## Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotic resistance develops by **natural selection**:

1. In a large population of bacteria there is variation. By chance **mutation**, a few bacteria are **resistant** to an antibiotic.
2. When the antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed, but the resistant ones **survive**.
3. The survivors **reproduce** rapidly, passing on resistance.
4. Soon most of the population is resistant, and the antibiotic **no longer works** against it.

The more antibiotics are used, the more often resistant bacteria are selected. Resistance is encouraged by taking antibiotics for viral infections, not taking them as prescribed, sharing or keeping left-over antibiotics, buying them without a prescription and giving them routinely to farm animals to make them grow faster.

### MRSA (Cambridge Supplement)

**MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) is a strain of a common skin bacterium that is resistant to many antibiotics. MRSA infections, often picked up in hospitals, are very difficult to treat. To limit the development of resistant bacteria such as MRSA:

- use antibiotics **only when essential**, for serious bacterial infections;
- take them **exactly as prescribed**, never sharing them or keeping some for later;
- do not use them routinely in animal feed;
- keep hospitals clean and **wash hands** so that resistant bacteria do not spread between patients.

:::example{title="Example 2: an antibiotic sensitivity test"}
Bacteria from a patient's infected wound are spread over a nutrient agar plate. Paper discs soaked in four different antibiotics are placed on the agar, and the plate is incubated. A clear zone forms around a disc wherever the antibiotic has stopped the bacteria growing. The antibiotic with the **largest clear zone** is the most effective against these bacteria; an antibiotic with **no clear zone** is one the bacteria are resistant to. Doctors use tests like this to choose the right antibiotic.
:::

## Other medicinal drugs (ZIMSEC)

| Type of drug | What it does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Analgesic (painkiller) | Relieves pain; some also lower fever. It treats the symptom, not the cause | Paracetamol, aspirin |
| Antimalarial | Kills *Plasmodium* in the blood, or prevents infection | Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) |
| Antibiotic | Kills bacteria or stops them growing | Penicillin |
| Antiretroviral (ARV) | Stops HIV multiplying, so the immune system can recover | Antiretroviral therapy (ART), taken every day |

Analgesics are useful, but they are still drugs: taking more paracetamol than the recommended dose can seriously damage the **liver**, and aspirin can irritate the stomach lining.

## Using medicines safely (ZIMSEC)

- Take medicines only as directed by a doctor, nurse or pharmacist, and follow the instructions on the label.
- Never share prescription medicines or take medicines prescribed for someone else.
- Check the **expiry date**, and store medicines in a cool, dry place out of reach of children.
- Do not buy medicines from unlicensed street sellers: they may be fake, out of date or the wrong strength.
- Tell a health worker about any side effects and about other medicines you are taking.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying antibiotics kill viruses"}
Antibiotics work only against **bacteria**. Colds, flu and HIV are caused by viruses.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying people become resistant to antibiotics"}
It is the **bacteria** that become resistant, not the patient.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying bacteria 'get used to' antibiotics"}
Resistant bacteria arise by chance **mutation** and are then **selected**: they survive and reproduce while others die.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a painkiller cures an infection"}
Analgesics relieve pain but do nothing to the pathogen causing it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A drug is any substance taken into the body that modifies or affects chemical reactions in the body.
- Antibiotics, such as penicillin, kill bacteria or stop them growing; they have no effect on viruses.
- Resistant bacteria arise by mutation and are selected when antibiotics are used, so overuse makes antibiotics less effective.
- Supplement: MRSA is resistant to many antibiotics; using antibiotics only when essential limits resistance.
- ZIMSEC: analgesics relieve pain, antimalarials kill Plasmodium, and medicines must be used safely, as directed.
:::
