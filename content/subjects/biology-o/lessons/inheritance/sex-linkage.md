## Introduction

In an eye test, a page of coloured dots hides a number that most people see at once. A few people, mostly boys and men, cannot see it, because they cannot easily tell red from green. This is **red-green colour blindness**. Why is it so much more common in males?

The answer lies in where the gene is: on the **X chromosome**. This lesson is **Supplement** content for Cambridge candidates (topic 17.4). You should already know that females are XX and males XY, and how to draw a genetic diagram for a monohybrid cross.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sex-linked characteristic"}
A characteristic controlled by a gene on a sex chromosome, which makes it more common in one sex than in the other.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrier"}
A heterozygous female who has one recessive allele for a sex-linked condition but does not show it. She can pass the allele on to her children.
:::

## Genes on the X chromosome

The X chromosome is large and carries many genes. The Y chromosome is much smaller and carries only a few genes, mostly to do with being male. The gene for red-green colour vision is on the X chromosome, and **there is no copy of it on the Y**.

- A **female** (XX) has **two** copies of the gene, one on each X.
- A **male** (XY) has **one** copy, on his single X.

We write the alleles as superscripts on the X:

- $\text{X}^\text{B}$ = X chromosome carrying the dominant allele for normal colour vision;
- $\text{X}^\text{b}$ = X chromosome carrying the recessive allele for red-green colour blindness;
- Y = Y chromosome, which carries no allele of this gene.

| Genotype | Phenotype |
| --- | --- |
| $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{B}$ | Female, normal colour vision |
| $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$ | Female, normal colour vision, **carrier** |
| $\text{X}^\text{b}\text{X}^\text{b}$ | Female, colour-blind |
| $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{Y}$ | Male, normal colour vision |
| $\text{X}^\text{b}\text{Y}$ | Male, colour-blind |

A male with one $\text{X}^\text{b}$ is colour-blind, because he has no second X that could carry a dominant allele. A female needs **two** recessive alleles to be colour-blind. This is why the condition is much more common in males.

## Two rules that come from the X and Y

1. **A son always receives his X chromosome from his mother** (his Y comes from his father). So a son's colour vision depends only on his mother's alleles.
2. **A father passes his X chromosome to every daughter** and his Y to every son. So a colour-blind father never passes the condition to his sons, but all his daughters receive his $\text{X}^\text{b}$.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a carrier mother and a father with normal vision]
1. Parents: mother $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$ (carrier) × father $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{Y}$ (normal vision).
2. Gametes: eggs $\text{X}^\text{B}$ or $\text{X}^\text{b}$; sperm $\text{X}^\text{B}$ or Y.
3. Punnett square:

| Gametes | $\text{X}^\text{B}$ (sperm) | Y (sperm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\text{X}^\text{B}$ (egg) | $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{B}$ normal daughter | $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{Y}$ normal son |
| $\text{X}^\text{b}$ (egg) | $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$ carrier daughter | $\text{X}^\text{b}\text{Y}$ colour-blind son |

4. No daughters are colour-blind; half of the daughters are carriers.
5. Half of the sons are colour-blind.
6. For any child: probability of being colour-blind $= \frac{1}{4}$, and that child will be a boy.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a colour-blind father and a mother who is homozygous for normal vision]
1. Parents: mother $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{B}$ × father $\text{X}^\text{b}\text{Y}$.
2. Gametes: eggs all $\text{X}^\text{B}$; sperm $\text{X}^\text{b}$ or Y.
3. Daughters: all $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$, so every daughter has normal vision but is a carrier.
4. Sons: all $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{Y}$, so every son has normal vision: the father's $\text{X}^\text{b}$ never reaches a son.
5. The condition can "skip" a generation: the grandsons of this man, through his carrier daughters, each have a 1 in 2 chance of being colour-blind.
:::

### Can a girl be colour-blind?

Yes, but only if she inherits $\text{X}^\text{b}$ from **both** parents. Her father's only X must carry $\text{X}^\text{b}$, so her father must be colour-blind; her mother must be a carrier or colour-blind herself. Because this needs both conditions together, colour-blind girls are much rarer than colour-blind boys.

## Another example: haemophilia

**Haemophilia** is a condition in which the blood does not clot properly, so cuts and internal bleeding take a long time to stop. It is caused by a recessive allele on the X chromosome, written $\text{X}^\text{h}$, with $\text{X}^\text{H}$ for normal clotting. It is inherited in exactly the same way as colour blindness, so almost everyone with haemophilia is male. Treatment with the missing clotting factor lets people with haemophilia lead active lives.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the allele on the Y chromosome"}
The Y chromosome carries no allele of this gene. Write a male as $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{Y}$ or $\text{X}^\text{b}\text{Y}$, never with a letter on the Y.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing the alleles without the X"}
Write $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$, not Bb. Without the X the diagram does not show that the gene is sex-linked, and the examiner cannot tell males from females.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a man can be a carrier"}
A man has only one X. If it carries $\text{X}^\text{b}$ he is colour-blind; if it carries $\text{X}^\text{B}$ he is not. Only females can be carriers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing 'a child' with 'a son'"}
From a carrier mother and a father with normal vision, the probability that **a child** is colour-blind is $\frac{1}{4}$, but the probability that **a son** is colour-blind is $\frac{1}{2}$. Read which one the question asks for.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Always give a key such as "$\text{X}^\text{B}$ = normal vision, $\text{X}^\text{b}$ = colour blindness", show the gametes (eggs and sperm, with Y as one of the sperm), and label each offspring with both its sex and its phenotype.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain why more males are affected, give two linked points: males have **only one X chromosome**, and the Y has **no allele** of the gene, so a single recessive allele is expressed.
:::

:::exam-tip
In family trees, use the two rules: sons get their X from their mother; daughters get their father's X.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A sex-linked characteristic is controlled by a gene on a sex chromosome, so it is more common in one sex.
- The gene for red-green colour vision is on the X chromosome and not on the Y: write $\text{X}^\text{B}$ (normal) and $\text{X}^\text{b}$ (colour-blind).
- Males with one $\text{X}^\text{b}$ are colour-blind; females need two. A female $\text{X}^\text{B}\text{X}^\text{b}$ is a carrier.
- Sons receive their X from their mother; fathers pass their X to all daughters and their Y to all sons.
- Carrier mother × normal father: half the sons colour-blind, half the daughters carriers, no colour-blind daughters.
- Haemophilia is inherited in the same way.
:::
