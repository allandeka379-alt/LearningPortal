## Introduction

A Mashona cow mated with a Mashona bull gives birth to a Mashona calf, never a goat. A child often has her mother's eyes and her father's height, yet no two brothers or sisters (apart from identical twins) are exactly alike. The instructions that make each organism what it is are passed from parents to offspring in the nucleus of every egg and sperm.

This lesson gives you the vocabulary of genetics. Every later topic, from Punnett squares to natural selection, uses these words, and examiners expect them to be used precisely. You should already know that the nucleus contains DNA and that sexual reproduction involves the fusion of an egg and a sperm.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inheritance"}
The passing on of genetic information from one generation to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Chromosome"}
A thread-like structure in the nucleus, made of DNA, that carries genetic information in the form of genes.
:::

:::definition{term="Gene"}
A length of DNA that codes for a protein.
:::

:::definition{term="Allele"}
An alternative form of a gene. For example, the gene for horns in cattle has an allele for polled (hornless) and an allele for horned.
:::

:::definition{term="Genotype"}
The genetic make-up of an organism in terms of the alleles it has, written with letters such as **Pp**.
:::

:::definition{term="Phenotype"}
The observable features of an organism, such as being polled or horned.
:::

:::definition{term="Homozygous and heterozygous"}
**Homozygous**: having two identical alleles of a particular gene (**PP** or **pp**). **Heterozygous**: having two different alleles of a particular gene (**Pp**).
:::

:::definition{term="Dominant and recessive alleles"}
A **dominant** allele is expressed in the phenotype whenever it is present. A **recessive** allele is expressed only when there is no dominant allele of the gene present.
:::

## Chromosomes, DNA and genes

The nucleus of a cell contains **chromosomes**. Each chromosome is one very long molecule of **DNA**, coiled and packed tightly. Along the DNA are many **genes**, each at its own fixed position on its chromosome.

Each gene is a length of DNA that **codes for one protein**. Proteins, such as enzymes, build the cell and control what happens in it, so they produce the features we can see. For example, one gene codes for an enzyme that helps to make **melanin**, the dark pigment in skin, hair and eyes.

A gene can exist in different forms called **alleles**. Different alleles of the melanin gene give different versions of the enzyme. One allele gives a working enzyme, so melanin is made. Another allele gives an enzyme that does not work, so little or no melanin is made: this is one cause of **albinism**.

:::keyfact{title="From DNA to features"}
Chromosome (made of DNA) → gene (a length of DNA) → protein (for example, an enzyme) → feature (for example, skin colour).
:::

## Chromosomes come in pairs

Human body cells have **46 chromosomes in 23 pairs**. One chromosome of each pair came from the mother's egg and the other from the father's sperm. The two chromosomes of a pair carry the **same genes in the same positions**, but the **alleles may differ**. This is why a body cell has **two alleles of each gene**: one inherited from each parent. (The X and Y chromosomes of a male are the exception: they are different sizes and carry mostly different genes.)

Eggs and sperm (gametes) carry only **one chromosome from each pair**: 23 chromosomes, and so **one allele of each gene**. At fertilisation the two sets combine, and the zygote again has 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs.

### Haploid and diploid nuclei (Supplement)

:::definition{term="Haploid nucleus"}
A nucleus containing a single set of unpaired chromosomes, as in a gamete. In humans the haploid number is 23.
:::

:::definition{term="Diploid nucleus"}
A nucleus containing two sets of chromosomes, in pairs, as in a body cell. In humans the diploid number is 46 (23 pairs).
:::

Other species have different numbers, but the rule is the same: a gamete has **half** the number of chromosomes in a body cell.

:::worked-solution[Example 1 (Supplement): chromosome numbers in maize]
The body cells of a maize plant contain 20 chromosomes. How many chromosomes are in a maize egg cell, how many pairs are in a leaf cell, and how many chromosomes are in the zygote?
1. A body cell is diploid: 20 chromosomes in $20 \div 2 = 10$ pairs.
2. A gamete is haploid, with one chromosome from each pair: $20 \div 2 = 10$ chromosomes.
3. At fertilisation a haploid egg (10) fuses with a haploid male nucleus (10): the zygote has $10 + 10 = 20$ chromosomes.
4. Check: the zygote has the same number as the body cells of the parents, so the number stays the same from one generation to the next.
:::

## Describing alleles: genotype and phenotype

In cattle, the allele for **polled** (no horns) is dominant to the allele for **horned**. We use one letter for the gene: a capital for the dominant allele and the same letter in lower case for the recessive allele.

- **P** = allele for polled (dominant)
- **p** = allele for horned (recessive)

| Genotype | Description | Phenotype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PP | Homozygous dominant | Polled |
| Pp | Heterozygous | Polled |
| pp | Homozygous recessive | Horned |

:::example{title="Example 2: reading genotypes"}
A farmer near Chinhoyi has three bulls. Bull 1 is **PP**, bull 2 is **Pp** and bull 3 is **pp**.

Bulls 1 and 2 look the same: both are polled, because one P allele is enough for the dominant feature to show. They have the **same phenotype** but **different genotypes**. Bull 3 is horned: a recessive feature only shows when **no dominant allele** is present.

Bull 1 makes sperm that all carry P. Bull 2 makes two kinds of sperm, half carrying P and half carrying p. Bull 3's sperm all carry p. Homozygous organisms are **pure-breeding**: they pass on the same allele every time.
:::

Notice that "dominant" does not mean "more common" or "better". It only means that the allele shows in the phenotype of a heterozygote.

## The inheritance of sex

One of the 23 pairs of human chromosomes decides sex. These are the **sex chromosomes**, X and Y. The Y chromosome is much smaller than the X.

- Females are **XX**: every egg carries an **X** chromosome.
- Males are **XY**: half of the sperm carry an **X** and half carry a **Y**.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: the inheritance of sex]
Use a genetic diagram to show why the chance of a baby being a boy is 50%.
1. Parents: mother **XX** × father **XY**.
2. Gametes: the mother's eggs are all X; the father's sperm are X or Y, in equal numbers.
3. Combine the gametes in a Punnett square:

| Egg ↓ / Sperm → | X | Y |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **X** | XX (girl) | XY (boy) |
| **X** | XX (girl) | XY (boy) |

4. Offspring: 2 XX : 2 XY, a ratio of 1 girl : 1 boy.
5. So each baby has a $\frac{1}{2}$ (50%) chance of being a boy and a $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of being a girl.
:::

Two things follow from this diagram:

- **The sperm decides the sex.** Every egg carries X, so whether the baby is a boy or a girl depends on whether the egg is fertilised by an X sperm or a Y sperm. In the past some families blamed a mother for having "only daughters"; biology shows this is a matter of chance, and it is the father's sperm that carries the Y chromosome.
- **Each birth is a separate event.** A couple who already have three daughters still have a 50% chance that their next baby is a boy. The sperm has no memory of earlier births.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up gene and allele"}
A **gene** is a length of DNA coding for a protein (for example, the gene for horns). An **allele** is one version of that gene (polled or horned). Two alleles of the same gene, not two genes, decide whether a cow has horns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the mother decides the sex of a baby"}
Every egg carries an X chromosome. The baby's sex depends on whether the sperm carries X or Y.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a heterozygote shows both features"}
With complete dominance, a heterozygote (**Pp**) shows only the dominant feature. Both features show only in codominance, which you will meet later.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unrelated letters for the alleles"}
Write the alleles of one gene with the same letter, **P** and **p**, not **P** and **h**. Different letters suggest different genes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up haploid and diploid numbers (Supplement)"}
Human gametes are haploid (23 chromosomes); human body cells and zygotes are diploid (46 chromosomes in 23 pairs). A gamete never has 46.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the definitions word for word in meaning: a gene is "a length of DNA that codes for a protein"; an allele is "an alternative form of a gene". Vague answers such as "a gene is a feature" score nothing.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **state** a genotype, write the letters (for example **Pp**); when asked for a phenotype, write the feature in words (for example "polled").
:::

:::exam-tip
In a genetic diagram, label every line: parents' phenotypes, parents' genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes, offspring phenotypes and the ratio. Give a key for any letters you choose.
:::

:::exam-tip
A probability can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: $\frac{1}{2}$, 0.5 and 50% are all correct. Do not write "1 : 1" when a probability is asked for, because a ratio is not a probability.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chromosomes are made of DNA; a gene is a length of DNA that codes for a protein, and alleles are alternative forms of a gene.
- The genotype is the alleles an organism has; the phenotype is its observable features.
- Homozygous means two identical alleles; heterozygous means two different alleles. A dominant allele shows whenever it is present; a recessive allele shows only when no dominant allele is present.
- Females are XX and males XY; every egg carries X and half the sperm carry Y, so each baby has a 50% chance of being a boy.
- Supplement: gametes have haploid nuclei (one set of chromosomes, 23 in humans); body cells have diploid nuclei (two sets, 23 pairs in humans).
:::
