## Introduction

Almost every cell in your body carries the same set of about 20 000 genes. Yet a cell in your pancreas makes insulin, a cell in a salivary gland makes amylase, and a developing red blood cell makes haemoglobin. How does a gene, which is just a length of DNA, produce something useful, and how do different cells end up doing different jobs?

The answer is **proteins**. Genes carry the instructions for making proteins, and proteins do almost all the work in a cell. This lesson is **Supplement** content for Cambridge candidates (topic 17.1). You should already know that DNA is made of two strands with bases A, T, C and G, that proteins are chains of amino acids, and how enzymes work.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gene"}
A length of DNA that codes for a protein.
:::

:::definition{term="Messenger RNA (mRNA)"}
A molecule made in the nucleus as a copy of the base sequence of one gene. It carries this copy out of the nucleus to the ribosomes.
:::

:::definition{term="Ribosome"}
A very small structure in the cytoplasm where amino acids are joined together to make a protein.
:::

:::definition{term="Gene expression"}
A gene is **expressed** when the cell uses it to make its protein. Most genes are expressed only in the cells that need their protein.
:::

## From base sequence to protein shape

A protein is a long chain of **amino acids**. There are about 20 different kinds, and a protein may contain hundreds of them joined in a particular order. Once the chain is made, it folds into a particular **shape**, and the shape decides what the protein can do.

The order of the amino acids is set by the gene:

:::keyfact{title="The chain of control"}
Sequence of **bases** in the gene → sequence of **amino acids** in the protein → **shape** of the protein → **function** of the protein.
:::

Different genes have different base sequences, so they code for different amino acid sequences, which fold into different shapes. This is why an enzyme has an active site that fits only its own substrate, why an antibody fits only one antigen, and why a receptor fits only one signal molecule.

## How DNA controls the cell

DNA controls a cell by controlling **which proteins it makes**. These proteins include:

- **enzymes**, which control almost every reaction in the cell, from respiration to making starch;
- **membrane carrier proteins**, which move substances into and out of cells by active transport, such as the carriers that take up mineral ions in root hair cells;
- **receptors for neurotransmitters**, the proteins on the membrane of a neurone at a synapse that the neurotransmitter binds to;
- other proteins, such as haemoglobin, antibodies and the hormone insulin.

Because enzymes make the cell's other substances, such as melanin pigment, starch and fats, controlling the enzymes means controlling the whole cell.

## Making a protein: an outline

DNA is far too precious to leave the nucleus. Instead, the cell sends out a working copy of just one gene.

1. The gene that codes for the protein **stays in the nucleus**.
2. A molecule of **mRNA** is made in the nucleus as a **copy of the gene**.
3. The mRNA **moves out of the nucleus** into the cytoplasm.
4. The mRNA **passes through a ribosome**.
5. The ribosome **assembles amino acids** into a protein molecule, joining them one at a time.
6. The **specific order** of the amino acids is set by the **sequence of bases in the mRNA**.
7. The finished chain **folds** into the shape of the protein.

Think of the nucleus as a library holding a valuable recipe book that may never be taken out. A cook photocopies one recipe (the mRNA) and takes it to the kitchen (the ribosome), where the ingredients (amino acids) are added in exactly the order the recipe gives. Many photocopies can be made, so many kitchens can cook at once, while the original book stays safe.

The amino acids themselves are not made by the ribosome. In animals they come mainly from digested food carried in the blood; plants make them from sugars and the nitrate ions they absorb.

:::example{title="Example 1: following one instruction in a salivary gland cell"}
A cell in a salivary gland secretes the enzyme amylase.

1. The amylase gene, on a chromosome in the nucleus, is copied as mRNA.
2. The mRNA leaves the nucleus and passes through ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
3. The ribosomes join amino acids in the order set by the mRNA bases, and each chain folds into an amylase molecule with an active site that fits starch.
4. The amylase is secreted into the saliva, where it begins digesting starch in the mouth.

Because one gene can be copied many times, the cell can make large amounts of amylase quickly.
:::

## Not every gene is used in every cell

All the body cells of an organism developed from one zygote by mitosis, so almost all of them contain the **same genes**. But a cell expresses only the genes for the proteins **it needs**; the others are not used.

:::example{title="Example 2: why a skin cell does not make insulin"}
Insulin is made by certain cells in the pancreas. A skin cell from the same person contains exactly the same insulin gene, but in the skin cell this gene is not expressed: no mRNA copies of it are made, so no insulin is made. The skin cell instead expresses genes such as the one for keratin, the tough protein of skin and hair.

In the same way, palisade cells in a leaf express the genes for the enzymes of photosynthesis, while root cells, deep in the dark soil and without chloroplasts, do not.
:::

| Cell | Some genes it expresses | Proteins made |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pancreas cell (in the islets) | Insulin gene | Insulin |
| Salivary gland cell | Amylase gene | Amylase |
| Developing red blood cell (in bone marrow) | Haemoglobin genes | Haemoglobin |
| Root hair cell | Genes for carrier proteins | Carriers for mineral ions |

:::example{title="Example 3: one changed base, one changed protein"}
In one form of albinism, one base in the gene for an enzyme needed to make melanin is different. The mRNA copy carries the same change, so the ribosome may put a different amino acid into the chain. The enzyme folds into a different shape, its active site no longer fits its substrate, and little or no melanin is made. A tiny change in the base sequence can change the function of the protein completely.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the gene or the DNA leaves the nucleus"}
The DNA stays in the nucleus. Only the **mRNA copy** of the gene moves out to the ribosomes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ribosomes make amino acids"}
Ribosomes **join** amino acids together. The amino acids come from digested food (animals) or are made from sugars and nitrate ions (plants).
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking different cells contain different genes"}
Almost all body cells contain the **same** genes. They differ in **which genes are expressed**, so they make different proteins.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing the direction of control"}
The base sequence decides the amino acid sequence, not the other way round. Information flows from DNA to mRNA to protein.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To **outline** protein synthesis, give the steps in order and use the key words: gene stays in the **nucleus**; **mRNA** copy made; mRNA moves to the **cytoplasm**; passes through a **ribosome**; **amino acids** joined in the order set by the **sequence of bases**.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked how a gene controls a feature, build the chain: base sequence → amino acid sequence → shape of protein → function (for example, the shape of an enzyme's active site).
:::

:::exam-tip
"Why do cells make different proteins?" needs two points: the cells contain the **same genes**, but each expresses only the genes for the proteins **it needs**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The sequence of bases in a gene determines the sequence of amino acids in a protein, which determines the protein's shape and so its function.
- DNA controls the cell by controlling the proteins it makes: enzymes, membrane carriers, receptors for neurotransmitters and others.
- The gene stays in the nucleus; an mRNA copy moves to the cytoplasm and passes through a ribosome, which joins amino acids in the order set by the mRNA bases.
- Almost all body cells contain the same genes, but each cell expresses only the genes for the proteins it needs.
:::
