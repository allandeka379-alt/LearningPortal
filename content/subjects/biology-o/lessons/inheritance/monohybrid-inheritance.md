## Introduction

A cattle farmer in Masvingo province wants a herd without horns: polled animals are safer to handle and do not injure each other in a crowded kraal. If she buys a polled bull, will all his calves be polled? Genetics lets her predict the answer before a single calf is born.

In the 1860s an Austrian monk, Gregor Mendel, worked out the basic rules by crossing pea plants and counting thousands of offspring. The same rules apply to cattle, maize and people. A **monohybrid cross** follows the inheritance of **one gene**. You should already know the meanings of gene, allele, genotype, phenotype, dominant, recessive, homozygous and heterozygous.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monohybrid cross"}
A cross that follows the inheritance of a single gene, and so of one characteristic.
:::

:::definition{term="Pure-breeding"}
Homozygous for a gene. A pure-breeding organism passes the same allele to all of its offspring, so crossing two pure-breeding organisms of the same type always gives offspring like the parents.
:::

:::definition{term="F1 and F2 generations"}
The **F1** generation is the offspring of a cross between two parents (the P generation). The **F2** generation is the offspring of crossing F1 individuals with each other.
:::

:::definition{term="Punnett square"}
A grid used to show every possible combination of the gametes from two parents.
:::

:::definition{term="Test cross (Supplement)"}
Crossing an organism that shows the dominant phenotype, but has an unknown genotype, with an organism that is homozygous recessive.
:::

## Setting out a genetic diagram

Examiners give marks for each line of a genetic diagram, so set it out the same way every time:

1. **Key**: state what each letter means (for example R = round, r = wrinkled).
2. **Parents' phenotypes** and **parents' genotypes**.
3. **Gametes**: each gamete carries **one** allele of the gene. Circle them.
4. **Punnett square**: combine every gamete of one parent with every gamete of the other.
5. **Offspring genotypes** and **offspring phenotypes**.
6. **Ratio** (or probability, or percentage) of the phenotypes.

## Crossing pure-breeding parents: the F1 and F2

In peas, the allele for round seeds (**R**) is dominant to the allele for wrinkled seeds (**r**).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: pure-breeding round × pure-breeding wrinkled]
1. Key: R = round seed (dominant); r = wrinkled seed (recessive).
2. Parents: round (**RR**) × wrinkled (**rr**).
3. Gametes: all **R** from the round parent; all **r** from the wrinkled parent.
4. Every offspring receives one R and one r: all the F1 are **Rr**.
5. F1 phenotype: all **round**, because R is dominant. The wrinkled allele is still there, hidden.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: crossing two F1 plants (Rr × Rr)]
1. Parents: round (**Rr**) × round (**Rr**).
2. Gametes: each parent makes R and r gametes in equal numbers.
3. Punnett square:

| Gametes | R | r |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R** | RR (round) | Rr (round) |
| **r** | Rr (round) | rr (wrinkled) |

4. Offspring genotypes: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr.
5. Offspring phenotypes: 3 round : 1 wrinkled.
6. Probability that one F2 seed is wrinkled $= \frac{1}{4}$ (25%); round $= \frac{3}{4}$ (75%).

:::

The recessive feature skipped the F1 and reappeared in the F2. This happens because two heterozygous parents can each pass on the hidden recessive allele.

## The 1 : 1 ratio

:::worked-solution[Example 3: a heterozygous polled bull with horned cows]
In cattle, polled (**P**) is dominant to horned (**p**).
1. Parents: polled bull (**Pp**) × horned cow (**pp**).
2. Gametes: the bull makes P and p in equal numbers; the cow makes only p.
3. Offspring: Pp, Pp, pp, pp, which is 2 Pp : 2 pp.
4. Phenotypes: 1 polled : 1 horned. Each calf has a 50% chance of being polled.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Three crosses to know"}
Homozygous dominant × homozygous recessive → all show the dominant feature. Heterozygous × heterozygous → 3 dominant : 1 recessive. Heterozygous × homozygous recessive → 1 dominant : 1 recessive.
:::

## Ratios are probabilities

A 3 : 1 ratio does not mean that out of every four calves exactly one will be horned. Fertilisation is random: which sperm meets which egg is a matter of chance, and each fertilisation is a separate event. The ratio tells us the **probability** for each offspring. With large numbers, the results come close to the expected ratio. Crossing many Rr plants together might give 600 seeds, 437 round and 163 wrinkled: close to the expected 450 : 150. A family of four children, by contrast, could easily include no child with the recessive feature, or two.

## Pedigree diagrams

A **pedigree diagram** is a family tree that shows which members have a characteristic. In the usual layout:

- squares are males and circles are females;
- a shaded shape means the person or animal shows the characteristic;
- a horizontal line joins two parents, and a vertical line leads down to their children;
- generations are numbered I, II, III from the top.

:::example{title="Example 4: albinism in a family"}
A family tree, written out in words:

| Generation | Person | Has albinism? | Parents |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | Mr Ncube | No | — |
| I | Mrs Ncube | No | — |
| II | Thandeka | No | Mr and Mrs Ncube |
| II | Sipho | Yes | Mr and Mrs Ncube |
| II | Nomsa | No | Mr and Mrs Ncube |

Two parents without albinism have a son with albinism, so the allele for albinism must have been **hidden** in both parents: it is **recessive**. Using A (normal pigment) and a (albinism), Sipho is **aa**. He received one a from each parent, so both parents must be **Aa**. Thandeka and Nomsa could be AA or Aa: from Aa × Aa, two of the three genotypes without albinism are Aa, so each sister has a $\frac{2}{3}$ chance of being heterozygous.

:::

People with albinism have little or no melanin, so they need to protect their skin and eyes from strong sunlight. Albinism does not affect intelligence or ability, and it is caused by a gene, not by anything the parents did.

![Pedigree of the Ncube family: Mr and Mrs Ncube (both Aa, no albinism) have three children, Thandeka and Nomsa without albinism and Sipho, shown shaded, who has albinism and is aa; two unaffected parents with an affected child shows the allele for albinism is recessive.](media:diagrams/albinism-pedigree.png "Figure: pedigree diagram of albinism in the Ncube family")

## The test cross (Supplement)

An animal showing the dominant feature could be homozygous or heterozygous: a polled bull could be **PP** or **Pp**. To find out, breed it with homozygous recessive (**pp**) cows.

| Bull's genotype | Gametes from bull | Calves | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PP | all P | all Pp | all polled |
| Pp | P or p | Pp and pp | about half polled, half horned |

If **even one** horned calf is born, the bull must be **Pp**. If many calves are born and all are polled, the bull is almost certainly **PP**. A recessive parent is used because it shows exactly which allele the unknown parent passed on.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting two alleles into a gamete"}
Gametes carry **one** allele of each gene: Rr gives gametes R and r, never "Rr".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the genotype ratio when the phenotype ratio is asked"}
Rr × Rr gives genotypes 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr but phenotypes 3 round : 1 wrinkled. Read the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a ratio as a promise"}
A 1 in 4 chance for each child does not mean the fourth child must show the recessive feature. Every fertilisation is independent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming a dominant phenotype means homozygous"}
A dominant phenotype can come from a homozygous or a heterozygous genotype. Only the recessive phenotype tells you the genotype for certain.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Use a genetic diagram" means set out every line: phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes and phenotypes, and the ratio. A Punnett square on its own may not earn all the marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
Write ratios with the phenotypes named: "3 round : 1 wrinkled". Write probabilities as $\frac{1}{4}$, 0.25 or 25%.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a pedigree, look for **two unaffected parents with an affected child**: that proves the allele is recessive and both parents are heterozygous.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A monohybrid cross follows one gene; gametes carry one allele each, and a Punnett square shows every combination.
- Pure-breeding (homozygous) dominant × recessive parents give an F1 that all show the dominant feature; crossing the F1 gives an F2 of 3 dominant : 1 recessive.
- Heterozygous × homozygous recessive gives 1 : 1.
- Ratios are probabilities for each offspring; real results only approach them in large numbers.
- In a pedigree, two unaffected parents with an affected child show the allele is recessive.
- Supplement: a test cross with a homozygous recessive organism reveals whether a dominant-phenotype organism is homozygous or heterozygous.
:::
