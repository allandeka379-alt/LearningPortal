## Introduction

A large msasa tree can be more than 10 metres tall, yet every one of its leaves needs a steady supply of water from the soil, and every root needs sugar from the leaves. Plants have no heart to pump these materials, but they do have two transport tissues: **xylem**, which carries water and mineral ions up from the roots, and **phloem**, which carries sugars and amino acids around the plant.

In this lesson you will learn the main parts of a flowering plant, how to tell monocotyledons from dicotyledons, where xylem and phloem are found and how water enters the root. You should already know the structure of plant cells and what osmosis is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Xylem"}
A transport tissue that carries water and mineral ions from the roots to the stems and leaves. Its thick walls also help to support the plant.
:::

:::definition{term="Phloem"}
A transport tissue that carries sucrose and amino acids from where they are made or stored to where they are needed, in both directions.
:::

:::definition{term="Vascular bundle"}
A group of xylem and phloem running together, for example in a stem or a leaf vein.
:::

:::definition{term="Root hair cell"}
An epidermal cell of a young root with a long, thin extension (the root hair) that grows between soil particles and absorbs water and mineral ions.
:::

## The external structure of a flowering plant

A flowering plant has a **shoot system** above ground and a **root system** below.

- **Shoot system**: the stem, with **nodes** (where leaves join) and **internodes** (the stem between them); leaves; a **terminal (apical) bud** at the tip, which grows to lengthen the shoot; **axillary buds** in the angle between a leaf and the stem, which can grow into side shoots or flowers; flowers and fruits.
- **Root system**: anchors the plant, absorbs water and mineral ions, and sometimes stores food (as in carrots and sweet potatoes). Dicotyledons usually have a **tap root** with lateral roots; monocotyledons have **fibrous roots** growing from the base of the stem.

### Monocotyledons and dicotyledons

| Feature | Monocotyledons | Dicotyledons |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seed | One cotyledon (seed leaf) | Two cotyledons |
| Leaf | Usually long and narrow, with parallel veins | Usually broad, with a network of veins |
| Roots | Fibrous roots | Tap root with lateral roots |
| Vascular bundles in the stem | Scattered | Arranged in a ring |
| Flower parts | Usually in threes | Usually in fours or fives |
| Examples | Maize, sorghum, sugar cane, onion, banana, grasses | Bean, groundnut, sunflower, tomato, pumpkin, msasa, mango |

## What xylem and phloem do

**Xylem** carries water and dissolved mineral ions **upwards** from the roots to the leaves. **Phloem** carries **sucrose and amino acids**, both upwards and downwards, from the leaves (or stores) to the places that use or store them, such as roots, fruits and growing tips.

### The structure of xylem vessels (Cambridge Supplement)

Xylem vessels are made from long cells joined end to end that have died. Their structure suits their job:

- **No cross walls**, so they form long, continuous tubes from root to leaf.
- **No cell contents** (no cytoplasm or nucleus), so water flows freely.
- **Thick walls strengthened with lignin**, which make them strong enough not to collapse and help to support the plant; lignin is also waterproof.

## Where xylem and phloem are found

You will often be asked to identify tissues in a transverse section (a slice cut across a root or stem). Here is what each one looks like, described from the outside in.

**Dicotyledon root:** epidermis with root hairs; a wide **cortex** of large, thin-walled cells that often store starch; and in the centre the vascular tissue, where the **xylem forms a star shape** (an X with three or four arms) and the **phloem lies in groups between the arms** of the star. There is no pith: the xylem fills the centre.

**Dicotyledon (non-woody) stem:** epidermis; cortex; **vascular bundles arranged in a ring** near the outside, each with **phloem on the outer side and xylem on the inner side**; and a central **pith** of large storage cells.

**Monocotyledon stem**, such as maize: vascular bundles **scattered** through the stem, with no single ring.

**Leaf:** in the midrib and veins, **xylem towards the upper surface and phloem towards the lower surface**.

:::note{title="Why these positions?"}
In a root, a central core of xylem resists the pulling forces as the plant sways. In a stem, a ring of strong bundles near the outside resists bending.
:::

## How water enters the root and reaches the leaves

Each root hair cell has a long, thin extension that greatly increases the **surface area** for absorption. The soil water is a dilute solution and the cell sap is more concentrated, so water enters the root hair cell by **osmosis**. Mineral ions are absorbed mainly by active transport.

The water then follows this pathway:

1. root hair cell (osmosis from the soil water);
2. across the cells of the **cortex**;
3. into the **xylem** in the centre of the root;
4. up the xylem of the stem;
5. along the xylem of the leaf veins;
6. into the **mesophyll cells** by osmosis;
7. it evaporates from the mesophyll cell surfaces into the air spaces and diffuses out through the stomata as water vapour (transpiration).

:::example{title="Example 1: tracing water with a dye"}
Tatenda stands a leafy celery stalk (or a balsam shoot) in a beaker of water coloured with a red dye and leaves it in a warm, bright room.

- After an hour the veins of the leaves are red.
- He places a short length of stem on a white tile and, cutting away from his fingers with a sharp blade, cuts a thin transverse slice. Under a hand lens the slice shows **red dots arranged in a ring**: the xylem of the vascular bundles. The rest of the stem is not coloured.
- A balsam stem is translucent, so the red xylem strands can even be seen through the side of the stem.

**Conclusion:** water moves up the plant in the xylem and continues into the leaf veins.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how fast did the dye rise?]
The red dye in Tatenda's celery reached 9 cm above the water level in 30 minutes. Calculate the mean speed of the dye in mm per minute.

1. Convert to millimetres: $9\ \text{cm} = 90\ \text{mm}$.
2. Speed $=$ distance $\div$ time $= 90 \div 30 = 3$ mm per minute.
3. In a cooler, more humid room the dye would rise more slowly, because water is pulled up by transpiration.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: transplanting seedlings"}
When Rudo moves tomato or tobacco seedlings from a seedbed to the field, many of the fine root hairs are torn off. With fewer root hairs the seedlings absorb less water, so they often wilt for a day or two. She reduces the damage by watering the seedbed first, lifting each seedling with a ball of soil around its roots, transplanting in the cool of the late afternoon and watering straight away.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up xylem and phloem"}
Xylem carries **water and mineral ions**, upwards only. Phloem carries **sucrose and amino acids**, up or down.
:::

:::mistake{title="Water enters roots by active transport"}
Water enters root hair cells by **osmosis**. It is the mineral ions that are taken up mainly by active transport.
:::

:::mistake{title="Xylem in the outside of the bundle"}
In a dicotyledon stem the **phloem** is on the outside of each bundle and the **xylem** is on the inside, towards the pith.
:::

:::mistake{title="Xylem vessels are living tubes"}
Mature xylem vessels are **dead**: they have no cytoplasm or nucleus, which is why water can flow through them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the positions: root, xylem star in the centre with phloem between its arms; dicotyledon stem, ring of bundles with xylem inside and phloem outside; leaf, xylem above phloem.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "describe the pathway of water", name each tissue in order and the process at the root hair (osmosis). Missing out the cortex or the mesophyll loses marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "explain how a root hair cell is adapted", link the long extension to a **large surface area** for absorbing water and mineral ions.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A flowering plant has a shoot system (stem, leaves, buds, flowers) and a root system; monocotyledons have parallel-veined leaves, fibrous roots and scattered bundles; dicotyledons have net-veined leaves, a tap root and bundles in a ring.
- Xylem carries water and mineral ions upwards and supports the plant; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in both directions.
- Supplement: xylem vessels are dead, hollow, continuous tubes with thick lignified walls.
- Root: central star of xylem with phloem between the arms; dicotyledon stem: ring of bundles, phloem outside, xylem inside, with a central pith.
- Root hair cells absorb water by osmosis over a large surface area; water passes through the cortex to the xylem, up to the leaves and into the mesophyll cells.
:::
