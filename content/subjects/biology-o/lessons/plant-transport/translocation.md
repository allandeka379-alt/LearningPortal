## Introduction

A sweet potato plant in a garden in Mutare makes sugar in its leaves, yet the sugar ends up as starch in swollen roots underground. A young msasa leaf unfolding in September cannot yet make enough food for itself, so food must be sent to it. And when elephants in Hwange National Park strip a ring of bark from a tree, the tree may stay green for months and then die. All three stories are about **translocation**, the movement of food in the phloem.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Translocation is **Supplement** content for Cambridge IGCSE (Extended candidates). Translocation and ring-barking are part of the ZIMSEC syllabus.
:::

You should already know that the phloem is found in the vascular bundles of roots, stems and leaves, and that plants make glucose by photosynthesis and store starch.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Translocation"}
The movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem from the parts of a plant where they are made or released (sources) to the parts where they are used or stored (sinks).
:::

:::definition{term="Source"}
A part of a plant that releases sucrose or amino acids into the phloem, for example a photosynthesising leaf, or a storage organ whose stored food is being used.
:::

:::definition{term="Sink"}
A part of a plant that takes sucrose or amino acids out of the phloem to use or store, for example roots, growing shoot tips, flowers, developing fruits and seeds, and storage organs being filled.
:::

:::definition{term="Ring-barking"}
Removing a complete ring of bark, including the phloem, from around a woody stem or trunk, leaving the xylem (wood) intact.
:::

## What is moved, and where

Glucose made in photosynthesis is converted to **sucrose** for transport. Sucrose is soluble, so it is carried dissolved in the phloem sap (insoluble starch could not be moved). Amino acids made in the leaves are carried too. Unlike the xylem, which carries water only upwards, the phloem can carry substances **upwards or downwards**, depending on where the sources and sinks are.

| Sources | Sinks |
| --- | --- |
| Mature leaves in the light | Roots |
| Storage organs that are sprouting (potato tubers, sweet potato roots) | Growing shoot tips and young leaves |
| | Flowers and developing fruits and seeds |
| | Storage organs that are being filled |

At a sink, sucrose is used for **respiration**, converted to **cellulose** for new cell walls, or converted to **starch** for storage.

## Sources and sinks can swap (Cambridge Supplement)

A part of a plant is a source when it releases more sucrose than it uses, and a sink when it takes in more than it releases. The same organ can change its role during its life.

:::example{title="Example 1: a sweet potato through the year"}
- **While it grows (rainy season):** the leaves photosynthesise and are the sources. The swelling storage roots are sinks: sucrose arrives in the phloem and is converted to starch.
- **When an old storage root sprouts:** its starch is broken down to sugars, converted to sucrose and sent up the phloem to the new shoots. The storage root is now a **source**, and the new shoots are the **sinks**.
- **Once the new leaves are fully grown:** they photosynthesise and become sources, and new storage roots become sinks again.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: a leaf grows up"}
A very young leaf, such as a new msasa leaf in early spring, has few chloroplasts working and is growing fast, so it imports sucrose: it is a **sink**. Once it is fully expanded and photosynthesising well, it makes more sucrose than it needs and exports it: it has become a **source**.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a maize plant before and after flowering"}
Before flowering, the leaves send sucrose to the roots and to the growing stem and leaves. After pollination the developing grains on the cob become the main sinks, and much of the sucrose from the leaves flows to the cob, where it is stored as starch. If drought or leaf damage reduces photosynthesis at this stage, the grains are poorly filled.
:::

## Evidence that phloem carries sugar

Aphids feed by pushing their needle-like mouthparts into the phloem of a stem. If an aphid is cut off from its mouthparts while feeding, sap continues to flow out of the mouthparts, and this sap contains sucrose and amino acids.

## Ring-barking (ZIMSEC)

In a woody stem, the **phloem is in the bark** and the **xylem is the wood** inside. Removing a complete ring of bark therefore cuts the phloem but leaves the xylem working.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: predicting the effects of ring-barking a tree trunk]
1. Water and mineral ions still travel up the xylem (the wood), so at first the leaves stay green and keep photosynthesising.
2. Sucrose made in the leaves moves down the phloem but cannot pass the gap. It builds up above the ring, so the bark **above the ring swells**.
3. Tissue below the ring, including the **roots**, receives no sucrose. The roots use up their stored food, then cannot respire to release energy.
4. Without energy the roots stop growing and cannot absorb mineral ions by active transport. The roots die, then the whole tree dies, sometimes months later.
:::

Farmers and foresters sometimes ring-bark unwanted trees to kill them without felling them. Elephants, goats and porcupines can kill trees in the same way by stripping or gnawing bark all the way round the trunk. If only part of the ring of bark is removed, the remaining phloem can still carry sugar and the tree usually survives.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Translocation happens in the xylem"}
Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in the **phloem**. The xylem carries water and mineral ions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Phloem only carries food downwards"}
Phloem carries food from sources to sinks in **any direction**: up to fruits and shoot tips, down to roots.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ring-barking stops water reaching the leaves"}
The xylem is not removed, so water still reaches the leaves. The tree dies because the **roots are starved** of sucrose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaves are always sources"}
Young, growing leaves are sinks; storage organs can be sinks (filling) or sources (sprouting).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
A full definition of translocation mentions **sucrose and amino acids**, the **phloem**, and **sources to sinks**.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked why an organ can be both a source and a sink, name the organ and describe **both** stages, saying in each case whether it releases or takes in sucrose.
:::

:::exam-tip
For ring-barking, state which tissue is removed (phloem) and which remains (xylem) before explaining the swelling above the ring and the death of the roots.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Translocation is the movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem from sources to sinks, in any direction.
- Sources release sucrose (photosynthesising leaves, sprouting storage organs); sinks use or store it (roots, shoot tips, fruits, seeds, filling storage organs).
- Supplement: an organ can switch roles, e.g. a sweet potato root is a sink while it fills and a source when it sprouts; a young leaf is a sink until it matures.
- Ring-barking removes the phloem but not the xylem: sugars build up and swell the bark above the ring, the roots starve and the tree eventually dies.
:::
