## Introduction

On a hot October afternoon in Masvingo, the big leaves of a pumpkin plant hang limp, and the leaves of the maize beside it roll up into tubes. By the next morning both look healthy again. What happened? The plants were losing water vapour from their leaves faster than their roots could replace it.

This loss of water vapour is called **transpiration**. It is not just a waste: it is what pulls water and mineral ions up the plant. In this lesson you will learn how transpiration happens, what affects its rate, how to measure it and how plants survive in dry conditions. You should already know the structure of a leaf and the pathway of water through the plant.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transpiration"}
The loss of water vapour from the leaves of a plant. Water evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, and the water vapour then diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata.
:::

:::definition{term="Transpiration stream"}
The continuous movement of water (with dissolved mineral ions) from the roots, up the xylem, to the leaves, caused by transpiration.
:::

:::definition{term="Wilting"}
Drooping of leaves and soft stems when the plant loses water faster than it absorbs it, so that its cells lose turgor and become flaccid.
:::

:::definition{term="Potometer"}
Apparatus that measures the rate at which a leafy shoot **takes up** water, which is used as an estimate of its rate of transpiration.
:::

## How transpiration happens

1. Water reaches the mesophyll cells of the leaf in the xylem.
2. A thin film of water covers the walls of the mesophyll cells. It **evaporates** into the air spaces, so the air inside the leaf is saturated with water vapour.
3. The air outside is usually drier, so water vapour **diffuses** out through the open stomata, down a concentration gradient.

**Supplement:** the spongy mesophyll has a very large **internal surface area** of wet cell walls exposed to the air spaces, so a lot of water can evaporate. The more stomata a leaf has, and the wider they open, the faster water vapour escapes.

## Why transpiration matters

- It moves **water and mineral ions** up the xylem to the leaves (the transpiration stream).
- **Evaporation cools** the leaves, like sweat cooling the skin.
- It supplies water for **photosynthesis** and keeps leaf cells **turgid**, which supports the leaves.

### Transpiration pull (Cambridge Supplement)

As water evaporates from the mesophyll cells, they take water from the xylem in the leaf veins. This creates a pull (tension) on the water in the xylem. Water molecules are **attracted to each other**, so the water in the xylem forms a continuous column that is pulled up the stem from the roots, like a drink through a straw.

## Factors that affect the rate of transpiration

| Factor (increase) | Effect on rate | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Faster | Water molecules have more kinetic energy, so they evaporate and diffuse faster |
| Humidity | Slower | The air outside holds more water vapour, so the concentration gradient is smaller |
| Wind speed | Faster | Water vapour is blown away from the leaf surface, keeping the gradient steep |
| Light intensity | Faster | Stomata open wider in the light (and the leaf is warmed) |
| Leaf surface area | Faster | More surface and more stomata to lose water from |

The **distribution of stomata** also matters: having most stomata in the shaded, cooler lower epidermis reduces water loss.

## Measuring transpiration

### The potometer

1. Cut a leafy shoot **under water**, at a slant, so no air enters the xylem (an air bubble would block the water column).
2. Fit the shoot into the potometer under water and seal the joints with petroleum jelly so the apparatus is airtight and watertight.
3. Dry the leaves, then let a single air bubble into the capillary tube.
4. Measure how far the bubble moves in a fixed time. Reset it with water from the reservoir and repeat.
5. Change one condition at a time: a fan for wind, a lamp for light, a clear plastic bag for humidity.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: rate of water uptake from a potometer]
The bubble moves 36 mm along a capillary tube of radius 0.5 mm in 4 minutes. Calculate (a) the rate of movement and (b) the volume of water taken up per minute.

1. (a) Rate $= 36 \div 4 = 9$ mm per minute.
2. (b) The water taken up fills a cylinder of the capillary: $V = \pi r^2 l$.
3. Volume per minute $= \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 9 = 7.07\ \text{mm}^3$ per minute (3 s.f.).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: comparing two conditions]
In still air the bubble moved 18 mm in 5 minutes; with a fan blowing on the shoot it moved 45 mm in 5 minutes. By what percentage did the fan increase the rate?

1. Still air: $18 \div 5 = 3.6$ mm per minute. Fan: $45 \div 5 = 9.0$ mm per minute.
2. Increase $= 9.0 - 3.6 = 5.4$ mm per minute.
3. Percentage increase $= 5.4 \div 3.6 \times 100 = 150\%$.
4. Explanation: moving air removes water vapour from around the leaf, keeping the concentration gradient steep.
:::

A potometer actually measures **water uptake**. This is close to the rate of transpiration, but slightly higher, because a little of the water is used in photosynthesis and to keep the cells turgid.

### Other methods

- **Loss of mass**: cover the pot and soil of a potted plant with a plastic bag (so water cannot evaporate from the soil) and weigh it at intervals. The loss in mass is the water transpired.
- **Plastic bag over a shoot**: droplets collect inside the bag; they turn blue cobalt chloride paper pink, showing they are water.

:::example{title="Example 3: petroleum jelly on leaf surfaces"}
Four similar leaves were treated and hung up for three days: A had no jelly, B had jelly on the upper surface, C on the lower surface and D on both surfaces. Petroleum jelly is waterproof and blocks the stomata. A and B lost much more mass than C and D. Covering the **lower** surface stopped most of the water loss, so most of the stomata must be in the lower epidermis.
:::

## Wilting

When a plant loses water by transpiration faster than its roots can absorb it, the cells lose water by osmosis and become **flaccid**. They no longer press against their cell walls, so the leaves and soft stems droop. On hot afternoons many plants wilt temporarily and recover at night; if the soil stays dry they may wilt permanently and die.

Wilting reduces the leaf area facing the sun, and the stomata close, so less water is lost. But with the stomata closed, carbon dioxide cannot enter, so **photosynthesis slows**.

## Leaves that reduce water loss (ZIMSEC)

Plants of dry places, and crops in a dry spell, show features that reduce transpiration:

- a **thick waxy cuticle**;
- **fewer stomata**, mostly on the lower surface, sometimes **sunken in pits** where moist air is trapped;
- **hairs** on the leaf that trap a layer of moist air;
- **rolled leaves**, as in maize on a hot afternoon, which trap moist air and expose less surface;
- **small leaves or spines** (cacti, euphorbias), giving a small surface area;
- **shedding leaves** in the dry season, as many woodland trees in Zimbabwe do.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Transpiration is evaporation from the soil"}
Transpiration is the loss of water **vapour from the leaves**, mostly through the stomata.
:::

:::mistake{title="Humid air speeds up transpiration"}
Humid air already contains a lot of water vapour, so the gradient is **smaller** and transpiration is **slower**.
:::

:::mistake{title="A potometer measures water loss"}
A potometer measures water **uptake**, which is slightly more than the water lost.
:::

:::mistake{title="Cutting the shoot in air"}
Air enters the cut xylem and blocks the water column. Always cut and fit the shoot under water.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transpiration: water evaporates from mesophyll cell surfaces into air spaces, then water vapour diffuses out through the stomata.
- It moves water and mineral ions up the xylem, cools the leaf and keeps cells turgid; Supplement: evaporation creates a transpiration pull on a continuous water column held together by attraction between water molecules.
- Rate increases with temperature, wind speed, light intensity and leaf area, and decreases with humidity.
- A potometer measures water uptake; rate = distance ÷ time and volume = $\pi r^2 l$; loss of mass and plastic bags are other methods.
- Wilting happens when loss exceeds uptake and cells become flaccid; dry-area leaves have thick cuticles, fewer or sunken stomata, hairs, rolling or small leaves.
:::
