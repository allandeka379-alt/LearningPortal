## Introduction

When someone starts frying onions in a kitchen, the smell soon reaches every corner of the house. Nobody carries it there: the particles of the smell spread out by themselves. This spreading is **diffusion**, and living things depend on it every second. Oxygen diffuses into your blood in the lungs, carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaves of a maize plant, and digested food diffuses from your small intestine into your blood.

Sometimes, however, a cell needs to take in a substance that is **more** concentrated inside the cell than outside. Then diffusion would carry it the wrong way, and the cell must use energy to pump it in. This is **active transport**. In this lesson you will learn how both processes work, why they matter and what affects the rate of diffusion. You should already know that particles are always moving and that the cell membrane is partially permeable.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diffusion"}
The net movement of particles from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration (down a concentration gradient), as a result of their random movement.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentration gradient"}
A difference in the concentration of a substance between two regions. The bigger the difference, the steeper the gradient.
:::

:::definition{term="Active transport"}
The movement of particles through a cell membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (against a concentration gradient), using energy released by respiration.
:::

## How diffusion works

All particles in liquids and gases move about randomly, colliding and changing direction. Where there are more particles, more of them happen to move away than move in, so overall there is a **net movement** from high concentration to low concentration. The energy comes from the particles' own **kinetic energy** (energy of movement), so diffusion is a passive process: the cell does not have to supply energy from respiration.

When the particles are evenly spread, they keep moving, but equal numbers move in every direction, so there is no further net movement. Many substances, such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, cross cell membranes by diffusion.

## Why diffusion matters to organisms

| Where | What diffuses | From | To |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alveoli of the lungs | Oxygen | Air in the alveoli | Blood |
| Alveoli of the lungs | Carbon dioxide | Blood | Air in the alveoli |
| Leaves | Carbon dioxide | Air (through the stomata) | Mesophyll cells |
| Small intestine | Glucose and amino acids | Digested food in the intestine | Blood in the villi |
| Respiring muscle | Oxygen | Blood | Muscle cells |
| Gills of a fish | Oxygen | Water | Blood |

## Factors that affect the rate of diffusion

1. **Surface area**: the larger the surface, the more particles can cross at the same time.
2. **Temperature**: at a higher temperature particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so diffusion is faster.
3. **Concentration gradient**: the steeper the gradient, the faster the net movement.
4. **Distance**: the shorter the distance the particles must travel, the faster they arrive. The walls of alveoli and capillaries are only one cell thick for this reason.

## Investigating diffusion with agar cubes

:::example{title="Pink agar in acid"}
Agar jelly is made with an alkali and phenolphthalein indicator, so it is pink. Cubes are cut with sides of 1 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm and placed in dilute hydrochloric acid. As the acid diffuses in, it neutralises the alkali and the pink colour disappears. The time taken for each cube to become completely colourless is recorded. The smallest cube loses its colour first.
:::

The explanation lies in the **surface area to volume ratio**.

:::worked-solution[Surface area to volume ratios of three cubes]
1. A cube has 6 faces, so surface area $= 6 \times \text{side}^2$ and volume $= \text{side}^3$.
2. 1 cm cube: surface area $= 6 \times 1 \times 1 = 6\ \text{cm}^2$; volume $= 1\ \text{cm}^3$; ratio $= 6 : 1$.
3. 2 cm cube: surface area $= 6 \times 2 \times 2 = 24\ \text{cm}^2$; volume $= 8\ \text{cm}^3$; ratio $= 24 : 8 = 3 : 1$.
4. 3 cm cube: surface area $= 6 \times 3 \times 3 = 54\ \text{cm}^2$; volume $= 27\ \text{cm}^3$; ratio $= 54 : 27 = 2 : 1$.
5. As the cube gets bigger, the ratio gets smaller: each unit of volume has less surface through which acid can enter, and the centre is further from the surface. So larger cubes take longer to change colour.
:::

The same apparatus can test other factors: put identical cubes in acid at different temperatures, or in acid of different concentrations. Keep everything else the same.

:::keyfact
Large organisms have a small surface area to volume ratio, and most of their cells are far from the surface. Diffusion alone would be far too slow, so they need exchange surfaces with a large area (lungs, gills, villi, leaves) and transport systems (blood, xylem and phloem).
:::

## Active transport

Root hair cells contain mineral ions, such as nitrate, at a higher concentration than the soil water around them. Diffusion would carry these ions **out** of the roots, yet the plant keeps absorbing them. It does this by active transport, using energy released by respiration in the root cells.

Evidence that active transport needs respiration: if the soil is waterlogged (so there is little oxygen), or if the roots are treated with a poison that stops respiration, the uptake of mineral ions slows down or stops.

Active transport also lets the small intestine absorb glucose from digested food even when the concentration in the intestine has fallen below that in the blood. ZIMSEC calls this "active uptake".

### How active transport works (Supplement)

The membrane contains **carrier proteins**. A particle such as a nitrate ion fits into a carrier on one side of the membrane; using energy from respiration, the carrier changes shape and releases the particle on the other side. Each carrier usually moves only one type of particle.

| | Diffusion | Active transport |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | Down a concentration gradient | Against a concentration gradient |
| Energy | Kinetic energy of the particles; none from respiration | Energy from respiration |
| Carrier proteins | Not needed | Needed (Supplement) |
| Example | Oxygen entering the blood in the lungs | Mineral ions entering root hair cells |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying diffusion uses energy from respiration"}
Diffusion is passive. The particles move because of their own kinetic energy; the cell spends no energy. Only active transport needs energy from respiration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying particles stop moving at equilibrium"}
The particles keep moving randomly. At equilibrium there is simply no **net** movement, because as many move one way as the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking bigger cubes diffuse faster because they have more surface"}
A bigger cube has a larger total surface area but a **smaller** surface area to volume ratio, so it takes longer for substances to reach its centre.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the direction of active transport wrong"}
Active transport moves particles from low to high concentration, against the gradient. If particles move from high to low, it is diffusion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Define diffusion with all three ideas: **net movement**, **from higher to lower concentration** (down a concentration gradient) and **random movement**.
:::

:::exam-tip
In surface area to volume calculations, show the surface area (with cm²) and the volume (with cm³), then give the ratio as a number to 1, such as 3 : 1.
:::

:::exam-tip
If a question describes particles moving from low to high concentration, or uptake that stops without oxygen, the answer is active transport.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient as a result of their random movement; the energy comes from the particles' kinetic energy.
- Organisms rely on diffusion for gas exchange in lungs, gills and leaves, and for absorbing digested food.
- The rate of diffusion increases with surface area, temperature and the steepness of the concentration gradient, and decreases with distance.
- Smaller objects have a larger surface area to volume ratio, so substances reach their centres faster.
- Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient using energy from respiration; Supplement: carrier proteins move the particles, as in ion uptake by root hairs.
:::
