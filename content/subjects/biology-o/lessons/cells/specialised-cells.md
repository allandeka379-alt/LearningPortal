## Introduction

A single-celled organism such as *Amoeba* must carry out every life process in one cell. A large organism, such as a cow or a maize plant, is made of billions of cells, and they share out the work. Each type of cell becomes **specialised**: its shape and contents are suited to one particular job. This sharing of jobs is called **division of labour**, and it lets each process be carried out very efficiently.

In this lesson you will learn how the structure of the main specialised cells suits their function, and how specialised cells are grouped into tissues, organs and organ systems. You should already know the structures found in plant and animal cells.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Specialised cell"}
A cell whose structure is adapted to carry out a particular function.
:::

:::definition{term="Tissue"}
A group of cells with similar structures that work together to perform a shared function.
:::

:::definition{term="Organ"}
A structure made of a group of tissues that work together to perform specific functions.
:::

:::definition{term="Organ system"}
A group of organs with related functions that work together to perform a body function.
:::

## Specialised cells: structure and function

| Cell | Where it is found | Function | How its structure helps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ciliated cell | Lining of the trachea and bronchi | Moves mucus | Tiny hairs (cilia) on its surface beat in waves, sweeping mucus with trapped dust and bacteria up to the throat, away from the lungs |
| Root hair cell | Just behind the tip of a root | Absorbs water and mineral ions from the soil | A long, thin extension (the root hair) gives a large surface area; no chloroplasts, as it is underground |
| Palisade mesophyll cell | Upper layer of a leaf | Photosynthesis | Many chloroplasts to absorb light; tall, column shape packed closely near the upper surface |
| Neurone (nerve cell) | Nervous system | Carries electrical impulses | A very long fibre carries impulses over long distances; branched endings connect with many other cells |
| Red blood cell | Blood | Carries oxygen | Contains haemoglobin; no nucleus, leaving more room for haemoglobin; a biconcave disc shape gives a large surface area for oxygen to diffuse in and out; flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries |
| Sperm cell | Testes, then the female reproductive system | Reaches and fertilises an egg | A flagellum (tail) for swimming; many mitochondria to release energy for swimming; enzymes in the acrosome (in the head) to digest a way into the egg |
| Egg cell | Ovaries, then the oviduct | Is fertilised and provides for the early embryo | Large, with energy stores in the cytoplasm; a jelly coat that changes after fertilisation so that only one sperm can enter |

The ZIMSEC syllabus also names white blood cells (two kinds) and muscle cells:

| Cell | Function | How its structure helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White blood cell: phagocyte | Engulfs and digests pathogens | Flexible membrane and cytoplasm allow it to change shape and surround bacteria; contains digestive enzymes; lobed nucleus |
| White blood cell: lymphocyte | Makes antibodies | Large nucleus; makes and releases antibody proteins |
| Muscle cell | Contracts to cause movement | Long cells containing protein filaments that slide to shorten the cell; many mitochondria release energy for contraction |

:::keyfact
Sperm and egg cells (gametes) each carry only half the normal number of chromosomes in their nucleus, so that when they fuse at fertilisation the zygote has the full number.
:::

## Explaining an adaptation

Good answers link a **feature** to the **function** with a reason.

:::example{title="Root hair cells"}
Feature: the root hair is a long, thin extension of the cell. Reason: this greatly increases the surface area of the cell in contact with the soil water. Function: so the cell can absorb more water and mineral ions in a given time.
:::

:::example{title="Red blood cells"}
Feature: no nucleus. Reason: this leaves more space inside the cell. Function: so it can hold more haemoglobin and carry more oxygen.
:::

## Levels of organisation

Specialised cells are organised into larger and larger working units:

| Level | Plant example | Animal example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell | Palisade mesophyll cell | Muscle cell |
| Tissue | Palisade mesophyll (a layer of palisade cells); xylem | Muscle tissue; the epithelium lining the stomach |
| Organ | Leaf, root, stem | Stomach, heart, kidney, lung |
| Organ system | Shoot system | Digestive system, circulatory system, nervous system |
| Organism | Maize plant | Human |

:::example{title="From cell to organism in digestion"}
Epithelial cells that make enzymes form glandular tissue in the stomach lining. The stomach also contains muscle tissue that churns food and nervous tissue that controls it, so the stomach is an **organ**. The stomach works with the mouth, oesophagus, intestines, liver and pancreas in the **digestive system**, one of several organ systems that together make up the **organism**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cilia trap dust"}
Mucus traps dust and bacteria; the cilia beat to sweep the mucus up and away from the lungs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing root hairs with cilia"}
A root hair is a long extension of one plant cell, used for absorption. Cilia are many tiny hair-like structures on the surface of some animal cells, used to move fluids.
:::

:::mistake{title="Incomplete adaptation answers"}
"Red blood cells have no nucleus" earns little on its own. Add the reason and the result: "so there is more space for haemoglobin to carry more oxygen".
:::

:::mistake{title="Muddling tissues and organs"}
A tissue is made of similar cells doing one job (for example muscle tissue). An organ, such as the heart, is made of several different tissues working together.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use linking words: "the root hair gives a large surface area, **so** more water is absorbed". Examiners want the feature and how it helps.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "state the function" questions (Cambridge Core), a short answer is enough: ciliated cells move mucus; root hair cells absorb water and mineral ions; palisade cells photosynthesise; neurones conduct impulses; red blood cells transport oxygen; sperm and egg cells take part in reproduction.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to place structures in order of organisation, check that each level is made of the one before: cells make tissues, tissues make organs, organs make organ systems.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Specialised cells have structures suited to their functions, allowing division of labour in multicellular organisms.
- Ciliated cells move mucus; root hair cells absorb water and ions through a large surface area; palisade cells are packed with chloroplasts.
- Neurones have long fibres for impulses; red blood cells have haemoglobin, no nucleus and a biconcave shape; sperm have a flagellum, mitochondria and acrosome enzymes; eggs have food stores and a jelly coat.
- White blood cells engulf pathogens or make antibodies; muscle cells contract and have many mitochondria.
- Cells form tissues, tissues form organs, organs form organ systems, and organ systems make up an organism.
:::
