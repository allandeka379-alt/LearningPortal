## Introduction

Cells, pollen grains and small insects are too small to study with the naked eye, so biologists look at them through microscopes, photograph them (micrographs) and draw them larger than life. To know how big a specimen really is, you need to know **how many times** it has been enlarged: its **magnification**.

Magnification questions appear in theory papers (a micrograph with a measurement to make) and in practical papers (the magnification of your own drawing). They are reliable marks if you learn one formula and take care with units. You should be able to measure lengths in millimetres with a ruler.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Magnification"}
The number of times larger an image is than the real object. It has no units and is written with a times sign, for example ×400.
:::

:::definition{term="Image size"}
The length of the drawing, photograph or micrograph, measured with a ruler.
:::

:::definition{term="Actual size"}
The real length of the specimen.
:::

:::definition{term="Micrometre (μm)"}
One thousandth of a millimetre. It is the usual unit for measuring cells.
:::

## The formula

:::formula{name="Magnification"}
$$
\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{actual size}}
$$
:::

Rearranged, the same formula gives the other two quantities:

$$
\text{actual size} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}} \qquad \text{image size} = \text{magnification} \times \text{actual size}
$$

A memory aid is the triangle with **I** (image) on top and **A** (actual) and **M** (magnification) underneath: cover the quantity you want and the others show you what to do.

:::keyfact
The image size and the actual size must be in the **same unit** before you divide. The units then cancel, which is why magnification has no unit.
:::

## Units (Supplement)

$$
1\ \text{mm} = 1000\ \text{μm} \qquad 1\ \text{cm} = 10\ \text{mm}
$$

- To change millimetres into micrometres, **multiply by 1000**: $0.25\ \text{mm} = 250\ \text{μm}$.
- To change micrometres into millimetres, **divide by 1000**: $80\ \text{μm} = 0.08\ \text{mm}$.

Images are measured in millimetres, but cells are usually given in micrometres, so most cell questions need this conversion.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the magnification of a drawing]
A student draws a msasa leaflet. The drawing is 120 mm long; the real leaflet is 40 mm long.
1. Write the formula: magnification $= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{actual size}}$.
2. Both lengths are already in millimetres: $\dfrac{120\ \text{mm}}{40\ \text{mm}} = 3$.
3. The magnification is $\times 3$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the actual size of a cell (Supplement)]
In a micrograph magnified ×1200, a liver cell is 36 mm across. Find its actual width in micrometres.
1. Actual size $= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}} = \dfrac{36\ \text{mm}}{1200}$.
2. $= 0.03\ \text{mm}$.
3. Convert to micrometres: $0.03 \times 1000 = 30\ \text{μm}$.
4. Check: a liver cell about 30 μm across is a sensible size for an animal cell.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: the size of an image]
A pollen grain is 0.04 mm across. How wide will it appear in a photograph magnified ×500?
1. Image size $=$ magnification $\times$ actual size.
2. $= 500 \times 0.04\ \text{mm} = 20\ \text{mm}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 4: using a scale bar (Supplement)]
A micrograph has a scale bar labelled 10 μm. On the page, the bar is 25 mm long. Find the magnification.
1. The scale bar's image size is 25 mm and its actual size is 10 μm.
2. Put both in the same unit: $25\ \text{mm} = 25 \times 1000 = 25\,000\ \text{μm}$.
3. Magnification $= \dfrac{25\,000\ \text{μm}}{10\ \text{μm}} = 2500$, so $\times 2500$.
:::

:::note{title="Magnification of a light microscope"}
The total magnification of a light microscope is the magnification of the eyepiece lens multiplied by that of the objective lens: an eyepiece of ×10 with an objective of ×40 gives ×400.
:::

## Measuring an image

Measure with a ruler marked in millimetres, along the exact line or axis the question names (for example "along line XY"), from one edge of the structure to the other. Record the length to the nearest millimetre and include the unit. If you measure in centimetres, change to millimetres before calculating.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Turning the formula upside down"}
Magnification is image ÷ actual. Dividing actual by image gives a number smaller than 1 for an enlarged picture, which cannot be right.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing millimetres and micrometres"}
$\dfrac{25\ \text{mm}}{10\ \text{μm}}$ is not 2.5. Convert first: 25 mm = 25 000 μm, so the magnification is ×2500.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving magnification a unit"}
"×400 mm" is wrong. Magnification is a ratio of two lengths, so it has no unit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Multiplying instead of dividing for the actual size"}
The actual size of an enlarged specimen must be **smaller** than its image. If your answer is bigger than the image, you multiplied by mistake.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show three steps: the formula, the values with their units, and the answer with its unit. A slip in the final step can still earn a mark for correct working.
:::

:::exam-tip
Check that your answer is sensible: most plant and animal cells are 10–100 μm across, bacteria are about 1–5 μm long, and a drawing in a practical paper is usually magnified only a few times.
:::

:::exam-tip
Follow any instruction about precision, such as "give your answer to the nearest whole number", and do not round until the final step.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Magnification = image size ÷ actual size; it has no units and is written as, for example, ×400.
- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification; image size = magnification × actual size.
- Both lengths must be in the same unit before dividing.
- Supplement: 1 mm = 1000 μm; multiply by 1000 to change mm to μm, divide by 1000 to change μm to mm.
- Scale bars give an image length and an actual length, so they can be used to calculate magnification.
:::
