## Introduction

Walk past a maize field in February and you will see two kinds of flower on every plant: the **tassel** at the top, shaking out clouds of yellow dust, and the **silks** hanging from the young cobs lower down. In a garden nearby, bees crawl over sunflower heads and in and out of the big yellow flowers of a pumpkin vine. These are two very different ways of solving the same problem: getting **pollen** from one flower to another.

Flowers are the organs of sexual reproduction in flowering plants. In this lesson you will learn the parts of a flower, how insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated flowers differ, and why it matters whether a flower is pollinated by its own pollen or by pollen from another plant.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollination"}
The transfer of pollen grains from an anther to a stigma.
:::

:::definition{term="Self-pollination"}
The transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of the **same flower**, or of another flower on the **same plant**.
:::

:::definition{term="Cross-pollination"}
The transfer of pollen from an anther of a flower on one plant to a stigma of a flower on **another plant of the same species**.
:::

:::definition{term="Stamen and carpel"}
The **stamen** is the male part (anther on a filament). The **carpel** is the female part (stigma, style and ovary containing ovules).
:::

## An insect-pollinated flower

A large Hibiscus flower is a good one to examine with a hand lens. From the outside inwards, its parts are:

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sepals | Green, leaf-like, outermost | Protect the flower while it is a bud |
| Petals | Large and brightly coloured, often with lines that guide insects | Attract insects |
| Nectary | Small gland at the base of the petals | Makes sugary nectar, a reward for insects |
| Stamens: **anther** and **filament** | Anther on a thin stalk (filament) | The anther makes pollen; the filament holds it up |
| Carpel: **stigma** | Sticky tip of the carpel | Receives pollen |
| Carpel: **style** | Stalk joining stigma to ovary | Holds the stigma in position |
| Carpel: **ovary** with **ovules** | Swollen base containing ovules | Each ovule contains a female gamete; the ovary becomes the fruit |

The flower is held on a stalk (the **pedicel**), and its parts are attached to the swollen tip of the stalk (the **receptacle**). In Hibiscus the many stamens are joined into a column around the style, with the stigmas sticking out at the top, where a visiting insect brushes against them.

## A wind-pollinated flower: maize

Maize has **separate male and female flowers** on the same plant:

- **Male flowers** are in the **tassel** at the top of the stem. Each tiny flower has dull green parts, no scent and no nectar, and **large anthers** that dangle outside on long, thin filaments, so the wind shakes the pollen out.
- **Female flowers** are on the young **cob**, wrapped in leafy husks. Each **silk** is a very long **style** with a sticky, hairy stigma surface, and each silk leads to one ovule. The silks hang out of the husk, giving a huge surface to catch pollen blowing past.

Grasses, sorghum and wheat have similar small, dull, wind-pollinated flowers.

### Comparing the two types

| Feature | Insect-pollinated (e.g. Hibiscus, sunflower, pumpkin) | Wind-pollinated (e.g. maize, sorghum, grasses) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Petals | Large, brightly coloured, often scented | Small and dull green, or absent |
| Nectar | Usually present | Absent |
| Anthers | Inside the flower, firmly attached | Outside the flower, loosely attached, swinging |
| Stigmas | Inside the flower, sticky, small | Outside the flower, large and feathery |
| Pollen grains | Larger, sticky or spiky, produced in smaller amounts | Small, light, smooth and dry, produced in huge amounts |

The spikes and stickiness of insect-carried pollen help it cling to an insect's hairy body. Wind-carried pollen must be light to travel far, and huge amounts are needed because most of it never reaches a stigma.

## Self-pollination and cross-pollination

Bean and groundnut flowers are mostly **self-pollinated**: pollen often reaches the stigma before the flower even opens. Maize, pumpkins and sunflowers are mostly **cross-pollinated**.

Some plants have features that make cross-pollination more likely:

- **Separate male and female flowers**, as in maize and pumpkins.
- **Anthers and stigmas ripening at different times**: in maize the tassel often begins shedding pollen a day or more before the silks of the same plant appear.
- **Separate male and female plants**, as in many pawpaw trees.

### Effects on a population (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

| | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-pollination | Reliable: needs no pollinators and no other plant nearby; keeps the good features of the parent | Less variation, so the population is less able to adapt to change or disease; repeated self-pollination can give weaker offspring |
| Cross-pollination | More variation, so some offspring may survive changes or disease; often more vigorous offspring | Depends on wind or pollinators, so it may fail; much pollen is wasted; plants must grow near others of their species |

:::example{title="Example 1: why some pumpkin flowers never become fruits"}
A pumpkin plant has separate male and female flowers, and a female flower only forms a fruit if bees bring pollen from male flowers. After a farmer sprayed a broad-spectrum insecticide near her pumpkins, few bees visited and many female flowers withered without forming fruits. Her neighbour solved the problem by hand: he picked an open male flower, removed its petals and dabbed its anthers onto the stigmas of the female flowers early in the morning.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: reading a maize cob"}
When you peel a mature maize cob, each grain marks an ovule that was pollinated and fertilised; gaps show silks that caught no pollen. A plant on the edge of a field, or a cob whose silks appeared after the nearby tassels had finished shedding pollen, often has many gaps, because wind pollination depends on large amounts of pollen arriving at the right time.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: identifying a flower from its features"}
A student finds a flower with dull green parts, no scent, anthers dangling on long filaments outside the flower and large feathery stigmas. It must be **wind-pollinated**: every feature helps pollen leave the anthers and be caught from the air. A flower with large red petals, nectar and sticky pollen is **insect-pollinated**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pollination is fertilisation"}
Pollination is the **transfer of pollen** to a stigma. Fertilisation happens later, when a male gamete nucleus fuses with the female gamete inside an ovule.
:::

:::mistake{title="Maize silks are petals"}
Each silk is a **style** with a stigma surface, leading to one ovule.
:::

:::mistake{title="Self-pollination needs one flower"}
Pollen moving to **another flower on the same plant** is also self-pollination.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sepals attract insects"}
The **petals** attract insects; sepals protect the bud.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When comparing flowers or pollen, give **matched pairs**: "insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower; wind-pollinated anthers hang outside".
:::

:::exam-tip
Link each feature to its purpose: "feathery stigmas give a **large surface area** to trap pollen from the air".
:::

:::exam-tip
Definitions of cross-pollination must say "another plant of the **same species**".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Flower parts: sepals (protect), petals (attract insects), nectaries, stamens (anther makes pollen, filament supports it), carpels (stigma receives pollen, style, ovary with ovules).
- Insect-pollinated flowers are large, coloured, scented, with nectar, enclosed anthers and stigmas, and larger sticky or spiky pollen.
- Wind-pollinated flowers (maize tassels and silks) are small and dull, with dangling anthers, feathery stigmas and huge amounts of small, light, smooth pollen.
- Pollination is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma; self-pollination is within one plant, cross-pollination between plants of the same species.
- Self-pollination is reliable but reduces variation; cross-pollination increases variation but depends on pollinators or wind.
:::
