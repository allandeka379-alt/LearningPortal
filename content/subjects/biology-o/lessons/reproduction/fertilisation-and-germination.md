## Introduction

Peel back the husk of a ripe maize cob and count the rows of grains. Every grain is there because a pollen grain landed on a silk, a tube grew down that silk, and a male nucleus reached one ovule. A few months later, a grain kept dry in a storeroom can lie unchanged for a long time, yet within days of being planted in warm, moist soil after the first rains it splits open and sends out a root.

This lesson follows that story: from pollination to **fertilisation**, from fertilised ovule to **seed** and ripened ovary to **fruit**, and then from dry seed to young seedling during **germination**. You should already know the parts of a flower and what pollination is. It also helps to remember that enzymes are proteins whose activity depends on temperature, and that aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose using oxygen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fertilisation"}
The fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete (carried by the pollen) with the nucleus of a female gamete in an ovule, forming a **zygote**.
:::

:::definition{term="Pollen tube"}
A tube that grows out of a pollen grain on the stigma, down through the style and into an ovule, carrying the male nucleus.
:::

:::definition{term="Seed and fruit"}
A **seed** develops from a fertilised ovule: it contains an embryo and a food store inside a protective testa. A **fruit** develops from the ovary and contains one or more seeds.
:::

:::definition{term="Germination"}
The start of growth of the embryo in a seed into a seedling, when conditions are suitable.
:::

## From pollination to fertilisation

Pollination only delivers pollen to the stigma. The male nucleus still has to reach the female gamete, which lies deep inside an ovule in the ovary.

### Growth of the pollen tube (Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC)

1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the sticky stigma, which provides a sugary fluid.
2. The pollen grain absorbs the fluid and a **pollen tube** grows out of it.
3. The tube grows down through the tissue of the **style**, secreting enzymes that digest a path. The male nucleus moves down near the tip of the tube.
4. The tube grows through the ovary to an ovule and enters it through the **micropyle**, a tiny gap in the ovule's outer layers.
5. The tip of the tube opens and releases the male nucleus.
6. The male nucleus **fuses** with the female nucleus inside the ovule. This is fertilisation, and the new cell is a **zygote**.

For Cambridge Core you need the key statement: fertilisation happens when a pollen nucleus fuses with a nucleus in an ovule.

:::keyfact{title="One pollen grain for each ovule"}
Each ovule needs its own pollen grain and its own pollen tube. A pod containing seven beans needed at least seven pollen grains on its stigma, and every grain on a maize cob needed pollen on its own silk.
:::

### After fertilisation: seeds and fruits

| Before fertilisation | What it becomes |
| --- | --- |
| Zygote | Embryo (by mitosis): radicle, plumule and one or two cotyledons |
| Ovule | Seed |
| Outer layers of the ovule | Testa (seed coat) |
| Micropyle | A tiny pore that remains in the testa |
| Ovary | Fruit |
| Ovary wall | Fruit wall |
| Petals, stamens, stigma and style | Usually wither and fall off |

Food is moved into each developing seed and stored in the cotyledons or in an endosperm. Finally the seed loses most of its water and becomes **dormant**: alive, but not growing.

ZIMSEC examines these changes; Cambridge needs only the fertilisation step itself, but knowing what happens next makes the rest of this topic easier to follow.

:::example{title="Example 1: reading a bean pod"}
A young bean pod is the ovary of a bean flower. After fertilisation its wall grows into the pod (the fruit wall) and each fertilised ovule swells into a bean (a seed) attached to the pod by a short stalk. The pointed tip of the pod is what remains of the style. Sometimes you find a tiny, shrivelled "bean" between the normal ones: that ovule was never fertilised, so it did not develop.
:::

## Germination

A dry seed has very little water, so its enzymes are inactive and its respiration is extremely slow. Germination starts when the conditions are right.

### The conditions needed

- **Water**: the seed absorbs water through its micropyle and testa. It swells and the testa splits. Water activates the enzymes, dissolves the products of digestion and carries them to the embryo, and makes the new cells swell as they grow.
- **Oxygen**: for aerobic respiration, which releases the energy needed for cell division and growth.
- **A suitable temperature**: enzymes work fastest near their optimum. Cold soil slows germination; very high temperatures denature enzymes and kill the embryo.

Most seeds do **not** need light to germinate, because the embryo uses its stored food. The seedling needs light later, once its leaves begin to photosynthesise.

### Enzymes in germination (ZIMSEC)

The food store is made of large, insoluble molecules that cannot move to the growing regions. Enzymes digest them into small, soluble molecules:

| Enzyme | Food stored | Products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amylase | Starch (e.g. maize endosperm, bean cotyledons) | Maltose (which maltase then breaks down to glucose) |
| Protease | Proteins (e.g. beans) | Amino acids |
| Lipase | Fats and oils (e.g. groundnuts, sunflower seeds) | Fatty acids and glycerol |

The products move to the radicle and plumule. Glucose is used in respiration and to build cellulose for new cell walls; amino acids are built into new proteins. The **radicle** emerges first and grows down, anchoring the seedling and absorbing water. The **plumule** then grows up towards the light.

Until its first leaves photosynthesise, the seedling lives on its food store, so its **dry mass falls**: some stored food is respired and lost as carbon dioxide and water. Once the leaves photosynthesise faster than the seedling respires, the dry mass rises again.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the fall in dry mass during germination]
Twenty maize grains had a dry mass of $6.40\ \text{g}$. After 6 days of germinating in the dark, the twenty seedlings were dried and had a dry mass of $5.12\ \text{g}$.

1. Decrease in dry mass $= 6.40 - 5.12 = 1.28\ \text{g}$.
2. Percentage decrease $= \dfrac{1.28}{6.40} \times 100 = 20\%$.
3. Explanation: stored starch was digested and the glucose was used in respiration; the carbon dioxide produced was lost, and in the dark no photosynthesis replaced the food.
:::

### Investigating the conditions for germination

Five containers each hold ten bean seeds of similar size on cotton wool. Container A is the control; each of the others changes **one** condition.

| Container | Set-up | Condition tested | Result after 5 days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Moist cotton wool, air, $25^\circ\text{C}$ | Control: all conditions present | Most seeds germinate |
| B | Dry cotton wool, air, $25^\circ\text{C}$ | No water | None germinate |
| C | Moist cotton wool, air, in a fridge at about $4^\circ\text{C}$ | Low temperature | None, or very slow |
| D | Seeds under boiled, cooled water with a layer of oil on top, $25^\circ\text{C}$ | Very little oxygen | None, or very few |
| E | Boiled seeds on moist cotton wool, air, $25^\circ\text{C}$ | Seeds must be alive | None germinate |

Boiling the water in D drives out dissolved oxygen, and the oil layer stops oxygen from the air dissolving again. This is safer than using corrosive chemicals to absorb oxygen. The boiled seeds in E show that only living seeds germinate: boiling kills the embryo and denatures its enzymes.

To keep the test fair, use the same type, number and size of seeds and the same volume of water in every moist container, and count the seeds after the same time. Repeating the whole experiment makes the results more reliable. Seed bought for planting is often coated with a coloured chemical dressing, so wash your hands after handling it.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: percentage germination]
Before the rains, Nyasha checks the sorghum seed she kept from last season. She rolls 50 seeds in a damp cloth and keeps it warm. After 6 days, 42 have germinated.

1. Percentage germination $= \dfrac{\text{seeds germinated}}{\text{seeds tested}} \times 100$.
2. $= \dfrac{42}{50} \times 100 = 84\%$.
3. About 16 seeds in every 100 will not grow, so she can sow a little more thickly to make up for them.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pollination and fertilisation are the same"}
Pollination is the **transfer of pollen** to a stigma. Fertilisation comes later: the **fusion of nuclei** inside the ovule.
:::

:::mistake{title="The pollen grain travels down to the ovule"}
The pollen grain stays on the stigma. Only the **pollen tube** grows down the style, carrying the male nucleus.
:::

:::mistake{title="The ovary becomes the seed"}
The **ovule** becomes the seed; the **ovary** becomes the fruit.
:::

:::mistake{title="Seeds need light and soil to germinate"}
Most seeds germinate in the dark, and on cotton wool or paper. They need water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
:::

:::mistake{title="Boiled seeds failed because they had no water"}
Boiled seeds had water. They did not germinate because boiling **killed the embryo** and denatured its enzymes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When you **explain** why a condition is needed, link it to a process: water activates enzymes and transports food; oxygen is for aerobic respiration, releasing energy for growth; temperature affects enzyme activity.
:::

:::exam-tip
Describe the pollen tube in order, using the key words **stigma, style, ovary, micropyle, ovule, male nucleus, fuses**.
:::

:::exam-tip
In investigation questions, name the **control**, the **one condition** changed in each container, and the variables kept the same (type, number and size of seeds; volume of water; time).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A pollen tube grows from the pollen grain down the style and enters the ovule through the micropyle; the male nucleus fuses with the female nucleus, forming a zygote.
- After fertilisation the zygote becomes the embryo, the ovule becomes the seed, its outer layers become the testa and the ovary becomes the fruit.
- Germination needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature; most seeds do not need light.
- Water activates amylase, protease and lipase, which digest the food store into soluble products used for respiration and growth.
- Dry mass falls during early germination and rises once the leaves photosynthesise.
:::
