## Introduction

Walk home through a weedy field late in the rainy season and you may find your socks covered in thin black **blackjack** fruits. On a hot, dry afternoon in msasa woodland you may hear sharp cracks as ripe pods split open and fling out their seeds. When children eat **mazhanje** and spit out the seeds, they are helping the tree too. All of these are ways in which plants spread their seeds.

Each seed carries a tiny plant, the **embryo**, with a store of food to keep it alive until it can make its own. In this lesson you will learn the parts of a bean seed and a maize grain, why seeds are carried away from the parent plant, and how fruits and seeds are adapted for the journey. You should already know that a seed develops from a fertilised ovule and a fruit from an ovary.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Seed"}
A structure formed from a fertilised ovule, containing an embryo and a food store, protected by a seed coat (the **testa**).
:::

:::definition{term="Fruit"}
A structure formed from the ovary of a flower after fertilisation, containing one or more seeds.
:::

:::definition{term="Seed dispersal"}
The carrying of seeds, or the fruits that contain them, away from the parent plant.
:::

## The bean seed: a dicotyledon

Soak a bean seed in water overnight. It swells and the testa becomes soft enough to peel off. The table lists the parts you can find.

| Part | Where it is | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testa | Tough outer seed coat | Protects the embryo from damage, insects, fungi and drying out |
| Hilum | Oval scar on the inner curve of the seed | Marks where the seed was attached to the pod by a short stalk |
| Micropyle | Tiny pore at one end of the hilum | Lets water in at the start of germination |
| Cotyledons (two) | The two large, fleshy halves of the seed | Store food (mainly starch and protein) for the embryo |
| Radicle | Small pointed structure near the micropyle | The young root: the first part to grow out |
| Plumule | Lies between the cotyledons, with tiny folded leaves | The young shoot |

The radicle, plumule and cotyledons together make up the **embryo**. A bean is a **dicotyledon** ("two seed leaves"). Its food is stored in the cotyledons, so a mature bean seed has no separate endosperm.

## The maize grain: a monocotyledon

A maize grain is not just a seed: it is a small **fruit** containing a single seed. The thin fruit wall is fused to the testa, so you cannot peel off a separate seed coat as you can with a bean. On the outside you can see a pointed base where the grain was attached to the cob, and you may find a tiny scar left by the silk (the style). The embryo shows as a pale patch on one flat face.

Cut a soaked grain lengthwise through the pale patch and you can find:

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Fruit wall fused with the testa | Protects the contents |
| Endosperm | The large food store, mainly starch; it fills most of the grain |
| Cotyledon (one) | A shield-shaped structure pressed against the endosperm; it absorbs digested food and passes it to the embryo |
| Plumule, inside a protective sheath | The young shoot |
| Radicle, inside a protective sheath | The young root |

Maize is a **monocotyledon** ("one seed leaf"). Sorghum, millet, rice and wheat grains have the same plan.

:::example{title="Example 1: finding the food stores with iodine"}
Rudo splits a soaked bean seed and cuts a soaked maize grain lengthwise, then adds a drop of iodine solution to each cut surface. On the bean, both cotyledons turn blue-black. On the maize grain, the large endosperm turns blue-black but the small embryo stays a paler yellow-brown. She concludes that the bean stores starch in its **cotyledons** and maize stores starch in its **endosperm**, while the embryo itself holds very little starch.
:::

### Comparing the bean seed and the maize grain

| Feature | Bean seed | Maize grain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Group | Dicotyledon | Monocotyledon |
| Cotyledons | Two | One |
| Main food store | The cotyledons | The endosperm |
| Covering | Testa only, separate from the pod | Testa fused with the fruit wall |
| Seed or fruit? | A seed | A one-seeded fruit |

A useful rule: a **seed** has one scar, the hilum, plus a micropyle. A **fruit** has two scars: one where it was attached to the plant and one left by the style.

## Why seeds are dispersed

If every seed fell beneath its parent, the seedlings would be **overcrowded**. They would compete with each other and with the parent for light, water, mineral ions and space, and many would die. Pests and diseases of the parent could also spread to them easily.

Dispersal:

- reduces **overcrowding and competition**;
- lets the species **colonise new areas**, such as cleared land, riverbanks or disturbed ground;
- improves the **chance that the species survives**, because seedlings are spread over many places with different conditions.

## Methods of dispersal

| Method | Adaptations | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wind | Small, light seeds; wings or tufts of hairs that give a large surface area, so the fruit falls slowly and drifts further | Combretum (bushwillow) fruits with four papery wings; flat winged jacaranda seeds; milkweed seeds with tufts of silky hairs |
| Animals: hooks | Hooks, barbs or spines that catch on fur, feathers or clothes | Blackjack fruits with barbed bristles; spiny devil's thorn fruits |
| Animals: food | Fleshy, sweet, brightly coloured or scented fruits; seeds with hard coats that survive digestion | Mazhanje, marula (eaten by elephants and other animals), wild figs (birds), guavas |
| Water | Fruits that float, with air spaces or fibres and a waterproof skin | Coconut |
| Self (explosive) | Pods dry unevenly until they split suddenly and twist, flinging the seeds out | Bean and cowpea pods; msasa pods |

Seeds eaten in fleshy fruits pass out in the animal's droppings, often far from the parent tree and with a little manure around them. Hooked fruits fall off when the animal grooms itself or rubs against something. Explosive pods throw seeds only a short distance, but they need no help from wind or animals.

:::example{title="Example 2: what do the wings do?"}
Tendai dropped winged Combretum fruits from a height of 2 m in a still room and timed their fall. Then he cut the wings off and repeated it.

| Fruits | Fall times / s | Mean / s |
| --- | --- | --- |
| With wings | 2.8, 3.1, 3.0 | 2.97 |
| Wings removed | 0.8, 0.7, 0.9 | 0.80 |

With wings, the fruits took more than three times as long to fall. The wings do not make the fruit lighter: they give it a large surface area, which increases air resistance, so it falls slowly and a breeze has longer to carry it away from the parent tree.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: identifying a dispersal method"}
A fruit is small and dry, with a hard pointed end bearing two stiff barbed spines. It has no wings, no flesh and no air spaces. The barbs are the clue: it is dispersed by **animals**, hooking onto fur or clothing, like a blackjack fruit.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="A maize grain is a seed"}
A maize grain is a one-seeded **fruit**: its fruit wall is fused with the testa.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the hilum and the micropyle"}
The **hilum** is a scar where the seed was attached. The **micropyle** is the tiny pore beside it that lets water in.
:::

:::mistake{title="Bean seeds store food in the endosperm"}
A bean stores its food in its two **cotyledons**. The endosperm is the food store of the maize grain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wings make fruits lighter"}
Wings increase the **surface area** and air resistance, so the fruit falls more slowly and drifts further.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dispersal and pollination are the same"}
Pollination moves **pollen** to a stigma. Dispersal moves **seeds or fruits** away from the parent plant.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "how is this fruit adapted?", give the **feature and its purpose** together: "it has four papery wings, which slow its fall so the wind carries it further".
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn one named local example for each method of dispersal, and say which feature of that example matches the method.
:::

:::exam-tip
When comparing a bean seed and a maize grain, write **matched pairs**: "a bean has two cotyledons; maize has one".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A bean seed has a testa, hilum and micropyle outside, and two food-storing cotyledons, a radicle and a plumule inside.
- A maize grain is a one-seeded fruit: its fruit wall is fused with the testa, it stores food in the endosperm and it has one cotyledon.
- Dispersal reduces overcrowding and competition, lets the species colonise new areas and improves its chances of survival.
- Wind-dispersed fruits are light, with wings or hairs; animal-dispersed fruits have hooks or are fleshy and edible with hard seeds.
- Water-dispersed fruits float; explosive pods such as bean and msasa pods split open and fling out their seeds.
:::
