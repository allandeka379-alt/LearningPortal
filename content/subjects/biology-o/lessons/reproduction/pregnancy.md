## Introduction

Rudo's aunt is pregnant and goes to the antenatal clinic in Bulawayo every month. The nurses weigh her, check her blood pressure, test her for HIV and syphilis, give her iron and folic acid tablets, and warn her not to smoke or drink alcohol. Each of these steps makes sense once you know how a baby develops inside the uterus and how it is supplied by its mother.

In this lesson you will follow the zygote from fertilisation to implantation, learn how the placenta, umbilical cord and amniotic fluid support the developing baby, and find out what can harm it. You should already know the parts of the female reproductive system and what fertilisation is.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Both boards: development of the zygote, implantation, and the functions of the umbilical cord, placenta, amniotic sac and amniotic fluid. **Cambridge Supplement**: the substances exchanged across the placenta, and the pathogens and toxins that can cross it. **ZIMSEC**: the roles and limitations of the placenta and the dangers of drugs, smoking and alcohol in pregnancy.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Implantation"}
The embedding of the embryo (a ball of cells) in the thick lining of the uterus, about a week after fertilisation.
:::

:::definition{term="Embryo and fetus"}
The developing baby is called an embryo while its main organs are forming; after about eight weeks, once these have begun to form, it is called a fetus.
:::

:::definition{term="Placenta"}
An organ in the wall of the uterus, formed from tissues of both the embryo and the mother, through which substances are exchanged between the mother's blood and the fetus's blood.
:::

:::definition{term="Amniotic fluid"}
The liquid inside the amniotic sac (amnion) that surrounds and protects the fetus.
:::

## From zygote to embryo

:::example{title="Example 1: the first week"}
1. A sperm fertilises an egg in the **oviduct**, forming a **zygote**.
2. The zygote divides by **mitosis** again and again as cilia and muscle contractions move it down the oviduct, forming a **ball of cells**: the embryo.
3. After a few days it reaches the **uterus**.
4. About a week after fertilisation it **implants** in the thick, blood-rich lining of the uterus.
5. Its cells keep dividing and begin to **specialise**, forming tissues and organs. Some of the outer cells grow into the uterus wall and help to form the **placenta**.
:::

A pregnancy lasts about nine months: around 38 weeks from fertilisation, usually counted as 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period.

## The placenta and the umbilical cord

The **umbilical cord** joins the fetus to the placenta. It contains:

- **umbilical arteries**, carrying blood **from the fetus** to the placenta, with carbon dioxide and urea;
- an **umbilical vein**, carrying blood **to the fetus** from the placenta, with oxygen and nutrients.

In the placenta, the fetus's blood flows through capillaries inside many finger-like **villi**. The villi sit in spaces filled with the mother's blood. The two bloods come very close but **do not mix**.

:::keyfact
The placenta is adapted for exchange: the villi give a **large surface area**, the barrier between the two bloods is **very thin**, and blood flows constantly on both sides, keeping **concentration gradients** steep.
:::

### What crosses the placenta (Cambridge Supplement)

| From mother to fetus | From fetus to mother |
| --- | --- |
| Oxygen | Carbon dioxide |
| Glucose, amino acids, fatty acids | Urea and other excretory products |
| Water, mineral ions and vitamins | |
| Antibodies, giving passive immunity | |

Substances cross mainly by **diffusion**. Later in pregnancy the placenta also secretes the hormones **progesterone** and oestrogen, which keep the lining of the uterus in place.

### Why the two bloods are kept apart

- The mother's blood is at a **higher pressure**, which could damage the fetus's delicate blood vessels.
- Mother and fetus may have **different blood groups**, which could make the blood clump.
- The barrier keeps out **many** pathogens and large molecules.

### Limitations of the placenta

The placenta is not a perfect barrier. Some **pathogens** cross it, such as the **rubella** (German measles) virus, **HIV** and the bacterium that causes **syphilis**. Many **toxins and drugs** also cross it: nicotine and carbon monoxide from cigarette smoke, alcohol, and drugs such as heroin and codeine.

## The amniotic sac and amniotic fluid

The fetus grows inside the **amniotic sac**, a membrane filled with **amniotic fluid**. The fluid:

- **protects** the fetus from bumps and knocks by absorbing shocks;
- **supports** it and lets it **move**, which helps its muscles and bones develop;
- helps to keep its **temperature** steady.

Just before or during birth the sac breaks and the fluid flows out ("the waters break").

## Dangers to the developing baby

| Danger | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Smoking | Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen carried by the blood; nicotine narrows blood vessels, so less blood reaches the placenta. The baby may grow slowly and have a **low birth mass**, or be born early; the risk of miscarriage and stillbirth rises |
| Alcohol | Crosses the placenta and can damage the developing brain and other organs (**fetal alcohol spectrum disorders**), causing learning difficulties and poor growth. No amount is known to be safe |
| Drugs | Heroin, codeine and other drugs cross the placenta; the baby may be born **dependent** and suffer withdrawal. Some medicines can cause birth defects, so only medicines advised by a health worker should be taken |
| Pathogens | Rubella early in pregnancy can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects; syphilis can cause stillbirth; HIV can pass to the baby |

:::example{title="Example 2: preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV"}
At the antenatal clinic, every pregnant woman is offered an HIV test. If a mother has HIV, she takes **antiretroviral therapy** throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding, which keeps the amount of virus in her blood very low; her baby is given preventive medicine after birth and is tested. These **prevention of mother-to-child transmission** (PMTCT) programmes make it very unlikely that the baby is infected.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a warning from history"}
In the late 1950s and early 1960s, a medicine called thalidomide was given to some pregnant women for morning sickness. It crossed the placenta, and thousands of babies were born with shortened or missing limbs. This is why medicines are tested carefully for safety in pregnancy and why pregnant women should take only medicines advised by a health worker.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the mother's blood flows into the fetus"}
The two bloods **never mix**. Substances diffuse across the thin barrier between them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the placenta stops everything harmful"}
Alcohol, nicotine, carbon monoxide, many drugs and some pathogens (such as HIV and the rubella virus) **can** cross.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying amniotic fluid feeds the fetus"}
Food reaches the fetus through the **placenta and umbilical cord**. Amniotic fluid protects and supports it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the umbilical artery and vein"}
As in the rest of the body, arteries carry blood **away from** the fetus's heart (to the placenta), and the vein carries blood **back** to the fetus.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain how the placenta is adapted", give the feature **and** its effect: villi (large surface area), thin barrier (short diffusion distance), good blood supply (steep gradient).
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked what passes across the placenta, give the **direction** too: oxygen and glucose to the fetus; carbon dioxide and urea to the mother.
:::

:::exam-tip
For dangers in pregnancy, name the substance, say that it **crosses the placenta**, and give a specific effect on the fetus.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The zygote divides by mitosis to form a ball of cells (embryo), which implants in the uterus lining about a week after fertilisation.
- The placenta exchanges substances between mother and fetus without the bloods mixing; the umbilical cord links the fetus to it.
- Supplement: oxygen, nutrients, water, ions and antibodies pass to the fetus; carbon dioxide and urea pass to the mother.
- Amniotic fluid in the amniotic sac protects and supports the fetus and lets it move.
- Some pathogens (HIV, rubella virus) and toxins (nicotine, carbon monoxide, alcohol, drugs) cross the placenta; smoking, alcohol and drugs harm the fetus, and PMTCT programmes protect babies from HIV.
:::
