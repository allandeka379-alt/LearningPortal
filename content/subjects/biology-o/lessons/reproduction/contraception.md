## Introduction

Couples may wish to decide whether and when to have children, and how far apart. Clinics across Zimbabwe, including those of the Zimbabwe National Family Planning Council, offer **family planning** advice and methods. Choosing a method is a personal decision, best made with information from a health worker.

This lesson explains the **biology** of each method: which step of reproduction it blocks, how reliable it is, and whether it also protects against sexually transmitted infections (STIs). You should already know the reproductive systems and the menstrual cycle.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
This lesson covers ZIMSEC topic 8.6.7: the methods of contraception named in the syllabus, how they work, and their reliability, advantages and disadvantages.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Contraception"}
Any method used to prevent pregnancy.
:::

:::definition{term="Barrier method"}
A method that stops sperm reaching the egg, such as a condom or a diaphragm.
:::

:::definition{term="Hormonal method"}
A method that uses hormones, similar to oestrogen and progesterone, mainly to stop ovulation; examples are the pill, the injection and the implant.
:::

:::definition{term="Sterilisation"}
A surgical method that permanently stops sperm getting into the semen (vasectomy in men) or stops eggs and sperm meeting in the oviducts (tubal ligation in women).
:::

## How contraception works

For a pregnancy to begin, an **egg must be released**, a **sperm must reach and fertilise it**, and the **embryo must implant**. Every method blocks one or more of these steps.

## The methods

| Method | How it prevents pregnancy | Reliability | Protects against STIs? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Abstinence | No sexual intercourse, so sperm never enter the vagina | Complete | Yes |
| Withdrawal | The penis is withdrawn before ejaculation | Low: sperm may be released before ejaculation | No |
| Rhythm method | Intercourse is avoided during the fertile phase of the cycle | Low to medium: ovulation varies | No |
| Spermicide | A cream, gel or foam in the vagina kills sperm | Low when used alone | No |
| Male or female condom | A thin sheath traps semen, so sperm cannot enter the uterus | High if used correctly every time | **Yes** |
| Diaphragm | A soft dome over the cervix, used with spermicide, stops sperm entering the uterus | Medium | Very little |
| Intra-uterine device (IUD) | A small copper device in the uterus kills sperm and prevents implantation | Very high | No |
| Pill, injection or implant | Hormones stop ovulation and thicken the mucus at the cervix | Very high if used correctly | No |
| Vasectomy | The sperm ducts are cut and tied, so semen contains no sperm | Very high; permanent | No |
| Tubal ligation | The oviducts are cut and tied, so eggs and sperm cannot meet | Very high; permanent | No |

### Natural methods

**Abstinence** is the only method that is completely effective against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections. **Withdrawal** needs great self-control and fails often, because fluid released before ejaculation can contain sperm. The **rhythm method** uses the menstrual cycle to work out the fertile days; it has no side effects and is accepted by some religions, but because the day of ovulation varies, and sperm can survive for several days, it often fails.

### Barrier and chemical methods

**Condoms** are thin sheaths: the male condom covers the erect penis and the female condom lines the vagina. Used correctly every time, they are reliable and are the only method, apart from abstinence, that also reduces the spread of **STIs, including HIV**. They are widely available and often free at clinics, but they can tear or slip if used wrongly. A **diaphragm** is placed over the cervix before intercourse and used with **spermicide**, which kills any sperm that get past its edge; it must be fitted correctly and left in place for some hours afterwards.

### Intra-uterine device

An **IUD** (sometimes called a loop) is a small device, usually containing copper, placed in the uterus by a trained health worker. Copper is toxic to sperm, so fertilisation is prevented, and an embryo is unlikely to implant. It works for many years, but it can make periods heavier and gives no protection against STIs.

### Hormonal methods

The **pill** is taken every day; the **injection** is given every few months; the **implant** is a small rod placed under the skin of the upper arm that releases hormones for several years. They work mainly by **stopping ovulation**, and they also thicken the mucus at the cervix so that sperm cannot pass. They are very reliable when used correctly, but missing pills or late injections makes pregnancy possible, some women have side effects such as irregular bleeding or headaches, and they give **no protection against STIs**.

### Surgical methods

In a **vasectomy**, the sperm ducts are cut and tied. The man still produces semen, but it contains no sperm, and his hormones are unchanged. Sperm already beyond the cut can remain for some weeks, so another method is needed until a test shows the semen is free of sperm. In **tubal ligation**, the oviducts are cut and tied or blocked. Both methods are **permanent**, so they are chosen only by people who are sure they want no more children.

:::example{title="Example 1: matching a method to a need"}
1. A couple who have the number of children they want, and are certain they want no more: **vasectomy** or **tubal ligation**, because these are permanent.
2. A woman who wants to delay her next pregnancy for a few years without taking a pill every day: an **implant**, an **injection** or an **IUD**.
3. A couple who are not sure of each other's HIV status: **condoms**, which reduce the risk of STIs as well as pregnancy, used together with another method if they wish (**dual protection**).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking all contraceptives protect against HIV"}
Only **condoms** (and abstinence) reduce the risk of STIs. The pill, injection, implant, IUD and sterilisation protect against pregnancy only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a vasectomy stops ejaculation"}
After a vasectomy, a man still ejaculates semen; it simply contains no sperm.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking withdrawal or the 'safe period' is reliable"}
Sperm can be released before ejaculation, and ovulation varies, so both methods often fail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the pill kills sperm"}
The pill works mainly by **stopping ovulation**. Spermicides and copper IUDs are the methods that kill sperm.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "how does this method work?", name the **step it blocks**: no egg released (hormonal), sperm cannot reach the egg (barrier, sterilisation), sperm killed (spermicide, copper IUD), embryo does not implant (IUD).
:::

:::exam-tip
In "evaluate" questions, give an advantage **and** a disadvantage for each method, and always comment on protection against STIs.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the correct names: sperm ducts are cut in a vasectomy; oviducts are cut in tubal ligation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Contraception prevents pregnancy by stopping ovulation, stopping sperm meeting the egg, killing sperm or preventing implantation.
- Natural methods: abstinence (completely effective), withdrawal and the rhythm method (both unreliable).
- Barrier and chemical methods: condoms, diaphragms and spermicides; condoms also reduce the risk of STIs, including HIV.
- IUDs and hormonal pills, injections and implants are very reliable but give no STI protection.
- Vasectomy (sperm ducts cut) and tubal ligation (oviducts cut) are permanent methods.
:::
