## Introduction

How do you find out whether a bowl of sadza contains starch, or whether peanut butter contains fat? You cannot see the nutrients, but you can add a **reagent**, a chemical that changes colour (or appearance) when a particular substance is present. These simple tests are among the most common practical tasks in biology: they appear in the Cambridge practical papers (Paper 5 and Paper 6) and in ZIMSEC Paper 3, and they are easy marks once you know the method, the colours and the safety precautions.

In this lesson you will learn five tests: starch, reducing sugars, proteins, fats and oils, and (Cambridge only) vitamin C. You should already know the main groups of food molecules from the lesson on carbohydrates, proteins and lipids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reagent"}
A chemical added to a sample to show whether a particular substance is present.
:::

:::definition{term="Positive result"}
The colour change (or appearance) that shows the substance is present. A **negative result** shows that it was not detected.
:::

:::definition{term="Reducing sugar"}
A sugar that gives a positive result with Benedict's solution: glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose. Sucrose (table sugar) is **not** a reducing sugar.
:::

:::definition{term="Control"}
A tube set up in exactly the same way but with distilled water instead of the food. It shows what a negative result looks like.
:::

## Preparing a food sample

Most tests work on a liquid. Crush a small piece of solid food with a little distilled water using a pestle and mortar, then let the solids settle (or filter) and pour about 2 cm³ of the liquid into a clean test tube. Liquids such as milk or fruit juice can be tested directly. For the iodine test you can also drop the reagent straight onto a cut surface, such as a slice of potato. Use clean apparatus for every test, so that one food cannot contaminate another.

## The five tests

### Starch: the iodine test

1. Add a few drops of iodine solution to the food (solid or liquid). No heating is needed.
2. **Positive:** the orange-brown iodine solution turns **blue-black**.
3. **Negative:** it stays orange-brown.

### Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

1. Add an equal volume of Benedict's solution (for example 2 cm³ to 2 cm³ of food solution).
2. Stand the tube in a **hot water bath** (a beaker of water kept close to boiling) for about five minutes.
3. **Positive:** the blue solution turns green, yellow, orange or **brick-red**, depending on how much reducing sugar is present.
4. **Negative:** it stays blue.

| Colour after heating | Amount of reducing sugar |
| --- | --- |
| Blue | None detected |
| Green | Very little |
| Yellow | A little |
| Orange | A moderate amount |
| Brick-red | A lot |

The colours come from a brick-red solid (a precipitate) that forms in the blue solution: the more sugar, the more precipitate. This makes Benedict's test **semi-quantitative**: it can compare amounts, but only if the volumes, the heating time and the water-bath temperature are the same for every tube.

### Proteins: the biuret test

1. Add an equal volume of biuret solution to the food solution, or add dilute sodium hydroxide solution and then a few drops of dilute copper(II) sulfate solution.
2. Shake gently and wait a minute or two. **Do not heat.**
3. **Positive:** the blue colour turns **purple** (violet or lilac).
4. **Negative:** it stays blue.

### Fats and oils: the ethanol emulsion test

1. Crush a small amount of food and shake it with about 2 cm³ of **ethanol** in a dry test tube. Any fat dissolves in the ethanol.
2. Let the solids settle, then pour the ethanol into a second tube containing about 2 cm³ of **water**.
3. **Positive:** a **cloudy, milky-white emulsion** forms. Fat does not dissolve in water, so it comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.
4. **Negative:** the liquid stays clear.

The order matters: ethanol first, then water. Fat will not dissolve in water, so shaking the food with water first extracts nothing.

### Vitamin C: the DCPIP test (Cambridge only)

1. Put 1 cm³ of blue **DCPIP** solution in a test tube.
2. Add the fruit juice drop by drop from a dropping pipette, shaking gently after each drop.
3. **Positive:** the blue colour disappears and the DCPIP becomes **colourless**.
4. Count the drops needed. The **fewer** drops needed, the **more** vitamin C the juice contains.

:::note{title="Judging the DCPIP end-point"}
Strongly coloured juices can hide the change, so compare with a tube of the juice alone. In acidic juices DCPIP may look pink for a moment before it fades. Do not heat the juice: heating destroys vitamin C.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The tests at a glance"}
| Nutrient | Reagent | Heat? | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starch | Iodine solution | No | Orange-brown to blue-black |
| Reducing sugar | Benedict's solution | Yes, in a water bath | Blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red |
| Protein | Biuret solution | No | Blue to purple |
| Fat or oil | Ethanol, then water | No | Milky-white emulsion |
| Vitamin C (Cambridge) | DCPIP | No | Blue to colourless |
:::

## Safety

- Wear **eye protection**. Biuret solution contains sodium hydroxide, which can damage the eyes and skin; wash any splash off with plenty of water.
- Heat Benedict's tests in a **water bath**, not directly in a flame, which can make the hot liquid spit out of the tube. Point tubes away from people.
- **Ethanol is highly flammable.** Make sure no flames are lit while it is in use; if you need hot water for Benedict's test, heat it before the ethanol is opened, or use a kettle or an electric water bath.

## Controls and fair comparisons

A **water control** for each reagent shows the negative colour, which makes small changes easier to see. A **positive control**, such as a known glucose solution with Benedict's solution, checks that the reagent works. When you compare foods, keep the mass of food, the volume of water, the volumes of reagent and (for Benedict's test) the heating time and temperature the same.

## Examples

:::example{title="Interpreting the results for milk"}
Thandeka tested a sample of fresh milk.

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Iodine solution | Stayed orange-brown |
| Benedict's solution, heated | Turned orange |
| Biuret solution | Turned purple |
| Ethanol emulsion | Milky-white emulsion |

Milk contains a reducing sugar (lactose), protein and fat, but no starch. Notice that the conclusion says "a reducing sugar": Benedict's test cannot tell you **which** reducing sugar is present.
:::

:::example{title="Why table sugar gives a negative result"}
Farai dissolved table sugar (sucrose) in water and heated it with Benedict's solution. It stayed blue. Sucrose is a sugar, but it is not a reducing sugar, so the test is negative. If sucrose is first boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, it breaks down into glucose and fructose; after the acid is neutralised, Benedict's test is positive. This extra step is not one of the four tests named in the syllabus, but it explains why sucrose behaves differently.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing vitamin C in fruit juice (Cambridge)]
1 cm³ of DCPIP was decolourised by 0.40 cm³ of a standard solution containing 1.0 mg of vitamin C per cm³. The same volume of DCPIP needed 0.80 cm³ of fresh orange juice.
1. Mass of vitamin C that decolourises 1 cm³ of DCPIP $= 0.40\ \text{cm}^3 \times 1.0\ \text{mg/cm}^3 = 0.40\ \text{mg}$.
2. The orange juice supplied the same 0.40 mg in 0.80 cm³.
3. Concentration of vitamin C in the juice $= \dfrac{0.40\ \text{mg}}{0.80\ \text{cm}^3} = 0.50\ \text{mg/cm}^3$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to heat Benedict's test, or heating the others"}
Only Benedict's test needs heating. Without heat a reducing sugar gives no colour change. The biuret, iodine, emulsion and DCPIP tests are all done cold.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'clear' when you mean 'colourless'"}
"Clear" means see-through: a blue solution can be clear. DCPIP becomes **colourless**, and a negative emulsion test stays **clear**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Expecting every sugar to turn Benedict's solution red"}
Sucrose is not a reducing sugar, so on its own it leaves Benedict's solution blue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the emulsion a precipitate"}
The positive emulsion test is a milky-white **emulsion** of fat droplets, not a solid precipitate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one colour"}
"It went purple" is weaker than "it changed from blue to purple". State the colour before and after.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "Describe how you would test…", give four things: how you prepare the sample, the reagent you add (named exactly: iodine solution, Benedict's solution, biuret solution), whether you heat it, and the positive **and** negative results.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link every safety precaution to its hazard: "keep flames away **because** ethanol is highly flammable" earns the mark; "be careful" does not.
:::

:::exam-tip
When results are given in a table, write "contains starch" or "does not contain protein" and quote the colour that tells you.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Iodine solution turns from orange-brown to blue-black when starch is present.
- Benedict's solution, heated in a water bath, turns from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red with reducing sugars; the further along, the more sugar. Sucrose stays blue.
- Biuret solution turns from blue to purple with protein; no heating.
- Fat dissolved in ethanol forms a milky-white emulsion when poured into water; keep ethanol away from flames.
- Cambridge: DCPIP turns from blue to colourless with vitamin C; fewer drops needed means more vitamin C.
- Use a water control, and keep volumes and conditions the same when comparing foods.
:::
