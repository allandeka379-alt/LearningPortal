## Introduction

Every cell of a maize plant carries the instructions for building a maize plant, and every cell of your body carries instructions inherited from your parents. These instructions are stored in a molecule called **DNA**. Its structure explains two of the most important facts in biology: how the instructions are written, and how they can be copied exactly every time a cell divides.

For Cambridge candidates, the structure of DNA is **Supplement** (Extended) content. ZIMSEC candidates also need the elements and building blocks of nucleic acids and the roles of DNA and RNA. You should already know that chromosomes are found in the nucleus, that bacteria have a circular loop of DNA, and that proteins are chains of amino acids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nucleic acid"}
A large molecule made of a chain of nucleotides. The two kinds are DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid).
:::

:::definition{term="Nucleotide"}
The building block of a nucleic acid: a sugar, a phosphate group and a base joined together.
:::

:::definition{term="Base"}
The part of a nucleotide that varies. DNA has four bases, known by their letters: A, T, C and G.
:::

:::definition{term="Complementary base pairing"}
The rule that a base on one strand of DNA always pairs with the same partner on the other strand: A with T, and C with G.
:::

:::definition{term="Double helix"}
The shape of a DNA molecule: two strands coiled around each other, like a twisted ladder.
:::

:::definition{term="Gene"}
A length of DNA that codes for a protein.
:::

## What nucleic acids are made of (ZIMSEC)

Nucleic acids contain five elements: **carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus**. Compare this with carbohydrates and fats (carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only) and proteins (carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and sometimes sulfur).

Each nucleotide has three parts. Think of a bead with a hook: the sugar is the bead, the phosphate group joins it to the next bead, and the base sticks out to one side. Thousands, or even millions, of nucleotides joined sugar to phosphate make one long strand.

## The structure of DNA

A DNA molecule can be described like a twisted ladder:

- **Two strands**, each a long chain of nucleotides. The sugars and phosphates of each strand form the **sides of the ladder** (the backbone).
- The **bases** point inwards. Each base on one strand is joined to a base on the other strand by weak bonds (hydrogen bonds), forming the **rungs** of the ladder.
- The bases always pair in the same way: **A with T** and **C with G**.
- The two strands are **coiled** around each other to make a **double helix**.

:::keyfact{title="Base-pairing rules"}
A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. You do not need the full names of the bases for Cambridge: the letters are enough.
:::

Because of these rules, the two strands are **complementary**, not identical: if you know the sequence of bases on one strand, you can work out the other. It also means that in any double-stranded DNA, the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G.

:::note{title="A famous discovery"}
The double-helix structure was worked out in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick, using X-ray images of DNA made by Rosalind Franklin.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: writing a complementary strand]
One strand of a section of DNA reads T A C G G A T C. Write the sequence of bases on the other strand.
1. Pair each base in turn, starting from the first: T pairs with A, A with T, C with G and G with C.
2. T → A, A → T, C → G, G → C, G → C, A → T, T → A, C → G.
3. The complementary strand reads A T G C C T A G.
4. Check: every pair is either A–T or C–G.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: base percentages]
In a sample of DNA, 28% of the bases are A. Find the percentages of T, C and G.
1. A pairs with T, so T = A = 28%.
2. A and T together make $28 + 28 = 56\%$ of the bases.
3. C and G make up the rest: $100 - 56 = 44\%$.
4. C pairs with G, so they are equal: $C = G = 44 \div 2 = 22\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: numbers of bases]
A section of DNA has 1200 base pairs, and 30% of its bases are C. How many bases are A?
1. Number of bases $= 1200 \times 2 = 2400$.
2. C $= 30\%$ of $2400 = 0.30 \times 2400 = 720$, and G $=$ C $= 720$.
3. A + T $= 2400 - 720 - 720 = 960$.
4. A $=$ T, so A $= 960 \div 2 = 480$.
:::

## Why the structure matters

**Copying DNA.** Before a cell divides, its DNA is copied. The two strands separate, and new nucleotides pair with the bases on each old strand, A with T and C with G. Each old strand therefore builds a new partner that matches the one it lost, giving two identical DNA molecules, one for each new cell.

**Storing information.** The order of the bases along a strand is a code. The sequence of bases in a gene decides the order of amino acids in a protein, and the order of amino acids decides the shape and job of the protein, for example an enzyme.

## Roles of DNA and RNA (ZIMSEC)

| | DNA | RNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strands | Two, coiled into a double helix | One |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | A, T, C, G | A, U, C, G (uracil, U, instead of thymine) |
| Where found | Chromosomes in the nucleus (a loop in the cytoplasm in bacteria) | Made in the nucleus; works in the cytoplasm |
| Role | Stores the genetic information (genes) | Helps to make proteins from the instructions in genes |

**DNA and inheritance.** Genes are carried on the chromosomes. Sperm and egg cells each carry one set of chromosomes, so a child inherits DNA, and therefore genes, from both parents.

**DNA and RNA in protein synthesis.** The DNA stays in the nucleus. When a protein is needed:

1. A copy of the gene is made in the nucleus as a molecule of **messenger RNA (mRNA)**.
2. The mRNA moves out of the nucleus into the cytoplasm, to a **ribosome**.
3. The ribosome reads the sequence of bases on the mRNA and joins amino acids together **in the order the bases code for**.
4. The finished chain of amino acids folds into a protein, such as an enzyme or haemoglobin.

:::example{title="RNA as genetic material"}
Some viruses use RNA, not DNA, to store their genetic information. HIV, the virus that causes AIDS, is one of them. RNA has a single strand, so its bases are not paired and the rule "A equals T" does not apply to it.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pairing the wrong bases"}
A pairs only with T, and C only with G. A–G, A–C or C–T pairs never form in DNA. U (uracil) belongs to RNA, not DNA.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the two strands are the same"}
The strands are **complementary**: the sequence on one strand fixes the sequence on the other, but they are not copies of each other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming all four bases are 25%"}
Only the pairs are equal: A = T and C = G. A sample can have 35% A and 15% C.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the bases on the outside"}
The sugars and phosphates form the backbone on the outside; the bases are on the inside, paired across the middle.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When writing a complementary strand, keep the same order: write the partner of the first base first. Check every pair is A–T or C–G before you move on.
:::

:::exam-tip
For base percentages, use two facts: A = T and C = G, and all four add up to 100%. So A + C = 50% in double-stranded DNA.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the key words in descriptions: "two strands", "coiled into a double helix", "bonds between complementary bases", "A with T and C with G".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- DNA is made of two strands of nucleotides coiled into a double helix.
- The strands are held together by bonds between pairs of bases: A always with T, and C always with G.
- The strands are complementary, so in DNA A = T and C = G, and one strand's sequence gives the other.
- ZIMSEC: nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P and are built from nucleotides (sugar, phosphate, base).
- ZIMSEC: DNA carries the genes passed from parents to offspring; mRNA carries a copy of a gene to the ribosomes, where amino acids are joined in the coded order.
:::
