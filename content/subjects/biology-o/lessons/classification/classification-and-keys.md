## Introduction

Scientists have named about two million species, and many more have not yet been described. To make sense of so many organisms, biologists sort them into groups, give each one a name that is understood all over the world, and use **keys** to identify them.

These skills are practical. A fisheries officer at Lake Kariba needs to identify the fish in a catch, and an agronomist needs to tell a harmless insect from a crop pest. In this lesson you will learn how organisms are classified and named, how to use and build dichotomous keys and, for the Cambridge Supplement, how DNA is used to show relationships. You should already know the seven characteristics of living organisms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Classification"}
Sorting organisms into groups according to the features they share.
:::

:::definition{term="Species"}
A group of organisms that can reproduce with each other to produce fertile offspring.
:::

:::definition{term="Genus"}
A group of closely related species. Its name is the first part of an organism's scientific name.
:::

:::definition{term="Binomial system"}
The international system of naming each species with two names: its genus followed by its species.
:::

:::definition{term="Dichotomous key"}
A key that identifies organisms by a series of pairs of contrasting statements about features you can see.
:::

## Groups within groups

Organisms are placed in large groups (kingdoms), which are split into smaller and smaller groups. The smaller the group, the more features its members share. The lion, for example, belongs to the animal kingdom, the mammals, the cat family, the genus *Panthera* and finally the species *Panthera leo*. Only the species contains organisms that can breed together to produce fertile young.

:::example{title="Is it a separate species?"}
A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is almost always infertile. Because they cannot produce **fertile** offspring together, horses and donkeys are separate species.
:::

## The binomial system

Every species has a two-part scientific name:

- the **genus** name comes first and starts with a capital letter;
- the **species** name comes second and starts with a small letter;
- the whole name is printed in *italics* (underlined when handwritten).

| Common name | Scientific name |
| --- | --- |
| Maize | *Zea mays* |
| Human | *Homo sapiens* |
| African elephant | *Loxodonta africana* |
| Lion | *Panthera leo* |
| Leopard | *Panthera pardus* |
| Tigerfish | *Hydrocynus vittatus* |
| Msasa | *Brachystegia spiciformis* |

Maize is called chibage in Shona, umbila in isiNdebele and maize in English, but scientists everywhere call it *Zea mays*. One scientific name avoids confusion. The names also show relationships: the lion and the leopard share the genus *Panthera*, so they are more closely related to each other than either is to the cheetah, *Acinonyx jubatus*.

## Using a dichotomous key

"Dichotomous" means dividing into two. Each numbered step (a **couplet**) gives two contrasting statements. Choose the one that matches your organism and follow it to the next step, until you reach a name.

:::example{title="A key to four fish from Lake Kariba"}
| Step | Statement | Go to or name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1(a) | Barbels (whisker-like feelers) around the mouth | sharptooth catfish |
| 1(b) | No barbels around the mouth | go to 2 |
| 2(a) | Large, sharp teeth visible even when the mouth is closed | tigerfish |
| 2(b) | Teeth not visible when the mouth is closed | go to 3 |
| 3(a) | Deep body with a spiny fin along the back; adults longer than 20 cm | Kariba bream |
| 3(b) | Slender, silvery body; adults shorter than 10 cm | kapenta |

A fish with no barbels, no visible teeth and a slender silvery body 7 cm long follows 1(b), 2(b), 3(b): it is a **kapenta**.
:::

## Constructing a dichotomous key

1. List the organisms and the features you can **see** in each.
2. Choose a feature that splits the group into two. Write it as a pair of opposites: "legs present / legs absent".
3. Keep splitting each smaller group until every organism is on its own.
4. Number the couplets. A key for $n$ organisms needs $n - 1$ couplets.

:::worked-solution[Construct a key for an earthworm, a snail, a millipede and a grasshopper]
1. Features: earthworm (no legs, no shell); snail (no legs, shell); millipede (many pairs of legs); grasshopper (three pairs of legs).
2. Couplet 1 splits by legs: (a) legs absent, go to 2; (b) legs present, go to 3.
3. Couplet 2: (a) shell present ... snail; (b) no shell ... earthworm.
4. Couplet 3: (a) three pairs of legs ... grasshopper; (b) more than three pairs of legs ... millipede.
5. Check: four organisms and $4 - 1 = 3$ couplets. Each organism has exactly one route.
:::

Good keys use features that can be seen on every specimen and do not change: legs, wings, shells, the shape of leaves. Avoid behaviour ("is aggressive"), vague sizes ("big") and features that vary, such as the exact colour of a leaf.

## Classification and evolution (Supplement)

Classification aims to show **evolutionary relationships**: organisms in the same group are thought to share a common ancestor. Traditionally organisms were grouped by their body structure (morphology and anatomy). Today scientists also compare the **sequences of bases in DNA**. Species that share a more recent common ancestor have DNA base sequences that are more similar.

:::worked-solution[Which species is most closely related to species W?]
Part of the same gene was sequenced in four species:

| Species | Base sequence |
| --- | --- |
| W | `ATGGCATTCG` |
| X | `ATGGCATACG` |
| Y | `ATCGCTTACG` |
| Z | `TTCGATGACC` |

1. Compare each sequence with W, base by base.
2. X differs from W at 1 position, Y at 3 positions and Z at 7 positions.
3. X has the fewest differences, so X is most closely related to W: they share the most recent common ancestor. Z is the most distantly related.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing scientific names incorrectly"}
*Zea mays* is correct. "zea mays", "Zea Mays" and "ZEA MAYS" are all wrong. Give both parts, and underline the name if you are writing by hand.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving 'fertile' out of the definition of a species"}
Horses and donkeys can reproduce, but their offspring are infertile, so they are different species. The word "fertile" is essential.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting two features in one couplet"}
"Has wings and six legs / has no wings and eight legs" fails for an animal with wings and eight legs. Use one feature per couplet.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using features you cannot see"}
"Lives in rivers", "is fast" and "is poisonous" cannot be checked on a specimen or a drawing. Use visible structures.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When you use a key, write down your route (for example 1(b), 2(b), 3(a)). It helps you check your work and can earn credit even if you slip at the last step.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to construct a key, base every statement on features you can observe in the specimens or pictures you are given, and check that your key really does lead to every organism.
:::

:::exam-tip
In DNA questions, count the differences carefully, base by base. Fewer differences means a closer relationship (a more recent common ancestor).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organisms are classified into groups by shared features; smaller groups share more features.
- A species is a group of organisms that can reproduce to produce fertile offspring.
- Binomial names give the genus (capital letter) then the species (small letter), in italics, for example *Zea mays*.
- Dichotomous keys use pairs of contrasting statements about visible features; $n$ organisms need $n - 1$ couplets.
- Supplement: classification reflects evolutionary relationships; more similar DNA base sequences mean a more recent common ancestor.
:::
