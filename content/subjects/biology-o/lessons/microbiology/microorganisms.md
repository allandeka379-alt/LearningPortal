## Introduction

Leave a slice of bread in a warm kitchen for a week and fuzzy patches grow on it. Leave fresh milk standing in a covered pot and it turns into thick, tangy sour milk (amasi, or mukaka wakakora). A compost heap turns garden waste into dark, crumbly humus. Meanwhile, cholera, HIV and maize streak disease cause great harm. All of these are the work of organisms too small to see.

Most microorganisms are harmless, and many are essential. In this lesson you will learn the main features of viruses, bacteria and fungi, how they feed and reproduce, and why they matter to farmers, food producers, doctors and everyone else. You should already know the parts of plant and animal cells.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Microorganism"}
An organism too small to be seen clearly without a microscope, such as a bacterium, a yeast or a mould. Viruses are studied with them, although they are not cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Saprotroph"}
An organism that feeds on dead organic matter by secreting enzymes onto it and absorbing the small, soluble products.
:::

:::definition{term="Parasite and pathogen"}
A **parasite** lives on or in another living organism (its host) and feeds from it, harming it. A **pathogen** is an organism that causes disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Binary fission"}
Asexual reproduction in which one cell divides into two identical cells.
:::

## Viruses

- **Size**: most are about 20 to 300 nanometres across (1 nm is a millionth of a millimetre), so they can be seen only with an electron microscope.
- **Structure**: a core of genetic material (DNA or RNA) inside a **protein coat**. There is no cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosome or nucleus: a virus is **not a cell**.
- **Reproduction**: only **inside living host cells**. The virus's genetic material enters a cell and makes the cell build new viruses, which are released, often killing the cell.
- Outside a host, a virus does not feed, respire, excrete or grow. Every virus is a parasite.
- **Examples**: HIV, influenza, measles, polio, foot-and-mouth disease of cattle, and maize streak virus, spread from plant to plant by leafhoppers.

Antibiotics have **no effect** on viruses.

## Bacteria

Bacteria are single cells about 0.5 to 5 micrometres long (1 μm is a thousandth of a millimetre), visible with a light microscope at high power. A bacterial cell has:

- a **cell wall** (not made of cellulose) and a **cell membrane**;
- **cytoplasm** with **ribosomes**, but no mitochondria or chloroplasts;
- **no nucleus**: a single loop of **DNA** lies free in the cytoplasm;
- small extra rings of DNA called **plasmids**;
- in some, a **flagellum** for swimming or a slimy capsule.

Bacteria are spherical, rod-shaped, spiral or comma-shaped. The cholera bacterium, for example, is comma-shaped. They reproduce by **binary fission**. In warm, moist conditions with plenty of food, some divide as often as every 20 minutes. Some form tough spores that survive heat and drying. Most bacteria are saprotrophs or parasites.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how fast bacteria multiply]
One bacterium lands in a pot of stew left out on a warm evening. It divides every 30 minutes. How many bacteria are there after 4 hours?

1. 4 hours $= 240$ minutes, so the number of divisions $= 240 \div 30 = 8$.
2. The number doubles at each division: $2^8 = 256$.
3. One bacterium has become 256 in 4 hours, and each of those keeps dividing. This is why cooked food should be eaten hot or cooled and refrigerated quickly.
:::

## Fungi

Fungi have cells with **nuclei**, cell walls made of **chitin** (not cellulose) and **no chlorophyll**, so they cannot photosynthesise. They store carbohydrate as glycogen, not starch.

- **Body**: most fungi are made of thread-like **hyphae** that branch to form a network, the **mycelium**. **Yeast** is single-celled.
- **Nutrition**: most are **saprotrophs**, secreting enzymes onto their food and absorbing the products. Some are parasites, such as the rust fungi of maize and wheat and the fungi that cause ringworm and athlete's foot.
- **Reproduction**: by **spores**, which are tiny and carried by air. Yeast also reproduces by **budding**: a small bud grows out of the cell and separates.

| Fungus | What you see |
| --- | --- |
| Bread mould | White, fluffy hyphae; tiny black dots are spore cases (sporangia) full of spores |
| Yeast | Single oval cells, some with buds |
| Mushroom | The cap with gills and the stalk are only the spore-producing part; the mycelium spreads through the soil or wood |
| Penicillium | A blue-green mould; it makes the antibiotic penicillin |

Wild mushrooms collected in the rainy season are a valued food, but some are deadly: never eat one unless an expert has identified it.

### Comparing the three groups

| Feature | Virus | Bacterium | Fungus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A cell? | No | Yes, a single cell | Yes: single cells (yeast) or hyphae |
| Nucleus | None | None: DNA loop in cytoplasm | Present |
| Outer layer | Protein coat | Cell wall (not cellulose) | Cell wall of chitin |
| Reproduction | Only inside host cells | Binary fission | Spores; yeast by budding |
| Nutrition | Parasitic | Mostly saprotrophic or parasitic | Saprotrophic or parasitic |

## Economic importance

### Useful microorganisms

- **Decomposition**: bacteria and fungi break down dead organisms, dung and litter, releasing carbon dioxide and mineral ions such as nitrate for plants to reuse. Compost heaps and sewage works rely on them.
- **Bioremediation**: using microorganisms to break down pollutants, such as spilled oil or pesticides, into harmless substances.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: bacteria in the root nodules of groundnuts, soya beans and cowpeas turn nitrogen gas into compounds the plants can use. Farmers often coat soya bean seed with these bacteria before planting.
- **Food and drink**: yeast in bread, traditional opaque (sorghum) beer and other alcoholic drinks; bacteria in sour milk, yoghurt and cheese; edible mushrooms such as oyster mushrooms, grown on straw or chopped maize stalks.
- **Medicines**: penicillin from the mould *Penicillium*; human insulin from genetically modified bacteria.

### Harmful microorganisms

- **Human diseases**: bacteria cause cholera, tuberculosis and typhoid; viruses cause HIV infection, measles and influenza; fungi cause ringworm, athlete's foot and thrush.
- **Crop and livestock diseases**: maize streak virus; rust fungi; bacterial wilt of tomatoes, potatoes and tobacco; foot-and-mouth disease (a virus) and anthrax (a bacterium) in cattle.
- **Food spoilage**: bacteria and fungi feed on food, making it rot, sour or go mouldy, and some make toxins. **Aflatoxins**, made by moulds growing on damp groundnuts and maize, can damage the liver.

Food is preserved by taking away what microorganisms need: **drying** (kapenta, dried vegetables), **cooling**, **salting**, adding **sugar** (jam) or **vinegar** (pickles), and **heating** to kill them (boiling, pasteurising, canning).

:::example{title="Example 2: what does bread mould need?"}
Chipo put four slices of bread from the same loaf in sealed plastic bags: A moistened and kept at $25^\circ\text{C}$; B dry at $25^\circ\text{C}$; C moistened in a fridge at $4^\circ\text{C}$; D moistened, sprinkled with salt, at $25^\circ\text{C}$. After 5 days, A was covered in mould, D had a few small patches, B had very little and C had almost none. Mould needs **moisture** and grows fastest when **warm**; salt slows it. She left the bags sealed and threw them away unopened, because mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Viruses are very small bacteria"}
Viruses are **not cells**: just genetic material in a protein coat. They reproduce only inside living cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Antibiotics cure viral infections"}
Antibiotics act on **bacteria**. They do not affect viruses such as HIV or influenza.
:::

:::mistake{title="Fungi are plants"}
Fungi have **no chlorophyll**, have **chitin** cell walls and feed by absorbing food. They are a separate kingdom.
:::

:::mistake{title="Malaria and bilharzia are caused by bacteria"}
Malaria is caused by a protoctist (*Plasmodium*) and bilharzia by a flatworm (*Schistosoma*), not by bacteria or viruses.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the precise structural terms: **protein coat, genetic material, cell wall, loop of DNA, plasmids, hyphae, mycelium, spores, budding, binary fission**.
:::

:::exam-tip
For economic importance, give a **named microorganism and what it does**: "yeast releases carbon dioxide, which makes bread dough rise".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to compare groups, write **matched pairs** or a table: "bacteria have no nucleus; fungi have nuclei".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Viruses are genetic material inside a protein coat; they are not cells and reproduce only inside living host cells.
- Bacteria are single cells with a cell wall, no nucleus, a loop of DNA and plasmids; they reproduce by binary fission.
- Fungi have nuclei and chitin walls, feed as saprotrophs or parasites, are made of hyphae (or single cells, like yeast) and reproduce by spores or budding.
- Useful microorganisms decompose waste, clean up pollution, fix nitrogen and make bread, beer, sour milk, yoghurt, cheese, penicillin and insulin.
- Harmful microorganisms cause diseases of people, crops and livestock and spoil food; preservation removes the water, warmth or food they need, or kills them.
:::
