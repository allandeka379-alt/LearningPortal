## Introduction

Heart disease is one of the leading causes of death in the world, and many of the people affected could have lowered their risk years earlier. High blood pressure, one of the main risk factors, often causes no symptoms at all, so a person can feel perfectly well for years while it damages their arteries. That is why clinics in Zimbabwe and elsewhere encourage adults to have their blood pressure checked regularly.

This lesson explains how coronary heart disease develops, what increases the risk and what reduces it. You should already know that the coronary arteries supply the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Coronary heart disease (CHD)"}
A disease in which the coronary arteries become narrowed or blocked, so the heart muscle receives too little oxygen and glucose.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk factor"}
Anything that increases the chance that a person will develop a disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Blood pressure"}
The pressure that blood exerts on the walls of the arteries, measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
:::

:::definition{term="Heart attack"}
Damage to, or death of, part of the heart muscle when its blood supply through a coronary artery is blocked.
:::

## How coronary heart disease develops

1. **Fatty deposits** containing **cholesterol** build up in the inner wall of a coronary artery.
2. The **lumen** (the space inside) becomes **narrower**, and the artery wall becomes rough and less elastic.
3. **Less blood** reaches the heart muscle beyond the narrowing, so it gets **less oxygen and glucose** and cannot respire aerobically as fast as it needs to.
4. During exercise, when the heart works harder, this may cause chest pain.
5. A **blood clot** can form on the rough surface of a deposit and **block** the artery completely. The heart muscle beyond the blockage receives no oxygen, stops contracting and may die: a **heart attack**.

:::keyfact{title="It is the heart's own blood supply that fails"}
In CHD the blood inside the heart's chambers is not the problem. The muscle is starved because the **coronary arteries** on its surface are narrowed or blocked.
:::

## Risk factors

| Risk factor | Why it raises the risk |
| --- | --- |
| Diet high in saturated fat | Raises blood cholesterol, so fatty deposits form faster |
| Diet high in salt | Raises blood pressure |
| Smoking | Chemicals in smoke damage the artery lining and make clots more likely; nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure |
| Lack of exercise | Heart muscle is less fit; body mass and blood pressure tend to rise |
| Obesity | Linked to high blood pressure, high cholesterol and type 2 diabetes |
| Stress | Raises blood pressure over long periods |
| High blood pressure | Damages artery walls, so deposits build up more easily |
| Genetic predisposition | Some people inherit a greater chance of high cholesterol or heart disease |
| Age | Risk increases as people get older |
| Sex | Men tend to develop CHD at a younger age than women |

The first seven can be **changed** by the person's choices; genes, age and sex **cannot**.

## Reducing the risk: diet and exercise

**Diet:**

- Eat less saturated fat (fatty meat, butter, deep-fried foods) to keep blood cholesterol down.
- Eat less salt, which helps keep blood pressure down.
- Eat more vegetables, fruit, beans and whole grains, which are low in fat and high in fibre.
- Match energy intake to energy use so as not to become obese.

**Exercise:**

- Regular exercise strengthens the heart muscle, so it pumps more blood with each beat.
- It helps lower blood pressure and keep body mass healthy.
- It can improve the balance of cholesterol in the blood.

Not smoking and managing stress also lower the risk.

## High blood pressure (ZIMSEC)

Blood pressure is measured with an inflatable cuff around the upper arm. It is given as two numbers, for example **120/80 mmHg**: the higher number is the pressure when the ventricles contract, the lower when they relax. Readings of about 120/80 are typical of a healthy young adult; readings that are repeatedly **140/90 or higher** are usually counted as high blood pressure.

**Causes and risk factors:** inheritance (high blood pressure runs in families), stress, lack of exercise, a diet high in salt and saturated fat, obesity, smoking, drinking too much alcohol, ageing (arteries become less elastic) and some kidney diseases.

**Effects:** the heart has to work harder; artery walls are damaged, so fatty deposits form more easily (raising the risk of CHD); an artery in the brain may become blocked or burst, causing a **stroke**; the kidneys can be damaged.

## Interpreting data about risk

:::worked-solution[Example 1: smoking and CHD (hypothetical data)]
A long-term study recorded deaths from CHD per 1000 men over ten years: 8 among non-smokers and 28 among men who smoked 25 or more cigarettes a day. How many times greater was the death rate for heavy smokers, and what can be concluded?
1. $\dfrac{28}{8} = 3.5$, so the rate was 3.5 times greater for heavy smokers.
2. This shows a **correlation**: more smoking goes with more CHD deaths.
3. A correlation alone does not prove that smoking **causes** CHD; smokers might also differ in diet or exercise.
4. The case for cause is strong when many large studies agree after allowing for such factors, and when a mechanism is known (here, damage to the artery lining and more clotting).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a change in blood pressure]
Mr Ncube's blood pressure was 150/95 mmHg. After three months of eating less salt and walking every day, it was 135/85 mmHg. Calculate the percentage fall in the higher (systolic) reading.
1. Fall $= 150 - 135 = 15$ mmHg.
2. Percentage fall $= \dfrac{15}{150} \times 100 = 10\%$.
3. His reading is now below 140/90, so it is no longer in the high range.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying CHD is a blockage inside the heart's chambers"}
CHD is a narrowing or blockage of the **coronary arteries**, which supply the heart muscle.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a correlation proves a cause"}
Data showing that two things rise together show a link. Other factors must be ruled out before you can say one causes the other.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a stroke a heart attack"}
A heart attack affects the heart muscle; a stroke affects the brain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all fat in the diet causes CHD"}
It is mainly a diet high in **saturated** fat, raising blood cholesterol, that increases the risk. The body still needs some fat.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Discuss the roles of diet and exercise" needs points about **both**, each linked to the risk: "less saturated fat lowers blood cholesterol, so fewer fatty deposits form in the coronary arteries".
:::

:::exam-tip
When you use data, **quote numbers** with units and compare them ("3.5 times higher"). When asked whether data prove a cause, mention correlation and other factors.
:::

:::exam-tip
Describe CHD as a chain: deposits → narrow lumen → less blood → less oxygen and glucose → less respiration → clot → heart attack.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- CHD is the narrowing or blocking of the coronary arteries by fatty deposits, so the heart muscle gets too little oxygen and glucose.
- A clot on a deposit can block a coronary artery completely, causing a heart attack.
- Risk factors include a diet high in saturated fat and salt, smoking, lack of exercise, obesity, stress, high blood pressure, genes, age and sex.
- Less saturated fat and salt, a healthy body mass, regular exercise and not smoking reduce the risk.
- ZIMSEC: high blood pressure (repeatedly 140/90 mmHg or more) is caused by inheritance, stress, lack of exercise and diet, and raises the risk of CHD and stroke.
- Data showing a correlation do not on their own prove a cause.
:::
