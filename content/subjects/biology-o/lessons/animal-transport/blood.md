## Introduction

An adult has roughly five litres of blood. It looks like a simple red liquid, but under a microscope it is a busy mixture of cells floating in a pale yellow fluid. Each part has a job: carrying oxygen, fighting disease, sealing wounds or transporting dissolved substances around the body.

You should already know that blood flows through the heart, arteries, capillaries and veins.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plasma"}
The pale yellow liquid part of blood, mostly water, in which the blood cells and many dissolved substances are carried.
:::

:::definition{term="Haemoglobin"}
The iron-containing red protein in red blood cells that combines with oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues.
:::

:::definition{term="Phagocytosis"}
The engulfing and digesting of pathogens (or other particles) by a white blood cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Antibody"}
A protein made by white blood cells (lymphocytes) that attaches to a particular pathogen and helps to destroy it.
:::

## The components of blood

If blood is left to stand with a chemical that stops it clotting, it separates: the cells sink and the plasma forms the top layer, a little over half the volume.

| Component | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red blood cells | The most numerous cells; biconcave discs with no nucleus | Transport oxygen, using haemoglobin |
| White blood cells | Larger cells with a nucleus; far fewer than red cells | Defend the body: phagocytosis and making antibodies |
| Platelets | Tiny fragments of cells, with no nucleus | Help the blood to clot |
| Plasma | Liquid, mostly water | Transports blood cells, ions, nutrients (such as glucose and amino acids), urea, hormones and carbon dioxide; also distributes heat |

## Red blood cells

Red blood cells are well adapted for carrying oxygen:

- They contain **haemoglobin**. In the lungs, where there is plenty of oxygen, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form **oxyhaemoglobin**. In the tissues, where oxygen is scarce, it releases the oxygen.
- They have **no nucleus**, leaving more room for haemoglobin.
- Their **biconcave** shape (a disc pressed in on both sides) gives a **large surface area** for oxygen to diffuse in and out, and a short distance to the centre of the cell.
- They are small and **flexible**, so they can squeeze through narrow capillaries.

A cubic millimetre of blood contains about 5 million red blood cells.

## White blood cells

There are several types of white blood cell. Two are important here (Supplement):

- **Phagocytes** have a **lobed nucleus** (divided into several lobes). They move towards pathogens, **engulf** them and digest them: phagocytosis.
- **Lymphocytes** have a **large, round nucleus** that fills most of the cell. They make **antibodies**.

White blood cells are much less numerous than red cells, but their numbers rise during an infection.

## Platelets and clotting

When a blood vessel is cut, **platelets** collect at the wound and, with the damaged tissue, set off a chain of reactions.

**Supplement:** these reactions convert the soluble plasma protein **fibrinogen** into insoluble **fibrin**. Fibrin forms a **mesh of threads** across the wound that traps red blood cells, forming a **clot**. The clot dries and hardens into a scab, and new skin grows underneath.

Clotting is important because it:

- **stops blood loss**, and
- **stops pathogens entering** the body through the wound.

## Identifying blood cells in a photomicrograph

A photomicrograph of a stained blood smear typically shows:

- **red blood cells**: very many small, round cells, paler in the centre (because of the biconcave shape), with **no nucleus**;
- **phagocytes**: larger cells with a dark-stained nucleus divided into **several lobes**;
- **lymphocytes**: cells only a little larger than red cells, almost filled by one **large round nucleus**;
- **platelets**: tiny specks, much smaller than red cells.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the real size of a red blood cell]
In a photomicrograph magnified ×2000, a red blood cell is 15 mm across. Calculate its actual diameter in micrometres (μm).
1. Convert the image size to micrometres: $15\ \text{mm} = 15 \times 1000 = 15\,000\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. Actual size $= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}} = \dfrac{15\,000}{2000} = 7.5\ \mu\text{m}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how many red cells for each white cell?]
A blood sample contains 5 000 000 red blood cells and 7500 white blood cells in each mm³. How many red cells are there for every white cell?
1. Ratio $= \dfrac{5\,000\,000}{7500} = 666.7$.
2. So there are about 670 red cells for every white cell.
:::

## When blood does not work properly

**Anaemia** means too little haemoglobin in the blood, so less oxygen is carried. It can be caused by a lack of iron in the diet, by blood loss or by diseases such as malaria. People with anaemia feel tired and become breathless easily.

**Sickle-cell anaemia** is an inherited condition in which red blood cells can become sickle-shaped. They carry less oxygen and can block capillaries. You will meet it again in the topic on inheritance.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving red blood cells a nucleus"}
Mature human red blood cells have no nucleus, which leaves more room for haemoglobin.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying phagocytes make antibodies"}
Phagocytes engulf and digest pathogens. Lymphocytes make antibodies.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling platelets cells"}
Platelets are fragments of cells, with no nucleus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying platelets turn into fibrin"}
(Supplement) Fibrin is made from fibrinogen, a protein dissolved in the plasma. Platelets help to start the process.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **state the function** of a component, be precise: "red blood cells carry oxygen" (not "carry blood"); "platelets help blood to clot" (not "heal wounds").
:::

:::exam-tip
For photomicrograph questions, use the **nucleus** to identify cells: none (red cell), lobed (phagocyte), large and round (lymphocyte).
:::

:::exam-tip
Convert millimetres to micrometres (× 1000) before dividing by the magnification.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Blood consists of red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets carried in plasma.
- Red blood cells carry oxygen using haemoglobin; they have no nucleus and a biconcave shape for a large surface area.
- White blood cells defend the body: (Supplement) phagocytes, with lobed nuclei, engulf pathogens; lymphocytes, with large round nuclei, make antibodies.
- Platelets help blood to clot, which stops blood loss and keeps pathogens out; (Supplement) fibrinogen is converted into a mesh of fibrin.
- Plasma transports blood cells, ions, nutrients, urea, hormones and carbon dioxide.
:::
