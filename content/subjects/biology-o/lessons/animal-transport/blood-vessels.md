## Introduction

The heart pumps blood, but it is the blood vessels that deliver it. They range from the aorta, about as wide as a thumb, to capillaries so narrow that red blood cells must pass through them in single file. Each type of vessel is built for the pressure of the blood inside it and for the job it does.

You should already know the path of blood through the heart and that diffusion is fastest across a short distance.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Artery"}
A blood vessel that carries blood away from the heart, at high pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Vein"}
A blood vessel that carries blood back towards the heart, at low pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Capillary"}
A tiny blood vessel with a wall one cell thick, through which substances are exchanged between the blood and the tissues.
:::

:::definition{term="Lumen"}
The space inside a blood vessel through which the blood flows.
:::

## Arteries, veins and capillaries

Arteries divide into smaller **arterioles**, which lead into networks of **capillaries** running between the cells of every tissue. Capillaries join to form **venules**, which join to form **veins**.

| Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carries blood | Away from the heart | Towards the heart | Through the tissues, from arterioles to venules |
| Blood pressure | High, in surges (the pulse) | Low and steady | Falling along its length |
| Wall | Thick, with a lot of muscle and elastic tissue | Thinner, with less muscle and elastic tissue | One cell thick |
| Lumen | Narrow compared with the wall | Wide | Very narrow, about one red blood cell wide |
| Valves | None | Yes, along its length | None |

### Relating structure to pressure and function (Supplement)

**Arteries** receive blood in surges each time the ventricles contract. Their **thick, strong walls** withstand this high pressure without bursting. The **elastic tissue** stretches as each surge arrives and then recoils, which keeps the blood moving between beats and evens out the flow. The **muscle** in the walls of arterioles can contract or relax to control how much blood flows to each organ.

**Veins** carry blood at low pressure, so their walls do not need to be thick. Their **wide lumen** gives little resistance to the slow flow. **Valves** stop the blood flowing backwards, which matters most in the legs, where blood must return against gravity. When the leg muscles contract they squeeze the veins, pushing blood towards the heart; the valves make sure it can only go that way.

**Capillaries** are where materials are exchanged. Their wall is **one cell thick**, giving a short distance for diffusion. They are so **narrow** that red blood cells pass close to the wall, and blood flows through them slowly, giving time for exchange. They form **huge branching networks**, so every cell is close to a capillary and the surface area for exchange is enormous. A wall this thin is possible because the blood pressure has already fallen by the time blood reaches the capillaries.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how slowly does blood move through a capillary?]
Blood moves through a capillary at about 0.5 mm per second. How long does it take to pass along a capillary 1 mm long, and how far would blood in the aorta, moving at about 300 mm per second, travel in the same time?
1. Time $= \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}} = \dfrac{1}{0.5} = 2\ \text{s}$.
2. Distance in the aorta $= 300 \times 2 = 600\ \text{mm}$.
3. The slow flow in capillaries gives time for oxygen, glucose and wastes to be exchanged.
:::

:::example{title="Identifying vessels from measurements"}
A student measured three vessels in a microscope slide of tissue.

| Vessel | Wall thickness | Lumen diameter | Valves seen? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P | 1.0 mm | 4 mm | No |
| Q | 0.5 mm | 10 mm | Yes |
| R | 0.001 mm | 0.008 mm | No |

- **P** has a thick wall and a narrow lumen with no valves: an **artery**.
- **Q** has a thinner wall, a wide lumen and valves: a **vein**.
- **R** has a wall only one cell thick and a lumen just wide enough for one red blood cell: a **capillary**.
:::

## The main blood vessels

| Organ | Vessel bringing blood | Vessel taking blood away |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart | Vena cava (from the body); pulmonary vein (from the lungs) | Aorta (to the body); pulmonary artery (to the lungs) |
| Lungs | Pulmonary artery | Pulmonary vein |
| Kidneys | Renal artery | Renal vein |
| Liver (Supplement) | Hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta); hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the intestine) | Hepatic vein (to the vena cava) |

The liver is unusual: it receives blood from **two** vessels.

## Tissue fluid and exchange (ZIMSEC)

1. At the **arterial end** of a capillary the blood pressure is high. It forces water and small dissolved substances (glucose, amino acids, oxygen, mineral ions) out through the capillary wall. This is **pressure filtration**, and the liquid formed is **tissue fluid**, which bathes the cells.
2. Red blood cells, platelets and the large plasma proteins are too big to pass through, so they stay in the blood.
3. Substances are exchanged between the tissue fluid and the cells by **diffusion**: oxygen and glucose diffuse into the cells; carbon dioxide and urea diffuse out.
4. At the **venous end** the pressure is lower. Much of the tissue fluid passes back into the capillary; the rest drains into lymph vessels as **lymph**, which is returned to the blood in veins near the heart.

:::keyfact{title="Capillaries never move"}
Capillaries are fixed in the tissues. When we say more blood reaches the skin, it is because arterioles widen, not because capillaries move.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood"}
The definition is about direction. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting valves in arteries"}
Veins have valves along their length. Arteries do not (the semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery are part of the heart).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying tissue fluid is the same as plasma"}
Tissue fluid has no red blood cells, no platelets and very little of the large plasma proteins.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain how the structure of an artery is related to its function", link each feature to **high pressure**: thick wall to withstand it, elastic tissue to stretch and recoil, maintaining pressure.
:::

:::exam-tip
In vessel-identification questions, use **several** clues: wall thickness, lumen size and valves. One clue alone can mislead.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure: thick, muscular, elastic walls and a narrow lumen.
- Veins return blood at low pressure: thinner walls, a wide lumen and valves to stop backflow.
- Capillaries have walls one cell thick, a very narrow lumen and huge networks, for exchange with the tissues.
- The renal artery and vein serve the kidneys; (Supplement) the liver receives the hepatic artery and hepatic portal vein and is drained by the hepatic vein.
- ZIMSEC: pressure filtration forces plasma out of capillaries as tissue fluid; materials are exchanged with cells by diffusion, and fluid returns to the blood directly or as lymph.
:::
