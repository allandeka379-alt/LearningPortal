## Introduction

Put two fingers on the inside of your wrist, below the thumb, and you can feel your heart at work. At rest it beats about 70 times a minute; that is $70 \times 60 \times 24 = 100\,800$ beats a day, every day of your life.

Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose and must get rid of carbon dioxide. In a large animal these substances cannot simply diffuse that far, so blood is pumped around the body in a **circulatory system**. This lesson explains how the heart and circulation work and how exercise changes the heart rate.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Circulatory system"}
A system of blood vessels with a pump (the heart) and valves that keep blood flowing in one direction.
:::

:::definition{term="Double circulation"}
A circulation in which blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit of the body: once through the lungs and once through the rest of the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Pulmonary and systemic circulation"}
The **pulmonary** circulation carries blood between the heart and the lungs; the **systemic** circulation carries blood between the heart and the rest of the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Heart rate"}
The number of times the heart beats per minute; it can be measured as the pulse rate.
:::

## Single and double circulation (Supplement; ZIMSEC: pulmonary and systemic)

A **fish** has a **single circulation**. Its heart has one atrium and one ventricle and pumps blood to the gills, where it picks up oxygen; the blood then flows on round the body and back to the heart. Blood passes through the heart **once** per circuit, and after squeezing through the gill capillaries it travels to the body at low pressure.

A **mammal** has a **double circulation**:

- **pulmonary circulation:** right side of the heart → lungs → left side of the heart;
- **systemic circulation:** left side of the heart → body → right side of the heart.

![Double circulation: the right side of the heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs in the pulmonary artery and oxygenated blood returns to the left side in the pulmonary veins; the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body in the aorta and deoxygenated blood returns to the right side in the vena cava, so blood passes through the heart twice per circuit.](media:diagrams/double-circulation.png "Figure: double circulation, the pulmonary circuit through the lungs and the systemic circuit through the body")

**Advantages of a double circulation:**

- Blood returning from the lungs is pumped again by the heart, so it reaches the body at **high pressure** and flows quickly, delivering oxygen and glucose fast enough for an active, warm-blooded animal.
- The lungs receive blood at a **lower pressure**, which does not damage their delicate capillaries.
- Oxygenated and deoxygenated blood are kept **separate**, so the body receives blood carrying as much oxygen as possible.

## The structure of the heart

The heart is a muscular pump about the size of a fist, made of **cardiac muscle**, which contracts without tiring. On a diagram of the heart seen from the front, the heart's **right** side is on **your left**.

Labelled parts:

- **Right atrium**: receives deoxygenated blood from the body through the **vena cava**.
- **Right ventricle**: pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs through the **pulmonary artery**.
- **Left atrium**: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the **pulmonary veins**.
- **Left ventricle**: pumps oxygenated blood to the body through the **aorta**. It has the thickest wall.
- **Septum**: the wall of muscle separating the left and right sides.
- **Atrioventricular valves** (Supplement): between each atrium and its ventricle; the tricuspid valve on the right and the bicuspid (mitral) valve on the left. Tendons attached to them stop them turning inside out.
- **Semilunar valves** (Supplement): at the bases of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
- **Coronary arteries**: branch from the aorta and run over the surface of the heart, supplying the heart muscle with oxygen and glucose.

**The path of blood:** vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body → vena cava.

![Front view of the heart: deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium from the vena cava and leaves the right ventricle by the pulmonary artery; oxygenated blood enters the left atrium from the pulmonary veins and leaves the left ventricle, which has the thickest wall, by the aorta; a septum separates the two sides.](media:diagrams/heart-structure.png "Figure: the structure of the heart seen from the front, with the vessels and valves labelled")

:::keyfact{title="Arteries leave, veins return"}
Arteries carry blood **away from** the heart; veins carry blood **back to** it. That is why the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.
:::

### Why the walls differ in thickness (Supplement)

- **Atria** have thin walls: they only push blood a short distance into the ventricles.
- **Ventricles** have thicker walls: they pump blood out of the heart.
- The **left ventricle** wall is the thickest, because it pumps blood at **high pressure** all round the body. The right ventricle pumps blood only to the nearby lungs, at a lower pressure that will not damage the lung capillaries.

Both ventricles pump the **same volume** of blood with each beat; they differ in pressure, not volume. The **septum** stops oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mixing.

## How the heart beats (Supplement; ZIMSEC: the cardiac cycle)

1. The atria relax and fill with blood from the veins.
2. The **atria contract**, pushing blood through the open atrioventricular valves into the ventricles.
3. The **ventricles contract**. The pressure inside them rises, so the atrioventricular valves **close** (stopping backflow into the atria) and the semilunar valves **open**: blood is forced into the aorta and pulmonary artery.
4. The ventricles relax. Their pressure falls below that in the arteries, so the semilunar valves **close**, stopping blood flowing back. The cycle starts again.

The two heart sounds heard through a stethoscope are the valves closing: "lub" as the atrioventricular valves close, "dub" as the semilunar valves close.

## Monitoring the heart

- **Electrocardiogram (ECG):** electrodes on the skin record the electrical activity of the heart as a trace.
- **Pulse rate:** each heartbeat sends a surge of blood along the arteries, felt at the wrist or the neck.
- **Heart sounds:** a stethoscope picks up the sounds of the valves closing.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: from a pulse count to a heart rate]
Nyasha counts 19 pulse beats in 15 seconds. What is her heart rate?
1. There are four 15-second periods in a minute.
2. Heart rate $= 19 \times 4 = 76$ beats per minute.
:::

## Exercise and heart rate

To investigate, measure the resting pulse rate, do a fixed exercise (for example two minutes of skipping), then measure the pulse immediately and every minute until it returns to the resting rate. Keep the exercise the same for each trial, rest before starting, and repeat to calculate means.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: describing an exercise trial]
Tatenda's resting pulse was 72 beats per minute. Straight after two minutes of skipping it was 126; one, two, three and four minutes later it was 108, 90, 78 and 72.
1. Increase $= 126 - 72 = 54$ beats per minute.
2. Percentage increase $= \dfrac{54}{72} \times 100 = 75\%$.
3. The rate was back to 72 after 4 minutes, so the recovery time was 4 minutes.

:::

![Graph of Tatenda's pulse after two minutes of skipping: 126 beats per minute straight after exercise, then 108, 90, 78 and 72 over four minutes, back to the resting rate of 72, so the increase was 54 beats per minute (75%) and the recovery time 4 minutes.](media:diagrams/heart-rate-recovery-graph.png "Figure: pulse rate after exercise falls back to the resting rate of 72 beats per minute in 4 minutes")

**Explaining the increase (Supplement):** during exercise the muscles contract more, so they **respire faster**. They need more oxygen and glucose and produce more carbon dioxide. The heart beats faster (and more strongly) so that blood delivers oxygen and glucose to the muscles, and removes carbon dioxide, more quickly. After exercise the rate stays high for a while, then falls back.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all arteries carry oxygenated blood"}
Arteries carry blood away from the heart. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the left ventricle pumps more blood"}
Both ventricles pump the same volume. The left pumps at a higher pressure because the blood must travel round the whole body.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the valves pump the blood"}
Muscle contraction pumps the blood. Valves only stop it flowing backwards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Swapping left and right on a diagram"}
A heart diagram is drawn as if the person faces you, so the left ventricle is on the right of the page.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When tracing the path of blood, name **every** chamber and vessel in order. Missing out the lungs or one chamber loses marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why the left ventricle wall is thicker" needs the idea of **higher pressure** and pumping blood **all round the body**; "more blood" is wrong.
:::

:::exam-tip
In exercise questions, **describe** with data (rest, peak, recovery time) and **explain** using respiration: more oxygen and glucose needed, more carbon dioxide removed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A circulatory system has a pump (heart), vessels and valves that keep blood flowing one way.
- Fish have a single circulation; mammals have a double circulation (pulmonary and systemic), which delivers blood to the body at high pressure.
- Blood flows: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.
- Supplement: atrioventricular and semilunar valves prevent backflow; the left ventricle wall is thickest because it pumps at high pressure round the body; the septum separates the two sides.
- The heart can be monitored by ECG, pulse rate and heart sounds; exercise raises the heart rate so more oxygen and glucose reach the respiring muscles.
:::
