## Introduction

Chew a mouthful of sadza slowly and it starts to taste slightly sweet. Something in your saliva is breaking the starch down into sugar. That something is an **enzyme**, and enzymes are at work in every living cell: digesting food, releasing energy, building new molecules and getting rid of harmful ones.

Thousands of chemical reactions take place in a cell, and together they are called **metabolism**. At body temperature most of these reactions would happen far too slowly to keep an organism alive. Enzymes speed them up. In this lesson you will learn what enzymes are, how they work and how to investigate them. You should already know that proteins are chains of amino acids, and the iodine and Benedict's food tests.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Catalyst"}
A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction and is not changed by the reaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Enzyme"}
A protein that acts as a biological catalyst. Enzymes are involved in all the metabolic reactions of living things.
:::

:::definition{term="Substrate"}
The substance that an enzyme acts on.
:::

:::definition{term="Product"}
A substance made by the reaction that an enzyme catalyses.
:::

:::definition{term="Active site"}
The part of an enzyme molecule into which the substrate fits. Its shape is complementary to the shape of the substrate.
:::

:::definition{term="Enzyme-substrate complex (Supplement)"}
The structure formed while a substrate molecule is held in the active site of an enzyme.
:::

## Why living things need enzymes

Without enzymes, the reactions of metabolism at about 37 °C would be so slow that cells could not get energy, grow or repair themselves fast enough to stay alive. Heating cells to speed the reactions up is not an option, because high temperatures damage cells. Enzymes make the reactions fast at the temperature of the body.

Enzymes catalyse two kinds of reaction:

- **breaking down** large molecules into smaller ones, as in digestion (starch to maltose);
- **building up** large molecules from smaller ones, such as joining glucose molecules to make starch, glycogen or cellulose.

:::keyfact{title="Properties of enzymes"}
- They are proteins.
- They are **specific**: each enzyme catalyses one reaction, or one type of reaction.
- They are **not used up**: the same enzyme molecule can catalyse the reaction again and again, so only small amounts are needed.
- They are affected by temperature and pH (see the next lesson).
:::

## How an enzyme works: the lock-and-key model

An enzyme is a large protein molecule folded into a particular shape. One small region of it, the **active site**, has a shape that is **complementary** to its substrate, in the same way that a lock fits only the right key.

1. The substrate molecule collides with the enzyme and fits into the active site.
2. (Supplement) The enzyme and substrate together form an **enzyme-substrate complex**.
3. The reaction takes place: the substrate is broken into smaller pieces (or two substrate molecules are joined).
4. The products no longer fit the active site, so they are released.
5. The enzyme is unchanged and its active site is free for another substrate molecule.

Imagine a diagram of this: a large enzyme with a notch cut into its edge (the active site); a substrate shaped exactly to fill the notch; the two locked together (the enzyme-substrate complex); and then two smaller product molecules moving away, leaving the notch empty.

### Specificity

Only a molecule with the right shape can fit into the active site. That is why amylase digests starch but has no effect on protein or sucrose, and why a different enzyme is needed for almost every reaction in a cell.

## Naming enzymes

Most enzyme names end in **-ase**, and many are named after their substrate.

| Enzyme | Substrate | Products | Where found (examples) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amylase | Starch | Maltose (a sugar) | Saliva, pancreatic juice, germinating seeds |
| Protease | Protein | Amino acids | Stomach, small intestine |
| Lipase | Fats and oils | Fatty acids and glycerol | Pancreatic juice |
| Catalase | Hydrogen peroxide | Water and oxygen | Most living cells, e.g. liver, potato, yeast |

Catalase protects cells. Hydrogen peroxide is a poisonous by-product of some reactions in cells, and catalase breaks it down very quickly:

$$
\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}
$$

This is also why hydrogen peroxide fizzes when it is dabbed on a cut: catalase in the damaged tissue releases oxygen.

## Investigating enzymes

:::example{title="Catalase in liver or potato"}
Drop a small piece of fresh liver or potato into a test tube of dilute hydrogen peroxide solution. It fizzes and froth rises up the tube. A glowing splint held in the tube relights, showing the gas is **oxygen**.

- **Control:** a piece that has been boiled and cooled gives no bubbles, because boiling denatured the catalase (changed the shape of its active site).
- **Not used up:** when the fizzing stops, add more hydrogen peroxide. The fizzing starts again, showing that the substrate had run out, not the enzyme.
- **Measuring the rate:** collect the oxygen in a gas syringe or an upturned measuring cylinder and record the volume every minute, or measure the height of the froth after a fixed time.
:::

:::example{title="Amylase and specificity"}
Tendai sets up three tubes at 37 °C for 10 minutes: (1) starch solution and amylase; (2) sucrose solution and amylase; (3) starch solution and boiled, cooled amylase.

- Tube 1: iodine solution stays orange-brown (the starch has gone) and Benedict's solution turns brick-red (maltose, a reducing sugar, has formed).
- Tube 2: Benedict's solution stays blue. Sucrose is not a reducing sugar, and amylase cannot break it down because sucrose does not fit its active site.
- Tube 3: iodine solution turns blue-black. The denatured amylase cannot digest starch.

Tube 2 shows that amylase is **specific**; tube 3 is the control that shows the active enzyme caused the change in tube 1.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are killed"}
Enzymes are proteins, not living things, so they cannot be killed. High temperatures **denature** them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the active site is the same shape as the substrate"}
The active site and the substrate have **complementary** shapes: they fit together like a lock and key, not like two identical keys.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are used up"}
An enzyme is unchanged at the end of the reaction and can be used again. If a reaction stops, it is usually because the substrate has run out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the active site on the substrate"}
The active site is part of the **enzyme**. The substrate fits into it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming the wrong gas"}
Catalase breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and **oxygen**, not hydrogen.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Define an enzyme with both key ideas: it is a **protein** and it is a **biological catalyst**. One without the other loses the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain specificity, use three steps: the active site has a particular shape; only a substrate with a complementary shape fits; other molecules do not fit, so they are not acted on. Supplement candidates should add "an enzyme-substrate complex forms".
:::

:::exam-tip
In experiment questions, look for the control (boiled enzyme, or water instead of enzyme) and say what it shows: that the change was caused by the active enzyme and nothing else.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A catalyst speeds up a reaction without being changed; enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts in all metabolic reactions.
- Without enzymes, reactions at body temperature would be too slow to sustain life.
- The substrate fits into the enzyme's active site, which has a complementary shape (lock and key); Supplement: an enzyme-substrate complex forms, then the products are released.
- Enzymes are specific, are not used up and can be reused.
- Catalase breaks hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen; amylase breaks starch into maltose; boiled enzyme is used as a control.
:::
