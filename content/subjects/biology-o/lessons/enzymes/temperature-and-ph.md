## Introduction

Why does milk keep longer in a fridge? Why does a high fever become dangerous? Why does the amylase in your saliva stop working once it reaches your stomach? All three questions have the same answer: enzymes work well only within a narrow range of **temperature** and **pH**.

This lesson explains how temperature and pH affect enzymes, and how to investigate the effects in the laboratory, a favourite topic for practical and theory questions. For Cambridge candidates, describing the effects (optimum, denaturation) is Core, and explaining them in terms of kinetic energy, effective collisions and the shape of the active site is Supplement; ZIMSEC candidates need both. You should already know how an enzyme works: the substrate fits into an active site with a complementary shape. You should also know how to calculate a rate as $\frac{1}{\text{time}}$.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Optimum temperature"}
The temperature at which an enzyme works fastest.
:::

:::definition{term="Optimum pH"}
The pH at which an enzyme works fastest.
:::

:::definition{term="Denaturation"}
A permanent change in the shape of an enzyme, including its active site, caused by high temperature or extreme pH. The substrate no longer fits, so the enzyme stops working.
:::

:::definition{term="Effective collision (Supplement)"}
A collision between a substrate molecule and an enzyme in which the substrate enters the active site and an enzyme-substrate complex forms.
:::

## How enzymes work: a reminder (lock and key)

The active site of an enzyme has a shape **complementary** to its substrate, like a lock and its key. The substrate fits into the active site, an enzyme-substrate complex forms, the reaction takes place and the products are released. Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, and that is the key to everything in this lesson.

## The effect of temperature

### Describing the graph

Picture a graph of the rate of an enzyme reaction (y-axis) against temperature (x-axis, 0 °C to 70 °C) for an enzyme from the human body:

- At 0–10 °C the rate is very low, but not zero.
- As the temperature rises, the rate increases, gently at first and then more steeply.
- The rate is highest at the **optimum temperature**, close to body temperature (about 37 °C) for most human enzymes.
- Above the optimum the rate falls steeply, reaching zero by about 60 °C.

The curve is not symmetrical: the fall after the optimum is much steeper than the rise before it.

![Graph of enzyme rate against temperature: the rate is low but not zero at 0 to 10 degrees Celsius, rises to a peak at the optimum of about 37 degrees Celsius, then falls much more steeply to zero by about 60 degrees Celsius as the enzyme is denatured.](media:diagrams/enzyme-temperature-graph.png "Figure: the effect of temperature on the rate of an enzyme reaction")

### Explaining the graph

**Below the optimum.** As the temperature rises, the enzyme and substrate molecules gain **kinetic energy** and move faster. They collide more often, so there are more **effective collisions** and more enzyme-substrate complexes form each second. The rate increases.

**Above the optimum.** The enzyme molecule vibrates so strongly that the bonds holding its shape begin to break. The shape of the **active site changes**, so the substrate no longer fits (it is no longer complementary). Fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form and the rate falls. The enzyme has been **denatured**, and this is permanent: cooling it down again does not bring its activity back.

:::keyfact{title="Cold slows enzymes; heat denatures them"}
At low temperatures enzymes are **inactive** (they work very slowly) but they are **not denatured**: warm them up and they work again. High temperatures **denature** enzymes permanently.
:::

Uses of these ideas:

- **Fridges and freezers** keep food fresh because the enzymes of decomposing bacteria and fungi, and of the food itself, work very slowly at low temperatures.
- **Blanching**: vegetables are dipped briefly in boiling water before freezing, which denatures their enzymes so they do not slowly spoil the colour and flavour in the freezer.
- **Different optimum temperatures**: enzymes of bacteria that live in hot springs can have optimum temperatures of 70 °C or more, because their proteins keep their shape at high temperatures.

## The effect of pH

Each enzyme also has an **optimum pH**, which suits the place where it works.

| Enzyme | Where it works | Optimum pH (about) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pepsin (a protease) | Stomach, which is acidic because of hydrochloric acid | 2 |
| Salivary amylase | Mouth | 7 |
| Trypsin (a protease) | Small intestine | 8 |

A graph of rate against pH is a peak shape: the rate is highest at the optimum and falls on **both** sides.

![Graph of enzyme rate against pH for three enzymes: pepsin peaks at about pH 2 in the stomach, salivary amylase at about pH 7 in the mouth and trypsin at about pH 8 in the small intestine; each rate falls on both sides of its optimum pH.](media:diagrams/enzyme-ph-graph.png "Figure: each enzyme has its own optimum pH, and the rate falls on both sides of it")

**Explaining it:** if the pH moves away from the optimum, the shape of the active site changes, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme-substrate complexes form. At extreme pH the enzyme is **denatured**. For example, salivary amylase that is swallowed with food stops working in the stomach, because the acid changes the shape of its active site.

## Investigating temperature and pH

### Measuring the rate

**Amylase and starch.** Put a drop of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile. Mix starch solution and amylase solution, start a stopclock, and every 30 seconds put one drop of the mixture into a new well. While starch remains, the iodine turns blue-black. Record the time when the iodine first stays orange-brown: the starch has all been digested.

**Catalase and hydrogen peroxide.** Mix a catalase source (potato extract or yeast suspension) with hydrogen peroxide solution and collect the oxygen in a gas syringe or an upturned measuring cylinder full of water. Record the volume of oxygen produced in a fixed time.

:::formula{name="Rate from a time"}
$$
\text{rate} = \frac{1}{\text{time}} \qquad \text{(often given as } \frac{1000}{\text{time}} \text{ to avoid very small numbers)}
$$
:::

### Making it a fair test

- **Temperature:** use a thermostatically controlled water bath. Put the enzyme and the substrate in separate tubes in the water bath for a few minutes **before** mixing them, so that both start at the test temperature.
- **pH:** add a **buffer solution** of known pH to each tube; a buffer keeps the pH constant.
- Keep the same volumes and concentrations of enzyme and substrate. When you change the temperature, keep the pH the same, and when you change the pH, keep the temperature the same.
- Include a **control**, such as boiled enzyme or water instead of enzyme, and repeat each test at least three times.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: rates from times]
Starch and amylase were mixed at different temperatures and the time for the starch to disappear was recorded: 400 s at 15 °C, 200 s at 25 °C, 64 s at 35 °C and 100 s at 45 °C. At 55 °C the starch had still not disappeared after 600 s.
1. Rate $= \frac{1000}{\text{time}}$. At 15 °C: $\frac{1000}{400} = 2.5$; at 25 °C: $\frac{1000}{200} = 5.0$.
2. At 35 °C: $\frac{1000}{64} = 15.625 \approx 15.6$; at 45 °C: $\frac{1000}{100} = 10.0$.
3. At 55 °C the starch never disappeared, so the rate is effectively zero: the amylase was denatured.
4. The fastest rate measured was at 35 °C, so the optimum lies between 25 °C and 45 °C. Testing more temperatures in that range would find it more precisely.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: rates from volumes of gas]
Catalase was tested with buffers of different pH, and the oxygen collected in 2 minutes was 8 cm³ at pH 5, 30 cm³ at pH 7 and 12 cm³ at pH 9.
1. Rate $= \frac{\text{volume}}{\text{time}}$: at pH 5, $\frac{8}{2} = 4\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min}$; at pH 7, $\frac{30}{2} = 15\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min}$; at pH 9, $\frac{12}{2} = 6\ \text{cm}^3/\text{min}$.
2. The optimum of the values tested is pH 7.
3. Compare pH 9 with the optimum: $\frac{6}{15} \times 100 = 40\%$. At pH 9 the enzyme works at only 40% of its rate at pH 7.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are killed by heat"}
Enzymes are not alive, so they cannot be killed. They are **denatured**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are denatured by cold"}
Low temperatures make enzymes **inactive**, not denatured. They work again when warmed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking every enzyme works best at 37 °C or pH 7"}
Each enzyme has its own optimum. Pepsin works best at about pH 2, and enzymes of hot-spring bacteria at 70 °C or more.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the substrate is denatured"}
It is the **enzyme** whose shape changes. The substrate is unchanged; it simply no longer fits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing time with rate"}
A **longer** time means a **slower** rate. On a graph of time against temperature, the optimum is at the lowest point of the curve, not the highest.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
**Describe** a graph with numbers: "The rate increases from 10 °C to a maximum at 40 °C, then falls to zero at 60 °C." **Explain** it with reasons: kinetic energy and collisions below the optimum; change in the shape of the active site and denaturation above it.
:::

:::exam-tip
Supplement candidates should use the phrases "kinetic energy", "more frequent effective collisions", "enzyme-substrate complexes", "active site changes shape" and "no longer complementary to the substrate".
:::

:::exam-tip
If the fastest rate was at one of the values you tested, say the optimum lies **between** the values on either side of it, and suggest testing smaller intervals in that range.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and an optimum pH at which it works fastest.
- Below the optimum temperature, raising the temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so more effective collisions and more enzyme-substrate complexes form (Supplement).
- Above the optimum, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured: the substrate no longer fits, and the change is permanent.
- Low temperatures make enzymes inactive but do not denature them; away from the optimum pH the active site changes shape, and extreme pH denatures enzymes.
- Investigate with water baths and buffers, keep other variables constant, and calculate rate as $\frac{1}{\text{time}}$ or volume ÷ time.
:::
