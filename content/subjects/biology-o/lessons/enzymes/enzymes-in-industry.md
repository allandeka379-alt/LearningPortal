## Introduction

Look at a washing-powder packet in a Harare supermarket and you may see the word **biological**. Biological powders contain enzymes that digest egg, gravy, oil and sadza stains, so school uniforms come clean in cool water. Enzymes are also used to squeeze more juice out of fruit, to make milk that people who cannot digest milk sugar can drink, and even to tenderise meat.

Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts. Each one has an **active site** that fits only its own substrate, and each works best at an optimum temperature and pH; too much heat **denatures** it. In this lesson you will see how these properties make enzymes useful outside living cells, and how to investigate them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biological washing powder"}
A detergent that contains enzymes, mainly proteases and lipases and often amylases, which digest the substances in stains.
:::

:::definition{term="Protease and lipase"}
A **protease** breaks down proteins into amino acids. A **lipase** breaks down fats and oils into fatty acids and glycerol.
:::

:::definition{term="Pectinase (Cambridge)"}
An enzyme that breaks down **pectin**, a substance in plant cell walls that helps to hold cells together.
:::

:::definition{term="Lactase (Cambridge Supplement)"}
An enzyme that breaks down **lactose**, the sugar in milk, into glucose and galactose.
:::

## Why use enzymes?

- They work at **moderate temperatures** and normal pressure, so less energy is needed than for many chemical processes.
- They are **specific**, so they make the wanted product with few unwanted by-products.
- They can be made cheaply in large amounts by microorganisms grown in fermenters.
- They are **biodegradable**.

Their limitations follow from their nature: they are **denatured** by high temperatures and extremes of pH, they can be costly to purify, and some people react to them.

## Biological washing powders

| Stain | Main substance | Enzyme | Products |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Egg, blood, milk, gravy | Protein | Protease | Amino acids (and short chains) |
| Cooking oil, butter, grease | Fat or oil | Lipase | Fatty acids and glycerol |
| Sadza, porridge, flour-thickened sauce | Starch | Amylase | Sugars |

The products are small, soluble molecules that dissolve in the water and are rinsed away.

**Advantages**: stains come out at low temperatures, about $30$ to $40^\circ\text{C}$, so less electricity or firewood is used to heat water, and colours and synthetic fabrics are less damaged. The enzymes remove stains that detergent alone struggles with.

**Limitations**: in very hot water, above about $60^\circ\text{C}$, the enzymes are denatured, so a boiling wash wastes them. Biological powders are not suitable for **wool or silk**, because these fibres are proteins that proteases can digest. Some people find that the powders irritate their skin.

### Investigating a biological washing powder

1. Stain identical squares of white cotton cloth with the same volume of the same stain, such as egg yolk, and let them dry.
2. Put the same volume of washing-powder solution of the same concentration into beakers in water baths at different temperatures.
3. Add one stained square to each beaker, stir in the same way, and time how long each stain takes to disappear.
4. Calculate the **rate** of stain removal as $\dfrac{1}{\text{time}}$.

The **control** is the same experiment with powder solution that has been **boiled and cooled**, which denatures the enzymes; a non-biological powder can be used in the same way. If the stain disappears much faster with the active biological powder, the enzymes are responsible.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: finding the optimum temperature]
The times taken to remove an egg stain were:

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time / min | 25 | 14 | 8 | 10 | 22 |

1. Rate $= \dfrac{1}{\text{time}}$: $\dfrac{1}{25} = 0.040$, $\dfrac{1}{14} = 0.071$, $\dfrac{1}{8} = 0.125$, $\dfrac{1}{10} = 0.100$, $\dfrac{1}{22} = 0.045$ per minute.
2. The fastest rate, $0.125$ per minute, was at $40^\circ\text{C}$, so the optimum is close to $40^\circ\text{C}$ (somewhere between 30 and $50^\circ\text{C}$).
3. Below the optimum, molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer collisions between enzyme and substrate. Above it, the enzyme starts to be denatured: its active site changes shape and no longer fits the protein in the stain.
:::

## Pectinase in fruit juice production (Cambridge)

When fruit is crushed, much of the juice stays trapped inside cells, held together by **pectin** in and between their walls. Adding **pectinase** to the crushed fruit breaks the pectin down, so:

- the cells come apart and release more juice, giving a **higher yield**;
- the juice is **clearer**, because pectin makes juice cloudy and thick.

:::example{title="Example 2: testing pectinase on guavas"}
Two $100\ \text{g}$ samples of mashed guava are kept at $40^\circ\text{C}$ for 15 minutes, one with pectinase and one with the same volume of boiled (denatured) pectinase, and each is then filtered for 10 minutes. The sample with active pectinase gives $34\ \text{cm}^3$ of juice and the control gives $18\ \text{cm}^3$: about 89% more juice, because $\dfrac{34 - 18}{18} \times 100 = 88.9\%$. Using boiled pectinase as the control shows that the extra juice comes from the enzyme's activity, not from adding liquid.
:::

## Lactase and lactose-free milk (Cambridge Supplement)

Milk contains the sugar **lactose**. Many adults make little lactase, so lactose passes undigested into the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing wind, bloating, stomach pain and diarrhoea. These people are **lactose intolerant**.

To make **lactose-free milk**, lactase is added to milk, or the milk is passed over lactase fixed onto small beads so that the enzyme can be reused:

$$
\text{lactose} \xrightarrow{\text{lactase}} \text{glucose} + \text{galactose}
$$

Lactose-free milk tastes slightly sweeter, because glucose and galactose taste sweeter than lactose. To show that the lactose has been broken down, use a **glucose test strip**: Benedict's solution is no help, because lactose itself is a reducing sugar. Fermented milks such as yoghurt and sour milk also contain less lactose, because bacteria have used some of it.

## Other uses in the home and industry

Proteases tenderise meat. Wrapping tough meat in pawpaw leaves, or cooking it with green pawpaw, softens it, because pawpaw contains **papain**, a protease. Amylases turn starch into sugar syrups and are made naturally when grain is malted for brewing, and the enzyme rennet clots milk in cheese-making.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Hotter water always cleans better"}
With biological powder, water much above the optimum **denatures** the enzymes, so stains are removed more slowly, not faster.
:::

:::mistake{title="The enzymes are killed by heat"}
Enzymes are proteins, not living things. They are **denatured**: the active site changes shape.
:::

:::mistake{title="Pectinase digests cellulose"}
Pectinase digests **pectin**. Cellulose is broken down by a different enzyme, cellulase.
:::

:::mistake{title="Benedict's test shows that the lactose has gone"}
Lactose is a reducing sugar too, so Benedict's test is positive before and after. Use a **glucose** test strip.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Always name the **enzyme, its substrate and its products**: "lipase breaks down fats into fatty acids and glycerol".
:::

:::exam-tip
Explain temperature effects in two parts: below the optimum, **kinetic energy and collisions**; above it, **denaturation and the shape of the active site**.
:::

:::exam-tip
A boiled-enzyme control shows that any effect is due to the enzyme's activity. Say **why**: boiling denatures the enzyme.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Enzymes are useful because they work at moderate temperatures, are specific and biodegradable, and are cheaply made by microorganisms.
- Biological washing powders contain proteases, lipases and amylases that digest protein, fat and starch stains into soluble products at low temperatures.
- They are denatured in very hot water and are not suitable for wool or silk; a boiled-powder control shows that the enzymes remove the stains.
- Pectinase breaks down pectin in plant cell walls, giving more and clearer fruit juice (Cambridge).
- Lactase breaks lactose into glucose and galactose to make lactose-free milk for lactose-intolerant people (Cambridge Supplement).
:::
