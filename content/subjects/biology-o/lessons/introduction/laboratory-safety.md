## Introduction

A school biology laboratory contains Bunsen burners, glassware, sharp instruments, electrical equipment, chemicals and living material such as soil, pond water and microorganisms. Used carefully, all of these are safe. Most accidents happen when someone rushes, ignores a rule or does not know what a label means.

In this lesson you will learn what causes laboratory accidents, the rules that prevent them, what to do in an emergency, simple first aid, and how to read the hazard symbols printed on chemical containers. ZIMSEC examines these directly, and in the Cambridge practical papers you are expected to spot the hazards in a method and suggest sensible precautions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hazard"}
Anything that could cause harm, such as a flammable liquid, a corrosive chemical, a hot object, broken glass or a pathogen.
:::

:::definition{term="Risk"}
How likely it is that a hazard will actually cause harm, and how serious that harm would be.
:::

:::definition{term="Precaution"}
An action taken to reduce a risk, for example wearing eye protection, or heating ethanol in a water bath instead of over a flame.
:::

:::definition{term="Hazard symbol"}
A picture (pictogram) on a container label that warns of the main danger of the substance inside.
:::

## Causes of laboratory accidents

| Cause | What can go wrong | Typical example in biology |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fire | Burns and spreading flames | Ethanol vapour catching fire near a Bunsen burner |
| Fumes | Breathing in toxic or irritating gases | Vapour from specimens preserved in methanal (formalin) |
| Acids and alkalis | Burns to the skin and serious damage to the eyes | Sodium hydroxide from the biuret test splashing into an eye |
| Microorganisms | Infection | Handling cultures of bacteria grown on agar |
| Misuse of apparatus | Cuts, burns and broken glass | Cutting towards your fingers with a scalpel |
| Electricity | Electric shock or fire | Touching a microscope lamp switch with wet hands |

## Laboratory rules

Every school has its own rules, but they are all based on the same ideas.

- Never eat, drink or taste anything in the laboratory, and never pipette by mouth.
- Wear eye protection whenever you handle chemicals or heat anything, and wear a laboratory coat.
- Tie back long hair and keep loose clothing away from flames.
- Read every label before you use a chemical, and use only the amount the method asks for.
- Heat a test tube gently, with its open end pointing away from yourself and other people.
- Never heat ethanol, or any other flammable liquid, over a flame: use a hot water bath.
- Cut with a scalpel on a tile, moving the blade away from your fingers.
- Carry a microscope with two hands: one under the base and one holding the arm.
- Keep water away from sockets and cables, and switch off before you unplug anything.
- Report every spill, breakage, cut or burn to the teacher at once, however small.
- Wash your hands after handling chemicals, specimens, soil or cultures.

## Fire drills and emergency procedures

Before you start, know where the exits, fire extinguisher, fire blanket, sand bucket, first-aid kit and eye-wash bottle are. In a fire:

1. Stay calm, warn the people around you and tell the teacher.
2. Turn off the gas supply and electrical equipment if it is safe to do so.
3. Smother a small fire, such as burning ethanol in a beaker, with a fire blanket or a heat-proof mat.
4. If a person's clothes catch fire, they should stop, drop to the ground and roll, and be wrapped in a fire blanket.
5. Never throw water on an electrical fire: water conducts electricity and could give someone a shock.
6. If the fire spreads, leave by the nearest exit, close the doors behind you and go to the assembly point. Never go back for bags.

Fire drills let everyone practise leaving quickly and in order, so that nobody panics in a real emergency.

## First aid

First aid is the help given straight away while the teacher or a health worker is called. A first-aid kit usually contains sterile dressings, plasters, bandages, cotton wool, disposable gloves, scissors and an eye-wash bottle.

| Injury | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Heat burn or scald | Cool it under cold running water straight away for at least 10 minutes (about 20 minutes is better). Do not burst blisters or put oil or butter on it. |
| Chemical on the skin | Wash it off with plenty of running water and remove any contaminated clothing. |
| Chemical in the eye | Hold the eye open under gently running clean water, or use an eye-wash bottle, for at least 10 minutes. Do not rub it. |
| Cut | Put on gloves. Press a clean dressing firmly on the cut to stop the bleeding, then cover it. Report any broken glass. |
| Swallowed chemical | Rinse the mouth with water but do not make the person vomit. Get medical help and take the container along. |

Every accident is reported, even when nobody seems to be hurt.

## Hazard symbols

Chemical containers carry hazard symbols so that anyone can see the main danger at a glance. You may meet two systems. Older bottles, still common in school stores, use a **black symbol on an orange square**. The current international system uses a **black symbol inside a white diamond with a red border**.

| Hazard | Older symbol (orange square) | Current symbol (red-bordered diamond) | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flammable | A flame | A flame | Catches fire easily |
| Oxidising | A flame above a circle | A flame above a circle | Releases oxygen, which makes other substances burn more fiercely |
| Corrosive | Liquid dripping from two test tubes onto a hand and a bar, eating into them | The same picture | Destroys living tissue such as skin and eyes, and attacks materials |
| Toxic | A skull and crossbones | A skull and crossbones | Can cause serious illness or death in small amounts if swallowed, breathed in or absorbed through the skin |
| Harmful or irritant | A diagonal cross (X) | An exclamation mark (!) | Can cause illness, or inflame the skin and eyes, but less seriously than a toxic substance |
| Hazardous to the environment | A dead tree and a dead fish | A dead tree and a dead fish | Harms living things, especially in rivers and lakes |
| Explosive | An exploding bomb | An exploding bomb | May explode if heated, knocked or sparked |
| Serious health hazard | No separate symbol | A person's outline with a star shape on the chest | May cause long-term harm such as cancer or breathing problems |

Two other warning signs are common in laboratories: the **biohazard** sign (three joined crescent shapes) on material that may contain pathogens, and the **electrical hazard** sign (a lightning flash in a yellow triangle).

:::keyfact
Learn each symbol by its meaning, not just its picture: a flame means flammable, but a flame above a circle means oxidising. A substance can carry more than one symbol. Ethanol, for example, often carries both the flame and the exclamation mark, because it is highly flammable and irritates the eyes.
:::

## Spotting hazards in a method

:::example{title="Testing a leaf for starch"}
A student wrote this method: "Boil the leaf in water for 2 minutes. Put it in a boiling tube of ethanol and heat the tube over the Bunsen flame until the leaf turns pale. Rinse it in water and add iodine solution."

| Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanol is flammable | Its vapour could catch fire in the flame | Turn the Bunsen burner off and stand the boiling tube in a beaker of hot water from a kettle (a water bath) |
| Boiling water and hot glassware | Scalds and burns | Move the leaf with forceps and let the apparatus cool |
| Iodine solution irritates the eyes and stains | Eye damage if it splashes | Wear eye protection |
:::

:::example{title="Catalase in liver with hydrogen peroxide"}
In a catalase investigation, pieces of fresh liver are dropped into hydrogen peroxide solution. Hydrogen peroxide can damage the eyes and skin, so eye protection is worn and spills are washed away with plenty of water. Raw liver may carry bacteria, so the bench is wiped with disinfectant and hands are washed afterwards. The liver is cut with a scalpel on a tile, cutting away from the fingers.
:::

:::example{title="Growing bacteria on agar"}
Any culture could contain pathogens, so the precautions are strict. Open the plate for as short a time as possible. Hold the lid on with two short strips of tape rather than sealing it all the way round, which could favour harmful bacteria that grow without oxygen. Incubate the plate upside down at no more than about 25 °C, so that bacteria adapted to human body temperature (37 °C) are not encouraged. Never open a plate after incubation, and have used plates sterilised before they are thrown away.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Heating ethanol over a flame"}
Ethanol boils at about 78 °C and its vapour catches fire easily. Always heat it in a hot water bath with no flame nearby.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague precautions"}
"Be careful" or "take care with chemicals" earns no marks. Name the hazard and give a specific precaution: "wear eye protection, because sodium hydroxide is corrosive to the eyes".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing flammable with oxidising"}
A flame on its own means flammable (the substance itself burns). A flame above a circle means oxidising (the substance releases oxygen that makes other things burn more fiercely).
:::

:::mistake{title="Rubbing a splash"}
Rubbing an eye or a splash on the skin spreads the chemical. Rinse with plenty of clean running water for at least 10 minutes instead.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using water on an electrical fire"}
Water conducts electricity, so the person could get an electric shock. Switch off the power and use a fire blanket, sand or a suitable extinguisher such as a carbon dioxide one.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "suggest a safety precaution" questions, link your precaution to a hazard in the method you were given. A precaution that does not fit the method (for example "wear gloves" when nothing harmful is handled) may not earn the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Identify the hazard" asks for the danger ("ethanol is flammable"). "Explain the precaution" asks how it reduces the risk ("a water bath means there is no flame to ignite the ethanol vapour").
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions may show the older orange-square symbols or the current red diamonds. Both use the same pictures for flammable, oxidising, corrosive, toxic and environmental hazards; harmful or irritant changed from a cross (X) to an exclamation mark (!).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Laboratory accidents are caused by fire, fumes, acids and alkalis, microorganisms, misuse of apparatus and electricity.
- Safety rules include eye protection, tied-back hair, no eating or drinking, reading labels and reporting every accident.
- In a fire: warn others, turn off gas and electricity, smother small fires, never use water on electrical fires, and leave in an orderly way.
- First aid: cool burns under running water, rinse chemicals from eyes and skin with plenty of water, and press on cuts to stop bleeding.
- Hazard symbols show the main danger: flammable, oxidising, corrosive, toxic, harmful or irritant, and hazardous to the environment.
:::
