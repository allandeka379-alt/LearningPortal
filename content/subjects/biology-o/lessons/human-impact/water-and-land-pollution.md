## Introduction

Lake Chivero, on the Manyame River just downstream of Harare, is the city's main source of drinking water. The rivers that flow into it, such as the Mukuvisi and the Marimba, pass through the city and carry partly treated sewage, industrial waste and fertiliser washed off farms and gardens. The lake has suffered thick blooms of algae, mats of water hyacinth and deaths of fish, and the water needs more and more chemicals to make it safe to drink.

On land, plastic bags and bottles block drains, litter roadsides and are swallowed by cattle and goats. This lesson explains how pollution of water and land harms living things, and what can be done about it. You should already know about the nitrogen cycle, decomposers and aerobic respiration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollution"}
The release into the environment of substances (or energy) that harm living organisms.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication (Supplement)"}
The process in which a lake or river receives extra mineral ions, such as nitrate and phosphate, leading to rapid growth of producers, then decomposition, loss of dissolved oxygen and the death of organisms that need it.
:::

:::definition{term="Biodegradable and non-biodegradable"}
**Biodegradable** materials, such as food scraps and paper, are broken down by decomposers. **Non-biodegradable** materials, such as most plastics, are not broken down by decomposers and remain in the environment for a very long time.
:::

## Untreated sewage and excess fertiliser

**Untreated sewage** contains:

- **organic matter** (faeces and food waste), which decomposers feed on; as they multiply and respire aerobically, they use up the **dissolved oxygen** in the water;
- **pathogens**, such as the bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid, which spread disease to people who drink or wash in the water;
- **mineral ions** such as nitrate and phosphate, released as the sewage is broken down.

**Excess fertiliser** applied to fields is washed by rain into rivers and dams, or leaches down through the soil into ground water. It adds nitrate and phosphate ions to the water.

### Eutrophication (Supplement)

Both sewage and fertiliser can cause eutrophication. Learn the sequence in order:

1. **Increased availability of nitrate and other ions** (such as phosphate) in the water.
2. **Increased growth of producers**: algae multiply rapidly (an algal bloom) and water plants grow, turning the water green.
3. The algae near the surface block the light from the plants and algae below, and algae have short lives, so producers die in large numbers: there is **increased decomposition** after the death of the producers.
4. **Increased aerobic respiration by decomposers** (bacteria), whose populations grow on the dead material.
5. **Reduction in dissolved oxygen** in the water.
6. **Death of organisms that need dissolved oxygen**, such as fish and many insect larvae.

:::keyfact{title="Who uses up the oxygen?"}
In eutrophication the oxygen is used up by the **decomposers** respiring as they break down dead producers, not by the algae while they are alive. Living algae release oxygen in the light.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: oxygen below a sewage pipe]
Students measured the dissolved oxygen in a river at four sites: 9.0 mg per dm³ upstream of a sewage pipe, 2.5 mg per dm³ 100 m below it, 4.0 mg per dm³ 1 km below and 7.5 mg per dm³ 5 km below.
1. Percentage fall from upstream to 100 m below the pipe $= \dfrac{9.0 - 2.5}{9.0} \times 100 = 72.2\%$.
2. Explanation of the fall: bacteria decomposing the organic matter in the sewage multiply and respire aerobically, using up the oxygen.
3. Recovery downstream: as the organic matter is used up, fewer bacteria respire, and oxygen dissolves in from the air and is released by photosynthesis, so the concentration rises again.
4. Fish and insect larvae that need high oxygen levels would be absent near the pipe but present upstream and far downstream.
:::

## Non-biodegradable plastics

Most plastics are not broken down by decomposers, so they build up in the environment.

**In water:**

- fish and water birds become entangled in plastic, or swallow it and starve because their stomachs are blocked;
- plastics slowly break into tiny pieces (microplastics) that are taken in by many organisms;
- floating plastic and rubbish block rivers and dam intakes.

**On land:**

- cattle and goats eat plastic bags, which block their stomachs, so they cannot feed properly and may die;
- plastic litter blocks drains, causing flooding, and discarded containers hold rainwater where mosquitoes breed;
- burning plastic releases toxic gases;
- buried plastic stays in the soil and stops water soaking in where it collects.

## Waste from mines

Mining can pollute land and water. Gold panners use **mercury** to collect gold, and it escapes into rivers, where it builds up in fish and in the people who eat them. Water draining from some mine dumps is acidic and carries toxic metals. Mine waste also smothers soil and riverbeds.

:::example{title="Example 2: how pollution can be reduced"}
- **Sewage**: repair and extend sewers, and treat all sewage fully before releasing it into rivers.
- **Fertiliser**: apply the right amount at the right time, never just before heavy rain, and leave strips of natural vegetation along streams to trap runoff.
- **Plastics**: reduce, reuse and recycle; use biodegradable bags and baskets; collect litter and dispose of waste in proper landfill sites.
- **Mining**: control mining by law, use methods that do not need mercury, and treat water from mine dumps.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the algae use up the oxygen"}
Living algae photosynthesise and release oxygen. The oxygen is used up by **decomposers** respiring as they break down dead producers.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fertiliser poisons the fish"}
The fertiliser itself rarely kills fish. It causes eutrophication, and the fish die from **lack of dissolved oxygen**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying plastics rot away like food waste"}
Most plastics are **non-biodegradable**: decomposers cannot break them down, so they do not rot. Sunlight and weathering only break them into smaller and smaller pieces, which stay in the environment for hundreds of years.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For eutrophication, write the events **in order**, using the key words: extra nitrate (ions) → more growth of producers (algae) → producers die → more decomposition → more aerobic respiration by decomposers (bacteria) → less dissolved oxygen → death of organisms that need oxygen (fish).
:::

:::exam-tip
For sewage, remember three separate harms: oxygen used up by decomposers, nutrients causing eutrophication, and pathogens spreading disease.
:::

:::exam-tip
For plastics, say **where** the harm happens (in water or on land) and **how** (entanglement, blocked stomachs, blocked drains, toxic gases when burned).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Untreated sewage adds organic matter (decomposers use up the oxygen), mineral ions and pathogens to water.
- Excess fertiliser adds nitrate and phosphate to rivers and lakes, such as Lake Chivero.
- Supplement: eutrophication: more nitrate → more growth of producers → more decomposition of dead producers → more aerobic respiration by decomposers → less dissolved oxygen → death of fish and other organisms.
- Non-biodegradable plastics entangle and block the stomachs of animals in water and on land, block drains and release toxic gases when burned.
- Pollution is reduced by treating sewage, using fertiliser carefully, cutting plastic waste and controlling mining.
:::
