## Introduction

Every year there are more people to feed, but there is not much more farmland. Farmers have met the demand by producing more food from each hectare and each animal. A combine harvester can clear a wheat field in a day; a broiler house can raise thousands of chickens to market size in about six weeks; the irrigated sugar-cane estates of the south-eastern Lowveld grow one crop over thousands of hectares.

These methods have greatly increased food production, but they also have costs: to wildlife, soils, rivers and the welfare of animals. In this lesson you will learn the main methods, then weigh up the advantages and disadvantages of monocultures and intensive livestock farming. It will help to remember how plants use mineral ions, and that energy is lost at each step of a food chain.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monoculture"}
Growing a single crop species, often a single variety, over a large area, often year after year.
:::

:::definition{term="Intensive livestock production"}
Keeping large numbers of animals in a small space, often indoors, with their food, water, temperature and lighting controlled, so that meat, eggs or milk are produced quickly and cheaply.
:::

:::definition{term="Selective breeding"}
Choosing parents with useful features to breed together, selecting the best offspring, and repeating this over many generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Pesticides"}
Chemicals that kill pests: **insecticides** kill insects, **herbicides** kill weeds and **fungicides** kill fungi.
:::

## How food production has increased

### Agricultural machinery

Tractors, ploughs, planters, sprayers and combine harvesters let fewer workers farm much larger areas. Land can be prepared and planted quickly when the rains arrive, and a crop harvested before it is damaged by weather or pests. Irrigation pumps and centre-pivot sprinklers let farmers grow crops such as winter wheat in the dry season.

### Chemical fertilisers

Every harvest removes mineral ions from the soil. Fertilisers replace them. Nitrate is used to make amino acids and proteins, so the crop grows bigger; magnesium is needed for chlorophyll; phosphate and potassium help roots, flowering and fruiting. Maize farmers typically apply a fertiliser at planting and a nitrogen top dressing a few weeks after the crop emerges.

### Insecticides

Insecticides kill insects that eat or damage crops, such as fall armyworm and stalk borers in maize, aphids on vegetables and tobacco, and bollworms in cotton. Less of the crop is lost and its quality is higher.

### Herbicides

Weeds compete with crops for light, water, mineral ions and space. Herbicides kill them, saving the labour of hand weeding. **Selective** herbicides kill broad-leaved weeds but not grasses, so they can be sprayed over a whole maize field.

### Selective breeding

Over many generations, farmers and plant breeders have produced:

- crop varieties with **higher yields**, such as hybrid maize;
- **early-maturing** and **drought-tolerant** varieties for low-rainfall areas;
- varieties **resistant** to diseases and pests;
- livestock with useful features: dairy cows that give more milk, broiler chickens that grow fast, hens that lay many eggs, and beef cattle that put on weight quickly.

Zimbabwe's indigenous Mashona and Tuli cattle are hardy in heat and on poor grazing, and breeders use them to add hardiness to their herds.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: what fertiliser and weed control add]
An agricultural college grew maize on three plots with the same variety, spacing and rainfall.

| Plot | Treatment | Yield / tonnes per hectare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | No fertiliser; weeded by hand once | 1.2 |
| B | Fertiliser; weeded by hand once | 3.0 |
| C | Fertiliser; herbicide kept the plot weed-free | 4.2 |

1. Effect of fertiliser (A → B): $\dfrac{3.0 - 1.2}{1.2} \times 100 = 150\%$ increase.
2. Effect of fertiliser and full weed control together (A → C): $\dfrac{4.2 - 1.2}{1.2} \times 100 = 250\%$ increase.
3. Conclusion: the extra mineral ions gave the largest gain, and removing competition from weeds raised the yield further.
:::

## Large-scale monocultures

Tobacco, maize, cotton, wheat and sugar cane are all grown as monocultures on large commercial farms.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Efficient: one set of machinery, methods and chemicals for the whole area | **Biodiversity falls**: woodland or grassland is cleared and few other species find food or shelter |
| Planting, spraying and harvesting are simple and fast | **Pests and diseases spread quickly** through a large area of one host plant and build up if the crop is grown year after year |
| A large, uniform harvest suits processing (a sugar mill, a tobacco auction floor) and lowers the cost of food | More **pesticide** is used, harming pollinators and the natural enemies of pests, and pests may become **resistant** |
| The crop chosen suits the soil, climate and market | The same mineral ions are removed every year, so more **fertiliser** is needed; runoff can pollute rivers and dams |
| Farm workers and managers become skilled with one crop | Bare soil after harvest is easily **eroded**; and the farm depends on one crop, so a disease, drought or price fall hits hard |

## Intensive livestock production

Broiler houses, cages or barns for laying hens, piggeries, cattle feedlots and dairy units are examples. In a broiler house, thousands of chickens live on a deep litter floor with food and water always available and the temperature kept warm.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Animals move little and are kept warm, so less energy is used for movement and keeping warm, and **more of the food becomes meat, eggs or milk** | **Animal welfare**: crowding, stress, little natural behaviour, and injuries such as feather-pecking |
| Less land per animal; units can be built close to towns and markets | **Diseases spread quickly** in crowded animals, so vaccines and antibiotics are used routinely, which can lead to **antibiotic-resistant bacteria** |
| Food, water, temperature and health are easy to monitor and control | **Waste** (manure and slurry) collects in one place and can pollute water and cause smells and flies |
| Animals are protected from predators and weather; products are cheap and available all year | Animals are fed grain and soya that people could eat, so **energy is lost** along the food chain; heating, lighting and ventilation also use energy |

:::example{title="Example 2: broilers and road-runner chickens"}
A broiler chicken reaches market size in about six weeks. It lives indoors, moves little and eats a balanced feed, so much of its food becomes body mass, and the meat is cheap. A village "road-runner" chicken scavenges outdoors, grows far more slowly and lays fewer eggs, but it needs little bought feed, few medicines and no special housing, and it can behave naturally. Each system suits a different farmer: the broiler unit needs money for chicks, feed and electricity; the road-runner flock needs almost none.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a cotton farmer's problem"}
A farmer grows cotton on the same large fields every year. Each season he sprays more often, because bollworms are becoming harder to kill. Two effects of monoculture are at work: the pest population builds up with a huge food supply always present, and the repeated spraying selects for **resistant** insects, which survive and breed. Rotating crops and encouraging the pest's natural enemies would help.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Fertilisers are plant food"}
Fertilisers supply **mineral ions**. Plants make their own food, glucose, by photosynthesis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Herbicides kill insects"}
**Herbicides** kill weeds (plants); **insecticides** kill insects; **fungicides** kill fungi.
:::

:::mistake{title="Intensive animals grow fast because they eat less"}
They may eat a lot. They grow fast because **less energy is lost** in movement and keeping warm, so more of each meal becomes body mass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Selective breeding works in one generation"}
It needs **many generations** of choosing and breeding the best individuals.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For **discuss** or **evaluate** questions, give advantages **and** disadvantages, then finish with a brief, balanced conclusion.
:::

:::exam-tip
Make each point specific: not "it harms the environment" but "fewer habitats, so biodiversity falls" or "fertiliser runoff pollutes rivers".
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining why intensive farming is efficient, use energy: **less energy lost in movement and heat**, so more is transferred into meat, eggs or milk.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Machinery, chemical fertilisers, insecticides, herbicides and selective breeding have all increased food production.
- Fertilisers replace mineral ions; insecticides and herbicides reduce losses to pests and weeds; selective breeding gives higher-yielding, hardier crops and animals.
- Monocultures are efficient and cheap, but reduce biodiversity, encourage pests and diseases, and increase pesticide and fertiliser use.
- Intensive livestock production converts feed into food efficiently on little land, but raises welfare concerns, spreads disease, concentrates waste and wastes energy along the food chain.
- Good answers weigh both sides and reach a conclusion.
:::
