## Introduction

Zimbabwe's national parks, such as Hwange, Mana Pools and Gonarezhou, protect elephants, lions and painted dogs, and guarded parks and conservancies protect black rhinos, which are critically endangered. Mana Pools is a World Heritage Site, and Gonarezhou forms part of the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park shared with Mozambique and South Africa. Outside the parks, villages earn money from wildlife through community schemes, and every year on National Tree Planting Day, the first Saturday of December, people across the country plant trees.

All of these are forms of **conservation**: using and protecting resources so that they last. You should already know why habitats are destroyed, and how selective breeding and genetic variation work.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sustainable resource"}
A resource that is produced as rapidly as it is removed from the environment, so that it does not run out.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
Protecting and managing species, habitats and resources so that they are not lost.
:::

:::definition{term="Endangered species"}
A species at risk of becoming extinct.
:::

:::definition{term="Captive breeding"}
Breeding animals of an endangered species in zoos, sanctuaries or protected areas, often to release some back into the wild.
:::

:::definition{term="Seed bank"}
A store in which seeds of many plant species and crop varieties are kept dry and cold, so that they stay alive for many years.
:::

## Sustainable and non-renewable resources

**Fossil fuels** such as coal are **non-renewable**: they formed over millions of years, and once used they cannot be replaced. **Forests** and **fish stocks** are living resources that can be **sustainable**, but only if no more is taken than can be replaced by growth and reproduction. When more is removed than is replaced, the resource runs down and can collapse.

## Conserving forests (Supplement methods)

- **Education**: teaching communities why woodland matters and how to use it without destroying it.
- **Protected areas**: national parks and forest reserves where trees may not be cut.
- **Quotas**: limits on how much timber or firewood may be taken from an area.
- **Replanting**: replacing the trees that are cut (reforestation), and planting trees where there were none (afforestation), such as fuel woodlots of eucalyptus on tobacco farms.

## Conserving fish stocks (Supplement methods)

- **Education** of fishers and buyers.
- **Closed seasons**: no fishing while the fish are breeding, so that young fish are produced.
- **Protected areas**: zones, such as breeding grounds, where no fishing is allowed.
- **Control of net types and mesh sizes**: nets with large meshes let young fish escape, so they can grow and breed before being caught.
- **Quotas**: limits on the mass of fish that may be caught, or on the number of fishing permits or boats.
- **Monitoring**: surveys of fish numbers and catches, so that the rules can be changed if stocks fall.

## Why species become endangered or extinct

A species becomes endangered when its numbers fall so low that it may die out. The main causes are:

- **habitat destruction** (clearing woodland, draining wetlands);
- **hunting and overharvesting**, such as poaching rhinos for their horns and elephants for ivory;
- **pollution** of water and land;
- **introduced species** that compete with, prey on or spread disease to native species;
- **climate change**, which alters habitats faster than some species can adapt.

## How endangered species are conserved

- **Monitoring and protecting species and their habitats**: national parks and conservancies, anti-poaching patrols, counting and tracking animals.
- **Education**, so that people value wildlife and understand the threats.
- **Captive breeding programmes**, which build up numbers in safe places before releasing animals into the wild.
- **Seed banks**, which store seeds of wild plants and of traditional crop varieties, such as local types of sorghum and millet, keeping their genes safe.

:::example{title="Example 1: protecting black rhinos"}
Black rhinos were once common in the Zambezi Valley, but poaching for their horns destroyed most of them. Today many of Zimbabwe's rhinos live in heavily guarded areas and private conservancies in the Lowveld. Rangers patrol on foot, and rhinos are counted and tracked so that losses are noticed quickly. Some rhinos have had their horns removed (dehorning) so that they are of less value to poachers. Moving rhinos to safer areas also helps the populations to breed and grow.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: CAMPFIRE"}
CAMPFIRE (the Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources) began in 1989. Rural communities living alongside wildlife receive a share of the money earned from it, mostly from controlled safari hunting and tourism, and use it for projects such as schools, clinics and boreholes. Because the wildlife brings an income, communities have a reason to protect it and its habitat instead of seeing elephants and lions only as a threat to crops and cattle.
:::

## Reasons for conservation programmes (Supplement)

- **To reduce extinction**: once a species is lost, it is lost for ever.
- **To protect vulnerable environments**, such as wetlands, that are easily damaged.
- **To maintain ecosystem functions**: nutrient cycling (decomposers, nitrogen-fixing bacteria) and the provision of resources such as food, drugs (many medicines come from plants), fuel and genes that may be useful in future, for example for breeding crops resistant to drought or disease.

## Captive breeding with AI and IVF (Supplement)

- **Artificial insemination (AI)**: sperm collected from a male is placed into the female's reproductive tract. Sperm can be frozen and transported between zoos or countries, so a female can be bred with a suitable male far away, without moving the animals.
- **In vitro fertilisation (IVF)**: eggs collected from a female are fertilised by sperm in a laboratory, and the embryos are placed in the uterus of a female, which may be a surrogate of a closely related species. Scientists are using IVF to try to save the northern white rhino, of which only two animals, both female, were left alive in the mid-2020s.

### The danger of losing genetic variation

When a population becomes very small, the individuals are closely related, so **inbreeding** becomes likely. Harmful recessive alleles are then more often inherited from both parents and expressed. The population also has **less genetic variation**, so it is less able to adapt to changes such as a new disease or a change in climate: if one individual is vulnerable, all may be. This increases the risk of extinction. Cheetahs, for example, have very little genetic variation. AI and IVF help by bringing in sperm or eggs from unrelated animals.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a resource sustainable just because it is natural"}
Wood and fish are sustainable only when they are taken **no faster than they are replaced**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking captive breeding can bring back extinct species"}
Captive breeding can only increase the numbers of species that still have living members.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving methods without explaining them"}
"Mesh size" alone is vague. Write "nets with a larger mesh let young fish escape, so they can grow and breed".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the lists: forests (education, protected areas, quotas, replanting); fish (education, closed seasons, protected areas, net types and mesh sizes, quotas, monitoring). For each method, add **how** it conserves the resource.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked why a species is endangered, link the cause to the species: "poaching for horn reduces the number of black rhinos that can breed".
:::

:::exam-tip
For genetic variation, use the words **inbreeding**, **harmful recessive alleles** and **less able to adapt**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A sustainable resource is produced as rapidly as it is removed; fossil fuels are non-renewable, while forests and fish stocks can be managed sustainably.
- Forests: education, protected areas, quotas and replanting. Fish: education, closed seasons, protected areas, controlled nets and mesh sizes, quotas and monitoring.
- Species become endangered through habitat destruction, hunting and overharvesting, pollution, introduced species and climate change.
- They are conserved by protecting species and habitats (national parks, anti-poaching patrols, CAMPFIRE), education, captive breeding and seed banks.
- Supplement: conservation prevents extinction, protects vulnerable environments and maintains ecosystem functions; AI and IVF help captive breeding; small populations lose genetic variation and become less able to adapt.
:::
