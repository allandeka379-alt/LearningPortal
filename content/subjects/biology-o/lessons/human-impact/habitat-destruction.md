## Introduction

Zimbabwe's woodlands, vleis, rivers and mountains are home to a remarkable variety of life, from baobabs and msasa trees to pangolins, painted dogs and the many kinds of fish in Lake Kariba. Every year, though, some of these habitats are lost: woodland is cleared for fields and firewood, vleis near towns are drained and built on, and riverbanks are dug up for gold.

When a habitat is destroyed, the organisms that depended on it lose their food, shelter and breeding sites. This lesson explains why habitats are destroyed and what the consequences are. You should already know about food webs, the carbon cycle and the importance of ground cover.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Biodiversity"}
The number of different species that live in an area.
:::

:::definition{term="Habitat"}
The place where an organism lives, providing the food, water, shelter and breeding sites it needs.
:::

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The clearing of large areas of forest or woodland, without replanting.
:::

:::definition{term="Invasive species"}
A species brought by people from another part of the world that spreads quickly in its new home and harms the native species.
:::

:::definition{term="Extinction"}
The permanent loss of a species: no living members remain anywhere.
:::

## Why habitats are destroyed

1. **Land for crops, livestock and housing.** As the population grows, woodland and grassland are cleared for fields and grazing, and towns spread outwards. Some vleis around Harare, which store water and support many birds and plants, have been drained and built on.
2. **Extraction of natural resources.** Trees are cut for timber, firewood and charcoal, and to cure tobacco. Mining for gold, diamonds, chrome and coal strips away vegetation and soil, and gold panning destroys riverbeds and banks.
3. **Pollution of fresh water (and the sea).** Sewage, fertiliser runoff, industrial waste and mercury from mining make lakes and rivers unfit for many species.

## Changing food webs and invasive species

Humans also damage habitats by **altering food webs**:

- **Overharvesting**: poaching rhinos and elephants, hunting for bushmeat or overfishing removes species and affects everything linked to them in the web.
- **Introducing species**: species brought from elsewhere may have no natural predators or diseases in their new home, so they spread rapidly.

:::example{title="Example 1: invasive species in Zimbabwe"}
- **Water hyacinth**, a floating plant from South America, forms thick mats on dams and lakes such as Lake Chivero. The mats block light from plants and algae below, use up oxygen as they rot, block water intakes and boats, and give shelter to the snails that carry bilharzia.
- **Lantana camara**, a garden shrub from the Americas, has spread into woodland and along rivers. It forms dense, prickly thickets that crowd out native plants, and its leaves are poisonous to livestock.
- **Kariba weed**, a floating fern, spread over large areas of Lake Kariba in the years after the lake was filled.
- **Black wattle**, an Australian tree, has invaded grassland and streams in the Eastern Highlands, crowding out native plants and using up water.
:::

## The undesirable effects of deforestation

| Effect | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Reduced biodiversity** | Trees provide food, shelter and nesting sites for many species; when they go, so do the animals, plants and fungi that depend on them |
| **Extinction** | A species that lives only in one area of forest can disappear completely if that forest is cleared |
| **Loss of soil** | Without roots to bind the soil and leaves and litter to break the force of the rain, topsoil is washed or blown away; the land becomes infertile and rivers silt up |
| **Flooding** | Trees and leaf litter slow the rain and help it soak in; on cleared land, water runs off quickly into rivers, which overflow (and silted rivers hold less water) |
| **More carbon dioxide in the atmosphere** | Fewer trees remove carbon dioxide by photosynthesis, and burning or rotting the cleared wood releases carbon dioxide, adding to the enhanced greenhouse effect |

:::example{title="Example 2: tobacco and the miombo woodland"}
Many smallholder farmers cure their tobacco in barns heated by wood fires. Much of this wood has come from indigenous woodland, including trees such as msasa, munhondo and mukwa. Clearing the woodland removes the habitat of many birds, insects and mammals, exposes the soil to erosion and releases carbon dioxide. Planting woodlots of fast-growing trees for fuel, using more efficient barns and replanting cleared land all reduce the damage.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: rain on woodland and on cleared land]
In a field trial, 50 mm of rain fell on two plots of the same slope. From the woodland plot, 6 mm of water ran off and 0.4 tonnes of soil per hectare were lost. From the cleared plot, 20 mm ran off and 6.0 tonnes per hectare were lost.
1. Runoff from woodland $= \dfrac{6}{50} \times 100 = 12\%$ of the rain.
2. Runoff from cleared land $= \dfrac{20}{50} \times 100 = 40\%$ of the rain.
3. Soil lost from cleared land compared with woodland $= \dfrac{6.0}{0.4} = 15$ times as much.
4. Conclusion: clearing the trees made far more water run off (risking floods downstream) and far more topsoil wash away.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing biodiversity with the number of organisms"}
Biodiversity is the number of different **species**, not the total number of individuals. A maize field may contain millions of plants but very low biodiversity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stating an effect without explaining it"}
"Deforestation causes erosion" earns less than "with no roots to bind the soil and no leaves to break the rain, the topsoil is washed away".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one reason for the rise in carbon dioxide"}
There are two: less photosynthesis (fewer trees) **and** release of carbon dioxide when the wood is burned or decays.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain the undesirable effects of deforestation", aim for five effects, each with a reason: reduced biodiversity, extinction, soil erosion, flooding and increased carbon dioxide.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked how humans destroy habitats, group your answer: land for farming and housing, extraction of resources, pollution, and changes to food webs (overharvesting and introduced species).
:::

:::exam-tip
Use local examples where they fit: water hyacinth on Lake Chivero, gold panning in rivers, woodland cleared for tobacco curing.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Biodiversity is the number of different species living in an area.
- Habitats are destroyed to provide land for crops, livestock and housing, to extract resources (timber, firewood, minerals) and by pollution.
- Overharvesting and introduced (invasive) species, such as water hyacinth and Lantana camara, alter food webs and damage habitats.
- Deforestation reduces biodiversity, can cause extinction, leads to soil erosion and flooding, and increases carbon dioxide in the air.
- Woodlots, efficient barns, replanting and controlling invasive species reduce the damage.
:::
