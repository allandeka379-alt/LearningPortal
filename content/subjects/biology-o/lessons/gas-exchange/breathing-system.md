## Introduction

At rest you breathe in and out roughly 12 to 16 times a minute without thinking about it. Each breath brings fresh air deep into your lungs, where oxygen enters your blood and carbon dioxide leaves it.

This lesson describes the parts of the breathing system, explains why the lungs are such an efficient gas exchange surface and shows how muscles move air in and out. You should already know that cells need oxygen for aerobic respiration and produce carbon dioxide, and that gases move by diffusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ventilation (breathing)"}
The movement of air into and out of the lungs.
:::

:::definition{term="Gas exchange"}
The diffusion of oxygen from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and of carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveoli.
:::

:::definition{term="Alveoli"}
Tiny air sacs at the ends of the bronchioles, where gas exchange takes place.
:::

:::note{title="Breathing is not respiration"}
Breathing moves air in and out of the lungs. Respiration is the chemical reactions in cells that release energy from food. Keep the two words apart.
:::

## The parts of the breathing system

Following the air from the outside inwards:

| Part | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nose and mouth | Openings to the airways | Air enters; the nose warms, moistens and filters it |
| Larynx | The voice box, at the top of the trachea | Produces sounds |
| Trachea (windpipe) | Tube supported by C-shaped rings of cartilage | Carries air to the bronchi |
| Bronchi (one bronchus to each lung) | Branches of the trachea, also supported by cartilage | Carry air into each lung |
| Bronchioles | Narrow, branching tubes | Carry air to the alveoli |
| Alveoli | Millions of tiny air sacs, surrounded by capillaries | Gas exchange |
| Ribs | Bones forming a cage around the lungs | Protect the lungs; move during breathing |
| Intercostal muscles | Muscles between the ribs (internal and external, Supplement) | Move the ribcage |
| Diaphragm | Dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tissue below the lungs | Contracts to increase the volume of the chest |

## The alveoli: an efficient gas exchange surface

Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. Diffusion is fast because the alveoli have all the features of a good gas exchange surface:

- **Large surface area:** hundreds of millions of alveoli.
- **Thin:** the wall of each alveolus and of each capillary is one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is very short.
- **Moist:** gases dissolve in the film of moisture before they diffuse across.
- **Good blood supply:** a dense network of capillaries carries oxygenated blood away and brings more carbon dioxide, keeping the concentration gradients steep.
- **Good ventilation:** breathing keeps the air in the alveoli rich in oxygen and low in carbon dioxide.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how large is the surface of the lungs?]
Suppose a pair of lungs contains 400 million alveoli, each a sphere 0.25 mm across. The surface area of a sphere is $\pi \times d^2$. Estimate the total surface area in m² ($1\ \text{m}^2 = 1\,000\,000\ \text{mm}^2$).
1. One alveolus: $\pi \times 0.25^2 = 0.19635\ldots\ \text{mm}^2$.
2. All of them: $400\,000\,000 \times 0.19635\ldots = 78\,539\,816\ \text{mm}^2$.
3. In square metres: $\dfrac{78\,539\,816}{1\,000\,000} = 78.5\ \text{m}^2$ (to 3 significant figures), about the floor area of a classroom.
:::

## How we breathe in and out (Supplement; ZIMSEC)

Air moves because the **volume** of the chest (thorax) changes, which changes the **pressure** inside the lungs. Air always flows from a higher pressure to a lower pressure.

| | Breathing in (inhalation) | Breathing out (exhalation) at rest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract | Relax |
| Ribcage | Moves up and out | Moves down and in |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens (moves down) | Relaxes and returns to a dome shape |
| Volume of the thorax | Increases | Decreases |
| Pressure inside the lungs | Falls below atmospheric pressure | Rises above atmospheric pressure |
| Air | Flows in | Flows out |

During **forced exhalation**, such as coughing or hard exercise, the **internal intercostal muscles contract**, pulling the ribs down and in, and the muscles of the abdomen wall contract, pushing the diaphragm up. This empties the lungs faster.

:::keyfact{title="Muscles change the volume; pressure moves the air"}
The muscles do not suck air in. They make the chest bigger, the pressure inside falls, and the higher air pressure outside pushes air in.
:::

### The bell-jar model

A glass bell jar has a Y-shaped tube through its stopper with a balloon on each branch, and a rubber sheet stretched across its open base.

- The **Y-tube** represents the trachea and bronchi, the **balloons** the lungs, the **jar** the wall of the thorax and the **rubber sheet** the diaphragm.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: explaining the bell-jar model]
What happens when the rubber sheet is pulled down, and why?
1. Pulling the sheet down increases the volume inside the jar.
2. The air pressure inside the jar, around the balloons, falls.
3. Air from outside, at higher pressure, flows down the tube into the balloons.
4. The balloons inflate, just as the lungs fill when the diaphragm contracts and flattens.
5. Pushing the sheet back up reverses every step, and the balloons deflate.
:::

**Limitations of the model:** the glass jar cannot move, unlike the ribcage; the rubber sheet is pulled by hand rather than contracting; the balloons are not divided into alveoli and no gas exchange happens.

## Keeping the airways clear (Supplement)

- **Cartilage** in the walls of the trachea and bronchi keeps them open, so they do not collapse when the pressure inside falls during breathing in.
- **Goblet cells** in the lining of the airways make sticky **mucus**, which traps dust, pollen and pathogens.
- **Ciliated cells** have tiny hairs (**cilia**) that beat together, sweeping the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed. Acid in the stomach then kills the trapped pathogens.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the diaphragm moves up when you breathe in"}
When it contracts, the diaphragm **flattens and moves down**, increasing the volume of the chest.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying muscles pull or suck air into the lungs"}
Muscles increase the volume of the thorax; the pressure falls, and air is pushed in by the higher pressure outside.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cilia trap dust"}
Mucus from goblet cells traps dust and pathogens; the cilia sweep the mucus away.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying gases cross the alveolar wall by active transport"}
Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by **diffusion**, down their concentration gradients.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Describe breathing in as a chain: muscles contract → ribs up and out, diaphragm flattens → **volume increases** → **pressure decreases** → air flows in. Marks are given for each link.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "features of a gas exchange surface", give the feature **and** its effect: "thin walls, so a short diffusion distance".
:::

:::exam-tip
Spell **alveoli** (plural) and **alveolus** (singular) correctly; examiners expect the right form.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Air passes through the trachea, bronchi and bronchioles to the alveoli, where gas exchange takes place.
- Alveoli have a large surface area, thin walls, a moist lining, a rich blood supply and good ventilation.
- Breathing in: external intercostal muscles and diaphragm contract, ribs move up and out, the diaphragm flattens, volume increases and pressure falls, so air flows in.
- Breathing out at rest is the reverse; in forced exhalation the internal intercostal muscles contract.
- Supplement: cartilage keeps the trachea open; goblet cells make mucus that traps particles and pathogens; cilia sweep the mucus to the throat.
:::
