## Introduction

Tobacco is one of Zimbabwe's most important export crops, and growing and selling it supports many farming families and workers. Smoking tobacco, however, is one of the biggest preventable causes of illness and early death in the world. The value of tobacco as a crop and the effect of smoke on the body are separate questions: this lesson is about what happens inside the body of a smoker, and of the people who breathe in their smoke.

You should already know the structure of the breathing system, how goblet cells and cilia keep the airways clean, and how haemoglobin carries oxygen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nicotine"}
An addictive drug in tobacco smoke that increases heart rate and blood pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Tar"}
A sticky brown mixture of chemicals in tobacco smoke that collects in the airways and lungs; it contains carcinogens.
:::

:::definition{term="Carcinogen"}
A substance that can cause cancer.
:::

:::definition{term="Passive smoking"}
Breathing in smoke from other people's cigarettes.
:::

## What is in tobacco smoke?

| Substance | Effect on the body |
| --- | --- |
| Nicotine | Addictive. Raises heart rate and blood pressure, narrows blood vessels and makes blood clot more easily |
| Tar | Contains **carcinogens**. Coats the airways, damages and paralyses cilia and irritates the lining so more mucus is made |
| Carbon monoxide | Combines with haemoglobin more readily than oxygen does, so the blood carries **less oxygen** |
| Smoke particles | Irritate the airways, causing extra mucus and coughing; they settle deep in the lungs |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how carbon monoxide cuts the oxygen supply]
Suppose 100 cm³ of a non-smoker's blood can carry about 20 cm³ of oxygen. In a smoker, 5% of the haemoglobin is combined with carbon monoxide. How much oxygen can 100 cm³ of the smoker's blood carry?
1. Haemoglobin still available for oxygen $= 100\% - 5\% = 95\%$.
2. Oxygen carried $= 20 \times 0.95 = 19\ \text{cm}^3$ per 100 cm³ of blood.
3. The heart must pump harder to deliver the same oxygen, and the smoker becomes breathless sooner during exercise.
:::

## Short-term effects

- **Cilia** are damaged and stop beating, so mucus is not swept out of the airways.
- **Goblet cells** make **extra mucus**, which builds up and has to be coughed up ("smoker's cough").
- The airways become irritated and narrower.
- Nicotine raises the **heart rate and blood pressure**.
- Carbon monoxide reduces the **oxygen** carried by the blood, so fitness falls.

## Long-term effects

**Chronic bronchitis.** The bronchi become inflamed and blocked by mucus that the damaged cilia cannot clear. The person has a persistent cough, is often breathless and suffers repeated chest infections, because bacteria multiply in the trapped mucus.

**Emphysema.** Repeated coughing and long-term inflammation break down the walls of the alveoli. Many small alveoli merge into fewer, larger air spaces, so the **surface area** for gas exchange falls sharply and the lungs lose their elasticity. Less oxygen is absorbed; the person becomes breathless after even mild activity. The damage cannot be reversed.

**Lung cancer.** Carcinogens in tar can damage the genes of cells lining the airways, so the cells divide out of control and form a **tumour**, which may spread to other parts of the body. Smoking also raises the risk of cancers of the mouth, throat and oesophagus.

**Heart and blood vessel disease.** Nicotine and carbon monoxide make the heart work harder, chemicals in smoke damage the lining of the arteries and the blood clots more easily, so smokers are more likely to develop coronary heart disease and to have strokes.

**Addiction.** Nicotine is addictive. When smokers try to stop, they feel cravings, irritability and restlessness (withdrawal symptoms), which is why stopping is hard and why it helps never to start.

**In pregnancy,** carbon monoxide and nicotine reduce the oxygen reaching the fetus, so babies of smokers tend to be born with a lower birth mass.

## Passive smoking

People near a smoker breathe in the same harmful substances. Passive smoking increases the risk of lung disease and heart disease in non-smokers. Children who live with smokers suffer more chest infections and asthma attacks. This is why many countries restrict smoking in enclosed public places, and why smokers should never smoke near children.

## Interpreting data

:::worked-solution[Example 2: smoking and lung cancer (hypothetical data)]
A study reported lung cancer deaths per 100 000 people per year: 10 for non-smokers and 250 for people who smoked 25 or more cigarettes a day. How many times greater was the death rate in the heavy smokers, and what does the evidence show?
1. $\dfrac{250}{10} = 25$, so the rate was 25 times greater.
2. When groups smoking more cigarettes have steadily higher death rates, this is called a **dose-response** relationship, and it strengthens the case for a causal link.
3. On its own the survey shows a **correlation**. Together with laboratory evidence that chemicals in tar cause cancer, and many studies that agree, the evidence that smoking causes lung cancer is very strong.
:::

When you are given data, look for: the trend, the size of the effect (calculate ratios or percentages), whether other factors were controlled, and the size of the sample.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying nicotine causes lung cancer"}
The **carcinogens in tar** cause cancer. Nicotine is the addictive substance; it also affects the heart and blood vessels.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing carbon monoxide with carbon dioxide"}
Carbon **monoxide** (CO) in smoke combines with haemoglobin. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is the normal waste gas of respiration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up bronchitis and emphysema"}
Bronchitis: inflamed bronchi blocked by mucus. Emphysema: alveolar walls broken down, so less surface area.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking passive smoking is harmless"}
Second-hand smoke contains the same harmful substances and raises the risk of disease in non-smokers.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Link each substance to its effect: "**tar** contains carcinogens, causing **lung cancer**"; "**carbon monoxide** combines with **haemoglobin**, so less oxygen is carried". Vague answers such as "smoke damages the lungs" score little.
:::

:::exam-tip
For emphysema, always mention the **reduced surface area** of the alveoli and its effect on **gas exchange**.
:::

:::exam-tip
In data questions, describe the trend with numbers, then say whether the data show a correlation or a cause, and why.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tobacco smoke contains nicotine (addictive; raises heart rate and blood pressure), tar (carcinogens), carbon monoxide (reduces oxygen carried) and particles (irritate the airways).
- Short-term effects: damaged cilia, extra mucus, coughing, raised heart rate and less oxygen in the blood.
- Long-term effects: chronic bronchitis, emphysema (less surface area for gas exchange), lung cancer, heart disease and stroke.
- Passive smoking harms non-smokers, especially children.
- Data showing a dose-response relationship, supported by laboratory evidence, show that smoking causes disease.
:::
