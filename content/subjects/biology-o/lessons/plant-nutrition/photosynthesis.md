## Introduction

Every cob of maize harvested in Mashonaland, every flush of tea leaves picked on the estates of the Eastern Highlands and every new red leaf that appears on the msasa trees in August was built from two very simple raw materials: carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil. The process that turns them into sugar is **photosynthesis**, and the energy that drives it comes from light.

Photosynthesis feeds the plant itself, and through food chains it feeds almost every other living thing, including us. It also releases the oxygen that most organisms need for respiration.

Before you start, you should know that plant cells contain **chloroplasts**, that **enzymes** control the reactions in cells, and that gases move by **diffusion**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Photosynthesis"}
The process by which green plants make carbohydrates (glucose) from carbon dioxide and water, using light energy. Oxygen is released as a by-product.
:::

:::definition{term="Chlorophyll"}
The green pigment inside chloroplasts. It absorbs light energy and transfers it into chemical energy stored in the carbohydrate molecules the plant makes.
:::

:::definition{term="Destarching"}
Keeping a plant in the dark for about 48 hours before an experiment, so that it uses up the starch in its leaves. Any starch found at the end must then have been made during the experiment.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Word equation for photosynthesis"}
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

The reaction needs **light energy** and **chlorophyll**, so these are written on the arrow: light above it and chlorophyll below it.
:::

:::formula{name="Balanced chemical equation (Cambridge Supplement)"}
$$
\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}
$$
:::

## How photosynthesis happens in a leaf

Photosynthesis takes place inside the chloroplasts, most of which are in the **palisade mesophyll** cells near the upper surface of the leaf.

- **Carbon dioxide** diffuses from the air into the leaf through tiny pores called **stomata**, then through the air spaces to the mesophyll cells.
- **Water** is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells and carried up to the leaf in the **xylem**.
- **Light energy** is absorbed by chlorophyll. The energy is used to combine carbon dioxide and water into glucose, so light energy becomes **chemical energy** in the glucose.
- **Oxygen** is the by-product. Some is used by the leaf cells for respiration; the rest diffuses out through the stomata.

Remember that plants **respire all the time**, day and night. In bright light they simply photosynthesise faster than they respire, so overall they take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen.

## What the plant does with its glucose

Glucose is the first carbohydrate made, but very little stays as glucose for long.

| Product | Use in the plant |
| --- | --- |
| Glucose | Broken down in respiration to release energy |
| Starch | Insoluble energy store in leaves, seeds (maize grains) and storage organs (potato tubers) |
| Cellulose | Builds cell walls in new cells |
| Sucrose | The form in which sugar is carried in the phloem to other parts of the plant |
| Nectar | A sugary liquid in flowers that attracts pollinating insects |
| Amino acids and proteins | Made by combining sugars with nitrate ions absorbed from the soil |
| Fats and oils | Energy stores in seeds such as groundnuts and sunflower |

:::keyfact{title="Why plants store starch, not glucose"}
Starch is **insoluble**, so it does not dissolve in the cell sap and does not draw water into the cell by osmosis. It is also compact and cannot diffuse out of the cell. Glucose is soluble and would do both.
:::

## Testing a leaf for starch

We test leaves for starch, not glucose, because glucose is quickly converted to starch, and starch builds up in a leaf that has been photosynthesising.

:::worked-solution[The starch test on a leaf, with a reason for each step]
1. Dip the leaf in **boiling water** for about one minute. This kills the cells, stops all reactions and breaks down the cell membranes so that iodine solution can reach the starch.
2. **Turn off the Bunsen burner.** Put the leaf in a boiling tube of **ethanol** and stand the tube in the hot water. Ethanol is flammable, so it must never be heated over a naked flame.
3. Leave it for several minutes until the leaf turns white. The hot ethanol dissolves the chlorophyll, so the green colour no longer hides the result.
4. Dip the leaf in warm water. The ethanol makes the leaf brittle; the water softens it so it can be spread flat without breaking.
5. Spread the leaf on a white tile and cover it with **iodine solution**. A **blue-black** colour shows starch is present. If the leaf stays **orange-brown** (the colour of iodine solution), there is no starch.
:::

## Investigating what photosynthesis needs

Every investigation below starts with a **destarched** plant. A good investigation changes only one factor and includes a **control** that is identical except for that factor.

:::example{title="Is chlorophyll needed? A variegated leaf"}
A variegated spider plant has leaves with green stripes and white stripes; the white parts have no chlorophyll.

1. Destarch the plant, then place it in bright light for six hours.
2. Draw the leaf to record where the green and white areas are.
3. Test the leaf for starch.

**Result:** only the parts that were green turn blue-black; the white parts stay orange-brown.

**Conclusion:** chlorophyll is needed for photosynthesis. The green parts act as the control, because they had the same light, carbon dioxide and water as the white parts.
:::

:::example{title="Is light needed? A foil cover"}
Clip a strip of aluminium foil over both sides of one leaf of a destarched bean plant and leave the plant in bright light for six hours. When the leaf is tested, the covered strip stays orange-brown and the uncovered parts turn blue-black. Light is needed. The uncovered part of the same leaf is the control.
:::

:::example{title="Is carbon dioxide needed? Soda lime"}
Two destarched potted plants are each enclosed in a clear polythene bag. Bag A also contains a dish of **soda lime**, which absorbs carbon dioxide; bag B contains a dish of water instead. After six hours in bright light, a leaf from plant B turns blue-black but a leaf from plant A stays orange-brown. Carbon dioxide is needed.

Plant B is the control. It is also in a bag so that the **only** difference between the plants is the carbon dioxide. Soda lime is corrosive: wear eye protection and handle it with a spatula.
:::

## Why photosynthesis matters in ecosystems

- **It is the source of food energy.** Plants are **producers**. In a mopane woodland, for example, energy captured by mopane leaves passes to the mopane worms (madora) that eat them, and then to the birds and people that eat the worms.
- **It releases oxygen**, which almost all organisms use in respiration.
- **It removes carbon dioxide** from the air, which is an important part of the carbon cycle.
- **It provides materials**: food crops, timber, cotton fibre and fuel wood all come from carbohydrates made by photosynthesis. Even the coal mined at Hwange formed from plants that photosynthesised millions of years ago.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Plants get their food from the soil"}
Plants absorb water and mineral ions from the soil, but mineral ions are not food. Plants **make** their carbohydrates by photosynthesis, using carbon dioxide from the air.
:::

:::mistake{title="Plants photosynthesise by day and respire only at night"}
Plants respire **all the time**. In the light they photosynthesise faster than they respire, which hides their respiration.
:::

:::mistake{title="Chlorophyll makes energy"}
Energy cannot be made. Chlorophyll **absorbs** light energy and **transfers** it into chemical energy in glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Brown means starch"}
Orange-brown is the colour of the iodine solution itself, so it means **no** starch. Starch gives a **blue-black** colour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heating ethanol with a flame"}
Ethanol catches fire easily. Turn off the Bunsen burner and heat the ethanol in a hot water bath.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked for the word equation, write the raw materials and products correctly **and** write light and chlorophyll on the arrow (light above, chlorophyll below). In the balanced equation (Supplement), check the formulae and the 6s carefully.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain" a step of the starch test means give its **reason**: "boiled in water **to** kill the cells and break the membranes so iodine can enter", not just "boiled in water".
:::

:::exam-tip
In experiments, examiners reward: destarching first (and why), the variable you changed, the control, the factors kept the same, and the expected colour in each part of the leaf.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the precise phrase "light energy is transferred into chemical energy". Avoid "sunlight makes food" and avoid saying that energy is produced.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, using light energy absorbed by chlorophyll in chloroplasts.
- Supplement: $\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2}$.
- Glucose is used for respiration and converted to starch (storage), cellulose (cell walls), sucrose (transport), nectar, amino acids and oils.
- Test a leaf for starch: boiling water, hot ethanol in a water bath, warm water, iodine; blue-black means starch.
- Destarched plants, variegated leaves, foil covers and soda lime show that chlorophyll, light and carbon dioxide are needed; always include a control.
- Photosynthesis supplies food energy and oxygen to ecosystems and removes carbon dioxide from the air.
:::
