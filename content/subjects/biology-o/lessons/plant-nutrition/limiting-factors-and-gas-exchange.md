## Introduction

A tomato grower with greenhouses near Harare notices that her plants grow quickly on warm, sunny days in the rainy season but hardly at all on cold, cloudy days in June. The reason is that the **rate** of photosynthesis depends on the conditions around the leaves. If she understands which condition is holding her plants back, she can spend money on the change that will actually increase her harvest.

In this lesson you will learn how three factors affect the rate of photosynthesis, how to investigate them, and how plants exchange carbon dioxide and oxygen with the air. You should already know the word equation for photosynthesis and that enzymes work fastest at an optimum temperature.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rate of photosynthesis"}
How quickly a plant photosynthesises, measured for example as the volume of oxygen released (or carbon dioxide taken in) per minute.
:::

:::definition{term="Limiting factor"}
The factor that is in the shortest supply and so holds the rate of a process down. Increasing a limiting factor increases the rate; increasing any other factor does not.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrogencarbonate indicator"}
A solution whose colour shows the carbon dioxide concentration: **orange-red** at the concentration in normal air, **yellow** when carbon dioxide has increased and **purple** when carbon dioxide has decreased.
:::

:::definition{term="Stoma (plural: stomata)"}
A pore in the epidermis of a leaf, surrounded by two **guard cells** that open and close it. Gases diffuse into and out of the leaf through the stomata.
:::

## How three factors affect the rate

### Light intensity

Light provides the energy for photosynthesis. As light intensity increases, the rate increases, because chlorophyll absorbs more light energy. Eventually the rate stops rising and the graph levels off (a **plateau**). Photosynthesis has not stopped: another factor is now holding the rate down.

### Carbon dioxide concentration

Carbon dioxide is a raw material. Normal air contains only about 0.04% carbon dioxide, so it is often in short supply. Increasing the carbon dioxide concentration increases the rate until the graph levels off.

### Temperature

The reactions of photosynthesis are controlled by **enzymes**. As temperature rises towards the optimum, molecules move faster and the rate increases. Above the optimum the enzymes are **denatured**, so the rate falls quickly.

:::keyfact{title="Shape of the graphs"}
Light intensity and carbon dioxide: the rate rises, then levels off. Temperature: the rate rises to an optimum, then falls.
:::

## Investigating light intensity with pondweed

Pondweed such as *Elodea* gives off bubbles of oxygen from its cut stem when it photosynthesises. The faster the bubbles appear, the faster the rate.

1. Put a cut piece of pondweed, cut end up, in a test tube of water containing a little **sodium hydrogencarbonate**, which supplies carbon dioxide so that it is not limiting.
2. Place a lamp a measured distance from the pondweed. Put a beaker of water between them, or use an LED lamp, so the lamp does not warm the pondweed.
3. Leave the pondweed for a few minutes to adjust to the new light intensity.
4. Count the bubbles released in one minute. Repeat three times and calculate a mean.
5. Repeat at other distances. The closer the lamp, the higher the light intensity.

![Pondweed apparatus: a lamp at a measured distance from a boiling tube of water and sodium hydrogencarbonate holding pondweed with its cut end up, inside a beaker of water; oxygen bubbles from the cut end are counted per minute at each lamp distance.](media:diagrams/pondweed-apparatus.png "Figure: apparatus for investigating how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis")

| Variable | In this investigation |
| --- | --- |
| Independent | Light intensity (changed by moving the lamp) |
| Dependent | Number of bubbles per minute |
| Controlled | Temperature, carbon dioxide concentration, the same piece of pondweed, the same lamp, background light (dim the room) |

Counting bubbles is quick but not very accurate, because bubbles vary in size. Collecting the gas in a gas syringe or a graduated capillary tube and measuring its volume is more precise.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: processing pondweed results]
Farai counted the bubbles from a piece of pondweed three times at each lamp distance.

| Lamp distance / cm | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 | Mean bubbles per minute |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 52 | 48 | 50 | ? |
| 20 | 34 | 31 | 34 | 33 |
| 30 | 20 | 22 | 18 | 20 |
| 40 | 12 | 11 | 13 | 12 |
| 50 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 7 |

1. Mean at 10 cm: add the trials, $52 + 48 + 50 = 150$, then divide by 3, giving 50 bubbles per minute.
2. Describe the trend with data: as the lamp moves from 10 cm to 50 cm away, the mean rate falls from 50 to 7 bubbles per minute.
3. Notice the pattern: the rate falls steeply at first and then more slowly.
4. Explain: a more distant lamp gives a lower light intensity, so less light energy is absorbed, photosynthesis is slower and less oxygen is released.
:::

## Limiting factors

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Explaining limiting factors is Supplement content for Cambridge and is part of the ZIMSEC syllabus.
:::

At any moment, the rate of photosynthesis is held down by **whichever factor is in shortest supply**. To find the limiting factor, ask: "If I increased this factor, would the rate go up?"

![Graph from Example 2: at 0.04% carbon dioxide the rate rises from 10 to 30 and levels off from light intensity 4; at 0.10% carbon dioxide it is the same up to intensity 2, then climbs higher to about 46, showing light limits the rate first and then carbon dioxide.](media:diagrams/limiting-factors-graph.png "Figure: rate of photosynthesis against light intensity at two carbon dioxide concentrations")

:::worked-solution[Example 2: reading a limiting-factor graph]
The table gives the rate of photosynthesis of a plant at two carbon dioxide concentrations. (These values would form two curves on a graph of rate against light intensity.)

| Light intensity / arbitrary units | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rate at 0.04% carbon dioxide | 10 | 20 | 27 | 30 | 30 | 30 |
| Rate at 0.10% carbon dioxide | 10 | 20 | 30 | 39 | 45 | 46 |

1. At light intensities 1 and 2 the two rates are identical, so extra carbon dioxide makes no difference. **Light intensity** is the limiting factor.
2. At 0.04% carbon dioxide the rate levels off at 30 from intensity 4 upwards, so more light makes no difference.
3. At the same light intensities, the rate is higher at 0.10% carbon dioxide, so **carbon dioxide concentration** was limiting the lower curve.
4. The 0.10% curve also levels off, at about 46. Another factor, such as **temperature**, is now limiting.
:::

### Using limiting factors in a greenhouse

Growers can raise the light intensity with lamps, the temperature with heaters and the carbon dioxide concentration with carbon dioxide gas or paraffin burners (burning paraffin releases carbon dioxide and heat). Raising a factor that is **not** limiting wastes money: on a cold, bright winter morning, more lamps will not help if temperature is the limiting factor.

## Gas exchange in plants

Plants **respire all the time**, taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide. In the light they also **photosynthesise**, taking in carbon dioxide and giving out oxygen. What we measure is the **net** (overall) exchange:

| Conditions | Rates | Net gas exchange |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bright light | Photosynthesis faster than respiration | Carbon dioxide taken in, oxygen given out |
| Dim light (at one particular low intensity) | Photosynthesis equal to respiration | No net exchange |
| Dark | Respiration only | Oxygen taken in, carbon dioxide given out |

:::example{title="Example 3: hydrogencarbonate indicator tubes"}
Four sealed tubes each contain orange-red hydrogencarbonate indicator. Tubes A, B and C each contain a piece of pondweed; tube D has none. A is in bright light, B is wrapped in foil (dark), C is covered with gauze (dim light) and D is in bright light.

After two hours: **A is purple**, because photosynthesis was faster than respiration and the pondweed removed carbon dioxide. **B is yellow**, because the pondweed only respired and added carbon dioxide. **C is still orange-red**, because photosynthesis and respiration were balanced. **D is still orange-red**: it is the control, showing that light alone does not change the indicator.

The tubes are sealed with bungs so that carbon dioxide cannot enter from, or escape to, the air.
:::

## Stomata and their distribution

Guard cells take up water and become turgid in the light, which opens the stoma; in the dark, or when the plant is short of water, they lose water and the stoma closes.

In most **dicotyledon** leaves, such as bean, msasa and mango leaves, most stomata are in the **lower epidermis**. The lower surface is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates through the stomata there. Upright monocotyledon leaves such as maize have stomata on both surfaces, and floating leaves such as water lily pads have them on the upper surface only.

:::example{title="Example 4: counting stomata"}
Clear nail varnish was painted on each surface of a bean leaf, peeled off when dry and examined under a microscope. In three fields of view the lower surface showed 24, 26 and 22 stomata (mean 24) and the upper surface 4, 6 and 5 (mean 5). The lower surface has nearly five times as many stomata. Cobalt chloride paper clipped to both surfaces gives the same conclusion: it turns from blue to pink faster on the lower surface, where more water vapour escapes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="A plateau means photosynthesis has stopped"}
On a levelled-off graph the rate is still high but **constant**: another factor is limiting it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hotter is always faster"}
Above the optimum temperature the enzymes are denatured and the rate falls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Plants respire only in the dark"}
Plants respire all the time. In bright light photosynthesis is faster, so the **net** exchange is carbon dioxide in and oxygen out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Hydrogencarbonate indicator detects oxygen"}
The indicator responds to **carbon dioxide** concentration: yellow for more, purple for less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Letting the lamp heat the pondweed"}
If the lamp warms the water, temperature changes as well as light intensity. Use a heat shield or an LED lamp and check the water temperature.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe" a graph: give the trend in each region with data and units, such as "the rate increases from 10 to 30 units between light intensity 1 and 4, then stays constant".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain" a plateau: say that the factor on the x-axis is **no longer limiting** and name the factor that probably is.
:::

:::exam-tip
In planning questions, name the independent, dependent and controlled variables and say **how** you would control each one (water bath for temperature, sodium hydrogencarbonate for carbon dioxide).
:::

:::exam-tip
For indicator results, compare the **rates** of photosynthesis and respiration and use the word "net".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature affect the rate of photosynthesis; temperature acts through enzymes, which have an optimum.
- The rate of oxygen bubbles from pondweed measures the rate; control temperature and carbon dioxide while changing light intensity.
- A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply; only increasing it raises the rate.
- Plants respire all the time; in bright light the net exchange is carbon dioxide in and oxygen out, in the dark the reverse.
- Hydrogencarbonate indicator: orange-red (air), purple (carbon dioxide down), yellow (carbon dioxide up).
- Most stomata are in the lower epidermis of dicotyledon leaves; guard cells open them in the light.
:::
