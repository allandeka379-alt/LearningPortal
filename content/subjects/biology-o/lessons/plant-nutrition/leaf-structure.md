## Introduction

A leaf is a factory. Its raw materials are carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil, its power supply is light, and its product is sugar that must be sent to the rest of the plant. Look at the leaves of a bean plant in a school garden in Gweru: they are broad, flat and thin, and they are held out on stalks so that each one catches the sun. Inside, the leaf is just as carefully organised.

In this lesson you will learn the parts of a dicotyledonous leaf, from the outside and in a thin slice cut across it (a **transverse section**), and how each part helps photosynthesis. You should already know the word equation for photosynthesis and that gases move by diffusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Lamina"}
The broad, flat blade of a leaf, where most photosynthesis takes place.
:::

:::definition{term="Mesophyll"}
The tissue between the upper and lower epidermis. It is made of **palisade mesophyll** (tall, tightly packed cells) and **spongy mesophyll** (irregular cells with air spaces).
:::

:::definition{term="Vascular bundle (vein)"}
A strand of transport tissue containing **xylem**, which brings water and mineral ions into the leaf, and **phloem**, which carries sucrose and amino acids out of it.
:::

:::definition{term="Stoma (plural: stomata)"}
A pore in the epidermis, between two guard cells, through which carbon dioxide, oxygen and water vapour diffuse.
:::

## External features of a leaf

- The **lamina** is broad, giving a large surface area to absorb light and carbon dioxide.
- The lamina is **thin**, so light reaches the cells inside and gases diffuse only a short distance.
- The **petiole** (leaf stalk) holds the lamina out towards the light.
- A **midrib** and a network of **veins** support the lamina and carry water in and sugars out.

## Inside the leaf: a transverse section

Imagine a thin slice cut across a bean leaf and viewed under a microscope. From the upper surface to the lower surface you would see these layers:

1. **Waxy cuticle**: a thin, non-cellular layer of wax.
2. **Upper epidermis**: one layer of transparent, closely fitting cells with no chloroplasts.
3. **Palisade mesophyll**: tall, column-shaped cells packed closely together, each with many chloroplasts.
4. **Spongy mesophyll**: rounded, irregular cells with fewer chloroplasts and large **air spaces** between them.
5. **Vascular bundle**: xylem towards the upper side and phloem towards the lower side, running through the mesophyll.
6. **Lower epidermis**: one layer of cells containing many **stomata**, each between two sausage-shaped **guard cells**.
7. **Lower cuticle**: usually thinner than the upper one.

| Structure | How it helps the leaf |
| --- | --- |
| Waxy cuticle | Waterproof, so it reduces water loss; transparent, so light passes through |
| Upper epidermis | Transparent (no chloroplasts), so light reaches the palisade layer; protects the cells beneath |
| Palisade mesophyll | Main site of photosynthesis: many chloroplasts, near the upper surface where light is brightest, cells packed tightly so many fit in |
| Spongy mesophyll | Air spaces let carbon dioxide and oxygen diffuse through the leaf; the many moist cell surfaces give a large internal surface area for gas exchange |
| Xylem | Brings water (a raw material) and mineral ions to the mesophyll cells |
| Phloem | Carries sucrose and amino acids away to the rest of the plant |
| Stomata and guard cells | Let carbon dioxide in and oxygen out; guard cells open the stomata in light and close them when water is short |

:::keyfact{title="Chloroplasts in the epidermis"}
In most dicotyledon leaves the only epidermal cells with chloroplasts are the **guard cells**. The other epidermal cells are transparent.
:::

## Examples: following the raw materials

:::example{title="Example 1: the journey of a carbon dioxide molecule"}
A molecule of carbon dioxide in the air next to a bean leaf on a sunny morning:

1. diffuses through an open **stoma**;
2. moves through the **air spaces** of the spongy mesophyll;
3. dissolves in the thin film of water on the surface of a mesophyll cell;
4. diffuses through the cell wall, cell membrane and cytoplasm into a **chloroplast**, where it is used in photosynthesis.

It keeps moving inwards because photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide, so its concentration inside the leaf stays lower than in the air outside.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: the journey of a water molecule"}
Water arrives in the leaf in the **xylem** of a vein. It passes into the surrounding mesophyll cells by **osmosis** and reaches the chloroplasts of the palisade cells. Some of the water is used in photosynthesis; much of it evaporates from the cell surfaces into the air spaces and leaves through the stomata as water vapour.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: identifying tissues from a description"}
A student viewing a slide describes three layers. Layer X is one cell thick, with pores between pairs of bean-shaped cells. Layer Y has tall cells, close together and full of green dots. Layer Z has loosely arranged cells with gaps between them.

- **X** is the lower epidermis: pores between guard cells are stomata.
- **Y** is the palisade mesophyll: tall cells packed with chloroplasts.
- **Z** is the spongy mesophyll: the gaps are air spaces.
:::

:::example{title="Example 4: sun leaves and shade leaves"}
On many trees, the leaves in full sun are thicker, with a deeper palisade layer, than the leaves deep in the shade of the same tree. In bright light, extra layers of chloroplast-filled cells can use the extra light. In shade there is little light to share, so a thin leaf with a large surface area is more efficient. Structure matches the conditions.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Stomata are in the mesophyll"}
Stomata are pores in the **epidermis**, mostly the lower epidermis. The air spaces they open into are in the spongy mesophyll.
:::

:::mistake{title="The upper epidermis photosynthesises"}
Upper epidermis cells have **no chloroplasts**: they are transparent so that light passes through to the palisade cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Xylem carries sugar"}
Xylem carries water and mineral ions **into** the leaf. Sucrose leaves in the **phloem**.
:::

:::mistake{title="The cuticle is a layer of cells"}
The cuticle is a layer of **wax** made by the epidermis. It has no cells.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to "explain how a structure is adapted", give the **feature** and link it to the **function**: "palisade cells contain many chloroplasts **so** they absorb a lot of light energy for photosynthesis".
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the order of layers from top to bottom; examiners often describe a layer in words and ask you to name it.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a vein of a leaf, the xylem is on the **upper** side and the phloem on the **lower** side. Do not mix them up when labelling a diagram.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Thin" earns marks only when linked to a reason: a **short diffusion distance** for carbon dioxide, or light reaching all the cells.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Leaves are broad (large surface area) and thin (short diffusion distance, light reaches every cell).
- From top to bottom: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll with air spaces, lower epidermis with stomata.
- Palisade cells are the main site of photosynthesis: many chloroplasts, tightly packed, near the light.
- Air spaces in the spongy mesophyll and stomata in the lower epidermis allow gas exchange.
- Xylem brings water and mineral ions in; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids out.
- Guard cells, which contain chloroplasts, open and close the stomata.
:::
