## Introduction

Digestion has turned a meal into a soup of small, soluble molecules: glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins, mineral ions and water. None of it is any use until it crosses the wall of the gut into the blood, and then reaches the cells that need it.

This lesson explains **absorption** (getting nutrients into the blood) and **assimilation** (cells using them). You should already know the products of digestion and how substances move by diffusion, osmosis and active transport.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Absorption"}
The movement of digested food molecules, mineral ions and water from the intestine into the blood (or lymph).
:::

:::definition{term="Assimilation"}
The uptake and use of absorbed nutrients by body cells, for example to release energy or to build new cell parts.
:::

:::definition{term="Villus (plural villi)"}
One of millions of tiny finger-like projections, about 1 mm long, that cover the inner surface of the small intestine.
:::

:::definition{term="Hepatic portal vein (ZIMSEC)"}
The blood vessel that carries blood rich in absorbed nutrients from the small intestine to the liver.
:::

## Where absorption happens

- **Small intestine** (mainly the ileum): nearly all the digested food, and most of the water.
- **Colon**: some more water and mineral ions from the undigested remains.

Very little is absorbed through the stomach wall.

:::worked-solution[Example: how much water is absorbed where?]
Each day about 9 dm³ of fluid (drinks plus digestive juices) passes into the small intestine. About 7.5 dm³ is absorbed there and about 1.4 dm³ is absorbed in the colon. How much leaves in the faeces, and what percentage of the 9 dm³ does the small intestine absorb?
1. Faeces: $9 - 7.5 - 1.4 = 0.1\ \text{dm}^3$.
2. Small intestine: $\dfrac{7.5}{9} \times 100 = 83.3\%$.
3. So the small intestine absorbs most of the water, and the colon absorbs most of what is left.
:::

## How the small intestine is adapted for absorption

Absorption is fastest when there is a **large surface area**, a **short distance** to cross and a **steep concentration gradient**. The small intestine has all three.

| Feature | How it helps absorption |
| --- | --- |
| Very long (several metres) | Gives a large surface area and time for absorption |
| Inner wall folded and covered in villi | Greatly increases the surface area |
| (Supplement) Microvilli on the epithelial cells | Increase the surface area even further |
| Wall of each villus only one cell thick | Short distance for molecules to cross |
| Dense network of capillaries in each villus | Carries absorbed molecules away, keeping the concentration gradient steep |
| (Supplement) A lacteal in each villus | Absorbs fatty acids and glycerol |
| Epithelial cells with many mitochondria (ZIMSEC) | Release energy for active uptake |

### Inside a villus (Supplement)

From the outside inwards, a villus has:

- **epithelium**: a single layer of cells whose surface membranes are folded into **microvilli**;
- **blood capillaries**: a network just under the epithelium, which absorbs glucose and amino acids;
- **lacteal**: a lymph vessel in the centre, which absorbs fatty acids and glycerol (these are made back into fats and carried in the lymph, which later empties into the blood near the heart).

Blood from the capillaries drains into venules and then into the hepatic portal vein.

:::keyfact{title="Where each product goes"}
Glucose and amino acids → **blood capillaries**. Fatty acids and glycerol → **lacteal** (lymph).
:::

## How nutrients cross the lining (ZIMSEC)

- **Diffusion:** just after a meal, glucose and amino acids are more concentrated in the intestine than in the blood, so they diffuse into the blood.
- **Active uptake:** when their concentration in the intestine falls below that in the blood, the epithelial cells keep absorbing them by **active transport**, using energy from respiration. Nothing useful is wasted in the faeces.
- **Osmosis:** water follows the absorbed nutrients and ions into the blood.

## From the intestine to the liver (ZIMSEC)

The capillaries of the villi join to form the **hepatic portal vein**, which takes the nutrient-rich blood **straight to the liver**. The liver processes the nutrients before the blood flows on (in the hepatic vein) to the rest of the body.

## Assimilation and the liver (ZIMSEC)

| Nutrient | How cells use it (assimilation) | Role of the liver |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glucose | Respired in all cells to release energy | Stores extra glucose as **glycogen**; turns glycogen back into glucose when the blood level falls, keeping blood glucose steady |
| Amino acids | Built into proteins: enzymes, antibodies, hormones, new cells | Makes plasma proteins (such as the clotting protein fibrinogen); breaks down excess amino acids, forming **urea** (deamination) |
| Fatty acids and glycerol | Built into cell membranes; stored as fat under the skin and around organs | Can store some fat and convert it for use |

The liver also **breaks down alcohol, drugs and other toxins** (detoxification) and **stores** glycogen, iron and vitamins A and D.

:::example{title="Where does a meal of beans and sadza end up?"}
- The **starch** in the sadza becomes glucose, absorbed into the capillaries of the villi. In the liver some is stored as glycogen; the rest is carried to cells, such as muscle cells, which respire it.
- The **protein** in the beans becomes amino acids, which travel the same way. Cells build them into new proteins, for example to repair skin; the liver turns any excess into urea.
- The **fibre** is not absorbed at all: it carries on to the large intestine.
:::

## The large intestine (ZIMSEC)

- The **colon** absorbs water and mineral ions from the undigested remains, so the faeces become semi-solid.
- Bacteria living in the colon make some vitamins, such as vitamin K, which are absorbed.
- The **rectum** stores the faeces until they are egested through the anus.

If food passes through the colon too quickly, too little water is absorbed and the faeces are watery (diarrhoea).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling villi cells"}
A villus is made of many cells. Microvilli are tiny folds on the surface of each epithelial cell.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fats are absorbed into the capillaries"}
Fatty acids and glycerol go into the **lacteal**. Glucose and amino acids go into the capillaries.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the large intestine absorbs digested food"}
The colon absorbs mainly water and mineral ions. Digested food is absorbed in the small intestine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up absorption and assimilation"}
Absorption: into the blood. Assimilation: used by the cells.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To **explain** an adaptation, link the feature to its effect: "villi **increase the surface area**, so **more** glucose is absorbed **per second**". A feature on its own scores less.
:::

:::exam-tip
Remember the three ideas that make absorption fast: large surface area, short diffusion distance, steep concentration gradient (kept steep by blood flow).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Digested food and most water are absorbed in the small intestine; the colon absorbs some water and mineral ions.
- The small intestine is long and folded, with villi (and, Supplement, microvilli) giving a huge surface area, a one-cell-thick lining and a rich blood supply.
- Supplement: capillaries absorb glucose and amino acids; lacteals absorb fatty acids and glycerol.
- ZIMSEC: absorption is by diffusion and active uptake; the hepatic portal vein carries nutrients to the liver.
- ZIMSEC: assimilation is the use of nutrients by cells; the liver stores glycogen, iron and vitamins A and D, controls glucose and amino acid levels, and breaks down alcohol and toxins.
:::
