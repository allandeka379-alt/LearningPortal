## Introduction

A bowl of beans contains starch, protein and a little fat. None of these can get into your blood as they are: their molecules are far too large to pass through the wall of the intestine. Before your body can use them, enzymes must break them into small, soluble molecules. This is **chemical digestion**.

In this lesson you will learn which enzymes digest which foods, where each enzyme is made and where it works, and the jobs of hydrochloric acid and bile. You should already know that enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts, that each has an active site that fits its substrate, and that they work best at an optimum temperature and pH.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chemical digestion"}
The breakdown of large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble molecules by enzymes, so that they can be absorbed.
:::

:::definition{term="Substrate"}
The molecule that an enzyme acts on, for example starch for amylase.
:::

:::definition{term="Product"}
The molecule that an enzyme reaction makes, for example maltose from starch.
:::

## Why chemical digestion is needed

Starch, proteins and fats are large molecules. The lining of the small intestine only lets small, soluble molecules through into the blood. Chemical digestion therefore turns:

- **starch** into **simple sugars** (maltose, then glucose);
- **proteins** into **amino acids**;
- **fats and oils** into **fatty acids and glycerol**.

:::example{title="A model gut made of Visking tubing"}
Visking tubing is a partially permeable membrane, like the lining of the gut. Two bags are set up in warm water at 37 °C:

- **Bag A:** starch solution + amylase.
- **Bag B:** starch solution + boiled (denatured) amylase.

After 30 minutes the water outside each bag is tested.

| Water outside | Iodine test (starch) | Benedict's test (reducing sugar) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bag A | Stays orange-brown: no starch | Brick-red: sugar present |
| Bag B | Stays orange-brown: no starch | Stays blue: no sugar |

Starch molecules are too large to pass through the tubing. In bag A, amylase digested the starch into small maltose molecules, which passed out. In bag B the boiled enzyme could not work, so no sugar was made. This is exactly why food must be digested before it can be absorbed.
:::

## The digestive enzymes

| Enzyme | Substrate → product(s) | Made in | Where it acts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amylase | Starch → maltose (a simple reducing sugar) | Salivary glands; pancreas | Mouth; small intestine |
| Protease | Protein → amino acids | Stomach; pancreas; small intestine | Stomach; small intestine |
| Lipase | Fats and oils → fatty acids and glycerol | Pancreas | Small intestine |

### More detail for Supplement candidates

- **Maltase** breaks **maltose → glucose**. It is not released into the gut: it sits on the membranes of the cells lining the small intestine (the epithelium), so the glucose is made right where it is absorbed.
- **Pepsin** is the protease made by the stomach. It works in the **acidic** conditions of the stomach, at an optimum of about pH 2.
- **Trypsin** is a protease made by the pancreas. It works in the **alkaline** conditions of the small intestine.

### Rennin (ZIMSEC)

**Rennin** is an enzyme made by the stomach that **clots milk protein**, turning liquid milk into a semi-solid curd. The clotted milk stays in the stomach longer, giving proteases more time to digest it. Rennin is mainly important in **young mammals**, such as calves, which feed only on milk. It is also used to make cheese.

## Hydrochloric acid

The stomach lining releases **hydrochloric acid** as part of gastric juice. It has two jobs:

1. It **kills harmful microorganisms** in food and drink, helping to protect us from disease.
2. It gives the **acidic pH** that is the optimum for the stomach's protease (pepsin).

The acid does **not** digest food itself: the enzymes do the digesting.

:::keyfact{title="Salivary amylase stops in the stomach"}
Amylase from saliva works best at about pH 7. In the strongly acidic stomach its active site changes shape and it stops working. Starch digestion starts again in the duodenum, where pancreatic amylase works.
:::

## Bile

Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder and released into the duodenum. It contains **no enzymes**, but it helps digestion in two ways:

- **(Supplement)** Bile is **alkaline**. It **neutralises** the acidic mixture of food and gastric juice arriving from the stomach, giving the slightly alkaline pH that the enzymes of the small intestine need.
- It **emulsifies** fats, breaking them into tiny droplets with a large surface area for lipase (see the lesson on physical digestion).

## Following each food through the gut

| Food | Mouth | Stomach | Small intestine |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starch | Salivary amylase → maltose | Amylase stops working (acid) | Pancreatic amylase → maltose; maltase → glucose |
| Protein | No digestion | Protease (pepsin) in acid | Protease (trypsin and others) → amino acids |
| Fat | No digestion | Little digestion | Bile emulsifies; lipase → fatty acids and glycerol |

:::worked-solution[Example: does the acid digest protein?]
Rudo set up four tubes of cloudy egg-white suspension at 37 °C and timed how long each took to go clear (clear means the protein had been digested). Tube 1 (pepsin + hydrochloric acid) cleared in 150 s. Tube 2 (pepsin + water), tube 3 (boiled pepsin + acid) and tube 4 (acid only) were all still cloudy after 30 minutes (1800 s).
1. Rate $= \dfrac{1000}{\text{time}}$. Tube 1: $\dfrac{1000}{150} = 6.7$. The other tubes did not clear in 1800 s, so their rates were below $\dfrac{1000}{1800} = 0.56$: tube 1 was more than 12 times faster than any of them.
2. Tube 2 shows that pepsin does not work without the acid: the acid gives it the low pH it needs (its optimum is about pH 2).
3. Tube 4 shows that the acid alone does **not** digest protein.
4. Tube 3 shows that boiling denatures pepsin, so active enzyme is needed: the enzyme digests the protein, and the acid only provides the right pH.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hydrochloric acid digests protein"}
The acid kills microorganisms and gives pepsin its optimum pH. The enzyme does the digesting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying bile is an enzyme"}
Bile contains no enzymes. It neutralises stomach acid and emulsifies fats.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying amylase makes glucose"}
Amylase makes **maltose**. (Supplement) Maltase then breaks maltose into glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sending pancreatic juice to the stomach"}
Pancreatic juice enters the **duodenum**, the first part of the small intestine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'lipase makes fatty acids' only"}
Lipase produces fatty acids **and glycerol**. Give both products.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Learn the enzyme table by heart: for each enzyme be ready to give the **substrate**, the **product**, **where it is made** and **where it acts**. Questions often give a table with gaps.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Describe the functions of hydrochloric acid" needs **two** points: kills microorganisms; provides the acidic (optimum) pH for the stomach protease.
:::

:::exam-tip
In practical questions, a tube with **boiled enzyme** is the control that shows the enzyme causes the change. Say "boiling **denatures** the enzyme", never "kills" it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Chemical digestion uses enzymes to turn large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones that can be absorbed.
- Amylase: starch → maltose (mouth and small intestine). Protease: protein → amino acids (stomach and small intestine). Lipase: fats → fatty acids and glycerol (small intestine).
- Hydrochloric acid kills microorganisms and gives the acidic pH needed by the stomach protease; it does not digest food.
- Supplement: maltase on the intestinal lining makes glucose; pepsin works in acid, trypsin in alkali; bile neutralises the acid from the stomach.
- ZIMSEC: rennin clots milk protein, mainly in young mammals; bile also emulsifies fats.
:::
