## Introduction

Think about a typical day of meals: tea and bread in the morning, sadza with beans and covo at lunch, and perhaps sadza with kapenta or chicken in the evening. Each food on the plate does a different job. Sadza gives energy, beans and kapenta give protein for growth, and covo gives vitamins, iron and fibre.

Your body cannot make most of these substances, so it must get them from food. A diet that leaves one out, or gives far too much of another, can make you ill. This lesson explains what each nutrient does, where to find it, and how much energy foods provide.

You should already know the chemical make-up of carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and the food tests for them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nutrient"}
A substance in food that the body uses to release energy, to grow and repair tissues, or to keep its cells working properly.
:::

:::definition{term="Balanced diet"}
A diet that contains all the nutrients, fibre and water the body needs, in the correct proportions, and that provides the right amount of energy for the person.
:::

:::definition{term="Deficiency disease"}
A disease caused by a lack of one particular nutrient in the diet, such as scurvy (lack of vitamin C).
:::

## The components of a balanced diet

A balanced diet has seven components. Five of them are nutrients; fibre and water are also essential.

| Component | Good sources | Why the body needs it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates (starch and sugars) | Sadza (maize meal), rice, bread, potatoes, sweet potatoes, sugar | The main source of energy, released in respiration |
| Fats and oils | Cooking oil, margarine, butter, groundnuts and peanut butter, fatty meat, milk | A concentrated energy store; insulation under the skin; part of cell membranes; they carry fat-soluble vitamins |
| Proteins | Beans, groundnuts, kapenta and matemba, eggs, milk, meat, chicken, soya | Growth and repair of tissues; making enzymes, antibodies, some hormones and muscle |
| Vitamin C | Oranges, lemons, guavas, tomatoes, fresh green vegetables such as covo and rape | Healthy skin, gums and the tissue that holds the body together; helps wounds heal |
| Vitamin D | Made by the skin in sunlight; eggs, oily fish, liver, butter | Helps the gut absorb calcium, so bones and teeth grow strong |
| Calcium | Milk, sour milk (maas), cheese, kapenta and matemba eaten with their bones, green vegetables | Strong bones and teeth; needed for blood clotting and for muscles to contract |
| Iron | Red meat, liver, beans, dark green leafy vegetables, eggs | Making haemoglobin, the red pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen |
| Fibre (roughage) | Whole-grain maize meal (roller meal), vegetables, fruit, beans | Adds bulk to the food so the gut muscles can push it along; prevents constipation |
| Water | Drinking water, drinks, fruit and vegetables | The solvent in which reactions take place; transports substances in blood; removes wastes in urine; cools the body as sweat evaporates |

Notice three things about the table:

- **Vitamins and mineral ions do not provide energy.** They are needed in small amounts to keep cells and tissues working.
- **Fibre is not digested.** It is mainly cellulose from plant cell walls, which humans cannot digest, so it passes through the gut and helps peristalsis.
- **Proteins are not mainly for energy.** The body uses protein for energy only when it has more than it needs or when it is starving.

### Extra vitamins and mineral ions (ZIMSEC)

| Nutrient | Good sources | Why the body needs it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vitamin A | Carrots, pumpkin, orange-fleshed sweet potato, mangoes, pawpaw, dark green vegetables, liver, eggs, milk | Seeing in dim light; healthy skin and moist linings such as those of the eyes |
| Iodine | Iodised salt, sea fish and other seafood | The thyroid gland in the neck uses it to make the hormone thyroxine, which controls the rate of metabolism |

## Deficiency diseases

When one nutrient is missing for a long time, a particular deficiency disease develops.

| Disease | Missing nutrient | Main signs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scurvy | Vitamin C | Swollen, bleeding gums; wounds heal slowly; tiredness |
| Rickets | Vitamin D or calcium | Soft, weak bones in children that bend under the body's weight, so the legs bow |
| Anaemia | Iron | Too little haemoglobin: tiredness, pale skin, shortness of breath |
| Night blindness (ZIMSEC) | Vitamin A | Poor vision in dim light |
| Goitre (ZIMSEC) | Iodine | A swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck |

:::keyfact{title="Two pairs to learn for both boards"}
Lack of **vitamin C** causes **scurvy**. Lack of **vitamin D** or **calcium** causes **rickets**.
:::

## Energy in food

The energy in food is measured in **kilojoules (kJ)**. When one gram of each nutrient is respired completely, it releases roughly:

| Nutrient | Energy released per gram |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrate | about 17 kJ |
| Protein | about 17 kJ |
| Fat | about 39 kJ |

Fat releases more than twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate, which is why it is a good energy store. Exam questions always give you the values to use.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: energy in a meal]
A plate of sadza, beans and covo contains 95 g of carbohydrate, 18 g of protein and 9 g of fat. Use 17 kJ/g for carbohydrate and protein and 39 kJ/g for fat to calculate the energy it provides.
1. Carbohydrate: $95 \times 17 = 1615\ \text{kJ}$.
2. Protein: $18 \times 17 = 306\ \text{kJ}$.
3. Fat: $9 \times 39 = 351\ \text{kJ}$.
4. Total: $1615 + 306 + 351 = 2272\ \text{kJ}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: what fraction of a day's needs?]
Suppose the person eating the meal in Example 1 needs 11 500 kJ a day. What percentage of this does the meal supply?
1. Percentage $= \dfrac{2272}{11\,500} \times 100$.
2. $= 19.76\ldots\%$.
3. So the meal supplies about $19.8\%$ (just under one fifth) of the day's energy.
:::

## How needs change from person to person (ZIMSEC)

A balanced diet is not the same for everyone. Needs depend on:

- **Age.** Children and teenagers are growing, so they need plenty of protein, calcium and iron for each kilogram of body mass, and a lot of energy. Elderly people are usually less active, so they need less energy.
- **Sex.** Teenage boys and men usually need more energy than girls and women of the same age, because they tend to be larger and have more muscle. Girls and women need more iron, to replace the blood lost in menstruation.
- **Activity.** A farm worker digging a field or a footballer in training uses far more energy than someone sitting at a desk all day, so needs more energy-giving food.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** A pregnant woman needs extra protein for the baby's growth, extra calcium for its bones and extra iron for its blood. A breastfeeding mother needs extra energy, protein and calcium to make milk.

:::example{title="Comparing members of one family"}
Rudo's family lives in Masvingo. Her grandfather is 75 and spends most of the day resting at home. Her brother Tatenda is 16 and trains for football every afternoon. Her aunt is six months pregnant.

- Tatenda needs the most energy of the three: he is growing **and** very active.
- The grandfather needs the least energy, because he is not growing and is not very active.
- The aunt needs extra protein, calcium and iron for the growing baby, not just extra energy.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying protein is the main source of energy"}
Carbohydrates are the main source of energy. Protein is used mainly for growth and repair.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fibre provides energy or is digested"}
Humans cannot digest cellulose, so fibre provides no energy. Its job is to add bulk so the food moves along the gut.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a balanced diet means equal amounts of each food"}
"Balanced" means the **correct proportions** for the person's needs. We need far more carbohydrate than vitamins.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up scurvy and rickets"}
Scurvy: vitamin **C**, bleeding gums. Rickets: vitamin **D** or calcium, soft bent bones.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **state a source**, name a food ("oranges", "milk", "beans"), not a vague group such as "healthy food".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **describe the importance** of a nutrient, say what it does in the body: "iron is needed to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen", not just "iron is good for the blood".
:::

:::exam-tip
In energy calculations, multiply each mass by its own energy value, add the answers and give the unit, kJ. Show every step: method marks are awarded even if the final answer is wrong.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A balanced diet contains carbohydrates, fats and oils, proteins, vitamins, mineral ions, fibre and water in the correct proportions for the person.
- Carbohydrates and fats supply energy; proteins are for growth and repair; vitamins and mineral ions keep the body working but give no energy.
- Vitamin C keeps skin and gums healthy; vitamin D and calcium build strong bones and teeth; iron is needed to make haemoglobin.
- Scurvy is caused by a lack of vitamin C and rickets by a lack of vitamin D or calcium.
- Fat releases about 39 kJ/g, more than twice as much as carbohydrate or protein (about 17 kJ/g).
- ZIMSEC: vitamin A prevents night blindness, iodine prevents goitre, and needs vary with age, sex, activity and pregnancy.
:::
