## Introduction

When people hear the word **malnutrition**, they often picture a hungry child. That is part of the story, but a person who eats far too much fatty and sugary food is also malnourished. Malnutrition simply means that the diet is not balanced.

In Zimbabwe, clinics weigh young children regularly and plot their mass on a growth chart, so that a child who is not growing well can be helped early. This lesson explains the main forms of malnutrition, how to recognise them and how they can be prevented.

You should already know what a balanced diet is and why the body needs each nutrient.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Malnutrition"}
A condition caused by a diet that does not contain the right amounts of nutrients: too little, too much, or the wrong balance.
:::

:::definition{term="Under-nutrition"}
Malnutrition caused by too little food energy, or too little of one or more nutrients.
:::

:::definition{term="Over-nutrition"}
Malnutrition caused by too much food, especially energy-rich fat and sugar.
:::

:::definition{term="Oedema"}
Swelling caused by fluid collecting in the tissues of the body.
:::

## Protein-energy malnutrition

Young children grow quickly, so they need plenty of protein and energy for their size. Two serious conditions develop when they do not get enough.

### Kwashiorkor: too little protein

Kwashiorkor is caused by a diet that lacks **protein**, even if it supplies enough energy. It often appears after a young child is weaned from breast milk onto a diet made mostly of thin maize porridge, with little milk, eggs, beans or meat.

Signs of kwashiorkor:

- **oedema**: swollen feet, legs and face;
- a swollen abdomen;
- thin muscles, often hidden by the swelling;
- patches of dark, cracked or peeling skin;
- thin hair that may turn pale or reddish and falls out easily;
- poor growth, loss of appetite and a miserable, inactive child.

Because of the swelling, a child with kwashiorkor may look plump at first glance. In fact the child is seriously ill. One reason for the swelling is that there is too little protein in the blood plasma, so fluid that leaks out of the capillaries is not drawn back in and collects in the tissues.

### Marasmus: too little energy and protein

Marasmus is caused by a severe lack of **both energy and protein**: the child simply gets too little food. It can happen in famine, when a baby is not breastfed and is given very diluted feeds, or after repeated bouts of diarrhoea.

Signs of marasmus:

- severe wasting: the child is very thin, with ribs and bones showing;
- loose, wrinkled skin, so the face can look old;
- very low body mass for the child's age;
- **no** oedema.

| Feature | Kwashiorkor | Marasmus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main cause | Too little protein | Too little energy and protein |
| Oedema | Yes | No |
| Appearance | Swollen face, legs and abdomen | Very thin, "skin and bones" |
| Skin and hair | Patchy skin; thin, pale hair | Loose, wrinkled skin |

Children with either condition catch infections easily, because antibodies are proteins and they cannot make enough of them.

### Prevention

- Breastfeed babies, then wean them onto a varied diet that includes protein foods such as beans, groundnuts, eggs, milk, kapenta and soya.
- Give young children several small meals a day, because their stomachs are small.
- Weigh children regularly at the clinic so that poor growth is noticed early.
- Treat diarrhoea quickly with oral rehydration and continue feeding.
- Teach families about balanced diets, and grow vegetables and legumes in nutrition gardens.

A child who is already severely malnourished must be treated under medical care, because feeding has to be restarted carefully.

## Deficiency diseases

| Disease | Missing nutrient | Main signs | Good sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Night blindness | Vitamin A | Poor vision in dim light; a severe lack can damage the eyes | Carrots, pumpkin, mangoes, dark green vegetables, liver, eggs |
| Scurvy | Vitamin C | Swollen, bleeding gums; wounds heal slowly | Oranges, guavas, tomatoes, fresh green vegetables |
| Rickets | Vitamin D or calcium | Soft bones that bend; bowed legs in children | Sunlight on the skin, milk, eggs, small fish eaten whole |
| Anaemia | Iron | Tiredness, pale skin, shortness of breath | Liver, red meat, beans, dark green vegetables |
| Goitre | Iodine | Swollen thyroid gland at the front of the neck | Iodised salt, sea fish |

Girls and women need more iron than boys and men because blood is lost during menstruation, and pregnant women need extra iron for the baby's blood. (Anaemia can also be caused by blood loss or by diseases such as malaria, not only by diet.)

**Food fortification** means adding vitamins or mineral ions to everyday foods. Adding iodine to salt is the best-known example; some brands of maize meal and cooking oil also have vitamins and mineral ions added.

## Over-nutrition and obesity

When a person regularly takes in more energy than they use, the extra is stored as **fat** under the skin and around the organs. Any energy-rich food can cause this, not only fatty food: excess sugar and starch are also turned into fat. A person with so much stored fat that their health is at risk is **obese**.

Doctors often check an adult's body mass using the **body mass index (BMI)**:

:::formula{name="Body mass index (adults)"}
$$
\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{mass in kg}}{(\text{height in m})^2}
$$
:::

For adults, a BMI of 18.5 to 24.9 is usually taken as a healthy range, 25 to 29.9 as overweight and 30 or more as obese. (Children are assessed with growth charts instead.)

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calculating a BMI]
A man has a mass of 95 kg and a height of 1.75 m. Calculate his BMI and state what it shows.
1. Square the height: $1.75^2 = 3.0625$.
2. BMI $= \dfrac{95}{3.0625} = 31.02\ldots \approx 31.0$.
3. A BMI of 30 or more counts as obese, so his health is at risk.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the highest healthy mass]
A woman is 1.60 m tall. What is the greatest mass she can have and still have a BMI below 25?
1. Rearrange: mass $= \text{BMI} \times (\text{height})^2$.
2. Mass $= 25 \times 1.60^2 = 25 \times 2.56 = 64\ \text{kg}$.
3. She should keep her mass below about 64 kg.
:::

Health risks of obesity and of too much fat, sugar and salt:

- **coronary heart disease** and **stroke**: too much saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which helps fatty deposits build up in the arteries;
- **high blood pressure**, made worse by a diet high in salt;
- **type 2 diabetes**, more likely in people who are overweight;
- painful joints, especially knees and hips, which carry the extra mass;
- tooth decay from frequent sugary food and drinks;
- breathlessness and reduced fitness, and sometimes low self-esteem.

Obesity is prevented by a balanced diet that matches energy intake to energy use (fewer fried foods and sugary drinks, more vegetables, fruit and fibre) and by regular exercise.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking malnutrition only means not enough food"}
Over-nutrition, such as obesity, is also malnutrition: the diet is unbalanced.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a child with kwashiorkor is well fed"}
The child may look plump, but the swelling is fluid (oedema), not fat. Muscles are wasting underneath.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up kwashiorkor and marasmus"}
Kwashiorkor: lack of **protein**, with oedema. Marasmus: lack of **energy and protein**, severe wasting and no oedema.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying only fatty food makes people obese"}
Any food eaten in excess of energy needs, including sugar and starch, can be converted to fat and stored.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When you **describe** the symptoms of a condition, list what can be seen ("swollen legs, peeling skin"). When you **explain** the cause, name the missing nutrient and say why it matters ("too little protein for growth and to make antibodies").
:::

:::exam-tip
"Compare kwashiorkor and marasmus" needs points about **both** conditions in each sentence: "Kwashiorkor causes oedema, **whereas** marasmus does not."
:::

:::exam-tip
In a BMI calculation, square the height in metres **before** dividing, and give the category when the question asks what the value shows.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Malnutrition is any unbalanced diet: under-nutrition (too little) or over-nutrition (too much).
- Kwashiorkor is caused by a lack of protein and shows oedema, a swollen abdomen and changes to skin and hair.
- Marasmus is caused by a lack of energy and protein and shows severe wasting without oedema.
- Lack of vitamin A causes night blindness, vitamin C scurvy, vitamin D or calcium rickets, iron anaemia and iodine goitre.
- Too much energy-rich food leads to obesity, which raises the risk of heart disease, high blood pressure and type 2 diabetes.
- $\text{BMI} = \dfrac{\text{mass in kg}}{(\text{height in m})^2}$; an adult BMI of 30 or more counts as obese.
:::
