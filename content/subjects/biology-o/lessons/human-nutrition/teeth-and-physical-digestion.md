## Introduction

Bite into a cob of roasted maize and your front teeth slice off the grains; your back teeth then crush them into a paste. Nothing about the chemicals in the maize has changed yet, but you have made the next stage of digestion much faster.

Breaking food into smaller pieces is **physical digestion**. This lesson covers the teeth that do most of it, the stomach and bile that continue it, and (for ZIMSEC) why teeth decay and how to look after them. You should already know that enzymes digest large food molecules into small ones.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Physical (mechanical) digestion"}
The breakdown of food into smaller pieces without any chemical change to the food molecules.
:::

:::definition{term="Surface area"}
The total area of the outside of the food pieces: the area that enzymes can act on.
:::

:::definition{term="Emulsification (Supplement)"}
The breaking up of large drops of fat into many tiny droplets, as bile does in the small intestine.
:::

:::definition{term="Plaque (ZIMSEC)"}
A sticky layer of bacteria, saliva and food remains that forms on the surface of the teeth.
:::

## Why physical digestion matters

Enzymes can only work on the **surface** of a piece of food. Cutting the food into smaller pieces exposes far more surface, so enzymes digest it faster. Smaller pieces are also easier to swallow.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: how cutting increases surface area]
A cube of food has sides of 2 cm. It is cut into cubes with sides of 1 cm, then into cubes with sides of 0.5 cm. Find the total surface area each time.
1. One 2 cm cube: $6 \times (2 \times 2) = 24\ \text{cm}^2$.
2. Cut into 1 cm cubes: there are $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ cubes, each with area $6 \times 1 = 6\ \text{cm}^2$, so the total is $8 \times 6 = 48\ \text{cm}^2$.
3. Cut into 0.5 cm cubes: there are $4 \times 4 \times 4 = 64$ cubes, each $6 \times 0.25 = 1.5\ \text{cm}^2$, so the total is $64 \times 1.5 = 96\ \text{cm}^2$.
4. The mass of food is the same, but every halving of the side doubles the surface area for enzymes.
:::

## Types of teeth

| Type | Position | Shape | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Incisors | Front | Chisel-shaped with a sharp, straight edge | Biting and cutting |
| Canines | Beside the incisors | Pointed | Gripping and tearing |
| Premolars | Behind the canines | Broad, usually with two cusps (points) | Crushing and grinding |
| Molars | At the back | Broad, with four or five cusps | Crushing and grinding |

An adult has **32** permanent teeth. Each quarter of the mouth holds 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars and 3 molars, so the full set is 8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars and 12 molars (including the four "wisdom teeth").

:::worked-solution[Example 2: counting the teeth]
How many grinding teeth (premolars and molars) does an adult have, and what fraction of the set is this?
1. In one quarter of the mouth: 2 premolars + 3 molars = 5 grinding teeth.
2. Four quarters: $4 \times 5 = 20$ grinding teeth.
3. Fraction of the set: $\dfrac{20}{32} = \dfrac{5}{8}$.
:::

A young child's first set, the **milk teeth**, has 20 teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines and 8 molars. There are **no premolars** in the milk set.

## The structure of a tooth

Imagine a molar cut in half from top to bottom. From the outside inwards, and from top to bottom, you would see:

- **Crown** (ZIMSEC term): the part above the gum.
- **Neck** (ZIMSEC term): the narrow part at the level of the gum.
- **Root**: the part fixed in a socket in the jaw bone. Molars have two or three roots; incisors have one.
- **Enamel**: the hard white layer covering the crown. It is the hardest substance in the body, made mainly of calcium salts. It contains no living cells, so a hole in it cannot heal.
- **Dentine**: under the enamel, forming most of the tooth. It is hard and bone-like but living, with fine channels running through it.
- **Pulp cavity**: the soft centre, containing **nerves** and **blood vessels**. The blood brings oxygen and nutrients to the living cells; the nerves detect pain, pressure and temperature.
- **Cement**: a thin bone-like layer covering the root. Fibres embedded in it hold the tooth firmly in its socket.
- **Gum**: the soft tissue covering the jaw bone around the neck of the tooth.

## The stomach and bile

**The stomach** has thick muscular walls. Their contractions churn the food and mix it with gastric juice, breaking it into smaller pieces and producing a creamy liquid. This is physical digestion too: more surface area, and better mixing with enzymes.

**Bile (Supplement)**, made by the liver and released into the duodenum, **emulsifies** fats and oils. It breaks large fat globules into many tiny droplets. No chemical bonds are broken, so this is physical digestion. The droplets have a much larger total surface area, so the enzyme **lipase** can digest the fat much faster. Bile itself contains no enzymes.

:::keyfact{title="Physical digestion never changes the molecules"}
Chewing, churning and emulsifying only make pieces or droplets smaller. Enzymes do the chemical digestion.
:::

## Tooth decay and caring for teeth (ZIMSEC)

**How decay happens:**

1. Bacteria in plaque feed on sugars left on the teeth.
2. As they respire, they produce **acid**.
3. The acid dissolves the enamel, making a small hole (a cavity).
4. Decay spreads faster in the softer dentine.
5. When it reaches the pulp cavity, the nerves are affected (toothache) and bacteria may infect the pulp, causing an abscess. The tooth may be lost.

Each time sugar is eaten, the acid attack lasts for some time afterwards, so **how often** you eat sugary food matters as much as how much you eat. Plaque left along the gum line also causes **gum disease**: the gums become red, swollen and bleed easily, and eventually the teeth can loosen.

:::example{title="Two ways to drink the same soft drink"}
Farai drinks a can of fizzy drink with his lunch. Nyasha sips the same amount slowly through the whole afternoon. Both take in the same mass of sugar, but Nyasha's teeth are under acid attack for hours, while Farai's are attacked only once. Nyasha's teeth are more likely to decay.
:::

**Caring for teeth:**

- Brush at least twice a day, especially before bed, to remove plaque.
- Use a **fluoride** toothpaste: fluoride makes enamel more resistant to acid.
- Clean between the teeth with dental floss.
- Cut down sugary foods and drinks, especially between meals.
- Eat foods rich in calcium and vitamin D for strong teeth.
- Visit a dentist for regular check-ups, so small cavities are treated early.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying chewing breaks food into smaller molecules"}
Chewing makes smaller **pieces**. The molecules are unchanged until enzymes act on them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying bile digests fat"}
Bile emulsifies fat and contains no enzymes. Lipase digests fat into fatty acids and glycerol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sugar dissolves teeth"}
Bacteria in plaque turn sugar into **acid**; it is the acid that dissolves the enamel.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving children premolars"}
The milk set has incisors, canines and molars, but no premolars.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To **explain** how physical digestion helps chemical digestion, use the phrase "**increases the surface area** for enzymes to act on, so chemical digestion is faster".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **describe** tooth structure, name each layer and say where it is ("dentine lies beneath the enamel and forms most of the tooth").
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC: explain decay as a sequence: bacteria → sugar → acid → enamel dissolved → dentine → pulp.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Physical digestion breaks food into smaller pieces without changing the molecules, increasing the surface area for enzymes.
- Incisors bite and cut, canines grip and tear, premolars and molars crush and grind; adults have 32 teeth.
- A tooth has enamel over dentine, a pulp cavity with nerves and blood vessels, and cement holding the root in the jaw bone.
- The stomach churns food; (Supplement) bile emulsifies fats into tiny droplets so lipase works faster.
- ZIMSEC: bacteria in plaque turn sugar into acid, which dissolves enamel; brushing, fluoride, less sugar and dental visits prevent decay.
:::
