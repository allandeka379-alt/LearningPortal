## Introduction

Every allele that exists today, from the allele for polled cattle to the alleles for the ABO blood groups, first appeared as a **mutation**: a change in the DNA of one individual, long ago. Mutations are the original source of all genetic variation.

Most mutations happen by chance, when DNA is copied before a cell divides. Some things make mutations more likely. This is why a radiographer at a hospital stands behind a screen or wears a lead apron while taking X-rays, and why smoking damages far more than the lungs. You should already know that a gene is a length of DNA coding for a protein, and the meanings of dominant, recessive and heterozygous.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mutation"}
A genetic change. Mutation is the way in which new alleles are formed.
:::

:::definition{term="Gene mutation (Supplement)"}
A random change in the base sequence of DNA.
:::

:::definition{term="Chromosome mutation (ZIMSEC)"}
A change in the number or the structure of the chromosomes, such as an extra chromosome.
:::

:::definition{term="Mutagen"}
Anything that increases the rate of mutation, such as ionising radiation or certain chemicals. Many mutagens can also cause cancer; anything that causes cancer is called a **carcinogen**.
:::

## What mutations do

A change in the base sequence of a gene can change the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for, and so the shape of the protein. The result is a **new allele**.

- **Most** mutations are harmful or have no noticeable effect. A changed enzyme, for example, may no longer fit its substrate.
- **A few** mutations are useful. A bacterium with a mutation that lets it survive an antibiotic has an advantage when that antibiotic is used.

Where the mutation happens matters:

- A mutation in a **body cell**, such as a skin cell, affects only that cell and the cells it produces. It is not passed to children, but it can sometimes lead to cancer.
- A mutation in a cell that makes **gametes** can be passed on to the next generation.

## What increases the rate of mutation?

Mutations happen naturally and at random, but the rate is increased by:

- **Ionising radiation**: X-rays, gamma rays and the radiation from radioactive substances. Hospital staff limit their exposure with lead aprons and screens, and patients are given X-rays only when needed.
- **Some chemicals**: for example, many chemicals in tobacco smoke, and aflatoxins, poisons made by moulds that grow on badly stored maize and groundnuts. Drying and storing grain properly reduces the risk.
- **Ultraviolet light** from the Sun also damages the DNA of skin cells and can cause skin cancer.

## Gene mutations

### Sickle-cell anaemia

A change in **one base** of the gene for **haemoglobin** produces the sickle-cell allele. The changed DNA codes for haemoglobin with one different amino acid. This abnormal haemoglobin forms long fibres when the oxygen concentration is low, pulling red blood cells into a stiff, curved **sickle** shape.

Sickle cells:

- carry less oxygen;
- get stuck in narrow capillaries, blocking blood flow and causing severe pain;
- are destroyed quickly, so the person becomes **anaemic** (short of red blood cells), tired and breathless.

A person with **two** sickle-cell alleles has sickle-cell anaemia. A person with **one** sickle-cell allele and one normal allele is said to have the **sickle-cell trait**; they are usually healthy, and they are less likely to become seriously ill with malaria. That protection helps to explain why the allele is more common in places where malaria has been common for a long time.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: two parents with the sickle-cell trait]
Use $\text{Hb}^\text{A}$ for the normal allele and $\text{Hb}^\text{S}$ for the sickle-cell allele. Both parents are $\text{Hb}^\text{A}\text{Hb}^\text{S}$.
1. Gametes from each parent: $\text{Hb}^\text{A}$ or $\text{Hb}^\text{S}$.
2. Offspring: 1 $\text{Hb}^\text{A}\text{Hb}^\text{A}$ : 2 $\text{Hb}^\text{A}\text{Hb}^\text{S}$ : 1 $\text{Hb}^\text{S}\text{Hb}^\text{S}$.
3. Probability that a child has sickle-cell anaemia ($\text{Hb}^\text{S}\text{Hb}^\text{S}$) $= \frac{1}{4}$.
4. Probability that a child has the sickle-cell trait $= \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.
5. Probability that a child has no sickle-cell allele $= \frac{1}{4}$.
:::

### Albinism

A mutation in one of the genes needed to make **melanin** produced a recessive allele that codes for a protein that does not work. A person with two copies of this allele makes little or no melanin. People with albinism have very pale skin, light hair and eyes that are sensitive to bright light, and many have poor eyesight.

Melanin normally absorbs ultraviolet light, so people with albinism burn easily and have a high risk of **skin cancer**. Hats, long sleeves, sunscreen and sunglasses protect them. Albinism is inherited; it is not caused by anything the parents did and it cannot be caught. People with albinism have the same abilities as anyone else, and they deserve the same respect.

## A chromosome mutation: Down syndrome (ZIMSEC)

Sometimes the two chromosomes of a pair fail to separate when gametes are made by meiosis. An egg (or, less often, a sperm) then carries an **extra copy of chromosome 21**.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: counting the chromosomes]
1. A normal egg has 23 chromosomes; this egg has 24, including two copies of chromosome 21.
2. It is fertilised by a normal sperm with 23 chromosomes.
3. The zygote has $24 + 23 = 47$ chromosomes, with three copies of chromosome 21.
4. The zygote divides by mitosis, which copies every chromosome, so every body cell of the child has 47 chromosomes.
:::

The extra chromosome causes **Down syndrome**. People with Down syndrome have some degree of learning difficulty and characteristic facial features, and some have heart problems. With support, they learn, work and take part fully in family and community life. The chance of the condition increases with the **age of the mother**.

## Sources of genetic variation (Supplement)

Genetic variation in a population comes from four sources:

1. **Mutation**: the only source of **new** alleles.
2. **Meiosis**: each gamete receives a different mixture of the parent's chromosomes and alleles.
3. **Random mating**: which individuals pair up is a matter of chance.
4. **Random fertilisation**: which of the many gametes fuse is also a matter of chance.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying all mutations are harmful"}
Most are harmful or neutral, but some are useful, such as mutations giving bacteria resistance to an antibiotic.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking mutations need radiation or chemicals"}
Mutations happen naturally and at random. Radiation and some chemicals only **increase the rate**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking every mutation is inherited"}
Only mutations in gametes, or in the cells that make them, can be passed to children.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing sickle-cell anaemia with iron-deficiency anaemia"}
Sickle-cell anaemia is inherited and caused by abnormal haemoglobin. Iron-deficiency anaemia is caused by a lack of iron in the diet and is not inherited.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Define a gene mutation precisely: "a **random** change in the **base sequence** of DNA". Name factors that raise the rate as **ionising radiation** (X-rays, gamma rays) and **chemicals** (for example in tobacco smoke).
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain sickle-cell anaemia, follow the chain: base change → different amino acid → abnormal haemoglobin → sickle-shaped red cells → blocked capillaries and less oxygen carried.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use respectful language: "a person with albinism", "people with Down syndrome".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mutation is genetic change and the way new alleles form; most mutations are harmful or neutral, a few are useful.
- Ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays) and some chemicals, such as those in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
- Sickle-cell anaemia: a single base change in the haemoglobin gene gives abnormal haemoglobin and sickle-shaped red cells; the sickle-cell trait gives some protection against malaria.
- Albinism: a recessive allele means little or no melanin is made.
- ZIMSEC: Down syndrome is a chromosome mutation, an extra chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes).
- Supplement: genetic variation comes from mutation, meiosis, random mating and random fertilisation.
:::
