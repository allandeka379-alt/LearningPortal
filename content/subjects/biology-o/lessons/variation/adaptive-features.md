## Introduction

At the end of the dry season in Hwange National Park, the temperature can pass 35 °C, the pans have shrunk to mud, and many trees stand bare. Yet elephants, baobabs and mopane trees thrive there year after year. On Lake Kariba, water lilies and water hyacinth float in the opposite conditions: more water than they could ever need.

Each species has features that suit it to where it lives. These **adaptive features** are inherited: they are passed from parents to offspring in the genes. In the next lesson you will see how natural selection produces them. Here you will learn to spot and explain them. You should already know how leaves lose water by transpiration through their stomata.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Adaptive feature"}
An inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Xerophyte (Supplement)"}
A plant adapted to live in dry conditions, where water is scarce.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrophyte (Supplement)"}
A plant adapted to live in water or in very wet conditions.
:::

## Interpreting adaptive features

To explain any adaptive feature, work in three steps:

1. **What is the problem** in this environment? (Heat, drought, predators, finding food, cold, currents.)
2. **What is the feature**? (Describe the structure or behaviour precisely.)
3. **How does the feature help** the organism survive or reproduce? (Link the structure to its function.)

:::example{title="Example 1: animals of the savanna"}
- **African elephant**: very large, thin ears with many blood vessels close to the surface. In hot weather the elephant flaps them, and the blood flowing through them loses heat to the air, helping it keep cool.
- **Giraffe**: a long neck and legs reach leaves high in acacia trees that other browsers cannot reach, so it faces less competition for food.
- **Impala**: eyes on the sides of the head give a wide field of view for spotting predators; long, powerful legs let it run fast and leap away.
- **Lion**: a sandy-coloured coat gives camouflage in dry grass while it stalks prey; forward-facing eyes help it judge distance when it pounces; sharp canine teeth and claws hold and kill prey.
- **Ground pangolin**: hard, overlapping scales protect it, and it rolls into a tight ball when threatened; strong curved claws break open ant and termite nests, and a long sticky tongue collects the insects.
:::

## Xerophytes: plants of dry places (Supplement)

A plant in a dry place must reduce water loss by transpiration, and may store water when it can get it.

| Adaptive feature | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Thick, waxy cuticle | Waterproof layer: less water evaporates from the leaf surface |
| Few stomata, mostly on the lower surface | Fewer openings for water vapour to diffuse out |
| Stomata sunk in pits, or surrounded by hairs | Moist air is trapped near the stomata, so the water vapour gradient is smaller and less water diffuses out |
| Rolled leaves (in some grasses) | Stomata face inwards into trapped, humid air |
| Small leaves, or leaves reduced to spines | Smaller surface area for transpiration |
| Succulent (fleshy) tissues | Water is stored after rain and used in the dry season |
| Deep roots, or wide shallow roots | Reach water deep underground, or absorb rain quickly before it evaporates |
| Shedding leaves in the dry season | Almost no transpiration while water is scarce |

:::example{title="Example 2: xerophytes of Zimbabwe"}
- **Baobab** (in the Zambezi Valley and the Lowveld): its huge, spongy trunk stores water, and it sheds its leaves for the long dry season.
- **Aloes** (on rocky kopjes): thick, fleshy leaves store water and have a waxy cuticle; their spiny edges also deter animals from eating these precious water stores.
- **Mopane** (in hot, low-lying areas): each leaf has two leaflets shaped like butterfly wings. In strong sun the leaflets fold together and turn their edges towards the Sun, so a smaller area is heated and less water is lost.
- **Euphorbias** such as the candelabra tree: tiny leaves that soon fall, and thick green stems that store water and carry out photosynthesis.
:::

Xerophytes cannot simply close all their stomata for good: they need carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, which enters through the stomata. Their features reduce water loss while still allowing some gas exchange.

## Hydrophytes: plants of wet places (Supplement)

A plant living in water has plenty of water but other problems: getting enough light and oxygen, staying afloat, and not being torn by moving water.

| Adaptive feature | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Large air spaces in leaves, stems and leaf stalks | Make the plant float, keeping leaves at the surface in the light; store and carry oxygen to submerged parts |
| Stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves | The upper surface is in contact with the air, so gases can be exchanged; the lower surface touches the water |
| Thin cuticle, or none on submerged parts | Water loss is not a problem; submerged parts can absorb gases and ions directly |
| Little supporting (woody) tissue | The water supports the plant |
| Long, flexible stems and leaf stalks; finely divided underwater leaves | Bend with currents and changing water levels without breaking |

:::example{title="Example 3: water lily and water hyacinth"}
- **Water lily** (in vleis, pans and slow rivers): large, flat, floating leaves catch plenty of light; stomata are on the upper surface; long, flexible leaf stalks with air channels reach down to the roots in the mud.
- **Water hyacinth** (an invasive weed on Lake Chivero and other dams): swollen, spongy leaf stalks are full of air and work as floats, so the plant drifts across the surface and forms thick mats.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying an organism develops a feature because it needs it"}
A giraffe does not grow a long neck by stretching. Adaptive features are **inherited**; they become common in a population through natural selection over many generations.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a feature without explaining it"}
"Thick cuticle" alone may earn nothing. Add how it helps: "so less water evaporates from the leaf".
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving hydrophytes a thick cuticle"}
A thick, waxy cuticle saves water, which a plant living in water does not need. Hydrophytes have a thin cuticle, or none on submerged parts.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When given a picture or description of an unfamiliar organism, list the features and link each one to a problem of its environment: "feature → how it works → how it helps survival".
:::

:::exam-tip
For xerophytes, use the language of diffusion: features "trap moist air", "reduce the water vapour concentration gradient" or "reduce the surface area for transpiration".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain" questions usually give 1 mark for the feature and 1 for its function. Make every feature earn both.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An adaptive feature is an inherited feature that helps an organism to survive and reproduce in its environment.
- Explain each feature by linking it to a problem of the environment: elephants' ears lose heat, impalas' side-facing eyes spot predators.
- Supplement: xerophytes reduce water loss (thick waxy cuticle, few or sunken stomata, hairs, rolled or small leaves, leaf fall) and store water (baobab trunk, aloe leaves).
- Supplement: hydrophytes have air spaces for floating, stomata on the upper surface of floating leaves, thin cuticles and little support tissue.
- Adaptive features are inherited, not developed by an individual during its life.
:::
