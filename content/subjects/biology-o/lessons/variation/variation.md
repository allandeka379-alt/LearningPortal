## Introduction

Look around your classroom. Your classmates differ in height, mass, hand span and skin tone; some can roll their tongue and some cannot; they have different blood groups. In a field of maize, no two cobs are exactly the same length, and in a herd of cattle the animals differ in size, coat colour and milk yield. These differences are called **variation**.

Variation matters. Plant and animal breeders choose the best individuals from it, and in nature it is the raw material for natural selection. In this lesson you will learn the two kinds of variation, what causes them, and how to measure and graph them. You should already know what genes, alleles and phenotypes are.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Variation"}
The differences between individuals of the same species.
:::

:::definition{term="Continuous variation"}
Variation that results in a **range of phenotypes between two extremes**, with many intermediates. Examples: height, body mass, leaf length, length of maize cobs.
:::

:::definition{term="Discontinuous variation"}
Variation that results in a **limited number of distinct phenotypes with no intermediates**. Examples: ABO blood groups, sex, the ability to roll the tongue, round or wrinkled pea seeds.
:::

## Continuous variation

Measure the height of every student in a school and you will not find a few separate heights. Instead you find a smooth range from the shortest to the tallest, with most people near the middle and few at the extremes. Plotted as a histogram, the results usually form a **bell-shaped curve**, called a normal distribution.

Continuous variation is usually caused by:

- **many genes**, each adding a small amount to the feature; and
- **the environment**: for a plant, the light, water, temperature and mineral ions it receives; for an animal or a person, diet, exercise and disease.

Two maize plants with exactly the same genes will grow to different heights if one is given fertiliser and the other is not. Identical twins have the same genes, yet one may become heavier than the other because of a different diet or amount of exercise.

## Discontinuous variation

Some features fall into a few clear groups. A person has blood group A, B, AB or O, and there is nothing in between. A person is either able to roll their tongue or not. A pea seed is round or wrinkled.

Discontinuous variation is usually caused by **genes alone**, often a single gene with a few alleles. The environment has little or no effect: no diet or amount of sunlight can change a person's blood group.

:::keyfact{title="Comparing the two kinds"}
| | Continuous | Discontinuous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phenotypes | A range between two extremes | A few distinct categories |
| Intermediates | Many | None |
| Usually caused by | Many genes and the environment | Genes only (often one gene) |
| Graph | Histogram, bars touching | Bar chart, bars separate |
| Examples | Height, mass, leaf length, milk yield | Blood group, sex, tongue rolling, seed shape |
:::

## Investigating variation

### Continuous data: a histogram

To investigate a feature showing continuous variation, measure it in a large sample, such as 30 or more individuals chosen at random. Record the measurements, sort them into **equal class intervals** that do not overlap, count the number in each class, and draw a **histogram**. The class intervals go on the horizontal axis and the frequency (number of individuals) on the vertical axis. The bars **touch**, because the classes run on from one another.

![Histogram of 20 bean pod lengths in 2 cm classes: frequencies 2, 4, 7, 5 and 2 with touching bars, peaking in the 13.0 to 14.9 cm modal class and falling on both sides, which shows continuous variation.](media:diagrams/bean-pod-histogram.png "Figure: a histogram of bean pod lengths shows continuous variation")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: lengths of bean pods]
A student in Mutare measured 20 bean pods from one variety, to the nearest 0.1 cm: 9.2, 10.5, 11.8, 12.1, 12.4, 12.9, 13.3, 13.5, 13.8, 14.0, 14.2, 14.6, 14.9, 15.1, 15.4, 15.8, 16.3, 16.9, 17.5 and 18.6 cm.
1. Range $= 18.6 - 9.2 = 9.4\ \text{cm}$.
2. Mean $= \dfrac{\text{total of lengths}}{\text{number of pods}} = \dfrac{282.8}{20} = 14.14 \approx 14.1\ \text{cm}$.
3. Class intervals of 2 cm: 9.0–10.9, 11.0–12.9, 13.0–14.9, 15.0–16.9 and 17.0–18.9 cm.
4. Frequencies: 2, 4, 7, 5 and 2 pods (check: $2 + 4 + 7 + 5 + 2 = 20$).
5. The histogram rises to a peak in the 13.0–14.9 cm class (the modal class) and falls on both sides: continuous variation.

:::

### Discontinuous data: a bar chart

For a feature with distinct categories, count how many individuals are in each category and draw a **bar chart** with a **gap between the bars**, because there are no values in between.

![Bar chart of tongue rolling in a class of 40 students: 26 (65%) can roll their tongue and 14 (35%) cannot, drawn as two separate bars with a gap because the feature falls into distinct categories with no intermediates, which is discontinuous variation.](media:diagrams/tongue-rolling-bar-chart.png "Figure: a bar chart with a gap between the bars shows discontinuous variation")

:::worked-solution[Example 2: tongue rolling]
In a class of 40 students, 26 could roll their tongue and 14 could not.
1. Percentage who could roll $= \dfrac{26}{40} \times 100 = 65\%$.
2. Percentage who could not $= \dfrac{14}{40} \times 100 = 35\%$.
3. The data fall into two categories with no intermediates, so this is discontinuous variation, shown as a bar chart with two separate bars.

:::

## Genes, environment and phenotype

For continuous features, the phenotype depends on both the genotype and the environment. A maize variety bred for long cobs still produces short cobs in a drought. Selective breeding can change the genes, but good farming (water, fertiliser, weeding) is needed for the genes to show their full effect. Features caused only by the environment, such as a scar or a language a person speaks, are not inherited.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing a histogram for discontinuous data"}
Blood groups, sex and tongue rolling are categories, so use a bar chart with separate bars. Use a histogram, with touching bars, only for continuous measurements.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying continuous variation is caused by the environment only"}
Continuous variation is caused by **many genes and the environment**. Tall parents tend to have tall children, but diet also affects height.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overlapping class intervals"}
Classes such as 10–12 cm and 12–14 cm overlap: where does 12 cm go? Use 10.0–11.9 and 12.0–13.9 cm, all of equal width.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using too small a sample"}
Five measurements cannot show the shape of the variation. Measure a large number of individuals, chosen at random, so the results are representative.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **identify** the type of variation, give a reason: "continuous, because there is a range of lengths with intermediates" or "discontinuous, because there are only four distinct groups".
:::

:::exam-tip
When plotting a histogram: label both axes with units, use equal class widths, make the bars touch and do not leave out empty classes.
:::

:::exam-tip
Show the working for a mean (total ÷ number) and a range (largest − smallest), and give units. Round only at the end.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species.
- Continuous variation gives a range of phenotypes between two extremes; it is caused by many genes and the environment (height, mass, leaf length).
- Discontinuous variation gives a few distinct phenotypes with no intermediates; it is usually caused by genes only (blood group, sex, tongue rolling).
- Continuous data are shown on a histogram with touching bars; discontinuous data on a bar chart with separate bars.
- Investigate variation by measuring a large random sample, grouping it into equal, non-overlapping classes, and calculating the mean and range.
:::
