## Introduction

Every year the Ministry of Agriculture estimates Zimbabwe's maize harvest, ZIMSTAT reports on the population, and schools analyse examination results. Each of these starts with **data**: facts collected so that a question can be answered. Statistics is the science of collecting, organising, presenting, analysing and interpreting data.

Before you can draw a chart or calculate a mean, you must know what kind of data you have and where it came from, because these decide which methods are valid. ZIMSEC Paper 2 Probability and Statistics tests these definitions directly, often as the first part of a longer question.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Statistics"}
The collection, organisation, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical and other information. "Statistics" can also mean the numbers themselves, such as the mean and range.
:::

:::definition{term="Data"}
Facts or observations collected for a purpose. A single item is a datum or an observation.
:::

:::definition{term="Variable"}
A characteristic that can take different values from one member of a group to another, such as height, mode of transport or number of siblings.
:::

:::definition{term="Frequency"}
The number of times a value, or a value in a class, occurs.
:::

:::definition{term="Tally"}
A quick way of counting: one stroke per observation, with every fifth stroke drawn across the previous four to make a "gate" of five.
:::

:::definition{term="Population"}
The whole set of people or items about which information is wanted, for example all Form 4 learners in Bulawayo.
:::

:::definition{term="Sample"}
Part of a population, selected so that information about the population can be obtained from it.
:::

:::definition{term="Census"}
A survey that collects data from every member of the population.
:::

## Sources of data

**Primary data** are collected first-hand by the person or organisation that will use them, for a particular purpose: a questionnaire you give to your class, a count of kombis passing a corner, measurements from an experiment.

**Secondary data** were collected by someone else, often for another purpose, and are reused: ZIMSTAT census reports, school registers, Meteorological Services rainfall records, newspapers and websites.

| | Primary data | Secondary data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Merits | collected exactly for your purpose; you know how they were collected; up to date | cheap and quick to obtain; often large data sets; available for past years |
| Demerits | time-consuming and costly to collect; may need many helpers | may not fit your question exactly; may be out of date; collection method and errors unknown |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: say whether each source gives primary or secondary data.]
1. Tatenda counts the number of cars passing her school gate between 07:00 and 08:00: **primary** (she collects it herself, for her purpose).
2. A farmer uses the district's rainfall records for the last ten years from the Meteorological Services Department: **secondary**.
3. Rudo hands out a questionnaire on study habits to her class: **primary**.
4. A student uses the school's examination results from last year's records: **secondary**.
:::

## Census or sample?

A census gives complete information but is expensive and slow; Zimbabwe holds a national population census only about once every ten years. A sample is cheaper and quicker, and it is the only choice when testing destroys the item (for example testing how long light bulbs last) or when the population is very large. A sample must be chosen carefully so that it represents the population.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: should each investigation use a census or a sample?]
1. The lifetime of the bulbs in a batch from a factory: **sample**, because testing a bulb until it fails destroys it.
2. The number of learners in one class of 35 who own a bicycle: **census**, as it is small and quick to ask everyone.
3. The average yield of all maize farms in Mashonaland West: **sample**, as the population is large and spread out.
:::

## Types of data

**Qualitative (categorical) data** describe a quality and are not numbers: colour, home language, mode of travel, favourite sport.

**Quantitative (numerical) data** are numbers obtained by counting or measuring. They split into two kinds:

- **Discrete** data can take only particular separate values, usually whole numbers obtained by **counting**: number of siblings, goals scored, cattle in a herd, shoe size.
- **Continuous** data can take any value in a range and are obtained by **measuring**: height, mass, time, temperature. They are always rounded when recorded, so 1.62 m means a height between 1.615 m and 1.625 m.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: a survey of Form 4 learners in Gweru records the items below. Classify each.]
1. Home language: **qualitative**.
2. Number of brothers and sisters: **quantitative, discrete** (counted).
3. Height in centimetres: **quantitative, continuous** (measured).
4. Shoe size: **quantitative, discrete** (only sizes such as 6, 6½, 7 exist).
5. Time taken to travel to school: **quantitative, continuous**.
6. Mark in a test out of 50: **quantitative, discrete** (whole marks only).
:::

## Tallies and frequencies

When data are collected, a tally chart counts them quickly and then gives a frequency table.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: thirty learners were asked how they travel to school. The tally chart showed three gates of five and three more strokes for walking, one gate for kombi, four strokes for bus and three strokes for car. Write the frequency table.]
1. Walking: $3 \times 5 + 3 = 18$.
2. Kombi: $5$. Bus: $4$. Car: $3$.
3. Check: $18 + 5 + 4 + 3 = 30$, the number of learners asked.
:::

| Mode of travel | Frequency |
| --- | --- |
| Walk | 18 |
| Kombi | 5 |
| Bus | 4 |
| Car | 3 |
| Total | 30 |

These data are qualitative, so a bar chart or pie chart suits them; a histogram does not.

Statistics is examined in ZIMSEC Paper 2 Section C only; Cambridge 0606 has no statistics.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Numbers are not always quantitative"}
A telephone number or a learner's index number is a label, so it is qualitative even though it is written in digits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Age as discrete"}
Age is continuous (time since birth), even though we usually record it in completed years.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sample and population mixed up"}
The population is everyone you want to know about; the sample is the part you actually ask. Results from a sample are estimates for the population.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to "state the type of data", give both levels: for example "quantitative, continuous".
:::

:::exam-tip
For merits and demerits, give a reason linked to the situation in the question, not a general list.
:::

:::exam-tip
Check a frequency table by adding the frequencies: the total must equal the number of observations.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A population is the whole group; a sample is part of it; a census collects from everyone.
- Primary data are collected first-hand; secondary data come from existing records.
- Qualitative data are categories; quantitative data are numbers.
- Discrete data are counted (separate values); continuous data are measured (any value in a range).
- Tallies count observations in fives, and the frequencies must add up to the total.
:::
