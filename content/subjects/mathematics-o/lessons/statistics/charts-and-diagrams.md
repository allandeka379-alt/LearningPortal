## Introduction

A district education office wants to show parents how learners travel to school, how the numbers of boys and girls compare in each form, and how marks are spread out. A table of numbers is accurate but slow to read; a well-chosen diagram shows the main message at a glance.

In this lesson you will draw and read the diagrams used in O-Level and IGCSE examinations: pictograms, bar charts, dual and composite bar charts, pie charts and stem-and-leaf diagrams. In examinations you draw them on graph paper; here each diagram is described by its table of values.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pictogram"}
A diagram that uses repeated symbols to show frequencies. A **key** states how many items one symbol represents; parts of a symbol show fractions of that number.
:::

:::definition{term="Bar chart"}
A diagram of separate bars of equal width, with gaps between them, whose heights (or lengths) show frequencies. The axes are labelled and the frequency axis starts at zero.
:::

:::definition{term="Dual and composite bar charts"}
A **dual** bar chart puts two bars side by side for each category to compare two groups. A **composite** (stacked) bar chart divides each bar into parts, so it shows both the total and how it is made up.
:::

:::definition{term="Pie chart"}
A circle divided into sectors. Each sector angle is proportional to the frequency it represents, and the angles add up to $360^\circ$.
:::

:::definition{term="Stem-and-leaf diagram"}
A diagram that splits each value into a **stem** (the leading digits) and a **leaf** (the last digit). The leaves are written in order, and a **key** explains the values, for example $3 \mid 7$ means 37.
:::

:::formula{name="Pie chart angles"}
$$
\text{sector angle} = \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total frequency}} \times 360^\circ
$$

and in reverse, frequency $= \dfrac{\text{angle}}{360^\circ} \times$ total.
:::

## Pictograms and bar charts

In a pictogram where one symbol represents 8 learners, half a symbol represents 4 learners and a quarter symbol represents 2. All symbols must be the same size.

A bar chart for categories (such as favourite subjects) has gaps between the bars. A **dual** bar chart is useful when, for example, you want to compare the favourite sports of boys and girls category by category. A **composite** bar chart is useful when you want to see the total number of learners in each form and how it splits into boys and girls.

## Pie charts

:::worked-solution[Example 1: drawing a pie chart]
60 farmers in a ward were asked which crop gives them the most income. The results were maize 27, tobacco 15, cotton 10 and groundnuts 8.

1. Each farmer is represented by $360^\circ \div 60 = 6^\circ$.
2. Maize: $27 \times 6^\circ = 162^\circ$. Tobacco: $15 \times 6^\circ = 90^\circ$.
3. Cotton: $10 \times 6^\circ = 60^\circ$. Groundnuts: $8 \times 6^\circ = 48^\circ$.
4. Check: $162 + 90 + 60 + 48 = 360$. Draw each sector with a protractor, starting from a radius.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: reading a pie chart]
In a pie chart about transport to work, the sector for "kombi" has an angle of $72^\circ$ and represents 30 people. How many people were surveyed?

1. $72^\circ$ is $\dfrac{72}{360} = \dfrac{1}{5}$ of the circle.
2. So 30 people are $\frac{1}{5}$ of the total.
3. Total $= 30 \times 5 = 150$ people.
:::

A pie chart shows **proportions**, not actual numbers. Two pie charts with sectors of the same angle can represent very different numbers of people if the totals are different.

## Stem-and-leaf diagrams

:::worked-solution[Example 3: an ordered stem-and-leaf diagram]
The masses, in kg, of 15 learners are:

52, 45, 61, 38, 55, 70, 47, 52, 63, 42, 58, 67, 51, 72, 60

1. The stems are the tens digits 3, 4, 5, 6 and 7.
2. Write each leaf (units digit) against its stem, then put the leaves in order:

| Stem | Leaves |
| :---: | --- |
| 3 | 8 |
| 4 | 2 5 7 |
| 5 | 1 2 2 5 8 |
| 6 | 0 1 3 7 |
| 7 | 0 2 |

Key: $5 \mid 1$ means 51 kg.

3. Check: there are $1 + 3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 15$ leaves.
:::

A stem-and-leaf diagram shows the shape of the data like a bar chart, but it keeps every individual value, so you can still find the median and the range from it. Here the median is the 8th value, 55 kg, and the range is $72 - 38 = 34$ kg.

## Choosing a diagram

| Purpose | Suitable diagram |
| --- | --- |
| Show how a whole is shared out | pie chart |
| Compare frequencies of categories | bar chart or pictogram |
| Compare two groups category by category | dual bar chart |
| Show totals and what they are made of | composite bar chart |
| Show the shape of numerical data and keep the values | stem-and-leaf diagram |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Pie chart angles that do not add up to 360°"}
Rounding each angle can make the total $359^\circ$ or $361^\circ$. Check the total, and use exact values where possible.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the key"}
A stem-and-leaf diagram or pictogram without a key cannot be read: $3 \mid 7$ could mean 37, 3.7 or 370.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaves out of order"}
An **ordered** stem-and-leaf diagram must have the leaves in increasing order, and each leaf must be a single digit, written in a column so that the rows line up.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing pie charts with different totals"}
A bigger sector does not mean a bigger number unless the totals are the same.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For a pie chart, show the calculation of each angle (for example $15 \times 6^\circ = 90^\circ$). Then draw each angle carefully with a protractor: only a small tolerance is allowed when your sectors are checked.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to draw a bar chart, label both axes, use a linear scale starting at zero, and make all the bars the same width with equal gaps.
:::

:::exam-tip
Always write the key for a stem-and-leaf diagram, using a value from your own diagram, such as "Key: $5 \mid 1$ means 51 kg".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pictograms need a key and same-sized symbols; parts of a symbol show fractions of its value.
- Bar charts have equal-width bars with gaps; dual bar charts compare two groups, composite bar charts show totals and their parts.
- Pie chart angle $= \frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}} \times 360^\circ$; the angles add up to $360^\circ$.
- Ordered stem-and-leaf diagrams keep every value and need a key.
- Choose the diagram that best shows the message of the data.
:::
