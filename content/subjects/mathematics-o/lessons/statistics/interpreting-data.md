## Introduction

Newspapers, school reports, farming bulletins and adverts are full of tables and charts. A headline might say that one school "did better" than another, or that a new fertiliser "increases yields". To judge such claims you need to read the data carefully, compare like with like, and ask whether the data really support the conclusion.

In this lesson you will read information from tables and diagrams, compare two sets of data properly, recognise diagrams that mislead, and state the limits of what a set of data can tell you. You should already know how to find the mean, median, mode and range.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Measure of average"}
A single value that represents the centre of a data set: the **mean**, **median** or **mode**.
:::

:::definition{term="Measure of spread"}
A value that shows how spread out (how consistent) the data are, such as the **range** (largest − smallest) or the **interquartile range**. A smaller spread means more consistent data.
:::

:::definition{term="Representative sample"}
A sample whose members are like the whole group (population) being studied, usually chosen at random and large enough to be reliable.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Comparing two data sets"}
Make **two** comparisons, each in the context of the question:

1. compare an **average** (which set is higher or lower on average?);
2. compare a **spread** (which set is more consistent or more varied?).
:::

## Reading tables

:::worked-solution[Example 1: maize yields]
The table shows maize yields, in tonnes per hectare, on four farms in two seasons.

| Farm | A | B | C | D |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| First season | 2.4 | 3.1 | 1.8 | 2.6 |
| Second season | 3.0 | 2.9 | 2.7 | 2.6 |

1. Changes: A $+0.6$, B $-0.2$, C $+0.9$, D $0$.
2. Farm C had the greatest increase: $0.9$ tonnes per hectare, which is $\frac{0.9}{1.8} \times 100 = 50\%$ of its first-season yield.
3. Farm B is the only farm whose yield fell.
:::

Read the headings and units first, and check whether the question asks for an actual change or a percentage change.

## Comparing two sets of data

:::worked-solution[Example 2: two classes]
The test marks of two groups of ten learners are summarised below.

| | Mean | Range |
| --- | :---: | :---: |
| Class P | 65.8 | 40 |
| Class Q | 57.3 | 18 |

1. Average: on average Class P scored higher than Class Q (mean 65.8 compared with 57.3).
2. Spread: Class Q's marks were more consistent (range 18 compared with 40).
3. A complete answer makes both comparisons in context, for example: "Class P did better on average, but Class Q's marks were less spread out."
:::

Use the median and the interquartile range instead of the mean and range when the data contain extreme values, because they are not distorted by one very high or very low value.

## Misleading diagrams

A diagram can be accurate in its numbers but still give a false impression.

- **A vertical axis that does not start at zero** makes small differences look large. Sales of 92 and 98 look very different if the axis starts at 90.
- **Pictograms with symbols of different sizes**: a symbol drawn twice as tall and twice as wide has four times the area, so it looks like much more than double.
- **Three-dimensional or tilted pie charts** make the sectors at the front look bigger.
- **Missing scales or labels** mean the values cannot be read at all.

:::example{title="A misleading bar chart"}
Shop A sold 98 loaves and Shop B sold 92. On a bar chart whose vertical axis starts at 90, Shop A's bar is 8 units tall and Shop B's bar is 2 units tall, so A appears to have sold four times as many. In fact A sold only $\frac{98 - 92}{92} \times 100 \approx 6.5\%$ more.
:::

## The limits of conclusions

Even correct calculations can lead to wrong conclusions. Before you accept a conclusion, ask:

- **Is the sample large enough?** Results from a handful of people can be very different from the whole population.
- **Is the sample representative?** Asking only one class, one school or one town may not reflect everyone.
- **Does the conclusion go beyond the data?** Data from Form 4 learners in Gweru say little about all learners in Zimbabwe, or about next year.
- **Is a link being mistaken for a cause?** Two things that rise together are not necessarily cause and effect; something else may affect both.
- **Has detail been lost?** Grouped data and averages hide individual values.

:::example{title="How far can the conclusion go?"}
A survey of 30 learners at one school in Harare finds that 20 prefer football. It is fair to say "two-thirds of the learners in this sample preferred football". It is not justified to say "two-thirds of Zimbabwean teenagers prefer football": the sample is small and comes from one school.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Comparing only the averages"}
"Class P is better" is incomplete. Examiners want an average **and** a spread compared, each with numbers or clear reference to the data.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing statistics without context"}
"P has a higher mean" scores less than "on average, the learners in Class P scored higher marks". Say what the numbers mean.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying higher spread is better"}
A larger range means the data are **less** consistent. It does not mean the results are better.
:::

:::mistake{title="Generalising from a small or biased sample"}
Conclusions apply to the group the data came from. Be cautious about extending them to a larger or different group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"**Compare**" questions are usually worth 2 marks: one for an average comparison and one for a spread comparison. Write two separate sentences.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked "**Give a reason** why the conclusion may not be reliable", name a specific problem: the sample size, how the sample was chosen, or that the conclusion goes beyond the data.
:::

:::exam-tip
When reading a diagram, always look at where the vertical axis starts and at the scale before judging the size of a difference.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read headings, units and scales before reading values from a table or diagram.
- Compare data sets with an average and a measure of spread, both in context.
- A smaller spread means more consistent data; a larger spread means more varied data.
- Diagrams can mislead through truncated axes, unequal symbols, 3-D effects or missing scales.
- Conclusions are limited by sample size, how the sample was chosen, and how far they are extended beyond the data.
:::
