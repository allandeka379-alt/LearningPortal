## Introduction

Almost every reaction in a living cell needs an enzyme. Without amylase, the starch in a plate of sadza would take years to break down at body temperature; with it, digestion starts in your mouth within seconds. Enzymes are also big business: they are used to brew beer from sorghum, to make biological washing powders and to test for glucose in a clinic.

This lesson explains how enzymes speed up reactions and how to measure the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction. You should know the structure of globular proteins (tertiary structure and the bonds that hold it) from the protein lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Enzyme"}
A globular protein that acts as a biological catalyst: it speeds up a chemical reaction by lowering its activation energy and is not used up or permanently changed by the reaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Active site"}
The region of an enzyme, formed by a small number of amino acids in a particular three-dimensional arrangement, to which the substrate binds and where the reaction is catalysed.
:::

:::definition{term="Enzyme-substrate complex"}
The temporary structure formed when a substrate molecule binds to the active site of an enzyme.
:::

:::definition{term="Activation energy"}
The minimum energy that reacting molecules must have for the reaction to take place.
:::

:::definition{term="Intracellular and extracellular enzymes"}
Intracellular enzymes act inside the cell that made them (for example catalase in liver cells, enzymes of respiration). Extracellular enzymes are secreted and act outside the cell (for example amylase in saliva, and enzymes secreted by fungi onto their food).
:::

## Enzymes are globular proteins

An enzyme's polypeptide chain is folded into a precise tertiary (and sometimes quaternary) structure. Within this shape is the **active site**, a cleft or pocket whose shape and charges are **complementary** to those of the substrate. Because only a substrate with the right shape and chemical properties can fit and bind, each enzyme is **specific** to one substrate or a small group of closely related substrates. Amylase hydrolyses α-1,4 glycosidic bonds in starch but cannot hydrolyse the β-1,4 bonds in cellulose.

## How an enzyme catalyses a reaction

1. The substrate collides with the active site in the correct orientation.
2. It binds, held by temporary bonds (hydrogen and ionic bonds) with R groups in the active site, forming an **enzyme-substrate complex**.
3. The R groups of the active site put strain on bonds in the substrate, or hold two substrates close together in the right orientation, or provide a favourable charge environment.
4. This lowers the **activation energy**, so the reaction happens much faster at body temperature.
5. The products, which no longer fit the active site so well, are released (enzyme-product complex → enzyme + products).
6. The enzyme is unchanged and its active site can be used again, thousands of times a second in some enzymes.

### Lowering activation energy

Imagine an energy profile drawn as a curve of energy against the progress of the reaction. Both the catalysed and uncatalysed reactions start and finish at the same energy levels, so the overall energy change is the same. The difference is the height of the "hill" in between:

| | Without enzyme | With enzyme |
|---|---|---|
| Energy of reactants (arbitrary units) | 100 | 100 |
| Peak energy (arbitrary units) | 180 | 130 |
| Activation energy (arbitrary units) | 80 | 30 |
| Energy of products (arbitrary units) | 60 | 60 |

With a lower activation energy, a much larger proportion of substrate molecules have enough energy to react at any moment, so the rate is far higher.

## Lock-and-key and induced fit

**Lock-and-key hypothesis**: the active site has a fixed, rigid shape that is exactly complementary to the substrate, like a key in a lock. This explains specificity but not everything we observe.

**Induced-fit hypothesis**: the active site is flexible. When the substrate binds, the enzyme changes shape slightly, moulding itself more closely around the substrate. This change puts strain on the substrate's bonds and helps lower the activation energy. It explains why some enzymes can act on a small range of similar substrates and why binding can change the shape of other parts of the molecule. Most biologists accept induced fit as the better model.

## Following the progress of a reaction

The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is measured either as the **appearance of product** or the **disappearance of substrate** per unit time.

- **Catalase**, found in most cells (an intracellular enzyme), breaks down hydrogen peroxide: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$. The volume of oxygen can be collected in an inverted measuring cylinder or gas syringe and recorded every 10 s.
- **Amylase** hydrolyses starch to maltose. Samples are taken at intervals and added to iodine: as starch disappears, the blue-black colour fades, and the time when the colour no longer changes (stays orange-brown) is the end point.
- **Colorimeter**: if the reaction involves a colour change, such as the blue-black starch-iodine complex fading, absorbance can be measured at intervals; absorbance falls as substrate disappears.

### Initial rate

As the reaction proceeds, substrate is used up, collisions with active sites become less frequent and the rate falls. To compare conditions fairly, biologists use the **initial rate**: the gradient of a **tangent** to the curve at time zero, when substrate concentration is known and has not yet been depleted.

:::worked-solution[Initial rate of a catalase reaction]
1. Fresh potato extract was added to hydrogen peroxide, and the oxygen collected:

| Time / s | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of oxygen / cm³ | 0.0 | 4.2 | 7.6 | 10.2 | 12.0 | 14.0 | 14.8 | 15.0 |

2. Plotted, the curve rises steeply at first, then more slowly, and levels off at about 15 cm³ as the hydrogen peroxide runs out.
3. A tangent drawn to the curve at t = 0 passes through (0, 0) and (20 s, 9.0 cm³).
4. Initial rate = gradient $= 9.0 \div 20 = 0.45\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
5. Compare: the mean rate over the whole 100 s is only $15.0 \div 100 = 0.15\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{s}^{-1}$, because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
:::

:::example{title="Reading a starch-amylase experiment"}
Rudo mixed amylase with starch at 35 °C and every 30 s transferred one drop to a well of iodine on a spotting tile. The drops were blue-black at 0, 30 and 60 s, became paler at 90 s and stayed orange-brown from 120 s onwards.

Interpretation: starch was present until between 90 and 120 s; by 120 s it had all been hydrolysed (to maltose, which does not react with iodine). The time to the end point is about 120 s, so a rate can be expressed as 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹. The result is only accurate to the sampling interval (30 s); sampling every 10 s, or using a colorimeter, would give a more precise end point.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the enzyme and substrate have the same shape"}
The active site is **complementary** to the substrate, not the same shape.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes provide energy for the reaction"}
Enzymes lower the activation energy; they do not supply energy, and the overall energy change is the same.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the mean rate when the initial rate is asked for"}
The initial rate is the gradient of the tangent at time zero, not the total product divided by total time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are 'killed'"}
Enzymes are not alive; at high temperatures they are denatured.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Enzymes are globular proteins that catalyse reactions inside cells (intracellular) or after secretion (extracellular).
- The active site is complementary to the substrate; binding forms an enzyme-substrate complex, which makes enzymes specific.
- Enzymes lower the activation energy; they do not change the overall energy change of the reaction.
- Induced fit: the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.
- Rates are followed by product formation (oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearance (starch with iodine, colorimeter); the initial rate is the gradient of the tangent at time zero.
:::
