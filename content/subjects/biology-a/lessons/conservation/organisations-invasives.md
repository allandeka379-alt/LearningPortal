## Introduction

Wildlife does not respect borders. A pangolin poached in one country may be sold in another thousands of kilometres away, and a water plant sold for garden ponds can escape and choke rivers on another continent. Protecting biodiversity therefore needs international organisations that assess which species are at risk and agreements that control what crosses borders.

This lesson covers two global bodies, the **IUCN** and **CITES**, and one of the fastest-growing threats to biodiversity: **invasive alien species**. You should already know the main causes of extinction and the idea of a niche.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="IUCN"}
The International Union for Conservation of Nature: an international organisation of governments and non-governmental organisations that assesses the conservation status of species and publishes the Red List of Threatened Species.
:::

:::definition{term="CITES"}
The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora: an international agreement between governments that regulates international trade in wild animals and plants, and products made from them, so that trade does not threaten their survival.
:::

:::definition{term="Invasive alien species"}
A species introduced, deliberately or accidentally, by humans outside its natural range, that establishes, spreads and harms native biodiversity, ecosystems, human health or the economy.
:::

## The IUCN and the Red List

The IUCN gathers data from scientists worldwide and uses standard criteria to place each assessed species in a category of extinction risk.

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Extinct (EX) | No reasonable doubt that the last individual has died |
| Extinct in the Wild (EW) | Survives only in captivity or as a naturalised population outside its past range |
| Critically Endangered (CR) | Extremely high risk of extinction in the wild |
| Endangered (EN) | Very high risk of extinction in the wild |
| Vulnerable (VU) | High risk of extinction in the wild |
| Near Threatened (NT) | Close to qualifying for a threatened category, or likely to in the near future |
| Least Concern (LC) | Widespread and abundant; does not qualify for the categories above |
| Data Deficient (DD) | Not enough information to assess the risk |

CR, EN and VU together are the **threatened** categories. The criteria include how fast a population has declined, how small its geographic range is, how few mature individuals remain, and the probability of extinction calculated from models. For example, under one criterion a decline of at least about 80% over ten years or three generations qualifies a species as Critically Endangered, at least 50% as Endangered and at least 30% as Vulnerable.

The roles of the IUCN:

1. **Assessing status**: the Red List shows which species need action most urgently.
2. **Monitoring**: repeated assessments show whether conservation is working.
3. **Advising**: providing evidence to governments, CITES and conservation bodies to set priorities and laws.
4. **Raising awareness** and supporting conservation projects and protected-area planning.

:::worked-solution[Using a decline to suggest a Red List category]
An illustrative survey finds 5200 mature individuals of a tortoise species; ten years later there are 1820.
1. Percentage change $= \dfrac{1820 - 5200}{5200} \times 100 = -65.0\%$.
2. A decline of 65% is at least 50% but less than about 80%.
3. On this criterion alone, the species would qualify as **Endangered**. Other criteria (range, numbers) would also be checked, and the highest category met is used.
:::

## CITES

CITES is an agreement that countries (Parties) join voluntarily; it is then binding on them, and each country passes its own laws to enforce it. It regulates **international trade**, not hunting within a country.

| Appendix | Species included | Trade rules |
|---|---|---|
| I | Species threatened with extinction (for example most populations of rhinos and great apes) | Commercial international trade banned; non-commercial trade (such as for research or captive breeding) needs import and export permits |
| II | Species not necessarily threatened now, but which could become so without control (and look-alike species) | Trade allowed with an export permit showing it is legal and not harmful to the species' survival |
| III | Species protected in at least one country that has asked other Parties for help in controlling trade | Permits or certificates from that country |

How CITES works in practice:

- Customs officers check permits at borders; shipments without them are seized.
- Countries report their trade, so that the Parties can see whether trade is sustainable.
- Species are moved between appendices at regular meetings of the Parties, using scientific evidence including IUCN assessments.

**Limitations**: enforcement depends on each country's resources; illegal trade continues where profits are high; CITES does not protect habitat.

## Invasive alien species

### Why control them?

| Reason | How it harms biodiversity | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Competition | Outcompetes native species for light, food, water or space | Water hyacinth covers water surfaces, shading native plants |
| Predation | Eats native species that have no evolved defences | Nile perch introduced into Lake Victoria preyed on many native cichlid fish species, contributing to the loss of many of them |
| Disease | Carries pathogens to which native species have no resistance | Grey squirrels introduced to Britain carry squirrelpox virus, which kills native red squirrels |
| Hybridisation | Interbreeds with a related native species, diluting its distinct gene pool | Introduced mallard ducks hybridising with native duck species |
| Economic cost | Damages crops, fisheries, water supplies, infrastructure or health | Weeds blocking irrigation canals and water intakes |

Invasive species succeed because they often leave their natural predators, parasites and competitors behind, reproduce quickly and tolerate a wide range of conditions.

### Methods of control (outline)

1. **Prevention**: border checks (biosecurity), bans on importing known invasive species, and cleaning boats and equipment.
2. **Early detection and eradication**: removing a small new population before it spreads.
3. **Mechanical or physical control**: pulling, cutting, trapping or harvesting.
4. **Chemical control**: herbicides or pesticides, used carefully to limit harm to other species.
5. **Biological control**: releasing a natural enemy from the invader's home range, such as weevils that feed only on water hyacinth, after extensive testing to make sure it will not attack native species.

:::example{title="Predicting the spread of an introduced fish"}
A predatory fish is released into a lake where no similar predator exists. Its numbers are 12 in the first survey and 31 a year later.

**Calculate**: an increase of 158.3% in one year.

**Predict and explain.** Native fish have not evolved escape behaviour against this predator, and nothing preys on the newcomer, so its numbers are likely to keep rising quickly. Small native fish species may decline sharply or become extinct, and species that ate those fish lose their food. Control should start early, when numbers are still low and eradication may be possible.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying CITES bans hunting"}
CITES controls **international trade**. Hunting within a country is governed by that country's laws.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every introduced species invasive"}
Many introduced species, such as most crops, do not spread or cause harm. "Invasive" means it spreads and causes harm.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the IUCN makes laws"}
The IUCN assesses and advises. Laws are made by governments, and trade rules are agreed through CITES.
:::

:::

For "*explain* why invasive species should be controlled", give a named mechanism (competition, predation, disease, hybridisation) with an example and an economic reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The IUCN assesses species against standard criteria and publishes the Red List (EX, EW, CR, EN, VU, NT, LC, DD).
- CR, EN and VU are the threatened categories; Red List data guide priorities and laws.
- CITES regulates international trade: Appendix I commercial trade banned; Appendix II permits; Appendix III help requested by one country.
- Invasive alien species harm natives by competition, predation, disease and hybridisation, and have economic costs.
- Control: prevention, early eradication, mechanical, chemical and carefully tested biological control.
:::
