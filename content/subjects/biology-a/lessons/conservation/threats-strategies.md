## Introduction

Extinction is natural: most species that ever lived are extinct. What worries biologists is the **rate** of extinction today, driven by one species, our own. Species are being lost before they have even been described, along with the genes, medicines and ecosystem services they might provide.

Conservation is not just "protecting animals in parks". It ranges from keeping wild places untouched, through actively managing populations, to restoring damaged land, and it uses tools from fenced sanctuaries to liquid nitrogen. This lesson explains why species are lost, why it matters, and what can be done.

You should already know the levels of biodiversity (ecosystem, species, genetic) and what a niche is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Extinction"}
The permanent loss of a species when its last individual dies. A species may be **extinct in the wild** if it survives only in captivity.
:::

:::definition{term="Endangered species"}
A species at high risk of extinction in the wild in the near future because its numbers are small or falling, or its range is restricted.
:::

:::definition{term="Conservation"}
The protection and management of species, habitats and ecosystems so that biodiversity is maintained for the future, while allowing sustainable use where appropriate.
:::

:::definition{term="Preservation"}
Keeping an area or species as it is by protecting it from human interference, with little or no use.
:::

:::definition{term="Reclamation"}
Restoring a damaged or degraded habitat, such as an old mine dump or eroded land, so that it can again support a natural community.
:::

## Why species become extinct

### 1. Habitat loss and degradation

The single biggest cause. Clearing land for farms, settlements, roads, dams and mining removes food, breeding sites and shelter. Fragmentation splits populations into small, isolated groups that cannot interbreed, lose genetic diversity and are vulnerable to chance events. Pollution degrades habitats that remain.

### 2. Hunting and harvesting by humans

Overhunting for meat, trophies, skins, horns, ivory or traditional medicine, and overfishing, can reduce populations faster than they reproduce. Large, slow-breeding animals with long gestation and few young are most at risk.

### 3. Competition

Species introduced by humans can outcompete native species for food, light or space. Introduced predators and diseases can also wipe out species with no defences. On islands, where species evolved without these pressures, this has caused many extinctions.

### 4. Climate change

As temperature and rainfall patterns shift, the conditions a species tolerates may no longer exist where it lives. Mobile species may move, but plants and animals on mountain tops, in isolated reserves or with narrow niches may have nowhere to go. Changes in timing (flowering, insect emergence) can also break links between species.

Small populations face an **extinction vortex**: fewer individuals → more inbreeding and loss of alleles → lower fitness and adaptability → even fewer individuals.

## Why maintain biodiversity?

| Type of reason | Examples |
|---|---|
| Ecological | Species interact in food webs; losing one (such as a pollinator or seed disperser) can affect many others. Ecosystems provide services: pollination, nutrient cycling, water purification, soil formation, carbon storage, flood control. |
| Economic | Food, timber, fibres and fuel; wild relatives of crops carry useful alleles; tourism and photographic safaris; sustainable fisheries. |
| Medicinal | Many drugs come from, or were modelled on, natural compounds; most species have never been tested. |
| Aesthetic | People value the beauty of landscapes and wildlife for recreation and well-being. |
| Ethical | Many people believe other species have a right to exist and that we have a duty to pass on a living world to future generations. |

## Conservation strategies

### In situ: in the natural habitat

- **National parks and game reserves** (for example Hwange, Mana Pools and Gonarezhou) protect large areas of habitat with legal limits on hunting, settlement and land use. Rangers carry out anti-poaching patrols.
- **Marine parks** protect coral reefs, seagrass beds and fish breeding grounds by restricting fishing, anchoring and development, allowing stocks to recover and spill over into surrounding waters.
- **Management** includes controlled burning, removing invasive species, providing water points, culling or moving animals when numbers exceed what the habitat can support, and creating wildlife corridors to link fragments.

Advantages: species stay in the habitat to which they are adapted, with their whole community, and natural selection continues.

### Ex situ: outside the natural habitat

- **Zoos** keep captive populations of endangered species, run **captive breeding** programmes with studbooks to avoid inbreeding, carry out research and education, and may **reintroduce** animals to the wild.
- **Botanic gardens** grow rare plants, propagate them (including by tissue culture), and reintroduce them.
- **Seed banks** store seeds dried to a low water content and kept at low temperature (about −20 °C), slowing metabolism so they stay viable for decades. Samples are tested regularly for germination, and fresh seed is grown when viability falls.
- **Frozen zoos** store sperm, eggs, embryos and tissue samples of animals in liquid nitrogen (about −196 °C), preserving genetic diversity for future breeding.

:::example{title="Interpreting a seed bank viability test"}
A seed bank tests a sample of 50 seeds of a rare grass at intervals.

| Years in storage | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seeds germinating (out of 50) | 46 | 44 | 41 | 33 |
| Germination / % | 92 | 88 | 82 | 66 |

**Describe.** Germination falls from 92% to 66% over 30 years, slowly at first (4 percentage points in the first ten years) and faster later (16 points in the last ten years).

**Suggest.** Seeds slowly lose viability even when cold and dry, as stored food and membranes deteriorate. Before germination falls much further, the bank should grow plants from the stored seed and collect fresh seed to replace the sample.
:::

:::worked-solution[Percentage decrease in germination over 30 years]
1. Change in the number germinating $= 33 - 46 = -13$ seeds.
2. Percentage change $= \dfrac{-13}{46} \times 100 = -28.3\%$ (to 3 significant figures).
3. The number of seeds germinating fell by 28.3%. Note that this is not the same as the fall of 26 **percentage points** (92% to 66%): a percentage change is always relative to the starting value.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting the effect of fragmentation on a woodland bird"}
A forest bird that does not cross open ground lives in one large forest, which is cleared until only five small, separate patches remain.

**Predict and explain.** Each patch holds a small population. Birds cannot move between patches, so there is no gene flow: alleles are lost by chance (genetic drift) and inbreeding rises. A fire or disease in one patch can wipe out that population, and it cannot be recolonised. The species is more likely to become extinct even though some forest remains. A **wildlife corridor** of trees linking the patches would allow movement and interbreeding.
:::

### Assisted reproduction in endangered mammals

Captive and wild populations may be too small, or individuals too far apart, to breed naturally. Techniques used include:

1. **Artificial insemination**: semen collected from a male (fresh or from a frozen zoo) is placed in the female's reproductive tract.
2. **In vitro fertilisation (IVF)**: the female is given hormones so that she releases several eggs, which are collected and fertilised by sperm in the laboratory.
3. **Embryo transfer**: the resulting embryos are placed into the uterus of a female, sometimes after freezing.
4. **Surrogacy**: the embryo is carried by a female of a closely related, more common species (or a less valuable female of the same species), so that more offspring can be produced from the few endangered females.

These methods let genes move between populations without moving animals, and avoid inbreeding when founders are few.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect causes of extinction, reasons for conservation and the roles of parks, zoos, botanic gardens and seed banks. ZIMSEC frames conservation as preservation, management and reclamation; frozen zoos and IVF, embryo transfer and surrogacy in endangered mammals are Cambridge detail (A Level, Paper 4).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying zoos alone can save a species"}
Captive populations are small, may lose wild behaviour and genetic diversity, and need habitat to return to. Zoos support, not replace, in situ conservation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a vague reason such as \"for the future\""}
Name the type of reason and give a specific example: "economic: wild sorghum relatives carry drought-tolerance alleles for breeders".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying seed banks freeze seeds wet"}
Seeds are dried first; ice crystals in wet seeds would damage cells.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Paper 3 ask you to *explain* how one named factor causes extinction: link the factor to reduced birth rate or increased death rate and to small population size.
:::

:::exam-tip
To *outline the role* of a zoo or seed bank, give what it does (captive breeding, storage), how (studbooks, drying and cooling) and why (genetic diversity, reintroduction).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Habitat loss, hunting, competition from introduced species and climate change drive extinction; small populations spiral down through inbreeding.
- Biodiversity is maintained for ecological, economic, medicinal, aesthetic and ethical reasons.
- Conservation includes preservation, management and reclamation, in situ (national and marine parks) and ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens).
- Seed banks store dried seeds cold; frozen zoos store gametes, embryos and tissue in liquid nitrogen.
- IVF, embryo transfer and surrogacy increase offspring from few endangered animals.
:::
