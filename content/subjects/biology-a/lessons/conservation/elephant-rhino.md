## Introduction

Zimbabwe is home to some of Africa's best-known large mammals. Hwange National Park holds one of the largest elephant populations on the continent, while the lowveld conservancies protect rhinos that were moved there from areas where poaching was severe. These animals bring tourists and income, but they also raid crops, compete with people for water and attract criminal poaching networks.

ZIMSEC expects you to discuss elephant and rhino conservation as a real case study: the threats, the measures, the organisations involved and the conflicts between people's interests. This lesson gives you the biology and the structure for a balanced answer. You should already know the general causes of extinction and the meaning of in situ conservation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Poaching"}
The illegal killing or capture of wild animals, for example for ivory, rhino horn or meat.
:::

:::definition{term="Human-wildlife conflict"}
Any interaction in which wild animals harm people's lives, crops, livestock or property, or people harm wildlife in response.
:::

:::definition{term="Community-based natural resource management"}
An approach in which local communities are given the right to manage, use and benefit from the wildlife and other natural resources on their land, giving them a reason to conserve them.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrying capacity"}
The largest population of a species that an area's resources (food, water, space) can support in the long term without degrading the habitat.
:::

## The animals

| Feature | African elephant | White rhinoceros |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific name | *Loxodonta africana* | *Ceratotherium simum* |
| Feeding | Mixed feeder: grasses, leaves, bark, fruit | Grazer: wide, square lip for cropping grass |
| Reproduction | Gestation about 22 months; one calf about every 4-5 years | Gestation about 16 months; one calf about every 2-3 years |
| Valued illegally for | Ivory (tusks) | Horn, made of keratin |

Both are large, long-lived and **slow-breeding**, so populations recover slowly from losses. Both shape their ecosystems: elephants open up woodland and dig for water, and grazing rhinos maintain short-grass lawns used by other grazers. The black rhinoceros (*Diceros bicornis*), a browser with a hooked lip, is also conserved in Zimbabwe.

## Reasons for concern

1. **Poaching** for ivory and horn, driven by high prices in illegal international markets. Rhino horn has no proven medical value, but demand persists.
2. **Habitat loss** as settlement and farming expand, cutting migration routes and fragmenting populations.
3. **Human-wildlife conflict**: elephants raid crops, damage water points and can kill people; communities may retaliate.
4. **Local overpopulation of elephants**: where elephants are confined and supplied with artificial water points, as in parts of Hwange, numbers may exceed carrying capacity. They push over and debark trees, reducing woodland and the food and habitat of other species.
5. **Drought**, which concentrates animals around remaining water and can cause deaths.

## Conservation measures

- **Anti-poaching**: armed ranger patrols, intelligence networks, aerial surveillance, and prosecution of poachers and traffickers.
- **Dehorning rhinos**: horn is removed under anaesthetic by a vet. It is keratin and regrows, so it must be repeated, but a dehorned rhino is less attractive to poachers.
- **Translocation**: moving animals from high-risk or overcrowded areas to safer ones, or to start new populations, which also spreads genetic diversity.
- **Sanctuaries, intensive protection zones and conservancies**: smaller, heavily guarded areas, including privately managed lowveld conservancies, where rhinos are monitored individually.
- **Monitoring**: aerial counts of elephants, ear-notching and identification of individual rhinos, and recording carcasses to measure poaching.
- **Managing elephant numbers** where they exceed carrying capacity: translocation, closing some artificial water points, contraception trials, and, controversially, culling.

:::worked-solution[Calculating elephant density]
An illustrative aerial survey counts 3600 elephants in a protected area of 4500 km².
1. Density $= \dfrac{\text{number}}{\text{area}} = \dfrac{3600}{4500}$.
2. Density $= 0.80$ elephants per km² (2 significant figures).
3. Compare with an estimated carrying capacity for the habitat to judge whether the population may damage the woodland.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating rhino population trends (illustrative data)"}
The data below are illustrative, not real counts, for one conservancy.

| Year | 2000 | 2004 | 2008 | 2012 | 2016 | 2020 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rhinos counted | 64 | 71 | 58 | 39 | 47 | 60 |
| Carcasses of poached rhinos found that year | 2 | 3 | 14 | 22 | 9 | 4 |

**Describe.** The population rose from 64 to 71 by 2004, then fell to 39 in 2012, a decrease of 39.1% from 2000. It then recovered to 60 by 2020, an increase of 53.8% from 2012.

**Explain.** The fall coincides with the rise in poached carcasses (14 in 2008 and 22 in 2012). After 2012 poaching fell to 4 carcasses, suggesting that new measures, such as dehorning and more patrols, worked; births then exceeded deaths.

**Evaluate.** Carcasses found are only those recovered, so poaching may be underestimated. Counts may miss animals. Rhinos may have been moved in or out by translocation. Correlation does not prove the cause, but the timing strongly supports it.
:::

## Organisations and programmes

### The Environmental Management Agency (EMA)

EMA is the statutory body set up under Zimbabwe's Environmental Management Act. It:

- requires and reviews **environmental impact assessments** before projects such as mines, dams and roads go ahead;
- regulates pollution, waste, and the use of wetlands and riverbanks;
- monitors the environment and enforces environmental law;
- runs environmental education and awareness campaigns, for example on veld fires and land degradation.

Wildlife in national parks is managed by the Zimbabwe Parks and Wildlife Management Authority (ZimParks).

### CAMPFIRE

The Communal Areas Management Programme for Indigenous Resources began in the late 1980s. Rural district councils were given authority over wildlife in communal areas. Income from safari hunting quotas and photographic tourism is shared with local communities and used for schools, clinics, boreholes or household dividends.

The idea is that if wildlife brings benefits, communities will protect it rather than see it only as a pest. Strengths: local people gain income and a say; poaching and retaliation may fall; habitat is kept as wildlife land. Weaknesses: income has varied and often depended heavily on trophy hunting; benefits have sometimes not reached households fairly; crop damage costs can exceed benefits.

### CITES and international cooperation

**CITES** (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) is an international agreement that regulates trade in wildlife and wildlife products between member countries. Species on **Appendix I** may not be traded commercially; **Appendix II** trade is allowed only with permits showing it is legal and not harmful to the species' survival.

International commercial trade in ivory and rhino horn is banned. Elephant populations of Zimbabwe and some neighbouring countries are listed on Appendix II with strict conditions. Cooperation also includes transfrontier conservation areas such as Kavango-Zambezi (KAZA), which allow elephants to move between countries, and sharing intelligence against trafficking.

## Conflicts of interest and economic implications

- **The ivory trade debate**: Zimbabwe holds stockpiles of ivory from natural deaths and seizures and has argued that legal sales could fund conservation; other countries and many conservation groups argue that any legal trade can stimulate demand and provide cover for illegal ivory.
- **Farmers versus wildlife**: crop raiding by elephants threatens food security near parks; compensation and fencing are costly.
- **Too many elephants**: some managers favour reducing numbers in overcrowded areas to protect woodland and other species; others oppose culling on ethical grounds or because of the impact on tourism.
- **Economic value**: wildlife tourism brings foreign currency and jobs; trophy hunting generates income for parks and CAMPFIRE communities but is opposed by some on ethical grounds. Anti-poaching is expensive.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="One-sided answers"}
"Discuss" questions on ivory sales, culling or trophy hunting need arguments on **both** sides, attributed to groups, and a reasoned conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling rhino horn ivory or bone"}
Rhino horn is compacted keratin, the protein in hair and nails; it grows back after dehorning.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating carcass counts as complete"}
Data on poached animals are usually underestimates, because not every carcass is found.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Elephants (*Loxodonta africana*) and white rhinos (*Ceratotherium simum*) are slow-breeding, so poaching, habitat loss and conflict reduce numbers quickly.
- Measures: anti-poaching, dehorning, translocation, sanctuaries and conservancies, monitoring and managing elephant numbers.
- EMA enforces environmental law and impact assessments; CAMPFIRE gives communities income from wildlife.
- CITES regulates international trade; commercial ivory and horn trade is banned.
- Conflicts: ivory sales, crop raiding, elephant overpopulation and culling, and the economics of tourism and hunting.
:::
