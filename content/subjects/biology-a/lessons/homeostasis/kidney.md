## Introduction

A farm worker cutting sugar cane in the Lowveld on a hot afternoon may lose more than a litre of sweat an hour. Her kidneys respond within minutes, producing a small volume of dark, concentrated urine and conserving water. After she drinks plenty in the evening, they produce a large volume of pale, dilute urine. The kidneys excrete urea and keep the water potential of the blood almost constant.

This lesson explains how. You should already know the principles of homeostasis and negative feedback, how urea is formed in the liver, and the meaning of water potential, diffusion, osmosis and active transport.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nephron"}
The functional unit of the kidney: a tubule, about 3 cm long, with its associated blood vessels, that filters the blood and modifies the filtrate to form urine; each kidney has about a million.
:::

:::definition{term="Ultrafiltration"}
Filtration under high pressure in the glomerulus, which forces water and small solutes out of the blood into the Bowman's capsule, while cells and large proteins are kept in the blood.
:::

:::definition{term="Selective reabsorption"}
The uptake of useful substances such as glucose, amino acids, ions and water from the filtrate back into the blood, mainly in the proximal convoluted tubule.
:::

:::definition{term="Osmoregulation"}
The control of the water potential of the body fluids by regulating the amount of water and solutes excreted in urine.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Values (approximate)"}
About 125 cm³ of filtrate is formed per minute in an adult (about 180 dm³ per day), but only about 1-2 dm³ of urine is produced per day: about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed.
:::

## Structure of the kidney

A longitudinal section of a kidney shows, from outside in:

| Region | Description |
|---|---|
| Fibrous capsule | Tough outer layer protecting the kidney |
| Cortex | Outer dark region containing glomeruli, Bowman's capsules and convoluted tubules |
| Medulla | Inner region of cone-shaped pyramids containing loops of Henle and collecting ducts |
| Renal pelvis | Funnel-shaped cavity collecting urine from the collecting ducts |
| Ureter | Tube carrying urine from the pelvis to the bladder |
| Renal artery and renal vein | Bring blood to the kidney and take it away; branches run between the pyramids and into the cortex |

## The nephron and its blood supply

Follow the filtrate through one nephron:

1. **Afferent arteriole** brings blood to a knot of capillaries, the **glomerulus**.
2. The glomerulus sits inside the cup-shaped **Bowman's capsule** (renal capsule), in the cortex.
3. The **efferent arteriole** carries blood away from the glomerulus; it is narrower than the afferent arteriole. It then branches into capillaries around the tubule.
4. The filtrate passes along the **proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)** in the cortex.
5. Then the **loop of Henle**, with a descending limb and an ascending limb dipping into the medulla.
6. Then the **distal convoluted tubule (DCT)** in the cortex.
7. Several nephrons drain into a **collecting duct**, which runs down through the medulla to the renal pelvis.

## Ultrafiltration

Blood in the glomerulus is at **high hydrostatic pressure** because the efferent arteriole is narrower than the afferent arteriole. This pressure forces fluid out through three layers:

| Layer | Feature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Capillary endothelium | Pores (fenestrations) between and through cells | Lets plasma through but not blood cells |
| Basement membrane | Mesh of collagen and glycoproteins | The main filter: stops large proteins (relative molecular mass above about 68 000) |
| Podocytes (epithelium of the capsule) | Cells with foot-like processes leaving gaps (filtration slits) | Allow filtrate into the capsule space |

The **glomerular filtrate** contains water, glucose, amino acids, urea, ions and small proteins, at almost the same concentrations as in plasma. Blood cells and large plasma proteins stay in the blood, which lowers the water potential of the blood leaving the glomerulus, but the high hydrostatic pressure outweighs this.

## Selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule

The PCT reabsorbs all the glucose and amino acids and much of the water and ions. Its cells are adapted:

- **Microvilli** on the surface facing the lumen give a large surface area.
- **Many mitochondria** supply ATP for active transport.
- **Tight junctions** between cells stop filtrate leaking between them.
- **Many cotransporter proteins** in the luminal membrane and **sodium-potassium pumps** in the basal membrane.
- The cells are close to blood capillaries, with infoldings of the basal membrane.

The mechanism:

1. **Sodium-potassium pumps** in the basal membrane actively transport Na⁺ out of the PCT cell into the tissue fluid (and blood), using ATP. This keeps the Na⁺ concentration inside the cell low.
2. Na⁺ therefore diffuses from the filtrate into the cell through **cotransporter proteins**, each carrying a **glucose** (or amino acid) molecule with it, even against the glucose concentration gradient. This is **cotransport** (indirect active transport).
3. Glucose then moves out of the cell into the tissue fluid by facilitated diffusion and enters the blood.
4. Removal of solutes lowers the water potential of the cells and blood, so **water** follows by **osmosis**.
5. Some **urea** diffuses back too, because it is concentrated as water is removed.

Normally all glucose is reabsorbed. If blood glucose is very high (as in untreated diabetes), the cotransporters become saturated and glucose appears in urine.

:::example{title="Comparing plasma, filtrate and urine"}
Illustrative concentrations in a healthy person:

| Substance | Blood plasma / g per 100 cm³ | Filtrate in Bowman's capsule / g per 100 cm³ | Urine / g per 100 cm³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | 8.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Glucose | 0.10 | 0.10 | 0.00 |
| Urea | 0.03 | 0.03 | 2.00 |
| Sodium ions | 0.32 | 0.32 | 0.35 |

**Explain:** protein is absent from the filtrate because its molecules are too large to pass through the basement membrane. Glucose is in the filtrate at the plasma concentration but absent from urine because it is all reabsorbed in the PCT. Urea is about 67 times more concentrated in urine than in filtrate because most of the water is reabsorbed while much of the urea is not.
:::

:::worked-solution[How much filtrate is reabsorbed?]
An adult forms 125 cm³ of filtrate per minute and 1.0 cm³ of urine per minute.
1. Volume reabsorbed per minute $= 125 - 1.0 = 124\ \text{cm}^3$.
2. Percentage reabsorbed $= \dfrac{124}{125} \times 100 = 99.2\%$.
3. Filtrate formed per day $= 125 \times 60 \times 24 = 180\,000\ \text{cm}^3 = 180\ \text{dm}^3$, several times the volume of blood plasma, which is why reabsorption must be so efficient.
:::

## The loop of Henle (outline)

The loop of Henle acts as a **countercurrent multiplier**. The ascending limb actively transports Na⁺ and Cl⁻ out into the medulla but is impermeable to water; the descending limb is permeable to water. Because the filtrate flows in opposite directions in the two limbs, a gradient builds up so that the tissue fluid of the **medulla has a very low water potential**, lowest near the tips of the loops. This allows water to be drawn out of the collecting duct by osmosis.

The **distal convoluted tubule** adjusts the ion content and pH of the filtrate.

## Osmoregulation: ADH and the collecting duct

The permeability of the collecting duct to water is controlled by **antidiuretic hormone (ADH)**:

1. **Stimulus**: the water potential of the blood falls (for example after sweating or eating salty food).
2. **Receptors**: **osmoreceptors** in the **hypothalamus** detect the change; they lose water by osmosis and shrink.
3. Nerve impulses pass to the **posterior pituitary gland**, which releases more **ADH** into the blood. (ADH is made in the hypothalamus and stored in the posterior pituitary.)
4. ADH binds to receptors on collecting duct cells. A second messenger (cyclic AMP) causes vesicles containing **aquaporins** (water channel proteins) to fuse with the luminal membrane.
5. The collecting duct becomes **more permeable to water**, so more water moves by osmosis into the low water potential medulla and then into the blood.
6. A **small volume of concentrated urine** is produced, and the blood water potential rises back towards normal.
7. **Negative feedback**: as the water potential returns, less ADH is released, and aquaporins are removed from the membrane by endocytosis.

When the blood water potential rises (after drinking a lot), less ADH is released, the collecting duct becomes less permeable, and a large volume of dilute urine is produced.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying ADH is made in the posterior pituitary"}
ADH is **made in the hypothalamus** and **released from the posterior pituitary**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying glucose is filtered and then actively pumped back directly"}
Glucose is reabsorbed by **cotransport with Na⁺**; the active step is the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in the basal membrane.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ADH makes the kidney produce more urine"}
ADH increases water reabsorption, giving **less**, more concentrated urine ("anti-diuretic").
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The kidney has a cortex (glomeruli, convoluted tubules), a medulla (loops of Henle, collecting ducts) and a pelvis leading to the ureter.
- Ultrafiltration: high pressure in the glomerulus forces small molecules through the endothelium, basement membrane and podocytes; proteins and cells stay in the blood.
- The PCT reabsorbs all glucose and amino acids by cotransport with Na⁺, driven by Na⁺/K⁺ pumps; water follows by osmosis. Microvilli and mitochondria suit it to this.
- The loop of Henle makes the medulla's water potential low, so water can leave the collecting duct.
- Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus control ADH release from the posterior pituitary; ADH inserts aquaporins into the collecting duct, increasing water reabsorption by negative feedback.
:::
