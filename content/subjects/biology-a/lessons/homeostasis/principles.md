## Introduction

On an October afternoon in Kariba the air temperature can climb above 38 °C, while on a winter night in the Eastern Highlands it can fall close to freezing. A person moving between the two keeps a core body temperature of about 37 °C throughout. The concentration of glucose in their blood and the water potential of their blood also stay remarkably steady, whether they have just eaten a plate of sadza or skipped lunch.

Keeping the internal environment steady is called **homeostasis**. This lesson sets out the principles; the kidney and blood glucose lessons apply them. You should already know the outline of the nervous and endocrine systems, how enzymes are affected by temperature and pH, and the meaning of water potential.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Homeostasis"}
The maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment (the tissue fluid around cells) within narrow limits, despite changes in the external environment or in the body's activity.
:::

:::definition{term="Negative feedback"}
A control mechanism in which a change from the set point is detected and triggers responses that reverse the change, returning the factor towards the set point.
:::

:::definition{term="Positive feedback"}
A mechanism in which a change triggers responses that increase the change further, moving the factor further from its starting value.
:::

:::definition{term="Deamination"}
The removal of the amino group from an excess amino acid in the liver, forming ammonia and a keto acid.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Typical values (approximate)"}
Core body temperature about 37 °C. Fasting blood glucose about 4-6 mmol dm⁻³ (about 0.8-1.1 g dm⁻³). Blood water potential is kept nearly constant by the kidneys; plasma solute concentration varies by only a few per cent.
:::

## Why homeostasis matters

The internal environment of a mammal is the **tissue fluid** bathing its cells. Keeping its conditions steady matters because:

1. **Temperature.** Enzymes work fastest near their optimum. Too cold and reactions slow; too hot (above about 40-41 °C in humans) and enzymes and other proteins begin to denature. A constant core temperature also lets mammals stay active in cold conditions.
2. **Blood glucose.** Cells, especially brain cells, need a steady supply of glucose for respiration. Too low (hypoglycaemia) and cells lack energy; too high and water leaves cells by osmosis, and glucose is lost in the urine.
3. **Water potential.** If the blood water potential falls, water leaves cells by osmosis and they shrink; if it rises, water enters cells and they may swell and burst. Cell volume and the concentrations of ions and enzymes inside cells depend on it.
4. Other factors such as **pH** and the concentrations of ions and CO₂ are also controlled.

Homeostasis gives mammals a degree of **independence** from their external environment.

## The components of a control system

| Component | What it does | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | A change in a factor, internal or external | Rise in blood glucose; fall in air temperature |
| Receptor | Detects the stimulus | Thermoreceptors in the hypothalamus and skin; osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus; beta and alpha cells in the pancreas |
| Coordination (control centre) | Compares the input with the set point and sends instructions | Hypothalamus; islets of Langerhans |
| Communication | Carries the instructions | Nerve impulses (fast, short-lived, precise) or hormones in the blood (slower, longer-lasting, widespread) |
| Effector | Carries out the response | Muscles (shivering, constriction of arterioles) and glands (sweat glands, liver cells responding to hormones) |
| Response | Changes the factor | Heat released or lost; glucose taken up or released |

**Nervous and endocrine coordination.** The **nervous system** uses electrical impulses along neurones and chemical transmission at synapses; responses are rapid and localised, such as sweating and vasodilation. The **endocrine system** uses hormones secreted from glands into the blood; they reach all tissues, but only cells with the right receptors respond. Responses are slower and longer-lasting, such as the effects of insulin or ADH.

## Negative feedback

In negative feedback the response **reverses** the original change:

1. A factor rises above (or falls below) its set point.
2. Receptors detect the change.
3. The control centre triggers effectors.
4. The effectors bring the factor back towards the set point.
5. As the factor returns, the stimulus is reduced, so the corrective response is reduced too.

The factor therefore **fluctuates** around the set point within narrow limits rather than staying perfectly constant. Having separate mechanisms to correct a rise and a fall (for example insulin and glucagon) gives a faster, more precise response.

**Positive feedback** is unusual in homeostasis because it amplifies a change. An example is the release of oxytocin during childbirth: stretching of the cervix causes oxytocin release, which causes stronger contractions and more stretching, until the baby is born. Another is the opening of more voltage-gated sodium channels during the rising phase of an action potential.

:::example{title="Interpreting temperature data from a hot day"}
Chipo, working outdoors in the Zambezi valley, wore a temperature logger.

| Time | 06:00 | 09:00 | 12:00 | 15:00 | 18:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Air temperature / °C | 22 | 30 | 36 | 38 | 31 |
| Skin temperature / °C | 32.0 | 33.5 | 35.0 | 35.5 | 34.0 |
| Core temperature / °C | 36.8 | 37.0 | 37.1 | 37.2 | 37.0 |

**Describe:** air temperature rises by 16 °C (from 22 to 38 °C) and skin temperature by 3.5 °C, but core temperature changes by only 0.4 °C, staying close to 37 °C.

**Explain:** thermoreceptors detect the rise and the hypothalamus triggers sweating and vasodilation of arterioles supplying skin capillaries. Evaporation of sweat removes heat, keeping the core temperature almost constant by negative feedback. The skin temperature changes more because the skin is the site of heat exchange with the surroundings.
:::

:::worked-solution[Blood glucose after a meal]
Tendai's blood glucose was measured after a meal.

| Time after meal / min | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | 5.0 | 7.5 | 6.6 | 5.6 | 5.0 | 4.9 |

1. Percentage increase from 0 to 30 min $= \dfrac{7.5 - 5.0}{5.0} \times 100 = 50\%$.
2. Mean rate of fall from 30 to 120 min $= \dfrac{7.5 - 5.0}{120 - 30} = \dfrac{2.5}{90} = 0.028\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}\ \text{min}^{-1}$.
3. The concentration returns to the starting value by 120 min and dips slightly below it (4.9) before being corrected again: a typical negative feedback fluctuation around the set point.
:::

## Excess amino acids and urea

Mammals cannot store excess amino acids. In the **liver**:

1. **Deamination**: the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid, forming **ammonia** (NH₃) and a **keto acid**.
2. The keto acid can be respired or converted to glucose, glycogen or fat.
3. Ammonia is very soluble and highly toxic, so it is quickly combined with **carbon dioxide** to form **urea**, which is much less toxic.
4. Urea passes into the blood and is removed by the **kidneys** in urine.

The detailed cycle that makes urea is not required.

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying homeostasis keeps factors exactly constant"}
Factors **fluctuate** within narrow limits around a set point.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying positive feedback is used to correct changes"}
Positive feedback **amplifies** a change; correction is by **negative** feedback.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying urea is made in the kidneys"}
Urea is made in the **liver**; the kidneys excrete it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 4 often asks you to *explain* why a factor must be controlled. Link it to the mechanism: "temperature affects enzyme activity", "water potential affects osmosis into and out of cells".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *describe* negative feedback, use the sequence stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response → factor returns towards set point, and name each part for the example given.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Homeostasis keeps the internal environment (tissue fluid) within narrow limits: core temperature, blood glucose and water potential are key examples.
- A control system has receptors, a coordinator, nervous or hormonal communication and effectors (muscles and glands).
- Negative feedback reverses a change, so factors fluctuate about a set point; positive feedback amplifies a change.
- The nervous system gives fast, short-lived responses; hormones give slower, longer-lasting ones.
- Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver; ammonia is converted to urea, which the kidneys excrete.
:::
