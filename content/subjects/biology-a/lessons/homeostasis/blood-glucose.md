## Introduction

Health workers at clinics in Gweru or Mutare use a small meter and a drop of blood from a fingertip to check a person's blood glucose in a few seconds. Behind that reading lies one of the best-understood examples of homeostasis: two hormones from the pancreas that pull blood glucose back towards its set point whenever it rises after a meal or falls during a long walk to school without breakfast.

You should already know the principles of homeostasis and negative feedback, the structure of glycogen, and how enzymes and membrane proteins work.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Glycogenesis"}
The synthesis of glycogen from glucose, mainly in liver and muscle cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Glycogenolysis"}
The breakdown of glycogen to glucose (in the liver, glucose is then released into the blood).
:::

:::definition{term="Gluconeogenesis"}
The synthesis of glucose in the liver from non-carbohydrate sources such as amino acids and glycerol.
:::

:::definition{term="Second messenger"}
A small molecule inside a cell, such as cyclic AMP, whose concentration rises when a hormone binds to a receptor on the cell surface, and which passes the signal on to enzymes inside the cell.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Values (approximate)"}
Fasting blood glucose about 4-6 mmol dm⁻³ (about 0.8-1.1 g dm⁻³). It may rise to about 7-8 mmol dm⁻³ after a carbohydrate meal in a healthy person and returns within about two hours.
:::

## The pancreas as an endocrine gland

Scattered among the enzyme-secreting tissue of the pancreas are the **islets of Langerhans**. They contain:

- **Alpha (α) cells**, which secrete **glucagon** when blood glucose falls;
- **Beta (β) cells**, which secrete **insulin** when blood glucose rises.

These cells act as both **receptors** (they detect the glucose concentration of the blood flowing past them) and the **coordinator** (they secrete the hormone directly into the blood).

## When blood glucose rises: insulin

1. After a meal, glucose is absorbed and blood glucose rises.
2. **Beta cells** detect the rise and secrete **insulin**; alpha cells secrete less glucagon.
3. Insulin binds to **receptors** on the cell surface membranes of target cells, especially **muscle**, **liver** and fat cells.
4. In muscle and fat cells, binding causes vesicles containing **GLUT4** glucose transporter proteins to move to and fuse with the cell surface membrane. More transporters means faster **facilitated diffusion** of glucose into the cells.
5. Inside liver and muscle cells, insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to **glycogen** (**glycogenesis**). Removing glucose this way keeps its concentration inside the cell low, so glucose keeps diffusing in.
6. Insulin also increases the rate of respiration of glucose and the conversion of glucose to fat.
7. Blood glucose falls back towards the set point; as it falls, less insulin is secreted (**negative feedback**).

## When blood glucose falls: glucagon

1. Blood glucose falls, for example during exercise or a long time without food.
2. **Alpha cells** detect the fall and secrete **glucagon**; beta cells secrete less insulin.
3. Glucagon binds to receptors on **liver cells** (muscle cells have no glucagon receptors).
4. It activates enzymes that break down glycogen to glucose (**glycogenolysis**) and stimulates **gluconeogenesis**.
5. Glucose diffuses out of the liver cells into the blood, so blood glucose rises; glucagon secretion then falls.

Insulin and glucagon are **antagonistic**: their opposing actions give fine control in both directions.

## Cell signalling by glucagon

Glucagon is a peptide hormone and cannot cross the cell surface membrane. Its message is passed in by a chain of molecules:

1. **Binding.** Glucagon (the first messenger) binds to a specific **receptor** protein in the liver cell membrane, which changes its **shape (conformation)**.
2. **G-protein.** The changed receptor activates a **G-protein** on the inner side of the membrane.
3. **Adenylyl cyclase.** The G-protein activates the enzyme **adenylyl cyclase** in the membrane.
4. **Second messenger.** Adenylyl cyclase converts many molecules of **ATP** into **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**.
5. **Protein kinase A.** cAMP activates **protein kinase A**.
6. **Enzyme cascade.** Protein kinase A phosphorylates and activates further enzymes (including phosphorylase kinase), which in turn activate **glycogen phosphorylase**.
7. **Glycogenolysis.** Glycogen phosphorylase breaks glycogen down into glucose phosphate, which is converted to glucose and released.

At most steps, one active molecule activates **many** molecules of the next, so the signal is **amplified**: a few glucagon molecules lead to the release of a very large number of glucose molecules within seconds.

:::worked-solution[Amplification in the cascade]
Suppose (illustrative figures) that at each of four steps after receptor binding (G-protein → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → glycogen phosphorylase) one active molecule activates 10 molecules of the next.
1. Amplification $= 10 \times 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 10^4 = 10\,000$.
2. So one glucagon molecule bound to one receptor could lead to about 10 000 active molecules at the end of the chain, each releasing many glucose units from glycogen.
3. Real factors differ from step to step, but the principle is the same: a tiny hormone concentration (much lower than the glucose it controls) produces a rapid, large response.
:::

## Diabetes in outline

**Diabetes mellitus** is a condition in which blood glucose is not controlled properly.

- **Type 1**: the beta cells are destroyed by the person's own immune system, so little or no insulin is made. It usually begins in childhood or adolescence and is treated with insulin injections, together with diet and regular glucose monitoring.
- **Type 2**: target cells become less responsive to insulin, and later less insulin may be secreted. It is more common in older adults and is associated with factors such as being overweight and physically inactive; it is managed with diet, exercise and, if needed, medication.

Without treatment, blood glucose stays high after meals, glucose appears in the urine because the kidney cotransporters are saturated, and the person may feel thirsty and tired. Diabetes is a medical condition, not anyone's fault; diagnosis and treatment are for qualified health workers.

:::example{title="Interpreting a glucose tolerance test"}
Two adults drank a standard glucose solution after fasting overnight.

| Time after glucose drink / min | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Person A / mmol dm⁻³ | 4.8 | 7.6 | 6.5 | 5.5 | 5.0 |
| Person B / mmol dm⁻³ | 7.2 | 12.4 | 15.1 | 14.0 | 12.6 |

**Describe:** person A starts at 4.8, peaks at 7.6 at 30 minutes and returns to 5.0 by 120 minutes. Person B starts higher (7.2), rises for longer to a peak of 15.1 at 60 minutes and is still 12.6 at 120 minutes.

**Explain:** in person A, insulin secretion brings glucose back to normal by negative feedback. Person B's pattern is consistent with diabetes: too little insulin is secreted, or cells respond poorly to it, so glucose is taken up into cells and stored as glycogen too slowly.
:::

## Measuring glucose: test strips and biosensors

**Urine test strips (dipsticks)** carry two immobilised enzymes and a colourless dye (chromogen):

1. **Glucose oxidase** catalyses: glucose + oxygen → gluconolactone (which forms gluconic acid) + **hydrogen peroxide**.
2. **Peroxidase** catalyses the reaction of hydrogen peroxide with the chromogen, which becomes **coloured**.
3. The colour after a fixed time is compared with a colour chart: the more glucose, the darker the colour. The result is semi-quantitative, and it shows glucose that was in the urine over a period, not the blood level at that moment.

**Biosensors (blood glucose meters)** use glucose oxidase immobilised on an electrode in a disposable strip. A drop of blood is added; the reaction produces a small **electric current** proportional to the glucose concentration, which the meter shows as a number. This gives a quick, quantitative reading of the current blood glucose concentration.

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing glucagon and glycogen"}
**Glucagon** is a hormone; **glycogen** is a storage polysaccharide. Glucagon causes glycogen breakdown.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying glucagon acts on muscle cells"}
Glucagon's target is the **liver**; muscle cells lack glucagon receptors and cannot release glucose into the blood.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cAMP is a hormone"}
cAMP is the **second messenger** inside the cell; the hormone is the first messenger.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 4 often asks you to *describe* the cell signalling pathway: give every step in order (receptor, conformational change, G-protein, adenylyl cyclase, ATP → cAMP, protein kinase A, cascade, glycogen phosphorylase) and say where the amplification happens.
:::

:::exam-tip
In *explain* questions on insulin, name GLUT4, say it increases facilitated diffusion, and link glycogenesis to keeping the concentration gradient. For biosensors, name both enzymes and state the product detected (colour or current).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Beta cells secrete insulin when blood glucose rises; alpha cells secrete glucagon when it falls: antagonistic negative feedback.
- Insulin increases GLUT4 transporters in muscle and fat cell membranes and stimulates glycogenesis.
- Glucagon acts on liver cells, causing glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
- Glucagon → receptor → G-protein → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → enzyme cascade → glycogen phosphorylase, amplifying the signal.
- Diabetes is failure of insulin production or response; glucose oxidase and peroxidase test strips and biosensors measure glucose.
:::
