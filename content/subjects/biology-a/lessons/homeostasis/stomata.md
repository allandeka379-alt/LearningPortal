## Introduction

During the hot, dry weeks before the rains break in October, a maize or bean plant in a field near Masvingo faces a dilemma every afternoon. To photosynthesise it must let carbon dioxide into its leaves through pores in the epidermis, but every open pore also lets water vapour escape. Plants solve this by constantly adjusting the width of the pores, the **stomata**, in response to light, carbon dioxide and water supply: a form of homeostasis in plants.

You should already know the structure of a leaf, transpiration, water potential and osmosis, active transport and the idea of a second messenger.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stoma (plural stomata)"}
A pore in the epidermis of a leaf or stem, bounded by two guard cells, that allows gas exchange between the air spaces of the leaf and the atmosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Guard cell"}
One of a pair of curved epidermal cells, containing chloroplasts and with an unevenly thickened cell wall, that change shape as they gain or lose water, opening or closing the stoma between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Abscisic acid (ABA)"}
A plant growth regulator produced in larger amounts when a plant is short of water; it causes stomata to close, reducing water loss.
:::

## The compromise: CO₂ in, water out

Open stomata allow CO₂ to diffuse into the leaf for photosynthesis, but water vapour diffuses out by the same route, down a steep water potential gradient. If water is lost faster than it is taken up by the roots, cells lose turgor and the plant wilts. The stomatal aperture is therefore adjusted so that the plant gains as much CO₂ as possible without losing too much water.

**Stomata usually open** when:

- light intensity increases (in the morning);
- the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf falls (because photosynthesis is using it).

**Stomata usually close** when:

- it is dark (no photosynthesis, so no need for CO₂);
- the plant is short of water (ABA);
- the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high;
- temperatures are very high and the air very dry, increasing water loss.

Many plants also show a **daily (circadian) rhythm**: stomata open shortly before dawn and close in the evening, even if the plant is kept in constant conditions for a few days, because an internal clock helps time the response.

:::example{title="Describing a daily pattern of stomatal opening"}
Farai measured the mean width of stomata on bean leaves on a hot, sunny day.

| Time of day | 04:00 | 06:00 | 08:00 | 10:00 | 12:00 | 14:00 | 16:00 | 18:00 | 20:00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean stomatal aperture / µm | 0.5 | 2.0 | 6.5 | 8.0 | 7.5 | 4.0 | 5.5 | 2.5 | 0.5 |

**Describe:** stomata are almost closed at night (0.5 µm), open rapidly after dawn to a maximum of 8.0 µm at 10:00, partly close to 4.0 µm at 14:00, open again a little to 5.5 µm at 16:00 and close by 20:00.

**Explain:** opening in the morning follows rising light intensity, allowing CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis. The partial closure at 14:00 is likely to be a response to water stress in the hottest part of the day: water loss exceeds uptake, ABA is produced and some stomata close, reducing transpiration at the cost of some photosynthesis. As it becomes cooler, stomata reopen partly, then close as light fades.
:::

## Structure of guard cells

Seen in surface view under the microscope, each stoma has two **kidney-shaped guard cells** (bean-shaped in most dicotyledons; dumbbell-shaped in grasses such as maize). Compared with other epidermal cells, guard cells:

- have **chloroplasts**;
- have a cell wall that is **thicker on the side next to the pore** (the inner wall) and thinner on the outer side;
- have **cellulose microfibrils** arranged in hoops around the cell (radially), so the cell cannot get much wider when it swells and instead becomes longer;
- have many **proton pumps**, potassium ion channels and other transport proteins in their cell surface membrane.

## How stomata open

1. In light, **ATP-powered proton pumps** in the guard cell membrane actively transport **protons (H⁺)** out of the guard cells.
2. This makes the inside of the cell more negative, which opens **voltage-gated potassium channels**.
3. **Potassium ions (K⁺)** diffuse into the guard cells (helped by the electrical gradient). Negative ions such as chloride also enter, and malate is made from starch in the guard cells, balancing the charge.
4. The solute concentration rises, so the **water potential** of the guard cells **falls** below that of the surrounding epidermal cells.
5. **Water enters by osmosis**, and the guard cells become **turgid**.
6. Because the inner wall is thicker and less stretchable, and the hoops of microfibrils stop the cells getting wider, the cells **lengthen and curve outwards**, pulling apart at the pore. The stoma **opens**.

## How stomata close

The process reverses: proton pumping stops, potassium ions (and anions) leave the guard cells, the water potential rises, water leaves by osmosis, the guard cells become **flaccid** and the pore closes.

## Abscisic acid and water stress

When a plant is short of water:

1. Cells in the roots and leaves produce more **abscisic acid (ABA)**, which travels to the guard cells.
2. ABA binds to **receptors** on the guard cell membranes.
3. This causes **calcium ions (Ca²⁺)** to enter the cytoplasm of the guard cells, through channels in the cell surface membrane and from the vacuole. Calcium acts as a **second messenger**.
4. Calcium ions inhibit the proton pumps and open channels that let **anions and K⁺ leave** the guard cells.
5. The water potential of the guard cells rises, **water leaves by osmosis**, the cells become flaccid and the stoma **closes**, reducing water loss by transpiration.

ABA can close stomata within minutes, protecting the plant from wilting, but it also reduces CO₂ uptake, so photosynthesis slows.

:::worked-solution[Estimating stomatal density]
Rudo painted clear nail varnish on the lower surface of a leaf, peeled off the dried layer and counted stomata in five fields of view at high power. The field of view had a radius of 0.2 mm. Her counts were 22, 25, 23, 24 and 26.
1. Mean count $= (22 + 25 + 23 + 24 + 26) \div 5 = 120 \div 5 = 24$.
2. Area of field $= \pi r^2 = \pi \times 0.2^2 = 0.1257\ \text{mm}^2$ (to four significant figures).
3. Stomatal density $= 24 \div 0.1257 = 191$ stomata per mm².
4. Counting several fields and taking the mean gives a more representative value, because stomata are not evenly spread over the leaf.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying potassium ions are pumped into guard cells"}
**Protons** are actively pumped **out**; potassium ions then **diffuse in** through channels, down an electrochemical gradient.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying stomata close because guard cells become turgid"}
Turgid guard cells **open** the pore; flaccid guard cells close it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ABA is a second messenger"}
ABA is the signal arriving at the guard cell; **calcium ions** are the second messenger inside it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 4 often asks you to *explain* stomatal opening in a fixed number of marks. Give the sequence: proton pumps (ATP) → K⁺ channels open → K⁺ enters → water potential falls → water in by osmosis → turgid → thick inner wall makes cells bow apart.
:::

:::exam-tip
In data questions, link each change in aperture to light, CO₂ or water supply, and remember the trade-off: closing saves water but reduces photosynthesis. Quote times and values.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Stomata balance CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis against water loss by transpiration.
- They open in light and at low internal CO₂ and close in darkness, at high CO₂ and in water stress; many plants show a daily rhythm.
- Guard cells have chloroplasts, thick inner walls and radial microfibrils, so they bow apart when turgid.
- Opening: protons pumped out, K⁺ diffuses in, water potential falls, water enters by osmosis.
- In water stress ABA binds to guard cell receptors; Ca²⁺ acts as a second messenger, ions and water leave and stomata close.
:::
