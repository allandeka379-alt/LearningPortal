## Introduction

As maize leaves age at the end of the season in Mashonaland, they turn yellow before they turn brown. The yellow was there all along: it comes from carotenoid pigments that were hidden by the much larger amount of green chlorophyll. Separating leaf pigments by chromatography shows the same thing on a strip of paper.

This lesson covers where photosynthesis happens inside the cell and the pigments that capture light. You should already know the overall equation for photosynthesis, the structure of a leaf, and the principle of chromatography.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Photosynthetic pigment"}
A coloured molecule in the thylakoid membranes that absorbs particular wavelengths of light and passes the energy on for photosynthesis; chlorophylls and carotenoids are the main ones.
:::

:::definition{term="Absorption spectrum"}
A graph (or table) showing how much light of each wavelength is absorbed by a pigment or a mixture of pigments.
:::

:::definition{term="Action spectrum"}
A graph (or table) showing the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength of light.
:::

:::definition{term="Rf value"}
In chromatography, the distance moved by a substance divided by the distance moved by the solvent front from the same origin; it is constant for a given substance, solvent and medium.
:::

## Structure of the chloroplast

An electron micrograph of a chloroplast in a palisade mesophyll cell shows a lens- or disc-shaped organelle, typically about 3-10 µm long. Part by part:

| Structure | Description | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Envelope | Double membrane (outer and inner) | Controls entry and exit of substances |
| Thylakoids | Flattened membrane sacs; stacks of them form **grana** | Hold the photosystems (pigments), electron carriers and ATP synthase: site of the **light-dependent stage** |
| Thylakoid space (lumen) | Fluid inside each thylakoid | Protons accumulate here, forming the gradient for chemiosmosis |
| Intergranal lamellae | Thylakoids that link one granum to another | Increase membrane area; connect grana |
| Stroma | Fluid matrix around the thylakoids | Contains the enzymes of the Calvin cycle, including rubisco: site of the **light-independent stage** |
| Starch grains | Large pale granules in the stroma | Store carbohydrate made in photosynthesis |
| Lipid droplets | Small dark spheres (plastoglobuli) | Store lipids, including those for membranes |
| Circular DNA and 70S ribosomes | In the stroma | Code for and make some chloroplast proteins |

How the structure suits the function:

- Many stacked thylakoids give a **large membrane surface area** for pigments, carriers and ATP synthase, so more light is absorbed and more ATP and reduced NADP are made.
- Pigments are arranged in **photosystems**, so energy is funnelled to a reaction centre.
- The **thylakoid space is small**, so pumped protons quickly build a steep gradient.
- The **stroma** surrounds the thylakoids, so ATP and reduced NADP pass straight to the Calvin cycle enzymes.
- Chloroplast DNA and ribosomes let the chloroplast make some of its own proteins.

## Photosynthetic pigments

| Pigment | Group | Colour | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorophyll a | Chlorophyll | Blue-green | Primary pigment: found in the reaction centres of both photosystems; absorbs strongly in red and blue-violet light |
| Chlorophyll b | Chlorophyll | Yellow-green | Accessory pigment in the light-harvesting system; absorbs slightly different red and blue wavelengths |
| Carotene | Carotenoid | Orange | Accessory pigment; absorbs blue-violet light; also protects chlorophyll from damage by very bright light |
| Xanthophyll | Carotenoid | Yellow | Accessory pigment; absorbs blue-violet and blue-green light |

**Accessory pigments** absorb wavelengths that chlorophyll a absorbs poorly and pass the energy to chlorophyll a. Having several pigments widens the range of wavelengths that can be used.

Green light (about 500-570 nm) is absorbed least by chlorophylls and is mostly reflected or transmitted, which is why leaves look green.

| Wavelength / nm | 400 | 450 | 500 | 550 | 600 | 650 | 700 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absorption by leaf pigments / % | 70 | 95 | 40 | 12 | 20 | 80 | 10 |
| Rate of photosynthesis / % of maximum | 65 | 90 | 50 | 25 | 35 | 85 | 12 |

![Absorption by leaf pigments and rate of photosynthesis against wavelength: both are highest in blue-violet light (95% and 90% at 450 nm) and red light (80% and 85% at 650 nm), and lowest in green light (12% and 25% at 550 nm) and at 700 nm, so the action spectrum follows the absorption spectrum.](media:diagrams/absorption-action-spectra.png "Figure: absorption spectrum and action spectrum from the lesson's table (illustrative values)")

**Describe:** both are highest in blue-violet light (95% and 90% at 450 nm) and red light (80% and 85% at 650 nm), and lowest in green light (12% and 25% at 550 nm) and at 700 nm.

**Explain:** the two patterns are very similar, which is evidence that the light absorbed by these pigments is the light used in photosynthesis. The rate at 500-600 nm is slightly higher than the absorption suggests because accessory pigments absorb some of this light and pass the energy to chlorophyll a.
:::

## Separating the pigments by chromatography

1. Grind leaves (for example spinach or a fresh green maize leaf) with a little sand and propanone (acetone) to extract the pigments. Propanone is flammable and irritant: no flames, work in a ventilated room and wear eye protection.
2. Use a fine glass capillary to put a small, concentrated spot of extract on a pencil line about 1.5 cm from the bottom of a strip of chromatography paper (or a thin-layer chromatography plate). Let it dry between applications.
3. Stand the strip in a sealed tube with a small volume of solvent (for example a mixture of petroleum ether and propanone), with the origin **above** the solvent surface.
4. When the solvent front is near the top, remove the strip, mark the front at once and let it dry.
5. Measure from the origin to the **centre** of each spot and to the solvent front, and calculate Rf.

Pigments separate because they differ in solubility in the solvent and in how strongly they are attracted to the paper or plate. Carotene is the most soluble in the non-polar solvent and moves furthest. Thin-layer chromatography gives sharper, better-separated spots than paper and runs faster.

:::worked-solution[Calculating Rf values]
Tatenda's chromatogram has a solvent front 80 mm from the origin.

| Spot | Distance moved / mm | Rf |
|---|---|---|
| Spot 1 (orange-yellow) | 76 | 0.95 |
| Spot 2 (yellow) | 59 | 0.74 |
| Spot 3 (blue-green) | 51 | 0.64 |
| Spot 4 (yellow-green) | 36 | 0.45 |

![Chromatogram with a solvent front 80 mm from the origin: carotene moved 76 mm (Rf 0.95), xanthophyll 59 mm (Rf 0.74), chlorophyll a 51 mm (Rf 0.64) and chlorophyll b 36 mm (Rf 0.45); every Rf is below 1 and the most soluble pigment, carotene, travels furthest.](media:diagrams/pigment-chromatogram-rf.png "Figure: separation of leaf pigments by chromatography, with Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent")

1. Rf of spot 1 $= 76 \div 80 = 0.95$. Spot 2 $= 59 \div 80 = 0.74$; spot 3 $= 51 \div 80 = 0.64$; spot 4 $= 36 \div 80 = 0.45$.
2. Every Rf is less than 1, because no spot can move further than the solvent.
3. Matching colours and Rf with this solvent: spot 1 carotene, spot 2 xanthophyll, spot 3 chlorophyll a, spot 4 chlorophyll b. Rf values change with the solvent and paper, so identification should use colour as well as Rf, or compare with known pigments run on the same strip.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting the light-independent stage in the thylakoids"}
The **light-dependent** stage is on the thylakoid membranes; the **light-independent** stage (Calvin cycle) is in the **stroma**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying chlorophyll absorbs green light"}
Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue-violet light; green light is mostly **reflected**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring to the top of a spot"}
Measure to the **centre** of each spot, from the origin line, and use the solvent front marked as soon as the strip is removed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the two spectra"}
An absorption spectrum shows light **absorbed** by pigments; an action spectrum shows the **rate of photosynthesis**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Paper 2, an electron micrograph described in words is common: *identify* grana, stroma and starch grains, then *explain* how each helps. Always say which stage happens where.
:::

:::exam-tip
For spectra, *describe* with peaks and troughs quoting wavelengths and values; *explain* the similarity as evidence that absorbed light drives photosynthesis. In chromatography, Rf has no units and is always below 1; give it to two decimal places.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The chloroplast has a double envelope, stacked thylakoids (grana) and a stroma with starch grains, lipid droplets, circular DNA and 70S ribosomes.
- The light-dependent stage happens on the thylakoid membranes; the light-independent stage in the stroma.
- Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment; chlorophyll b, carotene and xanthophyll are accessory pigments that widen the range of wavelengths absorbed.
- Absorption and action spectra both peak in blue-violet and red light, showing that absorbed light drives photosynthesis.
- Chromatography separates pigments; Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
:::
