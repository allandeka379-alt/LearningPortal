## Introduction

Horticulture farms near Harare grow roses, tomatoes and peppers in greenhouses and tunnels for local markets and export. Growers who understand limiting factors can decide whether extra lighting, heating or carbon dioxide will actually raise their yield, or simply waste money.

This lesson covers the factors that control the rate of photosynthesis and how to investigate them. You should already know the light-dependent stage, the Calvin cycle and how enzymes are affected by temperature.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Limiting factor"}
The factor that is in shortest supply relative to the others, so that it alone determines the rate of a process; increasing it increases the rate, while increasing other factors does not.
:::

:::definition{term="Light saturation"}
The light intensity above which further increases in light no longer increase the rate of photosynthesis, because another factor has become limiting.
:::

:::definition{term="Inverse square law"}
Light intensity from a point source is inversely proportional to the square of the distance from it: $I \propto \dfrac{1}{d^2}$.
:::

## The principle of limiting factors

Photosynthesis needs light, carbon dioxide, a suitable temperature, water and chlorophyll at the same time. At any moment, the rate is set by whichever factor is **furthest below** the level needed for a faster rate.

1. **Light intensity.** In low light, the light-dependent stage makes too little ATP and reduced NADP, so the Calvin cycle is slowed. The rate rises in proportion to light intensity, then levels off at **light saturation**.
2. **Carbon dioxide concentration.** CO₂ is the substrate for rubisco. At low concentrations, too little RuBP is carboxylated. In air (about 0.04% CO₂) CO₂ is often the limiting factor on a bright day; raising it to about 0.1% can increase the rate considerably.
3. **Temperature.** Temperature affects the enzymes of the Calvin cycle (such as rubisco) much more than the light-dependent stage. The rate increases with temperature up to an optimum (often about 25-35 °C, depending on the species), then falls as enzymes denature and stomata close. Above about 25 °C, photorespiration also increases in C3 plants.
4. **Wavelength (light quality).** Red and blue-violet light are absorbed most and give the highest rates; green light gives the lowest rate (see the action spectrum).

Water is rarely directly limiting: water stress first closes the stomata, which reduces CO₂ supply.

:::example{title="Reading a limiting-factor graph given as a table"}
The rate of photosynthesis of a plant was measured at different light intensities at two CO₂ concentrations, with the temperature kept at 25 °C.

| Light intensity / arbitrary units | 0 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate at 0.04% CO₂ / arbitrary units | 0 | 12 | 20 | 23 | 24 | 24 | 24 |
| Rate at 0.10% CO₂ / arbitrary units | 0 | 13 | 25 | 36 | 44 | 48 | 48 |

**Describe:** at both CO₂ concentrations the rate rises steeply at low light intensity, then levels off: at 24 units by about 40 units of light for 0.04% CO₂, and at 48 units by about 50 units of light for 0.10% CO₂.

**Explain:** from 0 to 10 units of light, the two curves are almost the same, so **light** is the limiting factor. At high light intensity the rate at 0.04% CO₂ stays at 24 however bright the light, so **CO₂ concentration** is limiting; raising CO₂ to 0.10% doubles the plateau. At 0.10% CO₂ and above 50 units of light, the rate levels off again, so some other factor, such as **temperature** or the amount of rubisco, is now limiting.
:::

## Investigating photosynthesis

### Whole aquatic plants

A cut shoot of *Elodea* or *Cabomba* is placed upside down in a boiling tube of water containing **sodium hydrogencarbonate** solution, which provides a constant supply of CO₂. A lamp is placed at different distances.

- **Measuring the rate**: count the bubbles released from the cut stem per minute, or collect the gas in a photosynthometer (a capillary tube) and measure the length of the bubble, from which volume $= \pi r^2 l$. Counting bubbles assumes bubbles are of equal size, which is often not true; collecting gas is more accurate.
- **Controlling temperature**: a lamp gives off heat, so a heat filter (a beaker of water or a glass tank) is placed between the lamp and the tube, or an LED lamp is used, and the temperature of the water is checked.
- **Equilibration**: leave the plant for about 5 minutes at each new distance before counting.
- **Light intensity** is calculated as $\dfrac{1}{d^2}$ (or measured with a light meter). The room should be dark apart from the lamp.

The gas collected is mostly oxygen, but it also contains some nitrogen and CO₂, and some oxygen is used in respiration or dissolves in the water, so the measured rate is a **net** rate and underestimates the true rate.

:::worked-solution[Using the inverse square law]
Rudo counted bubbles from *Cabomba* with a lamp at different distances (three counts each).

| Distance / cm | Relative light intensity (10 000/d²) | Bubbles 1 | Bubbles 2 | Bubbles 3 | Mean bubbles per min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 100.0 | 62 | 60 | 61 | 61 |
| 15 | 44.4 | 44 | 46 | 45 | 45 |
| 20 | 25.0 | 30 | 29 | 31 | 30 |
| 30 | 11.1 | 14 | 13 | 15 | 14 |
| 35 | 8.2 | 11 | 10 | 9 | 10 |

1. At 20 cm: $\dfrac{10\,000}{20^2} = \dfrac{10\,000}{400} = 25.0$.
2. Halving the distance from 20 cm to 10 cm multiplies light intensity by $2^2 = 4$ (from 25.0 to 100.0).
3. But the mean rate only rises from 30 to 61 bubbles per minute, about double, not four times. So at high light intensities light is no longer the only limiting factor: the rate is approaching light saturation, and CO₂ or temperature limits it.
4. Multiplying by 10 000 just gives convenient numbers; the units are arbitrary.
:::

### Leaf discs

Discs are cut from a leaf with a cork borer, and the air is removed from their spaces with a syringe and sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, so they sink. In light, oxygen produced by photosynthesis collects in the air spaces and the discs float. The time for half of the discs to float is recorded, and the rate is taken as $1/\text{time}$.

### Redox indicators and chloroplast suspensions

Chloroplasts are extracted from leaves by grinding in ice-cold buffer and filtering, then mixed with **DCPIP** or **methylene blue**. In light, electrons from the photosystems reduce the indicator, which turns from blue to colourless. The time to decolourise (or the absorbance in a colorimeter) measures the rate of the light-dependent stage. Different **light intensities** (distances) or **wavelengths** (coloured filters) can be compared. A tube kept in the dark and a tube with boiled chloroplasts act as controls.

## Applications in greenhouses

Growers raise yield by removing limiting factors:

- **Lighting** extends the day length and raises light intensity in dull weather.
- **CO₂ enrichment** (for example burning paraffin or propane, or bottled CO₂) to about 0.1%.
- **Heating** in cold weather, keeping temperatures near the optimum for the crop.

Each is only worthwhile when that factor is limiting; extra CO₂ in dim light gives little benefit. The extra yield must also be worth more than the cost of fuel and electricity.

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the rate is limited by light when it has levelled off"}
On the plateau, **light is not limiting**: another factor is. Light limits only where the rate rises with light intensity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Halving the distance doubles the light"}
By the inverse square law, halving the distance gives **four times** the light intensity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the heat from the lamp"}
A lamp close to the plant also warms it. Use a heat filter or LED and record the temperature, or temperature becomes a second independent variable.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Cambridge Paper 4 and ZIMSEC Paper 2, *explain* limiting factors from a graph by naming the factor for each region and giving the evidence ("the curves are the same at low light, so light is limiting").
:::

:::exam-tip
Planning questions (Cambridge Paper 5, ZIMSEC Paper 4) on *Elodea* need: at least five distances or light intensities, 1/d², a hydrogencarbonate solution of fixed concentration, a heat filter, equilibration time, three repeats and a dark control. State that the rate is a net rate.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A limiting factor is the one in shortest supply; only increasing it raises the rate.
- Light intensity, CO₂ concentration and temperature are the main limiting factors; wavelength also matters.
- Graphs show light limiting on the rising part and another factor limiting on the plateau.
- Aquatic plants, leaf discs and redox indicators with chloroplasts are used to investigate photosynthesis; light intensity ∝ 1/d².
- Greenhouses use lighting, CO₂ enrichment and heating to remove limiting factors when it is economic.
:::
