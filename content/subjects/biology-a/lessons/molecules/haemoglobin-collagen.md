## Introduction

Haemoglobin and collagen are the two proteins examiners use to test whether you can link structure to function. Haemoglobin is a soluble globular protein packed into red blood cells, where it picks up oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. Collagen is an insoluble fibrous protein, the most abundant protein in mammals, giving strength to tendons, skin, bone and artery walls. Sickle cell disease, which affects many families in sub-Saharan Africa, shows what happens when haemoglobin's structure changes by a single amino acid.

You need the four levels of protein structure and the bonds that hold them, from the previous lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Haemoglobin"}
A globular, water-soluble protein in red blood cells made of four polypeptide chains (two α-globin and two β-globin), each bound to a haem group containing an iron(II) ion, that transports oxygen.
:::

:::definition{term="Prosthetic group"}
A non-protein group permanently bound to a protein and needed for its function; the haem group of haemoglobin is an example.
:::

:::definition{term="Collagen"}
A fibrous, insoluble structural protein made of three polypeptide chains wound into a triple helix, with cross-linked molecules arranged into fibrils and fibres.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Numbers to know"}
Haemoglobin: 4 chains, 4 haem groups, 4 iron(II) ions, so up to 4 oxygen molecules per haemoglobin molecule. Collagen: 3 chains per molecule, glycine at every third position.
:::

## Haemoglobin

### Structure

1. **Primary structure**: each α chain has 141 amino acids and each β chain 146; the sequence decides how each chain folds.
2. **Secondary structure**: much of each chain is coiled into α-helices.
3. **Tertiary structure**: each chain folds into a compact globular shape with a pocket that holds a haem group. Hydrophobic R groups point inwards; hydrophilic R groups lie on the outside.
4. **Quaternary structure**: two α and two β chains fit together into a near-spherical molecule, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions between the chains.
5. **Haem groups**: each chain carries one haem, a flat ring structure with an iron(II) ion ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) at its centre. Each iron(II) ion can bind one oxygen molecule reversibly, forming oxyhaemoglobin.

### How the structure suits oxygen transport

| Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Hydrophilic R groups on the outside | Haemoglobin is soluble, so very high concentrations can be held in the cytoplasm of red blood cells |
| Four haem groups with iron(II) | Each molecule carries up to four oxygen molecules |
| Quaternary structure: chains interact | When one oxygen binds, the shape of the whole molecule changes slightly, making it easier for the next oxygen to bind (cooperative binding), so haemoglobin loads oxygen readily in the lungs |
| Oxygen binds reversibly to iron(II) | Oxygen is released where its concentration is low, in respiring tissues |
| Compact, spherical shape | Many molecules packed into each cell; red cells themselves have no nucleus, leaving more room |

How the percentage saturation of haemoglobin changes with the partial pressure of oxygen (the dissociation curve) and the Bohr shift (Bohr effect) are in the gas transport lesson.

:::example{title="Sickle-cell haemoglobin"}
In sickle-cell haemoglobin ($Hb^S$) the sixth amino acid of each β chain is valine instead of glutamic acid. Glutamic acid has a charged, hydrophilic R group; valine has a non-polar, hydrophobic one, and it lies on the outside of the molecule.

When oxygen concentration is low, the hydrophobic valine of one deoxygenated $Hb^S$ molecule sticks to a hydrophobic region of another. Molecules link into long fibres that distort red blood cells into a sickle shape. Sickled cells carry less oxygen, are fragile and can block capillaries. This shows the chain of cause and effect: change in primary structure → change in surface R group → different interactions → altered behaviour of the protein → disease.
:::

## Collagen

### Structure

1. **Primary structure**: each polypeptide chain (about 1000 amino acids long) has **glycine at every third position**, often with proline or hydroxyproline in the other positions: a repeating Gly–X–Y pattern.
2. **Each chain** is coiled into a narrow, left-handed helix (not an α-helix).
3. **Three chains** wind around each other to form a **triple helix**: this is the collagen molecule (its quaternary structure). Glycine, with the smallest R group (a hydrogen atom), fits into the cramped centre of the triple helix, allowing the three chains to pack very closely. The chains are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between them.
4. **Covalent cross-links** form between the side chains of amino acids (lysine-derived groups) in neighbouring collagen molecules.
5. Molecules lie side by side in a **staggered** arrangement, so the ends of molecules do not line up; this forms **fibrils**.
6. Many fibrils are bundled into **fibres**.

### How the structure suits its function

- The tightly wound triple helix and many hydrogen bonds resist stretching.
- Covalent cross-links between molecules give great **tensile strength** (resistance to pulling forces).
- The staggered arrangement means there are no lines of weakness where all the molecule ends meet.
- Collagen is insoluble and does not react readily, so it is stable for long periods.

Collagen is found in **tendons** (joining muscle to bone, where it must withstand the pull of the muscle), **skin**, **bone**, cartilage, the walls of **arteries** (resisting the high pressure of blood), and the cornea.

:::worked-solution[Counting amino acids in haemoglobin and collagen]
1. Haemoglobin has two α chains of 141 amino acids and two β chains of 146: $2 \times 141 + 2 \times 146 = 574$ amino acids.
2. Each chain of n amino acids has $n - 1$ peptide bonds, so four chains have $574 - 4 = 570$ peptide bonds.
3. A collagen chain of 1050 amino acids with glycine at every third position contains $1050 \div 3 = 350$ glycines.
4. A collagen molecule of three such chains therefore contains $3 \times 350 = 1050$ glycine residues, one third of all its amino acids.
:::

## Comparing the two proteins

| Feature | Haemoglobin | Collagen |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Globular | Fibrous |
| Solubility | Soluble | Insoluble |
| Chains | Four (2 α, 2 β) | Three, in a triple helix |
| Prosthetic group | Haem (with $\text{Fe}^{2+}$) | None |
| Sequence | Irregular | Repeating, glycine every third |
| Function | Transport of oxygen | Structural strength |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying haemoglobin has one iron atom"}
There are four haem groups, each with one iron(II) ion, so one molecule carries up to four oxygen molecules.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing collagen chains as α-helices"}
Each collagen chain forms its own tight helix, and three of these wind together into a triple helix; they are not α-helices.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying collagen's strength comes only from hydrogen bonds"}
Hydrogen bonds hold the triple helix together, but the covalent cross-links between molecules and the staggered fibrils give its high tensile strength.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying oxygen binds to the protein chains"}
Oxygen binds to the iron(II) ion in each haem group.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. ZIMSEC Paper 3 often asks you to compare the structure and functions of haemoglobin and collagen.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* the structure of haemoglobin through the four levels and the haem group. *Explain* how a feature suits the function: always give the consequence (soluble → high concentration in red cells; cross-links → tensile strength). *Suggest* the effect of a changed amino acid: work through R group → bonds → shape → function.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the structure of haemoglobin and collagen and how each suits its function. ZIMSEC also names myoglobin and fetal haemoglobin, which are covered with oxygen transport.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Haemoglobin: globular, soluble, two α and two β chains (quaternary), each with a haem group holding iron(II) that binds one oxygen molecule reversibly.
- Its hydrophilic outer R groups make it soluble; cooperative binding helps loading of oxygen in the lungs.
- In sickle-cell haemoglobin, valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 of the β chain, causing fibres to form at low oxygen.
- Collagen: three chains with glycine every third residue form a triple helix held by hydrogen bonds; covalent cross-links join molecules into staggered fibrils and fibres.
- Collagen's tensile strength suits tendons, skin, bone and artery walls.
:::
