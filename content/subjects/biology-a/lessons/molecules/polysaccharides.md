## Introduction

A maize grain is mostly starch; your liver stores glycogen between meals; a cotton fibre from a field in Gokwe is almost pure cellulose. All three are polymers of glucose, yet one is a food store you can digest in minutes and another is a fibre strong enough to weave into cloth. The difference lies in which form of glucose is used and how the units are linked.

You need the previous lesson: α- and β-glucose, glycosidic bonds, condensation and hydrolysis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Starch"}
The storage polysaccharide of plants: a mixture of amylose (unbranched chains of α-glucose) and amylopectin (branched chains of α-glucose).
:::

:::definition{term="Glycogen"}
The storage polysaccharide of animals and fungi: highly branched chains of α-glucose with α-1,4 and α-1,6 glycosidic bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="Cellulose"}
The structural polysaccharide of plant cell walls: long unbranched chains of β-glucose joined by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds, held together in microfibrils by hydrogen bonds.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Bonds to learn"}
Amylose: α-1,4 only. Amylopectin and glycogen: α-1,4 in the chains and α-1,6 at the branch points. Cellulose: β-1,4, with every other glucose unit rotated by 180°.
:::

## Storage polysaccharides: starch and glycogen

### Amylose

Amylose is a long, unbranched chain of several hundred to a few thousand α-glucose units joined by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds. The angle of the α-1,4 bond makes the chain coil into a **helix**, held in shape by hydrogen bonds between –OH groups inside the coil. The helix is compact, and it is where iodine ions fit to give the blue-black colour of the starch test.

### Amylopectin

Amylopectin also has chains of α-1,4-linked α-glucose, but every 24–30 units or so a side branch is attached by an **α-1,6 glycosidic bond** (carbon 1 of the branch joins carbon 6 of the main chain). Starch grains usually contain more amylopectin than amylose.

### Glycogen

Glycogen is built like amylopectin but is **more highly branched**, with a branch about every 8–12 glucose units, so its chains are shorter and the molecule is a dense, roughly spherical granule. It is stored in liver and muscle cells.

### How the structures suit storage

1. **Insoluble**: large molecules that do not dissolve, so they do not lower the water potential of the cell and cause osmotic water uptake (they are osmotically inactive), and they cannot diffuse out of the cell.
2. **Compact**: helices and branched granules pack a lot of glucose into a small space.
3. **Easily hydrolysed**: α-glycosidic bonds are broken by enzymes (amylase, then maltase; glycogen phosphorylase in cells) to release glucose for respiration.
4. **Many ends**: enzymes remove glucose units from the ends of chains. The more branches, the more ends, so glucose can be released quickly. This suits glycogen in animals, which have higher and more variable energy demands than plants.

:::worked-solution[Comparing the number of chain ends]
1. Consider two molecules each of 1200 glucose units: a glycogen molecule branched about every 10 units and an amylopectin molecule branched about every 25 units.
2. Branch points: glycogen $1200 \div 10 = 120$; amylopectin $1200 \div 25 = 48$.
3. Each branch adds an end at which enzymes can work, so glycogen has about 121 ends and amylopectin about 49.
4. Ratio $121 \div 49 \approx 2.5$: in this simple model glycogen offers about two and a half times as many sites for enzymes, so glucose can be mobilised faster.
:::

## Structural polysaccharide: cellulose

Cellulose is made of **β-glucose** joined by **β-1,4 glycosidic bonds**. Because the –OH on carbon 1 of β-glucose is above the ring, each glucose unit must be rotated through 180° relative to its neighbour to form the bond. The result is a **straight, unbranched chain** rather than a helix.

Its strength builds up in levels:

1. –OH groups project from both sides of each straight chain.
2. Many **hydrogen bonds** form between –OH groups of neighbouring parallel chains.
3. Many parallel chains bonded together (textbooks often quote about 60–70) form a **microfibril**.
4. Microfibrils are bundled into **fibres**, laid down in layers at different angles in the cell wall and set in a matrix of other polysaccharides.

Individually each hydrogen bond is weak, but there are so many that cellulose has a very high **tensile strength**. The cell wall resists the pressure of the cell contents when the cell takes in water, so plant cells become turgid instead of bursting, which supports non-woody plants. Because there are spaces between the fibres, the wall remains fully permeable.

Mammals have no enzyme to hydrolyse β-1,4 bonds. Cattle and goats digest the cellulose of grass only with the help of microorganisms in their rumen.

:::example{title="Explaining a structure from data"}
A table gives information about three polysaccharides from a maize plant and a goat.

| Polysaccharide | Monomer | Bonds | Shape | Branch frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | α-glucose | 1,4 only | Helix | None |
| Q | β-glucose | 1,4 only | Straight chains in microfibrils | None |
| R | α-glucose | 1,4 and 1,6 | Dense granule | About every 10 units |

P is amylose, Q is cellulose and R is glycogen (it is from the goat, and is more highly branched than amylopectin). Q's straight chains can lie side by side and hydrogen bond into microfibrils, giving strength; P's helix and R's branched granule are compact stores, and R's many ends allow rapid release of glucose for the goat's muscles and liver.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cellulose is a storage molecule"}
Cellulose is structural. Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals and fungi) are the storage polysaccharides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying glycogen is made of β-glucose"}
Starch and glycogen are both polymers of α-glucose. Only cellulose uses β-glucose.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining insolubility vaguely"}
Do not just write "it is insoluble so it is good for storage". Say that it does not affect the water potential of the cell (no osmotic uptake of water) and does not diffuse out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cellulose chains are joined by glycosidic bonds"}
Within a chain the bonds are β-1,4 glycosidic; **between** chains they are hydrogen bonds.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays often ask you to compare starch, glycogen and cellulose and relate structure to function.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Compare*: use a table or matched statements (monomer, bonds, branching, shape, function). *Explain* how structure suits function: one feature, one consequence each time ("branched, so many ends, so rapid hydrolysis"). *Describe* cellulose: β-glucose, alternate units inverted, straight chains, hydrogen bonds, microfibrils, fibres.
:::

Both boards require the structure of amylose, amylopectin, glycogen and cellulose and the relationship of each structure to its function.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Amylose: unbranched α-1,4 chain coiled into a helix; amylopectin: α-1,4 chains with α-1,6 branches.
- Glycogen: like amylopectin but more highly branched, so it has more ends for rapid hydrolysis.
- Storage polysaccharides are compact, insoluble and osmotically inactive, and easily hydrolysed to glucose.
- Cellulose: β-1,4 chains with alternate units inverted, straight, hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils and fibres of high tensile strength.
- Cellulose walls stop plant cells bursting and allow turgor to support the plant.
:::
