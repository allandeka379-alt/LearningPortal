## Introduction

Enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin, collagen, insulin, the channel proteins in your membranes and the keratin of your hair are all proteins. They do very different jobs because each has its own precise three-dimensional shape, and that shape is decided by the order of amino acids in the chain. A change of a single amino acid can be enough to cause a disease such as sickle cell anaemia, which is common in parts of Africa where malaria has been present.

You should know condensation and hydrolysis from the carbohydrates lesson and the biuret test.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Amino acid"}
The monomer of proteins: a central carbon atom bonded to an amine group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable R group.
:::

:::definition{term="Peptide bond"}
The covalent C–N bond formed by condensation between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amine group of another.
:::

:::definition{term="Polypeptide"}
An unbranched chain of many amino acids joined by peptide bonds. A protein consists of one or more polypeptides folded into a specific shape.
:::

:::definition{term="Denaturation"}
A change in the three-dimensional shape of a protein, caused by breaking the bonds that hold its secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure, so that it can no longer carry out its function; the primary structure is unchanged.
:::

## Amino acids

About 20 different amino acids are found in proteins. All share the same basic structure; they differ only in their **R group**, which may be as small as a hydrogen atom (glycine) or a methyl group (alanine), or larger, and may be non-polar (hydrophobic), polar, or charged (acidic or basic). Some R groups contain sulfur (cysteine).

## Peptide bonds

1. The –OH of the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with an H of the amine group of the next.
2. A molecule of water is removed (condensation).
3. A **peptide bond** (–CO–NH–) forms between the carbon of one amino acid and the nitrogen of the next, making a **dipeptide**.
4. Repeating this builds a polypeptide with a free amine group at one end (N-terminal) and a free carboxyl group at the other (C-terminal).
5. Proteases and peptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds, adding water, for example in digestion.

:::worked-solution[Counting bonds and masses in peptides]
1. Glycine has $M_r = 75$ and alanine has $M_r = 89$. The dipeptide formed by condensation has $M_r = 75 + 89 - 18 = 146$.
2. A polypeptide of n amino acids in one chain has $n - 1$ peptide bonds.
3. Insulin has 51 amino acids in **two** chains, so it has $51 - 2 = 49$ peptide bonds, and 49 water molecules are needed to hydrolyse them all.
4. With 20 amino acids available at each position, the number of different possible tripeptides is $20^3 = 8000$, which shows how enormous the variety of possible proteins is.
:::

## The four levels of structure

### Primary structure

The **sequence** of amino acids in the polypeptide chain, held by peptide bonds. It is coded for by a gene, and it determines every higher level of structure.

### Secondary structure

Regular folding of parts of the chain, held by **hydrogen bonds** between the –C=O of one peptide bond and the –N–H of another (the R groups are not involved):

- the **α-helix**: the chain coils, with hydrogen bonds between groups four amino acids apart;
- the **β-pleated sheet**: lengths of chain lie side by side, hydrogen-bonded to each other in a zig-zag sheet.

### Tertiary structure

The further folding of the whole polypeptide into a precise three-dimensional shape, held by interactions between **R groups**:

| Interaction | Between | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrophobic interactions | Non-polar R groups, which cluster together in the centre of the molecule away from water | Weak individually; important overall |
| Hydrogen bonds | Polar R groups | Weak, but many |
| Ionic bonds | Positively and negatively charged R groups | Stronger than hydrogen bonds; broken by changes in pH |
| Disulfide bonds | The sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups (covalent S–S) | Strong |

### Quaternary structure

The association of **two or more polypeptide chains** (sometimes with a non-protein **prosthetic group**) into one functional protein, held by the same interactions as tertiary structure. Haemoglobin (four chains with haem groups) and insulin (two chains joined by disulfide bonds) have quaternary structure; proteins with one chain do not.

:::example{title="Predicting the effect of changing one amino acid"}
A protein folds so that non-polar R groups are buried in the centre and charged R groups are on the surface. A mutation replaces a surface glutamic acid, which has a charged R group, with valine, which has a non-polar R group.

Prediction: the new non-polar R group on the surface is hydrophobic, so the protein becomes less soluble in that region, and it may stick to similar regions on other molecules. In haemoglobin this exact substitution (in the β chain) produces sickle-cell haemoglobin, whose molecules link into long fibres when oxygen is low and distort red blood cells. One change in primary structure has changed the behaviour of the whole protein.
:::

## Globular and fibrous proteins

| Feature | Globular proteins | Fibrous proteins |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Compact, roughly spherical | Long strands or sheets |
| Solubility | Usually soluble: hydrophilic R groups on the outside, hydrophobic inside | Usually insoluble |
| Sequence | Irregular sequence | Often repetitive sequence |
| Role | Physiological: enzymes, antibodies, hormones (insulin), transport (haemoglobin) | Structural: collagen, keratin, elastin |
| Sensitivity | Easily denatured | More stable |

## Denaturation

High temperature increases the kinetic energy of the molecule, so it vibrates more and **hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds** break; changes of **pH** alter the charges on R groups and break ionic and hydrogen bonds. Heavy metal ions and some solvents also disrupt the bonds. The protein unfolds and loses its specific shape, so an enzyme loses its active site and an antibody can no longer bind its antigen. Peptide bonds are not broken, so the primary structure remains. Denaturation by heat is usually irreversible, as when egg white turns solid on cooking.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying secondary structure is held by R groups"}
Hydrogen bonds in α-helices and β-sheets form between the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone. R groups determine tertiary structure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds"}
Denaturation breaks the weaker bonds of higher-level structure. Breaking peptide bonds is hydrolysis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every protein quaternary"}
Only proteins of two or more polypeptides have quaternary structure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing disulfide bonds with hydrogen bonds"}
Disulfide bonds are covalent S–S bonds between cysteine R groups and are much stronger than hydrogen bonds.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An amino acid has an amine group, a carboxyl group, H and an R group on a central carbon; 20 R groups occur in proteins.
- Peptide bonds form by condensation and are broken by hydrolysis.
- Primary: sequence; secondary: α-helix and β-sheet (backbone hydrogen bonds); tertiary: 3D folding (hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds between R groups); quaternary: more than one chain.
- Globular proteins are soluble with physiological roles; fibrous proteins are insoluble and structural.
- Denaturation by heat or pH breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds, changing the shape and function, but not the primary structure.
:::
