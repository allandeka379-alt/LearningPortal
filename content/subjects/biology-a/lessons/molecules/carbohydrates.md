## Introduction

Sugar cane grown in the Lowveld around Chiredzi and Triangle stores sucrose in its stems; milk contains lactose; germinating sorghum grains produce maltose, the sugar used in brewing traditional opaque beer. All three are disaccharides, built from simple sugars joined by the same kind of bond. Understanding that bond explains digestion, the food tests and how polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose are built.

You should already know that carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and the Benedict's test.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Monomer"}
A small molecule that can be joined to other similar molecules to form a polymer, for example a monosaccharide, an amino acid or a nucleotide.
:::

:::definition{term="Polymer"}
A large molecule made of many repeating monomer units joined by covalent bonds.
:::

:::definition{term="Macromolecule"}
A very large molecule with a high relative molecular mass, such as a polysaccharide, protein or nucleic acid.
:::

:::definition{term="Monosaccharide"}
A single sugar unit with the general formula $(\text{CH}_2\text{O})_n$, where n is 3 to 7; for example glucose, fructose, galactose (hexoses, n = 6) and ribose (a pentose, n = 5).
:::

:::definition{term="Disaccharide"}
A sugar made of two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond; for example maltose, sucrose and lactose.
:::

:::definition{term="Polysaccharide"}
A polymer of many monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds, such as starch, glycogen and cellulose.
:::

:::definition{term="Glycosidic bond"}
The covalent C–O–C bond formed between two monosaccharides in a condensation reaction.
:::

## Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are sweet, soluble and small enough to cross membranes through carrier proteins. They are classified by the number of carbon atoms: **trioses** (3C), **pentoses** (5C, such as ribose and deoxyribose in nucleic acids) and **hexoses** (6C, such as glucose, fructose and galactose).

### Alpha- and beta-glucose

In solution, glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) exists mainly as a six-membered ring of five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom. Carbon 1 is to the right of the ring oxygen, and carbons are numbered clockwise; carbon 6 sits above the ring, attached to carbon 5.

- In **alpha-glucose** (α-glucose) the –OH group on carbon 1 is **below** the plane of the ring.
- In **beta-glucose** (β-glucose) the –OH group on carbon 1 is **above** the plane of the ring.

This single difference has big consequences: polymers of α-glucose (starch, glycogen) coil and are easily digested, while polymers of β-glucose (cellulose) form straight chains that most animals cannot digest.

| Carbon | Group attached (in α-glucose) |
|---|---|
| 1 | H above, OH below the ring |
| 2 | H above, OH below |
| 3 | OH above, H below |
| 4 | H above, OH below |
| 5 | CH₂OH above, H below |

## Condensation and hydrolysis

A **condensation reaction** joins two molecules with the removal of a molecule of water. When two monosaccharides join, the –OH on carbon 1 of one reacts with an –OH (usually on carbon 4) of the other; a water molecule is removed and a glycosidic bond forms.

A **hydrolysis reaction** breaks a bond by adding a molecule of water. Glycosidic bonds are hydrolysed in digestion (by enzymes such as maltase, sucrase and lactase) and in the laboratory by heating with dilute acid.

| Disaccharide | Monosaccharides | Bond | Reducing? | Where it is found |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maltose | α-glucose + α-glucose | α-1,4 glycosidic | Yes | Germinating seeds; product of starch digestion |
| Sucrose | α-glucose + fructose | 1,2 glycosidic | No | Transported in phloem; stored in sugar cane |
| Lactose | Galactose + glucose | 1,4 glycosidic | Yes | Milk |

### Why sucrose is non-reducing

Reducing sugars can reduce copper(II) ions because they have a free reactive group at carbon 1 (in glucose) or carbon 2 (in fructose). In maltose and lactose one of the two units still has a free carbon 1, so they are reducing sugars. In sucrose the glycosidic bond joins carbon 1 of glucose to carbon 2 of fructose, so both reactive groups are used in the bond and sucrose cannot reduce Benedict's solution. Hydrolysis frees them again, which is why the non-reducing sugar test boils the sample with acid first.

:::worked-solution[Relative molecular mass of maltose]
1. Glucose is $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$: $M_r = (6 \times 12) + (12 \times 1) + (6 \times 16) = 180$.
2. Two glucose molecules have a total $M_r$ of $2 \times 180 = 360$.
3. Condensation removes one water molecule, $M_r = 18$.
4. $M_r$ of maltose $= 360 - 18 = 342$, matching its formula $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$.
5. For a chain of three glucose units, two bonds form and two waters are lost: $3 \times 180 - 2 \times 18 = 504$.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting the products of hydrolysis"}
Lactase is an enzyme in the small intestine. A sample of milk is treated with lactase and tested.

Prediction: lactase hydrolyses the glycosidic bond in lactose, using one water molecule per bond, to give glucose and galactose. Both are reducing sugars, so a Benedict's test after treatment would still be positive but, because each lactose gives two monosaccharides, the colour would be stronger for the same volume. People with low lactase activity cannot hydrolyse lactose fully; lactose that reaches the large intestine is fermented by bacteria, which can cause discomfort.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hydrolysis 'removes water'"}
Hydrolysis **adds** water to break a bond; condensation **removes** water to make one.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a glycosidic bond a peptide or ester bond"}
Glycosidic bonds join sugars; peptide bonds join amino acids; ester bonds join fatty acids to glycerol.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing α- and β-glucose the wrong way round"}
Remember "**a**lpha **a**way": in α-glucose the OH on carbon 1 points down, on the opposite side from carbon 6. In β-glucose it points up, on the same side as carbon 6.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking all disaccharides are non-reducing"}
Maltose and lactose are reducing sugars. Only sucrose (of the three you need) is non-reducing.
:::

ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2: describe or identify α- and β-glucose, explain condensation and hydrolysis, and link sucrose to the non-reducing sugar test. ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays often combine this with polysaccharides.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* the formation of a glycosidic bond: name the carbons (1 and 4), the removal of water and the term condensation. *Explain* why sucrose is non-reducing: both reactive groups are used in the bond. *Draw* is replaced here by describing the ring in words: say where the OH on carbon 1 is.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose) are monomers; disaccharides and polysaccharides are made by joining them.
- α-glucose has the OH on carbon 1 below the ring; β-glucose has it above.
- Condensation removes water to form a glycosidic bond; hydrolysis adds water to break it.
- Maltose = glucose + glucose; sucrose = glucose + fructose; lactose = glucose + galactose.
- Maltose and lactose are reducing sugars; sucrose is non-reducing because both reactive groups are in the glycosidic bond.
:::
