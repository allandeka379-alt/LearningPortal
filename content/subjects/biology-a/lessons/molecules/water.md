## Introduction

About 70% of a typical cell is water, and every reaction in it takes place in solution. When the Zambezi valley bakes at over 40 °C, elephants cool themselves by spraying water on their skin; in winter the surface of a pond in the Eastern Highlands may cool sharply at night while the fish below stay alive. Each of these depends on the unusual properties of one small molecule, and almost all of them come from a single feature: the hydrogen bond.

You should know the basic idea of covalent bonds and that cells contain water and dissolved substances.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Polar molecule"}
A molecule with an uneven distribution of charge, so that one part is slightly negative (δ−) and another slightly positive (δ+).
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrogen bond"}
A weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen atom of one molecule (or group) and a slightly negative atom, such as oxygen or nitrogen, of another.
:::

:::definition{term="Specific heat capacity"}
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C; for water about 4.2 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.
:::

:::definition{term="Latent heat of vaporisation"}
The energy needed to change 1 g of a liquid into vapour without a change in temperature; for water about 2.4 kJ g⁻¹ at body temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Cohesion"}
The attraction of molecules of the same kind to each other; in water it is due to hydrogen bonds.
:::

## The water molecule and hydrogen bonds

A water molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) has two hydrogen atoms covalently bonded to one oxygen atom. Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, so:

1. the oxygen atom carries a small negative charge (δ−);
2. each hydrogen atom carries a small positive charge (δ+);
3. the molecule is bent (V-shaped), so the charges are not cancelled out: water is **polar**.

The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbour, forming a **hydrogen bond**. Each hydrogen bond is weak and short-lived, but liquid water contains enormous numbers of them, constantly breaking and re-forming. Together they give water properties very different from those of other small molecules: without hydrogen bonding, water would boil far below room temperature.

## Properties and their biological importance

### 1. Solvent

Polar and charged substances dissolve in water because water molecules cluster around them: the δ− oxygens face positive ions such as $\text{Na}^+$, and the δ+ hydrogens face negative ions such as $\text{Cl}^-$. Polar molecules such as glucose and amino acids form hydrogen bonds with water.

- **Reactions** in cells take place in solution, where molecules can move and collide.
- **Transport**: blood plasma, tissue fluid, xylem and phloem sap carry dissolved glucose, ions, urea, hormones and sucrose.
- **Excretion**: urea is removed dissolved in urine.
- Non-polar substances (lipids) do not dissolve, which allows membranes to form.

### 2. High specific heat capacity

Much energy is needed to break hydrogen bonds before the molecules can move faster, so water heats up and cools down slowly.

- The temperature inside organisms stays relatively stable, which keeps enzymes near their optimum.
- Lakes and rivers such as Lake Kariba change temperature far less than the air above them, giving aquatic organisms a stable habitat.

### 3. High latent heat of vaporisation

Much energy is needed to break the hydrogen bonds so that molecules can escape as vapour. When water evaporates from a surface, it takes this energy from the surface, cooling it.

- **Sweating** and **panting** cool mammals; **transpiration** cools leaves.
- Only a small mass of water has to be lost to remove a large amount of heat.

:::worked-solution[Cooling by sweating and the energy to warm water]
1. A farm worker loses 200 g of sweat by evaporation during an afternoon in the field. Latent heat of vaporisation is about 2.4 kJ g⁻¹.
2. Heat removed $= 200 \times 2.4 = 480\ \text{kJ}$.
3. Compare warming water: raising the temperature of 1000 g of water by 5 °C needs $1000 \times 4.2 \times 5 = 21\,000\ \text{J} = 21\ \text{kJ}$.
4. So evaporation of a small mass of water removes far more heat than is needed to change the temperature of a much larger mass, which is why evaporation is such an effective way of cooling.
:::

### 4. Cohesion, adhesion and surface tension

Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together (cohesion) and attract them to other polar surfaces such as cellulose (adhesion).

- In **xylem**, cohesion lets a continuous column of water be pulled up the plant by transpiration (the cohesion-tension mechanism is taught with plant transport).
- **Surface tension** at the water surface supports small animals such as pond skaters and allows mosquito larvae to hang from the surface film.

### 5. Density: ice floats

Water is most dense at about 4 °C. As it cools further and freezes, hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice, so **ice is less dense than liquid water** and floats.

- Ice forms on the surface and insulates the water below, so in cold climates organisms survive under the ice.
- The densest water (4 °C) sinks, and in temperate lakes this helps circulate nutrients and oxygen when seasons change.

### 6. Water as a reactant

Water is a reactant in **hydrolysis** (digestion of starch, proteins and lipids; breaking ATP) and is the source of electrons, hydrogen ions and oxygen in **photosynthesis** (photolysis). It is a product of condensation reactions and of aerobic respiration.

### 7. Water as a habitat (ZIMSEC detail)

Water provides a stable environment for aquatic life: temperature changes slowly, dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide are available, light penetrates the upper layers for photosynthesis, and water supports the bodies of organisms. Kapenta in Lake Kariba, hippopotamuses in the Zambezi and the plankton they depend on all live in this environment.

:::example{title="Explaining a field observation"}
On a hot October afternoon in the lowveld, the air temperature rose from 24 °C at 08:00 to 41 °C at 14:00, while the water in a shallow dam rose only from 22 °C to 26 °C.

Explanation: water has a high specific heat capacity because energy must be used to break hydrogen bonds before the molecules move faster; the same energy input therefore raises the temperature of the water far less than that of the air. Some energy is also lost as water evaporates from the surface (latent heat). Fish and invertebrates in the dam experience much smaller temperature changes than land animals.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing hydrogen bonds with covalent bonds"}
The O–H bonds **within** a water molecule are covalent. Hydrogen bonds are weak attractions **between** molecules.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sweat cools because it is cold"}
Sweat cools because evaporation absorbs a large amount of heat energy (latent heat) from the skin.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up specific heat capacity and latent heat"}
Specific heat capacity is about changing temperature; latent heat of vaporisation is about changing liquid to gas at the same temperature.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ice sinks"}
Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats and insulates the water below.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays commonly ask for the properties of water and their importance to living organisms.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain* a property: start from hydrogen bonding, state the property, then give a specific biological consequence. *Describe* the structure: polar, δ+ hydrogens, δ− oxygen, bent shape, hydrogen bonds between molecules. *Suggest* why an organism benefits: link to stable temperature, transport or cooling.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the polar water molecule, hydrogen bonding and its solvent and thermal properties. Water as an environment for organisms is a ZIMSEC detail; Cambridge questions focus on the solvent and thermal properties.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water is polar (δ− oxygen, δ+ hydrogens), so hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
- As a solvent it allows reactions in solution and transport of dissolved substances in blood, xylem and phloem.
- High specific heat capacity keeps the temperature of organisms and water bodies stable.
- High latent heat of vaporisation makes sweating and transpiration effective cooling.
- Cohesion supports water columns in xylem; ice floats and insulates; water is a reactant in hydrolysis and photosynthesis and a habitat.
:::
