## Introduction

A maize plant in a field near Gweru can lose several litres of water on a hot afternoon, yet its leaves stay firm, and the sugars made in those leaves reach the growing cob a metre below. Two tissues make this possible: **xylem**, which carries water and mineral ions up from the roots, and **phloem**, which carries sucrose and amino acids from where they are made or stored to where they are used. Together they form the **vascular tissue**, arranged in **vascular bundles** in stems and leaves and in a central **stele** in roots.

Plants need a transport system for the same reason large animals do: a tree or a maize plant is far too large, and its surface area to volume ratio far too small, for diffusion alone to supply every cell. Roots take in water but cannot photosynthesise; leaves photosynthesise but cannot reach soil water. You should already know the structure of a plant cell, the cellulose cell wall, and how to use a light microscope and an eyepiece graticule.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Vascular bundle"}
A strand of transport tissue in a stem or leaf containing xylem and phloem, usually separated by a layer of cambium in dicotyledonous stems.
:::

:::definition{term="Xylem vessel element"}
A dead, elongated cell with lignified walls and no end walls; many are joined end to end to form a continuous hollow tube (a xylem vessel) that carries water and mineral ions upwards.
:::

:::definition{term="Sieve tube element"}
A living, elongated phloem cell with no nucleus and few organelles, joined end to end with others through perforated end walls (sieve plates) to form a sieve tube that carries assimilates.
:::

:::definition{term="Companion cell"}
A phloem cell with a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria, linked to a sieve tube element by many plasmodesmata, which loads assimilates into the sieve tube and keeps the sieve tube element alive.
:::

:::definition{term="Plan diagram"}
A low-power drawing that shows the outlines and relative proportions of the tissues in a section, with no individual cells drawn.
:::

## Distribution of the tissues in a dicotyledonous plant

The positions of xylem and phloem differ between organs, and each arrangement suits the stresses that organ faces.

### Root

In a transverse section (TS) of a young dicotyledonous root, the vascular tissue is packed into a **central stele**. Working from the outside in:

1. **Epidermis** (piliferous layer), with root hairs near the tip.
2. **Cortex**: a wide region of large, thin-walled parenchyma cells with air spaces; it often stores starch.
3. **Endodermis**: a single ring of cells around the stele, with the Casparian strip in its walls.
4. **Pericycle**: one or more layers just inside the endodermis; lateral roots grow from here.
5. **Xylem** in the centre, often shaped like a star or cross with three to five arms.
6. **Phloem** in separate groups lying between the arms of the xylem.

A central core of vascular tissue resists the **pulling** forces on a root anchored in soil, much as a rope does.

### Stem

In a TS of a herbaceous dicotyledonous stem (for example a young sunflower or bean stem), the vascular bundles are arranged in a **ring** near the outside:

1. **Epidermis** with a waxy cuticle.
2. **Cortex**, often with collenchyma just under the epidermis for support.
3. A ring of **vascular bundles**, each with **phloem towards the outside**, **xylem towards the centre** and a strip of **cambium** between them.
4. A central **pith** of parenchyma.

A ring of strong, lignified tissue near the edge resists **bending** in the wind, like a hollow tube.

### Leaf

In the leaf, the **midrib** contains a large vascular bundle, and smaller veins branch through the mesophyll. In each bundle the **xylem lies on the upper side** (towards the upper epidermis) and the **phloem on the lower side**. The network of veins brings water within a few cells of every mesophyll cell and supports the thin lamina.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: distribution of xylem and phloem in TS of roots, stems and leaves of herbaceous dicotyledons, plan diagrams, and the structure of vessel elements, sieve tube elements and companion cells. ZIMSEC also compares monocotyledons: in a maize stem the vascular bundles are **scattered** through the ground tissue rather than in a ring, and lack cambium; a monocotyledonous root has many groups of xylem and phloem arranged in a ring around a central pith.
:::

## Structure related to function

### Xylem

Xylem tissue contains **vessel elements**, **tracheids** (narrower cells with tapering ends, important in conifers), **fibres** for support and some living **parenchyma**. The vessel elements are the main water-conducting cells in flowering plants.

| Feature of xylem vessel elements | How it suits the function |
|---|---|
| Dead at maturity, no cytoplasm or organelles | Nothing obstructs the flow of water; the vessel is an empty tube |
| End walls broken down completely | Elements form a continuous tube, so water moves as an unbroken column |
| Walls thickened with lignin (rings, spirals or networks) | Strong and waterproof; stops the vessel collapsing under the tension of the transpiration pull |
| Pits (unlignified areas) in the walls | Water and ions move sideways between vessels and into surrounding cells |
| Narrow lumen (about 20-200 µm across in many plants) | Supports capillarity and adhesion; a column is less likely to break than in a wide tube |

### Phloem

Phloem contains **sieve tube elements**, **companion cells**, parenchyma and fibres.

| Feature | How it suits the function |
|---|---|
| Sieve tube element: no nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm against the wall | More space for the flow of sap; little obstruction |
| Sieve plates: end walls with many pores | Sap passes from element to element while the plates hold the wall in shape |
| Living, with a cell surface membrane | Can hold sucrose at a high concentration and keep a high hydrostatic pressure |
| Companion cell: nucleus, many mitochondria and ribosomes | Supplies ATP and proteins; actively loads sucrose using ATP |
| Many plasmodesmata between companion cell and sieve tube element | Assimilates and molecules pass easily between the two cells |

The key contrast: **xylem vessel elements are dead, lignified, open tubes** carrying water up by a pull from the leaves; **sieve tube elements are living cells** that depend on companion cells and carry sap in either direction under pressure.

## Drawing plan diagrams

A plan diagram shows **tissues, not cells**. Draw the outline of each tissue region in its correct position and proportion, using sharp, continuous pencil lines and no shading. Label with ruled lines that do not cross. In a stem bundle, phloem, cambium and xylem are drawn as adjacent regions within one bundle outline; in a root, the endodermis is shown as a single line around the stele with the xylem and phloem regions inside it.

:::worked-solution[Actual width of a xylem vessel from a photomicrograph]
A photomicrograph of a sunflower stem TS has a magnification of ×600. One xylem vessel measures 24 mm across in the photomicrograph.
1. Convert the image width to µm: $24\ \text{mm} \times 1000 = 24\,000\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. Actual size $= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}} = \dfrac{24\,000}{600}$.
3. Actual width $= 40\ \mu\text{m}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Measuring a sieve tube element with a graticule]
At ×100, 30 divisions of a stage micrometer (each 10 µm) line up exactly with 24 divisions of the eyepiece graticule. A sieve tube element in a longitudinal section measures 9 eyepiece divisions long.
1. 30 stage divisions $= 30 \times 10 = 300\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. One eyepiece division $= 300 \div 24 = 12.5\ \mu\text{m}$.
3. Length of the element $= 9 \times 12.5 = 112.5\ \mu\text{m}$.
4. If this element is drawn 45 mm long, the drawing magnification $= 45\,000 \div 112.5 = \times 400$.
:::

:::example{title="Root or stem?"}
A student is given a TS described as "a central star-shaped core of thick-walled cells with groups of small thin-walled cells between its arms, surrounded by a single ring of cells and then a wide cortex". **Identify** the organ and **explain** your answer. It is a **root**: the vascular tissue is in a central stele with xylem in the centre and phloem between the xylem arms, enclosed by an endodermis. A stem would have separate bundles arranged in a ring with phloem outside xylem.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying xylem vessels contain cytoplasm"}
Mature vessel elements are **dead** and empty. Only phloem sieve tube elements are living, and even they have no nucleus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing cells in a plan diagram"}
A plan diagram shows tissue boundaries only. Drawing individual cells, or shading, loses marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting phloem inside xylem in a stem bundle"}
In a dicotyledonous stem bundle the **phloem is outside** (towards the epidermis) and the xylem inside. In a leaf the xylem is on the upper side.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling sieve plates 'end walls removed'"}
Xylem vessel elements lose their end walls completely; sieve tube elements keep perforated end walls called **sieve plates**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to identify tissues in a described section and to *explain* how a feature suits its function. Pair every feature with its function: "lignin, so the vessel does not collapse under tension".
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3 often ask for a plan diagram of a stem, root or leaf TS. Draw large (at least half the space), show only tissue outlines in the right proportions and label with ruled lines. To *compare* xylem and phloem, give both sides of each difference.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Roots have a central stele (xylem star with phloem between its arms) inside the endodermis; dicotyledonous stems have bundles in a ring with phloem outside xylem; in leaves xylem is uppermost.
- Xylem vessel elements are dead, lignified, with no end walls and with pits: a continuous, strong, waterproof tube.
- Sieve tube elements are living, have sieve plates and few organelles; companion cells have many mitochondria and plasmodesmata.
- Plan diagrams show tissue outlines in correct proportions, with no cells and no shading.
- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification, after converting to the same unit.
:::
