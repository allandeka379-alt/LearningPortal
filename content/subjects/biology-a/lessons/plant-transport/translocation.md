## Introduction

A sugar cane stalk in the Lowveld around Chiredzi is sweet because its stem cells are packed with sucrose that was made in the leaves weeks earlier. A potato tuber fills with starch made from sucrose that travelled down from the leaves; a maize cob fills with grain the same way. None of these storage organs photosynthesises much itself. The sugar reaches them by **translocation** in the phloem.

Unlike the xylem, which is a set of dead pipes carrying water one way, the phloem is living tissue that can carry sap up or down, and its direction can change with the seasons. You should already know the structure of sieve tube elements and companion cells, water potential and osmosis, active transport and cotransport.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Translocation"}
The movement of assimilates, such as sucrose and amino acids, in the phloem from one part of a plant to another, from sources to sinks, using energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Assimilates"}
Substances made by the plant from the products of photosynthesis, such as sucrose and amino acids, that are transported in the phloem.
:::

:::definition{term="Source"}
A part of the plant that releases assimilates into the phloem, for example a photosynthesising leaf or a storage organ being used up.
:::

:::definition{term="Sink"}
A part of the plant that removes assimilates from the phloem to use or store them, for example a root tip, a growing bud, a developing fruit or seed, or a storage organ being filled.
:::

:::definition{term="Mass flow"}
The movement of a whole body of fluid, with everything dissolved in it, from a region of higher hydrostatic pressure to a region of lower hydrostatic pressure.
:::

## Sources and sinks

Sucrose is the main sugar transported. It is a non-reducing sugar, so it is less reactive than glucose and is not used up by metabolism on the way, and being a disaccharide it carries more energy per molecule moved.

The same organ can change role. A potato tuber is a **sink** while it is filling with starch in the growing season. When it sprouts the next season, the starch is converted to sucrose and the tuber becomes a **source** for the new shoots. Because sources and sinks can be above or below one another, phloem sap can flow **up or down**, but in any one sieve tube it flows in one direction at a time.

## Loading sucrose at the source

Sucrose is made in mesophyll cells and moves to the companion cells of the leaf veins, partly through plasmodesmata and partly across the apoplast. It is then **actively loaded**:

1. **Proton pumps** in the companion cell membrane use ATP to pump hydrogen ions ($\ce{H+}$) out of the cell into the cell walls, building up a high $\ce{H+}$ concentration outside.
2. The hydrogen ions diffuse back into the companion cell down their concentration gradient through **sucrose-proton cotransporter** proteins.
3. Each $\ce{H+}$ carries a sucrose molecule with it, so sucrose enters the companion cell **against its own concentration gradient**. This is cotransport, an indirect form of active transport.
4. Sucrose then diffuses from the companion cell into the sieve tube element through the many **plasmodesmata**.

This explains why companion cells have so many mitochondria: they must make a lot of ATP for the proton pumps.

## Mass flow from source to sink

The mass flow hypothesis explains how sap moves along the sieve tubes:

1. Loading sucrose at the source **lowers the water potential** of the sieve tube sap.
2. Water enters the sieve tube from the nearby xylem **by osmosis**.
3. This raises the **hydrostatic pressure** in the sieve tube at the source end.
4. At the sink, sucrose is removed (by diffusion or active transport) and used in respiration or converted to starch, so the water potential there is higher and water leaves by osmosis, **lowering the hydrostatic pressure**.
5. Sap flows by **mass flow** down this hydrostatic pressure gradient from source to sink, carrying sucrose, amino acids and other solutes with it.
6. Water that reaches the sink can return to the xylem and be recirculated.

The process needs energy for loading, so translocation is described as an active process even though the flow itself is driven by pressure.

:::

## Evidence for and against mass flow

**Evidence for:**

- **Aphids**: an aphid feeding on a stem inserts its stylet (mouthparts) into a single sieve tube. If the aphid is anaesthetised and its body cut away, sap continues to ooze out of the stylet for hours, showing the sap is under **pressure**. The sap is rich in sucrose, and the rate of exudation is faster nearer the leaves (the source).
- **Ringing**: removing a ring of bark (which contains the phloem) from a woody stem leaves the xylem intact. The stem swells **above** the ring as sugars collect there, and tissues below are starved. Assimilates move in the phloem, not the xylem.
- **Radioactive tracers**: a leaf supplied with $\ce{^{14}CO2}$ makes radioactive sucrose. Autoradiographs then show radioactivity in the phloem, moving towards sinks, at speeds of up to about 1 m per hour, far faster than diffusion could achieve.
- **Metabolic inhibitors**: poisons that stop ATP production in companion cells stop translocation, supporting active loading.

**Evidence against, or not fully explained:**

- Different solutes in the same tissue have been reported moving at different speeds, and in some studies in opposite directions, which simple mass flow in one tube would not predict (opposite flows could be in different sieve tubes).
- Sieve plates would seem to resist flow; the role of sieve plates and of phloem proteins is not fully understood.
- Sucrose moves to some sinks faster than the pressure gradient alone seems to explain.

Mass flow is the most widely accepted explanation, but it may not be the whole story.

:::worked-solution[Rate of translocation from a tracer experiment]
Tendai supplied a bean leaf with $\ce{^{14}CO2}$. Radioactivity was first detected in the stem 60 cm below the leaf 75 minutes later.
1. Rate $= \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \dfrac{60\ \text{cm}}{75\ \text{min}} = 0.8\ \text{cm min}^{-1}$.
2. In cm per hour: $0.8 \times 60 = 48\ \text{cm h}^{-1}$.
3. This is an underestimate of the speed of the sap itself, because the first minutes were spent fixing $\ce{CO2}$, making sucrose and loading it.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a ringing experiment"}
A ring of bark was removed from a young mango branch. Three weeks later the bark just **above** the ring was swollen and had a higher sugar content than bark just **below** it, and fruits below the ring were smaller. **Explain**: the ring removed the phloem, so sucrose moving down from the leaves (source) accumulated above the ring and could not reach the fruits (sinks) below. The leaves stayed fresh because the xylem, deeper in the stem, was intact and still carried water upwards.
:::

## Xylem and phloem transport compared

| Feature | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| Main substances carried | Water and mineral ions | Sucrose, amino acids, other assimilates in water |
| Direction | Upwards only, roots to leaves | Up or down, source to sink |
| Conducting cells | Dead vessel elements, no end walls | Living sieve tube elements with sieve plates |
| Driving force | Transpiration pull (tension, negative pressure); some root pressure | Hydrostatic pressure gradient (positive pressure) created by active loading |
| Energy used by the plant | Passive: energy from the Sun evaporating water | Active: ATP used by companion cells to load sucrose |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying sucrose is pumped directly by ATP"}
ATP drives the **proton pumps**. Sucrose enters by **cotransport** with hydrogen ions moving down their gradient.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying phloem flows only downwards"}
Sap flows from **source to sink**, which can be upwards (to a growing shoot) or downwards (to roots or tubers).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying water enters the sieve tube by active transport"}
Water always moves by **osmosis**, following the lowered water potential caused by loaded sucrose.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 2, a full *explain* answer on mass flow has a chain: loading by cotransport → low water potential → water enters by osmosis → high hydrostatic pressure at source → removal at sink → pressure gradient → mass flow. Missing a link costs a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
In comparison questions (ZIMSEC Papers 1-3, Cambridge Papers 1 and 2), give both sides of each difference: "xylem moves water upwards only, whereas phloem moves assimilates up or down".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Translocation moves assimilates (mainly sucrose and amino acids) in sieve tubes from sources to sinks, up or down.
- Companion cells use ATP-driven proton pumps; sucrose enters with H⁺ through cotransporters, then passes to sieve tubes through plasmodesmata.
- Loading lowers water potential, water enters by osmosis, hydrostatic pressure rises; removal at sinks lowers it; sap moves by mass flow.
- Aphid stylets, ringing and radioactive tracers support mass flow; differing solute speeds and sieve plates are not fully explained.
- Xylem: passive, upward, dead tubes, tension. Phloem: active loading, either direction, living cells, positive pressure.
:::
