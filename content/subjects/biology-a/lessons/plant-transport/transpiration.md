## Introduction

A large tree in the Zambezi valley lifts water many metres from its roots to its highest leaves without a single pump. There are no muscles and no moving parts: the energy comes from the Sun, which evaporates water from the leaves, and the water column is held together by the hydrogen bonds between water molecules. This lesson explains how that works, how we measure it in the laboratory, and how plants of dry places such as the aloes of Matabeleland cut their losses.

Transpiration is often described as an unavoidable cost: the stomata must open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores. But the stream of water it drives also delivers mineral ions to the leaves and cools them. You should already know the structure of a leaf, the properties of water (hydrogen bonding, cohesion, latent heat of vaporisation), the structure of xylem vessels, and how water reaches the root xylem.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transpiration"}
The loss of water vapour from the aerial parts of a plant, mainly through the stomata of the leaves: water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces and the vapour diffuses out of the leaf down a water vapour potential gradient.
:::

:::definition{term="Transpiration stream"}
The continuous movement of water from the roots, up the xylem and through the leaves to the atmosphere.
:::

:::definition{term="Cohesion"}
The attraction between water molecules caused by hydrogen bonds, which holds the water column in the xylem together.
:::

:::definition{term="Adhesion"}
The attraction between water molecules and the walls of the xylem vessels (cellulose and lignin), also by hydrogen bonding.
:::

:::definition{term="Xerophyte"}
A plant adapted to live in a dry habitat by reducing water loss.
:::

## How transpiration happens

1. Water moves out of mesophyll cells into their cellulose cell walls, keeping the walls wet.
2. Water **evaporates** from the wet surfaces of the cell walls into the **air spaces** of the spongy and palisade mesophyll, which become saturated with water vapour.
3. When the **stomata** are open, water vapour **diffuses** out of the leaf into the drier air outside, down a water vapour potential gradient.
4. Water lost from the cell walls is replaced from the cytoplasm of the mesophyll cells and from neighbouring cells by osmosis and through the apoplast, and ultimately from the xylem in the veins.

Most transpiration is through the stomata. A small amount passes through the waxy cuticle, more where the cuticle is thin.

## Factors that affect the rate

| Factor | Effect on rate | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Light intensity | Increases | Stomata open in the light, so more vapour can diffuse out |
| Temperature | Increases | Faster evaporation from cell walls; warm air holds more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper |
| Humidity | Decreases | Moist air outside reduces the water vapour potential gradient |
| Air movement (wind) | Increases | Moving air carries away the layer of still, humid air outside the stomata, keeping the gradient steep |
| Water supply | Decreases when short | Wilting cells close the stomata (abscisic acid) to conserve water |

## Measuring water uptake with a potometer

A potometer measures the rate at which a leafy shoot **takes up** water, which is close to the rate of transpiration because almost all of the water taken up (often quoted as about 99%) is lost (a little is used in photosynthesis and to keep cells turgid).

Setting up a bubble potometer:

1. Cut a leafy shoot **under water** and fit it into the potometer **under water**, so no air enters the xylem and breaks the water column.
2. Make every joint airtight with petroleum jelly; dry the leaves.
3. Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube and let the shoot settle in constant conditions for several minutes.
4. Record the distance the bubble moves in a fixed time. Use the reservoir tap to return the bubble to the start between readings.
5. Repeat at least three times for each condition and calculate a mean.

The volume of water taken up is the volume of the capillary tube that the bubble has moved through: $V = \pi r^2 l$, where $r$ is the radius of the capillary bore and $l$ the distance moved.

:::worked-solution[Rate of water uptake from bubble movement]
The capillary tube of a potometer has an internal radius of 0.5 mm. The bubble moved 42 mm in 5 minutes.
1. Volume $= \pi r^2 l = \pi \times 0.5^2 \times 42$.
2. Volume $= 3.142 \times 0.25 \times 42 = 33.0\ \text{mm}^3$ (to 3 s.f.).
3. Rate $= 33.0 \div 5 = 6.60\ \text{mm}^3\ \text{min}^{-1}$.
4. Use the radius, not the diameter: if 0.5 mm were the diameter, $r = 0.25$ mm and the volume would be four times smaller.
:::

:::worked-solution[Comparing conditions]
Chipo measured the distance moved by the bubble in 5 minutes, three times in still air and three times in front of a fan.

| Condition | Reading 1 / mm | Reading 2 / mm | Reading 3 / mm | Mean / mm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Still air | 21 | 23 | 22 | 22 |
| Fan | 47 | 44 | 50 | 47 |

1. Percentage change $= \dfrac{47 - 22}{22} \times 100 = 114\%$ (to 3 s.f.).
2. The fan more than doubled the rate of water uptake.
3. **Explain**: moving air removes the humid air outside the stomata, so the water vapour potential gradient stays steep and vapour diffuses out faster.
:::

## Movement of water up the xylem: cohesion-tension

1. As water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls, more water moves out of the xylem in the leaf veins to replace it.
2. This creates a **tension** (negative pressure) at the top of the water column in the xylem.
3. Because water molecules are held together by **hydrogen bonds** (cohesion), the whole column is pulled upwards as one: the **transpiration pull**.
4. **Adhesion** of water to the hydrophilic cellulose and lignin of the vessel walls helps support the column and, in narrow tubes, causes **capillarity** (a small rise on its own).
5. Water moves by **mass flow** up the xylem from the roots, where it is replaced by uptake from the soil.

Evidence that xylem sap is under tension: the diameter of a tree trunk becomes slightly smaller during the day when transpiration is fastest; and when a xylem vessel is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. The lignified walls stop the vessels collapsing inwards under this tension.

## Xerophytes: reducing water loss

| Adaptation | Example | How it reduces water loss |
|---|---|---|
| Rolled leaves | Marram grass (*Ammophila arenaria*) | Traps humid air inside the roll, so the water vapour gradient is shallower |
| Sunken stomata in pits or grooves | Marram grass, many conifers | Humid air collects in the pit, reducing the gradient |
| Hairs on the inner (upper) leaf surface | Marram grass | Trap still, moist air near the stomata |
| Thick, waxy cuticle | Aloes, euphorbias, cacti | Reduces evaporation through the epidermis |
| Few stomata, mostly on the protected surface | Marram grass (inner surface) | Fewer pores for vapour to escape |
| Succulent leaves or stems storing water | Aloes, many euphorbias | Water stored in large parenchyma cells for dry periods |
| Small or spine-like leaves; photosynthesis in green stems | Many cactus-like euphorbias | Smaller surface area for water loss |

In a transverse section, a rolled marram grass leaf shows a thick cuticle on the outer (lower) surface with no stomata, and an inner surface folded into deep grooves lined with hairs, with the stomata sunk at the bases of the grooves. Many succulent plants, aloes among them, also open their stomata mainly at night when it is cooler and more humid (CAM photosynthesis), which saves water.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying water evaporates 'from the stomata'"}
Water evaporates from the **cell walls of mesophyll cells** into the air spaces. Water **vapour** then diffuses out through the stomata.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the diameter in πr²l"}
Halve the diameter to get the radius before squaring.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a potometer measures transpiration"}
It measures **water uptake**. Uptake approximately equals transpiration, but a little water is used by the plant.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting why wind increases transpiration"}
Say that wind removes humid air from around the leaf, so the **water vapour potential gradient** is maintained.
:::

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2, *explain* the cohesion-tension mechanism in order: evaporation → tension → cohesion (hydrogen bonds) → column pulled up → adhesion. Use the words "hydrogen bonds", "tension" and "mass flow".
:::

For ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3, potometer questions ask for precautions (cut under water, airtight joints, equilibration), the calculation of volume using $\pi r^2 l$ and rates per minute, and improvements such as replicates and controlling temperature. :::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transpiration: evaporation from mesophyll cell walls into air spaces, then diffusion of water vapour out through the stomata.
- Rate rises with light, temperature and wind, and falls with humidity.
- A potometer measures water uptake; volume = πr²l from the distance the bubble moves.
- Cohesion-tension: evaporation creates tension in the xylem; hydrogen-bonded water is pulled up as a column; adhesion helps.
- Xerophytes reduce loss with rolled leaves, sunken stomata, hairs, thick cuticles, succulent tissue and reduced leaf area.
:::
