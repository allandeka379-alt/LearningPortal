## Introduction

When a farmer near Masvingo irrigates a wilting tomato crop in the evening, the leaves are firm again by morning. The water poured on the soil has entered millions of root hairs, crossed the root and climbed the xylem to the leaves. The first part of that journey, from the soil solution to the xylem in the centre of the root, is controlled by the root itself, and one thin band of waterproof material in the endodermis makes all the difference.

Water carries more than itself: **mineral ions** such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium and magnesium travel in solution in the xylem, and organic compounds such as sucrose and amino acids travel in solution in the phloem. You should already know osmosis and water potential, active transport, and the arrangement of tissues in a root (epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, central xylem and phloem).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Water potential"}
A measure of the tendency of water to move out of a solution or a cell; pure water at atmospheric pressure has a water potential of zero, and water moves by osmosis from a higher (less negative) to a lower (more negative) water potential.
:::

:::definition{term="Apoplast pathway"}
The movement of water through the cell walls and the spaces between cells, without crossing a cell surface membrane.
:::

:::definition{term="Symplast pathway"}
The movement of water through the cytoplasm of cells, passing from cell to cell through plasmodesmata.
:::

:::definition{term="Casparian strip"}
A band of suberin (with lignin) in the radial and end walls of endodermis cells that is impermeable to water and blocks the apoplast pathway.
:::

:::definition{term="Root pressure"}
A positive pressure in the root xylem produced when cells actively secrete mineral ions into the xylem, lowering its water potential so that water follows by osmosis.
:::

## Water enters the root hairs

Root hairs are long, thin extensions of epidermal cells just behind the root tip. Thousands of them give a very **large surface area** in contact with the film of soil water around soil particles, and their walls are thin.

1. Soil water is a dilute solution, so it has a **high** (only slightly negative) water potential.
2. The vacuole and cytoplasm of a root hair cell contain sugars, amino acids and **ions that the cell has taken up by active transport**, so the cell has a **lower** water potential.
3. Water therefore moves into the root hair cell **by osmosis** across its partially permeable cell surface membrane.

Mineral ions are usually at a lower concentration in the soil than in the root hair cell, so they are taken up mainly by **active transport** through carrier proteins, using ATP from respiration. This is why waterlogged soils, which are short of oxygen, reduce ion uptake.

## Crossing the cortex: three pathways

Water now crosses the cortex towards the stele. It can follow three routes, and switches between them freely.

1. **Apoplast pathway.** Water moves through the cellulose cell walls and intercellular spaces. Cellulose microfibrils have spaces between them, and water molecules are pulled along by **cohesion** (hydrogen bonds between water molecules) as water further ahead moves into the xylem. Because it crosses no membranes, this route offers little resistance and carries most of the water in many plants.
2. **Symplast pathway.** Water enters the cytoplasm and moves from cell to cell through **plasmodesmata**, the strands of cytoplasm that pass through the cell walls. It moves down a water potential gradient across the cortex.
3. **Vacuolar pathway.** Water passes through the cytoplasm **and the vacuoles** of successive cells, crossing tonoplasts and membranes by osmosis. ZIMSEC names this pathway; Cambridge names only the apoplast and symplast pathways.

|---|---|---|---|
| Soil water | −30 | 0 | −30 |

1. Root hair cell: $-620 + 280 = -340$ kPa, lower than the soil's $-30$ kPa, so water enters by osmosis.
2. Cortex cell: $-700 + 300 = -400$ kPa, lower again, so water moves on inwards.
3. Xylem: $-150 + (-400) = -550$ kPa. The pressure potential is negative because the transpiration pull puts the xylem sap under **tension**.
4. Water potential falls steadily from soil to xylem, so there is a continuous gradient for water to move inwards.
:::

## The endodermis: the root's checkpoint

The endodermis is a single ring of cells around the stele. In the radial and end walls of each cell is the **Casparian strip**, a band of **suberin**, a waxy, waterproof substance.

1. Water moving in the apoplast reaches the Casparian strip and **cannot pass through** the wall at that point.
2. It must cross the **cell surface membrane** of an endodermis cell and enter the cytoplasm, joining the symplast.
3. Because every molecule of water and every ion must now cross a membrane, the plant **controls** which ions enter the xylem: carrier proteins in the membrane select them, and toxic ions can be kept out.
4. Once past the endodermis, water can return to the apoplast in the pericycle and enter the xylem vessels through their pits.

In older roots the endodermis cell walls become thickly suberised all round, except for a few **passage cells** opposite the xylem arms that still let water through.

## Active loading and root pressure

Cells of the endodermis and pericycle actively pump mineral ions **into the xylem**. This lowers the water potential of the xylem sap, so water follows by osmosis from the surrounding cells. Because the Casparian strip stops the ions leaking back out through the apoplast, a **positive hydrostatic pressure** builds up in the root xylem: root pressure.

Evidence for root pressure, and for its active nature:

- If a well-watered plant is cut off just above soil level, **sap oozes from the cut stump** for hours.
- **Guttation**: on cool, humid mornings, when transpiration is low, droplets of water are forced out of special pores at the edges of leaves of grasses and many seedlings.
- If the roots are given a **respiratory inhibitor** (such as cyanide), deprived of oxygen or cooled, root pressure falls, showing it depends on ATP from respiration.

Root pressure is small (usually no more than a few hundred kilopascals) and can push water up only a short distance. In tall plants and on sunny days the main force moving water up the xylem is the **transpiration pull** from the leaves.

:::worked-solution[Rate of exudation from a cut stem]
Tatenda cut a well-watered sunflower plant above soil level and fitted a graduated tube over the stump.

| Time after cutting / h | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of sap collected / cm³ | 0.00 | 0.12 | 0.25 | 0.36 | 0.48 |

1. Total volume collected in 8 hours $= 0.48\ \text{cm}^3$.
2. Mean rate $= 0.48 \div 8 = 0.06\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{h}^{-1}$.
3. The volume rises steadily, so sap is pushed out at a roughly constant rate: the roots are generating a positive pressure.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting an effect"}
The roots of a second cut plant are bathed in a solution containing a respiratory inhibitor. **Predict** and **explain** the result: exudation slows and stops, because without ATP the endodermis and pericycle cells cannot pump ions into the xylem, so the water potential of the xylem sap is no longer lowered and water stops entering by osmosis.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying root hairs take in water by active transport"}
Water always moves **passively** by osmosis down a water potential gradient. It is the **mineral ions** that are actively transported.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Casparian strip stops water entering the stele"}
It blocks only the **apoplast** route. Water still enters by passing through the cytoplasm of endodermis cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing suberin and lignin roles"}
The Casparian strip is mainly suberin (with lignin) and makes walls waterproof; lignin in xylem vessels mainly gives strength. Both are waterproof.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating root pressure as the main force"}
Root pressure helps, especially at night and in seedlings, but in tall plants the transpiration pull moves most water.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 2, *describe the pathway* answers should name the route (apoplast through cell walls, symplast through cytoplasm and plasmodesmata), then the Casparian strip, then entry to the xylem. *Explain* answers need the cause: "suberin is waterproof, so water must cross a membrane".
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions on root pressure and respiratory inhibitors test whether you link ATP → active ion uptake → lower water potential in the xylem → water entry by osmosis. Keep the chain complete.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water enters root hairs by osmosis because active ion uptake keeps their water potential lower than that of soil water.
- Water crosses the cortex by the apoplast (cell walls), symplast (cytoplasm and plasmodesmata) and vacuolar pathways.
- The suberin Casparian strip blocks the apoplast at the endodermis, so water and ions must cross a membrane, letting the plant control what enters the xylem.
- Active pumping of ions into the xylem draws water in by osmosis and creates root pressure, seen as sap oozing from a cut stem and as guttation.
- Root pressure depends on respiration; the transpiration pull moves most water in tall plants.
:::
