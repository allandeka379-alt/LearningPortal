## Introduction

In Hwange National Park a tourist might hear one animal called an elephant, *nzou* in Shona and *indlovu* in Ndebele, and something else again in German or Japanese. Scientists everywhere call it *Loxodonta africana*. **Classification** gives every kind of organism one internationally agreed name and places it in a system of groups that reflects how closely it is related to others. Without it, biologists could not record biodiversity, track disease organisms or plan conservation.

You should already know the features of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and the structure of viruses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Species (biological species concept)"}
A group of organisms with similar morphology, physiology and behaviour that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated from other such groups.
:::

:::definition{term="Taxonomy"}
The science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
:::

:::definition{term="Taxon (plural taxa)"}
A group of organisms at any level of a classification, such as a phylum or a genus.
:::

:::definition{term="Binomial nomenclature"}
The system of naming each species with two Latin words: the genus name followed by the specific name.
:::

:::definition{term="Domain"}
The highest taxonomic rank: all life is divided into three domains, Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.
:::

## What is a species?

No single definition works for every organism, so biologists use several **species concepts**.

| Species concept | A species is… | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Biological | A group that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring | Cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce asexually (bacteria, many fungi), to fossils, or to populations that never meet; some species form fertile hybrids |
| Morphological | A group that looks alike (structure, anatomy) | Males and females, or juveniles and adults, may look very different; some separate species look almost identical |
| Ecological | A group that occupies the same niche | Niches are hard to measure and can overlap or change |

In practice, biologists combine morphology, ecology, behaviour and, increasingly, **DNA sequence** data.

## The taxonomic hierarchy

Organisms are placed in a series of groups, each contained within a larger one, with **no overlap** between groups at the same rank. Moving down the hierarchy, groups contain fewer organisms that share more features and are more closely related.

| Rank | African elephant | Lion |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | Eukarya | Eukarya |
| Kingdom | Animalia | Animalia |
| Phylum | Chordata | Chordata |
| Class | Mammalia | Mammalia |
| Order | Proboscidea | Carnivora |
| Family | Elephantidae | Felidae |
| Genus | *Loxodonta* | *Panthera* |
| Species | *africana* | *leo* |

The elephant and the lion share every rank down to class, so both are mammals, but they belong to different orders. A useful memory aid for the order of ranks below domain is "**K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup".

### Binomial nomenclature

- The **genus** name comes first and starts with a **capital** letter; the **specific** name follows, all in lower case: *Panthera leo*.
- Both words are written in *italics* in print, or **underlined** when handwritten.
- After the first full use, the genus may be shortened to its initial: *P. leo*.
- The specific name alone is never used: "*leo*" on its own is not a species name.

## The three domains

Comparisons of **ribosomal RNA** sequences in the 1970s showed that prokaryotes form two groups that are as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes. All life is now placed in three domains.

| Feature | Bacteria | Archaea | Eukarya |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleus | Absent | Absent | Present, with a nuclear envelope |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Absent | Present |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 70S, but proteins and rRNA sequences more like those of eukaryotes | 80S in cytoplasm |
| Cell wall | Contains peptidoglycan (murein) | Never contains peptidoglycan; various proteins or other polymers | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi) or absent (animals) |
| Membrane lipids | Fatty acids joined to glycerol by **ester** bonds; unbranched chains | Branched chains joined to glycerol by **ether** bonds | Ester-linked, as in bacteria |
| Examples | *Vibrio cholerae*, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Methanogens in cattle rumens; species of hot springs and salt lakes | Protoctists, fungi, plants, animals |

Archaea were first found in extreme habitats such as hot springs and very salty water, but they are now known to live almost everywhere, including in soils, oceans and animal guts. Their ether-linked lipids are more stable at high temperatures.

## Classifying viruses

Viruses are not cells and are not placed in any domain. They are classified mainly by the type of **nucleic acid** they contain and whether it is **single-stranded** or **double-stranded**.

| Nucleic acid | Example |
|---|---|
| Double-stranded DNA | Smallpox virus; many bacteriophages |
| Single-stranded DNA | Parvoviruses |
| Double-stranded RNA | Rotavirus (a cause of severe diarrhoea in young children) |
| Single-stranded RNA | Influenza virus; tobacco mosaic virus; HIV (a retrovirus) |

## Dichotomous keys

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism by a series of paired statements. At each step the user chooses one of two contrasting descriptions, which leads either to another pair or to a name. Good keys use **features that are easy to see and do not vary** (number of legs, presence of wings) rather than size or colour, which vary with age and environment.

:::example{title="Using a key to identify invertebrates from a leaf-litter sample"}
Chipo found an animal with a segmented body, three pairs of jointed legs and no wings.

1. Jointed legs present → go to 2. No jointed legs → go to 5.
2. Three pairs of legs → go to 3. More than three pairs of legs → go to 4.
3. Wings present → winged insect. Wings absent → **wingless insect (for example a silverfish)**.
4. Four pairs of legs → spider or mite. More than four pairs → centipede, millipede or woodlouse.
5. Body segmented → earthworm. Body not segmented → slug or snail.

Chipo's animal: jointed legs (1 → 2), three pairs (2 → 3), no wings (3): a wingless insect.
:::

:::worked-solution[Building a dichotomous key for four trees]
Tendai wants a key for four trees in a school garden: msasa (compound leaves, leaflets in pairs, no thorns), jacaranda (compound leaves, very small leaflets, no thorns), acacia (compound leaves, thorns) and mango (simple leaves).
1. Find one feature that splits the group: leaf simple or compound. "Leaves simple → mango. Leaves compound → go to 2."
2. Split the three with compound leaves: "Thorns present → acacia. Thorns absent → go to 3."
3. Split the last two: "Leaflets large, in a few pairs → msasa. Leaflets very small and numerous → jacaranda."
4. Check: every organism ends at its own name after a series of yes-or-no choices, and each couplet uses contrasting forms of the same feature.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing species names incorrectly"}
*Loxodonta africana*, not *loxodonta Africana* or Loxodonta africana in plain type. Underline both words when handwriting.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Archaea are a kind of bacteria"}
Archaea are prokaryotes, but they form a separate domain with different membrane lipids, cell walls and rRNA.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using variable features in a key"}
"Large" or "dark green" depends on age and conditions. Use fixed, observable features.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Cambridge Paper 4, *explain* differences between Archaea and Bacteria with three matched points: cell wall (no peptidoglycan in Archaea), membrane lipids (ether-linked, branched in Archaea) and rRNA or ribosomal proteins (more like Eukarya).
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *state* the classification of an organism, give the ranks in order with each name, and set out the binomial name correctly.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Species can be defined by interbreeding (biological), appearance (morphological) or niche (ecological); each concept has limitations.
- Hierarchy: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species; groups do not overlap.
- Binomial names: *Genus species*, genus capitalised, both italicised or underlined.
- Three domains: Bacteria (peptidoglycan walls, ester lipids), Archaea (no peptidoglycan, ether lipids) and Eukarya.
- Viruses are classified by nucleic acid (DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded); dichotomous keys identify organisms with paired statements.
:::
