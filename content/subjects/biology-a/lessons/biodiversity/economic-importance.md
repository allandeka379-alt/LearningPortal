## Introduction

Think about a single day in Zimbabwe. Breakfast might include bread raised by yeast (a fungus) and sour milk (*amasi*) thickened by bacteria. Maize in the fields depends on soil bacteria that recycle nutrients, while groundnuts and soya beans host bacteria that fix nitrogen. A trip to Lake Kariba passes fishing rigs that catch kapenta, which feed on tiny animals that in turn depend on microscopic algae. And at the clinic, staff test for malaria, a disease caused by a protoctist and carried by an insect. Every one of the five kingdoms touches the economy and people's health, for good and for ill.

You should already know the diagnostic features of the five kingdoms (see the previous lesson).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Socio-economic importance"}
The effects of organisms on people's livelihoods, health, food supply, industry and culture, whether beneficial or harmful.
:::

:::definition{term="Fermentation (industrial)"}
The use of microorganisms, growing on a substrate, to make useful products such as ethanol, lactic acid, carbon dioxide or medicines.
:::

:::definition{term="Decomposer"}
An organism, usually a bacterium or fungus, that breaks down dead organic matter and releases mineral ions that plants can reuse.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself suffering the disease, such as the *Anopheles* mosquito for malaria.
:::

## Kingdom Prokaryotae (bacteria)

**Benefits**

- **Fermentation of food**: lactic acid bacteria turn milk into sour milk (*amasi*) and yoghurt, lowering the pH so that spoilage organisms cannot grow.
- **Biotechnology**: genetically modified bacteria such as *Escherichia coli* make human insulin and other medicines.
- **Nitrogen fixation**: *Rhizobium* in root nodules of legumes (groundnuts, soya beans, cowpeas) converts nitrogen gas into ammonium ions, reducing the need for expensive fertiliser.
- **Decomposition and sewage treatment**: saprotrophic bacteria break down dead matter and sewage, recycling carbon and nitrogen.

**Harm**

- **Disease**: *Vibrio cholerae* (cholera, spread in contaminated water), *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (TB) and anthrax in livestock.
- **Food spoilage**: bacteria make milk sour when it is unwanted and cause meat to rot.

## Kingdom Protoctista

**Harm**

- ***Plasmodium*** causes **malaria**, carried by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. It is a major cause of illness in the lowveld and the Zambezi valley, reducing people's ability to work and attend school.
- ***Trypanosoma*** causes **sleeping sickness** in people and **nagana** in cattle; it is carried by **tsetse flies** in parts of the Zambezi valley, which limits cattle farming in those areas.
- The syllabus lists ***Schistosoma*** (bilharzia) here, but it is a parasitic **flatworm** and belongs to the kingdom Animalia. Its larvae develop in freshwater snails and penetrate the skin of people wading or washing in infected water. Chronic bilharzia damages the bladder, intestines and liver.
- **Algal blooms** in lakes polluted with sewage and fertiliser (as has happened in Lake Chivero near Harare) use up oxygen when the algae die and decompose, killing fish and making water expensive to treat.

**Benefits**

- **Phytoplankton** (microscopic algae) are the **producers** at the base of aquatic food chains.

:::example{title="Following the kapenta food chain in Lake Kariba"}
Kapenta fishing is an important industry on Lake Kariba, providing income and an affordable source of protein. **Trace the chain:** microscopic algae (protoctists) photosynthesise in the sunlit upper water → small zooplankton animals graze on the algae → kapenta feed on the zooplankton → people and larger fish such as tigerfish eat kapenta. **Interpret:** the kapenta catch ultimately depends on the protoctists; anything that reduces algal growth, or pollution that causes harmful blooms, can affect the whole fishery. Kapenta are animals, so this example also shows the importance of Animalia.
:::

## Kingdom Fungi

**Benefits**

- **Yeast** (*Saccharomyces*) ferments sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide: CO₂ makes bread rise, and ethanol is produced in brewing, including traditional sorghum beer.
- **Antibiotics**: penicillin from the mould *Penicillium* was the first antibiotic used widely, and fungi remain a source of medicines.
- **Decomposition**: fungi break down cellulose and lignin in dead plant matter, recycling nutrients in forests and compost.
- **Food**: edible mushrooms, both wild species collected in the rainy season and cultivated oyster mushrooms, provide food and income.

**Harm**

- **Food spoilage**: moulds grow on bread, fruit and stored grain.
- **Toxins**: *Aspergillus flavus* growing on badly stored groundnuts and maize can produce **aflatoxins**, which damage the liver.
- **Crop diseases**: rusts and other fungi reduce yields of wheat, maize and other crops.

:::example{title="Why groundnuts must be dried before storage"}
A farmer in Masvingo province stores freshly harvested groundnuts in sacks in a warm, damp room, and some develop a greenish mould. **Explain:** warm, moist conditions allow *Aspergillus* spores to germinate and grow; the fungus can produce aflatoxins, which are not destroyed by normal cooking and can harm people and livestock that eat the nuts. **Suggest:** dry the nuts thoroughly in the sun before storage, store them in a cool, dry, ventilated place, and discard mouldy nuts.
:::

## Kingdom Plantae

- **Producers**: crops such as maize, sorghum, millet and groundnuts feed the nation; tobacco and cotton are important cash crops.
- **Carbon sinks**: forests and woodland such as miombo absorb carbon dioxide in photosynthesis and store carbon in wood and soil, helping to limit climate change.
- **Timber and fuel**: pine and eucalyptus plantations in the Eastern Highlands supply timber and paper; firewood is still the main cooking fuel in many rural homes, which contributes to deforestation.
- **Medicinal plants**: many traditional remedies come from plants, and some modern drugs were first found in plants, such as quinine (from cinchona bark) and aspirin (from a compound found in willow bark).
- **Tourism and culture**: landscapes such as the rainforest at Victoria Falls and large baobab trees attract visitors.
- **Harm**: weeds compete with crops, and some plants are poisonous to livestock.

## Kingdom Animalia

- **Tourism**: elephants, lions, rhinos and other wildlife in Hwange, Mana Pools and Gonarezhou National Parks attract visitors and bring foreign currency and jobs.
- **Food**: cattle, goats and chickens; fish such as kapenta and tilapia; edible insects such as mopane worms, which are also sold.
- **Leather and other products**: cattle hides for leather; honey and wax from bees.
- **Hunting**: regulated safari hunting brings income to some rural communities and wildlife areas.
- **Pollination**: bees and other insects pollinate many crops and fruit trees.
- **Harm**: pests such as locusts and quelea birds destroy crops; mosquitoes and tsetse flies are vectors of disease; ticks spread diseases of cattle.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling Schistosoma a protoctist"}
*Schistosoma* is a multicellular parasitic flatworm in the kingdom Animalia, even though the syllabus lists it with the protoctists.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing only harmful effects"}
Most kingdoms have both benefits and harmful effects. A balanced answer gives both, with examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague examples"}
"Bacteria are used in food" earns less than "lactic acid bacteria ferment milk to sour milk (*amasi*) and yoghurt".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 3, a *discuss* essay on the importance of a kingdom needs named organisms, what each does, and why it matters to people (food, income, health). Balance benefits with harm.
:::

:::exam-tip
For *explain* questions, link the organism's biology to its use: yeast respires anaerobically, producing CO₂ that raises bread; *Rhizobium* fixes nitrogen, so legumes need less fertiliser.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bacteria: fermentation (sour milk), biotechnology (insulin), nitrogen fixation, decomposition; also cholera, TB and spoilage.
- Protoctists: *Plasmodium* (malaria), *Trypanosoma* (sleeping sickness, nagana); algae as aquatic producers and as harmful blooms.
- Fungi: yeast in baking and brewing, penicillin, decomposition, edible mushrooms; also spoilage, aflatoxins and crop disease.
- Plants: food and cash crops, carbon sinks, timber, fuel, medicines and tourism.
- Animals: tourism, food (including kapenta), leather, hunting, pollination; also pests and disease vectors. *Schistosoma* is an animal (flatworm).
:::
