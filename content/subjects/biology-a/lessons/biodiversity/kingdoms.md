## Introduction

A single afternoon by the Mukuvisi River in Harare could turn up bacteria in the water, a green *Euglena*-like protoctist, bracket fungi on a dead log, mosses, ferns and msasa trees, and animals from earthworms and snails to dragonflies and frogs. Biologists sort this variety into **kingdoms** using **diagnostic features**: features that every member of a group has and members of other groups lack. Learning these features lets you place an unfamiliar organism in the right group from what you can see, which is exactly what ZIMSEC practical and theory questions ask.

You should already know the structure of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells and the taxonomic hierarchy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Kingdom"}
The highest rank of classification below the domain; the five-kingdom scheme recognises Prokaryotae, Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
:::

:::definition{term="Diagnostic feature"}
A characteristic shared by all members of a group and used to distinguish that group from others.
:::

:::definition{term="Heterotrophic nutrition"}
Obtaining organic molecules by taking in organic matter made by other organisms.
:::

:::definition{term="Autotrophic nutrition"}
Making organic molecules from simple inorganic substances, using energy from light (photosynthesis) or from chemical reactions.
:::

## The five kingdoms

| Kingdom | Cell type | Diagnostic features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prokaryotae | Prokaryotic | No nucleus; circular DNA, often with plasmids; 70S ribosomes; cell wall of peptidoglycan; mostly unicellular | *Vibrio cholerae*, *Rhizobium*, cyanobacteria |
| Protoctista | Eukaryotic | Mostly unicellular or simple multicellular; organisms that do not fit the other eukaryotic kingdoms; some photosynthetic, some heterotrophic | *Plasmodium*, *Amoeba*, *Trypanosoma*, seaweeds |
| Fungi | Eukaryotic | Body of thread-like **hyphae** forming a mycelium (or single cells in yeasts); cell walls of **chitin**; heterotrophic, feeding by extracellular digestion and absorption (saprotrophic or parasitic); store glycogen; reproduce by spores | Mushrooms, bread mould, yeast, *Penicillium* |
| Plantae | Eukaryotic | Multicellular; cell walls of **cellulose**; chloroplasts with chlorophyll; autotrophic by photosynthesis; store starch | Mosses, ferns, conifers, flowering plants |
| Animalia | Eukaryotic | Multicellular; **no cell walls**; heterotrophic, usually ingesting food; nervous coordination in most; store glycogen; most develop from a blastula | Sponges to mammals |

No single feature is enough on its own. Fungi and plants both have cell walls, and fungi and animals both store glycogen, so a kingdom is identified by a **combination** of features: cell type, cell wall material, method of nutrition and storage carbohydrate. Viruses are not placed in any kingdom, because they are not made of cells and cannot reproduce outside a host cell. Some protoctists, such as *Euglena*, photosynthesise like plants yet move like animals, which is one reason the kingdom Protoctista exists at all.

## Major groups of plants

| Group | Diagnostic features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mosses (bryophytes) | No true roots (rhizoids instead), no lignified xylem or phloem; small; reproduce by spores in capsules; need water for fertilisation | Mosses on damp rocks and tree trunks |
| Ferns | True roots, stems and leaves (fronds) with xylem and phloem; no seeds; spores in sori on the underside of fronds | Bracken; tree ferns in the Eastern Highlands |
| Conifers | Seeds carried naked on the scales of cones; usually needle-like leaves; no flowers or fruits | Pine plantations near Nyanga |
| Flowering plants | Flowers; seeds enclosed in an ovary that develops into a fruit; xylem with vessels | Maize (a monocot), msasa (a dicot) |

Flowering plants are divided into **monocotyledons** (one seed leaf, parallel leaf veins, scattered vascular bundles, as in maize and sorghum) and **dicotyledons** (two seed leaves, net-veined leaves, vascular bundles in a ring, as in beans and msasa).

## Major groups of animals

| Phylum | Diagnostic features | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cnidarians | Radial symmetry; two cell layers; one opening (mouth) to the gut; tentacles with stinging cells | *Hydra*, jellyfish |
| Flatworms | Flat, unsegmented body; bilateral symmetry; one opening to the gut (if a gut is present); many are parasites | Tapeworms, *Schistosoma* (blood fluke) |
| Annelids | Body divided into many similar segments; bristles (chaetae) on most segments; moist skin used for gas exchange | Earthworms, leeches |
| Molluscs | Soft, unsegmented body; muscular foot; mantle that often secretes a shell | Snails, slugs, freshwater mussels |
| Arthropods | Jointed legs; hard exoskeleton of chitin; segmented body | Insects, spiders, millipedes, crabs |
| Chordates | Notochord at some stage (a backbone in vertebrates); dorsal hollow nerve cord; gill slits at some stage | Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals |

The arthropods are divided further: **insects** (three body parts, three pairs of legs, usually two pairs of wings, one pair of antennae), **arachnids** (two body parts, four pairs of legs, no antennae), **crustaceans** (two pairs of antennae, many legs, mostly aquatic, as with freshwater crabs) and **myriapods** (many segments with one pair of legs each in centipedes or two pairs in millipedes).

:::example{title="Identifying an animal from its features"}
Tatenda found an animal under a stone in a stream. It had a soft, slimy, flat body about 1 cm long, no legs and no segments, and it glided along on its underside. **Reasoning:** no jointed legs or exoskeleton, so not an arthropod; no segments or bristles, so not an annelid; no shell, foot or mantle, so not a mollusc; flat and unsegmented with bilateral symmetry, so it is a **flatworm** (a free-living planarian).
:::

:::example{title="Placing a plant in its group"}
Nyasha collected a plant from a shady bank in the Eastern Highlands. It had large divided leaves with rows of brown spots on the underside, true roots, and no flowers or seeds. **Reasoning:** true roots and leaves with veins mean it has vascular tissue, so it is not a moss; no flowers or seeds, so it is neither a flowering plant nor a conifer; brown spots (sori) on the underside of fronds produce spores, so it is a **fern**.
:::

## Five kingdoms and three domains

The five-kingdom scheme was built mainly on cell structure and nutrition. Molecular studies later showed that the prokaryotes include two very different groups, so many biologists now use three **domains** above the kingdoms:

- **Bacteria** and **Archaea** together contain all the organisms of the kingdom Prokaryotae;
- **Eukarya** contains the four eukaryotic kingdoms: Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.

Protoctista is a group of convenience: its members are not all closely related to one another, which is why some newer schemes split it into several kingdoms.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the features of the kingdoms. ZIMSEC uses the five-kingdom scheme, with Prokaryotae as a kingdom, and expects you to divide the kingdoms into the major plant groups and animal phyla and to identify organisms from their features. Cambridge uses three domains with the four eukaryotic kingdoms.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling fungi plants"}
Fungi have chitin cell walls, no chlorophyll and feed by absorbing digested food: they are a separate kingdom.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling all small soft animals worms"}
Flatworms, annelids and roundworms are different phyla. Look for segments, bristles and body shape.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling spiders insects"}
Spiders are arachnids: two body parts, eight legs and no antennae. Insects have three body parts and six legs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 4, *identify* an organism by naming its group and giving the features you used ("jointed legs and an exoskeleton, so arthropod; eight legs and two body parts, so arachnid").
:::

:::exam-tip
To *compare* two kingdoms, give matched features: cell wall (chitin or cellulose), nutrition (absorptive heterotroph or photosynthetic autotroph), storage carbohydrate (glycogen or starch).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Prokaryotae: no nucleus, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan walls; Protoctista: eukaryotes that fit no other kingdom.
- Fungi: hyphae, chitin walls, absorptive heterotrophs, glycogen, spores; Plantae: cellulose walls, chloroplasts, starch.
- Animalia: multicellular, no cell walls, heterotrophic, usually with nervous coordination.
- Plants: mosses, ferns, conifers and flowering plants; animals: cnidarians, flatworms, annelids, molluscs, arthropods, chordates.
- Bacteria and Archaea contain the Prokaryotae; Eukarya contains the four eukaryotic kingdoms.
:::
