## Introduction

A human sperm and egg each carry 23 chromosomes. When they fuse, the zygote has 46, the same number as every body cell of the parents. If gametes were made by mitosis, the chromosome number would double in every generation. **Meiosis** prevents this by halving the chromosome number when gametes (or, in plants, spores) are formed. It also shuffles genetic information so that brothers and sisters, apart from identical twins, are never genetically the same.

You should already know chromosome structure, the cell cycle and the stages of mitosis. How meiosis creates variation (crossing over, independent assortment) is the next lesson; here the focus is on the stages.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diploid (2n)"}
Describes a nucleus or cell with two complete sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent, such as a human body cell (2n = 46).
:::

:::definition{term="Haploid (n)"}
Describes a nucleus or cell with one complete set of chromosomes, such as a human gamete (n = 23).
:::

:::definition{term="Homologous chromosomes"}
A pair of chromosomes, one maternal and one paternal, with the same shape and size, the same centromere position and the same genes at the same loci, though they may carry different alleles.
:::

:::definition{term="Meiosis"}
A reduction division in which one diploid nucleus divides twice to produce four haploid nuclei that are genetically different from each other and from the parent nucleus.
:::

:::definition{term="Bivalent"}
A pair of homologous chromosomes lying side by side during prophase I and metaphase I of meiosis; it contains four chromatids.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Meiosis in numbers"}
One diploid cell → **two** divisions (meiosis I and meiosis II) → **four** haploid cells. Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes (the reduction); meiosis II separates sister chromatids, as in mitosis.
:::

## Why a reduction division is needed

Sexual reproduction joins two gametes at fertilisation. If each gamete is haploid, the zygote is diploid and the chromosome number stays constant from generation to generation. In animals, meiosis forms gametes directly (in testes and ovaries). In flowering plants, meiosis in the anthers and ovules forms haploid spores, which then give rise to pollen grains and embryo sacs containing the gametes.

## The stages of meiosis

Before meiosis, DNA replicates in S phase of interphase, so each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids.

### Meiosis I (separates homologous chromosomes)

**Prophase I**
1. Chromosomes condense and become visible.
2. Homologous chromosomes pair up to form **bivalents**.
3. Non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes may touch at points called **chiasmata**, where crossing over occurs.
4. In animal cells, centrioles move to the poles; the **spindle** forms.
5. The nucleolus disappears and the **nuclear envelope breaks down**.

**Metaphase I**
1. Bivalents (pairs, not single chromosomes) line up on the equator.
2. Spindle microtubules from opposite poles attach to the centromere of each homologous chromosome.
3. The orientation of each pair is random (independent assortment).

**Anaphase I**
1. Homologous chromosomes are pulled to opposite poles. **Centromeres do not divide**: each chromosome still has two chromatids.
2. Each pole receives one chromosome of each pair, so the number is halved.

**Telophase I**
1. Chromosomes reach the poles; in many cells **nuclear envelopes reform** and chromosomes partly uncoil.
2. **Cytokinesis** usually follows (a cleavage furrow in animal cells; a cell plate or a delay in some plant cells), giving two haploid cells. Each chromosome still has two chromatids, which may no longer be identical because of crossing over.

### Meiosis II (separates sister chromatids)

There is no S phase between the two divisions: DNA does not replicate again.

1. **Prophase II:** chromosomes recondense; nuclear envelopes break down; new spindles form, often at right angles to the first.
2. **Metaphase II:** individual chromosomes line up on the equator of each spindle; the orientation of chromatids is random.
3. **Anaphase II:** centromeres divide and sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles.
4. **Telophase II:** nuclear envelopes reform around four haploid nuclei; cytokinesis gives four cells (in pollen formation, a **tetrad** of four cells).

## Identifying stages in an anther squash

Young flower buds (for example of lilies or onion) contain anthers in which pollen mother cells are dividing. A squash stained with acetic orcein shows:

- **Prophase I:** long threads, paired; chiasmata may be visible.
- **Metaphase I:** thick paired chromosomes (bivalents) on one equator.
- **Anaphase I:** two groups of chromosomes moving apart, each chromosome with two chromatids, often with arms spread apart.
- **Telophase I / prophase II:** two nuclei in one cell or a dyad of two cells.
- **Metaphase II / anaphase II:** two spindles side by side or at right angles in one dyad.
- **Telophase II:** a tetrad of four nuclei or four cells.

:::example{title="Is this mitosis or meiosis?"}
A photomicrograph shows a cell with chromosomes arranged in pairs on the equator, each pair forming a cross-like shape. **Identify and explain:** this is metaphase I of meiosis, because homologous chromosomes are lying together as bivalents. In mitosis, chromosomes line up singly.
:::

:::example{title="Tracking chromosome number and DNA content"}
A cell in the testis of an animal with 2n = 24 has 12 units of DNA in G1.

| Stage | Chromosomes per cell | DNA per cell / units |
|---|---|---|
| G1 before meiosis | 24 | 12 |
| Prophase I (after S phase) | 24 | 24 |
| After telophase I and cytokinesis | 12 | 12 |
| After telophase II and cytokinesis | 12 | 6 |

1. DNA doubles in S phase, but chromosome number does not change (24 chromosomes, 48 chromatids).
2. Meiosis I halves both the chromosome number and the DNA content per cell.
3. Meiosis II halves the DNA again but leaves the chromosome number at 12, because only sister chromatids separate.
4. Each gamete has 12 chromosomes and 6 units of DNA, half the G1 content: exactly what fertilisation needs.
:::

## Comparing mitosis and meiosis

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Number of divisions | One | Two |
| Number of daughter cells | Two | Four |
| Chromosome number of daughters | Same as parent (2n → 2n) | Half the parent (2n → n) |
| Genetic make-up of daughters | Identical to each other and parent | Different from each other and parent |
| Homologous chromosomes pair | No | Yes, in prophase I (bivalents) |
| Chiasmata and crossing over | No | Yes, in prophase I |
| Metaphase arrangement | Single chromosomes on equator | Bivalents (I); single chromosomes (II) |
| Anaphase | Chromatids separate | Homologues separate (I); chromatids separate (II) |
| Where in mammals | Body (somatic) cells | Testes and ovaries |
| Role | Growth, repair, replacement, asexual reproduction | Gamete formation, variation |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying centromeres divide in anaphase I"}
In anaphase I whole homologous chromosomes, each with two chromatids, move apart. Centromeres divide only in anaphase II.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking DNA replicates before meiosis II"}
There is no S phase between meiosis I and II. That is why four haploid cells result.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing cells after meiosis I as diploid"}
After meiosis I each cell has one chromosome from each homologous pair, so it is already **haploid**, even though each chromosome still has two chromatids.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Meiosis halves the chromosome number (2n → n), keeping the number constant after fertilisation.
- Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes (prophase I: bivalents and chiasmata; metaphase I: bivalents on the equator; anaphase I: homologues to poles).
- Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, like mitosis, with no DNA replication beforehand.
- One diploid cell produces four genetically different haploid cells.
- Mitosis and meiosis differ in divisions, daughter cell number, chromosome number, pairing and genetic identity.
:::
