## Introduction

Cancer is not one disease but many, all with the same root cause: cells that divide by mitosis when they should not. In Zimbabwe, as elsewhere, cancers of the cervix, breast, prostate and oesophagus are among those seen most often in hospitals, and some cancers are linked to infections or to toxins in poorly stored food. Understanding the cell biology explains why smoking, ultraviolet light and some viruses raise risk, why screening can save lives, and why treatments such as chemotherapy have side effects.

You should already know the cell cycle, how mitosis produces identical cells, and that genes code for proteins. This lesson treats a sensitive subject factually: many people reading it will know someone who has had cancer.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cancer"}
A group of diseases in which cells divide by mitosis in an uncontrolled way, forming a mass of abnormal cells that may invade surrounding tissues and spread to other parts of the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Tumour"}
A mass of cells formed by uncontrolled cell division. A **benign** tumour stays in one place, usually inside a capsule; a **malignant** tumour invades surrounding tissue and can spread.
:::

:::definition{term="Metastasis"}
The spread of cancer cells from a primary tumour, through the blood or lymph, to form secondary tumours in other parts of the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Carcinogen"}
Any agent, chemical, physical or biological, that causes cancer, usually by causing mutations in DNA.
:::

:::definition{term="Proto-oncogene and oncogene"}
A proto-oncogene is a normal gene that stimulates cell division when the cell receives a signal to divide; a mutated, permanently active form that drives division is an oncogene.
:::

:::definition{term="Tumour suppressor gene"}
A gene whose protein slows or stops the cell cycle, repairs DNA or triggers cell death (apoptosis) when DNA is damaged; loss of its function allows uncontrolled division.
:::

## How the cell cycle is normally controlled

Healthy cells divide only when they receive the right signals, and they check their DNA at checkpoints (for example before S phase and before mitosis). Two kinds of gene are involved:

- **Proto-oncogenes** code for proteins that **promote** division, such as receptors for growth factors and proteins in the signalling pathway. They act like an accelerator.
- **Tumour suppressor genes** code for proteins that **stop** division if DNA is damaged, and that trigger apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired. They act like a brake. A well-known example is the gene for the protein p53.

## How cancer develops

Cancer develops when several mutations accumulate in one cell line, usually over many years:

1. **A mutation** in a proto-oncogene turns it into an **oncogene**, so the protein stimulates division even without a signal (the accelerator is stuck down). One mutated copy is enough to have an effect.
2. **Mutations** in tumour suppressor genes stop their proteins working (the brake fails). Usually **both copies** must be inactivated.
3. The cell divides repeatedly by mitosis, ignoring signals from neighbouring cells and avoiding apoptosis. Each division can introduce further mutations.
4. A small mass of abnormal cells forms (a tumour). Many such cells also produce telomerase, so they are not limited by telomere shortening.
5. The tumour grows. It may stimulate the growth of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) to supply itself with oxygen and nutrients.
6. In a **malignant** tumour, cells lose the adhesion molecules that hold them in place, invade nearby tissue, enter blood or lymph vessels and form **secondary tumours** elsewhere (metastasis).

Because several mutations are needed, the risk of most cancers rises with age and with long exposure to carcinogens.

| Feature | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | Usually slow | Often rapid |
| Boundary | Often enclosed in a capsule | No capsule; invades surrounding tissue |
| Spread | Does not spread | Can metastasise through blood or lymph |
| Cell appearance | Similar to normal cells | Abnormal, poorly differentiated, irregular nuclei |
| Harm | By pressure on organs; usually removable by surgery | Destroys tissues; much more dangerous |

## Carcinogens and risk factors

- **Chemicals:** tar in tobacco smoke (linked to lung, mouth, throat and oesophageal cancers); **aflatoxins**, made by the fungus *Aspergillus flavus* on maize and groundnuts stored in damp, warm conditions, which damage DNA in liver cells; asbestos fibres; some industrial chemicals.
- **Ionising radiation:** X-rays, gamma rays and radon gas break DNA strands.
- **Ultraviolet radiation** in sunlight damages the DNA of skin cells, causing skin cancers, especially in people with little melanin.
- **Some viruses:** the human papillomavirus (HPV) is the main cause of cervical cancer; hepatitis B virus increases the risk of liver cancer.
- **Other risk factors:** increasing age; inherited mutations (for example in some breast cancer genes); heavy alcohol use; obesity and diets low in fibre.

## Prevention, screening and treatment (principles)

**Prevention** reduces exposure to carcinogens: not smoking, drying and storing grain and groundnuts properly to prevent mould, protecting skin from strong sun, and **vaccination against HPV** (given to girls before they are likely to be exposed) and hepatitis B.

**Screening** looks for early changes before symptoms appear, when treatment is most successful: examining cells of the cervix (including visual inspection after applying dilute acetic acid, used in many clinics), mammography for breast cancer and blood tests for some cancers.

**Treatment** principles:

- **Surgery** removes a localised tumour.
- **Radiotherapy** uses ionising radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells in a targeted area.
- **Chemotherapy** uses drugs that stop cells dividing, for example by blocking DNA replication or spindle formation. Because they affect all rapidly dividing cells, they also affect hair follicles, the gut lining and bone marrow, causing side effects such as hair loss, nausea and a higher risk of infection.

:::worked-solution[How many cells after repeated divisions?]
A single cancer cell divides every 3 days, and every daughter cell keeps dividing.
1. After $n$ divisions, the number of cells $= 2^n$.
2. In 90 days there are $90 \div 3 = 30$ divisions.
3. Number of cells $= 2^{30} = 1\,073\,741\,824 \approx 1.07 \times 10^{9}$.
4. This is about the size at which many solid tumours first become detectable, which shows why a tumour can grow for a long time before it is noticed. (Real tumours grow more slowly, because many cells die or stop dividing.)
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a family history"}
Rudo's mother and aunt both developed breast cancer at a young age. A doctor suggests genetic counselling. **Explain why:** an inherited mutation in a tumour suppressor gene means every cell already carries one faulty copy, so only one further mutation is needed to lose the brake completely. Cancer can develop earlier and more often. Testing and earlier, more frequent screening may be offered; the decision is the family's own.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cancer cells divide 'faster than normal'"}
The key point is that they divide **without control**: they do not stop when they should. Some cancer cells divide more slowly than healthy gut cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cancer is caused by 'a mutation'"}
Usually several mutations in different genes are needed, in both oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes"}
Oncogenes **gain** activity (accelerator stuck); tumour suppressor genes **lose** activity (brake fails).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cancer appears in ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 (it suits a Paper 3 essay) and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. In *explain* answers, link **mutation → loss of control of the cell cycle → uncontrolled mitosis → tumour**, and use the word **metastasis** for spread.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *suggest* why a chemotherapy drug has side effects, link the drug's target (DNA replication or spindle) to normal tissues that divide often: hair follicles, gut lining, bone marrow.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cancer is uncontrolled mitosis caused by accumulated mutations in genes controlling the cell cycle.
- Proto-oncogenes can mutate into oncogenes (division always on); tumour suppressor genes can lose function (no brake, no apoptosis).
- Benign tumours stay put; malignant tumours invade and metastasise through blood or lymph.
- Carcinogens include tobacco tar, aflatoxins, ionising and UV radiation and viruses such as HPV.
- Prevention (avoiding carcinogens, HPV vaccination), screening and treatment (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) all rest on this biology.
:::
