## Introduction

Every day the lining of your small intestine sheds and replaces a huge number of cells, and the bone marrow produces millions of new red blood cells every second. When a farmer in Gweru cuts a finger on a sickle, the wound closes as skin cells divide. All this depends on an orderly sequence of growth, copying of DNA and division called the **cell cycle**.

This lesson explains how DNA is packaged into chromosomes, what happens in each phase of the cycle, why chromosome ends need protective telomeres, and how stem cells supply new cells. The stages of mitosis itself are the next lesson; cancer, when the cycle goes out of control, follows that. You should already know the basic structure of a eukaryotic cell and that DNA carries genes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chromosome"}
A structure in the nucleus made of one long DNA molecule wound around histone proteins, carrying many genes; after replication it consists of two identical sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
:::

:::definition{term="Sister chromatids"}
The two identical copies of a chromosome formed by DNA replication in the S phase, held together at the centromere until anaphase.
:::

:::definition{term="Centromere"}
The region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are joined and where spindle microtubules attach (through the kinetochore) during nuclear division.
:::

:::definition{term="Telomere"}
A region of short, repeated, non-coding DNA sequences at each end of a chromosome that protects the genes near the end from being lost during replication.
:::

:::definition{term="Cell cycle"}
The regular sequence of events between one cell division and the next: interphase (G1, S and G2), nuclear division (mitosis) and cytoplasmic division (cytokinesis).
:::

:::definition{term="Stem cell"}
An unspecialised cell that can divide repeatedly by mitosis and whose daughter cells can either remain stem cells or differentiate into specialised cell types.
:::

## How DNA is packed into chromosomes

A human cell contains about 2 m of DNA in a nucleus only about 6 µm across. The DNA is packed in levels:

1. The DNA double helix (about 2 nm wide) winds around groups of eight positively charged **histone** proteins, which attract the negatively charged phosphate groups of DNA. Each DNA-histone bead is a **nucleosome**.
2. The chain of nucleosomes coils into a thicker fibre, which loops and folds further.
3. During interphase the material is loosely packed as **chromatin**, so genes can be transcribed and DNA replicated.
4. At the start of mitosis the chromatin **condenses** into short, thick chromosomes that can be moved without tangling and are visible with a light microscope.

A replicated chromosome, as seen in prophase and metaphase, looks like an X: two sister chromatids, each containing one identical DNA molecule, joined at the centromere. Human body cells have **46 chromosomes** (23 pairs); the number is constant for a species.

## The cell cycle

| Phase | Main events |
|---|---|
| G1 (gap 1) | Cell grows; proteins and organelles are made; normal metabolism; a checkpoint confirms the cell is ready to copy its DNA |
| S (synthesis) | DNA replicates semi-conservatively; each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids; DNA content doubles |
| G2 (gap 2) | Cell continues to grow; energy stores and proteins for division (such as tubulin for the spindle) are made; DNA is checked for errors |
| M (mitosis) | Nuclear division: sister chromatids separate into two genetically identical nuclei |
| Cytokinesis | Cytoplasm divides, forming two daughter cells |

**Interphase** (G1 + S + G2) is the longest part of the cycle. A cell is not "resting" during interphase: it is very active.

If we take the DNA content of a cell in G1 as 1 unit, it rises to 2 units during S, stays at 2 through G2 and mitosis, and returns to 1 unit in each daughter cell after cytokinesis. Cells that stop dividing, such as most neurones, leave the cycle in G1 and enter a non-dividing state (often called G0).

:::worked-solution[Estimating the duration of each phase]
A culture of mammalian cells has a cell cycle of 20 hours. A sample of 400 cells was classified using a stain that shows how much DNA each cell contains.

| Phase | Number of cells | Fraction | Estimated duration / h |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 180 | 0.45 | 9.0 |
| S | 140 | 0.35 | 7.0 |
| G2 | 60 | 0.15 | 3.0 |
| M | 20 | 0.05 | 1.0 |

1. The fraction of cells in a phase is proportional to the time that phase lasts (the cells are dividing at random times, not in step).
2. Fraction in G1 $= 180 \div 400 = 0.45$.
3. Duration of G1 $= 0.45 \times 20 = 9.0$ hours. Repeat for each phase.
4. Check: $9.0 + 7.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 20$ hours.
:::

## Telomeres

DNA polymerase cannot copy the very end of a linear DNA molecule completely, so a short length is lost from each end at every replication. Telomeres are long runs of a short repeated sequence (in humans, TTAGGG repeated many hundreds of times) that contain no genes.

- Each replication shortens the telomere, not a gene, so genes near chromosome ends are protected.
- After many divisions the telomeres become very short, and most body cells stop dividing. This limits the number of times a cell can divide.
- Stem cells, cells that make gametes, and most cancer cells contain the enzyme **telomerase**, which adds repeats back and allows continued division.

## Stem cells

Stem cells keep their ability to divide by mitosis. When a stem cell divides, one daughter cell can stay a stem cell while the other differentiates. Stem cells differ in **potency**:

| Type | What it can become | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Totipotent | Any cell type, including the placenta | The zygote and cells of the very early embryo |
| Pluripotent | Almost any cell type of the body, but not the placenta | Embryonic stem cells from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst |
| Multipotent | A limited range of related cell types | Bone marrow stem cells, which form red cells, white cells and platelets |

Adult (multipotent) stem cells **replace** cells that are lost, such as blood cells and the lining of the gut and skin, and **repair** damaged tissue. Bone marrow transplants, used for some leukaemias, rely on these cells.

:::example{title="Interpreting DNA content"}
A cell has 12 arbitrary units of DNA in G2. **Predict** its DNA content in G1 and in each daughter cell after cytokinesis: 6 units in both, because DNA content doubles in S and halves at division. A cell with 9 units is part-way through the S phase.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling interphase a resting phase"}
Interphase is when the cell grows, makes proteins and replicates its DNA. It is the busiest part of the cycle.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting chromatids as chromosomes"}
After S phase a human cell still has 46 chromosomes, but 92 chromatids. Chromosome number is counted by centromeres.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying telomeres code for proteins"}
Telomeres are non-coding repeats. Their job is to be lost instead of genes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A chromosome is a DNA molecule wound around histones; after S phase it has two sister chromatids joined at a centromere, with telomeres at each end.
- The cell cycle is G1 (growth), S (DNA replication), G2 (preparation), mitosis and cytokinesis; interphase is the longest part.
- The fraction of cells in a phase estimates the fraction of the cycle that phase lasts.
- Telomeres are non-coding repeats that shorten with each replication, protecting genes.
- Stem cells (totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent) divide by mitosis to replace and repair tissues.
:::
