## Introduction

A goalkeeper at a football match in Bulawayo sees a penalty kick and dives within a fraction of a second. Hours later, after the match, his kidneys are still adjusting how much water he loses in urine. Both responses are coordinated, but by two very different systems: the **nervous system**, which sends electrical impulses along neurones, and the **endocrine system**, which releases hormones into the blood.

A single-celled organism is in direct contact with its surroundings. In a large multicellular animal, most cells are far from the surface and are specialised for one job, so the activities of different organs must be **coordinated**. Cells need to detect changes (stimuli), pass information on and bring about responses, both to react to the outside world and to keep the internal environment steady (homeostasis).

You should already know the basic structure of cells and membranes, and that homeostasis uses negative feedback.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Stimulus"}
A change in the internal or external environment that is detected by a receptor and may lead to a response.
:::

:::definition{term="Hormone"}
A chemical messenger, secreted by an endocrine gland directly into the blood, that is carried around the body and acts on specific target cells that have receptors for it.
:::

:::definition{term="Endocrine gland"}
A ductless gland whose secretory cells release hormones into the blood capillaries that run through it.
:::

:::definition{term="Target cell"}
A cell with specific receptor molecules, on its cell surface membrane or inside it, to which a particular hormone binds, so that the cell responds to that hormone.
:::

:::definition{term="Neurone"}
A nerve cell specialised to transmit electrical impulses (action potentials) rapidly from one part of the body to another.
:::

:::definition{term="Reflex action"}
A rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that does not involve conscious thought, brought about by a reflex arc.
:::

## The endocrine system

Endocrine glands have no ducts. Their cells secrete hormones into the blood, which carries them everywhere, but only **target cells** with complementary receptors respond. Many hormones are proteins or peptides (insulin, glucagon, ADH), which cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer and bind to receptors on the cell surface; steroid hormones (such as oestrogen) are lipid-soluble and bind to receptors inside the cell.

Three examples you must know:

| Hormone | Secreted by | Stimulus for release | Target cells | Main effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) | Posterior pituitary gland (made by neurosecretory cells in the hypothalamus) | Water potential of the blood falls | Cells lining the collecting ducts (and distal convoluted tubules) of the kidney | More water reabsorbed; smaller volume of more concentrated urine |
| Insulin | Beta (β) cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas | Blood glucose concentration rises | Liver, muscle and fat cells | Glucose taken up and stored; blood glucose falls |
| Glucagon | Alpha (α) cells of the islets of Langerhans | Blood glucose concentration falls | Liver cells | Glycogen broken down and glucose made; blood glucose rises |

How these hormones act inside their target cells belongs to homeostasis; here the point is the pattern: gland → blood → specific receptors on target cells → response.

## Comparing nervous and endocrine control

| Feature | Nervous system | Endocrine system |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of signal | Electrical impulses (action potentials) along neurones, with chemical transmission at synapses | Chemical (hormones) |
| Route | Along neurones to a precise place | In the blood plasma to all parts of the body |
| Speed | Very rapid (milliseconds) | Slower (seconds to hours) |
| Duration of effect | Usually short-lived | Often long-lasting |
| Area affected | Localised: only the cells the neurone connects with | Can be widespread: every cell with the right receptors |
| Typical responses | Muscle contraction, secretion by glands | Growth, metabolic rate, blood glucose, water balance, reproductive cycles |

Both systems use chemicals at some point (neurotransmitters at synapses; hormones in blood), and both rely on specific receptor proteins. The two systems also interact: the hypothalamus is nervous tissue that controls the pituitary gland.

## Neurones: structure and function

All neurones have a **cell body** (containing the nucleus, many mitochondria, and plenty of rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi bodies for making neurotransmitter and proteins), thin cytoplasmic extensions called **dendrites** that receive impulses, and a long **axon** that carries impulses away from the cell body.

Many vertebrate neurones are wrapped in a **myelin sheath**, made by **Schwann cells** that wind their cell surface membranes many times around the axon. The sheath is interrupted at intervals by **nodes of Ranvier**, short gaps where the axon membrane is exposed. Myelin insulates the axon and greatly speeds up transmission (the next lessons explain how).

### Three types of neurone

1. **Sensory neurone.** Carries impulses from a receptor to the central nervous system (CNS: brain and spinal cord). In a spinal reflex its cell body lies in a **dorsal root ganglion** just outside the spinal cord, part way along the neurone; a long fibre (the dendron) runs from the receptor to the cell body and a shorter axon continues into the spinal cord.
2. **Intermediate (relay) neurone.** Lies wholly inside the CNS and links sensory and motor neurones. It is short and has many connections.
3. **Motor neurone.** Carries impulses from the CNS to an **effector** (a muscle or gland). Its cell body lies in the grey matter of the CNS, with many dendrites, and a long axon runs out to the effector, ending in branches that form **neuromuscular junctions** on muscle fibres.

:::keyfact{title="Direction of impulses"}
Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone (in the CNS) → motor neurone → effector. Impulses travel one way only because synapses pass them in one direction.
:::

## The reflex arc

A **reflex arc** is the pathway of neurones in a reflex action. In a spinal reflex, such as pulling a hand away from a hot pan:

1. Heat stimulates temperature receptors and pain receptors in the skin.
2. A **sensory neurone** carries impulses to the spinal cord through the dorsal root.
3. In the grey matter, the sensory neurone synapses with a **relay neurone**.
4. The relay neurone synapses with a **motor neurone**, which leaves through the ventral root.
5. The motor neurone stimulates the **effector**, the biceps muscle, which contracts and pulls the hand away.

Because only the spinal cord is involved and there are few synapses, the response is fast and automatic. Relay neurones also send impulses up to the brain, so you become aware of the pain shortly after you have already moved. Reflexes protect the body from damage.

:::example{title="Tracing a knee-jerk reflex"}
Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches receptors in the thigh muscle, and the leg kicks forward. In this reflex the sensory neurone synapses **directly** with the motor neurone in the spinal cord, with no relay neurone.

**Predict and explain:** the knee-jerk response time is shorter than the response time of the hand-withdrawal reflex over a similar distance, because there is only one synapse in its pathway, and every synapse adds a short delay while transmitter diffuses across the cleft.
:::

:::example{title="Deciding which system controls a response"}
Rudo records two responses after a sudden loud noise.

| Response | Starts after | Lasts for |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes blink | a fraction of a second | well under a second |
| Heart rate stays raised | a few seconds | several minutes |

**Interpret:** the blink is nervous (very fast, very short, localised to the eyelid muscles). The lasting rise in heart rate involves a hormone, adrenaline, carried in the blood: it starts more slowly and lasts longer, and it affects many organs at once.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hormones travel only to their target organ"}
Hormones are carried in the blood to **every** part of the body. Only target cells respond, because only they have the specific receptors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the positions of cell bodies"}
In a spinal reflex the sensory neurone's cell body is in the dorsal root ganglion, outside the grey matter; the motor neurone's cell body is inside the grey matter of the spinal cord.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the myelin sheath part of the axon"}
Myelin is made by separate Schwann cells wrapped around the axon. The axon is the extension of the neurone itself.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
*Compare* questions (ZIMSEC Paper 2, Cambridge Paper 4) need matched pairs: "nervous impulses are fast **whereas** hormonal responses are slower". A list of features of only one system scores little.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *describe* a neurone from a drawing or photomicrograph, name the visible parts with their functions: cell body, dendrites, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, Schwann cell nucleus.
:::

:::exam-tip
For a reflex arc, use the full sequence of terms (receptor, sensory neurone, relay neurone, motor neurone, effector) and say where each synapse is.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Multicellular organisms need communication to coordinate specialised cells and maintain homeostasis.
- Endocrine glands secrete hormones into the blood; only target cells with specific receptors respond (ADH, insulin and glucagon are examples).
- Nervous control is fast, short-lived and localised; hormonal control is slower, longer-lasting and often widespread.
- Sensory, relay and motor neurones differ in position of the cell body and length of fibres; many axons are myelinated with nodes of Ranvier.
- A reflex arc (receptor → sensory → relay → motor neurone → effector) gives a rapid, automatic, protective response.
:::
