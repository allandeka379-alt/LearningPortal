## Introduction

When an athlete at Gweru's athletics track pushes off the starting blocks, millions of protein molecules in her leg muscles pull on each other in a coordinated way, powered by ATP. Muscle contraction is where the nervous system's message finally becomes movement. This lesson follows that message from the motor neurone into a muscle fibre and explains, at the level of single molecules, how a muscle shortens.

You should already know how a cholinergic synapse works, the structure of proteins and how ATP releases energy when it is hydrolysed.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Neuromuscular junction"}
A synapse between the end of a motor neurone and a muscle fibre, at which acetylcholine released from the neurone depolarises the muscle fibre membrane (sarcolemma).
:::

:::definition{term="Sarcomere"}
The repeating contractile unit of a myofibril, running from one Z line to the next.
:::

:::definition{term="Sarcoplasmic reticulum"}
The specialised endoplasmic reticulum of a muscle fibre, which stores calcium ions and releases them when the fibre is stimulated.
:::

:::definition{term="T-tubules (transverse tubules)"}
Inward folds of the sarcolemma that run deep into the muscle fibre between the myofibrils, carrying action potentials into the fibre.
:::

:::definition{term="Sliding filament model"}
The explanation of muscle contraction in which thin actin filaments slide between thick myosin filaments, pulled by repeated cycles of myosin cross-bridge formation, so that each sarcomere shortens while the filaments themselves stay the same length.
:::

## From neurone to muscle fibre

A skeletal (striated) muscle is made of many **muscle fibres**. Each fibre is a single, very long cell with many nuclei, formed when many cells fused together. Its cell membrane is the **sarcolemma**, its cytoplasm the **sarcoplasm**, and it contains many mitochondria and bundles of **myofibrils**.

1. An action potential arrives at the **neuromuscular junction** at the end of a motor neurone.
2. Calcium ions enter the neurone ending, and acetylcholine is released by exocytosis.
3. ACh diffuses across the gap and binds to receptors on the sarcolemma, opening sodium channels.
4. The sarcolemma depolarises and an action potential spreads across it.
5. The action potential travels down the **T-tubules** deep into the fibre.
6. This causes the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** to open its calcium ion channels, and Ca²⁺ diffuses out of the SR into the sarcoplasm around the myofibrils.

Acetylcholinesterase in the junction breaks down ACh so that each nerve impulse causes only a brief stimulation. One motor neurone branches to supply many fibres; the neurone and its fibres form a **motor unit**.

## The ultrastructure of striated muscle

Under the electron microscope a myofibril shows alternating light and dark bands, which is why the muscle is called striated (striped).

| Feature | Appearance in an electron micrograph | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| Z line | Thin dark line crossing the light band | Where actin filaments are anchored; marks the ends of a sarcomere |
| I band | Light band, crossed by the Z line | Actin filaments only |
| A band | Dark band in the middle of the sarcomere | The whole length of the myosin filaments, overlapping with actin at each end |
| H zone | Lighter region in the centre of the A band | Myosin filaments only |
| M line | Line in the middle of the H zone | Where myosin filaments are held together |

**Thin filaments** are made mainly of two chains of **actin** twisted together. Wound along them is **tropomyosin**, a fibrous protein, with **troponin** molecules attached at intervals. **Thick filaments** are made of **myosin**, whose molecules have long tails and protruding globular **heads**. Each head can bind to actin and acts as an ATPase.

A cross-section through the region of overlap shows each thick filament surrounded by six thin ones, arranged in a hexagonal pattern.

## The sliding filament model

When a muscle contracts, the actin filaments slide between the myosin filaments towards the M line. The sequence of events is:

1. **At rest**, tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin, so the myosin heads cannot attach.
2. **Calcium ions bind to troponin.** Troponin changes shape and pulls tropomyosin away from the binding sites, exposing them.
3. **Cross-bridges form.** Myosin heads, which already carry ADP and Pi from an earlier hydrolysis, bind to the exposed sites on actin.
4. **Power stroke.** Each head releases ADP and Pi and tilts, pulling the actin filament a short distance towards the centre of the sarcomere.
5. **Detachment.** A new ATP molecule binds to the myosin head, which makes it detach from actin.
6. **Recovery.** The myosin head hydrolyses the ATP to ADP and Pi (myosin acts as an ATPase), using the energy to return to its upright, "cocked" position, ready to bind again further along.
7. The cycle repeats many times per second as long as Ca²⁺ and ATP are present, so the filaments slide further and the sarcomere shortens.
8. **Relaxation.** When stimulation stops, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by **active transport** (using ATP). Troponin returns to its original shape, tropomyosin covers the binding sites again, and the muscle relaxes (it is pulled back to length by an antagonistic muscle).

| Band or zone | During contraction | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Sarcomere (Z to Z) | Shorter | Z lines pulled together |
| A band | Stays the same | It is the length of the myosin filaments, which do not change |
| I band | Shorter | Actin slides further into the A band |
| H zone | Shorter (may disappear) | More overlap of actin and myosin |

:::worked-solution[Measuring a sarcomere from an electron micrograph]
An electron micrograph at a magnification of ×20 000 shows one sarcomere of a relaxed fibre measuring 48 mm from Z line to Z line. After contraction the same sarcomere measures 38 mm.
1. Actual length $= \dfrac{\text{image length}}{\text{magnification}}$. Convert 48 mm to µm: $48 \times 1000 = 48\,000$ µm.
2. Relaxed length $= 48\,000 \div 20\,000 = 2.4$ µm.
3. Contracted length $= 38\,000 \div 20\,000 = 1.9$ µm.
4. Percentage shortening $= \dfrac{2.4 - 1.9}{2.4} \times 100 = 20.8\%$ (3 s.f.).
:::

:::worked-solution[Predicting band lengths]
In a muscle, each myosin filament is 1.6 µm long and each actin filament extends 1.0 µm from the Z line. Find the I band and H zone for sarcomere lengths of 2.4, 2.2 and 2.0 µm.
1. The A band equals the myosin length: 1.6 µm at every sarcomere length.
2. I band = sarcomere length − A band: $2.4 - 1.6 = 0.8$; $2.2 - 1.6 = 0.6$; $2.0 - 1.6 = 0.4$ µm.
3. H zone = sarcomere length − (2 × actin length) $= 2.4 - 2.0 = 0.4$; $2.2 - 2.0 = 0.2$; $2.0 - 2.0 = 0$ µm.
4. At 2.0 µm the H zone has disappeared: the actin filaments from both ends meet in the middle.
:::

## The supply of ATP

Muscle uses ATP for three things: the detachment of myosin heads, the recovery stroke of the heads, and pumping Ca²⁺ back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum. ATP is supplied by aerobic respiration in the many mitochondria, by anaerobic respiration (glycolysis) during intense exercise, and by **creatine phosphate**, which can quickly transfer its phosphate to ADP to regenerate ATP.

:::example{title="Explaining rigor mortis"}
After death, muscles become stiff. **Explain:** respiration stops, so no new ATP is made. Calcium ions leak out of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and cannot be pumped back, so binding sites on actin stay exposed and cross-bridges form. Without ATP the myosin heads cannot detach, so the cross-bridges stay locked and the muscle cannot relax.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the filaments shorten"}
Neither actin nor myosin changes length. The filaments slide past each other, so the overlap increases and the sarcomere shortens.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ATP is needed to form the cross-bridge"}
ATP is needed to break the cross-bridge (detachment) and for the recovery stroke. The power stroke happens as ADP and Pi are released.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up troponin and tropomyosin"}
Calcium binds to **troponin**; troponin then moves **tropomyosin**, which was blocking the binding sites.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the A band shortens"}
The A band is the length of the myosin filaments and stays constant. The I band and H zone shorten.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Cambridge Paper 4 often gives an electron micrograph of contracted and relaxed sarcomeres described in words or as measurements. *Calculate* sarcomere lengths with actual size = image ÷ magnification, converting mm to µm, and state which bands changed.
:::

:::exam-tip
To *explain* the role of calcium, give the chain in order: Ca²⁺ released from the SR → binds troponin → troponin changes shape → tropomyosin moves → binding sites on actin exposed → cross-bridges form.
:::

:::exam-tip
For the role of ATP, give both uses at the myosin head (detachment and re-cocking) and the active transport of Ca²⁺ back into the SR.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- At the neuromuscular junction, ACh depolarises the sarcolemma; action potentials pass down T-tubules and cause Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
- A sarcomere runs Z line to Z line: I band (actin only), A band (myosin length), H zone (myosin only).
- Ca²⁺ binds troponin, which moves tropomyosin and exposes binding sites on actin; myosin heads form cross-bridges and perform the power stroke.
- ATP detaches myosin heads and re-cocks them, and powers the pumping of Ca²⁺ back into the SR.
- During contraction the sarcomere, I band and H zone shorten; the A band stays the same.
:::
