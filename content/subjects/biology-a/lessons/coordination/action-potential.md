## Introduction

A pinch of salt on a slice of sadza is detected within a fraction of a second. Chemoreceptor cells in your taste buds turn the chemical stimulus into an electrical change, and sensory neurones carry the message to the brain as a stream of **action potentials**. Every nerve impulse in your body, from the one that makes your heart speed up to the one that moves a finger, is the same kind of event: a brief reversal of the electrical charge across the membrane of a neurone.

This lesson explains how a neurone keeps a steady charge across its membrane when it is resting, how a stimulus changes that charge, and why the change is always the same size. You should already know the structure of the cell surface membrane, the difference between facilitated diffusion and active transport, and the structure of a neurone.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transducer"}
A cell or structure that converts energy or information in one form (such as a chemical, light or pressure stimulus) into another form, here an electrical change across a membrane.
:::

:::definition{term="Resting potential"}
The potential difference across the membrane of a neurone that is not transmitting an impulse, with the inside negative relative to the outside; about −70 mV.
:::

:::definition{term="Action potential"}
A rapid, temporary reversal of the potential difference across the membrane of a neurone or muscle cell, from about −70 mV to about +30 to +40 mV and back, caused by the opening and closing of voltage-gated ion channels.
:::

:::definition{term="Threshold"}
The potential difference (about −50 to −55 mV in many neurones) that must be reached by depolarisation before an action potential is triggered.
:::

:::definition{term="Refractory period"}
The short time after an action potential during which a further action potential cannot be generated (absolute) or needs a stronger stimulus than usual (relative).
:::

:::keyfact{title="Values to learn (approximate)"}
- Resting potential: about −70 mV.
- Threshold: about −50 to −55 mV.
- Peak of the action potential: about +30 to +40 mV.
- The sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in for each ATP hydrolysed.
:::

## Sensory receptors as transducers

A **sensory receptor** detects a particular stimulus. Each type is specialised: photoreceptors respond to light, mechanoreceptors to pressure, thermoreceptors to temperature and chemoreceptors to particular chemicals. Whatever the stimulus, the receptor converts it into an electrical change across a membrane, which is why receptors are called **transducers**.

### The chemoreceptor in a taste bud

Taste buds on the tongue contain chemoreceptor cells with microvilli that project into a pore on the surface. Salt detection works like this:

1. Sodium ions from salty food diffuse through sodium ion channels in the microvilli membrane into the receptor cell.
2. The entry of positive ions **depolarises** the receptor cell: its inside becomes less negative. This change is a **receptor potential**.
3. The bigger the stimulus, the bigger the receptor potential: it is graded, not all-or-nothing.
4. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated calcium ion channels; calcium ions enter and cause vesicles of neurotransmitter to be released onto a sensory neurone.
5. The neurotransmitter depolarises the sensory neurone. If this **generator potential** reaches the threshold, an action potential is set up and travels to the brain.

A stronger stimulus does not produce bigger action potentials. Instead, the receptor stays above threshold for longer and the sensory neurone fires action potentials **more often**. Frequency, not size, carries information about stimulus strength.

## The resting potential

When a neurone is not transmitting an impulse, its inside is about 70 mV more negative than the outside. Two things set this up.

1. **The sodium-potassium pump.** This carrier protein in the membrane uses energy from the hydrolysis of ATP to move **3 Na⁺ out** of the axon for every **2 K⁺ in**. This creates concentration gradients: much more Na⁺ outside, much more K⁺ inside. Because more positive charge leaves than enters, the pump itself makes the inside slightly negative.
2. **Differential permeability.** At rest, most voltage-gated channels are closed, but some potassium ion channels are open. The membrane is therefore much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺. Potassium ions diffuse **out** down their concentration gradient, leaving the inside more negative, until the electrical attraction pulling K⁺ back in balances the diffusion outwards.

Large negatively charged molecules inside the axon (such as proteins) cannot leave, which also contributes. The result is a steady potential difference of about −70 mV: the membrane is **polarised**.

## The action potential, stage by stage

A potential-against-time trace for one point on an axon has a characteristic shape. The stages are numbered here in the order they happen.

1. **Stimulus and threshold.** A stimulus (or a local current from a neighbouring region) makes the inside slightly less negative. A few voltage-gated sodium ion channels open. If the membrane reaches the **threshold**, about −50 mV, many more open: positive feedback.
2. **Depolarisation.** Voltage-gated **sodium** channels open, and Na⁺ floods **in** by diffusion down its electrochemical gradient. The inside becomes positive, reaching about **+30 to +40 mV**.
3. **Repolarisation.** About 1 ms after opening, the sodium channels close (they become inactivated). Voltage-gated **potassium** channels open, and K⁺ diffuses **out**, so the inside becomes negative again.
4. **Hyperpolarisation.** The potassium channels are slow to close, so too many K⁺ leave and the potential overshoots to about −80 mV, more negative than the resting value.
5. **Return to resting potential.** The voltage-gated potassium channels close, and the sodium-potassium pump with the leakage of K⁺ through the always-open channels restores the resting potential of about −70 mV.

| Stage | Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels | Voltage-gated K⁺ channels | Main ion movement | Potential |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Resting | Closed | Closed | Pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in; K⁺ leaks out | About −70 mV |
| Depolarisation | Open | Closed | Na⁺ diffuses in | Rises to about +30 to +40 mV |
| Repolarisation | Closed (inactivated) | Open | K⁺ diffuses out | Falls towards −70 mV |
| Hyperpolarisation | Closed | Still open | K⁺ continues to leave | About −80 mV |

The whole event lasts about 2-3 ms.

### All or nothing

If a stimulus fails to reach the threshold, no action potential occurs. If it reaches the threshold, a full-sized action potential always occurs. A bigger stimulus does not give a bigger action potential. This is the **all-or-nothing principle**.

### The refractory period

During and just after an action potential, the sodium channels are inactivated and cannot reopen at once. For about 1 ms (the **absolute refractory period**) no new action potential can be generated however strong the stimulus. For a few milliseconds more (the **relative refractory period**), during hyperpolarisation, a larger-than-normal stimulus is needed. The refractory period means action potentials are separate (discrete) events and sets a maximum frequency of impulses. Its role in one-way transmission is covered in the next lesson.

:::worked-solution[Reading a potential-against-time trace]
A trace for one point on an axon gives these readings:

| Time / ms | 0.0 | 0.5 | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 4.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potential / mV | −70 | −55 | +35 | −10 | −65 | −80 | −75 | −70 |

1. The resting potential is −70 mV (at 0 ms and again at 4 ms).
2. The peak is +35 mV at 1.0 ms. The total change from rest to peak is $35 - (-70) = 105$ mV.
3. Depolarisation (rising phase) occurs between 0.5 and 1.0 ms; sodium channels are open.
4. Repolarisation occurs between 1.0 and 2.0 ms; potassium channels are open and sodium channels closed.
5. Hyperpolarisation is at 2.5 ms (−80 mV, below the resting value), then the potential returns to −70 mV by 4.0 ms.
:::

:::worked-solution[Maximum frequency of impulses]
An axon has an absolute refractory period of 2 ms.
1. The shortest possible time between the starts of two action potentials is 2 ms $= 0.002$ s.
2. Maximum frequency $= \dfrac{1}{0.002} = 500$ impulses per second.
3. In practice the frequency is lower, because during the relative refractory period a stronger stimulus is needed.
:::

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the sodium-potassium pump causes depolarisation"}
Depolarisation is caused by sodium ions diffusing in through voltage-gated sodium channels. The pump works all the time and maintains the gradients; it does not produce the action potential.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a stronger stimulus gives a bigger action potential"}
Action potentials are all-or-nothing and always the same size. A stronger stimulus raises the frequency of action potentials.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up receptor potentials and action potentials"}
A receptor (generator) potential is graded: its size depends on the stimulus. An action potential is all-or-nothing and only occurs once the threshold is reached.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Trace questions (ZIMSEC Paper 2, Cambridge Paper 4) ask you to *describe* and *explain* each phase. For each phase name the channel (voltage-gated sodium or potassium), say whether it is open or closed, give the direction of the ion movement and quote the potential with its unit from the data.
:::

:::exam-tip
To *explain* the resting potential, give both causes: the pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, using ATP) and the membrane being more permeable to K⁺, which diffuses out.
:::

:::exam-tip
In *calculate* questions, a change from −70 mV to +35 mV is 105 mV, not 35 mV: subtract the negative value.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Receptors are transducers; a chemoreceptor in a taste bud produces a graded receptor potential that can trigger action potentials in a sensory neurone.
- The resting potential (about −70 mV) is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in) and a membrane more permeable to K⁺.
- At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ enters (depolarisation to about +30 to +40 mV); then K⁺ channels open and K⁺ leaves (repolarisation, then hyperpolarisation).
- Action potentials are all-or-nothing; stimulus strength is coded by frequency.
- The refractory period makes impulses discrete and limits their maximum frequency.
:::
