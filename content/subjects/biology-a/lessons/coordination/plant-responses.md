## Introduction

Plants have no nervous system and no muscles, yet they detect light, gravity, touch and water, and respond in coordinated ways. A maize seedling bends towards light, a sorghum grain germinates only when the soil is moist, and the leaves of some plants move within a fraction of a second. Plants use two kinds of signal: **electrical signals** (including action potentials) for rapid responses, and **plant hormones** (plant growth regulators) for slower responses such as growth and germination.

This lesson takes three examples set by the Cambridge syllabus: the Venus fly trap, auxin and elongation growth, and gibberellin in germinating barley. You should already know about cell walls, turgor and water potential, active transport and the action potential in neurones.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plant hormone (plant growth regulator)"}
A chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that affects growth or development, either in the cells that make it or in other parts of the plant after transport.
:::

:::definition{term="Auxin"}
A plant hormone (mainly indole-3-acetic acid, IAA) made in shoot tips that stimulates the elongation of cells behind the tip.
:::

:::definition{term="Gibberellin"}
A plant hormone that stimulates stem elongation and, in seeds such as barley, switches on the production of enzymes that mobilise food stores during germination.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid growth hypothesis"}
The explanation that auxin causes cell elongation by stimulating proton pumps to move H⁺ into the cell wall; the low pH activates proteins (expansins) that loosen the links between cellulose microfibrils, so the wall stretches as the cell takes up water.
:::

## Rapid responses: the Venus fly trap

The Venus fly trap (*Dionaea muscipula*) grows in nitrogen-poor bogs and gains extra nitrogen by trapping and digesting insects. Each trap is a modified leaf of two lobes, hinged along a midrib, with stiff bristles around the edges. On the inner surface of each lobe are a few **sensory hairs** (trigger hairs).

1. An insect touches a sensory hair. The hair bends, which deforms the membrane of cells at its base and opens ion channels. The cells depolarise, producing a **receptor potential**.
2. If this reaches threshold, an **action potential** is generated and spreads across the lobes. Unlike neurone action potentials, plant action potentials involve the movement of calcium ions in and chloride and potassium ions out, and they are much slower.
3. **One stimulus is not enough.** The trap closes only if a **second** hair is touched (or the same hair touched again) within about 20-30 seconds. The first action potential is "remembered", probably as a rise in calcium ion concentration in the cells. This prevents the trap wasting energy closing on raindrops or debris.
4. After the second action potential, the trap snaps shut in about 0.1-0.3 seconds. The action potentials cause **ions to move** in cells of the lobes and **proton pumping** into cell walls; water moves by osmosis, changing the **turgor** of cells on the inner and outer surfaces of the lobes.
5. The lobes are curved outwards (convex) when open. The rapid change in turgor and the **loosening of cell walls** make them **flip** suddenly to a concave shape, so the lobes snap together, and the bristles interlock to form a cage.
6. If the prey keeps moving, further stimulation causes the trap to seal tightly and glands secrete digestive enzymes. Nutrients are absorbed over several days, after which the trap reopens by growth of cells on the inner surface.

The details of the flipping mechanism are still being researched; the key points are the two stimuli, the action potentials, the ion movements and the turgor and cell wall changes.

## Auxin and elongation growth

Plant growth happens at **meristems**: cells divide there and then elongate in the region just behind the tip. Auxin is made in the shoot tip and moves down the shoot, from cell to cell, to the region of elongation.

1. Auxin binds to a **receptor protein** in the cell.
2. This stimulates **proton pumps** (ATPases) in the cell surface membrane to actively transport **hydrogen ions** out of the cytoplasm into the cell wall.
3. The cell wall becomes **acidic** (its pH falls to about 4.5-5).
4. The low pH activates proteins called **expansins**, which break the hydrogen bonds linking cellulose microfibrils to other polysaccharides in the wall.
5. The wall becomes more **extensible** (it loosens).
6. The cell absorbs water by **osmosis** (aided by the uptake of ions), and turgor pressure stretches the loosened wall, so the cell **elongates**.
7. As the cell matures, new wall material is laid down and the wall stiffens, fixing the new length.

Because auxin is moved away from light, and away from the upper side in a horizontal shoot, cells on one side elongate more than the other and the shoot bends: this is how phototropism and gravitropism are brought about.

:::example{title="Testing the acid growth hypothesis"}
Segments of oat coleoptile were placed in buffer solutions of different pH, without auxin. Segments in pH 4.5 elongated almost as much as segments given auxin, whereas segments in pH 7 hardly elongated. **Interpret:** acidity alone loosens the walls, which supports the idea that auxin acts by making the wall acidic. A further test: a drug that blocks proton pumps should prevent auxin-stimulated growth.
:::

## Gibberellin and the germination of barley

A barley grain is a fruit containing a single seed. It has an **embryo** at one end, a large **endosperm** full of starch, and around the endosperm a thin **aleurone layer** of living cells rich in protein. Germination follows this sequence:

1. The grain absorbs water (imbibition). This activates the embryo.
2. The embryo produces **gibberellin**, which diffuses to the **aleurone layer**.
3. In the aleurone cells, gibberellin switches on the transcription of the gene for **amylase** (how it does this, by causing the breakdown of DELLA proteins, is in the lesson on control of gene expression).
4. The aleurone cells synthesise amylase and secrete it into the endosperm.
5. Amylase **hydrolyses starch** into maltose, which is further broken down to glucose.
6. The sugars diffuse to the embryo, where they are used in respiration to provide ATP and as raw materials for growth, so the embryo grows into a seedling.

The same process occurs in the malting of barley for brewing, and in the germination of other cereals such as maize, sorghum and wheat.

:::worked-solution[Amylase activity in barley grains]
Half-grains of barley (with the embryo removed) were incubated with or without gibberellin, and amylase activity was measured after 24 hours.

| Treatment | Amylase activity / arbitrary units |
|---|---|
| Half-grains with no embryo, no gibberellin | 4 |
| Half-grains with no embryo, plus gibberellin | 50 |
| Whole grains (embryo present) | 46 |

1. Removing the embryo reduces activity to 4 units, so the embryo is needed for amylase production.
2. Adding gibberellin to half-grains without an embryo raises activity to 50 units, about the same as whole grains.
3. Increase caused by gibberellin $= \dfrac{50 - 4}{4} \times 100 = 1150\%$.
4. Conclusion: the embryo's role can be replaced by gibberellin, so the embryo normally stimulates amylase production by releasing gibberellin.
:::

:::worked-solution[Timing in a Venus fly trap]
A trap's sensory hairs are touched at the times shown. The trap closes only if two touches occur within 25 s.

| Touch | Time / s |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0 |
| 2 | 40 |
| 3 | 58 |

1. Interval between touches 1 and 2 $= 40 - 0 = 40$ s, longer than 25 s, so the trap does not close at touch 2.
2. Interval between touches 2 and 3 $= 58 - 40 = 18$ s, shorter than 25 s, so the trap closes at touch 3, 58 s after the first touch.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying auxin makes cells divide"}
In elongation growth, auxin causes cells to **elongate** by loosening their walls. Division happens in the meristem.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying gibberellin digests starch"}
Gibberellin is a signal. It causes the aleurone layer to make amylase; the amylase hydrolyses the starch.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the fly trap closes after one touch"}
Two stimuli within a short time are needed. One touch produces an action potential but no closure.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the aleurone layer stores starch"}
Starch is stored in the endosperm. The aleurone layer is a thin layer of living cells that secretes enzymes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
*Explain* questions on auxin (Cambridge Paper 4) reward the chain: auxin → proton pumps → H⁺ into wall → low pH → expansins → bonds broken → wall loosens → water uptake → elongation.
:::

:::exam-tip
For barley germination, name every location: embryo (gibberellin), aleurone layer (amylase made), endosperm (starch hydrolysed), back to the embryo (sugars used).
:::

:::exam-tip
In data questions on half-grains, compare treatments in pairs and quote values: the embryo can be replaced by gibberellin.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Plants use electrical signals for rapid responses and hormones for slower responses.
- The Venus fly trap closes after two stimuli of its sensory hairs within about 20-30 s; action potentials cause ion movements, turgor changes and wall loosening, so the lobes flip shut.
- Auxin stimulates proton pumping into cell walls; the low pH activates expansins, loosening the wall so cells elongate as they take up water.
- In barley, gibberellin from the embryo causes the aleurone layer to make amylase, which hydrolyses starch in the endosperm to sugars for the embryo.
:::
