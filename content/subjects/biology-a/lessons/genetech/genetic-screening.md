## Introduction

A couple whose first child has cystic fibrosis wants to know the chance that a second child will be affected, and whether anything can be done. A woman whose mother and aunt both had breast cancer wonders whether she has inherited a high-risk allele. A young man knows that his father has Huntington's disease and must decide whether he wants to know his own future. Genetic screening can answer these questions, but every answer raises further questions.

This lesson explains how screening is done, what it offers for four conditions, and why trained counsellors and careful ethical thinking are essential. You should already understand monohybrid inheritance (dominant, recessive and codominant alleles), carriers, and how PCR and gel electrophoresis analyse DNA.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Genetic screening"}
Testing a person's DNA (or chromosomes or gene products) to find out whether they have, or carry, alleles that cause or increase the risk of a genetic condition.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrier"}
A person who is heterozygous for a recessive allele: they do not have the condition but can pass the allele to their children.
:::

:::definition{term="Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)"}
Testing one or a few cells removed from embryos produced by IVF, so that only embryos without the harmful alleles are placed in the uterus.
:::

:::definition{term="Genetic counselling"}
Advice and support from a trained professional who explains the results of genetic tests, the risks to the person and their relatives, and the options available, so that people can make informed decisions of their own.
:::

## How screening is carried out

### Adults and children

A blood sample or a cheek swab provides cells. DNA is extracted, the relevant gene is amplified by PCR and the alleles are identified, for example by sequencing, by DNA probes or by gel electrophoresis of fragments. Newborn babies are screened in many countries by a heel-prick blood sample.

### Embryos: pre-implantation genetic diagnosis

1. Eggs are fertilised in vitro (IVF).
2. After a few days, one or a few cells are removed from each embryo; the embryo continues to develop normally.
3. The DNA of these cells is amplified and tested.
4. Only embryos without the harmful allele (or combination) are transferred to the uterus.

### Fetuses: prenatal testing

| Method | When (about) | Sample | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amniocentesis | From about 15 weeks of pregnancy | A needle, guided by ultrasound, withdraws a little amniotic fluid containing fetal cells | Small risk of miscarriage; results later in pregnancy |
| Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) | About 10-13 weeks | A small sample of the chorionic villi (part of the placenta, genetically fetal) | Earlier result; small risk of miscarriage, slightly higher than amniocentesis |

The fetal cells are cultured or their DNA amplified directly, then tested for the alleles or chromosome abnormalities in question.

## Four conditions

### Breast cancer: BRCA1 and BRCA2

*BRCA1* and *BRCA2* are **tumour suppressor genes** whose proteins help to repair damaged DNA. Some mutant alleles greatly **increase the risk** of breast and ovarian cancer (and prostate cancer in men), although not everyone who inherits one develops cancer. A woman who tests positive can choose more frequent screening (such as mammograms or MRI scans) to detect cancer early, and may consider preventive surgery. A negative result can bring relief to someone with a family history.

### Huntington's disease

Caused by a **dominant** allele of the *HTT* gene with an expanded CAG repeat. Symptoms (loss of muscle coordination, cognitive decline) usually start in middle age, often after people have had children, and there is no cure. Each child of an affected heterozygous parent has a $\frac{1}{2}$ chance of inheriting the allele. A test can tell an at-risk adult whether they will develop the disease; some choose not to know.

### Cystic fibrosis

Caused by **recessive** alleles of the *CFTR* gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel; thick, sticky mucus builds up in the lungs and pancreas. Screening identifies **carriers**, affected newborns (so treatment starts early) and affected embryos or fetuses.

### Sickle cell anaemia

Caused by the $Hb^S$ allele of the β-globin gene (codominant with $Hb^A$). Carriers ($Hb^A Hb^S$) are common in parts of Africa where malaria is common, because carriers are partly protected against severe malaria. Screening of couples and newborns allows informed choices and early treatment, such as antibiotics to prevent infections.

:::worked-solution[Risks for a couple who are both carriers of cystic fibrosis]
Let $F$ = normal allele, $f$ = cystic fibrosis allele. Both parents are carriers.

Parent genotypes: $Ff$ × $Ff$. Gametes: $F$, $f$ and $F$, $f$.

| | $F$ | $f$ |
|---|---|---|
| $F$ | $FF$ | $Ff$ |
| $f$ | $Ff$ | $ff$ |

1. Probability that a child has cystic fibrosis $= \frac{1}{4}$ (25%).
2. Probability that a child is a carrier $= \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$.
3. If a child is known to be **unaffected**, the probability that it is a carrier is $\frac{2}{3}$ (two of the three unaffected outcomes).
4. Each pregnancy is independent: having one affected child does not change the risk for the next.
:::

:::worked-solution[Probability for Huntington's disease]
A man is heterozygous for the Huntington's allele ($Hh$); his partner is $hh$.
1. Gametes: $H$, $h$ and $h$.
2. Offspring: $Hh$ and $hh$ in equal numbers.
3. Each child has a $\frac{1}{2}$ (50%) chance of inheriting the allele and developing the disease.
4. The probability that **both** of two children inherit it $= \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.
:::

## Why genetic counselling is needed

Results are rarely simple. A counsellor:

1. explains what a result means, including **risks rather than certainties** (a BRCA allele raises risk; it does not guarantee cancer);
2. explains the implications for **relatives**, who share alleles;
3. sets out the options (further tests, monitoring, PGD, prenatal testing, adoption, or none) without directing the choice;
4. provides emotional support before and after testing, especially for untreatable late-onset conditions such as Huntington's disease.

## Social and ethical considerations

- **Benefits**: early treatment or monitoring; informed reproductive choices; relief from uncertainty for those who test negative.
- **Psychological effects**: knowing about an incurable future condition can cause anxiety and depression; some people prefer not to know.
- **Decisions about embryos and fetuses**: PGD and prenatal tests can lead to choices about which embryos to implant or whether to continue a pregnancy. Some religious groups and others regard the destruction of embryos or termination as wrong; others see these as a matter of personal choice.
- **Privacy and discrimination**: concerns that employers or insurers might use results unfairly; a result for one person reveals information about relatives who did not consent.
- **"Designer babies"**: concerns that screening could extend from serious disease to selecting desired traits.
- **Access and cost**: tests and counselling may be available only to those who can pay.

:::example{title="Advising a family about Huntington's disease"}
Tatenda, aged 25, knows his mother has Huntington's disease. He and his partner are planning a family.

**Discuss.** Tatenda has a 50% chance of carrying the allele. A test could tell him for certain, allowing him to plan, and if positive the couple could use PGD to have children without the allele. But a positive result means knowing he will develop an incurable disease, which may cause great distress, and it may also reveal information to his relatives. A genetic counsellor would explain these options and support whatever decision the couple makes, including choosing not to be tested.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a BRCA allele means a person will get cancer"}
It increases the **risk**; many carriers never develop cancer, and most breast cancers are not caused by inherited BRCA alleles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying having one affected child lowers the risk for the next"}
Each child is an independent event, with the same probability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the counsellor's role as making the decision"}
Counsellors inform and support; the decision belongs to the individual or couple.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To *describe* how screening is carried out, name who is tested, how the sample is obtained, and how the DNA is analysed (PCR, probes or electrophoresis). For *discuss* questions on ethics, give arguments on both sides and attribute views to groups.
:::

:::exam-tip
Probability questions: draw the genetic diagram first, and note whether the question asks about any child, an unaffected child, or two children.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Genetic screening tests DNA for alleles that cause or raise the risk of genetic conditions, in adults, newborns, embryos (PGD) and fetuses (amniocentesis, CVS).
- BRCA1/BRCA2 alleles raise breast and ovarian cancer risk; screening allows monitoring and preventive choices.
- Huntington's disease is dominant and late-onset; cystic fibrosis and sickle cell anaemia are recessive, so carriers can be identified.
- Genetic counselling explains risks, implications for relatives and options, and supports decisions.
- Ethical issues include anxiety, decisions about embryos and fetuses, privacy, discrimination, designer babies and access.
:::
