## Introduction

Gene technology lets us read, copy and change DNA. Scientists can tell a family the chance that a child will inherit a disease, give a bacterium a human gene, or identify a person from a single hair. Whether we **should** do each of these things is not a question that biology alone can answer. It depends on values: about health, fairness, privacy, nature, religion and the rights of future generations.

ZIMSEC Paper 3 often asks you to *discuss* the social and ethical implications of gene technology. Examiners reward accurate biology, arguments on both sides attributed to groups of people, and a reasoned conclusion. This lesson gives you the issues and a method for writing about them. It builds on the lessons on recombinant DNA, insulin production, PCR and fingerprinting, genetic screening, gene therapy and GM crops.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ethics"}
The study of what is morally right and wrong, used to judge whether an action should be taken, not only whether it can be.
:::

:::definition{term="Social implication"}
An effect of a technology on society: on people's health, money, jobs, relationships, rights or equality.
:::

:::definition{term="Informed consent"}
Agreement to a procedure or the use of personal data, given freely by a person who understands what it involves, its risks and its alternatives.
:::

:::definition{term="Patent"}
A legal right, granted for a limited period, that gives the owner of an invention (such as a GM seed variety or a method) control over its use and sale.
:::

## A framework for ethical questions

For any use of gene technology, ask:

1. **Benefits**: who gains, and how much (health, food, income, justice)?
2. **Risks and harms**: to people, other organisms and the environment; are they known or uncertain?
3. **Rights and consent**: whose consent is needed, and can they give it (children, embryos, future generations, relatives)?
4. **Justice and access**: who can afford it, and who bears the costs?
5. **Values and beliefs**: does it conflict with religious, cultural or personal beliefs about life and nature?

## Issues in medicine

### Genetic screening

- **For**: early treatment and monitoring; informed choices about having children; relief for those who test negative.
- **Against or concerns**: anxiety from knowing about an untreatable condition; screening of embryos and fetuses can lead to decisions about destroying embryos or ending a pregnancy, which some religious groups oppose; results also reveal information about relatives who have not consented.

### "Designer babies"

Pre-implantation diagnosis and gene editing could in principle be used to choose traits that are not medical, such as eye colour or height. Many people fear this would treat children as products, increase inequality between those who can and cannot pay, and reduce acceptance of people with disabilities. Others argue that parents already make many choices for their children and that preventing serious disease is clearly good. Most countries limit selection to serious medical conditions.

### Gene therapy

- **For**: may treat or cure serious inherited diseases such as SCID.
- **Against or concerns**: risks in trials (immune reactions, cancer from insertion); very high cost; germ-line changes would affect future generations who cannot consent.

### Insurance, employment and privacy

If insurers or employers obtained genetic test results, people with high-risk alleles might be refused cover or jobs for a condition they may never develop. Many people therefore argue that genetic information should be confidential and protected by law, and some may avoid useful tests for fear of discrimination.

## Issues in agriculture

- **Food safety**: GM foods are tested before approval, and supporters point to their wide use without proven harm; critics want more long-term studies and clear **labelling** so consumers can choose.
- **Environment**: possible gene flow to wild relatives, resistance in pests and weeds, and effects on non-target species, balanced against reduced insecticide use and less tillage.
- **Farmers' rights and patents**: companies patent GM seed to recover research costs; farmers may have to buy new seed every season instead of saving it, which can be hard for small-scale farmers.
- **Food security**: supporters see higher yields as vital; critics argue that hunger is often due to poverty and distribution.
- **Animal welfare**: some groups object to modifying animals such as salmon for faster growth.

## Forensic DNA databases

National DNA databases help police link crimes to suspects and **exclude** innocent people, and fingerprinting has also freed wrongly convicted prisoners. But they raise questions:

- Should DNA be kept from people who were arrested but not convicted?
- Who can access the data, and for what purposes?
- Could family members be traced through a relative's profile without their consent?
- Could errors, contamination or misinterpretation lead to wrongful convictions?

## Religious and cultural viewpoints

Views vary within as well as between faiths and cultures:

- Some religious groups believe that altering genes, especially in humans or the germ line, is "playing God" or interfering with creation; others regard healing through medicine, including gene technology, as a duty.
- Some groups object to the destruction of embryos in screening and research.
- Dietary laws can make some sources of products unacceptable, which is one reason recombinant human insulin is preferred by some people to pork insulin.
- In many communities, decisions about health and children involve the wider family, which affects how consent and counselling are carried out.

:::example{title="Building a balanced argument: should newborns be screened for sickle cell anaemia?"}
**State the biology.** Sickle cell anaemia is caused by two $Hb^S$ alleles; a heel-prick blood test can identify affected babies and carriers.

**Arguments for.** Early diagnosis allows preventive antibiotics and vaccinations and education of parents, reducing serious infections and deaths in early childhood. Identifying carriers informs future family planning.

**Arguments against or concerns.** Identifying carriers could lead to stigma; results must be kept confidential; the programme costs money that might be spent on other health needs, and treatment must be available for screening to help.

**Conclude.** Many health professionals support newborn screening where treatment and counselling are available, provided results are confidential and families give informed consent.
:::

:::example{title="Evaluating an argument"}
Claim: "GM crops should be banned because they are unnatural."

**Evaluate.** "Unnatural" is not a clear reason on its own: selective breeding has changed crops greatly, and many accepted medicines are made by genetic engineering. A stronger argument against a GM crop would identify a specific risk (such as gene flow to wild relatives or dependence on seed companies) and the evidence for it. A balanced answer weighs these risks against measured benefits for that crop in that place.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
This concept is ZIMSEC only (Form 6), examined mainly in Paper 3 essays. Cambridge asks for the social and ethical considerations of screening, gene therapy and GM food within those topics.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving opinions without biology"}
Every ethical point should rest on an accurate biological fact: what the technology does and what it can and cannot achieve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Arguing only one side"}
*Discuss* needs both sides. Attribute views to groups ("some religious groups", "farmers' organisations", "medical researchers"), not to "people say".
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing risk with certainty"}
A positive BRCA test, or a GM crop's potential for gene flow, is a risk, not a certainty. Say so.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Plan a ZIMSEC Paper 3 *discuss* answer in four parts: the biology in a sentence or two; benefits; concerns (ethical, social, environmental, economic); a conclusion that weighs them. Each distinct, accurate point can earn a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use precise terms: informed consent, confidentiality, germ line, gene flow, patent, discrimination. Avoid emotional language.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ethical questions ask whether gene technology should be used, considering benefits, risks, consent, justice and values.
- Medicine: screening, designer babies, gene therapy and genetic privacy raise issues of consent, anxiety, discrimination and cost.
- Agriculture: food safety and labelling, environmental risks, patents and farmers' rights, food security and animal welfare.
- Forensic DNA databases help solve crimes and free the innocent, but raise privacy and consent issues.
- Religious and cultural views differ; good answers present both sides, attribute views to groups and conclude.
:::
