## Introduction

Cotton farmers in the Zambezi valley and the south-east lowveld spend much of the season fighting bollworms, caterpillars that bore into cotton bolls. Conventional control means spraying insecticide again and again. In many other cotton-growing countries, farmers grow cotton plants that make their own insecticidal protein, from a gene taken from a soil bacterium.

The world's population is still growing, and farmland, water and climate are under pressure. Genetic modification is one tool that may help to produce more food, but it raises scientific, economic and ethical questions on which reasonable people disagree. This lesson looks at three named examples and then weighs the benefits and hazards.

You should already know how recombinant DNA is made, the role of promoters, and the basic principles of natural selection.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Genetically modified organism (GMO)"}
An organism whose genetic material has been altered by genetic engineering, usually by inserting a gene from another organism (a transgenic organism).
:::

:::definition{term="Transgenic organism"}
An organism that contains a gene from a different species.
:::

:::definition{term="Herbicide"}
A chemical used to kill weeds (unwanted plants).
:::

:::definition{term="Bt toxin"}
An insecticidal Cry protein originally from the soil bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*, toxic to certain insect larvae but not to mammals.
:::

## Three examples

### 1. GM salmon

Atlantic salmon normally grow mainly in the warmer months, because their growth hormone gene is active only then. In GM salmon:

1. a **growth hormone gene from Chinook salmon** was inserted, together with
2. a **promoter from another fish** (ocean pout) that keeps the gene switched on all year, including in cold water.

The fish produce growth hormone continuously and reach market size in about half the usual time, using less food per kilogram of fish. To reduce the risk of escape and breeding with wild salmon, they are farmed as **sterile females** in land-based tanks.

### 2. Herbicide-resistant soybean

Weeds compete with crops for light, water and mineral ions. Glyphosate is a herbicide that kills almost all plants by inhibiting an enzyme needed to make certain amino acids. A gene from a bacterium coding for a form of the enzyme that **is not inhibited by glyphosate** was inserted into soybean. Farmers can spray the herbicide over the growing crop: weeds die, and the soybean survives, increasing yield. It also allows less ploughing, which can reduce soil erosion.

### 3. Insect-resistant (Bt) cotton

The gene for a **Bt toxin** was inserted into cotton with a promoter that makes it active in the plant. When a caterpillar such as a bollworm eats the plant, the protein is activated in its alkaline gut and binds to receptors on gut cells, damaging the gut and killing the larva. Mammals, birds and most other insects lack these receptors and are not affected.

:::worked-solution[Comparing Bt and conventional cotton (illustrative data)]
| Measurement | Conventional cotton | Bt cotton |
|---|---|---|
| Seed cotton yield / tonnes per hectare | 1.5 | 1.9 |
| Insecticide sprays per season | 9 | 3 |

1. Percentage change in yield $= \dfrac{1.9 - 1.5}{1.5} \times 100 = 26.7\%$ (an increase).
2. Percentage change in sprays $= \dfrac{3 - 9}{9} \times 100 = -66.7\%$ (a decrease).
3. Fewer sprays reduce costs, farmers' exposure to insecticides and harm to non-target insects; less bollworm damage increases yield. Real results vary with the pest pressure in each season.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting the evolution of resistance"}
Bt cotton is grown over a whole district, year after year, with no other cotton.

**Predict and explain.** Among millions of bollworms, a few may carry an allele that makes them resistant to the Bt toxin. Only these survive on Bt cotton and reproduce, so the frequency of the resistance allele rises each generation by natural selection until Bt cotton no longer controls the pest. Planting **refuges** of non-Bt cotton nearby keeps a population of susceptible bollworms, which mate with any resistant ones and slow the spread of resistance.
:::

## How GMOs may help to meet the demand for food

- Higher yields from the same land (less weed competition, less pest damage).
- Faster-growing livestock and fish (GM salmon).
- Crops that tolerate drought, salinity or disease, and crops with improved nutritional value (for example, rice modified to make β-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A).
- Lower losses after harvest.

## Benefits and hazards of gene technology in agriculture

| Benefits | Hazards and concerns |
|---|---|
| Higher yields and more reliable harvests | Genes may spread to wild relatives by **cross-pollination** (gene flow), possibly producing herbicide-resistant weeds |
| Less insecticide sprayed (Bt crops), so less harm to farmers and non-target insects | Pests may evolve **resistance** to Bt toxin; weeds may evolve resistance to heavily used herbicides |
| Can combine traits faster and more precisely than selective breeding | Possible effects on **non-target organisms**, such as insects feeding on pollen |
| Medical and nutritional products from plants and animals | Seed is often patented, so farmers may have to buy new seed each year: **economic dependence on seed companies** |
| Less tillage with herbicide-resistant crops, reducing erosion | Reduced genetic diversity if a few GM varieties replace many traditional ones |
| | Public concern about food safety and allergens, requiring testing and labelling |

## Ethical and social implications

- **Food security**: supporters argue GMOs can help feed a growing population and cope with climate change; critics argue that hunger is often caused by poverty and distribution rather than total food production.
- **Farmers' rights**: patented seed and licence agreements may prevent farmers saving seed; small-scale farmers may be unable to afford it.
- **Consumer choice**: many people want GM food to be labelled so that they can choose.
- **Animal welfare**: some groups question modifying animals for faster growth.
- **Environment**: views differ on whether GM crops reduce harm (fewer sprays) or increase it (gene flow, resistance).
- **Regulation**: countries take different approaches. Zimbabwe regulates GMOs through a national biotechnology authority and has been cautious about the commercial growing of GM crops, while some neighbouring countries grow GM cotton or maize commercially.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect benefits and hazards of gene technology. Cambridge (A Level, Paper 4) names GM salmon, herbicide-resistant soybean and insect-resistant cotton; ZIMSEC (Form 6) asks for benefits and hazards in general, with ethics in Paper 3 essays.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying Bt cotton is poisonous to all animals"}
The Bt protein acts only on insects with the right gut receptors and alkaline gut conditions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying herbicide-resistant crops need no herbicide"}
They are resistant **to** herbicide so that it can be sprayed on the field; they do not kill weeds themselves.
:::

:::mistake{title="One-sided ethics answers"}
Give arguments on both sides, attribute them to groups, and reach a balanced conclusion.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To *explain* how a GMO helps food production, give: gene inserted → protein made → effect on the organism → effect on yield. For GM salmon include the promoter's role.
:::

:::exam-tip
In *discuss* questions (ZIMSEC Paper 3, Cambridge Paper 4), organise points as benefits, hazards, and ethical and social issues, then conclude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- GM salmon carry a Chinook growth hormone gene with an ocean pout promoter, so they grow all year and reach market size faster.
- Herbicide-resistant soybean has a glyphosate-insensitive enzyme gene, so herbicide kills weeds but not the crop.
- Bt cotton makes Bt toxin, killing bollworm larvae and reducing insecticide spraying.
- Benefits: higher yields, fewer sprays, faster growth; hazards: gene flow, resistance in pests and weeds, non-target effects, seed dependence.
- Ethical and social issues include food security, farmers' rights, labelling, animal welfare and national regulation.
:::
