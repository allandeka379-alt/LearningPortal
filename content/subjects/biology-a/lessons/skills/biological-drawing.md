## Introduction

Every year, candidates who know their biology well lose easy marks on drawings: shading, sketchy lines, cells drawn inside a plan diagram, label lines with arrowheads. A biological drawing is not a work of art. It is a scientific record of what you can **see**, made so that another biologist could identify the specimen and its parts. Examiners in ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3 mark drawings against a short list of conventions, so the marks are there for anyone who follows the rules.

The microscope question in the practical paper usually combines a drawing, a measurement with a graticule or a scale bar, a magnification or actual size calculation, and a comparison of two specimens. You should already know how to use a light microscope and the formula magnification = image size ÷ actual size.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plan diagram"}
A low-power drawing showing the outlines of the different tissues or regions of a specimen in their correct positions and proportions, with no individual cells drawn.
:::

:::definition{term="High-power drawing"}
A detailed drawing of a small number of individual cells, showing their shapes, relative sizes and visible contents.
:::

:::definition{term="Eyepiece graticule"}
A transparent scale in the eyepiece of a microscope; it has no units until calibrated against a stage micrometer at each magnification.
:::

:::definition{term="Stage micrometer"}
A microscope slide engraved with a scale of known divisions (often 0.01 mm = 10 µm each), used to calibrate the eyepiece graticule.
:::

## Drawing conventions

These rules apply to every drawing:

1. Use a **sharp HB pencil** and draw **clear, continuous lines** (no sketching, no gaps, no overlapping strokes).
2. **No shading** and no colouring.
3. Draw **large**: use most of the space provided.
4. Keep the **correct proportions**: the relative thickness of layers in a plan; the relative sizes of cells and their contents in a high-power drawing.
5. **Label lines** are drawn with a ruler, end exactly on the structure, do not cross one another and have no arrowheads; labels are written horizontally, outside the drawing.

### Low-power plan diagrams

- Show only the **boundaries of tissues** (for example epidermis, cortex, vascular bundles, pith) as outlines.
- Do **not** draw any individual cells.
- Show every tissue that is visible, in its correct position, including the outline of each vascular bundle in a stem.
- If asked to draw part of a specimen (for example a sector of a stem), draw only that part, but include all its layers.

### High-power drawings of cells

- Draw only the **number of cells asked for** (often three or four adjacent cells).
- Plant **cell walls** are drawn as **double lines**, and cells that touch share their walls (the line between them is not doubled twice).
- Show only **what you can see**: nucleus, nucleolus, chloroplasts or starch grains if visible; do not add structures you know exist but cannot see (such as ribosomes).
- Draw the shapes and relative sizes accurately, with the nucleus in proportion to the cell.

## Comparing specimens

Questions often ask you to record **observable differences** between two specimens in a table. Each row compares the **same feature** in both specimens, and each entry must be something you can actually see.

| Feature | Specimen A (sunflower stem) | Specimen B (maize stem) |
|---|---|---|
| Arrangement of vascular bundles | In a ring | Scattered throughout |
| Central pith | Present | No distinct pith |
| Shape of bundles | Wedge-shaped | Oval, each surrounded by a sheath |

Write "present" and "absent", or give both values, rather than leaving a cell empty.

## Measuring with an eyepiece graticule

1. Place the stage micrometer on the stage and focus on it at the chosen objective.
2. Rotate the eyepiece so the graticule scale lies alongside the micrometer scale, and line up the zero marks.
3. Find a point further along where lines on both scales coincide, and count the divisions on each.
4. Calculate the value of one eyepiece division: $\dfrac{\text{number of stage divisions} \times \text{size of one stage division}}{\text{number of eyepiece divisions}}$.
5. Remove the micrometer, put the specimen in place and measure in eyepiece divisions. Recalibrate at every new magnification.

:::worked-solution[Calibrating a graticule and finding a magnification]
At ×400, 12 stage micrometer divisions (each 10 µm) line up with 40 eyepiece divisions. A guard cell measures 14 eyepiece divisions long, and Chipo draws it 63 mm long.
1. 12 stage divisions $= 12 \times 10 = 120\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. One eyepiece division $= 120 \div 40 = 3\ \mu\text{m}$.
3. Actual length of the guard cell $= 14 \times 3 = 42\ \mu\text{m}$.
4. Drawing length $= 63\ \text{mm} = 63\,000\ \mu\text{m}$; magnification $= 63\,000 \div 42 = \times 1500$.
:::

## Scale bars

A photomicrograph often carries a **scale bar**: a line labelled with the actual length it represents.

1. Measure the scale bar with a ruler (in mm) and convert to µm.
2. Magnification $= \dfrac{\text{length of scale bar on the image}}{\text{actual length it represents}}$.
3. Measure the structure on the image and divide by the magnification to find its actual size.

:::worked-solution[Using a scale bar]
A scale bar labelled 50 µm measures 20 mm on a photomicrograph. A cell on the same image is 34 mm long.
1. Magnification $= 20\,000\ \mu\text{m} \div 50\ \mu\text{m} = \times 400$.
2. Actual length $= 34\,000 \div 400 = 85\ \mu\text{m}$.
3. Quicker: the cell is $34 \div 20 = 1.7$ times the bar, so $1.7 \times 50 = 85\ \mu\text{m}$.
:::

## Counting in a field of view

To estimate the **density** of a structure, such as stomata on a leaf epidermis, count the structures in a field of view of known area and repeat in several fields.

:::example{title="Stomatal density"}
At high power the field of view has a diameter of 0.4 mm, so its area is $\pi \times 0.2^2 = 0.126\ \text{mm}^2$. Farai counted 23 stomata in one field. Density $= 23 \div 0.126 = 183$ stomata per mm². **Suggest** how to make this more reliable: count at least five fields chosen at random and calculate a mean, and decide in advance to count stomata cut by the edge of the field on one half of the circle only.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: plan diagrams, high-power drawings, comparison tables, counting, graticule calibration and actual size calculations. ZIMSEC Paper 4 often asks for both a low-power and a high-power drawing of the same specimen; Cambridge Paper 3 frequently uses photomicrographs with scale bars.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing cells in a plan diagram"}
A plan shows tissue outlines only. Individual cells earn no credit and lose the mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shading and sketchy lines"}
Use single, continuous, sharp lines. Never shade dense areas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Single lines for plant cell walls"}
At high power, draw each cell wall as a double line, shared between adjacent cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to recalibrate"}
An eyepiece division has a different value at each objective lens. Calibrate at the magnification you measure at.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing different features in one row"}
Each row must compare the same feature in both specimens.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3, read exactly what is asked: "a plan diagram of the part of the stem shown", "three adjacent cells", "label two features". Extra cells or labels do not earn marks and waste time.
:::

:::exam-tip
Show every step of size calculations with units: convert mm to µm (× 1000) before dividing. Give magnifications with the × sign and actual sizes in µm, rounded sensibly (to the precision of your measurement).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Draw with sharp continuous pencil lines, no shading, large, in correct proportions; ruled label lines that touch the structure.
- Plan diagrams show tissue outlines only; high-power drawings show a few cells with double cell walls and only visible contents.
- Comparison tables compare the same observable feature of both specimens in each row.
- One eyepiece division = (stage divisions × 10 µm) ÷ eyepiece divisions; recalibrate at each magnification.
- Magnification = image ÷ actual; with a scale bar, magnification = bar length on image ÷ length it represents.
:::
