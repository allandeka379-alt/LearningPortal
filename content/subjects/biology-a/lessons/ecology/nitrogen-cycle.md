## Introduction

A farmer in Mashonaland who grows groundnuts one season and maize the next often harvests more maize than a neighbour who plants maize year after year, even without extra fertiliser. The difference is made by bacteria living in swellings on the groundnut roots. They turn nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use.

Nitrogen makes up about 78% of the air, yet it is often the nutrient that limits plant growth, because almost no organism can use nitrogen gas (N₂) directly. Every amino acid, nucleotide, ATP molecule and chlorophyll molecule contains nitrogen. This lesson follows nitrogen around its cycle and names the bacteria that drive each step.

You should already know that proteins are made of amino acids, that decomposers feed on dead matter, and the difference between aerobic and anaerobic conditions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Nitrogen fixation"}
The conversion of nitrogen gas (N₂) into ammonia or ammonium ions (NH₄⁺), compounds that living organisms can use; carried out biologically by the enzyme nitrogenase in some prokaryotes.
:::

:::definition{term="Ammonification"}
The release of ammonia or ammonium ions from the proteins, nucleic acids and urea of dead organisms and wastes by saprotrophic (decomposer) bacteria and fungi.
:::

:::definition{term="Nitrification"}
The oxidation of ammonium ions to nitrite (by *Nitrosomonas*) and then of nitrite to nitrate (by *Nitrobacter*) by chemoautotrophic bacteria in well-aerated soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Denitrification"}
The reduction of nitrate to nitrogen gas by anaerobic bacteria such as *Pseudomonas* and *Clostridium*, which use nitrate in place of oxygen in waterlogged, poorly aerated soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Assimilation"}
The uptake of nitrate or ammonium ions by plant roots and their use to make amino acids, proteins, nucleotides and other nitrogen-containing compounds.
:::

## The stages of the cycle

### 1. Nitrogen fixation

N₂ has a very strong triple bond, so breaking it needs a lot of energy. It happens in four ways:

1. **Mutualistic bacteria in legumes.** *Rhizobium* lives in **root nodules** of legumes such as groundnuts, soya beans, cowpeas and beans. Its enzyme **nitrogenase** reduces N₂ to ammonia, using a large amount of ATP and electrons that come from respiring sugars supplied by the plant. The bacteria give the plant ammonium for amino acids; the plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (sugars from photosynthesis) and a protected home.
2. **Free-living bacteria** in soil, such as *Azotobacter* (aerobic) and some *Clostridium* species (anaerobic), and cyanobacteria in water and rice paddies, fix nitrogen without a host.
3. **Lightning** provides energy for N₂ and O₂ to combine into nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rain and reach the soil as nitrate.
4. **The Haber process** fixes nitrogen industrially, using high temperature, high pressure and a catalyst, to make ammonia for fertilisers such as ammonium nitrate.

Nitrogenase is inhibited by oxygen. Root nodules contain **leghaemoglobin**, a red pigment that binds oxygen, keeping the oxygen concentration around the enzyme low while still supplying the bacteria's respiration. A healthy nodule cut open is pink or red inside.

### 2. Assimilation and feeding

Plants absorb **nitrate** (and some ammonium) by active transport into root hair cells. They reduce nitrate to ammonium and combine it with organic acids to make amino acids, then proteins and nucleic acids. Animals obtain nitrogen by eating plants or other animals, digesting proteins to amino acids and building their own proteins.

### 3. Ammonification

When organisms die, or when animals excrete urea and produce faeces, **saprotrophic bacteria and fungi** secrete enzymes that break down proteins to amino acids and then remove the amino groups (deamination), releasing **ammonia**, which forms ammonium ions in soil water.

### 4. Nitrification

Two groups of **chemoautotrophic** bacteria gain energy by oxidising ammonium. They need oxygen, so they work best in well-aerated, well-drained soil.

| Step | Bacterium | Substrate → product | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Nitrosomonas* | ammonium (NH₄⁺) → nitrite (NO₂⁻) | aerobic |
| 2 | *Nitrobacter* | nitrite (NO₂⁻) → nitrate (NO₃⁻) | aerobic |

Nitrate is the main form of nitrogen taken up by most crop plants.

### 5. Denitrification

In **waterlogged** soil the air spaces fill with water and oxygen runs out. Anaerobic bacteria such as *Pseudomonas denitrificans* and some *Clostridium* species use nitrate as an electron acceptor in respiration, reducing it to nitrogen gas, which escapes to the air. This **removes** nitrogen that plants could have used. Ploughing, good drainage and avoiding compaction keep soil aerated, favouring nitrification over denitrification.

:::example{title="Interpreting soil nitrate after flooding"}
Nyasha measured the nitrate in a field soil after it was flooded by heavy rain.

| Days after flooding | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrate / mg per kg soil | 24 | 18 | 11 | 6 | 4 | 3 |

![Soil nitrate after flooding: it falls steeply from 24 to 18, 11 and 6 mg per kg over the first six days and then more slowly to 4 and 3 mg per kg by day 10, a total decrease of 87.5%, because waterlogged soil favours denitrifying bacteria.](media:diagrams/soil-nitrate-flooding.png "Figure: soil nitrate after flooding, from the lesson's table")

**Describe.** Nitrate falls steeply from 24 to 6 mg per kg in the first six days, then more slowly to 3 mg per kg at day 10: a decrease of 87.5%.

**Explain.** Water fills the soil air spaces, so the soil becomes anaerobic. Denitrifying bacteria such as *Pseudomonas* reduce nitrate to nitrogen gas, which is lost to the air. Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen, so little new nitrate is made. Some nitrate may also be leached downwards by the water. The fall slows as less nitrate remains.
:::

## The cycle in agriculture

### Legumes and crop rotation

Growing groundnuts, soya beans or cowpeas adds fixed nitrogen to the soil. When the legume residues are ploughed in, decomposers release ammonium, which is nitrified to nitrate for the next crop. Rotating maize with legumes therefore reduces the amount of fertiliser needed. Seed of soya beans is often coated with a *Rhizobium* **inoculant** where the right strain is not already in the soil.

:::worked-solution[Percentage increase in yield after a legume rotation]
In an illustrative trial, maize grown after maize yielded 3.2 tonnes per hectare and maize grown after groundnuts yielded 4.0 tonnes per hectare. Calculate the percentage increase.
1. Increase $= 4.0 - 3.2 = 0.8$ tonnes per hectare.
2. Percentage increase $= \dfrac{0.8}{3.2} \times 100 = 25.0\%$.
3. Divide by the **original** yield (3.2), not the new yield.
:::

### Fertilisers, leaching and eutrophication

Inorganic fertilisers (ammonium nitrate, urea, compound fertilisers) give quick yields, but nitrate is very soluble and is not held by negatively charged soil particles. Heavy rain **leaches** it below the roots and into rivers and dams. There it can cause **eutrophication**: algae grow rapidly and block light; submerged plants die; decomposers break down the dead material using oxygen by aerobic respiration, so oxygen levels fall and fish and other animals die. Applying fertiliser in split doses when the crop needs it, and keeping a buffer strip of vegetation beside rivers, reduces leaching.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Swapping Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter"}
*Nitrosomonas* acts first and makes nitrite; *Nitrobacter* acts second and makes nitrate. The order is ammonium → nitrite → nitrate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling nitrification nitrogen fixation"}
Fixation starts with N₂ gas. Nitrification starts with ammonium already in the soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying plants absorb nitrogen gas"}
Plants cannot use N₂. They absorb nitrate (and ammonium) ions by active transport.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying denitrification is aerobic"}
Denitrifying bacteria use nitrate instead of oxygen, so they thrive where oxygen is **absent**, as in waterlogged soil.
:::

:::

:::exam-tip

:::

:::exam-tip
For eutrophication, give the full chain: nitrate leached → algal bloom → light blocked → plants die → decomposers respire aerobically → oxygen falls → fish die.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Nitrogen fixation turns N₂ into ammonium: *Rhizobium* in legume nodules (nitrogenase, leghaemoglobin), free-living bacteria, lightning and the Haber process.
- Decomposers release ammonium from proteins and urea (ammonification).
- Nitrification is aerobic: *Nitrosomonas* (ammonium → nitrite) and *Nitrobacter* (nitrite → nitrate).
- Denitrification by *Pseudomonas* and *Clostridium* in anaerobic, waterlogged soil returns nitrate to N₂.
- Legume rotation adds nitrogen; excess nitrate fertiliser is leached and causes eutrophication.
:::
