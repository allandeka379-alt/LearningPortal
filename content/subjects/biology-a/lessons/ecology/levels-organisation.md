## Introduction

Drive from the miombo woodland around Harare to the mopane woodland of the Zambezi valley and the trees, grasses, insects and birds change almost completely. Ecologists need precise words to describe what changes and how to measure it. Is the valley "more diverse" because it has more species, because its species are more evenly spread, or because its elephants carry more genetic variation? Each question is about a different **level of biodiversity**.

This lesson sets out the vocabulary of ecology that every later topic uses: conservation, sampling, the nitrogen cycle and human impact. You should already know what a species is in outline and that alleles are different forms of a gene.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Species"}
A group of organisms with similar morphology, physiology and biochemistry that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated from other such groups.
:::

:::definition{term="Habitat"}
The place where an organism lives, described by its physical and biological features (for example, the sandy bed of a seasonal river).
:::

:::definition{term="Population"}
All the organisms of one species living in the same area at the same time, able to interbreed.
:::

:::definition{term="Community"}
All the populations of all the different species living in the same area at the same time and interacting with one another.
:::

:::definition{term="Ecosystem"}
A relatively self-contained, interacting community of organisms together with the non-living (abiotic) environment with which they exchange energy and matter.
:::

:::definition{term="Niche"}
The role of a species in its ecosystem: everything it does and needs, including where it lives, what it feeds on, what feeds on it, the conditions it tolerates and how it interacts with other organisms.
:::

:::definition{term="Biodiversity"}
The variety of life in an area, assessed at three levels: the range of ecosystems and habitats, the number of species and their relative abundance, and the genetic variation within each species.
:::

## Building up the levels

The terms nest inside one another, from the smallest unit to the largest.

| Level | What it contains | Example from Lake Kariba |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | One individual | One kapenta fish |
| Population | All individuals of one species in the area | All the kapenta in the lake |
| Community | All populations of all species in the area | Kapenta, tigerfish, plankton, crocodiles, water plants and microorganisms |
| Ecosystem | The community plus its abiotic environment | The lake: its organisms, water, dissolved oxygen, light, temperature and sediments |

A habitat is not a level in this list: it is the *place* an organism lives. A niche is not a place at all: it is a *role*.

### Habitat versus niche

Two species can share a habitat but not a niche. Grazing zebra and browsing giraffe live in the same savanna, but zebra eat grasses close to the ground while giraffe browse the upper canopy of acacia trees. Their niches differ in food, feeding height and time of activity.

The **competitive exclusion principle** states that two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche in the same habitat for long: one will outcompete the other. Where niches overlap, natural selection tends to reduce the overlap, for example by favouring different food sizes.

A species' niche includes:

1. **Spatial features**: where in the habitat it lives (soil surface, canopy, lake bottom).
2. **Trophic features**: what it eats and what eats it.
3. **Tolerance range**: the temperatures, pH, salinity or water availability it survives.
4. **Interactions**: competition, mutualism, parasitism and its effect on other species.

## The three levels of biodiversity

### 1. Ecosystem and habitat diversity

This is the number and range of different ecosystems and habitats in an area. Zimbabwe has miombo and mopane woodland, montane grassland and forest in the Eastern Highlands, wetlands (vleis), rivers, and the man-made lakes Kariba and Mutirikwi. A country or region with many ecosystems has many different niches, and so supports more species overall.

### 2. Species diversity

Species diversity has two components:

- **Species richness**: the number of different species present.
- **Species evenness**: how evenly the individuals are spread among those species (their relative abundance).

A community with high richness and high evenness has high species diversity. Two sites can have the same richness but very different diversity if one species dominates one of them.

### 3. Genetic diversity

Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles within a species, both within one population and between populations. It is measured as the number of different alleles of genes, or the proportion of loci that are heterozygous.

Genetic diversity matters because:

1. **Adaptation to change**: a population with many alleles is more likely to contain individuals that survive a new disease, a drought or a warmer climate, so natural selection has variation to act on.
2. **Avoiding inbreeding depression**: small populations with little variation are more likely to have offspring homozygous for harmful recessive alleles, reducing fertility and survival.
3. **A resource for breeding**: wild relatives and traditional varieties of crops such as sorghum and finger millet carry alleles for drought tolerance or disease resistance that plant breeders can use.

:::example{title="Comparing species diversity at two woodland sites"}
Rudo counted every tree taller than 2 m in a 50 m × 50 m plot at two woodland sites.

| Tree species | Site A: number of trees | Site B: number of trees |
|---|---|---|
| *Brachystegia spiciformis* (msasa) | 21 | 86 |
| *Julbernardia globiflora* | 19 | 3 |
| *Brachystegia boehmii* | 23 | 5 |
| *Combretum molle* | 18 | 2 |
| *Terminalia sericea* | 19 | 4 |

**Describe and compare.** Both sites contain 100 trees of the same five species, so species richness is the same (5). At Site A the trees are spread almost evenly (18 to 23 of each species), so evenness is high. At Site B one species, msasa, makes up 86 of the 100 trees, so evenness is low.

**Conclude.** Site A has the higher species diversity, even though richness is equal. (Cambridge students can confirm this with Simpson's index of diversity: about 0.798 for Site A against 0.255 for Site B.)
:::

:::example{title="Predicting the effect of low genetic diversity"}
A small herd of a large antelope is fenced in a private conservancy and has bred only among itself for many generations.

**Predict and explain.** Genetic diversity in the herd will fall, because few alleles were present in the founders and some are lost by chance each generation (genetic drift). Inbreeding increases homozygosity, so harmful recessive alleles are expressed more often, and calves may show lower survival. If a new disease arrives, there may be no resistant individuals. Bringing in animals from another population (translocation) adds new alleles.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards define species, population, community, ecosystem and niche (ZIMSEC also asks for habitat). Cambridge (A Level, Paper 4) expects biodiversity explained at the three levels and the use of Simpson's index; ZIMSEC teaches this concept in Form 6.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing habitat and niche"}
The habitat is the address; the niche is the job. "The niche of a dung beetle is a cattle pasture" is wrong: the pasture is the habitat; the niche includes feeding on and burying dung.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out the abiotic part of an ecosystem"}
A community is living organisms only. An ecosystem is the community **plus** the abiotic environment, with energy flowing and nutrients cycling between them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Equating diversity with species number"}
Species richness is only one part of species diversity. Always consider evenness (relative abundance) as well.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that a population is one species"}
"The population of animals in Hwange" is not a population in the ecological sense; "the elephant population of Hwange" is.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
*Define* questions (ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2; Cambridge Paper 4) reward the key qualifiers: one species, same area, same time, interbreeding for a population; interacting, plus abiotic factors, for an ecosystem.
:::

:::exam-tip
To *compare* two communities, quote richness and the numbers that show evenness, then state which has higher diversity and why. To *explain* why genetic diversity matters, link variety of alleles to survival under a changing environment.
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays on conservation often open with these definitions: write them precisely and briefly, then move on.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Organism → population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community plus abiotic environment).
- A habitat is where an organism lives; a niche is its role, including food, position, tolerances and interactions.
- Biodiversity is assessed at three levels: ecosystems and habitats, species (richness and evenness) and genetic diversity.
- Two sites with equal species richness can differ in diversity because of evenness.
- Genetic diversity allows adaptation to change, reduces inbreeding depression and supplies alleles for crop and livestock breeding.
:::
