## Introduction

From the air, the landscape around a growing town shows the human footprint clearly: cleared woodland, gullies where soil has washed away, rivers brown with silt and dams green with algae. Humans change ecosystems more than any other species. Some changes are deliberate, such as clearing land for crops; many are side effects, such as silt from mining smothering river life downstream.

ZIMSEC asks you to describe these effects and explain them using ecological ideas you already know: food chains, the nitrogen cycle, eutrophication, photosynthesis and the niche. This lesson works through each human activity in turn, giving the mechanism behind every effect.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Deforestation"}
The permanent clearing of forest or woodland for another land use, such as farming, settlement or fuel, without replanting.
:::

:::definition{term="Soil erosion"}
The removal of topsoil by water or wind, faster than new soil forms, usually after the vegetation that binds and covers the soil is removed.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
The enrichment of a body of water with nutrients, especially nitrate and phosphate, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants and then oxygen depletion as they decompose.
:::

:::definition{term="Bioaccumulation"}
The build-up of a persistent substance, such as an organochlorine pesticide or mercury, in an organism's tissues faster than it is broken down or excreted; concentrations increase along food chains (biomagnification).
:::

:::definition{term="Invasive species"}
A species introduced outside its natural range that spreads rapidly and harms native species, ecosystems or the economy.
:::

## Settlement and deforestation

Growing towns and villages replace natural vegetation with buildings, roads and fields. Woodland is also cut for **firewood**, for building poles and, in tobacco-growing areas, for **curing tobacco** in barns heated by wood fires.

Effects of clearing trees:

1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation**: species lose food, nesting sites and shelter; populations become small and isolated, losing genetic diversity.
2. **Soil erosion**: without a canopy to break the force of rain and roots to bind the soil, topsoil washes away, forming gullies.
3. **Siltation**: eroded soil fills rivers and dams, reducing their capacity and smothering aquatic organisms.
4. **Changes to the water cycle**: less transpiration and less infiltration, so more runoff and flooding in the rains and drier rivers in the dry season.
5. **Less carbon stored**: trees that are burned or left to decay release carbon dioxide, and less CO₂ is removed by photosynthesis.

:::worked-solution[Calculating the percentage of woodland lost]
An illustrative survey of a district found 1240 hectares of woodland in one year and 868 hectares ten years later.
1. Area lost $= 1240 - 868 = 372$ hectares.
2. Percentage change $= \dfrac{868 - 1240}{1240} \times 100 = -30.0\%$.
3. The woodland decreased by 30.0% over ten years. Always divide by the **starting** value.
:::

## Agriculture

- **Fertilisers**: excess nitrate and phosphate run off or are leached into water, causing **eutrophication**: algal bloom → light blocked → submerged plants die → aerobic decomposers use up oxygen → fish and invertebrates die.
- **Pesticides**: insecticides and herbicides may kill non-target organisms such as bees and natural predators of pests. Persistent pesticides (for example DDT, now restricted) **bioaccumulate**, reaching high concentrations in top carnivores such as fish eagles, where they can reduce breeding success. Pests may evolve **resistance**.
- **Soil erosion**: ploughing up and down slopes, overgrazing and leaving soil bare after harvest expose it to rain and wind. Contour ridges, conservation farming (minimum tillage with mulch) and controlled stocking reduce this.
- **Monoculture**: large areas of one crop have low species diversity and support few native species.

## Industry

Factories and power stations release **sulfur dioxide** and **nitrogen oxides** that form acid rain, harming plants and acidifying water. Untreated industrial and sewage effluent adds organic matter, which bacteria decompose using oxygen; this raises the **biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)** of rivers, so oxygen falls. Heavy metals such as lead and mercury are toxic and can bioaccumulate.

## Mining

### Gold panning and siltation

Small-scale gold panning along rivers digs up riverbanks and beds. Large amounts of silt are washed into the water. Some panners also use **mercury** to separate gold, and spilled mercury is toxic and bioaccumulates in fish.

:::example{title="Interpreting river data below a gold panning site"}
Chipo measured turbidity (cloudiness, in nephelometric turbidity units, NTU) and counted the number of taxa of mayfly and stonefly nymphs, which are sensitive to silt and low oxygen, at points along a river.

| Sampling point | Turbidity / NTU | Number of mayfly and stonefly taxa |
|---|---|---|
| 200 m upstream | 12 | 9 |
| At the panning site | 310 | 0 |
| 500 m downstream | 185 | 1 |
| 2 km downstream | 96 | 3 |
| 5 km downstream | 40 | 6 |

**Describe.** Turbidity rises from 12 NTU upstream to 310 NTU at the panning site, then falls with distance downstream to 40 NTU at 5 km. The number of sensitive taxa falls from 9 upstream to 0 at the site and recovers to 6 at 5 km. Higher turbidity goes with fewer sensitive taxa.

**Explain.** Silt blocks light, so algae and water plants photosynthesise less, reducing food and oxygen. Silt settles and clogs the gills of nymphs and the gaps between stones where they live. Downstream the silt settles out and water clears, so the community recovers. The data show a correlation; the upstream site acts as a control showing the river was healthy before the panning site.
:::

### Acid mine drainage

When sulfide minerals in mine waste are exposed to air and water, they are oxidised to sulfuric acid. The acid water dissolves heavy metals and lowers the pH of streams, killing many organisms.

## Global warming

Burning fossil fuels, clearing and burning forests, and agriculture (methane from cattle and rice, nitrous oxide from fertilised soils) increase the **greenhouse gases** carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide in the atmosphere. They absorb infrared radiation emitted by the Earth's surface, so the lower atmosphere warms (the enhanced greenhouse effect).

Expected effects in southern Africa include higher temperatures, less reliable rainfall with more droughts and intense storms, shifts in the ranges of species (some moving to higher, cooler ground such as the Eastern Highlands, others with nowhere to go), more evaporation from dams, and changes in the range of disease vectors such as mosquitoes.

## Invasive plant species

**Water hyacinth** (*Eichhornia crassipes*), a floating plant from South America, grows and spreads rapidly on nutrient-rich water. Dense mats block light and oxygen exchange, so submerged plants and fish die; they also block boats, water intakes and irrigation canals. Eutrophication makes infestations worse.

***Lantana camara***, an ornamental shrub from the Americas, forms dense thickets that shade out native plants, is toxic to livestock and is spread by birds that eat its berries.

Invasive species succeed because they often have **no natural predators, competitors or diseases** in their new range, and they reproduce quickly. Control is mechanical (removing by hand or machine), chemical (herbicides) or biological (introducing a host-specific natural enemy, such as weevils that feed on water hyacinth, after careful testing).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying fertilisers kill fish directly"}
Fish die from lack of oxygen caused by decomposers respiring, not from nitrate poisoning. Give the full chain.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing global warming with ozone depletion"}
Greenhouse gases trap infrared radiation and warm the atmosphere. Ozone depletion (by CFCs) lets more ultraviolet radiation through. They are different problems.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing data without figures"}
"Turbidity is higher downstream" earns little. Quote values with units and describe the pattern from start to end.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 gives a data table from a river, farm or forest and asks you to *describe* (trend with figures) and *explain* (ecological mechanism). Paper 3 essays ask you to *discuss* several human activities: for each, state the activity, the effect and the reason.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link every effect to a mechanism: less light → less photosynthesis; more decomposition → less oxygen; no roots → erosion; no natural enemies → invasive spread.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Settlement and deforestation (firewood, tobacco curing) cause habitat loss, erosion, siltation, altered water cycles and CO₂ release.
- Fertilisers cause eutrophication; persistent pesticides bioaccumulate and harm non-target species.
- Mining causes siltation (gold panning), mercury and heavy-metal pollution and acid mine drainage.
- Greenhouse gases enhance the greenhouse effect, changing temperature, rainfall and species ranges.
- Invasive plants such as water hyacinth and *Lantana camara* spread without natural enemies and outcompete native species.
:::
