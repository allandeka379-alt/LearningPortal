## Introduction

When a nurse at a clinic in Mutare takes blood, she presses on a vein in the inner elbow, not an artery: veins lie close to the surface, have thin walls and low pressure, so the puncture closes quickly. When she checks a pulse at the wrist, she feels an artery stretching with each heartbeat. These everyday observations reflect a basic principle of A-Level biology: each type of blood vessel has a structure suited to the pressure and job it has.

Mammals are large, active and warm, with a small surface area to volume ratio, so they need a mass transport system. You should already know the parts of blood, the structure of the heart in outline, diffusion and the idea of surface area to volume ratio.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Closed circulation"}
A circulation in which blood stays inside vessels throughout its journey around the body.
:::

:::definition{term="Double circulation"}
A circulation in which blood passes through the heart twice in each complete circuit: once in the pulmonary circulation (heart to lungs and back) and once in the systemic circulation (heart to the rest of the body and back).
:::

:::definition{term="Tissue fluid"}
The fluid that bathes the cells of a tissue, formed when plasma (without most of its proteins) is forced out of capillaries by hydrostatic pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Lymph"}
Tissue fluid that has drained into lymph capillaries and is returned to the blood through lymph vessels.
:::

## The double circulation

1. The **right ventricle** pumps deoxygenated blood through the **pulmonary artery** to the lungs.
2. Oxygenated blood returns to the **left atrium** through the **pulmonary veins**.
3. The **left ventricle** pumps it through the **aorta** to the body.
4. Deoxygenated blood returns to the **right atrium** through the **venae cavae** (superior from the head and arms, inferior from the rest of the body).

A double circulation means blood can be pumped to the body at **high pressure** after passing through the lungs at **lower pressure**. A high pressure in the lung capillaries would force fluid into the alveoli; a low pressure in the systemic circulation could not deliver blood quickly enough to active muscles.

Within each organ, blood flows: **artery → arterioles → capillaries → venules → vein**.

## Blood vessels: structure and function

Artery and vein walls have three layers: an inner **tunica intima** (endothelium, a single layer of flattened cells, on a basement membrane), a middle **tunica media** (smooth muscle and elastic fibres) and an outer **tunica externa** (collagen fibres).

| Vessel | Structure | How it suits the function |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic artery (e.g. aorta) | Thick wall; tunica media rich in elastic fibres; smooth endothelium; relatively narrow lumen for its size | Elastic fibres stretch at systole and recoil at diastole, smoothing the pressure surges and keeping blood moving; collagen resists bursting |
| Muscular artery (e.g. in limbs) | Thick wall with more smooth muscle and fewer elastic fibres | Smooth muscle contracts or relaxes to control blood flow to organs |
| Arteriole | Narrow; wall mainly smooth muscle | Constriction or dilation controls flow into each capillary bed and lowers pressure |
| Capillary | Wall of endothelium only (one cell thick), lumen about 7-10 µm, often with gaps between cells | Short diffusion distance; just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file; huge total surface area; leaks plasma to form tissue fluid |
| Vein | Thin wall with little muscle or elastic tissue; wide lumen; valves | Blood at low pressure; wide lumen reduces resistance; valves prevent backflow; contraction of skeletal muscles squeezes veins to return blood |

In a transverse section (TS) of an artery and vein side by side, the **artery** has a thick wall, a relatively small, round lumen, and an endothelium that often looks folded because the elastic fibres recoil after death. The **vein** has a thin wall, a large lumen and often an irregular or flattened shape. In a longitudinal section (LS) a vein may show a pocket valve.

:::worked-solution[Measurements from a photomicrograph of an artery]
A photomicrograph of an artery TS is at ×40. The lumen measures 22 mm across and the wall 6 mm thick in the photomicrograph.
1. Lumen: $22\ \text{mm} = 22\,000\ \mu\text{m}$; actual $= 22\,000 \div 40 = 550\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. Wall: $6\ \text{mm} = 6000\ \mu\text{m}$; actual $= 6000 \div 40 = 150\ \mu\text{m}$.
3. Ratio of lumen diameter to wall thickness $= 550 : 150 = 3.7 : 1$ (to 2 s.f.).
4. A vein of similar size at the same magnification had a lumen of 950 µm and a wall of only 50 µm, a ratio of 19 : 1. The much higher ratio is typical of veins.
:::

## Blood and blood cells

Blood is mostly **plasma**, which is about 90% **water**. Water is a good solvent and transport medium: it carries glucose, amino acids, ions, urea, hormones and plasma proteins in solution, and its high specific heat capacity helps distribute heat. Recognising the cells:

- **Red blood cells (erythrocytes):** biconcave discs about 7-8 µm across, no nucleus, full of haemoglobin; they appear pale in the centre.
- **Neutrophils:** phagocytes with a **lobed nucleus** (two to five lobes) and granular cytoplasm; the most numerous white cells.
- **Monocytes:** the largest white cells, with a large **kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus**; they become macrophages in tissues.
- **Lymphocytes:** small cells with a large, **round nucleus** filling most of the cell and a thin rim of cytoplasm.

A high-power drawing shows the cell outline, the shape of the nucleus and the proportion of the cell it fills, with no shading.

## Tissue fluid and lymph

1. At the **arterial end** of a capillary bed, the **hydrostatic pressure** of the blood is higher than the tendency of water to move back in, so water and small solutes (glucose, amino acids, ions, oxygen) are forced out through gaps in the capillary wall, forming tissue fluid.
2. Red blood cells and most **plasma proteins** are too large to leave, so they stay in the blood. These proteins give the plasma a lower water potential than the tissue fluid.
3. Along the capillary, hydrostatic pressure falls as fluid leaves and blood moves away from the arteriole. At the **venous end**, it is lower than the effect of the plasma proteins, so some water returns into the capillary by **osmosis**, carrying waste such as carbon dioxide and urea.
4. More fluid leaves than returns. The excess drains into blind-ended **lymph capillaries**, becoming **lymph**, which passes through lymph nodes (where lymphocytes act against pathogens) and is returned to the blood near the heart.

Tissue fluid supplies cells with oxygen and nutrients and removes their wastes. If lymph drainage fails, or blood pressure in capillaries is too high, tissue fluid accumulates, causing swelling (oedema).

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the double circulation, the main vessels, structure and function of arteries, veins and capillaries, recognising them and blood cells in slides and micrographs, and water as the main component of blood. Tissue fluid and lymph formation is named by Cambridge; it is useful background for ZIMSEC.
:::

:::example{title="Describing pressure along the systemic circulation"}
Approximate typical pressures in a resting young adult are: aorta about 120 mmHg at systole and 80 mmHg at diastole; small arteries slightly lower; arterioles falling from about 85 to 35 mmHg; capillaries from about 35 to 15 mmHg; veins below 15 mmHg, near zero at the right atrium. **Describe and explain**: pressure is highest and fluctuates most in the aorta because the left ventricle pumps intermittently; the fluctuation is smoothed by elastic recoil; the steepest fall is across the arterioles, whose narrow lumens give high resistance; pressure in the veins is low, so valves and skeletal muscle are needed to return blood.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying arteries carry oxygenated blood"}
Arteries carry blood **away from the heart**. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying capillary walls are one cell thick 'so oxygen diffuses faster'"}
State the link: a single layer of flattened endothelial cells gives a **short diffusion distance**, so diffusion is fast.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying elastic fibres pump blood"}
Arteries do not contract to pump blood; elastic fibres **recoil** passively, which maintains pressure and smooths flow between beats.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying red blood cells leave capillaries in tissue fluid"}
Tissue fluid lacks red blood cells and most plasma proteins; it is mostly water with small solutes. (Some white cells can squeeze out.)
:::

:::

In ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3 you may be asked for a plan diagram of an artery and vein TS. Show the layers as concentric regions in correct proportions, with no cells, and label tunica intima, media and externa and the lumen. Calculate actual sizes from scale bars or magnification.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Mammals have a closed double circulation: pulmonary (right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins) and systemic (left ventricle → aorta → body → venae cavae).
- Elastic arteries stretch and recoil; muscular arteries and arterioles control flow; capillaries are one cell thick; veins have thin walls, wide lumens and valves.
- Blood is mostly water; red cells are biconcave without nuclei; neutrophils have lobed nuclei, monocytes kidney-shaped nuclei, lymphocytes large round nuclei.
- Tissue fluid forms at the arterial end of capillaries by hydrostatic pressure; some water returns by osmosis at the venous end; the excess becomes lymph.
- Actual size = image size ÷ magnification; veins have a much larger lumen-to-wall ratio than arteries.
:::
