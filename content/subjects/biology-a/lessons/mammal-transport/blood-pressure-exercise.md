## Introduction

At many clinics and pharmacies in Zimbabwe, adults can have their blood pressure checked in a couple of minutes: a cuff around the upper arm, a short squeeze, and two numbers such as "120 over 80". Those two numbers tell a health worker a great deal about the heart and arteries. High blood pressure (hypertension) often causes no symptoms for years, yet over time it damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes, which is why routine checks matter.

This topic, required by ZIMSEC, links the structure of the heart and arteries to health and to exercise. You should already know the cardiac cycle, the structure of arteries and the formula for cardiac output.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Blood pressure"}
The force per unit area exerted by blood on the walls of the arteries, usually measured in the brachial artery of the upper arm in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
:::

:::definition{term="Systolic pressure"}
The highest pressure in the arteries, reached when the left ventricle contracts and ejects blood.
:::

:::definition{term="Diastolic pressure"}
The lowest pressure in the arteries, reached while the ventricles are relaxed and filling.
:::

:::definition{term="Hypertension"}
Blood pressure that is persistently higher than normal at rest, confirmed by repeated measurements.
:::

:::definition{term="Pulse"}
The rhythmic expansion and recoil of an artery wall caused by each ventricular contraction; the pulse rate equals the heart rate.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Typical values"}
A typical healthy young adult at rest: blood pressure about **120/80 mmHg** (systolic/diastolic) and pulse rate about **60-80 beats per minute**. Values vary with age, fitness, posture, emotion and time of day, so a single reading is never a diagnosis.
:::

## Measuring blood pressure

A **sphygmomanometer** has an inflatable cuff, a pump and a pressure gauge (a mercury column or a dial; electronic versions use a sensor).

1. The person sits quietly for a few minutes, with the arm supported at the level of the heart.
2. The cuff is wrapped round the upper arm and inflated until it squeezes the brachial artery shut: no blood flows and no pulse is felt or heard below the cuff.
3. A stethoscope is placed over the artery at the elbow and the cuff is slowly deflated.
4. When cuff pressure falls just below the **systolic** pressure, blood spurts through during each systole, making a tapping sound. The pressure at the first sound is recorded as the systolic pressure.
5. As the cuff pressure falls further, the sounds continue and then disappear when blood flows smoothly all the time. The pressure at which the sounds disappear is the **diastolic** pressure.
6. The reading is written systolic/diastolic, for example 118/76 mmHg.

## Hypertension

Hypertension is diagnosed only after repeated raised readings. **Risk factors** include:

- Increasing age, as arteries become less elastic.
- Family history (genetic factors).
- A diet high in salt, which increases water retention and blood volume.
- Obesity and lack of physical activity.
- Excessive alcohol intake and smoking.
- Long-term stress.
- Some kidney and hormonal disorders.

**Consequences** of untreated hypertension:

- The left ventricle works harder, its wall thickens and it may eventually fail (**heart failure**).
- Damage to the endothelium of arteries encourages **atherosclerosis** (fatty plaques), narrowing arteries and raising the risk of **coronary heart disease** and **heart attack**.
- A weakened or blocked artery in the brain can cause a **stroke**.
- Damage to kidney capillaries can lead to **kidney failure**, and damage to the retina to loss of vision.

Hypertension is managed by lifestyle changes (less salt, regular exercise, healthy weight, not smoking, moderate or no alcohol) and, where a doctor prescribes them, medicines.

## The pulse and how to measure it

The pulse is felt where an artery lies near the surface over a bone: at the **wrist** (radial artery) or the **neck** (carotid artery).

1. Press lightly with two fingers (not the thumb, which has its own pulse).
2. Count the beats for a fixed time, usually 15 s or 30 s, and multiply to give beats per minute, or count for a full minute for accuracy.
3. Take three readings and calculate a mean.

:::worked-solution[Pulse rate from a 15-second count]
Tatenda counted 18 beats in 15 s at rest.
1. Number of 15-second periods in a minute $= 60 \div 15 = 4$.
2. Pulse rate $= 18 \times 4 = 72$ beats per minute.
3. Counting for only 15 s means a miscount of one beat changes the result by 4 beats/min; counting for a full minute is more accurate.
:::

## Effects of exercise

### Short-term (during and just after exercise)

1. Working muscles respire faster, using more oxygen and producing more carbon dioxide.
2. **Heart rate increases** and **stroke volume increases**, so **cardiac output** rises (it can rise to several times the resting value in a fit person).
3. Arterioles supplying the skeletal muscles **dilate**, while those to the gut constrict, redirecting blood to the muscles.
4. **Systolic pressure rises**; diastolic pressure changes little.
5. Breathing becomes deeper and faster.
6. After exercise, heart rate gradually returns to the resting value. The **recovery time** is shorter in fitter people.

### Long-term (regular training over weeks and months)

- **Heart muscle** (especially the left ventricle wall) becomes larger and stronger, so **stroke volume increases**.
- **Resting heart rate falls**: the same resting cardiac output is delivered with fewer, stronger beats.
- **More capillaries** develop in trained skeletal muscles and in the heart, improving oxygen delivery.
- Resting **blood pressure** tends to fall, and arteries stay more elastic.
- Maximum cardiac output during exercise increases, and recovery is faster.

:::worked-solution[Effect of training on the heart at rest]
Before a 12-week training programme Rudo's resting heart rate was 76 beats/min and her stroke volume 66 cm³. Afterwards they were 62 beats/min and 81 cm³.
1. Cardiac output before $= 66 \times 76 = 5016\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{min}^{-1}$.
2. Cardiac output after $= 81 \times 62 = 5022\ \text{cm}^3\ \text{min}^{-1}$.
3. Change in resting heart rate $= \dfrac{62 - 76}{76} \times 100 = -18.4\%$.
4. Resting cardiac output hardly changed: the trained heart supplies the same blood flow at rest with a larger stroke volume and a lower heart rate, so it works more efficiently.
:::

## An efficient cardiovascular system

An efficient cardiovascular system delivers enough oxygen and nutrients to the tissues, and removes wastes, with the least work by the heart. Signs include:

- A low resting heart rate with a large stroke volume.
- A normal resting blood pressure.
- A large increase in cardiac output during exercise.
- Rapid recovery of pulse rate after exercise.

:::example{title="Interpreting recovery data"}
After two minutes of step-ups, Farai's pulse was 128 beats/min, falling to 84 beats/min after 3 minutes' rest and back to his resting 74 beats/min after 5 minutes. Nyasha's pulse rose from 70 to 110 beats/min and returned to 70 within 3 minutes. **Suggest** who is fitter and **explain**: Nyasha, because her pulse rose less for the same exercise (a larger stroke volume means fewer beats are needed) and recovered faster.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing systolic and diastolic"}
Systolic is the **higher** number (ventricles contracting); diastolic the lower (ventricles relaxing).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying training increases resting cardiac output"}
Training mainly increases **stroke volume** and lowers **resting heart rate**; resting cardiac output stays about the same. **Maximum** cardiac output rises.
:::

:::mistake{title="Diagnosing from one reading"}
Blood pressure varies; hypertension needs repeated raised readings at rest.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Papers 2 and 3, *describe* questions on measuring blood pressure need the sequence: inflate cuff to stop flow, deflate slowly, first sound = systolic, sounds disappear = diastolic. For hypertension give risk factors and consequences separately and avoid invented statistics.
:::

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 4 investigations on pulse rate need replicates, a fixed exercise (same step height, rate and time), a rest period to establish the baseline and readings at fixed intervals during recovery. *Calculate* percentage changes and recovery times, and *explain* changes using stroke volume, heart rate and cardiac output.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Blood pressure is written systolic/diastolic in mmHg; about 120/80 mmHg is typical for a healthy young adult at rest.
- A sphygmomanometer cuff stops flow; the first sound on deflation gives systolic pressure, the disappearance of sounds diastolic pressure.
- Hypertension risk factors: age, family history, salt, obesity, inactivity, alcohol, smoking, stress; consequences: heart failure, atherosclerosis, heart attack, stroke, kidney damage.
- Exercise raises heart rate, stroke volume, cardiac output and systolic pressure; training raises stroke volume and capillary density and lowers resting heart rate.
- An efficient system has a low resting heart rate, normal blood pressure and fast recovery.
:::
