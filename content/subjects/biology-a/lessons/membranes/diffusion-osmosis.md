## Introduction

Oral rehydration solution, the mixture of clean water, salt and sugar given to people with cholera, works because of how substances cross membranes. Sodium ions and glucose are absorbed together by cells lining the small intestine, and water follows them by osmosis. Understanding passive transport is the first step to understanding why that simple solution saves lives in outbreaks in Harare and elsewhere.

This lesson covers the three **passive** processes, which need no energy from respiration: simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis. You should already know the fluid mosaic model, especially the hydrophobic core of the bilayer and the channel and carrier proteins in it. Active transport is the next lesson.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diffusion"}
The net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random kinetic motion.
:::

:::definition{term="Facilitated diffusion"}
Diffusion of ions or polar molecules across a membrane through specific channel or carrier proteins, down their concentration gradient, without the use of ATP.
:::

:::definition{term="Water potential ($\psi$)"}
A measure of the tendency of water molecules to move from one place to another, measured in kilopascals (kPa). Pure water at atmospheric pressure has a water potential of 0 kPa; dissolving solutes lowers it, so every solution has a negative water potential.
:::

:::definition{term="Osmosis"}
The net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Water potential values"}
Pure water: $\psi = 0\ \text{kPa}$ (the highest possible). A more concentrated solution has a **more negative** water potential. Water always moves down a water potential gradient: from less negative to more negative.
:::

## Simple diffusion

Molecules in a gas or liquid move randomly. Where they are more concentrated, more of them happen to move away than move in, so there is a **net** movement down the concentration gradient until they are evenly spread. Molecules are still moving at equilibrium, but there is no net movement.

Across a membrane, simple diffusion works for **small, non-polar** molecules that dissolve in the hydrophobic core (O₂, CO₂) and, slowly, for very small polar molecules such as water.

### Factors affecting the rate

| Factor | Effect on rate of diffusion | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Concentration gradient | Steeper gradient, faster | Larger difference in the number of molecules moving each way |
| Temperature | Higher, faster | Molecules have more kinetic energy |
| Surface area | Larger, faster | More molecules can cross at once |
| Distance (thickness of surface) | Shorter, faster | Less distance to travel; rate falls sharply with distance |
| Size of molecule | Smaller, faster | Small molecules move faster at the same temperature |
| Nature of molecule | Non-polar, faster across the bilayer | Lipid-soluble molecules dissolve in the hydrophobic core |

Fick's law sums up the first four: rate is proportional to (surface area × concentration difference) ÷ thickness. This explains why gas exchange surfaces are large and thin and why a blood supply maintains a steep gradient.

## Facilitated diffusion

Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) and larger polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) cannot cross the hydrophobic core. They diffuse through proteins instead:

- **Channel proteins** form water-filled pores lined with polar R groups. Each lets only particular ions through, and many can open or close (they are **gated**), for example the voltage-gated sodium channels of neurones.
- **Carrier proteins** bind a specific molecule, change shape and release it on the other side. Glucose enters red blood cells this way.
- **Aquaporins** are channel proteins for water, allowing rapid osmosis in cells such as those of the kidney collecting duct.

Facilitated diffusion is still **passive**: movement is only down the gradient. Because there is a limited number of protein molecules, the rate levels off at high concentration difference when all the proteins are working at full speed; simple diffusion keeps increasing.

## Osmosis and water potential

Solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move. Water therefore moves from a dilute solution (higher, less negative $\psi$) to a concentrated solution (lower, more negative $\psi$) across a partially permeable membrane, until the water potentials are equal.

### Effects on animal cells

Animal cells have no cell wall.

- In a solution with **higher** $\psi$ than the cytoplasm, water enters, the cell swells and may **burst (lysis)**: red blood cells undergo haemolysis.
- In a solution with **lower** $\psi$, water leaves and the cell shrinks; red blood cells become shrivelled with a scalloped edge (**crenation**).
- With **equal** $\psi$, there is no net movement.

### Effects on plant cells

- In a solution with higher $\psi$, water enters the vacuole, the protoplast pushes against the wall and the cell becomes **turgid**. The wall resists expansion, so the cell does not burst.
- When $\psi$ outside equals the cell's, the cell is **flaccid** if it was on the point of losing turgor.
- In a solution with lower $\psi$, water leaves, the protoplast shrinks and pulls away from the wall: the cell is **plasmolysed**. The space between wall and membrane fills with the external solution, because the cellulose wall is fully permeable. The point at which half the cells in a tissue are plasmolysed is called **incipient plasmolysis**.

## Investigating osmosis in plant tissue

Cylinders are cut from a potato with a cork borer, trimmed to the same length, blotted and weighed, then left in a series of solutions for a set time (for example 24 hours at the same temperature), blotted the same way and reweighed. Solutions are made by **serial** or **proportional dilution** of a stock solution.

:::worked-solution[Percentage change in mass of one cylinder]
1. Initial mass = 3.20 g; final mass = 2.88 g.
2. Change in mass = 2.88 − 3.20 = −0.32 g.
3. Percentage change = $\dfrac{-0.32}{3.20} \times 100 = -10.0\%$.
4. The negative sign records a loss: the solution had a lower water potential than the tissue, so water left by osmosis.
:::

Percentage change, not change in grams, is used because the cylinders do not start with exactly the same mass.

:::worked-solution[Estimating the water potential of potato tissue]
Cylinders, each trimmed to 2.50 g, were placed in solutions of known water potential.

| Water potential of solution / kPa | Initial mass / g | Final mass / g | Change in mass / % |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2.50 | 2.79 | +11.6 |
| −250 | 2.50 | 2.66 | +6.4 |
| −500 | 2.50 | 2.58 | +3.2 |
| −750 | 2.50 | 2.47 | −1.2 |
| −1000 | 2.50 | 2.37 | −5.2 |
| −1250 | 2.50 | 2.29 | −8.4 |

1. Plot percentage change in mass (y) against water potential of the solution (x).
2. The line crosses 0% between −500 kPa (+3.2%) and −750 kPa (−1.2%).
3. Interpolating: $-500 + \dfrac{3.2}{3.2 + 1.2} \times (-250) = -500 - 182 = -682\ \text{kPa}$.
4. At this water potential there is no net movement of water, so the tissue's water potential is about **−680 kPa**.
:::

:::example{title="Visking tubing as a model cell"}
Tatenda fills a bag of Visking tubing with 10% sucrose solution and places it in distilled water. After an hour the bag is firm and heavier. **Explain:** the distilled water has a water potential of 0 kPa, higher than that of the sucrose solution, so water moves into the bag by osmosis. Sucrose molecules are too large to pass through the pores in the tubing, so they stay inside.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying water moves to a 'higher water potential'"}
Water moves from **higher** to **lower** water potential. A concentrated solution has a lower (more negative) water potential.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling −800 kPa 'higher' than −400 kPa"}
−400 kPa is the higher water potential, because it is closer to zero.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking facilitated diffusion uses ATP"}
Facilitated diffusion uses proteins but is passive. If a protein moves a substance against its gradient using ATP, that is active transport.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying plasmolysed cells contain air"}
The gap between the cell wall and the shrunken protoplast is filled with the external solution, which passes freely through the wall.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Osmosis appears on every paper: ZIMSEC Papers 1, 2 and 4, Cambridge Papers 1, 2 and 3. In *explain* answers, compare the water potentials explicitly ("the solution has a lower water potential than the cell"), name the partially permeable membrane and say **net** movement.
:::

:::exam-tip
For the practical, keep the sign in percentage change, explain why percentage change is used, and read the water potential where the line crosses 0%. Quote a sensible precision for the estimate.
:::

:::exam-tip
*Compare* simple and facilitated diffusion with a similarity (both passive, down the gradient) and a difference (proteins, specificity, saturation).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient by random motion; its rate depends on gradient, temperature, surface area, distance and molecule size and polarity.
- Facilitated diffusion moves ions and polar molecules through specific channel or carrier proteins; it is passive and can become saturated.
- Osmosis is net movement of water from higher to lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane; pure water is 0 kPa and solutions are negative.
- Animal cells burst or crenate; plant cells become turgid, flaccid or plasmolysed.
- The water potential of a tissue is read where percentage change in mass is zero.
:::
