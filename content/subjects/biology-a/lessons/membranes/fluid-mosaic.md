## Introduction

Every living cell is wrapped in a membrane about 7-10 nm thick, far too thin to see with a light microscope. That film decides what enters and leaves the cell, lets the cell recognise its neighbours and receive hormone messages, and holds the enzymes of many reactions. When a lymphocyte in a child in Mutare recognises a malaria parasite as foreign, or when a liver cell responds to insulin after a meal of sadza, the membrane is doing the work.

The model used to explain membrane structure is the **fluid mosaic model**, proposed by Singer and Nicolson in 1972. You should already know the structure of a phospholipid (glycerol, two fatty acids and a phosphate group) and the idea of hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules. The movement of substances across membranes is covered in the lessons on diffusion and osmosis and on active transport.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fluid mosaic model"}
A model of membrane structure in which a bilayer of phospholipid molecules, free to move sideways, has proteins of different kinds scattered through it like the pieces of a mosaic; cholesterol, glycolipids and glycoproteins are also present.
:::

:::definition{term="Intrinsic (integral) protein"}
A membrane protein that is embedded in the bilayer, often spanning it from one side to the other, held there by hydrophobic regions on its surface; channel and carrier proteins are examples.
:::

:::definition{term="Extrinsic (peripheral) protein"}
A protein attached to the inner or outer surface of the membrane, usually to the polar heads of phospholipids or to intrinsic proteins, without entering the hydrophobic core.
:::

:::definition{term="Glycocalyx"}
The layer of carbohydrate chains of glycoproteins and glycolipids on the outer surface of the cell surface membrane.
:::

:::definition{term="Ligand"}
A signalling molecule, such as a hormone or neurotransmitter, that binds specifically to a receptor.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Why 'fluid' and why 'mosaic'"}
**Fluid**: individual phospholipids and many proteins move sideways within their own layer. **Mosaic**: seen from above, proteins of many types are scattered through the bilayer in a pattern.
:::

## Why phospholipids form a bilayer

A phospholipid has a **hydrophilic head** (the phosphate group, which is charged and attracts water) and two **hydrophobic tails** (fatty acid chains, which are non-polar). In water:

1. The heads form hydrogen bonds and other attractions with water molecules, so they face the watery medium.
2. The tails are excluded from water; they are most stable packed together, away from water.
3. Because cells have water on both sides (cytoplasm inside, tissue fluid outside), the molecules arrange themselves in **two layers**: heads facing out on both surfaces, tails meeting in the middle.

The result is a sheet with a **hydrophobic core** about 3-4 nm across. Small non-polar molecules (oxygen, carbon dioxide, steroid hormones) dissolve in this core and pass through easily. Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cannot cross the core and need proteins.

## The components and their roles

| Component | Position | Main roles |
|---|---|---|
| Phospholipids | Bilayer, heads out, tails in | Basic structure; barrier to ions and polar molecules; fluidity |
| Cholesterol | Between phospholipid tails, in both layers | Regulates fluidity; adds stability; reduces leakage of ions and water |
| Channel proteins | Intrinsic, span the bilayer | Water-filled pore for specific ions or water (facilitated diffusion) |
| Carrier proteins | Intrinsic, span the bilayer | Change shape to move specific molecules (facilitated diffusion or active transport) |
| Receptor proteins | Intrinsic, binding site outside | Bind ligands such as hormones; cell signalling |
| Glycoproteins | Proteins with carbohydrate chains outside | Cell recognition (antigens), receptors, cell adhesion |
| Glycolipids | Lipids with carbohydrate chains outside | Cell recognition (antigens, such as the ABO blood group antigens), stability |
| Extrinsic proteins | Inner or outer surface | Enzymes; links to the cytoskeleton; part of signalling pathways |

### Cholesterol and fluidity

Cholesterol molecules fit between the fatty acid tails. They **restrict the movement** of the tails at higher temperatures, so the membrane does not become too fluid and leaky. At low temperatures they **stop the tails packing closely** and solidifying, so the membrane stays fluid. Cholesterol therefore keeps fluidity within a useful range. Its hydrophobic bulk also makes the core less permeable to ions and water.

Fluidity also depends on the fatty acids: **unsaturated** tails have kinks (at C=C double bonds), so they cannot pack tightly and the membrane is more fluid; **saturated** tails pack closely and make it less fluid.

### Proteins: transport, enzymes and receptors

Proteins are held in place by their own pattern of amino acids. Regions with **non-polar R groups** sit in the hydrophobic core; regions with **polar R groups** face the water on either side. Channel and carrier proteins are specific: each allows only certain ions or molecules through. Some membranes carry enzymes, for example on the microvilli of the small intestine (where maltase and sucrase work on the surface) and on the inner mitochondrial membrane.

### Glycoproteins and glycolipids: recognition

The carbohydrate chains on the outer surface vary in sequence and branching from one cell type to another and from one person to another. They act as **antigens**, allowing cells to recognise "self" and the immune system to detect "non-self" cells. They also help cells to stick together in tissues and some act as receptors.

## Cell signalling in outline

Cells in a multicellular organism must coordinate. In cell signalling:

1. A **signalling cell** secretes a ligand (for example a peptide hormone such as glucagon, by exocytosis).
2. The ligand is **transported** to its target, in the blood (hormones) or across a short gap (neurotransmitters).
3. It binds to a **specific receptor** on the cell surface membrane of a target cell. The receptor's binding site is complementary in shape to the ligand.
4. Binding changes the shape of the receptor, which triggers a **response inside the cell**, often through a chain of reactions that amplifies the signal (the details of the cAMP cascade are studied with the control of blood glucose).

Only cells with the right receptor respond, which is why a hormone carried everywhere in the blood affects only its target cells. Steroid hormones are an exception: being lipid-soluble, they cross the bilayer and bind receptors inside the cell.

:::example{title="Interpreting a temperature experiment"}
Cubes of beetroot are kept in water at different temperatures for 30 minutes and the colour of the water is measured with a colorimeter (more red pigment, less light transmitted).

| Temperature / °C | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 | 70 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light transmitted / % | 96 | 94 | 91 | 72 | 35 | 18 |

**Describe:** transmission falls only slightly from 96% to 91% between 20 °C and 40 °C, then falls steeply to 18% at 70 °C.

**Explain:** the red pigment (betalain) is held in the vacuole by the tonoplast and the cell surface membrane. As temperature rises, phospholipids gain kinetic energy and the bilayer becomes more fluid and leaky. Above about 40 °C, membrane proteins are denatured, leaving gaps, so much more pigment escapes and less light is transmitted.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting an effect on fluidity"}
Some fish living in very cold water have membranes with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids than fish living in warm water. **Suggest why.** The kinks in unsaturated tails prevent close packing, so the membranes stay fluid at low temperature; the transport and receptor proteins can still move and change shape, and vesicles can still form.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the membrane rigid"}
The bilayer is fluid: phospholipids move sideways constantly. Proteins are not fixed in rows like bricks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cholesterol only makes membranes stiffer"}
Cholesterol reduces fluidity at high temperature but prevents the membrane solidifying at low temperature. It buffers fluidity in both directions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting glycoproteins on the inside"}
The carbohydrate chains of glycoproteins and glycolipids face the outside of the cell, where they act in recognition.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying hydrophobic tails 'repel' water"}
Better: the non-polar tails cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, so they are excluded from it and cluster together.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Fluid mosaic questions appear in ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. For *describe the structure*, give position and arrangement (heads facing out, tails inward, proteins spanning or on the surface, carbohydrate on the outside). For *explain*, link each component to a role.
:::

:::exam-tip
In the beetroot practical, *explain* answers need two ideas: increased fluidity or kinetic energy at moderate temperatures, and denaturation of membrane proteins at high temperatures.
:::

:::exam-tip
For cell signalling, use the words **ligand**, **specific receptor** and **complementary shape**, and say why only target cells respond.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Phospholipids form a bilayer with hydrophilic heads outward and hydrophobic tails forming a non-polar core.
- Intrinsic proteins (channels, carriers, receptors) span the bilayer; extrinsic proteins sit on its surfaces.
- Cholesterol keeps fluidity in a useful range and reduces permeability to ions and water.
- Glycoproteins and glycolipids carry carbohydrate chains on the outside for recognition, adhesion and signalling.
- In cell signalling a ligand is secreted, transported and bound by a specific receptor, triggering a response inside the target cell.
:::
