## Introduction

Root hair cells of a maize plant growing in Mashonaland soil hold potassium ions at a concentration many times higher than in the soil water around them. Diffusion alone would let those ions leak out, not in. The cells keep them by **active transport**, spending energy from respiration to pump ions against their gradient. In the same way, a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium, or a pancreatic cell releasing digestive enzymes, uses energy to move material across its membrane in bulk.

You should already know the fluid mosaic model, facilitated diffusion and the idea that ATP is the energy currency of cells (ATP synthesis is covered with respiration).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Active transport"}
The movement of molecules or ions across a membrane against their concentration gradient, through specific carrier proteins, using energy from ATP made in respiration.
:::

:::definition{term="Endocytosis"}
Bulk transport into a cell, in which the cell surface membrane folds inwards around material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Exocytosis"}
Bulk transport out of a cell, in which vesicles move to and fuse with the cell surface membrane, releasing their contents outside.
:::

:::definition{term="Phagocytosis and pinocytosis"}
Phagocytosis is endocytosis of solid particles, such as bacteria, forming a phagocytic vacuole; pinocytosis is endocytosis of liquid and dissolved substances, forming small vesicles.
:::

## How active transport works

Carrier proteins for active transport, often called **pumps**, span the bilayer. The steps are:

1. The ion or molecule binds to a specific site on the carrier protein on one side of the membrane.
2. ATP binds to the protein and is hydrolysed to ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pᵢ); the protein is phosphorylated.
3. The energy released changes the **shape** (conformation) of the carrier, so the binding site now opens to the other side.
4. The substance is released on the far side; the phosphate leaves and the protein returns to its original shape.

Because carriers are specific, each moves only particular substances. Because the process depends on ATP, anything that stops respiration stops active transport.

### The sodium-potassium pump

Almost every animal cell has Na⁺/K⁺ pumps in its cell surface membrane. In each cycle, using one ATP, the pump moves **three sodium ions out** of the cell and **two potassium ions in**, both against their concentration gradients.

This keeps the inside of the cell high in K⁺ and low in Na⁺. The gradients are used:

- by neurones, to set up the resting potential;
- for **co-transport**, where Na⁺ diffusing back into a cell down its gradient drags glucose with it through a co-transporter protein (in the small intestine and kidney);
- to control cell volume, since ion concentrations affect water potential.

## Evidence that uptake is active

| Observation | What it shows |
|---|---|
| A substance accumulates inside cells at a higher concentration than outside | Movement against the gradient: it cannot be diffusion |
| Uptake stops when respiratory inhibitors (such as cyanide) are added | Energy from respiration (ATP) is needed |
| Uptake stops when oxygen is removed (roots in nitrogen gas) | Aerobic respiration supplies the ATP |
| Uptake increases with temperature to an optimum, then falls sharply | Respiration and the carrier proteins are both temperature-sensitive; carrier proteins denature |
| Uptake is specific and can be saturated | Carrier proteins are involved |

:::example{title="Ion uptake by barley roots"}
Tendai measured the uptake of potassium ions by excised barley roots in aerated solution and in solution bubbled with nitrogen gas, at 25 °C.

| Time / min | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| K⁺ uptake, aerated / arbitrary units | 0 | 14 | 26 | 37 | 47 |
| K⁺ uptake, nitrogen / arbitrary units | 0 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |

**Describe:** with oxygen, uptake rises steadily to 47 units in 80 minutes. Without oxygen, about 6 units are taken up in the first 20 minutes and then uptake almost stops, reaching only 9 units.

**Explain:** in aerated solution, aerobic respiration makes ATP for active transport, so uptake continues. Without oxygen there is little ATP, so active uptake nearly stops. The small early uptake is passive: ions diffuse into the cell walls and spaces between cells (the apoplast) until concentrations equalise.
:::

## Bulk transport

Large molecules, particles and large volumes cannot pass through carrier proteins. Instead, the membrane itself moves, using its fluidity. Both directions need ATP, for the cytoskeleton (microtubules and microfilaments) that moves the membrane and vesicles.

### Endocytosis

1. The cell surface membrane folds inwards (invaginates) around the material.
2. The edges of the fold meet and fuse.
3. A vesicle (or vacuole) pinches off into the cytoplasm.

In **phagocytosis** ("cell eating"), neutrophils and macrophages engulf bacteria or cell debris into a phagocytic vacuole, which then fuses with lysosomes whose hydrolytic enzymes digest the contents. In **pinocytosis** ("cell drinking"), small vesicles take in droplets of tissue fluid with dissolved substances.

### Exocytosis

1. Proteins made on ribosomes of the rough endoplasmic reticulum are modified and packaged by the Golgi body into secretory vesicles.
2. Vesicles move along microtubules to the cell surface membrane.
3. The vesicle membrane fuses with the cell surface membrane and the contents are released outside.

Examples: secretion of digestive enzymes by pancreatic cells, insulin from β cells, neurotransmitters from the presynaptic membrane, and materials for new cell walls in plants. Exocytosis also adds membrane to the cell surface.

## Comparing the transport processes

| Process | Direction relative to gradient | Proteins involved | ATP needed | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | Down | None | No | O₂, CO₂ across alveolar cells |
| Facilitated diffusion | Down | Channel or carrier | No | Glucose into red blood cells; Cl⁻ channels |
| Osmosis | Down a water potential gradient | Sometimes (aquaporins) | No | Water into root hair cells |
| Active transport | Against | Carrier (pump) | Yes | Na⁺/K⁺ pump; mineral ions into root hairs |
| Endocytosis | Not relevant (bulk) | Membrane and cytoskeleton | Yes | Phagocytosis of bacteria |
| Exocytosis | Not relevant (bulk) | Membrane and cytoskeleton | Yes | Enzyme and hormone secretion |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying active transport 'gives energy to the molecules'"}
ATP is used to change the **shape of the carrier protein**, not to speed up the substance being moved.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the pump stoichiometry backwards"}
Three Na⁺ go **out** and two K⁺ come **in** per ATP.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling phagocytosis 'active transport'"}
Endocytosis and exocytosis need ATP, but they are **bulk transport** by vesicles, not movement through carrier proteins.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Active transport moves substances against their gradient through specific carrier proteins, using ATP to change the carrier's shape.
- The Na⁺/K⁺ pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, maintaining gradients used in nerve impulses and co-transport.
- Evidence: accumulation against a gradient, and inhibition by cyanide, lack of oxygen and high temperature.
- Endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis) and exocytosis move material in bulk by vesicles and need ATP.
- Only active transport and bulk transport use energy; diffusion, facilitated diffusion and osmosis are passive.
:::
