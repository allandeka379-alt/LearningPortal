## Introduction

Why are some people born with albinism, and why does a single change in one gene give sickle cell anaemia, a condition well known in Zimbabwe and across sub-Saharan Africa? The answer in every case runs through a protein. A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a polypeptide; the polypeptide folds into a protein such as an enzyme, a transport protein or a structural protein; and the protein's activity produces a characteristic that we can see or measure. Change the gene, and you may change the protein and so the characteristic.

This lesson links what you know about DNA, transcription and translation to inheritance. You should already know how the base sequence of DNA codes for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide, and what a gene mutation is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gene"}
A length of DNA at a particular position on a chromosome that codes for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA); the unit of inheritance.
:::

:::definition{term="Locus"}
The position of a gene on a chromosome. Homologous chromosomes carry the same genes at the same loci.
:::

:::definition{term="Allele"}
One of two or more alternative forms of a gene, at the same locus, that differ in base sequence.
:::

:::definition{term="Genotype"}
The alleles an organism has for a particular gene (or genes), for example $Hb^A Hb^S$.
:::

:::definition{term="Phenotype"}
The observable or measurable characteristics of an organism, produced by the interaction of its genotype with its environment.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The chain to remember"}
Gene (base sequence) → mRNA → polypeptide (amino acid sequence) → protein shape and function → phenotype.
:::

## From gene to phenotype

Diploid organisms have two copies of each chromosome (except the sex chromosomes in one sex), so they carry **two alleles** of each gene, one inherited from each parent. They may be the same (**homozygous**) or different (**heterozygous**).

Alleles differ because of **mutations**: changes in the base sequence. A different base sequence may give a different amino acid sequence, which can change the way the protein folds, its tertiary structure and so its function. Often an allele that codes for a non-functional protein is **recessive**: one working allele makes enough protein for a normal phenotype. Some alleles code for a protein with a new, harmful activity and are **dominant**.

## Five examples

### 1. The TYR gene, tyrosinase and albinism

The TYR gene codes for the enzyme **tyrosinase**, found in melanocytes in the skin, hair and eyes. Tyrosinase catalyses the first steps in converting the amino acid tyrosine into the dark pigment **melanin**.

- Several recessive alleles of TYR code for tyrosinase that is inactive or much less active.
- A person homozygous for these alleles cannot make melanin. They have very pale skin and hair and eye problems: this is a form of **albinism**.
- A heterozygous person makes enough tyrosinase from the normal allele and has normal pigmentation.

People with albinism need protection from the sun, because melanin normally absorbs ultraviolet radiation. In Zimbabwe, as elsewhere, people with albinism have often faced stigma, which has no basis in biology: the only difference is one enzyme.

### 2. The HBB gene, beta-globin and sickle cell anaemia

The HBB gene codes for **β-globin**, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin (two α and two β chains).

- In the allele $Hb^S$, a **base substitution** changes the DNA triplet for amino acid 6 so that CTC on the template strand becomes CAC, and the mRNA codon GAG becomes GUG. The amino acid **glutamic acid** (charged, hydrophilic) is replaced by **valine** (non-polar, hydrophobic).
- In deoxygenated blood, haemoglobin S molecules stick together by their hydrophobic patches into long fibres, distorting the red blood cells into a sickle shape.
- Sickled cells are less flexible, block capillaries and are destroyed quickly, causing pain, anaemia and organ damage. People with genotype $Hb^S Hb^S$ have **sickle cell anaemia**.
- Heterozygotes ($Hb^A Hb^S$) have **sickle cell trait**: usually no symptoms, and some protection against severe malaria.

### 3. The F8 gene, factor VIII and haemophilia

The F8 gene is on the **X chromosome** and codes for **factor VIII**, a protein needed in the cascade of reactions that makes blood clot.

- Recessive alleles code for a non-functional or reduced amount of factor VIII.
- Without it, blood clots very slowly, so bleeding after injury lasts much longer and bleeding into joints and muscles can occur: **haemophilia A**.
- Because the gene is X-linked, males (with one X) who inherit the allele have the condition, while females usually need two copies. This is studied with sex linkage.

### 4. The HTT gene, huntingtin and Huntington's disease

The HTT gene codes for a protein called **huntingtin**, found in many cells including neurones of the brain.

- The gene contains a repeated triplet, CAG, coding for a run of the amino acid glutamine. Normal alleles have a modest number of repeats.
- In the disease allele the CAG section is **expanded** (roughly 40 or more repeats). The huntingtin made has an abnormally long glutamine chain, misfolds and forms clumps that gradually damage and kill certain brain neurones.
- **Huntington's disease** causes progressive problems with movement, thinking and mood, usually starting in middle adulthood.
- The allele is **dominant**: one copy is enough, because the abnormal protein itself is harmful.

### 5. The Le/le alleles and stem height in peas

Mendel studied tall and dwarf pea plants. We now know the difference lies in the gene **Le**, which codes for an enzyme in the pathway that makes active **gibberellin** (it converts an inactive gibberellin into the active form, GA₁).

- The dominant allele **Le** codes for a functional enzyme. Active gibberellin stimulates cell division and elongation in the stem, so plants with at least one Le allele are **tall**.
- The recessive allele **le** has a single base substitution that changes one amino acid (alanine to threonine) near the active site, making the enzyme much less active.
- Plants with genotype **le le** make little active gibberellin and are **dwarf**.

| Gene | Protein | Effect of mutant allele | Inheritance |
|---|---|---|---|
| TYR | Tyrosinase (enzyme) | No melanin: albinism | Autosomal recessive |
| HBB | β-globin (haemoglobin) | HbS polymerises: sickle cell anaemia | Autosomal; codominant at the cell level |
| F8 | Factor VIII (clotting) | Slow clotting: haemophilia A | X-linked recessive |
| HTT | Huntingtin | Misfolded protein damages neurones | Autosomal dominant |
| Le | Gibberellin-synthesising enzyme | Little active gibberellin: dwarf | Autosomal recessive (le) |

:::worked-solution[How many nucleotides code for β-globin?]
β-globin has 146 amino acids in the finished polypeptide.
1. Each amino acid is coded by a triplet of three bases.
2. Bases needed $= 146 \times 3 = 438$.
3. Add a start codon (AUG for methionine, which is removed after translation) and a stop codon: $438 + 3 + 3 = 444$ nucleotides in the coding sequence of the mRNA.
4. The gene itself is longer, because it also contains introns and non-coding regions.
:::

:::example{title="Predicting the effect of a mutation"}
A base substitution in the TYR gene changes a codon in the middle of the active site from one coding for a polar amino acid to one coding for a non-polar amino acid. **Predict and explain:** the R group in the active site changes, so the shape and charge of the active site change; tyrosine no longer fits or binds, so the enzyme is inactive. A person homozygous for this allele would have albinism; a heterozygote would be normally pigmented, because one functional allele makes enough enzyme.
:::

## The gene concept: a short history

1. **1866, Gregor Mendel** proposed from pea crosses that characteristics are controlled by inherited "factors" that come in pairs and separate in gametes.
2. **Early 1900s:** Mendel's work was rediscovered; the word **gene** was introduced in 1909, and it was shown that genes are carried on chromosomes, in a linear order at fixed loci.
3. **1940s:** the "one gene, one enzyme" idea linked genes to proteins, later refined to **one gene, one polypeptide**.
4. **1950s onwards:** DNA was shown to be the genetic material, its double-helix structure was worked out (1953), and the genetic code was cracked.
5. **Today** a gene is a transcribed DNA sequence, often with introns and control regions; some code for functional RNA.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards expect the terms gene, locus, allele, genotype and phenotype and the link from gene to protein to phenotype. The five named examples (TYR, HBB, F8, HTT, Le) are those listed by Cambridge (A Level, Paper 4); ZIMSEC teaches the gene concept in Form 5 and uses sickle cell anaemia and albinism widely.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using gene and allele as if they mean the same"}
A gene is the length of DNA at a locus (for example the gene for haemoglobin β chain). Alleles are its different versions ($Hb^A$, $Hb^S$).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a mutation always changes the protein"}
A substitution may give a codon for the same amino acid (the code is degenerate), or change an amino acid outside the important regions, with little effect.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
*Explain how a mutation causes a phenotype* (Cambridge Paper 4, ZIMSEC Paper 2): change in base sequence → change in mRNA codon → different amino acid → different R group → changed tertiary structure → protein (enzyme) does not work → effect on the organism.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A gene is a length of DNA at a locus coding for a polypeptide; alleles are alternative forms with different base sequences.
- Genes affect phenotype through the proteins they code for: enzymes, structural and transport proteins.
- Recessive alleles often code for a non-functional protein; dominant harmful alleles often code for a protein with a damaging new property.
- Examples: TYR (albinism), HBB (sickle cell anaemia), F8 (haemophilia), HTT (Huntington's disease), Le (tall or dwarf peas).
- The gene concept developed from Mendel's factors to genes on chromosomes, one gene one polypeptide, and DNA sequences.
:::
