## Introduction

Much of the maize planted in Zimbabwe each season is grown from **hybrid** seed, bought new every year rather than saved from the last harvest. Farmers who save seed from a hybrid crop find that the next generation is uneven and yields less. The reason lies in how hybrid seed is bred, and it is one of the clearest illustrations of **artificial selection**: humans, not the environment, choosing which individuals breed. The same principle produced high-yielding dairy cows, disease-resistant wheat and almost every crop and domestic animal on Zimbabwean farms.

You should already understand natural selection, alleles and genotypes, and the difference between homozygous and heterozygous individuals.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Artificial selection (selective breeding)"}
The process in which humans choose individuals with desired characteristics to breed together, so that the alleles for those characteristics become more frequent over generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Inbreeding"}
Breeding between closely related individuals, which increases homozygosity in the offspring.
:::

:::definition{term="Inbreeding depression"}
Reduced vigour, fertility, growth or yield in inbred individuals, mainly because harmful recessive alleles become homozygous and are expressed.
:::

:::definition{term="Hybridisation (outbreeding)"}
Crossing genetically different individuals, such as two unrelated inbred lines, to produce offspring that are heterozygous at many loci.
:::

:::definition{term="Hybrid vigour (heterosis)"}
The improved growth, yield or fitness of hybrid offspring compared with either of their parents.
:::

## The principles of selective breeding

1. **Identify** the desired characteristic, such as high yield, disease resistance, drought tolerance or a short growing season.
2. **Select** parents that show it most strongly (or whose relatives do).
3. **Cross** them and grow or rear the offspring.
4. **Select** the best offspring and breed from them.
5. **Repeat** over many generations until the characteristic is reliably present.

Each round raises the frequency of the alleles that produce the desired phenotype. Because breeders choose individuals by phenotype, characteristics strongly affected by the environment respond slowly; breeders therefore grow candidates in uniform conditions or test their offspring.

### The costs

- **Loss of genetic diversity**: alleles not wanted by the breeder, including ones that might later be useful (for example, resistance to a new disease), are lost from the breed.
- **Inbreeding depression**: crossing related individuals makes harmful recessive alleles homozygous, causing lower fertility, weaker growth and more inherited disease.
- **Uniformity**: a crop of genetically identical plants can be wiped out by a single new pathogen.

Seed banks and rare-breed collections preserve the genetic diversity that breeding programmes remove.

## Example 1: disease resistance in wheat and rice

Modern wheat and rice varieties give high yields but can be devastated by fungal diseases such as rusts in wheat and blast in rice. Old local varieties and wild relatives often carry **resistance alleles** but yield poorly.

1. Cross the high-yielding variety with the resistant variety.
2. Grow the offspring and expose them to the pathogen; keep only resistant plants.
3. **Backcross** these repeatedly to the high-yielding parent, each time selecting resistant offspring.
4. After several generations, the plants have almost all the genome of the high-yielding parent plus the resistance allele.

This takes many years, and pathogens evolve, so breeders must keep introducing new resistance alleles.

## Example 2: inbred lines and hybrid maize

Maize is naturally cross-pollinated (wind carries pollen from the tassels to the silks of other plants), so a maize population is highly heterozygous.

1. **Inbreeding**: breeders self-pollinate selected plants for several generations, covering the tassels and silks with bags. Each generation becomes more homozygous, and the lines become genetically uniform but less vigorous (inbreeding depression).
2. **Hybridisation**: two different inbred lines are crossed. The tassels of one line are removed (detasselling) so that it can only be pollinated by the other.
3. The F1 hybrid seed is **heterozygous at many loci**, with dominant alleles from one parent masking harmful recessive alleles from the other, and it shows **hybrid vigour**: tall, high-yielding plants.
4. Because both parents were homozygous, all F1 plants have the **same genotype**, so the crop is uniform: the plants flower and mature together, which suits mechanical harvesting and planning.
5. If a farmer saves F1 seed, the **F2** generation is variable, because alleles segregate again in meiosis; yield falls and plants are uneven. Hybrid seed must therefore be bought each season.

:::worked-solution[Hybrid vigour in maize (illustrative data)]
In a breeding trial, inbred line P yielded 3.1 tonnes per hectare, inbred line Q yielded 2.8 tonnes per hectare and their F1 hybrid yielded 7.4 tonnes per hectare.
1. Mean of the two parents $= (3.1 + 2.8) \div 2 = 2.95$ tonnes per hectare.
2. Increase of the hybrid over the parental mean $= 7.4 - 2.95 = 4.45$ tonnes per hectare.
3. Percentage increase $= \dfrac{4.45}{2.95} \times 100 = 151\%$ (3 s.f.).
4. The hybrid yields more than two and a half times the mean of its parents: hybrid vigour.
:::

## Example 3: milk yield in dairy cattle

Milk yield is a quantitative characteristic controlled by many genes, and it is shown only by females. Bulls carry the alleles but cannot be judged by their own phenotype.

1. Record the milk yield of every cow in a herd over each lactation.
2. **Progeny testing**: mate young bulls with many cows and measure the milk yield of their daughters; bulls whose daughters yield most have the most favourable alleles.
3. Collect semen from the best bulls and use **artificial insemination** to fertilise many cows, possibly thousands, in many herds.
4. Select the highest-yielding daughters as the next generation of dairy cows; embryo transfer from top cows can multiply them further.

:::example{title="Interpreting a progeny test"}
Three young bulls are each mated with 40 cows. The mean yields of their daughters' first lactations are 5900 kg (bull A), 6800 kg (bull B) and 6100 kg (bull C), with similar herd conditions. **Explain the choice:** bull B's daughters yield most, so bull B probably carries more alleles for high yield; his semen should be used widely by artificial insemination. **Evaluate:** the daughters should have been kept under similar conditions, because yield also depends strongly on feed and health; using many daughters per bull reduces the effect of individual differences between the cows they were mated with.
:::

## Artificial selection compared

| Feature | Natural selection | Artificial selection | Genetic engineering |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who selects | The environment (selection pressures) | Humans | Humans |
| What is favoured | Characteristics that increase survival and reproduction | Characteristics useful to humans, even if harmful to the organism | A specific gene chosen by humans |
| Source of alleles | Existing variation within the species | Existing variation within the species (and close relatives) | Can come from any organism |
| Speed | Usually slow, many generations | Faster, but still many generations | Fast: one generation |
| Precision | Whole genotype | Whole genome selected; unwanted alleles linked to wanted ones may come too | A single gene transferred |

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge A Level) expect the principles of selective breeding and the maize and dairy cattle examples. Cambridge names disease resistance in wheat and rice explicitly. Genetic engineering itself is a separate topic.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying hybrid maize is genetically modified"}
Hybrid maize is produced by conventional crossing of inbred lines. Genetic modification inserts a gene by recombinant DNA technology.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying saved hybrid seed gives the same crop"}
The F2 generation segregates, so it is variable and loses much of the hybrid vigour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Selecting bulls by their own milk yield"}
Bulls produce no milk; they are judged by the yields of their daughters (progeny testing).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For a *describe* question on selective breeding, give the sequence: choose parents with the trait, cross, select the best offspring, breed from them, repeat over many generations. Then *explain* using alleles: the frequency of the desired alleles increases.
:::

:::exam-tip
Questions on hybrid maize often ask you to *explain* both uniformity (homozygous parents, so all F1 have the same genotype) and vigour (heterozygosity, dominant alleles masking harmful recessive ones).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Humans select parents with desired traits and breed from the best offspring over many generations, raising the frequency of desired alleles.
- Costs: loss of genetic diversity, inbreeding depression and vulnerability of uniform crops.
- Disease resistance is bred into wheat and rice by crossing with resistant varieties and backcrossing.
- Hybrid maize: inbred lines crossed to give uniform, vigorous F1 seed that must be bought each season.
- Dairy bulls are chosen by progeny testing and used widely through artificial insemination.
:::
