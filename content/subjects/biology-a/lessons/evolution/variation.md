## Introduction

Walk through a crowded market in Harare and no two people look alike: they differ in height, skin colour, blood group, the shape of their ears. The maize on a smallholding varies too: some cobs long, some short, some plants tall, some stunted where the soil is poor. This **variation** matters for two reasons. It is the raw material on which natural selection acts, so it drives evolution. And for farmers and doctors it raises a practical question: how much of a difference is inherited, and how much is caused by the environment?

You should already know the meaning of gene, allele, genotype and phenotype, and how meiosis and random fertilisation produce genetic variation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Variation"}
Differences between individuals of the same species.
:::

:::definition{term="Phenotype"}
The observable or measurable characteristics of an organism, resulting from the interaction of its genotype with its environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Discontinuous variation"}
Variation in which individuals fall into a few distinct categories with no intermediates; it is usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little or no environmental effect.
:::

:::definition{term="Continuous variation"}
Variation in which a characteristic shows a complete range of values between two extremes; it is controlled by many genes (polygenes) and is strongly affected by the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Polygenes"}
Several genes, at different loci, that together affect one characteristic, each allele making a small, additive contribution to the phenotype.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Phenotype = genotype + environment"}
The genotype sets the possible range of a phenotype; the environment determines where within that range an individual ends up.
:::

## Causes of variation

### Genetic causes

Genetic variation between individuals arises from:

- **mutation**, which creates new alleles (the only source of entirely new genetic material);
- **meiosis**: independent assortment of homologous chromosomes and crossing over between them;
- **random fertilisation**: any male gamete can fuse with any female gamete;
- **random mating** between individuals in a population.

Only genetic variation can be passed on to offspring, so only genetic variation can be acted on by selection.

### Environmental causes

The environment changes how genes are expressed and how well an organism grows. Examples:

- **Diet and nutrients**: a child with too little protein grows less tall than their genes allow; maize short of nitrogen is stunted and yellow.
- **Light**: plants grown in the dark are pale and tall with long internodes (etiolated) because chlorophyll is not made.
- **Temperature**: in Himalayan rabbits and Siamese cats, an enzyme for dark pigment works only at the cooler extremities (ears, nose, feet, tail), so those parts are dark.
- **Exercise, disease and sunlight**: muscle mass, scars and skin tanning.

Environmentally caused variation is not inherited: a well-fed parent does not pass its extra height to its offspring.

## Discontinuous and continuous variation

| Feature | Discontinuous variation | Continuous variation |
|---|---|---|
| Phenotypes | A few distinct classes, no intermediates | A range of values between extremes |
| Number of genes | One or a few | Many (polygenes) |
| Effect of alleles | Different alleles have large effects | Each allele has a small, additive effect |
| Environment | Little or no effect | Large effect |
| Data | Qualitative; counted in categories | Quantitative; measured |
| Graph | Bar chart with gaps between bars | Histogram; often a normal distribution |
| Examples | ABO blood groups, sickle cell anaemia, flower colour in peas | Height, mass, skin colour, leaf length, milk yield |

In **discontinuous** variation, a single gene with two or three alleles gives clear categories. The ABO blood group gene, with alleles $I^A$, $I^B$ and $I^O$, gives four blood groups: a person cannot be "nearly group A". The environment has no effect on blood group.

### How polygenes give continuous variation

Suppose plant height depends on two genes, each with a **contributing** allele (A or B) that adds 5 cm to a base height of 100 cm, and a non-contributing allele (a or b) that adds nothing. The effects are **additive**: only the number of contributing alleles matters.

:::worked-solution[Height classes from two additive genes]
Cross two plants of genotype AaBb.
1. Each parent produces gametes AB, Ab, aB and ab in equal proportions, giving 16 equally likely combinations.
2. Count the contributing alleles in each offspring genotype:

| Contributing alleles | Example genotypes | Height / cm | Fraction of offspring |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | aabb | 100 | 1/16 |
| 1 | Aabb, aaBb | 105 | 4/16 |
| 2 | AAbb, AaBb, aaBB | 110 | 6/16 |
| 3 | AABb, AaBB | 115 | 4/16 |
| 4 | AABB | 120 | 1/16 |

3. Five height classes appear in a 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1 ratio, with most plants in the middle and few at the extremes.
4. With $n$ such genes there are $2n + 1$ classes. With many genes the classes become so numerous and close together that they merge, and environmental effects (soil, water, light) blur the boundaries completely. The result is a smooth, bell-shaped **normal distribution**.
:::

## Showing continuous variation

Continuous data are grouped into classes of equal width and plotted as a **histogram**: bars touching, with the variable on the x-axis and frequency on the y-axis.

:::example{title="Describing a histogram of groundnut kernel masses"}
Chipo weighed 117 groundnut kernels from one field.

| Seed mass / g | 0.30–0.39 | 0.40–0.49 | 0.50–0.59 | 0.60–0.69 | 0.70–0.79 | 0.80–0.89 | 0.90–0.99 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of seeds | 3 | 11 | 24 | 37 | 26 | 12 | 4 |

**Describe:** masses range from 0.30 g to under 1.00 g; the modal class is 0.60–0.69 g (37 kernels); numbers fall away fairly evenly on either side, giving a roughly symmetrical, bell-shaped (normal) distribution. **Explain:** kernel mass is a continuous variable controlled by many genes with small additive effects, and modified by the environment (water, nutrients, position on the plant), so a full range of values occurs rather than distinct classes.
:::

### Separating genes and environment

To find out how much of the variation is genetic, biologists compare individuals with the same genotype in different environments, or different genotypes in the same environment:

- **Clones** of a plant, such as cuttings of one sweet potato, grown in poor and rich soil differ only because of environment.
- **Identical (monozygotic) twins** have the same genotype, so differences between them are environmental.
- Different maize varieties grown side by side in one field differ mainly because of their genes.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge A Level) expect the two types of variation, their genetic basis and environmental effects. Cambridge also expects you to analyse variation statistically, using standard deviation and the t-test (see those lessons).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying environmental variation can be inherited"}
Only differences in genotype are inherited. A stunted plant from poor soil can produce tall offspring in good soil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Drawing gaps between histogram bars"}
Continuous data are shown as a histogram with touching bars; a bar chart with gaps is for discontinuous categories.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking continuous variation has no genetic basis"}
Continuous variation is controlled by many genes as well as by the environment.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge Paper 4, *explain* the shape of a distribution by linking it to the number of genes (one gene: distinct classes; many additive genes plus environment: normal distribution).
:::

:::exam-tip
When *describing* a histogram, give the range, the modal class with its frequency and the shape (symmetrical, skewed or bimodal), quoting numbers with units.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Phenotypic variation is caused by genes, by the environment and by their interaction; only genetic variation is inherited.
- Mutation, meiosis (independent assortment and crossing over) and random fertilisation produce genetic variation.
- Discontinuous variation: few genes, distinct classes, little environmental effect (ABO blood groups).
- Continuous variation: many additive polygenes plus environment give a normal distribution (height, mass).
- Diet, light and temperature alter phenotype without changing the genotype.
:::
