## Introduction

Lake Malawi, a short distance north-east of Zimbabwe, holds hundreds of species of cichlid fish found nowhere else, many of them brightly coloured and each with its own diet and breeding behaviour. Biologists think most of them arose within the lake from a small number of ancestral species. How can one species become many? The answer is **speciation**: the splitting of one population into two or more groups that can no longer interbreed. Over long periods, speciation, together with natural selection, has produced the whole diversity of life.

You should already understand natural selection, genetic drift, allele frequencies and what a species is.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Evolution"}
A change in the allele frequencies of a population over many generations, which can lead to the formation of new species from pre-existing species.
:::

:::definition{term="Speciation"}
The formation of a new species, which occurs when populations of one species become reproductively isolated and their gene pools change so much that they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
:::

:::definition{term="Reproductive isolation"}
The prevention of successful interbreeding, and so of gene flow, between two populations.
:::

:::definition{term="Allopatric speciation"}
Speciation that occurs when populations are separated by a geographical barrier.
:::

:::definition{term="Sympatric speciation"}
Speciation that occurs in populations living in the same area, through ecological or behavioural separation or through polyploidy.
:::

:::definition{term="Polyploidy"}
Having more than two complete sets of chromosomes in each cell.
:::

## From natural selection to new species

Natural selection changes allele frequencies within a population. If two populations of a species are kept apart, and so cannot exchange alleles (no **gene flow**):

1. Different **mutations** arise in each population.
2. The populations face different **selection pressures** (climate, food, predators), so different alleles are favoured.
3. **Genetic drift** changes allele frequencies by chance, especially if a population is small.
4. Over many generations, the **gene pools** become so different that, even if the populations meet again, they **cannot interbreed** to produce fertile offspring. They are now separate species.

## Allopatric speciation

A **geographical barrier** such as a mountain range, a river that changes course, a stretch of sea, or rising lake levels splits a population.

1. The barrier prevents gene flow between the two groups.
2. Each group adapts to its own conditions by natural selection, and accumulates its own mutations and drift.
3. Differences in structure, physiology or behaviour build up.
4. If the barrier is removed, the groups no longer interbreed successfully: speciation has occurred.

The finches of the Galápagos Islands are a classic example: birds from the mainland colonised separate islands, and populations on different islands evolved different beak shapes suited to different foods, eventually becoming separate species.

## Sympatric speciation

Populations in the **same area** can become reproductively isolated without a geographical barrier.

- **Ecological separation**: groups use different habitats or food within the area, such as different depths of a lake or different host plants, and mate mainly with individuals in the same micro-habitat.
- **Behavioural separation**: differences in courtship, song or colour mean that mates are chosen only from one group. In Lake Malawi cichlids, females of many species choose males by colour, which helps keep closely related species apart.
- **Polyploidy** (mainly in plants): an error in meiosis produces diploid gametes, or two species hybridise and the chromosome number then doubles. The polyploid offspring cannot produce fertile offspring with the parent species, because the chromosome sets do not match at meiosis, so a new species appears in one generation. Modern bread wheat is a polyploid that arose from hybridisation between three ancestral grass species.

## Reproductive isolating mechanisms

| Type | Mechanism | Example of how it works |
|---|---|---|
| Prezygotic | Ecological (habitat) | Populations live in different habitats in the same area and rarely meet |
| Prezygotic | Temporal | Breeding seasons or flowering times differ |
| Prezygotic | Behavioural | Courtship displays, songs or colours are not recognised by the other group |
| Prezygotic | Mechanical | Reproductive organs or flower structures are incompatible |
| Prezygotic | Gametic | Sperm cannot fertilise the egg; pollen cannot grow on the stigma |
| Postzygotic | Hybrid inviability | The hybrid zygote or embryo fails to develop |
| Postzygotic | Hybrid sterility | Hybrids survive but are sterile, e.g. the mule (horse × donkey) |
| Postzygotic | Hybrid breakdown | First-generation hybrids are fertile but their offspring are weak or sterile |

A mule is sterile because the horse contributes 32 chromosomes and the donkey 31; with no matching sets, homologous chromosomes cannot pair properly in meiosis.

## Molecular evidence of relationships

When species share a recent common ancestor, they have had less time to accumulate mutations, so their **DNA base sequences** (and the **amino acid sequences** of their proteins) are more similar. Counting the differences between aligned sequences shows how closely species are related.

:::worked-solution[Counting differences between DNA sequences]
Part of the same gene was sequenced in four related species, W, X, Y and Z (30 bases each, aligned).

W: ATGGCCTTAG CAGTCAAGCT GGAACGTTAC
X: ATGGCCTTGG CAGTCAAGCT GGAACGCTAC
Y: ATGGCTTTGG CAGTTAAGCT CGAACGCTAC
Z: ATGACTTTGG CCGTTAAACT CGAGCGCTAT

1. Compare each pair base by base and count positions that differ.
2. Results:

|  | W | X | Y | Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| W | – | 2 | 5 | 10 |
| X | 2 | – | 3 | 8 |
| Y | 5 | 3 | – | 5 |
| Z | 10 | 8 | 5 | – |

3. W and X differ at only 2 positions, so they are the most closely related; they probably shared a common ancestor most recently.
4. Z differs most from W (10 positions), so Z probably separated from the others earliest.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting protein sequence data"}
Cytochrome c, a protein of the electron transport chain, is found in almost all eukaryotes. Its amino acid sequence is identical in humans and chimpanzees, differs at a few positions between humans and dogs, and differs at many positions between humans and yeast. **Interpret:** the fewer the differences, the more recent the common ancestor. Protein data support DNA data, but DNA data are more detailed, because some base changes do not alter the amino acid (the genetic code is degenerate) and non-coding DNA can be compared too.
:::

Both boards (ZIMSEC Form 6; Cambridge A Level) expect evolution through natural selection, allopatric and sympatric speciation and molecular evidence. Cambridge asks you to explain the role of pre- and post-zygotic isolating mechanisms and to use DNA sequence data.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the barrier causes the change"}
The barrier only prevents gene flow. The change comes from different mutations, different selection pressures and genetic drift in each population.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling any isolated population a new species"}
Populations become separate species only when they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, even if they meet again.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting differences without aligning the sequences"}
Compare the bases position by position along aligned sequences.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays and Cambridge Paper 4, an *explain* answer on allopatric speciation needs: barrier, no gene flow, different mutations, different selection pressures, natural selection of different alleles, drift, many generations, unable to interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
:::

:::exam-tip
When *using* sequence data, state the number of differences for the relevant pairs and conclude: fewer differences, more closely related, more recent common ancestor.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Evolution is a change in allele frequencies over generations that can produce new species.
- Speciation needs reproductive isolation: no gene flow, then different mutations, selection and drift.
- Allopatric speciation follows geographical separation; sympatric speciation arises by ecological or behavioural separation or by polyploidy.
- Isolating mechanisms are prezygotic (ecological, temporal, behavioural, mechanical, gametic) or postzygotic (hybrid inviability, sterility, breakdown).
- Fewer differences in DNA or protein sequences mean a more recent common ancestor.
:::
