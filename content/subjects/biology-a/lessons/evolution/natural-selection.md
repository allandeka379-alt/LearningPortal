## Introduction

In the lowveld of Zimbabwe, malaria control has long relied on spraying the walls of houses with insecticide and on insecticide-treated bed nets. In many parts of Africa, monitoring teams have found mosquitoes that survive doses of insecticide that once killed nearly all of them. Hospitals face a similar problem with bacteria that no longer respond to common antibiotics. Nobody designed these resistant mosquitoes or bacteria: they arose by **natural selection**, the same process that Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace proposed in 1858 to explain how species become adapted to their environments.

You should already understand variation, the difference between genotype and phenotype, and how mutation produces new alleles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Natural selection"}
The process by which individuals with phenotypes better adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, so the alleles responsible are passed on and become more frequent in the population over generations.
:::

:::definition{term="Selection pressure"}
An environmental factor, such as a predator, a disease, competition, climate or a chemical, that affects the chances of survival and reproduction of individuals with different phenotypes.
:::

:::definition{term="Fitness"}
The ability of an organism to survive and to pass on its alleles to the next generation, measured by the number of fertile offspring it leaves.
:::

:::definition{term="Adaptation"}
A heritable feature that increases an organism's chances of survival and reproduction in its environment.
:::

## How natural selection works

1. **Overproduction.** Organisms produce more offspring than the environment can support: a single female mosquito may lay hundreds of eggs in her life.
2. **Competition.** Resources such as food, space, mates and breeding sites are limited, so there is a **struggle for existence** and many individuals die before reproducing.
3. **Variation.** Individuals in a population vary, and some of this variation is **genetic** (caused by different alleles, ultimately from mutation).
4. **Differential survival.** Individuals whose phenotypes are better suited to the current selection pressures are more likely to survive. This is sometimes summarised as "survival of the fittest", where fittest means best adapted, not strongest.
5. **Reproduction.** The survivors reproduce and pass on their **advantageous alleles**.
6. **Change in allele frequency.** Over many generations, the frequency of advantageous alleles **increases** and that of disadvantageous alleles **decreases**.

Selection acts on **phenotypes**, but what changes over time is the frequency of **alleles** in the gene pool. Individuals do not evolve: populations do.

## Three types of selection

| Type | Selection pressure favours | Effect on the distribution | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stabilising | Phenotypes near the mean; extremes are selected against | Mean stays the same; range narrows (smaller standard deviation) | Human birth mass: very small and very large babies have lower survival |
| Directional | Phenotypes at one extreme, usually after the environment changes | Mean shifts in one direction over generations | Antibiotic and insecticide resistance |
| Disruptive | Both extremes; intermediates are selected against | Two peaks may form; can lead to two populations | Two food sources of very different sizes favour small-beaked and large-beaked birds |

**Stabilising selection** is the usual situation when the environment is constant: the population is already well adapted and selection keeps it so. **Directional selection** occurs when conditions change, for example when an antibiotic or insecticide is introduced. **Disruptive selection** is rarer; it can split a population and may contribute to speciation.

## Antibiotic resistance in bacteria

1. In a large population of bacteria, a random **mutation** may produce an allele that gives resistance to an antibiotic, for example by coding for an enzyme that breaks the antibiotic down, or by changing the protein the antibiotic binds to. The mutation is not caused by the antibiotic.
2. When the antibiotic is used, it is the **selection pressure**: susceptible bacteria are killed or stop dividing, while resistant ones survive.
3. The survivors divide by binary fission and pass the resistance allele to their offspring: **vertical gene transfer**. With no competition from susceptible bacteria, they multiply rapidly.
4. Resistance genes are often carried on **plasmids**, which can be passed between bacteria (even of different species) by **conjugation**: **horizontal gene transfer**. Resistance can therefore spread very quickly.

Overuse of antibiotics (for viral infections, in animal feed) and patients failing to finish a course increase the selection pressure and allow resistant bacteria to dominate.

:::example{title="Interpreting a growth curve with an antibiotic"}
A culture of bacteria was treated with an antibiotic at 4 hours.

| Time / hours | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Live bacteria / millions per cm³ | 2 | 8 | 32 | 3 | 1 | 6 | 30 |

**Describe:** numbers rise from 2 to 32 million per cm³ in 4 hours, fall sharply to 1 million by 8 hours after the antibiotic is added, then rise again to 30 million by 12 hours. **Explain:** most bacteria were susceptible and were killed; a few had a resistance allele (from an earlier mutation) and survived; they then multiplied, so by 12 hours the population consists mainly of resistant bacteria. This is **directional selection**.
:::

## Insecticide resistance in mosquitoes

*Anopheles* mosquitoes that transmit malaria have been controlled with pyrethroids on bed nets and with indoor residual spraying. Resistance alleles can arise by mutation, for example in the gene for the sodium channel protein that pyrethroids act on (so the insecticide binds less well), or in genes for enzymes that break the insecticide down faster. Where insecticides are used widely, susceptible mosquitoes die and resistant ones survive to breed, so the frequency of resistance alleles rises. Control programmes therefore monitor resistance and rotate insecticides with different modes of action.

:::worked-solution[Mosquito survival in a resistance test (illustrative data)]
A team exposed batches of 100 female mosquitoes from the same district to a standard insecticide dose in different years.

| Year of test | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mosquitoes alive after 24 hours | 4 | 19 | 46 |

1. Percentage mortality in year 1 $= \dfrac{100 - 4}{100} \times 100 = 96\%$.
2. Percentage mortality in year 5 $= \dfrac{100 - 46}{100} \times 100 = 54\%$.
3. Mortality fell by 42 percentage points, so the proportion of mosquitoes able to survive the dose rose 11.5 times (from 4% to 46%).
4. Interpretation: directional selection by the insecticide has increased the frequency of resistance alleles in the population.
:::

## The sickle cell allele and malaria

The sickle cell allele, $Hb^S$, codes for an abnormal β-globin. People who are $Hb^S Hb^S$ have **sickle cell anaemia**, a serious condition that, without treatment, reduces survival. You might expect the allele to be selected against everywhere, yet it is common in parts of Africa where *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria is common.

- $Hb^A Hb^A$: normal haemoglobin, but **more likely** to suffer severe malaria.
- $Hb^A Hb^S$ (carriers, sickle cell trait): usually healthy, and **partly protected** against severe malaria, because infected red cells sickle and are destroyed with the parasite.
- $Hb^S Hb^S$: sickle cell anaemia.

In malarial regions the **heterozygote has the advantage** over both homozygotes, so both alleles are kept in the population (a balanced polymorphism). Where there is no malaria, heterozygotes have no advantage and the $Hb^S$ allele is rarer.

:::example{title="Explaining a distribution map"}
A map shows that the $Hb^S$ allele is most frequent in regions where *P. falciparum* malaria has long been common, and rare elsewhere. **Explain:** in malarial regions, carriers survive malaria better than $Hb^A Hb^A$ people and have far fewer health problems than $Hb^S Hb^S$ people, so they leave more offspring; the $Hb^S$ allele is passed on and maintained at a relatively high frequency. Outside malarial regions this advantage is absent, and selection against $Hb^S Hb^S$ keeps the allele rare.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the antibiotic causes the mutation"}
Mutations are random and happen whether or not the antibiotic is present. The antibiotic only selects bacteria that already carry the resistance allele.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying bacteria 'become immune'"}
Immunity is a property of the host's immune system. Bacteria are **resistant**; they do not develop immunity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying individuals adapt or evolve"}
An individual's alleles do not change during its life. The frequency of alleles in the **population** changes over generations.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays and Cambridge Paper 4, an *explain* answer on resistance earns marks for: mutation (random), selection pressure named, resistant individuals survive, reproduce, pass on the allele, allele frequency increases over generations. Use the word **allele**, not "gene" or "resistance", when describing what is passed on.
:::

:::exam-tip
To *describe* a type of selection from distribution data, say what happens to the mean and to the spread, and name the type.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Overproduction, competition, genetic variation, differential survival and reproduction change allele frequencies over generations.
- Selection pressures are environmental factors such as predators, disease, climate, antibiotics and insecticides.
- Stabilising selection narrows the range around the mean; directional selection shifts the mean; disruptive selection favours both extremes.
- Antibiotic resistance spreads by mutation, selection, vertical transfer and horizontal transfer of plasmids.
- In malarial regions, carriers of $Hb^S$ have an advantage, so the allele is maintained.
:::
