## Introduction

The first practical in almost every A-Level biology course is the same: peel a thin layer from an onion, mount it in a drop of stain, and look. It sounds simple, but good slides take skill, and the practical papers of both boards expect you to make them, observe them carefully and record only what you can actually see.

This lesson covers temporary slide preparation, what plant and animal cells look like in light and electron micrographs, and how to compare them. You should know the formula for magnification and the use of a graticule (see the lesson on microscopes) and the basic cell structures from O Level.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Temporary preparation (wet mount)"}
A thin specimen mounted in a drop of water or stain on a slide under a coverslip, made for observation on the day and not preserved.
:::

:::definition{term="Stain"}
A coloured chemical that binds to particular cell components and increases contrast so that structures that are almost transparent become visible.
:::

:::definition{term="Photomicrograph"}
A photograph taken through a light microscope. An **electron micrograph** is the equivalent image from an electron microscope.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Common stains"}
Iodine solution stains starch blue-black and makes nuclei and cell walls of plant cells show up yellow-brown. Methylene blue stains nuclei (DNA) and cytoplasm of animal cells blue.
:::

## Making temporary preparations

### Onion epidermis stained with iodine

1. Cut a piece of onion bulb and peel off the thin inner epidermis from a fleshy scale leaf with forceps.
2. Place a small piece flat in a drop of iodine solution on a clean slide; avoid folding it, so the layer is one cell thick.
3. Lower a coverslip at an angle with a mounted needle so that air bubbles are not trapped.
4. Blot away excess stain with filter paper.
5. Observe with the low-power objective first, focus with the coarse focus, then move to high power and use only the fine focus.

The epidermis is used because it is a single layer of cells, so light passes through it and cells do not overlap. Onion bulb cells have no chloroplasts (they grow underground), so the stain is needed to see the nucleus and cell walls.

### Cheek cells stained with methylene blue

1. Gently scrape the inside of the cheek with a clean cotton bud.
2. Smear it on a slide, add a drop of methylene blue and add a coverslip.
3. Observe at high power: flat, irregular cells with a blue nucleus.
4. Put used cotton buds and slides into disinfectant immediately: body fluids may carry pathogens. Each student uses only their own cells.

:::keyfact{title="Safety"}
Iodine and methylene blue stain skin and clothing and are irritants, so wear eye protection and avoid contact. Handle the mounted needle and coverslips carefully: broken glass cuts. With these precautions the risk is low.
:::

## What you see with a light microscope

| Structure | Onion epidermal cell | Human cheek cell |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Regular, elongated, roughly rectangular, in rows | Irregular, flattened, rounded or polygonal |
| Cell wall | Present, clear outline between cells | Absent; only the cell surface membrane (seen as a thin edge) |
| Nucleus | Present, often near the wall, with a nucleolus at high power | Present, central, stains blue |
| Vacuole | Large permanent vacuole fills most of the cell (cytoplasm a thin layer) | No large permanent vacuole |
| Chloroplasts | None in bulb tissue (present in leaf mesophyll) | None |

Cells in a leaf of a pondweed such as *Elodea* show chloroplasts moving around the cell as cytoplasm streams, a sign that the cell is alive.

## Comparing plant and animal cells

Most differences seen in electron micrographs add to those seen with the light microscope.

| Feature | Typical plant cell | Typical animal cell |
|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Cellulose cell wall | Absent |
| Plasmodesmata | Channels through walls linking neighbouring cytoplasm | Absent |
| Chloroplasts | In photosynthetic cells | Absent |
| Vacuole | Large permanent vacuole bounded by the tonoplast | Small temporary vacuoles only (for example food vacuoles) |
| Centrioles | Absent in flowering plants | Present, near the nucleus |
| Shape | Usually regular, fixed by the wall | Variable |
| Carbohydrate store | Starch grains | Glycogen granules |

Both kinds of cell have a nucleus, cell surface membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi body and lysosome-like vesicles. Their detailed structure is in the ultrastructure lesson.

:::keyfact{title="Cells need ATP"}
Every cell uses ATP made in respiration as its immediate source of energy for energy-requiring processes such as active transport, protein synthesis, movement and cell division.
:::

## Estimating cell size from the field of view

When there is no graticule, you can estimate the size of cells by counting how many fit across the field of view of known diameter.

:::worked-solution[Mean length of onion epidermal cells]
1. The field of view at ×100 is 1.8 mm across, which is $1.8 \times 1000 = 1800\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. Twelve cells lie end to end across the diameter.
3. Mean cell length $= 1800 \div 12 = 150\ \mu\text{m}$.
4. A student draws one cell 60 mm long: magnification $= 60\,000\ \mu\text{m} \div 150\ \mu\text{m} = \times 400$.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a described photomicrograph"}
A photomicrograph shows cells from a maize leaf. Each cell has a clear, thick outline shared with its neighbours, many small green oval bodies arranged around the edge, and a nucleus pushed against one side. The centre of each cell is clear and unstained.

Interpretation: the thick shared outline is the cellulose cell wall; the green oval bodies are chloroplasts; the clear centre is the large permanent vacuole, which pushes the cytoplasm, chloroplasts and nucleus to the edge. These features show that the cells are plant cells from a photosynthetic tissue. No centrioles or plasmodesmata can be identified, because they are below the resolution of a light microscope; their absence from the image does not show that they are absent from the cell.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the stain kills or fixes the cell"}
The purpose of a stain in a wet mount is to **increase contrast** so transparent structures can be seen. Some stains do kill cells, but that is not why they are used.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing ribosomes and mitochondria as plant-only or animal-only"}
Both cell types contain mitochondria, ribosomes, ER and Golgi. Differences are the wall, chloroplasts, large permanent vacuole, plasmodesmata and centrioles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording what you know rather than what you see"}
In a light micrograph you cannot see ribosomes, the ER or the cell surface membrane of a plant cell pressed against its wall. Record only observable features.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Paper 4 and Cambridge Paper 3 may ask you to prepare and draw a temporary mount; ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1-2 ask you to identify cells and compare them.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Describe* the method in numbered steps with the reason for each key step. *Compare* in a table or with "whereas", giving a feature of both cells each time. *Explain* the use of a stain by linking it to contrast. *Suggest* why a feature is not visible: think about resolution and staining.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: temporary mounts, stains, plant and animal cells in light and electron micrographs, and the statement that cells use ATP from respiration. Drawing conventions are in the biological drawing lesson.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Temporary mounts use a thin, single layer of cells, a drop of stain and a coverslip lowered at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
- Stains (iodine for plant cells, methylene blue for animal cells) increase contrast; cheek-cell material goes straight into disinfectant.
- Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, plasmodesmata, often chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole; animal cells have centrioles and no wall.
- Both cell types share the nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes, ER and Golgi body.
- Cell size can be estimated from the field of view or measured with a calibrated graticule; all cells use ATP from respiration.
:::
