## Introduction

Cholera, tuberculosis and typhoid are caused by bacteria; HIV, measles and influenza are caused by viruses. Treating them is completely different: antibiotics can kill bacteria but have no effect on viruses. The reason lies in their structure. Bacteria are cells, built on a simpler plan than our own; viruses are not cells at all.

This lesson describes a typical prokaryotic cell, compares it with eukaryotic cells and describes the structure of viruses. You should know the eukaryotic organelles and the sizes of cell structures from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Prokaryote"}
An organism whose cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm. Bacteria (and archaea) are prokaryotes.
:::

:::definition{term="Eukaryote"}
An organism whose cells have a nucleus surrounded by a nuclear envelope and membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria.
:::

:::definition{term="Virus"}
A non-cellular infectious particle made of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) inside a protein coat (capsid), which can reproduce only inside a living host cell.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Sizes"}
Most bacteria are about 1–5 µm long. Most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, so a typical virus is ten to a hundred times smaller than a bacterium and can be seen only with an electron microscope.
:::

## Structure of a typical bacterium

Described from the outside in, a bacterium such as *Escherichia coli* has:

1. **Capsule** (slime layer) in some species: a layer of polysaccharide outside the wall that protects against drying out and against phagocytosis, and helps cells stick to surfaces.
2. **Cell wall** made of **peptidoglycan** (murein): polysaccharide chains cross-linked by short peptides. It gives shape and prevents the cell bursting by osmosis. It is not cellulose.
3. **Cell surface membrane**: a phospholipid bilayer controlling entry and exit; in bacteria it also carries respiratory enzymes, since there are no mitochondria.
4. **Cytoplasm** with **70S ribosomes** (smaller than the 80S ribosomes of eukaryotic cytoplasm) and food stores such as glycogen granules or lipid droplets.
5. **Circular DNA** lying free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid; it is not enclosed by a membrane and is not wrapped around histones as eukaryotic chromosomes are.
6. **Plasmids**: small extra loops of DNA that often carry genes such as antibiotic resistance and can be passed between bacteria.
7. **Pili** in some species: thin protein projections for attachment to surfaces or other bacteria.
8. **Flagella** in some species: long, rotating protein filaments for movement (they are not the same structure as eukaryotic flagella and contain no microtubules).

Capsules, pili and flagella are found in some bacteria but not all. Bacteria are always unicellular, though some form chains or clusters.

## Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells compared

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Typical size | About 1–5 µm | About 10–100 µm |
| Nucleus | None; circular DNA free in the cytoplasm | DNA in a nucleus with a double nuclear envelope |
| DNA | Circular, no histones; plasmids often present | Linear chromosomes wound around histones |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in the cytoplasm (70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | None (no mitochondria, ER, Golgi or chloroplasts) | Present |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (in almost all bacteria) | Cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, none in animals |
| Respiration | On the cell surface membrane | In mitochondria |
| Cell division | Binary fission (no mitosis) | Mitosis or meiosis |

## Structure of viruses

A virus particle consists of:

- a core of nucleic acid: **either** DNA **or** RNA, never both; it may be single- or double-stranded;
- a **capsid**: a protein coat made of many repeating protein units (capsomeres), often arranged with geometrical symmetry;
- in some viruses, an outer **envelope** taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves, carrying viral glycoproteins. HIV and influenza are enveloped; the tobacco mosaic virus, which damages tobacco crops, has no envelope.

A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no enzymes for respiration. To reproduce, it attaches to a host cell, its nucleic acid enters, and the host cell's ribosomes, ATP and enzymes are used to make new viral nucleic acid and proteins, which assemble into new particles.

:::example{title="Are viruses alive?"}
Arguments that viruses are **not alive**: they are not made of cells; they do not respire, feed, excrete or grow; outside a host they are inert particles that can even be crystallised; they cannot reproduce on their own.

Arguments that they **are alive**, or on the boundary: they contain genetic material in the form of nucleic acid that codes for proteins; they reproduce (inside host cells); they mutate and evolve by natural selection, as the rapid evolution of HIV shows.

A balanced conclusion: most biologists describe viruses as non-living or as being on the border between living and non-living, because they show only some of the characteristics of life and only inside other cells.
:::

:::worked-solution[How much smaller is a virus than a bacterium?]
1. An electron micrograph at ×15 000 shows a rod-shaped bacterium 33 mm long. Its actual length = $33\,000\ \mu\text{m} \div 15\,000 = 2.2\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. An HIV particle is about 120 nm across. Convert the bacterium length to nm: $2.2\ \mu\text{m} = 2200\ \text{nm}$.
3. Ratio $= 2200 \div 120 = 18.3$, so the bacterium is about 18 times longer than the virus is wide.
4. A drawing of the HIV particle 48 mm across would have a magnification of $48 \times 10^{6}\ \text{nm} \div 120\ \text{nm} = \times 400\,000$, which only an electron microscope can reach with useful resolution.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying bacteria have a nucleus or mitochondria"}
Bacteria have no membrane-bound organelles. Their DNA is in the cytoplasm, and respiration takes place on the cell surface membrane.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving bacteria a cellulose wall"}
Bacterial walls are peptidoglycan (murein). Cellulose is in plant walls and chitin in fungal walls.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a virus a cell or a bacterium"}
A virus is a particle of nucleic acid and protein with no cytoplasm or ribosomes. Antibiotics act on bacterial structures such as the wall and 70S ribosomes, which viruses do not have.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving a virus both DNA and RNA"}
Each virus contains one type of nucleic acid only: DNA or RNA.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bacteria are unicellular prokaryotes about 1–5 µm long with a peptidoglycan wall, circular DNA, plasmids and 70S ribosomes, and no membrane-bound organelles.
- Capsules, pili and flagella occur in some bacteria only.
- Eukaryotic cells are larger, with a nucleus, linear chromosomes with histones, 80S cytoplasmic ribosomes and membrane-bound organelles.
- A virus is a non-cellular particle of DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, sometimes with an envelope, that reproduces only inside a host cell.
- Viruses show some features of life (genes, reproduction in hosts, evolution) but not others (no cells, no metabolism).
:::
