## Introduction

A pancreatic cell making insulin, a sperm cell swimming towards an egg and a maize leaf cell fixing carbon dioxide look very different, yet they are built from the same set of organelles. The electron microscope revealed this **ultrastructure**: the fine detail inside cells that is below the resolution of the light microscope.

Examiners expect you to recognise each organelle from a description or electron micrograph, state its function and explain how its structure suits that function. You should already know the difference between magnification and resolution and the main plant and animal cell features from the previous lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ultrastructure"}
The detailed structure of a cell as seen with an electron microscope, including organelles too small to be resolved with a light microscope.
:::

:::definition{term="Organelle"}
A distinct structure inside a cell with a particular function; many are surrounded by one or two membranes.
:::

:::definition{term="Compartmentalisation"}
The separation of the cell into membrane-bound compartments so that different reactions, enzymes and conditions can be kept apart.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Ribosome sizes"}
Ribosomes in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells (free or on rough ER) are 80S, about 25 nm across. Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain smaller 70S ribosomes, like those of prokaryotes.
:::

## Organelles with a double membrane

![Labelled animal cell as seen with an electron microscope: the nucleus has a double envelope with pores and a dark nucleolus; rough ER with ribosomes lies near the nucleus, a Golgi stack and lysosome sit towards the cell surface membrane, mitochondria have folded cristae, and centrioles and free 80S ribosomes lie in the cytoplasm.](media:diagrams/animal-cell-ultrastructure.png "Figure: a generalised animal cell showing the organelles named in the lesson")

### Nucleus

The nucleus is surrounded by the **nuclear envelope**, two membranes with **nuclear pores** that let mRNA and ribosome subunits out and proteins such as enzymes in. It contains **chromatin** (DNA wound around histone proteins), which condenses into visible chromosomes during division. The dark **nucleolus** makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosome subunits. The nucleus controls the cell's activities through the genes it holds.

### Mitochondria

Mitochondria are rod-shaped, about 1–5 µm long, with a smooth outer membrane and a folded inner membrane forming **cristae**. The fluid **matrix** contains enzymes of the Krebs cycle, small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes. Aerobic respiration here makes most of the cell's ATP; the cristae give a large surface area for the electron transport chain and ATP synthase. Cells with high energy demands, such as muscle and sperm cells, have many mitochondria with densely packed cristae.

### Chloroplasts

Found in photosynthetic plant cells, chloroplasts are lens-shaped, about 3–10 µm long, with a double envelope. Inside, membranes called **thylakoids** are stacked into **grana**, which hold chlorophyll and carry out the light-dependent reactions. The fluid **stroma** contains enzymes of the Calvin cycle, starch grains, lipid droplets, small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes.

![Two double-membrane organelles side by side: a mitochondrion with a smooth outer membrane, an inner membrane folded into cristae and a matrix holding Krebs cycle enzymes, and a chloroplast whose thylakoids are stacked in grana inside a stroma where the Calvin cycle occurs.](media:diagrams/mitochondrion-chloroplast-structure.png "Figure: mitochondrion and chloroplast, each with a double membrane and its own 70S ribosomes and circular DNA")

## Organelles with a single membrane

| Organelle | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Cell surface membrane | Phospholipid bilayer with proteins, about 7–10 nm thick | Controls what enters and leaves; cell recognition and signalling |
| Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) | Flattened sacs (cisternae) continuous with the nuclear envelope, covered in ribosomes | Proteins made on its ribosomes enter the cisternae, fold and are transported |
| Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER) | Tubular sacs without ribosomes | Synthesises lipids and steroids; stores calcium ions in muscle |
| Golgi body (Golgi apparatus) | Stack of flattened, curved cisternae with vesicles budding off | Modifies proteins (for example adding sugars to make glycoproteins), sorts and packages them into vesicles; makes lysosomes |
| Lysosomes | Spherical vesicles about 0.1–1.0 µm, containing hydrolytic enzymes | Digest worn-out organelles, engulfed material and, in some cases, whole cells |
| Large permanent vacuole (plants) | Sac of cell sap bounded by the tonoplast | Keeps cells turgid; stores ions, sugars and pigments |
| Vesicles | Small membrane-bound sacs | Transport substances within the cell and to the membrane |

## Structures without a membrane

- **Ribosomes**: two subunits of rRNA and protein; the site of translation (protein synthesis). Free ribosomes make proteins used in the cytoplasm; those on RER make proteins for secretion, for membranes or for lysosomes.
- **Centrioles**: a pair of short hollow cylinders at right angles, each of nine triplets of **microtubules**, in animal cells; the centrosome around them organises the spindle in cell division.
- **Microtubules**: hollow tubes of the protein tubulin that form the cytoskeleton, the spindle fibres and the core of cilia and flagella; they act as tracks along which vesicles and organelles move.
- **Cilia**: hair-like extensions of the membrane with a core of microtubules (nine pairs around a central two). They beat to move fluid, such as mucus in the trachea. **Microvilli** are much smaller finger-like folds of the cell surface membrane (no microtubule core) that increase the surface area for absorption, for example in the small intestine and kidney tubules.

Plant cells also have a **cellulose cell wall** (fully permeable, gives strength and prevents bursting) and **plasmodesmata**, channels through the walls that link the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells.

## Organelles working together: secreting a protein

1. A gene for the protein (for example a digestive enzyme) is transcribed in the nucleus; the mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore.
2. Ribosomes on the rough ER translate the mRNA; the growing polypeptide passes into the cisternae of the RER, where it folds.
3. Transport vesicles bud off the RER and carry the protein to the Golgi body, fusing with it.
4. In the Golgi body the protein is modified (for example carbohydrate is added) and packaged into secretory vesicles.
5. The vesicles move along microtubules to the cell surface membrane and fuse with it, releasing the protein by exocytosis.
6. ATP from mitochondria powers vesicle movement and protein synthesis.

:::example{title="Reading an electron micrograph of a secretory cell"}
An electron micrograph of a cell from the pancreas shows: a large nucleus with a prominent nucleolus; many parallel membranes studded with dark granules filling much of the cytoplasm; several stacks of curved flattened sacs with small round vesicles at their edges; many rod-shaped organelles with folded inner membranes; and dense round vesicles clustered at one edge of the cell.

Interpretation: the membranes with granules are rough ER with ribosomes; the stacks are Golgi bodies; the rod-shaped organelles are mitochondria; the dense vesicles at one edge are secretory vesicles waiting to release their contents. A large nucleolus, much rough ER and many Golgi stacks indicate a cell making and secreting large amounts of protein, such as digestive enzymes.
:::

:::worked-solution[Sizes of organelles from an electron micrograph at $\times 40\,000$]
1. On a micrograph at ×40 000 a Golgi stack is 20 mm wide and a lysosome is 14 mm across.
2. Convert to nm: 20 mm = $20 \times 10^{6}\ \text{nm}$ and 14 mm = $14 \times 10^{6}\ \text{nm}$.
3. Golgi stack: $20 \times 10^{6} \div 40\,000 = 500\ \text{nm}$ (0.5 µm).
4. Lysosome: $14 \times 10^{6} \div 40\,000 = 350\ \text{nm}$ (0.35 µm), within the usual range of 0.1–1.0 µm.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the nucleolus a nucleus or an organelle with a membrane"}
The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus with no membrane; it makes rRNA and ribosome subunits.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing cilia with microvilli"}
Cilia contain microtubules and beat to move fluid; microvilli are tiny folds of membrane with no microtubules that increase surface area.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the Golgi body makes proteins"}
Proteins are made on ribosomes. The Golgi body modifies, sorts and packages them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving ribosomes the wrong size"}
Cytoplasmic ribosomes of eukaryotes are 80S; 70S ribosomes occur in prokaryotes and inside mitochondria and chloroplasts.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1, 2 and 3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask you to identify organelles in electron micrographs, give functions and trace the route of a secreted protein.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Identify* or *name*: one word is enough. *Describe* the structure: give membranes, shape and contents. *Explain* how a structure suits its function: link a feature (cristae, large surface area) to the process (more electron transport chains and ATP synthase, more ATP). *Suggest* the function of a cell from its organelles: many mitochondria suggest active transport or movement; much RER and Golgi suggest secretion.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require every organelle in this lesson, 80S and 70S ribosomes and the circular DNA of mitochondria and chloroplasts. The membrane structure itself is taught in the fluid mosaic lesson.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Double-membrane organelles: nucleus (envelope with pores), mitochondria (cristae, matrix) and chloroplasts (thylakoids, grana, stroma); the last two contain circular DNA and 70S ribosomes.
- Single-membrane organelles: RER (protein transport), SER (lipids), Golgi body (modify and package), lysosomes (hydrolytic enzymes), vacuole and vesicles.
- No membrane: 80S ribosomes (protein synthesis), centrioles and microtubules (spindle, cytoskeleton, cilia).
- Cilia beat to move fluid; microvilli increase surface area for absorption.
- A secreted protein travels nucleus (mRNA) → RER ribosomes → vesicle → Golgi → secretory vesicle → exocytosis, powered by ATP from mitochondria.
:::
