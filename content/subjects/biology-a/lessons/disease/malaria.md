## Introduction

Ask anyone who has lived in Kariba, Binga, Mutoko or the Save valley about malaria and they will know the rainy-season pattern: the mosquitoes come with the rains, and the clinics fill a few weeks later. In Harare or Bulawayo, high on the cool highveld, local transmission is rare. Malaria is still one of the most important infectious diseases in the world, and most deaths from it occur in sub-Saharan Africa, mainly among young children.

Malaria is a good disease for studying how biology, climate, money and behaviour interact. Its pathogen has two hosts, its vector breeds in water, and both the parasite and the mosquito have evolved resistance to the chemicals used against them. You should already know the structure of a eukaryotic cell and the role of red blood cells.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Malaria"}
An infectious disease of the blood and liver caused by protoctists of the genus *Plasmodium* and transmitted mainly by the bites of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself suffering from the disease. The female *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector of malaria.
:::

:::definition{term="Endemic"}
Describes a disease that is always present in a population or region, often at a fairly constant level.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The pathogens"}
Malaria in humans is caused mainly by four species of the protoctist *Plasmodium*: ***P. falciparum*** (the most dangerous and the commonest in Africa), ***P. vivax***, ***P. ovale*** and ***P. malariae***. *Plasmodium* is a single-celled eukaryote, not a bacterium or a virus.
:::

## Transmission

1. **By the vector.** Only **female** *Anopheles* mosquitoes transmit malaria, because only females feed on blood, which they need to produce eggs. When a female bites an infected person she takes up parasites with the blood. After the parasite has developed inside her for about one to two weeks, her next bites inject parasites with her saliva.
2. **By blood transfusion** or by sharing needles with an infected person, if blood is not screened.
3. **From mother to fetus** across the placenta, or during birth (congenital malaria).

Malaria is not passed by touching, coughing or sharing food.

## The life cycle in outline

| Stage | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mosquito salivary glands → human blood | An infected female injects parasites (sporozoites) when she feeds |
| 2 | Human liver cells | Parasites enter liver cells and multiply asexually; no symptoms yet |
| 3 | Human red blood cells | Released parasites (merozoites) invade red blood cells, multiply and burst them; the cycle repeats, causing waves of fever and anaemia |
| 4 | Human blood | Some parasites develop into sexual forms (gametocytes) |
| 5 | Mosquito gut | A feeding mosquito takes up gametocytes; gametes fuse and the parasite develops, then migrates to the salivary glands |

Because the parasite spends most of its time inside liver cells and red blood cells, it is hidden from antibodies for much of its life. It also changes the antigens on its surface. This is one reason why developing a malaria vaccine took so long.

## Global distribution and climate

Malaria occurs mainly in the **tropics and subtropics**: sub-Saharan Africa, South and South-East Asia, and parts of Central and South America and Oceania. Its distribution depends on the vector and the parasite:

- *Anopheles* larvae develop in **standing water**, so transmission rises in the **rainy season** and near swamps, dams, irrigation schemes and puddles.
- The parasite develops inside the mosquito only when it is **warm**. Below roughly 16-18 °C development is too slow to complete before the mosquito dies, so transmission falls at **high altitude**.
- In Zimbabwe, transmission is highest in the hot, low-lying **lowveld**, the **Zambezi valley** and areas near the eastern and northern borders, and is low on the cooler **highveld**. It is strongly **seasonal**, peaking during and after the rains.

People who grow up in areas of high transmission gradually develop partial immunity, so severe disease is commonest in young children, pregnant women and visitors from malaria-free areas. The sickle cell allele is common in regions where malaria has long been present, because heterozygotes are partly protected (this is developed in the epidemiology lesson).

:::example{title="Interpreting seasonal data"}
A hypothetical clinic in the lowveld recorded rainfall and malaria cases.

| Month | Jul | Sep | Nov | Jan | Mar | May |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall / mm | 0 | 5 | 70 | 160 | 90 | 10 |
| Malaria cases | 12 | 9 | 35 | 140 | 210 | 60 |

**Describe:** cases are low in the dry months (9-12), rise from November and peak at 210 in March. **Explain:** the rains create breeding sites, so the mosquito population grows; the peak in cases comes after the peak in rainfall (160 mm in January) because larvae must develop into adults, the parasite must develop inside the mosquito, and infected people take time to become ill.
:::

## Prevention and control

**Controlling the vector**

- **Insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs):** *Anopheles* mosquitoes mostly bite at night, so sleeping under a net protects the sleeper, and the insecticide kills mosquitoes that land on it, which also protects the rest of the community.
- **Indoor residual spraying (IRS):** walls and ceilings are sprayed with a long-lasting insecticide, which kills mosquitoes resting indoors after feeding. Zimbabwe has run spraying programmes in high-risk districts for many years.
- **Removing breeding sites:** draining standing water, clearing blocked drains, covering water containers, or treating water with larvicides; introducing fish that eat larvae into ponds.

**Protecting people and treating cases**

- **Rapid diagnostic tests** and microscopy confirm malaria quickly, so treatment is given only to people who have it.
- **Drugs:** artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) is the standard treatment for *P. falciparum*. Preventive drugs are given to some travellers and, in some programmes, to pregnant women and young children.
- **Vaccines:** two vaccines that target the stage injected by the mosquito are now recommended by the World Health Organization for children in areas of moderate to high transmission. They reduce cases and severe malaria but do not give complete protection, so nets, spraying and treatment are still needed.

:::worked-solution[Percentage decrease after a net campaign]
A hypothetical district recorded 3,260 malaria cases in the year before a bed-net campaign and 1,890 in the year after.
1. Decrease $= 3260 - 1890 = 1370$ cases.
2. Percentage decrease $= \dfrac{1370}{3260} \times 100 = 42.0\%$ (3 s.f.).
3. Always divide by the value **before** the change. A decrease alone does not prove the nets caused it: rainfall, spraying or testing could also have changed between the years.
:::

## Why malaria is difficult to control

- **Biological:** the parasite has two hosts and many stages; it hides inside cells and changes its surface antigens; resistance has evolved in the parasite to drugs such as chloroquine and in mosquitoes to insecticides; some mosquitoes have shifted to biting outdoors or earlier in the evening.
- **Social:** nets are not always used or are used for other purposes; people move between areas; some live far from clinics and delay treatment; war and displacement disrupt programmes.
- **Economic:** nets, insecticides, drugs and tests cost money and must be replaced; spraying needs trained teams and transport; illness keeps adults from work and children from school, deepening poverty.
- **Environmental:** rains, dams and irrigation create breeding sites; a warming climate may allow transmission at higher altitudes.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling the mosquito the pathogen"}
The pathogen is *Plasmodium*; the mosquito is the **vector**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Plasmodium a bacterium or virus"}
*Plasmodium* is a **protoctist** (a single-celled eukaryote). Antibiotics such as penicillin do not treat malaria.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all mosquitoes spread malaria"}
Only **female *Anopheles*** mosquitoes transmit human malaria.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Malaria is caused by the protoctist *Plasmodium* (*P. falciparum*, *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae*).
- It is transmitted by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes, and also by blood transfusion and from mother to fetus.
- The parasite multiplies in the liver and red blood cells; it is hidden in cells and varies its antigens.
- Transmission needs warmth and standing water, so it is highest in the tropics, lowlands and rainy seasons.
- Control uses treated nets, indoor spraying, removal of breeding sites, rapid testing, artemisinin combination drugs and vaccines, but resistance and cost make it difficult.
:::
