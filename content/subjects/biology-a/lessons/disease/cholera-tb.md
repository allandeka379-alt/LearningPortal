## Introduction

In August 2008 cholera broke out in Chitungwiza and spread to every province of Zimbabwe. By mid-2009 nearly 100,000 cases and more than 4,000 deaths had been reported, making it one of the largest cholera outbreaks recorded in Africa. It spread where piped water and sewage systems had broken down and where clinics were under strain. Tuberculosis (TB) works on a completely different timescale: it spreads quietly through the air, can lie dormant for years, and became far more common across southern Africa as HIV weakened people's immune systems.

These two bacterial diseases show the main ideas of the topic: how a pathogen is transmitted, how it causes symptoms, and why control depends as much on water pipes, housing, money and health services as on biology. You should already know the structure of a prokaryotic cell, osmosis, and the outline of how the gas exchange system works.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Infectious disease"}
A disease caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, directly or indirectly.
:::

:::definition{term="Pathogen"}
An organism or virus that causes disease, such as a bacterium, virus, fungus or protoctist.
:::

:::definition{term="Transmission"}
The way a pathogen passes from an infected host, or from the environment, to a new host.
:::

:::definition{term="Carrier"}
A person who is infected with a pathogen and can pass it on, but shows no symptoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Oral rehydration therapy"}
Treatment of dehydration by drinking a solution of glucose and salts (sodium and potassium), which allows water to be reabsorbed from the small intestine.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The two pathogens"}
**Cholera:** the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*, a comma-shaped Gram-negative rod with a flagellum.
**Tuberculosis:** the bacteria *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and, less often in humans, *Mycobacterium bovis* (from cattle). Both are rod-shaped with a thick, waxy cell wall.
:::

## Cholera

### Transmission

Cholera is a **water-borne** and **food-borne** disease spread by the **faecal-oral route**. Infected people pass huge numbers of bacteria in their faeces. If faeces reach drinking water, or contaminate food or hands, the bacteria are swallowed by the next host. Raw or undercooked shellfish and food washed in contaminated water are also sources. Many infected people are carriers with mild or no symptoms, so they spread the disease without knowing.

### How the bacterium causes diarrhoea

1. Most bacteria are killed by stomach acid, so a large dose is usually needed; survivors reach the **small intestine**.
2. They swim with their flagella through the mucus and attach to the epithelial cells lining the intestine, where they multiply.
3. They release **cholera toxin**, a protein. Part of the toxin binds to receptors on the epithelial cells and the active part enters the cells.
4. Inside, the toxin keeps an enzyme permanently switched on, so the cells continuously secrete **chloride ions** into the lumen of the intestine through chloride channels.
5. The lowered water potential in the lumen draws **water out of the cells and blood by osmosis**, and sodium ions follow.
6. The result is profuse, watery diarrhoea ("rice-water stools"), which can be several litres a day. Without treatment, severe dehydration and loss of ions can kill within hours.

### Treatment

**Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** is the main treatment and saves most patients. Oral rehydration salts (ORS) contain glucose and sodium (with potassium) dissolved in clean water. The toxin does not damage the **sodium-glucose co-transporter** proteins in the epithelial cells, so glucose and sodium ions are still absorbed together, lowering the water potential of the cells, and water follows by osmosis. Severely dehydrated patients need fluids given intravenously. Antibiotics shorten the illness and reduce the number of bacteria passed on, but rehydration is what prevents death.

### Prevention and control

- **Safe water:** piped, treated (chlorinated) water; boiling or treating water at home.
- **Sanitation:** sewage treatment, toilets and latrines kept away from water sources; repairing burst sewers.
- **Hygiene and food safety:** washing hands with soap; cooking food thoroughly; safe burial practices.
- **Oral cholera vaccines** give partial protection for a few years and are used in outbreaks and high-risk areas, alongside, not instead of, clean water.
- **Surveillance:** reporting cases quickly, setting up cholera treatment centres and tracing sources.

## Tuberculosis

### Transmission

*M. tuberculosis* spreads in **airborne droplets** when a person with active lung TB coughs, sneezes or talks. Transmission is most likely in **crowded, poorly ventilated** housing, prisons and hospitals. *M. bovis* infects cattle and passes to humans mainly in **unpasteurised milk** (and, less often, by inhaling droplets from cattle).

### How the disease develops

1. Inhaled bacteria reach the alveoli and are engulfed by macrophages, but they survive and multiply inside them.
2. In most people the immune system walls off the infection in small masses of cells; the bacteria remain alive but inactive (**latent TB**). The person has no symptoms and is not infectious.
3. If the immune system is weakened, by **HIV**, malnutrition, old age or some medicines, the bacteria multiply and destroy lung tissue (**active TB**). Symptoms include a persistent cough, coughing up blood, fever, night sweats and weight loss. Bacteria can spread to other organs.

HIV and TB reinforce each other: HIV destroys T-helper cells, so latent TB is far more likely to become active, and TB is a leading cause of death among people living with HIV.

### Prevention and control

- **BCG vaccine** (a live attenuated strain of *M. bovis*) is given to babies in Zimbabwe and many other countries. It protects children well against severe forms of TB but gives variable protection to adults.
- **Finding and treating cases** quickly stops transmission. Treatment uses a combination of antibiotics (such as isoniazid and rifampicin) for **at least six months**, because the bacteria grow slowly and some are hidden inside macrophages.
- **DOTS** (directly observed treatment, short course): a health worker or trained supporter watches the patient take every dose, so the course is completed.
- **Contact tracing**, better ventilation and less crowded housing, and HIV testing with antiretroviral therapy.
- **Pasteurising milk** and testing and removing infected cattle prevent *M. bovis* infection.

### Drug-resistant TB

If treatment is interrupted, bacteria with chance mutations giving resistance survive and multiply. **Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB)** is resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; extensively drug-resistant forms are resistant to more drugs. These need longer, more expensive and more toxic treatment, which is why completing every course matters.

:::worked-solution[Case fatality rate]
In a hypothetical district outbreak, 1,250 cholera cases and 45 deaths were recorded.
1. Case fatality rate $= \dfrac{\text{deaths}}{\text{cases}} \times 100$.
2. $= \dfrac{45}{1250} \times 100 = 3.6\%$.
3. After oral rehydration points were opened in every village, the next outbreak had 830 cases and 8 deaths: $\dfrac{8}{830} \times 100 = 0.96\%$ (2 s.f.).
4. Most cholera deaths are from dehydration, so fast access to ORS lowers the fatality rate even though people are still infected.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting treatment data"}
Two hypothetical clinics treated TB patients for six months.

| Clinic | Patients starting treatment | Patients completing 6 months | Completion / % |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-administered | 240 | 192 | 80.0 |
| DOTS | 260 | 247 | 95.0 |

**Describe and explain:** completion was 15 percentage points higher with DOTS (95.0% against 80.0%). Watching every dose helps patients keep going after they feel better, so fewer stop early. Fewer incomplete courses means fewer chances for resistant bacteria to survive and be selected, and fewer people remain infectious.
:::

## Why control is difficult

| Factor | Cholera | Tuberculosis |
|---|---|---|
| Biological | Carriers without symptoms; bacteria survive in water; vaccine protection short | Latent infection; slow-growing bacteria need long treatment; HIV co-infection; drug resistance |
| Social | Overcrowding, informal settlements, flooding, displacement after disasters or conflict | Crowded, poorly ventilated housing and prisons; stigma delays testing; patients stop treatment when they feel better |
| Economic | Cost of water treatment, pipes and sewers; maintenance when budgets fall | Cost of drugs (much higher for MDR-TB), staff for DOTS, lost earnings of patients |

## Global distribution

**Cholera** is **endemic** in parts of Africa, South Asia and South-East Asia, wherever safe water and sanitation are lacking, and causes **epidemics** after floods, earthquakes or wars that damage water systems. **TB** occurs worldwide, but most cases are in South and South-East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; the highest rates are in countries with many people living with HIV, poverty and crowding.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cholera bacteria 'damage the gut wall'"}
The intestinal lining stays largely intact. The **toxin** makes cells secrete chloride ions, and water follows by osmosis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying antibiotics are the main cure for cholera"}
Rehydration saves lives; antibiotics only shorten the illness.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying BCG prevents all TB"}
BCG protects young children against severe TB well, but protection in adults is variable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling TB a viral disease or HIV 'causing' TB"}
TB is bacterial. HIV makes infection and progression to active TB more likely; it does not cause TB itself.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cholera (*Vibrio cholerae*) spreads by the faecal-oral route in water and food; its toxin makes intestinal cells secrete chloride ions, and water follows by osmosis, causing severe diarrhoea.
- Oral rehydration works because sodium-glucose co-transport still operates; clean water, sanitation, hygiene and vaccines prevent cholera.
- TB (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *M. bovis*) spreads in airborne droplets and unpasteurised milk; it is often latent and becomes active when immunity is weakened, especially by HIV.
- TB is controlled by BCG, case finding, six months or more of combined antibiotics under DOTS, and pasteurisation; incomplete treatment selects drug-resistant TB.
- Control of both diseases depends on biological, social and economic factors such as carriers, crowding and the cost of water systems and drugs.
:::
