## Introduction

Before the 1940s, a scratch that became infected, a difficult childbirth or a bout of pneumonia could easily kill a healthy young adult. Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and developed as a medicine during the Second World War, changed that. Today antibiotics are used in every clinic in Zimbabwe, from treating pneumonia in children to the long courses that cure tuberculosis.

That success is under threat. Every use of an antibiotic selects bacteria that can survive it, and resistant bacteria are now found in hospitals and communities worldwide. This lesson explains how penicillin kills bacteria, why antibiotics are useless against viruses, how resistance arises and spreads, and what can be done to slow it. You should know the structure of a bacterial cell, osmosis and water potential, and the idea of enzyme inhibition.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antibiotic"}
A substance, originally produced by a microorganism, that kills bacteria or inhibits their growth without harming the cells of the person taking it.
:::

:::definition{term="Bactericidal and bacteriostatic"}
A **bactericidal** antibiotic kills bacteria (penicillin is an example); a **bacteriostatic** antibiotic stops them multiplying, so the immune system can remove them (tetracycline is an example).
:::

:::definition{term="Antibiotic resistance"}
The ability of a bacterium to survive and reproduce in the presence of an antibiotic that would normally kill it or stop its growth.
:::

## Why antibiotics can be selective

Antibiotics work because bacterial cells differ from human cells. They target structures or enzymes that human cells do not have, or have in a different form:

| Target in bacteria | Example antibiotic | Why human cells are not affected |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis of peptidoglycan cell walls | Penicillin | Human cells have no cell wall |
| 70S ribosomes (protein synthesis) | Tetracycline, streptomycin | Human cytoplasm has 80S ribosomes |
| Bacterial enzymes for DNA replication | Ciprofloxacin | The human enzymes are different |

## How penicillin works

1. A bacterial cell wall is made of **peptidoglycan**: long polysaccharide chains joined by short peptide **cross-links**, which make the wall strong.
2. As a bacterium grows, enzymes break some cross-links so new peptidoglycan can be inserted, while other enzymes (**transpeptidases**) form new cross-links.
3. Penicillin **inhibits the transpeptidases** that form the cross-links. The breaking enzymes keep working, so the wall becomes weaker as the cell grows.
4. The cytoplasm of a bacterium has a lower water potential than its surroundings, so water enters by **osmosis**. Normally the wall resists the pressure.
5. With a weakened wall, the cell swells and **bursts** (osmotic lysis).

Because penicillin acts on wall building, it affects only bacteria that are **growing and dividing**. It works best against bacteria whose peptidoglycan wall is exposed on the outside of the cell.

## Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

Viruses are not cells. They have **no cell wall**, **no ribosomes** and **no metabolism** of their own: they use the host cell's enzymes and ribosomes to replicate. There is nothing in a virus for an antibiotic to act on. Colds, influenza, measles and HIV cannot be treated with antibiotics. Doctors may still prescribe an antibiotic to someone with a viral infection if a **secondary bacterial infection** develops, for example bacterial pneumonia after influenza.

## How resistance arises and spreads

1. **Variation by mutation.** A random mutation may give a bacterium a resistance allele, for example a gene for an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic (**penicillinase**, also called β-lactamase, breaks down penicillin), an altered target protein, or a pump that removes the antibiotic. The antibiotic does **not cause** these mutations.
2. **Selection.** When the antibiotic is used, susceptible bacteria die; resistant bacteria survive.
3. **Reproduction.** Survivors divide by binary fission (some every 20-30 minutes in good conditions), passing the resistance allele to all their descendants. The resistant strain soon dominates.
4. **Horizontal gene transfer.** Resistance genes are often carried on **plasmids**, which can be passed between bacteria, even of different species, by conjugation. Resistance can therefore spread faster than by mutation alone.

This is **natural selection**, developed fully in the evolution lessons. The more often an antibiotic is used, the stronger the selection pressure.

## Consequences of resistance

- Infections that were once easily cured become difficult or impossible to treat; patients stay ill longer, more die, and treatment costs more.
- **MRSA** (meticillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) is resistant to penicillin-type antibiotics. It causes skin and wound infections and serious infections in hospital patients, especially after surgery.
- **Drug-resistant TB** (MDR-TB and more extensively resistant forms) needs long courses of less effective, more toxic and more expensive drugs.
- Routine procedures that depend on antibiotics to prevent infection, such as caesarean sections, joint replacements and cancer chemotherapy, become riskier.

:::worked-solution[Area of a clear zone]
Bacteria were spread evenly on an agar plate and a paper disc soaked in an antibiotic was placed in the centre. After incubation the clear zone around the disc had a diameter of 18 mm.
1. Radius $= 18 \div 2 = 9$ mm.
2. Area $= \pi r^2 = \pi \times 9^2 = 254$ mm² (3 s.f.).
3. The larger the clear zone, the more effective the antibiotic is against that bacterium (if the discs held the same amount and the antibiotics diffuse at similar rates).
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a sensitivity test"}
A sample from a patient's wound infection was spread on agar, and three antibiotic discs (each 6 mm across) were placed on it.

| Antibiotic disc | Diameter of clear zone / mm |
|---|---|
| Penicillin | 6 |
| Tetracycline | 24 |
| Streptomycin | 17 |

**Interpret:** there is no clear zone beyond the 6 mm penicillin disc, so the bacteria are resistant to penicillin (they may produce penicillinase). Tetracycline produced the largest clear zone, so it would be the best choice of the three for this infection. Testing first avoids using an antibiotic that will not work, which would also select for further resistance.
:::

## Reducing the impact of resistance

- **Prescribe only when needed:** not for viral infections; use tests to confirm a bacterial cause.
- **Use the right antibiotic:** narrow-spectrum drugs chosen after sensitivity testing; keep some antibiotics in reserve for resistant infections.
- **Complete the course** as prescribed, so that partly resistant bacteria are not left to multiply. Never share or save antibiotics.
- **Combination therapy** for TB and some other infections: a bacterium is very unlikely to resist several drugs at once.
- **Infection control in hospitals:** hand washing, cleaning, isolating patients with resistant infections and screening patients for MRSA.
- **Limit use in farming:** avoid using antibiotics routinely to promote growth or prevent disease in healthy livestock and poultry.
- **Vaccination** prevents infections, so fewer antibiotics are needed.
- **Develop new antibiotics** and other treatments, which is slow and expensive.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying bacteria become 'immune' to antibiotics"}
Immunity is a property of the host's immune system. Bacteria are **resistant**, because they carry resistance alleles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the antibiotic causes the mutation"}
Mutations arise randomly, before exposure. The antibiotic **selects** bacteria that already carry them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying penicillin 'breaks down the cell wall'"}
Penicillin stops new **cross-links** forming as the cell grows; the cell then bursts by osmosis.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Antibiotics target features of bacteria absent from human cells: cell walls, 70S ribosomes, bacterial enzymes.
- Penicillin inhibits the enzymes that form peptidoglycan cross-links, so growing bacteria burst by osmotic lysis.
- Viruses have no walls, ribosomes or metabolism of their own, so antibiotics cannot affect them.
- Resistance arises by random mutation, is selected by antibiotic use and spreads by reproduction and plasmid transfer; MRSA and MDR-TB are examples.
- Prescribing only when needed, completing courses, infection control, limiting farm use and new drugs reduce the impact.
:::
