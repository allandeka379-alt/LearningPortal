## Introduction

Southern Africa, Zimbabwe included, has been one of the regions most affected by HIV since the 1980s. Almost every family has been touched by it. Yet the story has changed dramatically: a positive HIV test is no longer a death sentence. People who take antiretroviral therapy every day can expect to live long, healthy lives, and when the virus is suppressed in their blood they do not pass it on through sex. Clinics across the country test pregnant women routinely, and most babies born to mothers living with HIV are now born free of the virus.

This lesson explains the biology behind those changes: what the virus is, which cells it attacks and why, and how each prevention method breaks a route of transmission. It is written factually and without judgement, because stigma itself is one of the obstacles to control. You should already know the structure of viruses in outline, the roles of lymphocytes, and transcription and translation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="HIV"}
Human immunodeficiency virus: a retrovirus that infects T-helper lymphocytes and gradually destroys the body's ability to mount an immune response.
:::

:::definition{term="AIDS"}
Acquired immune deficiency syndrome: the late stage of HIV infection, in which the number of T-helper cells is so low that opportunistic infections and some cancers develop.
:::

:::definition{term="Retrovirus"}
A virus with RNA as its genetic material that uses the enzyme reverse transcriptase to make a DNA copy of its genome inside the host cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunistic infection"}
An infection by a pathogen that rarely causes serious disease in a person with a healthy immune system but does so when immunity is weakened.
:::

:::definition{term="Viral load"}
The number of copies of the virus's RNA in a given volume of blood, used to measure how well treatment is working.
:::

## Structure of HIV

From the inside out, a virus particle contains:

1. **Two identical single strands of RNA**, the genome.
2. **Enzymes**: **reverse transcriptase** (makes DNA from the RNA), **integrase** (inserts that DNA into the host's chromosome) and **protease** (cuts new viral proteins into their working forms).
3. A **capsid** of protein surrounding the RNA and enzymes.
4. A **lipid envelope** taken from the membrane of the last host cell, studded with viral **glycoproteins** (notably gp120) that bind to receptors on host cells.

## How HIV infects T-helper cells

1. The glycoprotein gp120 on the envelope binds to the **CD4 receptor** on the surface of a T-helper cell (and to a second co-receptor protein).
2. The envelope fuses with the cell surface membrane and the capsid enters the cytoplasm, releasing RNA and enzymes.
3. **Reverse transcriptase** uses the RNA as a template to make single-stranded and then double-stranded DNA.
4. **Integrase** inserts this DNA into a chromosome of the host cell, where it is called a **provirus**. It stays there for the life of the cell.
5. When the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed. Host ribosomes translate viral proteins, and new viral RNA is made.
6. New particles assemble and **bud** from the cell surface, taking membrane as their envelope. Large numbers of infected T-helper cells die or are destroyed by the immune system.

Because the viral DNA hides in long-lived cells as a provirus, current drugs can suppress HIV but cannot remove it from the body.

## Progression to AIDS

- **Early (acute) infection:** a few weeks after infection, some people have a short flu-like illness. Viral load is very high, so the person is highly infectious, and antibodies appear within weeks, which is why tests may be negative at first (the window period).
- **Chronic stage:** often lasting many years without treatment, with few or no symptoms. The virus keeps replicating and the **CD4 count** (number of T-helper cells per mm³ of blood) falls steadily. A healthy adult typically has about 500-1,500 cells per mm³.
- **AIDS:** diagnosed when the CD4 count falls below about 200 cells per mm³, or when certain opportunistic infections appear. Without T-helper cells, B-cells and T-killer cells are not activated properly. Typical illnesses include **TB**, pneumonia, oral thrush (a fungal infection), diarrhoea, severe weight loss and Kaposi's sarcoma (a cancer).

:::worked-solution[Rate of fall of the CD4 count]
Without treatment, a person's CD4 count fell from 950 to 190 cells per mm³ over 8 years.
1. Total fall $= 950 - 190 = 760$ cells per mm³.
2. Mean rate of fall $= 760 \div 8 = 95$ cells per mm³ per year.
3. Percentage fall $= \dfrac{760}{950} \times 100 = 80\%$.
4. At 190 cells per mm³ the count is below about 200, the level at which AIDS is diagnosed.
:::

## Transmission

HIV is carried in blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk. It is **not** spread by touching, hugging, sharing cups or toilets, or by mosquitoes. The main routes are:

- **Unprotected sexual intercourse** with a person who has a detectable viral load (the main route in southern Africa).
- **Infected blood:** shared needles and syringes, unsterilised instruments, and transfusion of unscreened blood.
- **Mother to child** during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding.

## Prevention and control

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| Testing and counselling | People who know their status can start treatment and protect partners; testing is the entry point to all other services |
| Condoms (male and female) | A physical barrier that stops body fluids passing between partners |
| Antiretroviral therapy (ART) | Combinations of drugs that inhibit reverse transcriptase, integrase or protease, so the virus cannot replicate; viral load falls and the CD4 count recovers |
| Treatment as prevention | A person whose viral load stays undetectable on ART does not transmit HIV sexually |
| Prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) | Testing in pregnancy and ART for the mother (and preventive drugs for the baby) greatly reduce transmission in pregnancy, birth and breastfeeding |
| Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) | HIV-negative people at high risk take antiretroviral drugs regularly to prevent infection; post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is a short course started soon after a possible exposure |
| Voluntary medical male circumcision | Removes the foreskin, whose inner surface has many cells HIV can infect; it lowers the risk of female-to-male transmission but is only partly protective, so condoms are still needed |
| Blood and needle safety | Screening donated blood; sterile, single-use needles |
| Education and reducing stigma | People who fear discrimination are less likely to test, disclose or take treatment |

:::example{title="Interpreting treatment data"}
A hypothetical patient started ART with a low CD4 count.

| Months after starting ART | 0 | 3 | 6 | 12 | 24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CD4 count / cells per mm³ | 180 | 260 | 330 | 420 | 560 |
| Viral load / copies per cm³ | 85 000 | 900 | 200 | undetectable | undetectable |

**Describe and explain:** the viral load fell sharply within 3 months (85,000 to 900 copies per cm³) and was undetectable by 12 months, because the drugs block replication. As fewer T-helper cells were infected and destroyed, the CD4 count rose steadily, from 180 to 560 cells per mm³. From 12 months the patient would not transmit HIV sexually and has a much lower risk of opportunistic infections.
:::

## Why control is difficult

- **Biological:** the virus integrates into host DNA as a provirus, so it cannot be cured; reverse transcriptase makes many copying errors, so HIV mutates quickly, which allows **drug resistance** and has defeated attempts to make a vaccine; there is a window period before tests turn positive; people may feel well for years without knowing they are infected.
- **Social:** stigma and discrimination discourage testing and disclosure; gender inequality and age gaps between partners can make it hard to negotiate condom use; migration for work separates families.
- **Economic:** ART is lifelong, so programmes need a reliable supply of drugs, tests and staff; illness reduces household income and leaves orphans; much funding has come from international donors.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: the virus, its effect on T-helper cells, transmission, global distribution, prevention and control. Cambridge emphasises the pathogen and "biological, social and economic factors"; ZIMSEC places HIV within human health and disease. Drug names are not required.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying people die of HIV itself"}
People die of opportunistic infections, such as TB, that develop when the immune system has failed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying HIV is a bacterium or can be treated with antibiotics"}
HIV is a virus. Antibiotics may treat bacterial opportunistic infections, but only antiretroviral drugs act on HIV.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ART cures HIV"}
ART suppresses replication but cannot remove the provirus, so it is taken for life.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
HIV appears on ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. In *explain* questions link **T-helper cells destroyed → B-cells and T-killer cells not activated → opportunistic infections**. Use "T-helper cell" or "CD4 cell", never just "white blood cell".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *suggest* how a prevention method works, name the route it blocks (sexual, blood or mother-to-child) and the biological reason (barrier, viral load reduced, fewer target cells).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- HIV is a retrovirus: RNA, reverse transcriptase, integrase, capsid and a lipid envelope with glycoproteins.
- It binds CD4 receptors on T-helper cells, integrates as a provirus and destroys these cells, so immunity fails and AIDS develops.
- It spreads through unprotected sex, infected blood and from mother to child, not through casual contact.
- ART suppresses the virus, restores T-helper cells and prevents transmission; condoms, testing, PMTCT, PrEP and circumcision add protection.
- High mutation rate, lifelong treatment, stigma and cost make control difficult.
:::
