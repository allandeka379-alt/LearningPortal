## Introduction

Why is sickle cell anaemia common in people whose families come from West and Central Africa but rare in northern Europe? Why did Ebola cause a huge epidemic in West Africa in 2014-2016 but not spread widely elsewhere? Why is heart disease, once called a disease of rich countries, now a leading cause of death in many lower-income countries too? **Epidemiology**, the study of the patterns, causes and effects of disease in populations, answers questions like these.

Health workers in Zimbabwe use epidemiological data every week: clinics report new cases of notifiable diseases so that outbreaks of cholera, measles or typhoid can be spotted early. In the examination you will be given tables, graphs and maps described in words, and asked to calculate rates, describe patterns and judge how strong the evidence is. You should already know about cholera, malaria, TB and HIV from the earlier lessons, and the structure of haemoglobin.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Epidemiology"}
The study of the occurrence, distribution and causes of disease in populations, used to plan prevention and control.
:::

:::definition{term="Incidence"}
The number of **new** cases of a disease in a population during a stated period, usually given per 1,000 or per 100,000 people per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Prevalence"}
The **total** number of people with a disease in a population at a given time (new and existing cases), usually given per 1,000 or per 100,000 people.
:::

:::definition{term="Mortality rate"}
The number of deaths from a disease in a population during a stated period, usually per 100,000 people per year.
:::

:::definition{term="Endemic, epidemic and pandemic"}
A disease is **endemic** when it is always present in a population at a fairly steady level; an **epidemic** is a sudden rise in cases well above the expected level in one area; a **pandemic** is an epidemic that spreads across several countries or continents.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Calculating rates"}
$$
\text{rate per } 100\,000 = \frac{\text{number of cases (or deaths)}}{\text{population}} \times 100\,000
$$
Rates, not raw numbers, allow fair comparison between populations of different sizes.
:::

## Incidence or prevalence?

Incidence measures **risk**: how quickly new cases are appearing. Prevalence measures **burden**: how many people are living with the disease and need care. A disease that lasts a long time (such as HIV on treatment) can have a low incidence but a high prevalence. A short illness such as cholera has a prevalence close to its incidence, because people recover or die quickly. When effective treatment keeps people alive longer, prevalence can **rise** even while incidence falls.

:::worked-solution[Incidence, prevalence and mortality]
A hypothetical town of 48,000 people recorded, in one year, 156 new cases of a chronic disease; 1,032 people already had the disease at the start of the year, and 18 people died of it.
1. Incidence $= \dfrac{156}{48\,000} \times 100\,000 = 325$ new cases per 100,000 per year.
2. Prevalence (at the end of the year, ignoring deaths and recoveries) $= \dfrac{156 + 1032}{48\,000} \times 100\,000 = 2475$ per 100,000.
3. Mortality rate $= \dfrac{18}{48\,000} \times 100\,000 = 37.5$ per 100,000 per year.
:::

## Evaluating epidemiological evidence

When you interpret a table or map, ask:

- **Rates or numbers?** Large populations have more cases even if the risk is the same.
- **Is the sample big enough and representative?** Data from one hospital may not represent a whole country.
- **How were cases counted?** Better testing or reporting can make a disease look as if it is increasing. Where few people reach clinics, cases are under-reported.
- **Correlation is not causation.** Two variables can rise together because both depend on a third factor (for example, age, income or smoking).
- **Is there a plausible biological mechanism and a consistent pattern** across many studies and places? Strong evidence combines both.

## Ebola virus disease

- **Pathogen:** Ebola virus, an RNA virus. Fruit bats are thought to be the natural reservoir; people can be infected by handling infected wild animals.
- **Transmission between people:** direct contact with the **blood and body fluids** (vomit, diarrhoea, sweat, semen) of a sick person or a dead body, or with contaminated bedding and needles. It is not spread through the air like influenza. Health workers and family caregivers are at high risk, and traditional burial practices that involve washing the body spread the virus.
- **Effects:** fever, weakness, vomiting and diarrhoea, and in some cases internal and external bleeding; many infected people die.
- **Distribution:** outbreaks have occurred in **Central Africa** (notably the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where the virus was first identified in 1976) and **West Africa**, where the 2014-2016 epidemic in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone was the largest so far, with more than 11,000 deaths.
- **Why there:** contact with wildlife reservoirs; health systems with too few staff, gloves and isolation units; dense, mobile populations in towns and across borders; mistrust delaying reporting.
- **Control:** isolating patients in treatment centres, **contact tracing** (finding and monitoring everyone a patient has touched, for 21 days), protective equipment for health workers, safe and dignified burials, community education, and a vaccine used for contacts and front-line workers in outbreaks.

## Coronary heart disease

- **The disease:** fatty deposits (atheroma) build up in the walls of the coronary arteries (**atherosclerosis**), narrowing them. Blood flow to the heart muscle is reduced, causing chest pain (angina); if a clot blocks an artery, part of the heart muscle dies (a heart attack, or myocardial infarction).
- **Risk factors:** a diet high in saturated fat and salt, high blood cholesterol, **smoking**, physical **inactivity**, obesity, **hypertension** (high blood pressure), diabetes, heavy alcohol use, increasing age, male sex and family history.
- **Distribution:** historically commonest in high-income countries, and still a leading cause of death worldwide. It is rising in many low- and middle-income countries, including in southern Africa, as more people live in towns, eat processed food, are less physically active and live longer. Unlike infectious diseases, it is a **non-communicable** disease: its distribution follows lifestyle, age and genes, not a pathogen.

## Sickle cell anaemia

- **The disease:** a mutation in the gene for the β-globin chain of haemoglobin (a single base substitution) gives the allele $Hb^S$. People with two copies ($Hb^S Hb^S$) have sickle cell anaemia: at low oxygen concentrations their haemoglobin forms long fibres, red blood cells become sickle-shaped, block capillaries and are destroyed early, causing pain, anaemia and organ damage.
- **Distribution:** the $Hb^S$ allele is common in sub-Saharan Africa (especially West and Central Africa), parts of the Mediterranean, the Middle East and India. These regions **overlap closely with areas where *P. falciparum* malaria has long been common**. In Zimbabwe the allele occurs but is less common than in West Africa.
- **Explanation: heterozygote advantage.** Heterozygotes ($Hb^A Hb^S$, sickle cell trait) are usually healthy but are partly protected against severe malaria, because infected red cells sickle and are removed. Where malaria is common, heterozygotes survive and reproduce more than either homozygote: $Hb^A Hb^A$ people more often die of malaria, and $Hb^S Hb^S$ people of sickle cell anaemia. Both alleles are kept in the population. Where there is no malaria, the $Hb^S$ allele gives no advantage and is rare.

:::example{title="Interpreting a distribution table"}
The table shows illustrative values of the kind found in surveys.

| Region (illustrative) | Malaria historically | Adults with sickle cell trait / % |
|---|---|---|
| A | High transmission all year | 20 |
| B | Seasonal transmission | 9 |

**Describe and explain:** the trait is commonest (20%) where malaria has been intense and rarest (1%) where it is absent. This fits heterozygote advantage: selection by malaria favours heterozygotes. **Evaluate:** this is a correlation; it is supported by a clear mechanism, but other factors such as migration history also affect allele frequencies.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing incidence and prevalence"}
Incidence counts only **new** cases in a period; prevalence counts **everyone** who has the disease at a time.
:::

:::mistake{title="Comparing raw numbers between countries"}
Convert to rates per 1,000 or 100,000 first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying Ebola spreads like flu"}
Ebola spreads through direct contact with body fluids, not through the air over long distances.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying sickle cell trait causes malaria resistance in everyone"}
Heterozygotes are **partly** protected against **severe** malaria; homozygotes for $Hb^S$ have a serious disease.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Incidence counts new cases in a period; prevalence counts all cases at a time; mortality counts deaths; all are compared as rates.
- Endemic diseases are always present; epidemics are sudden local rises; pandemics cross continents.
- Ebola spreads through body fluids in Central and West Africa and is controlled by isolation, contact tracing, safe burials and vaccination.
- Coronary heart disease is non-communicable; its risk factors (diet, smoking, inactivity, hypertension) explain its rising global spread.
- The sickle cell allele is common where malaria has long been present because heterozygotes are partly protected.
:::
