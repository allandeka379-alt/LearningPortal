## Introduction

Of the hundreds of millions of sperm released at ejaculation, only a few hundred reach the egg in the oviduct, and normally only one enters it. Within a day the fertilised egg begins to divide; about a week later it burrows into the wall of the uterus, and its outer cells start to build an organ that will sustain it for nine months: the placenta. For the whole of pregnancy, every molecule of oxygen and glucose the fetus uses, and every molecule of carbon dioxide and urea it produces, crosses this organ, yet the blood of mother and fetus never mixes.

Antenatal clinics in Zimbabwe advise pregnant women not to drink alcohol or smoke, and test them for HIV and syphilis, because some harmful substances and pathogens can cross the placenta. This lesson explains the biology behind that advice. You should know the structure of sperm and the secondary oocyte, oogenesis, the menstrual cycle, diffusion and active transport, and the oxygen dissociation curve.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fertilisation"}
The fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote.
:::

:::definition{term="Capacitation"}
Changes to a sperm in the female reproductive tract, lasting several hours, in which cholesterol and glycoproteins are removed from the membrane over the acrosome, making it more fluid and able to undergo the acrosome reaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Acrosome reaction"}
The release of hydrolytic enzymes from the acrosome of a sperm by exocytosis when it binds to the zona pellucida, allowing the sperm to digest a path to the oocyte.
:::

:::definition{term="Cortical reaction"}
The release of the contents of cortical granules from the oocyte by exocytosis when a sperm enters, which hardens the zona pellucida and prevents any more sperm entering (prevents polyspermy).
:::

:::definition{term="Implantation"}
The embedding of the early embryo (blastocyst) in the endometrium, about a week after fertilisation.
:::

## The events of fertilisation

Fertilisation normally happens in the **oviduct** (fallopian tube), within about a day of ovulation. The secondary oocyte is surrounded by a layer of follicle cells (the **corona radiata**) and a glycoprotein layer (the **zona pellucida**).

1. **Capacitation.** While swimming through the uterus and oviduct, sperm membranes are altered as cholesterol and glycoproteins are removed. Only capacitated sperm can fertilise.
2. **Passing the corona radiata.** Sperm push between the follicle cells, helped by an enzyme on the sperm head that breaks down the material holding them together.
3. **Acrosome reaction.** A sperm binds to receptor glycoproteins in the zona pellucida. Its acrosome releases **hydrolytic enzymes** (proteases) by exocytosis, digesting a path through the zona pellucida as the sperm's tail drives it forward.
4. **Fusion of membranes.** The sperm's head reaches the oocyte's cell surface membrane and the two membranes **fuse**; the sperm nucleus enters the cytoplasm.
5. **Cortical reaction.** Entry of the sperm triggers a rise in calcium ions in the oocyte, causing **cortical granules** just under the membrane to release their contents by exocytosis. These enzymes change the zona pellucida so it **hardens** and its sperm receptors are destroyed, so no further sperm can enter (**prevents polyspermy**, which would produce a zygote with too many chromosomes).
6. **Completion of meiosis II.** The secondary oocyte completes meiosis II, forming the ovum nucleus and a **second polar body**.
7. **Fusion of nuclei.** The haploid sperm and ovum nuclei fuse, restoring the **diploid** number: the **zygote**.

## Implantation in outline

The zygote divides by mitosis as it is moved along the oviduct by cilia and muscle contractions. After about five days it is a hollow ball of cells, the **blastocyst**: an inner cell mass that will become the embryo and an outer layer, the **trophoblast**. About **6-7 days after fertilisation** the blastocyst sinks into the thick, progesterone-maintained endometrium. Trophoblast cells secrete enzymes that digest endometrial cells, absorb nutrients from them, and grow finger-like projections, the beginnings of the **chorionic villi**. They also secrete **hCG**, which keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone so the endometrium is not shed.

## Structure of the placenta

The placenta is a disc-shaped organ formed from both **fetal tissue** (the chorion, from the trophoblast) and **maternal tissue** (the endometrium). It is connected to the fetus by the **umbilical cord**.

- **Chorionic villi:** highly branched, finger-like projections of fetal tissue containing fetal capillaries. They give a very large surface area.
- **Maternal blood spaces (lacunae):** spaces in the endometrium into which maternal arteries pour blood; the villi are bathed in maternal blood, which drains back through maternal veins.
- **The barrier:** only a few thin layers of cells separate fetal blood in the villus capillaries from maternal blood, so the diffusion distance is short. **Maternal and fetal blood do not mix**: this protects the fetus from the mother's high blood pressure and keeps the different blood groups apart.
- **Umbilical cord:** contains **two umbilical arteries**, carrying deoxygenated blood with carbon dioxide and urea **from the fetus to the placenta**, and **one umbilical vein**, carrying oxygenated blood with nutrients **from the placenta to the fetus**.

## Functions of the placenta

**1. Exchange**

| Substance | Direction | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Mother → fetus | Diffusion, helped by fetal haemoglobin's higher affinity for oxygen |
| Carbon dioxide | Fetus → mother | Diffusion |
| Glucose | Mother → fetus | Facilitated diffusion |
| Amino acids, many ions (e.g. calcium, iron) | Mother → fetus | Active transport |
| Water | Both | Osmosis |
| Urea | Fetus → mother | Diffusion |

Efficient exchange is maintained by the large surface area of the villi, the short diffusion distance, and the steep concentration gradients kept up by continuous flow of both bloods.

**2. Fetal haemoglobin.** The fetus has a different kind of haemoglobin with a **higher affinity for oxygen** than adult haemoglobin: its dissociation curve lies to the **left**. In the placenta, where the partial pressure of oxygen is low, adult haemoglobin releases oxygen and fetal haemoglobin loads it.

:::example{title="Interpreting dissociation data"}
Illustrative saturation values:

| Partial pressure of oxygen / kPa | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adult haemoglobin saturation / % | 20 | 55 | 80 | 90 | 95 |
| Fetal haemoglobin saturation / % | 40 | 75 | 90 | 95 | 97 |

**Describe:** at every partial pressure fetal haemoglobin is more saturated, the difference being largest at low partial pressures (75% against 55% at 4 kPa). **Explain:** at the low partial pressure in the placenta, maternal haemoglobin unloads oxygen while fetal haemoglobin, with its higher affinity, is still well loaded, so oxygen passes from mother to fetus.
:::

**3. Transfer of antibodies.** IgG antibodies are taken up from maternal blood by **endocytosis** (pinocytosis) and released into fetal blood, giving the baby **natural passive immunity** for its first months.

**4. A partial barrier.** The placenta stops maternal blood cells, most bacteria and many large molecules reaching the fetus, but it is **not a complete barrier**. Small or lipid-soluble substances and some pathogens cross:

- **Alcohol** crosses freely and can cause fetal alcohol syndrome.
- **Nicotine** and **carbon monoxide** from smoking cross, reducing oxygen supply and growth.
- Many **drugs**, including some medicines, cross; doctors check which are safe in pregnancy.
- **Pathogens**: rubella virus, the syphilis bacterium and *Plasmodium* can cross; **HIV** can be transmitted during pregnancy, and more often during birth and breastfeeding.

**5. Hormone production.**

- **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotrophin), from the trophoblast and then the placenta, maintains the corpus luteum for the first weeks of pregnancy; it is the hormone detected by pregnancy tests.
- From about the third month the placenta itself secretes enough **progesterone** and **oestrogen** to maintain the endometrium and pregnancy, and the corpus luteum is no longer needed. Progesterone also inhibits contractions of the uterine muscle and, with oestrogen, inhibits FSH and LH, so no further ovulation occurs during pregnancy. Oestrogen also stimulates growth of the uterus and mammary glands.

:::worked-solution[How fast does hCG rise?]
In early pregnancy the concentration of hCG in blood roughly doubles about every two days. A woman has 50 units of hCG eight days after implantation began.
1. Number of doublings in the next 8 days $= 8 \div 2 = 4$.
2. Concentration $= 50 \times 2^4 = 800$ units.
3. Because the concentration rises so quickly, a test that is negative very early may be positive a few days later.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying maternal and fetal blood mix in the placenta"}
They are always separated by the cells of the villi; substances cross by diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport and endocytosis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the umbilical artery carries oxygenated blood"}
Arteries carry blood **away from the fetal heart**: the umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood to the placenta; the umbilical vein carries oxygenated blood to the fetus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the placenta is a complete barrier"}
Alcohol, nicotine, carbon monoxide, many drugs and some pathogens cross it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Sperm are capacitated in the female tract; the acrosome reaction digests a path through the zona pellucida; membranes fuse and the sperm nucleus enters.
- The cortical reaction hardens the zona pellucida, preventing polyspermy; meiosis II completes and the nuclei fuse to form the zygote.
- The blastocyst implants about 6-7 days after fertilisation; the trophoblast forms chorionic villi and secretes hCG.
- Chorionic villi in maternal blood spaces give a large surface area and short diffusion distance; two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein connect the fetus.
- The placenta exchanges gases, nutrients and wastes, transfers IgG antibodies, partly protects the fetus (but alcohol, nicotine, drugs and some pathogens cross) and secretes hCG, progesterone and oestrogen.
:::
