## Introduction

About once a month, from puberty to the menopause, the female reproductive system prepares for a possible pregnancy: an egg matures and is released, and the lining of the uterus thickens to receive an embryo. If no pregnancy occurs, the lining breaks down and is lost in menstruation, and the cycle begins again. Four hormones, from two glands, coordinate these changes so precisely that ovulation and a receptive uterus line up in time.

Understanding the cycle is the basis for understanding how hormonal contraceptives, fertility treatments and hormone replacement therapy work, and for explaining common experiences such as premenstrual symptoms and the changes of the menopause. You should already know the structure of the ovary and oogenesis, and the principles of negative feedback.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Menstrual cycle"}
The monthly series of changes in the ovary and the lining of the uterus (endometrium), controlled by hormones, lasting on average about 28 days but varying between about 21 and 35 days in healthy women.
:::

:::definition{term="Ovulation"}
The release of a secondary oocyte from a mature (Graafian) follicle at the surface of the ovary, triggered by a surge of LH.
:::

:::definition{term="Endometrium"}
The inner lining of the uterus, which thickens and develops many blood vessels and glands during each cycle and is shed during menstruation.
:::

:::definition{term="Negative and positive feedback"}
In **negative** feedback a rise in a hormone reduces the secretion of the hormone that stimulated it; in **positive** feedback a rise increases it further, amplifying the change.
:::

## The four hormones

| Hormone | Made by | Main effects |
|---|---|---|
| FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) | Anterior pituitary | Stimulates growth of follicles; stimulates follicle cells to secrete oestrogen |
| LH (luteinising hormone) | Anterior pituitary | Surge triggers completion of meiosis I and ovulation; converts the follicle into the corpus luteum and stimulates it to secrete progesterone |
| Oestrogen | Follicle cells (and later the corpus luteum) | Repair and thickening of the endometrium; at low levels inhibits FSH and LH; at high levels stimulates the LH surge |
| Progesterone | Corpus luteum | Maintains the thick, glandular, well-vascularised endometrium; inhibits FSH and LH |

The **hypothalamus** controls the pituitary through **GnRH**, so feedback can act on the hypothalamus, the pituitary or both.

## The cycle, step by step

**Days 1-5: menstruation.** Progesterone and oestrogen are low, so the endometrium breaks down and is lost with blood. Low ovarian hormones allow the pituitary to secrete more FSH.

**Days 1-13: the follicular phase.**

1. FSH stimulates several follicles to grow; usually one becomes dominant and matures into a Graafian follicle.
2. Follicle cells secrete increasing amounts of **oestrogen**.
3. Oestrogen makes the endometrium **repair and thicken**.
4. While oestrogen is moderate, it **inhibits FSH and LH** (negative feedback), so only one follicle normally matures.

**Around day 14: ovulation.**

5. When oestrogen reaches a high level and stays there, its effect switches to **positive feedback**: the hypothalamus and pituitary release a large **surge of LH** (and a smaller rise in FSH).
6. The LH surge causes the primary oocyte to complete meiosis I and the follicle to rupture, releasing the secondary oocyte: **ovulation**, about a day after the surge.

**Days 15-28: the luteal phase.**

7. LH converts the empty follicle into the **corpus luteum**, which secretes **progesterone** (and some oestrogen).
8. Progesterone keeps the endometrium **thick, glandular and full of blood vessels**, ready for implantation, and makes cervical mucus thicker.
9. Progesterone (with oestrogen) **inhibits FSH and LH** by negative feedback, so no new follicles develop.
10. If there is **no pregnancy**, the corpus luteum degenerates after about 10-12 days. Progesterone and oestrogen fall, the endometrium breaks down (menstruation), and the fall removes the inhibition of FSH, which rises and starts the next cycle.
11. If an embryo **implants**, it secretes **hCG**, which keeps the corpus luteum active, so progesterone stays high and menstruation does not occur.

:::example{title="Interpreting hormone data"}
Typical concentrations (in arbitrary units; each hormone has its own scale) during a 28-day cycle:

| Day | 1 | 5 | 9 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 21 | 25 | 28 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FSH | 6 | 7 | 5 | 6 | 12 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 6 |
| LH | 5 | 5 | 6 | 14 | 60 | 8 | 4 | 4 | 5 |
| Oestrogen | 2 | 4 | 9 | 20 | 12 | 8 | 12 | 7 | 2 |
| Progesterone | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 6 | 18 | 9 | 1 |

**Describe and explain:** oestrogen rises from day 5 to a peak of 20 on day 12, as the growing follicle secretes it. The high oestrogen causes, by positive feedback, a sharp LH peak of 60 on day 14, ten times the value on day 9; ovulation follows. Progesterone then rises to 18 on day 21, as the corpus luteum secretes it, while FSH and LH stay low because of negative feedback. Both ovarian hormones fall by day 28 as the corpus luteum degenerates, and FSH starts to rise again.
:::

:::worked-solution[Estimating the day of ovulation]
The luteal phase (from ovulation to the next menstruation) is fairly constant at about 14 days, while the follicular phase varies.
1. For a 35-day cycle, ovulation is at about day $35 - 14 = 21$.
2. For a 25-day cycle, ovulation is at about day $25 - 14 = 11$.
3. This is why counting from the start of a period is unreliable for women whose cycle length varies: the follicular phase changes, so the day of ovulation changes.
:::

## Premenstrual tension

**Premenstrual tension** (premenstrual syndrome) is a set of physical and emotional symptoms in the days before menstruation, such as breast tenderness, bloating, headaches, irritability and low mood. It is linked to the changes in oestrogen and progesterone in the late luteal phase and how they affect neurotransmitters in the brain, though the exact causes are not fully understood. Symptoms vary widely between women. Regular exercise, sleep and diet help many people; doctors may suggest medicines, including hormonal treatment, when symptoms are severe.

## The menopause

At the **menopause**, usually between about 45 and 55 years of age, the ovaries contain few remaining follicles and stop responding to FSH and LH. Ovulation and menstruation stop, and oestrogen and progesterone fall to low levels. Because there is no longer negative feedback from the ovaries, **FSH and LH rise to high concentrations**. The fall in oestrogen can cause hot flushes, night sweats, sleep problems, vaginal dryness and mood changes. In the longer term, low oestrogen leads to faster **loss of bone density**, increasing the risk of **osteoporosis** and fractures, and is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.

## Hormone replacement therapy (HRT)

HRT supplies oestrogen, usually with a progestogen (a synthetic progesterone) for women who still have a uterus, because oestrogen alone stimulates the endometrium to grow and increases the risk of endometrial cancer.

- **Benefits:** relief of hot flushes and other menopausal symptoms; slows bone loss and lowers the risk of osteoporotic fractures.
- **Risks:** for some types and in some women, a small increase in the risk of breast cancer (rising with longer use), and of blood clots and stroke, particularly with tablets.

The balance of benefit and risk depends on a woman's age, health and family history, so the decision is made individually with a doctor.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying FSH and LH are made by the ovary"}
They are made by the **anterior pituitary**; oestrogen and progesterone are made by the ovary.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying oestrogen always inhibits LH"}
Moderate oestrogen inhibits LH, but sustained **high** oestrogen stimulates the LH surge by **positive** feedback.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying menstruation is caused by a rise in a hormone"}
Menstruation follows a **fall** in progesterone (and oestrogen) when the corpus luteum degenerates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- FSH (pituitary) grows follicles, which secrete oestrogen; oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium.
- Moderate oestrogen inhibits FSH and LH; high oestrogen triggers the LH surge (positive feedback), causing ovulation around day 14.
- LH forms the corpus luteum, whose progesterone maintains the endometrium and inhibits FSH and LH.
- Without pregnancy the corpus luteum degenerates, progesterone falls and menstruation follows; hCG from an embryo keeps it active.
- At the menopause ovarian hormones fall and FSH and LH rise; HRT relieves symptoms and protects bone but carries some risks.
:::
