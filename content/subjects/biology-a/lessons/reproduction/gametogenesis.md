## Introduction

A healthy man produces millions of sperm every day from puberty into old age, yet a woman is born with all the egg-producing cells she will ever have, and normally releases only one egg a month for about four decades. Both kinds of gamete are made by the same basic sequence of mitosis, growth and meiosis, but the timing and the outcome are strikingly different. Understanding these differences explains why fertility changes with age, how some contraceptives and fertility treatments work, and why the hormones FSH and LH are named after their effects in the ovary but act in men too.

You should already know the stages of meiosis and the difference between haploid and diploid cells, and the principle of negative feedback from homeostasis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gametogenesis"}
The formation of gametes: **spermatogenesis** in the testes and **oogenesis** in the ovaries.
:::

:::definition{term="Germinal epithelium"}
The layer of diploid cells that divide by mitosis to produce the cells that will become gametes: spermatogonia in the seminiferous tubules, oogonia in the fetal ovary.
:::

:::definition{term="Polar body"}
A small haploid cell with very little cytoplasm, formed by the unequal division of an oocyte in meiosis; it normally degenerates.
:::

:::definition{term="Follicle"}
A structure in the ovary consisting of an oocyte surrounded by one or more layers of follicle (granulosa) cells, which nourish it and secrete hormones.
:::

## Structure of the testis

Each testis contains hundreds of tightly coiled **seminiferous tubules**, where sperm are made. A transverse section seen with a microscope shows:

- **Germinal epithelium** around the outside of each tubule, containing diploid **spermatogonia**.
- Successive stages of development moving inwards: spermatocytes, then spermatids, with **spermatozoa** (tails visible) in the **lumen** at the centre.
- **Sertoli cells:** large cells reaching from the edge to the lumen. Developing sperm are embedded in them. They **nourish** and protect the developing cells, form a barrier that separates them from the blood, respond to **FSH** and secrete the hormone **inhibin**.
- **Interstitial (Leydig) cells:** clusters of cells in the connective tissue **between** the tubules, close to blood capillaries. They secrete **testosterone** in response to **LH**.

Sperm pass from the tubules to the **epididymis**, where they mature and are stored.

## Spermatogenesis

| Stage | Cell | Ploidy | Division |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multiplication | Spermatogonia | 2n | Mitosis, repeatedly, throughout adult life |
| Growth | Primary spermatocyte | 2n | Cell enlarges; DNA replicates |
| Maturation | Secondary spermatocytes (two) | n | **Meiosis I** |
| Maturation | Spermatids (four) | n | **Meiosis II** |
| Differentiation | Spermatozoa (four) | n | No division: spermatids develop an acrosome, a flagellum and many mitochondria, and lose most cytoplasm |

Each primary spermatocyte gives **four** functional spermatozoa. The process takes about two months and goes on continuously from puberty.

## Structure of the ovary

A section of an adult ovary shows, embedded in connective tissue, follicles at different stages:

- **Primary follicles:** a **primary oocyte** surrounded by a single layer of follicle cells; many thousands are present.
- **Developing (secondary) follicles:** several layers of follicle cells, beginning to secrete fluid.
- **Mature (Graafian) follicle:** a large fluid-filled cavity, with the oocyte at one side on a mound of follicle cells; it bulges from the ovary surface before ovulation.
- **Corpus luteum:** a yellowish mass of cells formed from the follicle after ovulation; it secretes **progesterone** and oestrogen.
- A thin **germinal epithelium** covers the ovary surface (a historical name: in adults it does not form new oocytes).

## Oogenesis

1. **Before birth**, in the fetal ovary, oogonia (2n) divide by **mitosis** many times.
2. They grow into **primary oocytes**, which **begin meiosis I** but stop in prophase I. At birth, the ovaries contain all the primary oocytes a woman will have, each in a primary follicle. Many degenerate during childhood.
3. **From puberty**, each month FSH stimulates a few follicles to develop; usually one matures. Just before ovulation its primary oocyte **completes meiosis I**. The division is **unequal**: one large **secondary oocyte** (n) and a small **first polar body**.
4. The secondary oocyte begins **meiosis II** but stops at metaphase II. It is this cell that is released at **ovulation**.
5. **Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm enters** the secondary oocyte. Again the division is unequal, giving the **ovum** and a **second polar body**. The nuclei of the ovum and sperm then fuse.

Each primary oocyte therefore gives **one** functional gamete. The unequal divisions keep almost all the cytoplasm, with its food reserves, mitochondria and ribosomes, in that one cell for the early embryo.

## Spermatogenesis and oogenesis compared

| Feature | Spermatogenesis | Oogenesis |
|---|---|---|
| Where | Seminiferous tubules of testes | Ovaries |
| Multiplication of germ cells | Throughout adult life | Only before birth |
| Gametes per primary cell | Four spermatozoa | One secondary oocyte (ovum), plus polar bodies |
| Cytoplasm division | Equal | Unequal |
| Meiosis | Continuous, completed in the testis | Arrested twice; completed only after fertilisation |
| Timing | Continuous from puberty | Cyclical, about one per month from puberty to menopause |
| Gamete | Small, motile, little cytoplasm | Large, non-motile, food reserves |

:::worked-solution[Counting cells and DNA]
1. In a tubule, 120 primary spermatocytes complete meiosis. Meiosis I gives $120 \times 2 = 240$ secondary spermatocytes and meiosis II gives $120 \times 4 = 480$ spermatids, which become 480 spermatozoa.
2. 120 primary oocytes completing oogenesis give only **120** ova, and up to $120 \times 3 = 360$ polar bodies (if each first polar body also divides).
3. Taking the DNA content of a spermatogonium in G1 as 2 units: a primary spermatocyte after replication has 4 units, a secondary spermatocyte 2 units, and a spermatid 1 unit.
:::

## Hormonal control of gametogenesis

The **hypothalamus** secretes **gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH)**, which travels in blood vessels to the **anterior pituitary gland** and stimulates it to release two gonadotrophins: **FSH** (follicle-stimulating hormone) and **LH** (luteinising hormone).

**In males**

- **LH** stimulates the interstitial cells to secrete **testosterone**.
- **FSH** acts on the Sertoli cells and, together with testosterone, stimulates spermatogenesis.
- Testosterone also maintains the male secondary sexual characteristics.
- **Negative feedback:** a high concentration of testosterone inhibits secretion of GnRH and LH; **inhibin** from Sertoli cells inhibits FSH secretion. Hormone levels, and therefore sperm production, stay fairly constant.

**In females**

- **FSH** stimulates the growth of follicles and the secretion of **oestrogen** by follicle cells.
- A surge of **LH** triggers completion of meiosis I and **ovulation**, and turns the follicle into the corpus luteum.
- The cycle of these hormones is described in detail in the lesson on the menstrual cycle.

:::example{title="Interpreting an experiment on hormone control"}
In an animal study, the anterior pituitary gland was removed from adult male rats. Spermatogenesis stopped and testosterone fell almost to zero. Daily injections of LH alone restored testosterone, and injections of LH and FSH together restored sperm production. **Interpret:** the pituitary is needed for both; LH controls testosterone secretion by interstitial cells; spermatogenesis needs testosterone and FSH acting together on the seminiferous tubules.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the ovum is released at ovulation"}
A **secondary oocyte**, arrested in metaphase II, is released. Meiosis II is completed only after a sperm enters.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing Sertoli and interstitial cells"}
**Sertoli** cells are inside the tubules and nourish sperm (FSH target). **Interstitial** cells are between tubules and make testosterone (LH target).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying women make new oocytes each month"}
All primary oocytes form before birth; each month some resume development.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Seminiferous tubules contain germinal epithelium, developing sperm and Sertoli cells; interstitial cells between tubules secrete testosterone.
- Spermatogenesis: spermatogonia (mitosis) → primary spermatocytes (growth) → meiosis I and II → four spermatids → spermatozoa, continuously from puberty.
- Oogenesis: oogonia multiply before birth; primary oocytes arrest in prophase I; one per cycle completes meiosis I (secondary oocyte + polar body); meiosis II is completed at fertilisation.
- Unequal division in oogenesis gives one large ovum; spermatogenesis gives four small, motile sperm.
- GnRH stimulates FSH and LH; LH → testosterone; FSH + testosterone → spermatogenesis; testosterone and inhibin give negative feedback.
:::
