## Introduction

At almost every rural clinic in Zimbabwe, a nurse can confirm malaria in about fifteen minutes from a single drop of blood, with no microscope and no laboratory. The same kind of strip, bought from a pharmacy, tells a woman whether she is pregnant. Both tests rely on **monoclonal antibodies**: identical antibody molecules made in the laboratory, all binding to one particular molecule.

To understand how such tests and antibody-based medicines work, you need to understand the antibody molecule itself: why it is shaped like a Y, why its two arms are identical, and how its structure lets it bind one antigen so precisely. You should already know protein structure (primary to quaternary structure, disulfide bonds), the immune response (B-cells and plasma cells), and the idea of a complementary shape from enzymes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antibody"}
A globular glycoprotein (immunoglobulin) secreted by plasma cells that binds specifically to one antigen.
:::

:::definition{term="Variable region"}
The part at the tip of each arm of an antibody whose amino acid sequence differs between antibodies; it forms the antigen-binding site, which has a shape complementary to one antigen.
:::

:::definition{term="Constant region"}
The part of an antibody whose amino acid sequence is the same in all antibodies of a class; it binds to receptors on phagocytes and other cells.
:::

:::definition{term="Monoclonal antibodies"}
Identical antibodies produced by a single clone of cells, all binding to the same site on one antigen.
:::

:::definition{term="Hybridoma"}
A cell formed by fusing a plasma cell (or activated B-cell) with a cancer (myeloma) cell; it makes one kind of antibody and divides indefinitely.
:::

## Antibody structure

An antibody molecule is a Y-shaped protein with **quaternary structure**:

| Part | Description | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Polypeptide chains | Four: two identical **heavy chains** and two identical **light chains** | Together form the Y shape |
| Disulfide bonds | Covalent bonds between cysteine R groups | Hold the heavy and light chains together and the two heavy chains to each other |
| Variable regions | Ends of both heavy and light chains at the tips of the arms | Form two identical **antigen-binding sites**, complementary in shape to one antigen |
| Constant regions | The rest of the chains, including the stem | Same within a class; bind receptors on phagocytes; activate other defences |
| Hinge region | Flexible section where the arms meet the stem | Lets the arms move apart, so one antibody can bind two antigens that are different distances apart |

Because the antibody has **two** binding sites, it can bind two antigens at once, linking pathogens together. Carbohydrate chains are attached to the heavy chains, making antibodies glycoproteins.

The enormous variety of antigen-binding sites comes from variation in the amino acid sequence of the variable regions, produced by rearrangement of gene segments as B-cells mature. Each B-cell, and all the plasma cells of its clone, makes antibody with only one binding site shape.

## How antibodies help destroy pathogens

1. **Agglutination.** Each antibody has two binding sites, so antibodies cross-link bacteria or viruses into clumps. Clumped pathogens cannot spread easily and are engulfed more readily by phagocytes.
2. **Neutralisation.** Antibodies bind to toxins (antitoxins) or to the parts of viruses that attach to host cells, blocking them. Neutralised viruses cannot enter cells, and toxins cannot act.
3. **Opsonisation and attracting phagocytes.** Antibodies coat a pathogen; phagocytes have receptors for the **constant regions**, so they bind and engulf coated pathogens much faster.
4. **Lysis.** Antibodies bound to a bacterium activate a group of blood proteins (complement) that make holes in the bacterial membrane, so the cell bursts.

Antibodies do not kill pathogens directly by themselves in most cases: they **mark** them and help other parts of the immune system destroy them.

## Making monoclonal antibodies: the hybridoma method

Plasma cells make a single antibody but do not divide, and they cannot survive in culture. Myeloma (cancer) cells divide indefinitely. The hybridoma method combines the two:

1. **Immunise** a mouse with the chosen antigen. Its B-cells that recognise the antigen undergo clonal expansion.
2. **Collect** activated B-cells (plasma cells) from the mouse's **spleen**.
3. **Fuse** them with myeloma cells, using a chemical such as polyethylene glycol or an electric pulse, to form **hybridoma cells**.
4. **Select** hybridoma cells by growing the mixture in a medium in which unfused cells die.
5. **Clone and screen.** Each hybridoma is grown separately and tested to find the clone producing the required antibody.
6. **Culture** the chosen clone in large fermenters; it divides by mitosis and secretes large amounts of one antibody, which is collected and purified.

Mouse antibodies can provoke an immune response in humans, so antibodies used as medicines are usually **humanised** by genetic engineering, replacing most of the mouse sequence with human sequence.

## Monoclonal antibodies in diagnosis

**Pregnancy tests** detect the hormone **hCG** (human chorionic gonadotrophin), made by the embryo soon after implantation and present in urine.

1. Urine is applied to the end of the strip and moves along by capillary action.
2. At the **reaction zone**, mobile monoclonal antibodies against hCG, attached to coloured particles, bind any hCG present.
3. At the **test line**, immobilised antibodies bind a different part of the hCG molecule, trapping the hCG-antibody-colour complexes: a coloured line appears only if hCG is present.
4. At the **control line**, immobilised antibodies bind the mobile antibodies themselves, so a line appears whether or not hCG is present, showing that the test has worked.

**Malaria rapid diagnostic tests** work in the same way, using monoclonal antibodies that bind antigens made by *Plasmodium*, such as a protein released by *P. falciparum* into the blood. Monoclonal antibodies are also used to detect HIV, to type blood and tissues, and to find where cancers are in the body.

:::worked-solution[How reliable is a rapid diagnostic test?]
In a hypothetical trial, 250 people had malaria confirmed by microscopy and 237 of them tested positive with a rapid diagnostic test; 400 people without malaria were tested and 388 tested negative.
1. Percentage of people with malaria detected $= \dfrac{237}{250} \times 100 = 94.8\%$.
2. Percentage of people without malaria correctly testing negative $= \dfrac{388}{400} \times 100 = 97\%$.
3. So about 1 in 20 infected people would be missed and 3 in 100 uninfected people wrongly told they have malaria. Clinicians combine the result with symptoms and may repeat or confirm the test.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting a pregnancy test"}
Rudo's test shows a coloured control line but no test line. **Interpret:** the control line shows that urine has moved along the strip and the mobile antibodies have reached the control line, so the test worked. No hCG was trapped at the test line, so no hCG was detected. **Suggest** why she might repeat it: if taken very early, hCG may be below the level the test can detect.

If no control line appears, the test is **invalid**, whatever the test line shows.
:::

## Monoclonal antibodies in treatment

Some medicines are monoclonal antibodies. Examples:

- **Trastuzumab** binds a receptor protein (HER2) present in large numbers on some breast cancer cells, blocking growth signals and marking the cells for destruction by the immune system.
- Other monoclonal antibodies block molecules that cause inflammation, or carry drugs or radioactive substances directly to cancer cells, reducing harm to healthy cells.

They are highly specific, so side effects can be fewer than with conventional drugs, but they are expensive to produce, must be injected (they are proteins, so would be digested if swallowed), and can still cause reactions.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying antibodies are made by B-cells or lymphocytes in general"}
Antibodies are secreted by **plasma cells**, which develop from B-cells.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the constant region binds the antigen"}
The **variable regions** form the antigen-binding sites. The constant region binds phagocyte receptors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the control line"}
In a lateral flow test, the control line must appear for any result to be valid.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An antibody has two heavy and two light chains held by disulfide bonds, with variable regions forming two identical antigen-binding sites, constant regions and a hinge.
- Antibodies agglutinate pathogens, neutralise toxins and viruses, coat pathogens for phagocytes (opsonisation) and trigger lysis.
- Monoclonal antibodies are made by fusing plasma cells with myeloma cells to form hybridomas, then selecting, screening and cloning.
- Lateral flow tests (pregnancy, malaria) use mobile and immobilised monoclonal antibodies with test and control lines.
- In treatment, monoclonal antibodies bind specific targets on cancer cells or block inflammatory molecules (Cambridge).
:::
