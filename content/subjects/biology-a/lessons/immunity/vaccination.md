## Introduction

Every child born in Zimbabwe is offered a series of vaccines in the first years of life, recorded on the child health card that parents carry to the clinic: BCG at birth, polio drops, combined vaccines against diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough and other infections, and vaccines against measles, rotavirus and pneumococcus. Smallpox, which once killed millions of people each year, is no longer on the list, because vaccination eradicated it from the world.

Vaccination is one of the most effective health measures ever developed. This lesson explains the biology behind it: the different ways the body can become immune, what is in a vaccine, why some diseases can be eradicated while others cannot, and what makes a vaccination programme succeed. You should already know the primary and secondary immune responses, memory cells and antibody structure.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Active immunity"}
Immunity that results from the body's own immune response to an antigen, in which lymphocytes are activated, antibodies are made and memory cells are formed.
:::

:::definition{term="Passive immunity"}
Immunity that results from receiving antibodies made by another person or animal; no immune response occurs and no memory cells are formed.
:::

:::definition{term="Natural and artificial immunity"}
**Natural** immunity is gained through normal life (infection, or antibodies crossing the placenta or in breast milk); **artificial** immunity is gained through medical intervention (vaccination or injected antibodies).
:::

:::definition{term="Vaccine"}
A preparation containing antigens, in the form of dead or weakened pathogens, parts of them or inactivated toxins, given to stimulate active immunity without causing the disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Herd immunity"}
The indirect protection of unvaccinated people that occurs when a large enough proportion of a population is immune that the pathogen cannot spread easily.
:::

## Four types of immunity

| | Active (own immune response; memory cells; long-term) | Passive (antibodies received; no memory cells; short-term) |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Recovering from an infection, such as measles | Antibodies crossing the placenta to the fetus; antibodies in breast milk, especially colostrum |
| **Artificial** | Vaccination | Injection of antibodies, such as antitoxin for tetanus or antivenom after a snakebite, or rabies immunoglobulin |

**Comparing active and passive immunity**

- **Speed:** passive immunity acts **immediately**, because antibodies are already present; active immunity takes days to weeks (the primary response).
- **Duration:** passive immunity lasts only **weeks to months**, as the antibodies are broken down and none are replaced; active immunity can last for years or life, because **memory cells** remain.
- **Use:** passive immunity is valuable when protection is needed at once (a puncture wound that may contain tetanus spores, a bite from a snake or a possibly rabid dog) and for newborn babies whose own immune systems are immature.

## What is in a vaccine?

Vaccines contain antigens that stimulate a primary response, so that memory cells are made without the person having the disease.

| Type | What it contains | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Live attenuated | Living pathogens weakened so they multiply only a little and cause no disease in healthy people | Measles, oral polio, BCG |
| Inactivated | Whole pathogens killed by heat or chemicals | Injected polio vaccine, some cholera vaccines |
| Subunit | Purified antigens or fragments of the pathogen, sometimes made by genetic engineering | Hepatitis B, HPV |
| Toxoid | A bacterial toxin made harmless but still with the same antigens | Tetanus, diphtheria |

Live attenuated vaccines usually give the strongest, longest-lasting immunity, because the pathogen multiplies a little and presents antigens as in a real infection; but they are not given to people with severely weakened immune systems. Inactivated and subunit vaccines are safer but often need several doses (**boosters**). Each dose causes a further secondary response, producing more memory cells.

## How vaccination programmes control disease

1. **Individual protection.** A vaccinated person has memory cells, so if the real pathogen enters, a fast secondary response destroys it before symptoms develop.
2. **Herd immunity.** If enough people are immune, an infected person is unlikely to meet a susceptible person, so chains of transmission break. This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: babies too young, people with weakened immunity, and the few in whom the vaccine did not work. The more infectious the disease, the higher the coverage needed: for measles, about 95% of the population must be immune.
3. **Mass vaccination** of most of a population over a short time, followed by **ring vaccination** (vaccinating contacts around each case) when cases become rare.

:::worked-solution[Vaccination coverage]
In a hypothetical ward, 1,360 children reached the age for measles vaccination in one year. 1,258 received the first dose and 1,142 the second.
1. Coverage with the first dose $= \dfrac{1258}{1360} \times 100 = 92.5\%$.
2. Coverage with the second dose $= \dfrac{1142}{1360} \times 100 = 84.0\%$ (3 s.f.).
3. Drop-out between doses $= \dfrac{1258 - 1142}{1258} \times 100 = 9.22\%$ (3 s.f.).
4. Coverage with two doses is well below about 95%, so herd immunity is not reliable and an outbreak could spread among unprotected children.
:::

:::example{title="Interpreting coverage and case data"}
Hypothetical data for one district:

| Year | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Measles vaccination coverage / % | 92 | 88 | 79 | 74 |
| Measles cases | 3 | 5 | 21 | 64 |

**Describe:** as coverage fell from 92% to 74%, cases rose from 3 to 64; cases increased sharply once coverage fell below about 80%. **Explain:** as the number of susceptible children grew, herd immunity was lost, so each case could infect several others. **Evaluate:** this is a correlation in one district; reporting may also have changed, but the pattern fits the known biology of herd immunity.
:::

## Successes: smallpox, polio and measles

- **Smallpox** was declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in **1980**, the first human disease eradicated. This was possible because the virus had **stable antigens** (one vaccine protected against all strains), **no animal reservoir**, infected people were easy to recognise (a distinctive rash), there were no symptomless carriers, the live vaccine was effective, **heat-stable** (freeze-dried) and easy to give, and countries cooperated in a global campaign using mass and then ring vaccination.
- **Polio** has been eliminated from most of the world by vaccination; Africa was certified free of wild poliovirus in 2020. It has not yet been eradicated, because wild virus persists in a few countries and many infections cause no symptoms, so the virus can spread unnoticed.
- **Measles** is highly infectious and needs very high two-dose coverage. Outbreaks still occur where coverage falls, in crowded settings, or among children who missed vaccination.

## Why some diseases are hard to control by vaccination

- **Antigenic variation:** pathogens that change their surface antigens escape memory cells made against earlier forms. Influenza viruses change gradually and sometimes suddenly, so vaccines must be updated; HIV mutates rapidly; *Plasmodium* changes its antigens and hides inside cells.
- **Pathogens inside cells or in the gut:** antibodies cannot reach pathogens inside cells, and cholera bacteria in the intestine are reached poorly by antibodies in the blood, so protection from oral cholera vaccines lasts only a few years.
- **Many strains:** colds are caused by over a hundred different viruses.
- **The cold chain:** most vaccines must be kept cold (usually between 2 and 8 °C) from the factory to the child, or the proteins denature and the vaccine stops working; this needs refrigerators, power and transport in remote areas.
- **Coverage:** reaching every child needs staff, transport, records and follow-up for later doses. Conflict, displacement, distance from clinics, misinformation and mistrust all reduce coverage.
- **Weak responses:** malnourished or immunocompromised people may respond poorly.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying vaccines contain antibodies"}
Vaccines contain **antigens**. Injected antibodies give passive, not active, immunity.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a baby's breast-milk antibodies give long-term immunity"}
Passive immunity lasts only weeks to months; no memory cells are made.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying herd immunity means everyone is vaccinated"}
It means **enough** people are immune that the pathogen cannot spread, protecting those who are not.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Active immunity comes from the body's own response and memory cells (natural by infection, artificial by vaccination); it is slow to develop but long-lasting.
- Passive immunity comes from received antibodies (natural across the placenta or in milk, artificial by injection); it is immediate but short-lived.
- Vaccines contain antigens: live attenuated, inactivated, subunit or toxoid; boosters strengthen memory.
- High coverage gives herd immunity; smallpox was eradicated by 1980 thanks to stable antigens, visible cases, no reservoir and a heat-stable vaccine.
- Antigenic variation, pathogens inside cells, the cold chain and incomplete coverage make some diseases hard to control.
:::
