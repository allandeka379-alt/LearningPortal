## Introduction

Every day you breathe in, eat and touch millions of microorganisms, yet you are rarely ill. When a child in Bulawayo has measles once, she will almost certainly never have it again, but the same child can catch a cold many times. Both facts are explained by the immune system: a fast, general first line of defence carried out by phagocytes, and a slower but highly specific response by lymphocytes that leaves behind a long-lasting memory.

This lesson follows a pathogen from the moment it enters the body to the formation of memory cells. You should already know the structure of eukaryotic cells (lysosomes, Golgi body, rough endoplasmic reticulum), the fluid mosaic model of membranes (glycoproteins), endocytosis and mitosis.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Antigen"}
A molecule, usually a protein or glycoprotein on the surface of a cell or virus, that is recognised as foreign by the immune system and stimulates an immune response.
:::

:::definition{term="Self and non-self"}
**Self** antigens are the body's own molecules, which lymphocytes normally do not attack; **non-self** antigens are foreign molecules, such as those on pathogens, transplanted cells or toxins, which trigger a response.
:::

:::definition{term="Phagocytosis"}
The engulfing of a pathogen or particle by a phagocyte into a vesicle (phagosome), followed by its digestion by enzymes from lysosomes.
:::

:::definition{term="Immune response"}
The complex series of responses of the body to the entry of a foreign antigen, involving the activity of lymphocytes and phagocytes.
:::

:::definition{term="Clonal selection and expansion"}
The activation of the particular lymphocyte whose receptors match an antigen (selection), followed by its repeated division by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells (expansion).
:::

:::definition{term="Memory cells"}
Long-lived B- or T-lymphocytes formed during a primary response that remain in the body and respond rapidly if the same antigen enters again.
:::

## Phagocytes: the non-specific response

Phagocytes are made in the bone marrow and attack any pathogen they meet, without needing to recognise a particular antigen.

| Feature | Neutrophils | Macrophages |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Lobed nucleus, granular cytoplasm | Large, kidney-shaped nucleus |
| Where | Most common white cell in blood; leave capillaries to enter infected tissue | Develop from monocytes; live in tissues (lungs, liver, lymph nodes) |
| Lifespan | Short; die after engulfing bacteria (forming pus) | Long-lived |
| Main role | Fast first responders | Phagocytosis and **antigen presentation** to lymphocytes |

**The steps of phagocytosis**

1. Damaged cells and pathogens release chemicals; phagocytes move towards them up the concentration gradient (**chemotaxis**).
2. Receptors on the phagocyte bind to the pathogen, especially if it is coated with antibodies (opsonised).
3. The phagocyte's membrane extends around the pathogen and engulfs it by **endocytosis**, enclosing it in a vesicle called a **phagosome**.
4. **Lysosomes** fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes (and reactive chemicals) into it.
5. The pathogen is digested; soluble products are absorbed or released, and macrophages display fragments of its antigens on their surface.

## Lymphocytes: the specific response

Lymphocytes are small cells with a large nucleus. Each one carries receptors of **one shape only**, so it can bind only one antigen. Millions of different lymphocytes are made, each with a different receptor, before any infection occurs.

- **B-lymphocytes** mature in the **bone marrow**. Their receptors are antibody molecules on the cell surface. When activated, they divide and become **plasma cells**, which secrete antibodies, and **memory B-cells**.
- **T-lymphocytes** are made in the bone marrow but mature in the **thymus**. Their receptors are not antibodies and only bind antigens presented on the surface of other cells. The two main types are **T-helper cells** and **T-killer** (cytotoxic) cells.

During maturation, lymphocytes whose receptors bind **self** antigens are destroyed, so the remaining cells normally respond only to non-self antigens.

## The primary immune response, step by step

1. **Antigen presentation.** A macrophage engulfs a pathogen, digests it and places fragments of its antigens on its cell surface membrane. It becomes an **antigen-presenting cell**.
2. **Clonal selection of T-helper cells.** The one T-helper cell whose receptors are complementary to the presented antigen binds to it and is activated.
3. **Clonal expansion.** The activated T-helper cell divides by mitosis to form a clone. These cells release **cytokines**, signalling molecules that stimulate B-cells, T-killer cells and phagocytes.
4. **Activation of B-cells.** A B-cell whose surface antibodies bind the same antigen takes it in and presents it; with cytokines from T-helper cells, it is selected and divides rapidly by mitosis.
5. **Differentiation.** Most of the clone become **plasma cells**: large cells packed with rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi bodies, each secreting thousands of identical antibody molecules per second. Some become **memory B-cells**.
6. **Cell-mediated response.** Activated **T-killer cells** bind to body cells that display the antigen (for example virus-infected cells or cancer cells) and release chemicals, such as perforin, that make holes in the target cell's membrane, so it dies. Some T-cells become **memory T-cells**.

This is called the **primary response**. It takes several days, because the few matching lymphocytes must be found and must multiply. During this time pathogens multiply and the person usually becomes ill.

## Memory cells and the secondary response

If the same antigen enters again, memory cells, which are present in much larger numbers than the original matching lymphocytes, recognise it at once. They divide quickly into plasma cells and T-killer cells. The **secondary response** is:

- **faster** (antibodies appear within a few days),
- **larger** (much higher antibody concentration), and
- **longer-lasting**.

The pathogen is usually destroyed before it causes symptoms: the person is **immune**.

:::example{title="Interpreting antibody data"}
A person was exposed to an antigen on day 0 and again on day 28. The concentration of the specific antibody in the blood was measured.

| Day | 0 | 7 | 14 | 21 | 28 | 35 | 42 | 49 | 56 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Antibody concentration / arbitrary units | 0 | 4 | 20 | 9 | 3 | 150 | 380 | 300 | 210 |

**Describe:** after the first exposure, antibody appears slowly, peaks at 20 units on day 14 and falls to 3 units by day 28. After the second exposure it rises much faster, to 150 units within 7 days, and peaks at 380 units on day 42, 19 times the first peak; it then falls more slowly. **Explain:** the first exposure caused a primary response, in which few lymphocytes matched the antigen and clonal expansion took time. Memory cells formed then responded immediately to the second exposure, producing many plasma cells quickly.
:::

:::example{title="Why can you catch a cold many times?"}
Colds are caused by many different viruses (over a hundred kinds of rhinovirus alone), each with different antigens. Memory cells from one infection do not match the antigens of another virus, so each new virus triggers a new primary response. Measles virus, by contrast, has stable antigens, so one infection gives lifelong immunity.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards: phagocytosis, antigens, B- and T-lymphocytes, plasma cells, memory cells, and the primary and secondary responses. Cambridge names neutrophils and macrophages and expects the role of T-helper cells in stimulating B-cells and T-killer cells. Autoimmune diseases such as myasthenia gravis are not part of this concept.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying B-cells or memory cells secrete antibodies"}
Only **plasma cells** secrete antibodies. Memory cells wait and divide when the antigen returns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying lymphocytes 'learn' or 'change shape' to fit an antigen"}
Lymphocytes with every receptor shape already exist. The antigen **selects** the matching one, which then divides.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying T-killer cells engulf pathogens"}
T-killer cells kill **infected body cells** by making holes in their membranes; phagocytes do the engulfing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing antigen and antibody"}
The **antigen** is on the pathogen and is recognised; the **antibody** is the protein made in response.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
The immune response appears on ZIMSEC Papers 1-3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2. In *describe* questions on phagocytosis, give the steps in order with the key terms: chemotaxis, endocytosis, phagosome, lysosome, hydrolytic enzymes.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *explain* a graph of antibody concentration, compare **speed** (days), **size** (quote peak values) and **duration**, then link the difference to **memory cells**. Always state that the secondary response destroys the pathogen before symptoms develop.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages) carry out non-specific defence: chemotaxis, endocytosis into a phagosome, fusion with lysosomes and digestion.
- Lymphocytes recognise specific non-self antigens; B-cells mature in bone marrow and T-cells in the thymus.
- In the primary response, macrophages present antigen, T-helper cells release cytokines, and clonal selection and expansion produce plasma cells (antibodies) and T-killer cells.
- Memory B- and T-cells remain after the primary response.
- The secondary response is faster, larger and longer-lasting, so the person is immune.
:::
