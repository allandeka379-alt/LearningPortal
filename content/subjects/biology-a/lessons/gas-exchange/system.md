## Introduction

Every minute at rest you breathe in and out about a dozen times, moving several litres of air. On a dusty day in Bulawayo, or in a kitchen filled with wood smoke, that air carries particles and microorganisms that would quickly damage the delicate surfaces where oxygen enters the blood. The human gas exchange system solves two problems at once: it brings air very close to a huge area of blood capillaries, and it cleans, warms and moistens that air on the way in.

A large, active mammal has a small surface area to volume ratio, so its body surface cannot supply enough oxygen by diffusion. The lungs provide an internal surface for exchange. You should already know the structure of cells, diffusion, and the structure and function of capillaries.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gas exchange"}
The diffusion of oxygen from the air in the alveoli into the blood, and of carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveolar air, across a gas exchange surface.
:::

:::definition{term="Ciliated epithelium"}
A layer of cells with many cilia on their free surface, whose coordinated beating moves mucus along the airways.
:::

:::definition{term="Goblet cell"}
A cell in the epithelium of the airways that secretes mucus.
:::

:::definition{term="Squamous epithelium"}
A layer of very thin, flattened cells; it forms the alveolar walls and the walls of capillaries.
:::

:::definition{term="Alveolus"}
A tiny air sac at the end of a bronchiole, with a wall one cell thick, surrounded by capillaries, where gas exchange takes place.
:::

## The airways

Air passes: **nasal cavity → trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveoli**. The airways branch repeatedly, so the bronchioles reach every part of each lung.

### Distribution of tissues

| Tissue | Trachea | Bronchus | Bronchiole (larger) | Alveolus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cartilage | C-shaped rings | Irregular plates | None | None |
| Ciliated epithelium | Present | Present | Present (in the larger ones; becoming cuboidal and losing cilia in the smallest) | None |
| Goblet cells | Many | Present | Few or none | None |
| Smooth muscle | Present (at the back, across the gap in the C) | Present | Present: a relatively thick layer | None |
| Elastic fibres | Present | Present | Present | Present, in the walls |
| Squamous epithelium | None | None | None | Forms the wall |

Mucous glands lie beneath the epithelium of the trachea and bronchi.

## Functions of the tissues

**Keeping the system clean and healthy:**

- **Goblet cells** and **mucous glands** secrete **mucus**, which traps dust, pollen, bacteria and viruses. Mucus also keeps the airway surface moist.
- **Ciliated epithelial cells** beat their cilia in a coordinated rhythm, sweeping the mucus and trapped particles **upwards** towards the throat, where it is swallowed and pathogens are destroyed by stomach acid. This is sometimes called the mucus escalator.
- Phagocytes (alveolar macrophages) engulf particles that reach the alveoli.

**Support and control of the airways:**

- **Cartilage** supports the trachea and bronchi and keeps them **open**, so they do not collapse when air pressure inside falls during inspiration. The C-shaped rings of the trachea are open at the back, which lets the oesophagus behind it expand when food is swallowed.
- **Smooth muscle** can contract to **narrow** the airways (and relax to widen them), controlling the flow of air. In the bronchioles, where there is no cartilage, this is especially important.
- **Elastic fibres** **stretch** when the lungs fill during inspiration and **recoil** during expiration, helping to push air out. They also stop alveoli bursting.
- **Squamous epithelium** forms the alveolar wall: its cells are extremely thin, giving a **very short diffusion distance**.

## Gas exchange in the alveoli

Oxygen diffuses from alveolar air, through the alveolar wall and the capillary wall, into the plasma and red blood cells. Carbon dioxide diffuses the other way.

Features that maximise the rate of diffusion:

1. **Very large surface area:** hundreds of millions of alveoli give a total surface area roughly the size of a tennis court.
2. **Short diffusion distance:** the alveolar wall and capillary wall are each one squamous cell thick, and the two are pressed close together, so the total barrier is typically less than 1 µm. Capillaries are narrow, so red blood cells are squeezed against the wall.
3. **Steep concentration gradients**, maintained by **ventilation** (breathing replaces alveolar air, keeping oxygen high and carbon dioxide low) and by **blood flow** (blood constantly carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide).
4. A thin film of **moisture** lining the alveoli lets gases dissolve before they diffuse through the cells.

These factors are summarised by Fick's law: the rate of diffusion is proportional to surface area × difference in concentration ÷ thickness of the exchange surface.

:::worked-solution[Relative rate of diffusion after lung damage]
In a damaged lung, the surface area for gas exchange has fallen to 0.6 of its original value and the barrier has thickened to 1.5 times its original thickness, while the concentration difference is unchanged.
1. Relative rate $\propto \dfrac{\text{surface area} \times \text{concentration difference}}{\text{thickness}}$.
2. Relative rate $= \dfrac{0.6 \times 1}{1.5} = 0.4$.
3. The rate of diffusion is only 40% of the original, so the person becomes breathless on mild exertion.
:::

## Recognising the tissues in slides and photomicrographs

- **Trachea (TS):** a C-shaped ring of **hyaline cartilage**, stained pale blue-grey or purple and appearing glassy, with small cells (chondrocytes) in spaces; a lining of tall **ciliated columnar epithelium** with interspersed pale, cup-shaped **goblet cells**; mucous glands in the connective tissue beneath; a band of smooth muscle across the open ends of the C.
- **Bronchus:** similar to the trachea, but with **plates** of cartilage instead of complete C-shaped rings, and a more obvious layer of smooth muscle.
- **Bronchiole:** no cartilage; a lining of ciliated cells (or, in the smallest, cuboidal cells), surrounded by a ring of **smooth muscle**; the lumen often appears star-shaped or folded because the muscle has contracted.
- **Alveoli:** a lace-like network of thin-walled air spaces separated by very thin walls containing capillaries, often with red blood cells visible in them.

:::worked-solution[Actual diameter of an alveolus]
On a photomicrograph at ×200, an alveolus measures 42 mm across.
1. Convert: $42\ \text{mm} = 42\,000\ \mu\text{m}$.
2. Actual diameter $= 42\,000 \div 200 = 210\ \mu\text{m}$.
3. This is typical: human alveoli are a few hundred micrometres across.
:::

### Plan diagrams of the trachea and bronchus wall

A plan diagram shows the **layers** of the wall as regions in the correct proportions, without drawing individual cells: from the lumen outwards, the ciliated epithelium (a thin band), the connective tissue with glands, the cartilage (in the trachea a thick C-shaped band; in a bronchus separate plates) and the outer connective tissue. Use sharp continuous lines and ruled label lines, and no shading.

:::example{title="Identifying a section"}
A section shows a small airway with no cartilage, a folded lumen lined with ciliated cells, and a thick ring of smooth muscle. **Identify** it and **explain**: it is a **bronchiole**, because cartilage is absent and smooth muscle is prominent; the folds are caused by contraction of the muscle.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cartilage is found in bronchioles"}
Cartilage is in the **trachea** (C-shaped rings) and **bronchi** (plates) only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying cilia trap dust"}
**Mucus** traps particles; **cilia** move the mucus up and out of the airways.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying smooth muscle 'pumps' air"}
Smooth muscle changes the **diameter** of the airways; breathing movements are made by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying elastic fibres contract"}
Elastic fibres are not muscle. They **stretch** and **recoil** passively.
:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Air passes trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.
- Cartilage (trachea C-rings, bronchus plates) keeps airways open; smooth muscle changes their diameter; elastic fibres stretch and recoil.
- Goblet cells and mucous glands make mucus that traps particles; cilia sweep it to the throat.
- Alveoli: large surface area, walls of squamous epithelium next to capillaries (short diffusion distance), steep gradients maintained by ventilation and blood flow.
- Identify airways by cartilage, cilia, goblet cells and smooth muscle; plan diagrams show wall layers without cells.
:::
