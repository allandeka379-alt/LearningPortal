## Introduction

A maize plant in a field near Chinhoyi and a farmer weeding between its rows look very different, yet inside every one of their cells the same small molecule is being broken down and remade thousands of times a second. That molecule is **adenosine triphosphate (ATP)**. Photosynthesis traps light energy, respiration releases energy from glucose, but almost every energy-requiring job in a cell is paid for in ATP.

This lesson explains why organisms need energy, why ATP rather than glucose is the immediate energy source, and the two ways cells make ATP. The details of respiration and photosynthesis follow in later lessons. You should already know the structure of nucleotides (from DNA and RNA), what an enzyme does, and the idea of a concentration gradient.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="ATP (adenosine triphosphate)"}
A phosphorylated nucleotide made of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups; hydrolysis of its terminal phosphate releases energy in a small, usable amount, so it acts as the immediate energy source of the cell.
:::

:::definition{term="Universal energy currency"}
A molecule used by all living organisms to transfer energy from energy-releasing reactions to energy-requiring processes; ATP fills this role.
:::

:::definition{term="Substrate-linked phosphorylation"}
Synthesis of ATP by the direct transfer of a phosphate group from a phosphorylated substrate molecule to ADP, catalysed by an enzyme (in glycolysis and the Krebs cycle).
:::

:::definition{term="Chemiosmosis"}
Synthesis of ATP using the energy of protons (hydrogen ions) diffusing down their concentration gradient through ATP synthase in a membrane, after the gradient has been set up using energy from electrons.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Values to know"}
Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and inorganic phosphate releases about 30 kJ per mole (often quoted as 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹). Complete oxidation of one mole of glucose releases about 2,870 kJ.
:::

## Why organisms need energy

Living cells must do **work** all the time, even when the organism seems to be at rest. Energy is needed for:

1. **Anabolic reactions**: building large molecules from small ones, such as protein synthesis from amino acids, DNA replication, polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose) synthesis.
2. **Active transport**: moving ions and molecules against their concentration gradient through carrier proteins, for example the sodium-potassium pump in every animal cell and mineral ion uptake by root hair cells.
3. **Movement**: muscle contraction, the beating of cilia and flagella, and the movement of chromosomes during cell division.
4. **Bulk transport**: endocytosis and exocytosis, such as the secretion of digestive enzymes.
5. **Maintaining body temperature** in mammals and birds (endotherms), using heat released by respiration.
6. **Activating molecules**: phosphorylating glucose at the start of glycolysis makes it more reactive.

## The structure of ATP

ATP is a **nucleotide** with two extra phosphate groups. Built up part by part:

| Part | What it is |
|---|---|
| Adenine | A nitrogen-containing organic base (a purine) |
| Ribose | A pentose (5-carbon) sugar; adenine + ribose = adenosine |
| Three phosphate groups | Linked in a chain to carbon 5 of ribose |

Adenosine + one phosphate is AMP; + two phosphates is ADP; + three phosphates is ATP.

## Releasing energy: hydrolysis of ATP

The enzyme **ATPase** (ATP hydrolase) catalyses the hydrolysis of the terminal phosphate:

$$
\text{ATP} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{ADP} + \text{P}_\text{i} \quad (\text{about } 30\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1} \text{ released})
$$

The bonds between phosphate groups are often called "high-energy bonds", but this is misleading. Breaking any bond **uses** energy; the energy released comes from the products (ADP and Pi) being more stable than ATP and water, so the reaction as a whole releases energy. ADP can be hydrolysed again to AMP, releasing a similar amount.

The reverse reaction, **phosphorylation** of ADP, needs the same amount of energy and is catalysed by **ATP synthase**. Many reactions use ATP by transferring its phosphate to another molecule (phosphorylating it), which makes that molecule more reactive.

## Why ATP is suited to its role

| Feature of ATP | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Energy released in small, manageable amounts | Little is wasted as heat; one ATP matches the needs of one step |
| Hydrolysis is a single, quick reaction catalysed by one enzyme | Energy is available immediately |
| Small and water-soluble | Moves easily within the cell to where energy is needed |
| Easily regenerated from ADP and Pi | Recycled many times; a small pool supplies a large demand |
| Cannot pass out of the cell (charged phosphates) | The cell keeps a supply ready for its own use |
| Used in all cells | A universal link between energy-releasing and energy-using reactions |

ATP is **not** an energy store. A cell holds only a small amount at any moment and remakes it continuously. Long-term stores are glucose, glycogen, starch and lipids.

:::example{title="Why not use glucose directly?"}
Tendai asks why a muscle fibre does not simply "burn" glucose next to each myosin head. **Explain**: oxidising one glucose molecule releases a large amount of energy (about 2,870 kJ per mole) that would be released all at once as heat, far more than one contraction step needs, and it takes many enzyme-controlled steps. Hydrolysing ATP releases about 30 kJ per mole in a single step at exactly the place it is needed. Glucose is a good transport and storage molecule; ATP is a good immediate energy source.
:::

## How ATP is made

### 1. Substrate-linked phosphorylation

A phosphate group is transferred directly from a phosphorylated intermediate to ADP by an enzyme. No membrane is needed. It happens in:

- **glycolysis** in the cytoplasm (when triose phosphate is oxidised to pyruvate);
- the **Krebs cycle** in the mitochondrial matrix (one ATP per turn).

### 2. Chemiosmosis

Most ATP is made this way, across a membrane:

1. Energy from electrons passing along an **electron transport chain** in a membrane is used to pump **protons** across it.
2. This builds up a high concentration of protons on one side: a **proton gradient** (an electrochemical gradient).
3. Protons diffuse back down the gradient through channels in the enzyme **ATP synthase**.
4. Their movement drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and Pi.

| | Mitochondrion | Chloroplast |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane | Inner membrane (cristae) | Thylakoid membrane |
| Protons pumped into | Intermembrane space | Thylakoid space |
| Source of energy for electrons | Oxidation of reduced NAD and reduced FAD | Light absorbed by chlorophyll |
| Name of process | Oxidative phosphorylation | Photophosphorylation |

The details are in the lessons on oxidative phosphorylation and the light-dependent stage.

:::worked-solution[How efficient is aerobic respiration?]
Use these values: complete oxidation of one mole of glucose releases 2,870 kJ; aerobic respiration yields about 32 ATP per glucose; hydrolysis of one mole of ATP releases 30.5 kJ.
1. Energy captured in ATP per mole of glucose $= 32 \times 30.5 = 976\ \text{kJ}$.
2. Efficiency $= \dfrac{976}{2870} \times 100 = 34.0\%$.
3. The other 66% is released as **heat**, which mammals and birds use to keep their body temperature high.
4. Textbooks quote between about 30 and 38 ATP per glucose, so efficiencies of roughly 30-40% are all acceptable when the values are given.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling ATP an energy store"}
ATP is an immediate energy source that is constantly recycled. Energy is **stored** in glycogen, starch and lipids.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that breaking the phosphate bond releases energy"}
Breaking a bond always needs energy. Energy is released overall because the products of hydrolysis are more stable. Write "hydrolysis of ATP releases energy".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ATP contains deoxyribose"}
ATP contains **ribose**, like the nucleotides of RNA.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying energy is produced"}
Energy is never produced or created. Respiration **releases** energy, which is used to make ATP.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 4 test this concept with recall and short explanations. To *explain* why ATP suits its role, link each feature to a benefit ("small and soluble, **so** it moves easily to where energy is needed").
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to *describe* chemiosmosis, give the sequence: electrons release energy → protons pumped across the membrane → gradient → protons diffuse back through ATP synthase → ATP made. For *compare* questions, use a table of the two organelles. ZIMSEC Paper 3 essays often begin with "Describe the structure of ATP and its role".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Cells need energy for anabolic reactions, active transport, movement, bulk transport and (in mammals and birds) body temperature.
- ATP is a phosphorylated nucleotide: adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups.
- Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and Pi by ATPase releases about 30 kJ per mole, in small, usable amounts.
- ATP is small, soluble, quickly hydrolysed and easily regenerated: the universal energy currency, not a store.
- ATP is made by substrate-linked phosphorylation (glycolysis, Krebs cycle) and by chemiosmosis through ATP synthase in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
:::
