## Introduction

Traditional brewers in rural Zimbabwe make sorghum beer by letting yeast ferment a sugary mash, and bakers in Bulawayo rely on yeast to make bread dough rise. A sprinter's leg muscles, a yeast cell in a fermenting mash and the roots of a rice plant in a flooded paddy have something in common: for a time they make ATP without oxygen.

This lesson explains how. You should already know glycolysis (glucose → 2 pyruvate, with a net 2 ATP and 2 reduced NAD) and why the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation stop without oxygen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anaerobic respiration"}
The release of energy from glucose or another organic substrate in the absence of oxygen, in which glycolysis is the only stage that makes ATP and pyruvate is converted into lactate or ethanol to regenerate NAD.
:::

:::definition{term="Lactate fermentation"}
The anaerobic pathway in mammals (and some bacteria) in which pyruvate is reduced to lactate by reduced NAD, catalysed by lactate dehydrogenase.
:::

:::definition{term="Ethanol fermentation"}
The anaerobic pathway in yeast and plants in which pyruvate is decarboxylated to ethanal, which is then reduced to ethanol by reduced NAD.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxygen debt"}
The extra oxygen taken in after exercise, above the resting need, used mainly to oxidise the lactate formed during anaerobic respiration and to restore ATP and oxygen stores.
:::

## Why NAD must be regenerated

Without oxygen, the electron transport chain stops, so reduced NAD cannot be oxidised there. Glycolysis needs **oxidised NAD** to accept hydrogen when triose phosphate is oxidised. A cell holds only a small amount of NAD; once it is all reduced, glycolysis would stop and no ATP at all would be made.

The solution is to pass the hydrogen from reduced NAD to **pyruvate** (or a product of pyruvate). This regenerates NAD so that glycolysis can continue, giving 2 ATP per glucose. That is the whole point of both fermentation pathways: they make no extra ATP themselves.

## Lactate fermentation in mammals

In muscle cells working faster than oxygen can be supplied:

1. Glycolysis produces pyruvate, 2 ATP (net) and reduced NAD.
2. **Pyruvate accepts hydrogen** from reduced NAD and is reduced to **lactate**, catalysed by **lactate dehydrogenase**.
3. **NAD is regenerated** and returns to glycolysis.

$$
\text{pyruvate} + \text{reduced NAD} \rightarrow \text{lactate} + \text{NAD}
$$

Lactate (as lactic acid) lowers the pH of muscle cells and the blood. After exercise:

- Lactate diffuses into the blood and is carried to the **liver**.
- In the liver it is oxidised back to pyruvate. Some pyruvate is respired aerobically to CO₂ and water; most is converted to glucose and glycogen.
- The extra oxygen taken in for this, and for restoring ATP and oxygen bound to myoglobin, is the **oxygen debt**. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high for a while after a race.

The reaction is reversible, so no carbon is lost.

## Ethanol fermentation in yeast and plants

1. Glycolysis produces pyruvate, 2 ATP (net) and reduced NAD.
2. Pyruvate is **decarboxylated** to **ethanal** (2C), releasing CO₂ (enzyme: pyruvate decarboxylase).
3. **Ethanal accepts hydrogen** from reduced NAD and is reduced to **ethanol** (enzyme: alcohol dehydrogenase).
4. **NAD is regenerated** for glycolysis.

$$
\text{pyruvate} \rightarrow \text{ethanal} + \text{CO}_2; \quad \text{ethanal} + \text{reduced NAD} \rightarrow \text{ethanol} + \text{NAD}
$$

Ethanol cannot be converted back by the yeast. It diffuses away, but it is toxic: in a closed fermentation it builds up until it kills the yeast.

| | Lactate fermentation | Ethanol fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| Organisms | Mammals (muscle), some bacteria | Yeast, plants (e.g. roots in waterlogged soil) |
| Hydrogen acceptor | Pyruvate | Ethanal |
| CO₂ released | No | Yes |
| Product | Lactate | Ethanol |
| Reversible in the organism | Yes (in the liver) | No |
| ATP per glucose | 2 (from glycolysis) | 2 (from glycolysis) |

## Why the energy yield is so small

In anaerobic respiration only glycolysis makes ATP. Most of the energy of glucose remains in the lactate or ethanol, which are still energy-rich molecules. Aerobic respiration oxidises glucose completely to CO₂ and water and makes far more ATP, mainly by oxidative phosphorylation.

:::worked-solution[Comparing aerobic and anaerobic ATP yields]
Use 32 ATP per glucose for aerobic respiration and 2 ATP per glucose for anaerobic respiration.
1. Aerobic ÷ anaerobic $= 32 \div 2 = 16$: aerobic respiration makes 16 times as much ATP per glucose.
2. Anaerobic yield as a percentage of aerobic $= \dfrac{2}{32} \times 100 = 6.25\%$.
3. So, to make ATP at the same rate without oxygen, a cell would have to break down glucose about 16 times faster, which is why glycogen in sprinting muscle is used up quickly.
:::

## Rice in flooded paddies

Rice is often grown with its roots submerged, where oxygen diffuses very slowly through water. Rice is adapted by:

- **Aerenchyma**: tissue with large air spaces in the stems and roots, through which oxygen diffuses down from the leaves to the roots.
- **Tolerance of ethanol**: root cells can carry out ethanol fermentation and have high levels of alcohol dehydrogenase, which breaks down ethanol so that it does not reach toxic concentrations.
- **Fast stem growth**: when flooded, stems elongate rapidly so that leaves stay above the water surface and can exchange gases with the air.

## Investigating fermentation by yeast

Rates of fermentation can be compared by measuring CO₂ release: counting bubbles, collecting gas in a syringe or measuring the height of a foam. The yeast suspension is covered with a layer of oil (or the air removed) to keep conditions anaerobic, and kept in a water bath at constant temperature.

:::example{title="Comparing the fermentation of different sugars"}
Nyasha mixed the same volume of yeast suspension with 5% solutions of four sugars at 30 °C and collected the gas released in 10 minutes, three times for each sugar.

| Sugar | Volume 1 / cm³ | Volume 2 / cm³ | Volume 3 / cm³ | Mean volume / cm³ | Mean rate / cm³ min⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glucose | 8.4 | 8.0 | 8.2 | 8.2 | 0.82 |
| Sucrose | 7.1 | 7.5 | 7.3 | 7.3 | 0.73 |
| Maltose | 5.2 | 4.8 | 5.0 | 5.0 | 0.50 |
| Lactose | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.0 | 0.1 | 0.01 |

**Describe:** glucose gives the fastest rate (0.82 cm³ min⁻¹), then sucrose and maltose; lactose gives almost no gas.

**Explain:** glucose enters glycolysis directly. Sucrose and maltose must first be hydrolysed by yeast enzymes (invertase and maltase), which slows fermentation. Baker's yeast has no enzyme to hydrolyse lactose, so it cannot use it. Standardised variables include temperature, volume and concentration of sugar solution, volume and source of yeast, and time.
:::

**Uses of fermentation.** In **baking**, CO₂ from yeast makes dough rise, and the ethanol evaporates during baking. In **brewing**, yeast ferments sugars from malted grain (such as sorghum or barley) to ethanol; CO₂ escapes or makes the drink fizzy.

:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying fermentation makes ATP"}
The conversion of pyruvate to lactate or ethanol makes no ATP. Its role is to **regenerate NAD** so glycolysis can continue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying lactate fermentation releases carbon dioxide"}
Only ethanol fermentation releases CO₂ (when pyruvate is decarboxylated).
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling lactate a waste product that is excreted"}
Lactate is taken to the liver and converted back to pyruvate, then respired or made into glucose and glycogen.
:::

:::

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Without oxygen only glycolysis makes ATP (net 2 per glucose); NAD must be regenerated for it to continue.
- In mammals pyruvate is reduced to lactate; lactate is later oxidised in the liver, repaying the oxygen debt.
- In yeast and plants pyruvate is decarboxylated to ethanal, which is reduced to ethanol; CO₂ is released and the reaction is irreversible.
- The yield is small because glucose is only partly broken down and energy remains in lactate or ethanol.
- Rice survives flooding with aerenchyma, ethanol tolerance and rapid stem growth; yeast fermentation is used in baking and brewing.
:::
