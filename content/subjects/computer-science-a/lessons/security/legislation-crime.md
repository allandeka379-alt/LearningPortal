## Introduction

Nyasha, a form 5 learner in Mutare, receives a message on her phone that appears to be from her mobile-money provider and asks for her PIN. A week later a stranger posts her private photographs on social media. A shop owner discovers that an employee has copied the customer database and sold it to a competitor. These are examples of **computer crime**: crimes that are done using a computer or network, or that are aimed at a computer or the data on it.

Technology alone cannot stop crime. Countries also pass **laws** that define what is a crime, set penalties and protect the victims, and they appoint **regulators** to enforce the rules. This lesson names the main types of computer crime, the control measures that reduce each, the purpose of the law, and the legal frameworks that apply in Zimbabwe, described in outline. It is general information, not legal advice. You should know the threats from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Computer crime"}
An illegal act that is carried out with the help of a computer or network, or that is directed against a computer system or its data.
:::

:::definition{term="Unauthorised access"}
Using a computer system, account or data without the permission of the owner, for example by guessing a password.
:::

:::definition{term="Identity theft"}
Using another person's personal details, such as their name, identity number or account details, without permission, usually to gain money or services.
:::

:::definition{term="Software piracy"}
Copying, sharing or using software without the permission of the copyright owner or without the licence that the owner requires.
:::

:::definition{term="Cybercrime legislation"}
Laws that define computer-related offences, set penalties and procedures, and protect personal data and the rights of victims.
:::

## Types of computer crime and control measures

| Crime | What it is | Control measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Unauthorised access (hacking)** | Entering a system or reading data without permission | Strong passwords, lock-out, two-factor authentication, firewalls, access rights, logging of access |
| **Fraud** | Deceiving someone through a computer to gain money, for example fake online shops or altering account records | Verification of payments, separation of duties, audit logs, staff awareness, secure payment systems |
| **Identity theft** | Using someone else's details to take money or services | Never share PINs, encrypt personal data, check messages before replying, shred documents, monitor accounts |
| **Software piracy** | Illegal copying or use of software | Licence checks and activation, buying licensed or open-source software, audits of installed software, education |
| **Cyberbullying and harassment** | Using messages or social media to threaten, humiliate or harass someone | School policies, reporting tools and blocking, privacy settings, counselling and discipline |
| **Distribution of illegal material** | Sharing material that is against the law, such as child abuse images or material that incites violence | Content filters, monitoring, reporting to the police, clear acceptable-use rules |
| **Data theft** | Copying confidential data, often by an insider or after a hack | Encryption, access control, logging, restricting USB drives, staff contracts |
| **Malware and denial of service used to attack systems** | Deliberately spreading malware or flooding a service | Anti-malware, updates, firewalls, network monitoring |

:::keyfact{title="Reading the table"}
Each control measure either **prevents** the crime (strong passwords, licences), **detects** it (logs, audits, anti-malware scans) or **limits the harm** (encryption, backups). A good answer says which of these a measure does.
:::

## Why computer security and crime laws are needed

- **Define offences.** Without a law, it may be unclear whether copying files or guessing a password is a crime.
- **Deter criminals.** Penalties discourage people from committing offences.
- **Protect individuals.** Laws on personal data tell organisations how they must treat information about people and give people rights, such as to see their data.
- **Protect businesses and trade.** Intellectual property laws protect software and creative work; computer crime laws protect systems that the economy depends on.
- **Cross-border crime.** Computer crime crosses borders, so countries need compatible laws and cooperation in investigation.

## Frameworks that apply in Zimbabwe (outline only)

The names below are given in outline, with no section numbers. A reviewer or teacher should confirm the exact titles and the current content against the official texts.

- **Cyber and Data Protection Act.** Deals with computer-related offences (such as unauthorised access and interference with data and systems) and with the protection of personal data.
- **Criminal law.** The general criminal law covers offences such as fraud and theft, which apply when they are done through computers. Where each computer-related offence is defined is for a reviewer to confirm.
- **Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act.** Protects the owners of creative works, including software, against copying and distribution without permission.
- **ICT regulation.** Regulators such as POTRAZ, the telecommunications regulator, license operators, set conditions for services and have a role in enforcing the rules.

## Applying the ideas

:::example{title="Identifying the crime and the control"}
**Scenario 1.** A technician at a college logs in with a password he found written on a notice board and changes marks. This is **unauthorised access** and also changes data, so it is also **fraud**. Controls: strong passwords that are never written down, access rights so that only the right staff can change marks, and a log that shows who changed what.

**Scenario 2.** A learner sets up a group chat to spread rumours and insults about a classmate. This is **cyberbullying**. Controls: a school policy with clear discipline, reporting tools, blocking and privacy settings, and support for the victim.

**Scenario 3.** A caller who knows a customer's name and identity number persuades a mobile-money agent to move money to another account. This is **identity theft and fraud**, helped by social engineering. Controls: verifying the caller by another method, requiring a PIN or code that cannot be guessed from public details, and staff training.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Counting illegal copies"}
A school buys a licence to install an office suite on 20 computers. An audit finds the suite installed on 35 computers.

1. Unlicensed copies = installed − licensed = 35 − 20 = 15.
2. These 15 copies are software piracy, even if nobody meant to break the law.
3. The school can remove the 15 copies, buy 15 more licences, or move the 15 computers to free or open-source software that allows copying.
4. To prevent it happening again, the school keeps a register of licences, restricts installation to the administrator and audits each term.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating every wrong act as a computer crime"}
A computer crime is illegal. Sharing a rumour that is rude but not unlawful may be unethical without being a crime, though bullying and threats often are.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only technical measures"}
Laws, school and company policies, training and reporting are also control measures. Do not list only firewalls and passwords.
:::

:::mistake{title="Quoting laws, sections or penalties from memory"}
The syllabus asks for the purpose of the law and its frameworks in outline. Inventing section numbers or penalties loses marks.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks you to *list* or *describe* types of computer crime and their control measures, to *state the purpose* of legislation, and to *identify* the crime in a scenario. Name the crime precisely, then give a control measure that matches it, and say whether it prevents, detects or limits harm. For "explain why legislation is needed" give two or three developed points. Paper 2 and Cambridge do not examine this topic.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Computer crime is illegal activity that uses a computer or network, or that targets a system or its data.
- Main types are unauthorised access, fraud, identity theft, software piracy, cyberbullying, illegal material and data theft; each has matching control measures.
- Controls prevent, detect or limit the harm of a crime, and include laws, policies and training as well as technology.
- Laws define offences, deter criminals, protect people and businesses and support cooperation across borders.
- In Zimbabwe the Cyber and Data Protection Act, the criminal law and the Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act apply, with ICT regulators such as POTRAZ; know them in outline only.
:::
