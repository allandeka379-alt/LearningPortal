## Introduction

Chipo in Gweru makes phone covers and sells them on a social media page. A customer in Harare pays by mobile money, Chipo sends the cover by bus, and the customer sends a thank-you message. Chipo has no shop, no rent for a town-centre building and no staff, yet she has customers in several towns. She is doing **e-commerce**. But after three months she is not sure whether the business makes money. Selling is only half of running a business: she also needs to understand her costs, her profit and her cash.

This lesson explains e-business and e-commerce and their models, the payment systems that make them work in Zimbabwe, benefits and risks for a small business, and the financial basics a business owner needs: income, costs, profit, break-even and cash flow. All the money examples are in United States dollars. You should know how the internet and mobile networks work in outline.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="E-business"}
Running all the activities of a business using electronic means and networks, including buying, selling, serving customers, keeping records and working with suppliers.
:::

:::definition{term="E-commerce"}
The buying and selling of goods and services over the internet or other electronic networks. It is one part of e-business.
:::

:::definition{term="Profit"}
Revenue (money received from sales) minus costs. If costs are larger than revenue the business makes a loss.
:::

:::definition{term="Break-even point"}
The number of units that must be sold so that revenue exactly covers all costs, with neither profit nor loss.
:::

:::definition{term="Cash flow"}
The movement of money into a business (cash in) and out of it (cash out) over a period of time.
:::

## E-commerce models

| Model | Who sells to whom | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **B2C** (business to consumer) | A business sells to individual customers | An online shop selling clothes to the public |
| **B2B** (business to business) | A business sells to another business | A supplier sells computer parts to a repair shop |
| **C2C** (consumer to consumer) | Individuals sell to each other, often through a platform | A person sells a used phone through an online notice board |

## Payment systems

- **Mobile money** (for example EcoCash or OneMoney in Zimbabwe): the customer pays from a phone account. It is widely used, needs no bank account and is quick, but fees may apply and the network must be working.
- **Cards** (debit and credit, often through a payment gateway such as Paynow): suitable for customers with bank accounts; the seller needs a secure checkout and the customer needs to trust it.
- **Bank transfer**: suitable for larger payments between businesses.
- **Cash on delivery**: the customer pays when the goods arrive; it avoids the problem of trust but needs a reliable delivery person and cash handling.

Payment systems should encrypt the details in transit (look for `https`), and sellers should never ask customers to send card numbers or PINs by chat.

:::worked-solution{title="A mobile money fee"}
An agent charges 1.5% of the amount sent, rounded to the nearest cent. Tendai sends US\$24 to a supplier.

1. Fee = 24 × 1.5 ÷ 100 = US\$0.36.
2. Tendai pays 24 + 0.36 = US\$24.36 in total; the supplier receives US\$24.
3. For a small business that makes many small sales, such fees are a running cost to be counted when setting prices.
:::

## Benefits and risks for a small business

| Benefits | Risks |
|---|---|
| Reach customers in many towns without a shop | Fraud, fake payments or scams; customers may not trust an unknown seller |
| Lower costs: less rent and fewer staff | Delivery problems, damaged or late goods |
| Open at all times and quick to start | Dependence on internet, mobile networks and electricity |
| Records of sales kept automatically | Customer data must be protected |
| Easy to advertise and to get customer feedback | Competition from sellers everywhere |

## Financial literacy for an ICT business

- **Revenue** (income) = price × units sold.
- **Fixed costs** do not change with the number of units sold in a month: rent, internet subscription, wages.
- **Variable costs** change with the number of units: the materials and delivery for each item.
- **Profit** = revenue − (fixed costs + variable costs).
- **Contribution per unit** = price − variable cost per unit.
- **Break-even units** = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.

:::worked-solution{title="Break-even for Chipo's phone covers"}
Chipo sells covers for US\$8. Each cover costs US\$3 in materials and delivery (variable). Her fixed costs are US\$300 a month (rent for a work space, internet and wages).

1. Contribution per cover = 8 − 3 = US\$5.
2. Break-even = 300 ÷ 5 = 60 units. She must sell 60 covers a month to cover her costs.
3. Check with a table.

| Units sold | Revenue | Variable costs | Fixed costs | Profit or loss |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| 0 | US\$0 | US\$0 | US\$300 | −US\$300 |
| 30 | US\$240 | US\$90 | US\$300 | −US\$150 |
| 60 | US\$480 | US\$180 | US\$300 | US\$0 |
| 90 | US\$720 | US\$270 | US\$300 | US\$150 |

4. At 60 units revenue (US\$480) equals total costs (180 + 300). Every cover above 60 adds US\$5 of profit.
:::

**Cash flow** is different from profit. A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash if customers pay late or if it must buy stock before it sells.

:::worked-solution{title="A three-month cash flow forecast"}
Chipo starts January with US\$200 of cash.

| Month | Opening cash | Cash in | Cash out | Net cash flow | Closing cash |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| January | US\$200 | US\$400 | US\$450 | −US\$50 | US\$150 |
| February | US\$150 | US\$700 | US\$520 | US\$180 | US\$330 |
| March | US\$330 | US\$500 | US\$640 | −US\$140 | US\$190 |

1. Net cash flow = cash in − cash out; closing cash = opening cash + net cash flow.
2. The closing cash of one month is the opening cash of the next.
3. Cash falls in January and March, so she must make sure it never reaches zero: she could delay buying stock or ask for part payment before she delivers.
:::

:::example{title="Choosing a model and a payment method"}
A school in Mutare wants to sell its farm vegetables to local restaurants and to parents. Sales to restaurants are **B2B**: orders are larger, repeated and can be invoiced. Sales to parents are **B2C**: orders are small and most parents prefer mobile money. The school accepts mobile money for parents and bank transfer for restaurants, checks every payment before delivery, and keeps a record of all sales to calculate profit each month.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing revenue and profit"}
Revenue is all the money received from sales. Profit is what is left after all costs have been taken away.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting fixed costs in break-even"}
Break-even uses the contribution per unit (price − variable cost), not the price. Dividing fixed costs by the price gives a break-even that is too low.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating profit and cash flow as the same"}
A sale on credit increases profit now but brings cash in later. A business can have a profit and no cash.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks you to *state* the models, *describe* payment methods, *explain* benefits and risks to a small business, and *calculate* profit, break-even or cash flow. Name the model and give an example, not just the letters. In a calculation write the formula, substitute the numbers, and give the unit (US dollars or units). For a cash flow table, carry the closing cash forward as the next month's opening cash.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- E-business uses electronic means for all business activities; e-commerce is the buying and selling part.
- The models are B2C, B2B and C2C; payment can be by mobile money, cards, bank transfer or cash on delivery.
- E-commerce widens the market and cuts costs but brings fraud, delivery, connectivity and data protection risks.
- Profit = revenue − costs; break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).
- Cash flow tracks cash in and out; closing cash = opening cash + cash in − cash out.
:::
