## Introduction

Rudo has finished Form 6 and has noticed that the teachers and learners in her part of Gweru wait weeks to repair broken phones and laptops. She thinks she can start a repair shop, but she needs money to buy tools and stock, and a bank or an uncle will want to know if the idea can work. A **business proposal** answers those questions in an organised document: what problem will be solved, how, for whom, at what cost and with what risks.

In the ZIMSEC Enterprising topic you must be able to design a viable ICT business such as a repair shop, a web design service or a school software supplier, to present it, and to judge its viability using simple financial figures. This lesson follows one example from idea to numbers. You should already know the basic terms of e-business and marketing from the earlier lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business proposal"}
A written plan that describes a business idea, shows that there is a market for it and that it can be operated and paid for, and asks for support such as a loan, a partner or permission.
:::

:::definition{term="Viability"}
Whether a business can cover its costs and earn enough profit to continue and to reward its owner and investors.
:::

:::definition{term="Start-up costs"}
The money spent once, before or when a business opens, on equipment, stock, registration and launch advertising.
:::

:::definition{term="Payback period"}
The time that it takes for the profit of a business to repay the money invested in starting it.
:::

:::definition{term="Return on investment (ROI)"}
The profit earned in a year as a percentage of the money invested: ROI = annual profit ÷ amount invested × 100.
:::

## Parts of a business proposal

| Part | What it contains | Repair shop example |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive summary** | One page that sums up the whole proposal, written last | A phone and laptop repair shop near a school, needing US\$2,400 to start |
| **Problem or opportunity** | The need that exists | Broken devices take weeks to repair and many families cannot afford new ones |
| **Solution** | The product or service | Same-week repairs, screen and battery replacement and a guarantee |
| **Market** | Who the customers are, how many, who competes, how to reach them | Learners, teachers and families; two competitors in town; promotion at schools |
| **Operations** | Where and how the work is done | A rented room, opening hours, how parts are ordered, record keeping |
| **Staffing** | Who does what, skills needed | Owner (technician) and a part-time assistant |
| **Finance** | Start-up costs, monthly budget, profit, payback | The figures below |
| **Risks** | What could go wrong and what will be done about it | Theft, power cuts, fewer customers than planned |

## Designing an ICT business

Choose a need that you understand and can serve, with skills that you have or can hire. Typical ICT businesses are a repair shop, a web design service, a computer training centre, a mobile app or software supplier to schools, and a printing and photocopying shop. For each, check that people need the service and can pay, that you can provide it, and that it earns more than it costs.

## Finance: working out viability

:::worked-solution{title="Start-up costs"}
| Start-up cost | Amount |
|:-:|:-:|
| Tools and test equipment | US\$900 |
| Furniture and shelving | US\$350 |
| Opening stock of spare parts | US\$650 |
| Registration and licences | US\$200 |
| Launch advertising | US\$300 |
| **Total** | **US\$2,400** |

1. Total = 900 + 350 + 650 + 200 + 300 = US\$2,400.
2. This is the amount that has to be raised from savings, family or a loan.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Monthly budget, profit and payback"}
Rudo expects 70 repair jobs a month at an average charge of US\$14.

| Item | Per month |
|:-:|:-:|
| Revenue (70 jobs at US\$14) | US\$980 |
| Rent | US\$220 |
| Electricity and internet | US\$70 |
| Spare parts used | US\$260 |
| Part-time assistant | US\$200 |
| Total costs | US\$750 |
| **Profit** | **US\$230** |

1. Revenue = 70 × 14 = US\$980.
2. Total costs = 220 + 70 + 260 + 200 = US\$750.
3. Profit = 980 − 750 = US\$230 a month.
4. Payback period = 2,400 ÷ 230 = 10.4 months, which is about 11 months.
5. Annual profit = 230 × 12 = US\$2,760, and ROI = 2,760 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 115%.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Testing the plan: what if there are fewer customers?"}
A business plan must also test what happens if the forecast is too hopeful. Suppose only 60 jobs a month are done and costs stay the same.

1. Revenue = 60 × 14 = US\$840.
2. Profit = 840 − 750 = US\$90 a month.
3. Payback period = 2,400 ÷ 90 = 26.7 months, more than two years.
4. The shop still makes a profit but is much riskier: Rudo should plan how to win more customers, for example school contracts, or cut costs by sharing a room.
:::

A reader will ask whether the figures are believable. Base the numbers on evidence: prices charged by competitors, quotations from suppliers, and the answers of customers you have asked. State each assumption (for example, "70 jobs a month") and say where it came from. Round figures sensibly, show the working in an appendix and keep a margin for the unexpected, because most new businesses earn less in their first months than their owners forecast.

## Risks and how to reduce them

| Risk | Reduction |
|---|---|
| Fewer customers than expected | Market research first, promotion at schools, flexible opening hours |
| Theft or damage of stock | A secure room, insurance, a stock record |
| Power cuts | A surge protector or small uninterruptible power supply, work that does not need power during outages |
| Customers' data on repaired devices | A written privacy rule and never copying data without permission |
| Late payment | Payment on collection, mobile money |

## Presenting the proposal

A good presentation is short, clear and honest. Start with the problem, show the solution and the market, then the numbers and the risks, and end with what you are asking for. Use a table or chart for the finance, speak to the questions the audience will ask (how much? when is it repaid? what if it fails?) and be prepared to defend each assumption.

:::example{title="Evaluating the viability of a second idea"}
Tendai proposes a web design service in Mutare. He needs US\$800 for a computer and licences. He expects a profit of US\$100 a month. Payback = 800 ÷ 100 = 8 months and annual profit = 1,200, which is an ROI of 150%. Compared with the repair shop, Tendai's plan repays faster because it costs less to start, but its profit is smaller in total, and his income depends on getting a few clients. A reviewer would ask for evidence that clients exist.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC only"}
This is a ZIMSEC Enterprising topic (business proposal development, designing a viable ICT business, presenting and evaluating it). Cambridge does not examine it. The formulas for payback period and ROI above are the ones to use unless an exam question gives its own.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using revenue instead of profit"}
Payback and ROI use *profit*, not revenue. Revenue of US\$980 a month is not what the owner keeps.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out start-up costs or risks"}
A proposal with no start-up figures or no risks is not believable. Show every cost and say what could go wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing the executive summary first"}
The summary is a short version of the finished proposal. Write it last so that it matches the details.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 1 asks you to *list* the parts of a proposal, to *design* an ICT business by describing the problem, solution, market and staffing, to *calculate* profit, payback or ROI from a table, and to *evaluate* viability. For calculations write the formula, substitute, and give units (US dollars, months, percent). For evaluation compare the result with a standard such as a payback of under a year, and say what could change it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A business proposal has an executive summary, problem, solution, market, operations, staffing, finance and risks.
- Choose an ICT business that meets a real need, that you can provide and that customers can pay for.
- Profit = revenue − costs; payback period = start-up cost ÷ monthly profit; ROI = annual profit ÷ amount invested × 100.
- Test the plan by changing an assumption, such as the number of customers, and list risks with ways to reduce them.
- Present the proposal briefly and honestly, ending with what you are asking for.
:::
