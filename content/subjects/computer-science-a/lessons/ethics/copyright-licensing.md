## Introduction

Farai spends three months writing a program that helps farmers in Chinhoyi keep records of their harvests. A friend copies it onto a memory stick and gives it to twenty other people. Farai receives nothing for his work, and he decides not to write another program. If copying were always allowed, few people would invest the time needed to create software, music, books and designs. **Copyright** and other forms of **intellectual property** protection exist to prevent this: they give creators control over their work for a period and a way to be rewarded.

This lesson explains what copyright protects and why it is needed, the different kinds of software licence and how to choose between them, what piracy and plagiarism are and how they are detected, and, for ZIMSEC, patents and trademarks. You should already know the meaning of software piracy from the lesson on computer crime.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Intellectual property"}
Creations of the mind, such as software, writing, music, designs and inventions, which the law lets the creator own and control.
:::

:::definition{term="Copyright"}
The legal right of the creator of an original work to control who may copy, change, distribute or perform it. It arises automatically when the work is created and lasts for a set period.
:::

:::definition{term="Software licence"}
A legal agreement that states how a piece of software may be used, copied, changed and shared.
:::

:::definition{term="Plagiarism"}
Presenting someone else's work or ideas as your own without acknowledging the source.
:::

:::definition{term="Patent"}
A legal right, granted for a limited time, that stops others from making, using or selling a new invention without the owner's permission. It protects the idea of how something works.
:::

:::definition{term="Trademark"}
A name, logo or sign that identifies the maker of goods or services and that others may not use without permission.
:::

## Copyright and the need for legislation

Copyright protects an original work in its fixed form: program code, a document, a photograph, a song. It does not protect a bare idea or method. Laws on copyright (in Zimbabwe the Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act, described in outline) are needed because digital copies are perfect, cost almost nothing and spread worldwide in seconds. The law:

- lets creators earn a living, which encourages new work;
- makes copying and selling copies without permission an offence;
- sets out what is allowed without permission, such as quoting a short piece with acknowledgement for study or criticism;
- gives the owner a way to take legal action.

## Types of software licence

| Licence | What the user may do | Typical conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial (proprietary)** | Use the program as the licence allows | Pay a fee; source code is not provided; copying and sharing are restricted |
| **Site licence** | Install on any number of computers at one site | One fixed fee; limited to the site or organisation |
| **Shareware** | Try the program for a limited time or with limited features | Pay to continue or to unlock the full program; copying the trial is allowed |
| **Freeware** | Use at no cost | Source code is usually not provided and changes are not allowed |
| **Free software** (as defined by the Free Software Foundation) | Run, study, change and share the program | Source code is available; those who share must pass on the same freedoms |
| **Open source** (as defined by the Open Source Initiative) | Use, study, change and share the source code | Conditions differ between open-source licences, but the source must be available |

:::keyfact{title="Free does not always mean free of cost"}
In *free software* "free" means freedom to use, study, change and share, not price. *Freeware* costs nothing but usually gives no right to change or share the program.
:::

:::worked-solution{title="Choosing between an individual and a site licence"}
A program costs US\$25 for each computer, or US\$400 for a site licence that covers every computer at the school.

| Computers | Individual licences (US\$) | Site licence (US\$) | Cheaper |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| 10 | 250 | 400 | Individual |
| 16 | 400 | 400 | Same |
| 30 | 750 | 400 | Site |

1. Individual cost = number of computers × 25, for example 30 × 25 = 750.
2. The break-even number is 400 ÷ 25 = 16 computers: at 16 computers the two options cost the same.
3. A school with 30 computers should buy the site licence. A school with 10 should buy individual licences, unless it expects to grow beyond 16.
:::

:::example{title="Justifying a licence for a situation"}
**A rural clinic with two old computers and no money for software** needs a word processor and spreadsheet. *Open-source* office software is suitable: there is no fee, it can be installed on both computers legally and the clinic can ask local volunteers to help adapt it.

**A software company that sells a bookkeeping program** wants to be paid for each customer. A *commercial licence* is suitable: it protects revenue, restricts copying and funds development and support.

**A student who writes a small game and wants it to spread quickly** might release it as *shareware* with a limited number of levels, so that people can try it and pay for the rest.
:::

## Piracy, plagiarism and detection

**Software piracy** is copying or using software without the licence. It can lead to fines, legal action, the risk of malware in cracked copies and no access to updates or support. Companies detect it with product activation, licence keys that are checked online, and audits of installed software.

**Plagiarism** is passing off someone else's work as your own. A learner who copies a paragraph from a web site into a project report without a reference commits plagiarism, and so does a programmer who submits code written by another person. Teachers and examiners use **similarity-checking software**, which compares a submitted document with a large store of documents and reports the share of text that matches. A high percentage is a warning to investigate, not proof by itself.

:::worked-solution{title="Reading a similarity report"}
A project report has 800 words. The checker finds that 176 words match other sources.

1. Similarity = 176 ÷ 800 × 100 = 22%.
2. The teacher looks at the matches and sees that 40 of the matched words are a quotation with the source acknowledged.
3. Excluding these, the unacknowledged match is 176 − 40 = 136 words, which is 136 ÷ 800 × 100 = 17%.
4. The teacher judges whether the remaining 17% is common phrases or copied passages. The percentage guides the decision but does not make it.
:::

## Patents, trademarks and respect for others' work

**Patents** protect new inventions (for example a new type of sensor), not the copyright in a program's code. **Trademarks** protect names and logos so that customers are not misled. ZIMSEC includes both under intellectual property rights.

Developers respect others' work by: using only software they are licensed to use; following the conditions of any licence on code or libraries they include; crediting sources; not copying code, images or music without permission; and writing their own work.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards need the need for copyright law, the licence types (Cambridge names the Free Software Foundation, the Open Source Initiative, shareware and commercial software), the ability to justify a licence for a situation, and piracy and plagiarism with their detection. Patents and trademarks are ZIMSEC detail. Do not quote Zimbabwean section numbers.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that free software is always without cost"}
"Free" in free software refers to freedom. Some free software is sold, and some freeware is free of cost but cannot be changed or shared.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying that anything on the internet can be copied"}
Most works on the internet are still protected by copyright. Copy only where the licence allows, and acknowledge the source.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a similarity percentage as proof of plagiarism"}
A percentage is a guide. Quotations that are acknowledged, common phrases and references raise the figure without being plagiarism.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Cambridge Paper 1 ask you to *state why copyright law is needed*, to *describe or compare* licences, and to *justify a licence for a described situation*. Pick the licence, then give two reasons that fit the scenario (cost, source code, number of computers, need to be paid). For piracy or plagiarism, *state* the meaning and *describe* how it is detected (activation, audits, similarity checkers). Calculations compare costs: show the break-even number.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Copyright gives creators control over copying and distribution of an original work; laws are needed because digital copying is easy and cheap.
- Licences include commercial, site, shareware, freeware, free software and open source; choose by cost, source code access, number of computers and need for support.
- Piracy is using or copying software without the licence; plagiarism is presenting another's work as your own.
- Piracy is detected by activation and audits; plagiarism by similarity-checking software, whose percentage is a guide, not proof.
- Patents protect inventions and trademarks protect names and logos (ZIMSEC detail).
:::
