## Introduction

When a heavy haulage truck rumbles past a house in Bulawayo you may feel the floor shake before you hear the engine clearly. Both are mechanical waves, but the particles that carry them move in different ways. Earthquake recordings show the same split: the first waves to arrive (P-waves) push and pull the rock along their direction of travel, and later waves (S-waves) shake it from side to side. Knowing which kind of wave you are dealing with tells you how it will behave: only one kind can be polarised, and only one kind forms compressions.

You should already know the terms amplitude, wavelength, period and frequency and the wave equation $v = f\lambda$ from the lesson on progressive waves.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transverse wave"}
A wave in which the particles of the medium (or the field) oscillate at right angles to the direction in which the wave transfers energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Longitudinal wave"}
A wave in which the particles of the medium oscillate parallel to the direction in which the wave transfers energy.
:::

:::definition{term="Compression and rarefaction"}
In a longitudinal wave, a compression is a region where the particles are closer together than normal (higher pressure); a rarefaction is a region where they are further apart than normal (lower pressure).
:::

:::definition{term="Polarisation"}
A wave is plane polarised when its oscillations take place in one plane only, the plane containing the direction of travel.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Examples to learn"}
Transverse: waves on a stretched string or rope, water ripples (approximately), all electromagnetic waves, seismic S-waves. Longitudinal: sound in any medium, waves along a slinky pushed and pulled along its length, seismic P-waves.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the comparison, the graphs and polarisation as evidence for transverse waves (ZIMSEC Form 5; Cambridge AS 7.2 and 7.5). Malus's law, $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$, is Cambridge only and has its own lesson.
:::

## Comparing the two kinds

| Feature | Transverse | Longitudinal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction of oscillation | Perpendicular to energy transfer | Parallel to energy transfer |
| Shape features | Crests and troughs | Compressions and rarefactions |
| Can be polarised? | Yes | No |
| Needs a medium? | Mechanical ones do; electromagnetic waves do not | Yes (sound cannot cross a vacuum) |
| Examples | Rope, string, light, radio, S-waves | Sound, ultrasound, P-waves |

Both kinds obey $v = f\lambda$, show reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference, and transfer energy without net transfer of the medium.

## Graphs of a longitudinal wave

For a longitudinal wave, displacement is measured **along** the direction of travel, so a displacement-distance graph does not look like the wave. Take displacement to the right as positive. A positive value means the particle is pushed towards larger $x$; a negative value towards smaller $x$.

The table is a snapshot of a sound wave of amplitude $5.0\ \mu\text{m}$ and wavelength $0.60\ \text{m}$ travelling to the right.

| $x\,/\,\text{m}$ | $s\,/\,\mu\text{m}$ | particle displaced |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0.000 | 0.0 | not displaced |
| 0.075 | 3.5 | right |
| 0.150 | 5.0 | right |
| 0.225 | 3.5 | right |
| 0.300 | 0.0 | not displaced |
| 0.375 | −3.5 | left |
| 0.450 | −5.0 | left |
| 0.525 | −3.5 | left |
| 0.600 | 0.0 | not displaced |

The graph of $s$ against $x$ is a sine curve. Particles just left of $x = 0.300\ \text{m}$ are pushed right and those just right of it are pushed left, so they crowd together: **a compression is centred at $x = 0.300\ \text{m}$**, where the curve crosses zero going downwards. At $x = 0$ and $x = 0.600\ \text{m}$ the neighbours move apart: **rarefactions**. Compressions and rarefactions sit at points of **zero** displacement, not at the peaks; the particles at the peaks are displaced most but are at normal spacing.

:::worked-solution[Locating compressions from a snapshot]
Use the table above.

1. A compression is where displacement changes from positive (right) to negative (left) as $x$ increases: $x = 0.300\ \text{m}$.
2. A rarefaction is where displacement changes from negative to positive: $x = 0.600\ \text{m}$ (and $x = 0$).
3. The distance from a compression to the next rarefaction is $0.600 - 0.300 = 0.300\ \text{m} = \lambda/2$; adjacent compressions are one wavelength, $0.60\ \text{m}$, apart.
:::

A displacement-time graph for one particle in a longitudinal wave looks exactly like the transverse case: a sine curve whose repeat time is the period. Only the meaning of "displacement" changes.

:::worked-solution[Spacing of compressions in a sound wave]
A loudspeaker produces a note of frequency $850\ \text{Hz}$ in air where sound travels at $340\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

1. Wavelength: $\lambda = \dfrac{v}{f} = \dfrac{340}{850} = 0.40\ \text{m}$.
2. Adjacent compressions are one wavelength apart: $0.40\ \text{m}$.
3. A compression and the next rarefaction are half a wavelength apart: $0.20\ \text{m}$.
4. Compressions pass a fixed microphone once every period, $T = 1/f = 1.18 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}$.
:::

## Polarisation as evidence for transverse waves

A transverse wave on a rope can be made to oscillate vertically, horizontally or in any plane containing the rope. Pass the rope through a vertical slot in a fence: vertical oscillations pass through, horizontal ones are stopped. The wave beyond the slot oscillates in one plane only: it is **plane polarised**.

A longitudinal wave oscillates along its direction of travel, so there is no choice of plane; a slot affects all orientations equally. **Only transverse waves can be polarised.** Light passing through one polarising filter and then a second, rotated filter dims to almost nothing when the filters are crossed at $90^\circ$; this is the evidence that light is transverse. Sound cannot be polarised, which confirms that it is longitudinal.

Microwaves from a school transmitter are already polarised. A metal grille of parallel rods placed in the beam absorbs the wave when the rods are parallel to the electric-field oscillation and lets it through when they are perpendicular.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Compressions at the peaks"}
On a displacement-distance graph of a longitudinal wave, compressions and rarefactions are at the points of **zero displacement**, where the curve crosses the axis. The peaks show maximum displacement at normal spacing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling sound transverse because the graph is wavy"}
The sine-shaped graph is a plot of displacement; the particles themselves move back and forth along the direction of travel.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying electromagnetic waves need a medium"}
Light and radio waves cross the vacuum of space. Only mechanical waves need a medium.
:::

:::mistake{title="Distance between compression and rarefaction"}
It is half a wavelength, not a whole wavelength.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
Tested in ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2, often with a graph to interpret or as the first part of a polarisation question.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*State the difference*: give the direction of oscillation **relative to the direction of energy transfer** for both kinds. *Explain why sound cannot be polarised*: say that its oscillations are along the direction of travel, so there is no single plane to select. *Mark the position of a compression*: use the zero-crossing where neighbouring particles move towards each other.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transverse: oscillation perpendicular to energy transfer; longitudinal: parallel.
- Electromagnetic waves and waves on strings are transverse; sound is longitudinal.
- Longitudinal waves have compressions and rarefactions, half a wavelength apart.
- On a displacement-distance graph of a longitudinal wave, compressions and rarefactions are at zero displacement.
- Only transverse waves can be polarised; this is the evidence that light is transverse.
:::
