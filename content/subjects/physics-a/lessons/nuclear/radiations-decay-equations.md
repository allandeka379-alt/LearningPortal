## Introduction

Smoke detectors in Harare offices contain a tiny alpha source; hospitals in Bulawayo use gamma rays to sterilise instruments and treat tumours; paper mills control the thickness of their product with beta sources. Each application depends on the different properties of the three main types of nuclear radiation, and each needs safe handling because the radiation ionises the atoms it passes through, including those in living cells.

This lesson describes what alpha, beta and gamma radiation are, how they differ in ionisation, penetration and deflection, how to write balanced decay equations, and why beta decay needs an extra, almost undetectable particle: the neutrino.

You should already know the nuclear model of the atom, nuclide notation ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$, and conservation of nucleon number and charge.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Radioactive decay"}
The spontaneous emission of radiation (an alpha particle, a beta particle and/or a gamma-ray photon) by an unstable nucleus, which becomes a more stable nucleus.
:::

:::definition{term="Ionising radiation"}
Radiation with enough energy to remove electrons from atoms it passes, leaving ion pairs.
:::

:::definition{term="Antiparticle"}
A particle with the same mass as its partner particle but the opposite charge (and other opposite quantum numbers). The positron is the antiparticle of the electron.
:::

:::definition{term="Background radiation"}
The ionising radiation present at all times from natural sources (rocks and soil, radon gas, cosmic rays, food) and artificial sources (medical X-rays, fallout).
:::

:::keyfact{title="The radiations"}
| Radiation | Nature | Charge | Mass | Typical speed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ | $+2e$ | about $4\ \text{u}$ | up to about $0.05c$ |
| $\beta^-$ | electron ${}^{0}_{-1}\text{e}$ | $-e$ | about $\tfrac{1}{1840}\ \text{u}$ | up to about $0.99c$ |
| $\beta^+$ | positron ${}^{0}_{+1}\text{e}$ | $+e$ | about $\tfrac{1}{1840}\ \text{u}$ | up to about $0.99c$ |
| $\gamma$ | electromagnetic photon | 0 | 0 | $c$ |
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}

:::

## Ionising and penetrating power

| Radiation | Ionisation | Stopped by | Range in air |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | very strong (about $10^4$ ion pairs per mm of air) | a sheet of paper or a few cm of air | a few cm |
| $\beta$ | moderate (about $10^2$ per mm) | a few mm of aluminium | up to about 1 m |
| $\gamma$ | weak | intensity reduced by several cm of lead or metres of concrete; never stopped completely | very long |

The two properties are linked: a radiation that ionises strongly loses its energy quickly and so is the least penetrating. Alpha particles are heavy and doubly charged, interact with almost every atom they pass, and lose all their energy within a few centimetres of air. Gamma photons are uncharged and interact rarely.

## Deflection in electric and magnetic fields

- **Alpha** and **beta-plus** are deflected one way, **beta-minus** the opposite way; **gamma** is undeflected.
- In a magnetic field each charged particle follows a circular arc ($r = mv/Bq$). Beta particles are deflected far more than alpha particles because their charge-to-mass ratio is thousands of times larger (about 3600 times for a positron compared with an alpha particle).
- Beta particles from one source spread into a fan of different curvatures because they have a **range of speeds**; alpha particles from one nuclide follow the same arc.

## Decay equations

| Decay | Change in nucleus | General equation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | $A$ falls by 4, $Z$ falls by 2 | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\text{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha$ |
| $\beta^-$ | a neutron becomes a proton: $Z$ rises by 1 | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z+1}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{-1}\text{e} + \bar{\nu}_e$ |
| $\beta^+$ | a proton becomes a neutron: $Z$ falls by 1 | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z-1}\text{Y} + {}^{0}_{+1}\text{e} + \nu_e$ |
| $\gamma$ | an excited nucleus loses energy: $A$ and $Z$ unchanged | ${}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^* \rightarrow {}^{A}_{Z}\text{X} + \gamma$ |

Gamma emission usually follows alpha or beta decay, when the daughter nucleus is left in an excited state.

## Neutrinos and the beta energy spectrum

Alpha particles from one nuclide have **discrete** energies: every alpha from radium-226 that leaves the daughter in its ground state has the same kinetic energy, because the decay energy is shared between just two bodies and momentum conservation fixes the split.

Beta particles from one nuclide have a **continuous** range of kinetic energies, from almost zero up to a maximum. The relative numbers of beta particles from a beta-minus emitter with a maximum energy of $0.546\ \text{MeV}$ have this shape (illustrative):

| $E_k\,/\,\text{MeV}$ | 0.00 | 0.05 | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.40 | 0.50 | 0.55 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| relative number | 0 | 87 | 100 | 85 | 53 | 21 | 2.4 | 0 |

The curve rises from zero to a peak at low energy and falls to zero at the maximum energy. Since each decay releases the same total energy, a third particle must carry away the difference: the **electron antineutrino** in beta-minus decay and the **electron neutrino** in beta-plus decay. Neutrinos are uncharged, have almost no mass and hardly interact with matter, which is why they were detected only decades after they were predicted.

## Safe handling

- Store sources in lead-lined boxes; handle them with tongs, never with fingers.
- Keep as far away as practicable (intensity of gamma falls with distance) and minimise exposure time.
- Point sources away from people; never eat, drink or apply cosmetics near them.
- Alpha emitters are most dangerous **inside** the body (inhaled or swallowed), because all their energy is deposited in a small volume of tissue.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[An alpha decay]
Radium-226 (${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra}$) decays by alpha emission to radon. Write the equation.

1. Nucleon number: $226 - 4 = 222$. Proton number: $88 - 2 = 86$.
2. ${}^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha$.
3. Check: $226 = 222 + 4$ and $88 = 86 + 2$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Counting decays in a series]
Uranium-238 (${}^{238}_{92}\text{U}$) decays through a series of alpha and beta-minus decays to lead-206 (${}^{206}_{82}\text{Pb}$). How many of each?

1. Only alpha decay changes $A$: $(238 - 206)/4 = 8$ alpha decays.
2. Eight alpha decays reduce $Z$ by 16, from 92 to 76.
3. Each beta-minus decay raises $Z$ by 1: $82 - 76 = 6$ beta-minus decays.
:::

:::worked-solution[Speed of an alpha particle]
An alpha particle (mass $4.0\ \text{u}$) is emitted with kinetic energy $4.8\ \text{MeV}$. Find its speed.

1. $E_k = 4.8 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 7.68 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$.
2. $m = 4.0 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$.
3. $v = \sqrt{2E_k/m} = 1.52 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, about 5% of the speed of light.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Beta particles come from the electron shells"}
A beta-minus particle is created in the **nucleus** when a neutron changes into a proton. It is not one of the orbiting electrons.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the (anti)neutrino"}
A complete beta-minus equation includes $\bar{\nu}_e$; a beta-plus equation includes $\nu_e$. Without it, energy (and lepton number) does not balance.
:::

:::mistake{title="More ionising means more penetrating"}
The opposite: strong ionisation uses up the energy quickly, so alpha is both the most ionising and the least penetrating.
:::

:::mistake{title="Gamma is completely absorbed by lead"}
Lead reduces gamma intensity exponentially; it never stops all of it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- $\alpha$: ${}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$, $+2e$, strongly ionising, stopped by paper; $\beta^-$: electron, $-e$; $\beta^+$: positron, $+e$; $\gamma$: photon, uncharged, weakly ionising, most penetrating.
- Alpha: $A - 4$, $Z - 2$. Beta-minus: $Z + 1$ with an antineutrino. Beta-plus: $Z - 1$ with a neutrino. Gamma: no change in $A$ or $Z$.
- Antiparticles have the same mass and opposite charge; the positron is the antielectron.
- Alpha energies are discrete; beta energies are continuous because an (anti)neutrino shares the energy.
- Background radiation is always present; handle sources with tongs, distance, short times and shielding.
:::
