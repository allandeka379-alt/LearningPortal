## Introduction

You can hear a radio playing in the next room through an open door even when you cannot see the radio. Sound bends round the edges of the doorway; light, passing through the same doorway, travels almost in straight lines and casts a sharp shadow. In the hilly country around Mutare, a long-wave or medium-wave radio station can still be heard in a valley where an FM signal or a phone signal fades. All of these are examples of **diffraction**, and the key to all of them is how the wavelength compares with the size of the gap or obstacle.

You should know $v = f\lambda$, the idea of a wavefront and the approximate wavelengths of sound, light and radio waves.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diffraction"}
The spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or round the edge of an obstacle.
:::

:::definition{term="Wavefront"}
A line or surface joining points of a wave that are in phase, such as successive crests of a water wave.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The rule to remember"}
Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap (or obstacle) is about the same size as the wavelength. The wavelength, frequency and speed of the wave do not change on diffraction; only the direction of travel of parts of the wave changes.
:::

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge"}
Both boards require the meaning of diffraction and its dependence on gap width relative to wavelength, with ripple-tank demonstrations (ZIMSEC Form 5; Cambridge AS 8.2). Formulae for the intensity pattern of a single slit are not required by either board.
:::

## Diffraction in a ripple tank

A straight dipper vibrating at constant frequency sends plane (straight) wavefronts across a ripple tank towards a barrier with a gap. A strobe lamp freezes the pattern.

| Gap width compared with wavelength | Pattern beyond the gap |
| --- | --- |
| Much larger than $\lambda$ (e.g. $10\lambda$) | Wavefronts pass through almost straight; only slight curving at the edges; a sharp "shadow" either side |
| A few wavelengths | Noticeable spreading; wavefronts curved at the edges |
| About equal to $\lambda$ | Strong spreading; wavefronts nearly semicircular, spreading into the whole region beyond the barrier |
| Much smaller than $\lambda$ | Wavefronts are semicircular but very weak, because little energy gets through |

In every case the **spacing of the wavefronts stays the same** on both sides of the gap: the speed and frequency are unchanged, so the wavelength is unchanged. Reducing the gap width or increasing the wavelength (lowering the dipper frequency) both increase the spreading.

![Ripple-tank wavefronts meeting a barrier: through a gap of 10 wavelengths the waves continue almost straight with only slight bending at the edges and a clear shadow; through a gap of one wavelength they spread out as nearly semicircular wavefronts; the wavelength is the same before and after the gap.](media:diagrams/ripple-tank-diffraction-gaps.png "Figure: diffraction is strongest when the gap is about one wavelength")

Obstacles behave in the same way: waves bend round the edges of a small obstacle and close up behind it, but leave a clear shadow behind an obstacle much larger than the wavelength.

:::worked-solution[Reading a ripple-tank photograph]
A strobe photograph shows wavefronts $1.5\ \text{cm}$ apart, and the dipper vibrates at $8.0\ \text{Hz}$. The gap in the barrier is $1.5\ \text{cm}$ wide.

1. Wavelength = wavefront spacing $= 1.5\ \text{cm}$.
2. Speed: $v = f\lambda = 8.0 \times 1.5 = 12\ \text{cm s}^{-1}$.
3. The gap equals the wavelength, so the waves spread out strongly, with nearly semicircular wavefronts still $1.5\ \text{cm}$ apart.
4. If the gap is widened to $10\ \text{cm}$ the waves pass through with little spreading.
:::

## Sound, light and microwaves

**Sound** in air has wavelengths from about $1.7\ \text{cm}$ (20 kHz) to $17\ \text{m}$ (20 Hz). Doorways and windows are of the same order, so sound diffracts strongly through them and you hear round corners.

**Light** has wavelengths of about $5 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$, millions of times smaller than a doorway, so it shows no noticeable diffraction there. Light does diffract through a very narrow slit (width of order $10^{-5}\ \text{m}$ or less) or round a fine hair, forming a spread-out pattern of bright and dark fringes.

**Microwaves** of wavelength about $3\ \text{cm}$ diffract strongly through a gap a few centimetres wide between two metal plates: a probe detects a signal well to the side of the straight-through direction.

:::worked-solution[Sound and light through a doorway]
A doorway is $0.90\ \text{m}$ wide. Compare the diffraction of a $500\ \text{Hz}$ note (speed $340\ \text{m s}^{-1}$) with that of light of wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$.

1. Sound: $\lambda = \dfrac{340}{500} = 0.68\ \text{m}$; gap ÷ wavelength $= \dfrac{0.90}{0.68} = 1.32$. The gap is about one wavelength: strong diffraction, so the note is heard well round the door frame.
2. Light: gap ÷ wavelength $= \dfrac{0.90}{500 \times 10^{-9}} = 1.80 \times 10^{6}$. Diffraction is negligible: light passes straight through and casts a sharp shadow.
:::

## Radio waves and hills

| Service | Typical frequency | Wavelength | Diffraction round hills (size of order 1 km) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Long-wave radio | 198 kHz | about 1520 m | Strong: the waves follow the ground over and round hills |
| FM radio | 98 MHz | about 3 m | Weak: valleys behind hills receive a poor signal |
| Mobile phone | 1.8 GHz | about 0.17 m | Very weak: needs many masts in line of sight |

Long-wavelength radio diffracts round hills because the hills are comparable with its wavelength. FM, television and phone signals have short wavelengths and travel almost in straight lines, which is why masts are put on high ground and repeater stations are needed in hilly areas.

## Diffraction and interference patterns

Diffraction is also what makes two-source interference and diffraction-grating patterns possible. Light from each slit spreads out by diffraction, so the beams from neighbouring slits **overlap** and can superpose. If the slits were too wide, the light from each would go straight on and there would be no region of overlap. This is why the slits in Young's experiment must be narrow.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the wavelength changes"}
Diffraction changes the direction of parts of the wave but not its speed, frequency or wavelength. Draw the wavefront spacing the same after the gap.
:::

:::mistake{title="Bigger gap, more diffraction"}
The spreading **increases** as the gap gets **narrower**, down to a gap about one wavelength wide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing diffraction with refraction"}
Refraction is a change of direction caused by a change of speed at a boundary between media; diffraction happens in one medium at an edge or gap.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying light cannot diffract"}
Light diffracts; it just needs a gap or object of comparable size, around a micrometre.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Which papers"}
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2; it also appears as the first part of interference and grating questions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Command words"}
*Explain what is meant by diffraction*: spreading of a wave through a gap or round an obstacle. *Describe* a ripple-tank pattern in words: curvature of the wavefronts, spread, and unchanged spacing. *Explain* why one wave diffracts and another does not: compare **both** wavelengths with the gap or obstacle size, with orders of magnitude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diffraction is the spreading of waves through a gap or round an obstacle.
- Spreading is greatest when the gap or obstacle is comparable with the wavelength.
- Wavelength, frequency and speed are unchanged by diffraction.
- Sound and long radio waves diffract round everyday objects and hills; light needs micrometre-sized gaps.
- Diffraction at each slit makes the overlapping beams of interference and grating patterns possible.
:::
