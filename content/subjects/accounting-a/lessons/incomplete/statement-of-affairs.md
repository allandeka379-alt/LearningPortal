## Introduction

Mazvita Moyo sells vegetables from a stall in Masvingo. She pays her suppliers in cash, takes what she needs from the till and has never kept a ledger. At the end of the year her bank manager asks, "How much profit did you make?" She cannot prepare a statement of profit or loss because she has no list of sales or expenses, but she can list what she owns and what she owes at the start and the end of the year. The difference between those two positions, adjusted for what she took out and put in, tells her how much she earned.

This is the foundation of all work on incomplete records. At A-Level you must be able to draw up a statement of affairs, calculate profit from the change in capital, rearrange the formula to find a missing figure and explain why the result is only an estimate. The later lessons use ledger accounts and margins to rebuild the missing figures and prepare full statements.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Incomplete records"}
A system in which the business has not kept a full set of double-entry books, so some transactions, balances or the profit itself must be worked out from other information.
:::

:::definition{term="Statement of affairs"}
A list of the assets and liabilities of a business at a given date. The difference between them is the owner's capital at that date. It is the same layout as a statement of financial position, but it is built from lists and valuations, not from a trial balance.
:::

:::definition{term="Accounting equation"}
Assets = capital + liabilities, so capital = assets - liabilities. Capital changes only through profit or loss, drawings and capital introduced.
:::

## The statement of affairs

To prepare a statement of affairs, list every asset the business owns at the date (equipment, inventory, trade receivables, prepayments, bank and cash) and every liability it owes (trade payables, accruals, loans, overdrafts). Assets are valued as they would be in a statement of financial position: non-current assets at carrying amount, inventory at the lower of cost and net realisable value, receivables after writing off bad debts. The balancing figure is the capital.

Because capital is a balancing figure, it can be negative if liabilities exceed assets. The statement is normally drawn up at the start and at the end of the year; the opening one is made from the previous year's records or from the owner's memory and papers.

## Profit from the change in capital

During the year the capital changes for three reasons only. Profit increases it. Drawings, which are cash or goods the owner takes for personal use, reduce it. Capital introduced, such as new cash from the owner's savings, increases it. Rearranging the equation gives the profit.

:::keyfact{title="Profit from the accounting equation"}
Closing capital = opening capital + profit for the year - drawings + capital introduced.

Profit for the year = closing capital - opening capital + drawings - capital introduced.
:::

If the answer is negative, the business made a loss. Learn the signs by thinking of what happened to the owner's money: capital rose by 100, the owner also took out 40 in drawings, so profit must have been 140. If the owner also put in 30 of new money, 30 of the rise was not earned, so the profit is 110.

## Finding a missing figure

The formula contains five items: opening capital, closing capital, profit, drawings and capital introduced. If four are known, the fifth follows. A question may give the profit (from other information) and ask for drawings, or give drawings and ask for the capital at the start. Always write the full equation with the known values and solve for the unknown, rather than rearranging in your head.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a missing figure and a loss]
(a) A hairdresser started 2025 with capital of US\$11,240 and ended it with US\$14,260. Her profit was US\$9,100 and she introduced US\$800 of cash. Find her drawings.

Closing capital = opening + profit - drawings + introduced, so drawings = 11,240 + 9,100 + 800 - 14,260 = 6,880.

(b) A bakery began 2025 with capital of US\$15,000 and ended with US\$12,400. Drawings were US\$1,800 and no capital was introduced.

Profit = 12,400 - 15,000 + 1,800 = (800). The negative figure is a **loss of US\$800**. Capital fell by 2,600, of which 1,800 was drawings and 800 was loss.
:::

## Adjusting the closing position

The accuracy of the profit depends on the accuracy of the valuations. If closing inventory is overvalued, closing capital and the estimated profit are overstated by the same amount. If depreciation is forgotten, the equipment is overstated and so is the profit. If an accrued expense is omitted, liabilities are understated and the profit overstated. When a question reveals an error in the closing statement of affairs, correct the capital first and then recalculate the profit.

## Limitations of the method

- The profit is a single figure. The method does not show sales, cost of sales, gross profit or the expenses, so the owner cannot see which costs are rising.
- Errors and omissions in the listed assets and liabilities pass straight into the profit, and nothing in the method detects them. A full set of books has a trial balance that reveals many errors.
- Opening capital is often based on memory and estimates, and drawings may be guessed.
- A business that never keeps records cannot prepare the statements banks and the tax authority require, and fraud or theft is harder to detect.
- It is, however, cheap and quick, and a good starting point for a small trader.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding capital introduced to profit"}
Capital introduced is not earned. Deduct it when you calculate profit, because it is part of the rise in capital.
:::

:::mistake{title="Deducting drawings"}
Drawings must be added back to the increase in capital, since they reduced the closing capital. Adding to profit is the common reversal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting a liability or valuing an asset incorrectly"}
List accruals, loans and overdrafts as well as trade payables. Show equipment at carrying amount and inventory at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the difference between assets and liabilities profit"}
The difference is the capital at that date. Profit is the change in capital after drawings and capital introduced.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Paper 3 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 set this as a calculation of opening and closing capital and the profit, often at the start of a longer incomplete-records question. For *prepare a statement of affairs*, give a heading with the date, list assets and liabilities in order and show totals so that the capital can follow through. For *calculate the profit*, write the formula line by line.
:::

:::exam-tip
For *explain* or *state* questions about limitations, give two or three distinct points, such as no detail on sales and expenses, errors going undetected, and reliance on estimates, and apply each to the business in the question. For *advise*, recommend full double-entry books with reasons.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A statement of affairs lists assets and liabilities at a date; the difference is capital.
- Profit for the year = closing capital - opening capital + drawings - capital introduced.
- A negative result is a loss; any one of the five items in the equation can be found if the other four are known.
- Errors in valuation, such as unrecorded depreciation or inventory overvalued, pass directly into the profit.
- The method shows only a single profit figure and cannot detect errors or show where profit came from.
- Full records are better, but the method is quick and cheap for a very small trader.
:::
