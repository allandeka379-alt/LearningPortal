## Introduction

Chipo wants a bank loan to open a second bakery in Chinhoyi. The bank asks for her last three years' accounts. Her accountant also sends figures to the tax authority, a supplier asks whether she can pay on 30 days' terms and her three bakers wonder whether their jobs are secure. One set of records serves all of them, but each wants different things from it. Understanding who the users are and what they need explains why statements are laid out as they are and why different reports exist for managers and for outsiders.

Both boards ask you to identify the users, state their needs and say which report or ratio answers each need. The ratios themselves are taught in the ratio lessons; here you only need to know which user cares about what.

:::keyfact{title="ZIMSEC and Cambridge terms"}
| ZIMSEC (common usage) | Cambridge 9706 |
| --- | --- |
| Stakeholders; interested parties | Users of accounting information |
| Income statement; profit and loss account | Statement of profit or loss |
| Balance sheet | Statement of financial position |

Both boards examine users and qualitative characteristics in words. Do not describe the IFRS Conceptual Framework in detail: name the characteristic and explain it with an example.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Users of accounting information"}
Individuals and groups who use financial information to make decisions about a business. They may be internal (owners who manage, managers, employees) or external (lenders, suppliers, customers, government, the public).
:::

:::definition{term="Financial statements"}
Standard reports of the business's performance (statement of profit or loss) and position (statement of financial position) prepared at the end of a period, together with the statement of changes in equity and, for companies, the statement of cash flows.
:::

:::definition{term="Management accounts (internal reports)"}
Detailed, frequently prepared reports for managers, such as budgets, variance reports and cost statements. They have no required format.
:::

:::definition{term="Qualitative characteristics"}
The qualities that make information useful: relevance, reliability, comparability and understandability.
:::

## The users and what they need

| User | Main interest | Likely source of information |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owners and investors | Profit, return on money invested, dividends, risk | Statement of profit or loss, statement of changes in equity, profitability ratios |
| Managers | Day-to-day performance, costs, budgets, which products pay | Internal management reports, budgets, variance reports |
| Employees and trade unions | Job security, pay, bonuses, pension security | Statement of profit or loss, announcements about the future of the business |
| Lenders (banks, loan note holders) | Ability to pay interest and repay the loan; security | Statement of financial position, cash flow statement, gearing and interest cover |
| Suppliers | Whether the business can pay on the due date | Statement of financial position, liquidity ratios, payment record |
| Customers | Whether the business will survive to supply and service goods | Published statements, trading record |
| Government and the tax authority | Tax due, compliance, employment and statistics | Statement of profit or loss, VAT and payroll records |
| Public and environmental bodies | Jobs, local spending, effect on the community and the environment | Annual reports and sustainability statements, where published |

Several groups have **conflicting** needs. Employees want high pay; owners want high profit. A bank wants security; a growing business wants cheap, flexible finance. An accountant must therefore present the same figures fairly to all rather than serve one group.

## Communicating information

| Feature | Published (financial) statements | Internal management reports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main readers | Owners, lenders, tax authority, other external users | Managers and directors |
| Content | Standard statements of past performance and position | Detailed figures by product, department or period; may include forecasts |
| Frequency | At least once a year | Weekly, monthly or when needed |
| Format | Set by law and accounting standards | Chosen by management to suit decisions |
| Time | Past (historic) | Past and future (budgets) |

A sole trader or partnership is not required to publish statements, but must still give them to the bank, the tax authority and (for a partnership) the partners. A public company must publish them for shareholders and the registrar.

## Qualitative characteristics

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | Information helps users make or confirm decisions | A lender needs the figure for current liabilities, not the owner's holiday plans |
| Reliability (faithful representation) | Free from material error and bias; supported by evidence | Inventory counted and valued, not guessed |
| Comparability | Can be compared with earlier years and similar businesses | The same depreciation method used each year |
| Understandability | Presented clearly for users with reasonable knowledge | Headings and notes in plain language |

Useful information is also **timely**: figures that arrive a year late are worth much less. Where characteristics conflict, such as the need for fast figures against the need to check them, accountants balance them for the purpose in hand.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: matching users to information]
Mutasa Bakery in Gweru has the following callers in March. State which user each is and what report would answer the question.

1. A supplier asks, "Can you pay in 30 days?" This is a supplier; the statement of financial position and liquidity ratios show whether current assets cover current liabilities.
2. The manager asks, "Which product line is losing money?" This is a manager; an internal report analysing sales and costs by product.
3. The bank asks, "Can you repay a loan of US\$5,000 and the interest?" This is a lender; the statement of financial position, cash flow forecast and interest cover.
4. ZIMRA asks for the year's profit. This is the tax authority; the statement of profit or loss.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: one set of figures, three users]
Mutasa Bakery's figures for the year ended 31 December 2025:

| Item | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | 96,000 |
| Profit for the year | 14,400 |
| Non-current assets | 31,000 |
| Current assets | 18,500 |
| Share capital and reserves | 28,100 |
| Non-current liabilities (bank loan) | 9,000 |
| Current liabilities | 12,400 |

1. An **investor** considers profit margin: 14,400 ÷ 96,000 × 100 = 15.0%. The owner compares it with last year and with similar bakeries.
2. A **supplier** considers the current ratio: 18,500 ÷ 12,400 = 1.49 : 1. Current assets cover current liabilities about one and a half times, so a short credit period looks safe.
3. A **bank** considers gearing: 9,000 ÷ (15,000 + 13,100 + 9,000) × 100 = 24.3%. Only about a quarter of the long-term finance is borrowed, so more lending looks reasonable.
4. Check that the statement of financial position balances: assets 31,000 + 18,500 = 49,500; equity 28,100 plus liabilities 9,000 + 12,400 = 49,500.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: judging information against the characteristics]
Chipo's bookkeeper offers four pieces of information to the bank. For each, state the characteristic that is weak.

1. Closing inventory estimated "by eye" without a count: **reliability**, because it is not supported by evidence.
2. Depreciation on vehicles at 20% this year and 10% last year with no explanation: **comparability**.
3. A 40-page schedule of every petty cash voucher sent with a one-line loan application: **understandability** (and relevance), because the user cannot find the useful figures.
4. Last year's statements delivered in June of the following year: weak **timeliness**, so the information is out of date for the decision.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving one need for all users"}
"They all want to know the profit" earns no marks. Match each user to a different interest, such as the bank wanting security and repayment ability.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the report with the user"}
The statement of financial position is not a user. Name the user first, then the information and the report that answers the question.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the Conceptual Framework"}
The syllabus asks for the characteristics in words with an application, not the detail of the framework.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying managers use published statements"}
Managers use detailed internal reports as well, because published statements are too general for day-to-day decisions.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 1 and 2 ask for the user, the information needed and the reason. For *identify* or *state* give a user and a precise need. For *explain* add why the information helps the decision. For *evaluate whether the statements meet users' needs*, name a need they meet and a need they do not (for example, they are historic and cannot show future cash flows).
:::

:::exam-tip
When a stem gives figures for two years, state which user is interested in the movement and name the measure (profit margin for owners, current ratio for suppliers, gearing for lenders).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Users are internal (managers, owners who manage, employees) or external (lenders, suppliers, customers, government, the public).
- Each group has a different interest: profit and return, repayment ability, ability to pay on time, security of jobs, tax due, community effect.
- Published statements follow a set format and serve external users; management reports are detailed and flexible and serve managers.
- Information is useful when it is relevant, reliable, comparable and understandable, and timely.
- Users' needs can conflict, so statements must be presented fairly to all.
:::
