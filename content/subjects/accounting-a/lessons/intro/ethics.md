## Introduction

Tatenda is the accountant of a growing building-materials company in Gweru. The managing director asks him to leave out a US\$4,800 invoice for electricity so that profit for the year looks higher before a meeting with the bank. Tatenda is not breaking a rule of arithmetic. He is facing an ethical problem. Users of accounts depend on the accountant's honesty; if they cannot, the whole system of financial reporting stops working.

This topic sits behind everything in the course. ZIMSEC teaches it in Form 5, and Cambridge examines it at A Level. You should already know that financial statements are used by lenders, owners and the tax authority (see the lesson on users of information).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Business ethics"}
The moral principles that guide how a business behaves towards its owners, employees, customers, suppliers, the community and the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Professional ethics"}
The standards of conduct expected of members of a profession, such as accountants, which go beyond what the law requires.
:::

:::definition{term="Ethical threat"}
A circumstance that could compromise an accountant's compliance with the fundamental principles.
:::

:::definition{term="Safeguard"}
An action or rule that removes a threat or reduces it to an acceptable level.
:::

## Why an ethical framework is needed

Accountants have information that outsiders cannot check easily. Owners, lenders and the tax authority trust their figures. Without agreed principles, an accountant could be pressed to flatter the results, hide a liability or favour a friend. An ethical framework gives the accountant a reason to refuse and gives users a reason to trust.

## The fundamental principles

| Principle | Meaning | Example of a breach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Integrity | Being straightforward and honest in all professional dealings | Recording an invoice that has not been received so that profit looks lower |
| Objectivity | Not letting bias, conflict of interest or the influence of others override professional judgement | Approving a client's accounts because the client is also a close relative |
| Professional competence and due care | Having the knowledge and skill needed and acting carefully and diligently | Preparing a company cash flow statement without understanding the standard |
| Confidentiality | Not disclosing information acquired through work unless there is a legal or professional duty | Telling a competitor about a client's costs |
| Professional behaviour | Complying with laws and avoiding conduct that discredits the profession | Exaggerating qualifications to win a client |

## Threats and safeguards

| Threat | Meaning | Possible safeguard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-interest | A financial or other interest could influence judgement | Decline the bonus tied to profit; disclose the interest |
| Familiarity | A close relationship makes the accountant too sympathetic | Rotate staff; have another person review the work |
| Intimidation | Pressure from a manager, client or owner | Refer to a senior person or the ethics committee; document the instruction |
| Pressure to manipulate figures | Instruction to overstate profit or hide liabilities | Refuse; consult professional body; resign if necessary |
| Conflict of interest | Serving two clients with opposing interests | Disclose to both; use separate teams or decline |

A safeguard may come from the profession (training, rules, discipline) or from the workplace (clear policies, a second review, a confidential way of raising concerns).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: pressure to overstate profit]
The managing director asks Tatenda to leave out an unpaid electricity bill of US\$4,800 at the year end. The profit before this item is US\$26,400, current assets are US\$38,000 and current liabilities are US\$20,000 before the bill.

|  | Correct figures US\$ | As the manager wants US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Profit for the year | 26,400 | 31,200 |
| Current liabilities | 24,800 | 20,000 |
| Current assets | 38,000 | 38,000 |
| Current ratio (x : 1) | 1.53 | 1.90 |

1. Omitting the bill raises profit by 4,800 to 31,200, which is 4,800 ÷ 26,400 × 100 = 18.2% more than the correct profit.
2. It understates liabilities, so the current ratio looks like 1.90 : 1 instead of 1.53 : 1.
3. The bank could lend on the wrong figures. This breaches **integrity** and **objectivity** and is a threat of **intimidation** or **pressure to manipulate figures**.
4. Safeguard: Tatenda refuses, records the accrual, explains the reason in writing and refers the matter to a senior person if the pressure continues.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: sales left out of the records]
A shopkeeper tells his bookkeeper not to record cash sales of US\$6,300 so that less tax is paid. Assume a tax rate of 25% (given for this example, not a real rate).

|  | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Cash sales not recorded | 6,300 |
| Profit understated by the same amount | 6,300 |
| Tax avoided at an assumed rate of 25% | 1,575 |

1. Profit is understated by 6,300, so tax of 1,575 would be avoided.
2. This is **tax evasion** (illegal), because income is hidden. **Tax avoidance** is arranging affairs within the law to pay less tax, for example by claiming allowances correctly.
3. The bookkeeper's breach would be of integrity and professional behaviour; the safeguard is to refuse and, if the owner insists, to consult the professional body or senior management and consider resigning.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: applying the principles to everyday transactions]
Match each situation to the principle at risk.

1. Chipo, an assistant accountant, tells her cousin which of the firm's clients are late payers. **Confidentiality.**
2. Nyasha audits a company whose managing director is her husband. **Objectivity** (a familiarity threat). A safeguard: another auditor reviews the work.
3. Farai accepts a task on international standards he has never studied and does not seek help. **Professional competence and due care.**
4. Rudo is offered a bonus based on the profit she reports. **Self-interest** threat to integrity. A safeguard: have the figures reviewed independently and disclose the bonus arrangement.
:::

## Effects on stakeholders and society

Unethical behaviour harms people beyond the business. Overstated profit can mislead lenders and investors who then lose money; understated profit reduces the tax available for public services; poor treatment of employees or pollution of a river by a factory affects the community; ignoring ethical standards loses customers' trust and may lead to legal penalties. Ethical behaviour builds long-term reputation, supports lenders' confidence and benefits employees and the community.

**Unhu/Ubuntu** stresses that a person is a person through other people. Applied to a business this means honesty in dealings, respect for employees and suppliers, fair prices to customers and care for the community.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Confusing tax avoidance and tax evasion"}
Avoidance is legal planning. Evasion is illegal, for example by hiding sales. Do not use the words as if they mean the same thing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a principle without applying it"}
Marks need the situation: say why the action breaches, for example, objectivity (the relationship biases the judgement).
:::

:::mistake{title="Suggesting only the law as a safeguard"}
Professional ethics go beyond the law. Safeguards include review, documentation, consultation and refusing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the effect on the business only"}
Include effects on stakeholders and society: lenders, employees, the tax authority and the community.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
ZIMSEC Papers 1 and 2 and Cambridge Papers 3 (A Level) test the principles, threats and dilemmas in short scenarios. For *identify* give the principle by name; for *explain* link it to the facts given; for *advise* give an action and a reason; for *evaluate* weigh the pressure on the accountant against the harm to users and conclude.
:::

:::exam-tip
When figures are given, show the effect on profit and on a ratio, as in Example 1. Numbers make the harm concrete and earn the application marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ethical standards make accounting information trustworthy for lenders, owners, the tax authority and the public.
- The fundamental principles are integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour.
- Threats include self-interest, familiarity, intimidation, pressure to manipulate figures and conflict of interest.
- Safeguards include refusal, review by another person, documentation, disclosure and consulting a professional body.
- Tax evasion is illegal; tax avoidance within the law is not.
- Unethical decisions harm stakeholders and society as well as the business.
:::
