## Introduction

Rudo Ncube runs Mutare Bread Works. She sells loaves at US\$0.70 each and wants to know whether that price covers what a loaf really costs her. To answer, she must first decide what counts as a cost of making the bread and what is a cost of running the shop. **Cost accounting** gives her a language and a method for that. The same language is used for pricing, for controlling spending and for the decisions in later lessons on absorption, marginal costing and break-even. ZIMSEC tests the terms in Papers 1 to 3 and Cambridge in Paper 1 and Paper 2 question 4.

You should already know the difference between an expense and an asset and how a statement of profit or loss is arranged.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cost unit"}
A unit of product or service to which costs are traced, such as a loaf, a job, a student-day or a kilometre.
:::

:::definition{term="Cost centre"}
A department, section, machine or activity for which costs are collected before they are charged to cost units.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct cost"}
A cost that can be traced to one cost unit in an economic way, for example flour in a loaf. A cost that cannot be traced economically is **indirect** (an overhead).
:::

:::definition{term="Cost coding"}
Giving every cost a code (for example a letter for the element and a number for the department) so that it is recorded, sorted and charged to the right cost centre or unit without error.
:::

:::definition{term="Relevant cost"}
A future cash cost that will change because of a decision. Costs that are the same whichever option is chosen are **irrelevant**.
:::

:::definition{term="Opportunity cost and sunk cost"}
Opportunity cost is the benefit given up by choosing one course of action instead of the next best. A sunk cost has already been incurred and cannot be changed by the decision.
:::

## The elements and classifications of cost

Costs are first grouped by **element**: materials, labour and expenses. Each element can be direct or indirect. Direct materials, direct labour and direct expenses together are the **prime cost**. The indirect costs of production are **production overheads**. Indirect costs of selling, distribution and administration are **non-production overheads**.

A second classification separates **product costs** from **period costs**. Product costs (prime cost plus production overheads) attach to the units made and are carried in inventory until the goods are sold. Period costs are charged to the statement of profit or loss of the period in which they occur, whether or not goods are sold. Distribution, selling and administrative costs are period costs.

A third classification is by behaviour (fixed, variable and so on), covered in the next lesson. A fourth, by usefulness in decisions, gives the relevant, irrelevant, opportunity and sunk costs shown below.

**Cost codes** make recording reliable. A bakery might use M for materials, L for labour and E for expenses, followed by a department number: M-01 for bakery materials and L-02 for delivery labour. A wrongly coded invoice puts a cost in the wrong cost centre and misstates the unit cost.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: classifying and totalling the costs of Mutare Bread Works]
Mutare Bread Works made 23,000 loaves in June 2026. The costs of the month are classified below.

| Cost | US\$ | Direct or indirect | Product or period |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Flour, yeast and sugar used | 4,800 | Direct material | Product |
| Bakers' wages | 3,600 | Direct labour | Product |
| Packaging bags for loaves | 400 | Direct material | Product |
| Bakery rent and rates | 1,200 | Indirect (production overhead) | Product |
| Production supervisor's salary | 1,500 | Indirect (production overhead) | Product |
| Delivery driver's wages | 900 | Indirect (distribution) | Period |
| Shop manager's salary | 1,100 | Indirect (administration) | Period |
| Advertising on radio | 250 | Indirect (selling) | Period |

**Mutare Bread Works: cost statement for June 2026**

|  | US\$ | US\$ |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Direct materials (4,800 + 400) | 5,200 |  |
| Direct labour | 3,600 |  |
| **Prime cost** |  | 8,800 |
| Production overheads (1,200 + 1,500) |  | 2,700 |
| **Total product cost** |  | 11,500 |
| Period costs (900 + 1,100 + 250) |  | 2,250 |
| **Total costs of the month** |  | 13,750 |

The prime cost is US\$8,800. The cost of a loaf is the total product cost divided by the loaves made: 11,500 ÷ 23,000 = US\$0.50. The period costs of 2,250 are not part of the product cost; they are charged in full to June's statement of profit or loss. At a selling price of US\$0.70 each loaf earns US\$0.20 above product cost, from which the period costs must still be covered.
:::

## Purposes of cost accounting

Cost accounting is used for four main purposes.

1. **Cost control:** comparing actual costs with plans or earlier periods and finding the causes of differences.
2. **Pricing:** knowing the cost of each unit so that a selling price covers cost and gives a profit.
3. **Inventory valuation:** valuing finished goods and work in progress at product cost for the statement of financial position.
4. **Decision making:** providing the relevant costs needed for make-or-buy, special-order and closure decisions.

**Cost accounting against management accounting.** Cost accounting records and analyses costs, usually of production, in detail. Management accounting is wider: it uses cost data, budgets and forecasts to help managers plan, control and decide, and its reports are for internal use and look forward. **Financial accounting** reports past results to outside users (owners, lenders, the tax authority) in a standard format. Cost and management accounting need not follow the accounting standards because only managers use them.

## Costs for decisions

Decisions look forward, so only some costs matter. A **relevant** cost is a future cash flow that differs between the options. A **sunk** cost is history: the price paid for materials already in the store does not decide whether to use them. An **opportunity cost** is the best alternative use given up: if the steel in stock could be sold for US\$5 a kilogram, using it in an order costs the business that sale value.

A common test: ask "will this cash flow be different if I accept?" If the answer is no, ignore it. Apportioned fixed costs and costs already paid are usually irrelevant.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: relevant cost of a special order]
Tatenda Moyo owns Gweru Steel Works. A school offers US\$2,100 for a set of gates. The order needs 200 kg of steel that is in the store (bought at US\$6.00 a kilogram; the same steel now costs US\$7.50 to replace and the store is needed for normal jobs), 18 hours of overtime at an extra US\$5 an hour, normal wages of US\$260 already paid to the workers, a share of workshop rent of US\$400 and paint of US\$140 bought for the order.

| Item | Book cost US\$ | Relevant cost US\$ | Reason |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Steel: 200 kg (bought at 6.00 a kg; replacement 7.50 a kg) | 1,200 | 1,500 | The steel will be bought again for normal work, so the replacement cost is caused by the order; the original price is sunk |
| Overtime: 18 hours at US\$5 extra | 90 | 90 | Extra cash cost caused by the order |
| Normal wages of the workers (already paid) | 260 | 0 | Paid whether or not the order is accepted |
| Share of workshop rent | 400 | 0 | Fixed; does not change with the order |
| Paint bought for this order | 140 | 140 | Cost caused by the order |
| **Total** | 2,090 | 1,730 |  |

|  | US\$ |
| --- | ---: |
| Price offered for the order | 2,100 |
| Relevant costs | (1,730) |
| **Gain from accepting** | 370 |

On a book basis the order seems to cost US\$2,090, leaving almost nothing; on a relevant basis the cost is US\$1,730 and the order adds US\$370 to profit. Non-financial factors still matter: the school may order again, but a full workshop might lose better-paying work.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every direct cost variable and every indirect cost fixed"}
Direct and indirect describe whether a cost can be traced to a cost unit. Fixed and variable describe behaviour as output changes. A supervisor's salary is indirect and fixed, but indirect power can be variable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating distribution costs as product costs"}
Selling, distribution and administration costs are period costs. They are not added to the unit cost used to value inventory.
:::

:::mistake{title="Including sunk and apportioned costs in a decision"}
The original price of stock already held and a share of rent that does not change are irrelevant. Use the future, avoidable cash cost, or the sale value forgone when the material has an alternative use.
:::

:::mistake{title="Defining opportunity cost as the cost of the next best option"}
Opportunity cost is the benefit given up, not the money cost of the alternative.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
This lesson feeds ZIMSEC Paper 1 (definitions and classification as multiple choice), Papers 2 and 3, and Cambridge Paper 1 and Paper 2 question 4. For *state* and *define*, write one precise sentence. For *classify*, use a table with a reason for each cost. For *calculate*, show the totals of prime cost, product cost and cost per unit.
:::

:::exam-tip
For *explain*, *advise* and *evaluate* on a decision, name each relevant cost with its figure, say why a sunk or apportioned cost is ignored, and add one non-financial factor (jobs, quality, a regular customer). Do not include depreciation of existing equipment or fixed rent unless the question says it changes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A cost unit is what costs are traced to; a cost centre is where they are collected; cost codes keep the records accurate.
- Direct costs are traceable; indirect costs are overheads. Prime cost is direct material, labour and expenses.
- Product costs are valued in inventory; period costs go straight to profit or loss.
- Cost accounting serves control, pricing, inventory valuation and decisions; management accounting is wider and forward looking.
- Decisions use relevant costs: future cash flows that change. Sunk costs are ignored and opportunity costs included.
:::
