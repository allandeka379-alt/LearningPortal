## Introduction

A summary is not a shorter copy of the whole passage. It is an answer to one particular question: *Summarise the advantages of…*, *the reasons why…*, *the problems faced by…*, *the ways in which…*. Most of the marks in a summary are for **content**: one mark for each relevant point you include. If you choose the wrong points, beautiful writing cannot save the answer.

ZIMSEC Paper 2 and Cambridge 0500 Paper 1 both reward careful **selection**. This lesson teaches you to read the question precisely, sort what is relevant from what is not, and record your points as clear notes, ready to be written up (see the lesson on writing the summary).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Summary focus"}
The particular aspect of the passage the question asks about, such as benefits, problems, reasons, methods or changes.
:::

:::definition{term="Relevant point"}
A distinct idea in the passage that directly answers the summary question. Each one is usually worth one mark.
:::

:::definition{term="Supporting detail"}
An example, statistic, anecdote, quotation or explanation that illustrates a point. Usually it is not a point itself.
:::

## Step 1: dissect the question

Underline three things:

1. **The focus**: *advantages*, *problems*, *reasons*, *ways*.
2. **Whose or what**: *of keeping indigenous trees*, *faced by the farmers*.
3. **Limits**: which paragraphs, how many words, and whether a time is specified (*now*, *in the past*, *in future*).

:::example{title="Dissecting a question"}
*Using paragraphs 2 to 5, summarise the **difficulties** that **the new teacher** faced **during her first term**.*

- Focus: difficulties (not successes, not her feelings in general).
- Who: the new teacher (not the pupils' difficulties).
- Limits: paragraphs 2-5; first term only.
:::

## Step 2: read the whole passage once

Read for the gist before you start underlining. Points are often scattered, and a paragraph that seems irrelevant may contain one important point.

## Step 3: underline only what answers the focus

Go through the passage sentence by sentence and ask: **Does this answer the question?** If it does, underline the key words of the point. If it is only an example, a repetition or background, leave it.

:::example{title="Relevant or not?"}
**Task:** *Summarise the ways in which families save water.*

1. *Bulawayo's supply depends on dams that fill only in good rainy seasons.* → **Not relevant** (a cause of shortage, not a way of saving).
2. *Many households keep a bucket in the shower to catch the cold water.* → **Relevant point**: catch shower water in a bucket.
3. *One grandmother says her two drums carried her garden through a whole winter.* → **Example** of the point about collecting rainwater; not a new point.
4. *Careful families check taps and pipes every month and replace worn washers at once.* → **Relevant point**: repair leaks promptly.
:::

## What to leave out

| Leave out | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Examples and anecdotes (*for instance, one farmer…*) | They illustrate a point already made |
| Statistics and numbers (unless the number *is* the point) | Supporting evidence |
| Repetition of a point already made | Earns no extra mark |
| Quotations and direct speech | Usually illustrate a point; extract the idea instead |
| Background and history | Usually outside the focus |
| Points on a different aspect (problems when asked for benefits) | Irrelevant to the question |
| Your own opinions | A summary reports the passage only |

## Step 4: one idea per point

Some sentences contain two points; some points are spread across two sentences.

:::example{title="Splitting and combining"}
*Gardeners water early in the morning or in the evening, when less water evaporates, and cover the soil with grass cuttings to keep it moist.*

This sentence holds **two** points: (1) water at cool times of day; (2) cover the soil (mulch) to keep moisture in.
:::

## Step 5: make numbered notes

Write each point as a short note in your own words, and number it. Numbering lets you check the total against what you expect: many summary questions have 8 to 15 possible points.

:::example{title="Numbered notes"}
1. reuse kitchen water on garden
2. catch shower water in bucket
3. wash cars with bucket, not hosepipe
4. bottle of sand in toilet cistern
5. flush less often in shortages
6. repair leaks promptly
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Answering a different question"}
Including problems when asked for advantages, or the pupils' views when asked for the head's, earns nothing however well written.
:::

:::mistake{title="Collecting examples instead of points"}
*Fourteen bird species were counted* is evidence. The point is *the trees attract birds and other wildlife*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Counting the same point twice"}
*Classrooms are cooler* and *pupils are more comfortable in the heat* may be one point. Check whether the passage treats them as separate ideas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Skipping paragraphs"}
Points can hide in unexpected places, especially near the end of the passage.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use a different underline for each focus"}
If a question has two parts (for example *reasons and results*), mark them differently so you do not mix them up.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Expect points all through the specified section"}
Most paragraphs in the specified section contain at least one point. If a paragraph seems to have none, reread it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Dissect the question: focus, who or what, and limits (paragraphs, words, time).
- Read the whole passage, then underline only what answers the focus.
- Leave out examples, statistics, repetition, quotations, background and opinions.
- Split sentences with two points; combine ideas that make one point.
- Record each point as a short, numbered note in your own words.
:::
