## Introduction

Clear pronunciation helps people understand you the first time, whether you are giving a speech at assembly, answering an interview question or making a telephone call. You do not need to sound British or American. You need to be **clear**: to put the stress in the right place, to pronounce the sounds that change meaning, and to use your voice to show whether you are asking or telling.

Pronunciation is part of the oral English in the ZIMSEC syllabus, and it affects your performance in any spoken assessment. This lesson explains the main points in **plain spelling**. Stressed syllables are written in CAPITALS: *TEAcher*, *comPUter*. Say the examples aloud as you read: pronunciation is learnt with the mouth, not only the eyes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Syllable"}
A unit of sound containing one vowel sound: *pen* (1), *pen-cil* (2), *com-pu-ter* (3).
:::

:::definition{term="Word stress"}
The extra force, length and loudness given to one syllable of a word: *TA-ble*, *ho-TEL*, *ba-NA-na*.
:::

:::definition{term="Sentence stress"}
The extra force given to the most important words in a sentence.
:::

:::definition{term="Intonation"}
The rise and fall of the voice across a sentence, which shows whether you are asking, telling, listing or expecting agreement.
:::

:::definition{term="Silent letter"}
A letter that is written but not pronounced: the *k* in *knife*, the *b* in *doubt*.
:::

## Word stress

Every English word of two or more syllables has one main stressed syllable. Putting the stress in the wrong place can make a word hard to recognise.

:::example{title="Stress in common words"}
Two syllables: *TEAcher, WAter, MARket, beGIN, toDAY, hoTEL*
Three syllables: *BEAUtiful, HOSpital, comPUter, imPORtant, enginEER*
Five syllables: *e-lec-TRI-ci-ty*, *u-ni-VER-si-ty*. Count carefully.
:::

Some suffixes help you find the stress. Words ending in **-tion**, **-sion** and **-ic** are stressed on the syllable **just before** the ending:

| Word | Stress |
| --- | --- |
| education | edu-CA-tion |
| decision | de-CI-sion |
| economic | eco-NO-mic |
| scientific | scien-TI-fic |

Notice that stress can move inside a word family: *PHO-to-graph*, *pho-TO-gra-pher*, *pho-to-GRA-phic*.

## Noun and verb pairs

In many two-syllable words, the **noun** is stressed on the **first** syllable and the **verb** on the **second**. The spelling is the same, so only the stress tells the listener which word you mean.

| Noun (first syllable) | Verb (second syllable) |
| --- | --- |
| a REcord | to reCORD |
| a PREsent (a gift) | to preSENT |
| an OBject | to obJECT |
| PROgress | to proGRESS |
| an INcrease | to inCREASE |
| a PERmit | to perMIT |
| EXports | to exPORT |
| a REbel | to reBEL |
| CONduct (behaviour) | to conDUCT |

:::example{title="Hear the difference"}
*The school choir will reCORD a new song. The REcord will be ready in May.*
*Did anyone obJECT to the plan? There is a strange OBject on the table.*
*Prices continue to inCREASE. There has been a sharp INcrease in prices.*
:::

## Silent letters

Many English words contain letters that were once pronounced but are now silent.

| Silent letter | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| k before n | knife, know, knee, knock, knowledge |
| w before r | write, wrong, wrist, wrap |
| b after m, or before t | climb, lamb, comb, thumb, doubt, debt |
| h | hour, honest, honour, heir |
| t | listen, castle, fasten, whistle, often (often may also be said with t) |
| l | half, calm, talk, walk, should, could |
| n after m | autumn, column, condemn |
| s | island, aisle |
| p | psychology, receipt, cupboard |
| d | Wednesday, handsome |

Because *hour* and *honest* begin with a vowel **sound**, we say *an hour* and *an honest man*.

## Sounds that are often confused

Some pairs of sounds change the meaning of a word, so it is worth practising them.

:::example{title="Short and long vowels"}
*ship / sheep*, *sit / seat*, *live / leave*, *full / fool*, *pull / pool*.
The short sound is quick and relaxed; the long sound is held longer, with the lips spread for *ee* and rounded for *oo*. *I want to live here* and *I want to leave here* mean opposite things.
:::

:::example{title="Consonant pairs"}
*l / r*: *light / right*, *collect / correct*, *fly / fry*.
*th* (tongue between the teeth): *think* is not *sink* or *fink*; *three* is not *tree*; *this* is not *dis*.
*v / b*: *vote / boat*, *very / berry*.
:::

### The three sounds of -ed

The past tense ending *-ed* is pronounced in three ways:

- like **t** after the sounds *p, k, f, s, sh, ch*: *walked* (walkt), *stopped* (stopt), *washed* (washt)
- like **d** after other voiced sounds and vowels: *played* (playd), *cleaned* (cleand), *opened* (opend)
- as an extra syllable **id** after *t* and *d*: *wanted* (WAN-tid), *needed* (NEE-did), *started* (STAR-tid)

A common error is to add an extra syllable to every *-ed*: *walk-ed*, *play-ed*. Only words ending in a *t* or *d* sound get the extra syllable.

## Sentence stress

In a sentence, we stress the **content words** (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) and say the small grammar words quickly: *I WANT to GO to the MARket on SATurday.*

You can also move the stress to change the meaning:

:::example{title="Contrastive stress"}
*I didn't take your PEN.* (I took something else of yours.)
*I didn't take YOUR pen.* (I took someone else's.)
*I DIDn't take your pen.* (I deny it firmly.)
*I didn't TAKE your pen.* (Perhaps I borrowed it, or moved it.)
:::

## Intonation

| Pattern | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Falling (voice goes down at the end) | statements, commands, wh- questions | *I live in TOWN.* ↘ *Where do you LIVE?* ↘ |
| Rising (voice goes up at the end) | yes/no questions, showing surprise | *Are you READy?* ↗ |
| Rise then fall | lists | *We need maize ↗, beans ↗ and salt.* ↘ |
| Question tags | falling = expecting agreement; rising = a real question | *It's hot, ISn't it?* ↘ (I'm sure) / *You're coming, AREn't you?* ↗ (I'm not sure) |

Flat intonation, with no rise or fall, makes a speaker sound bored or uninterested, even when the words are polite.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Stressing every syllable equally"}
English is a stress-timed language. *com-PU-ter* with a strong middle syllable is clearer than *com-pu-ter* with equal weight on each part.
:::

:::mistake{title="Pronouncing silent letters"}
Saying the *k* in *knife* or the *b* in *doubt* makes the word harder to recognise.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding a syllable to -ed"}
*Walked* has one syllable (walkt), not two.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In stress questions, decide first whether the word is being used as a **noun** or a **verb** in the sentence. Nouns in these pairs are stressed on the first syllable; verbs on the second.
:::

:::exam-tip
For silent-letter questions, say the word aloud quietly and listen for which letter your mouth does not pronounce.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Every word has one main stressed syllable; words ending in *-tion, -sion, -ic* stress the syllable before the ending.
- Noun/verb pairs: noun on the first syllable (a REcord), verb on the second (to reCORD).
- Learn common silent letters: *knife, write, climb, doubt, hour, listen, half, autumn, island, receipt, Wednesday*.
- Practise sounds that change meaning: *ship/sheep, live/leave, light/right, think/sink*.
- *-ed* sounds like *t*, *d* or *id*; only after *t* or *d* does it add a syllable.
- Falling intonation for statements and wh- questions; rising for yes/no questions.
:::
