## Introduction

At some point you will be asked to speak in front of others: a vote of thanks at prize-giving, a talk to Form 1s about the school library, a speech when you hand over as head girl, or a debate against a neighbouring school. Good speakers are not born: they plan, structure and practise.

These skills are assessed in oral English and also in writing. ZIMSEC guided compositions often ask you to **write a speech** for a given audience, and Cambridge directed-writing tasks may ask for a speech or talk. Knowing how a speech is built and how a debate works helps you in both the spoken and the written form.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Speech"}
A prepared, formal talk given to an audience for a purpose: to inform, persuade, thank, welcome or celebrate.
:::

:::definition{term="Debate"}
A formal discussion in which two sides argue for and against a **motion** (a statement such as *This house believes that school uniforms should be abolished*), following set rules.
:::

:::definition{term="Proposition and opposition"}
The **proposition** (or proposers) argue **for** the motion; the **opposition** argue **against** it.
:::

:::definition{term="Rebuttal"}
Answering and challenging the points made by the other side.
:::

:::definition{term="Impromptu speech"}
A short talk given with little or no preparation time.
:::

## The structure of a speech

| Part | What it does | Example phrases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salutation | Greets the audience in order of importance | *The Guest of Honour, the Head, members of staff, fellow pupils…* |
| Opening | Grabs attention and states the topic and purpose | *Have you ever wondered…? Today I want to talk about…* |
| Body | Two to four main points, each explained and supported | *Firstly… Furthermore… For example…* |
| Conclusion | Sums up and ends memorably (a call to action, a thank-you) | *In conclusion… Let us all… Thank you for listening.* |

:::example{title="A strong opening for a speech on litter"}
*The Guest of Honour, the Headmaster, staff and fellow pupils, good morning. Every day, more than a hundred plastic bottles are thrown on our school grounds. This morning I want to show you how each of us can change that.*
The salutation is respectful, the fact grabs attention, and the purpose is clear.
:::

Make each point clear by using the **point, explain, example** pattern: state the point, explain why it matters, then give an example or evidence.

## Knowing your audience

The audience decides your register, vocabulary and examples.

:::example{title="Same topic, different audience"}
To Form 1 pupils: *The library is a great place to find stories you'll love. Come and borrow a book this week!*
To parents at an open day: *The library supports our pupils' reading and research, and we would welcome donations of books.*
Both are polite, but the first is warmer and simpler, the second more formal and focused on what parents can do.
:::

## Persuasive and spoken devices

Speeches are heard, not read, so speakers use devices that help listeners follow and remember.

- **Direct address**: *you*, *we*, *all of us* involve the audience.
- **Rhetorical questions**: *Do we want our children to grow up without trees?*
- **Rule of three**: *We need clean water, safe roads and good schools.*
- **Repetition**: *We can do it. We must do it. We will do it.*
- **Discourse markers** (signposts): *firstly, moreover, however, as a result, in conclusion*.
- **Facts and examples**: relevant, believable evidence, never invented statistics presented as fact.

Use these devices with judgement. One rhetorical question in an opening is powerful; ten in a row are irritating.

## Debating

A formal debate follows rules. A typical format:

1. The **chairperson** introduces the motion and the speakers and keeps time.
2. The **main speakers** for each side speak in turn, starting with the proposition.
3. **Floor** speakers (the audience) may add points.
4. The **summers-up** for each side answer the other side and summarise their own case.
5. The audience or judges **vote**.

Debate speeches use particular formulae:

:::example{title="Formulae for debate speeches"}
Opening: *Madam Chairperson, honourable judges, worthy opponents and fellow pupils, I stand before you to support the motion that…*
Rebuttal: *My opponent claimed that uniforms are expensive. However, she forgot that…*
Closing: *For these reasons, I urge you to vote for the motion. I thank you.*
:::

Good debaters **listen** to the other side and respond to their actual points. Attack the **argument**, never the person: *That argument ignores the cost to parents* is fair; *My opponent is clearly foolish* is not.

## Impromptu speaking

When you have only a minute to prepare, use a simple frame:

- **Point**: give your view in one sentence.
- **Reason**: say why.
- **Example**: give one example from your own experience.
- **Point again**: restate your view to finish.

:::example{title="An impromptu answer: 'Should pupils have homework at weekends?'"}
*I believe pupils should have some weekend homework, but not too much. Weekends give us time to revise properly. For example, I understood simultaneous equations only after practising them on a Saturday. So a small amount of homework helps, as long as we still have time to rest.*
:::

## Delivery

- **Eye contact**: look around the whole audience, not at your notes.
- **Pace and volume**: speak slowly and loudly enough for the back row; pause after important points.
- **Notes**: use short cue cards with key words, not a full script.
- **Posture**: stand still and upright; avoid fidgeting.
- **Time**: practise aloud and keep to the time limit.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="No salutation or a muddled one"}
Greet the audience in order of importance, starting with the most senior person present.
:::

:::mistake{title="Listing points without support"}
*Uniforms are good. They are cheap. They are smart.* Each point needs a reason and an example.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the other side in a debate"}
A debate speech that never answers the opponents' points is weaker than one with clear rebuttal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing a speech like an essay"}
In a written speech task, include the salutation, direct address and a closing thank-you. Without them it is just an essay.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a written speech, the examiner looks for **audience awareness**: a suitable salutation, direct address (*you, we*), a register that fits the listeners, and a clear closing.
:::

:::exam-tip
When choosing the best rebuttal or response in a multiple-choice item, pick the one that answers the opponent's **actual point** politely, with a reason.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A speech has a **salutation**, an attention-grabbing **opening**, a **body** of supported points and a memorable **conclusion**.
- Match register and examples to the **audience**.
- Use direct address, rhetorical questions, the rule of three, repetition and discourse markers, with judgement.
- In a debate, the proposition supports the motion and the opposition opposes it; **rebuttal** answers the other side's points, not the people.
- For impromptu speaking, use **point, reason, example, point**.
:::
