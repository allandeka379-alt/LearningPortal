## Introduction

Every day you listen to announcements at school assemblies, instructions from teachers, radio news, messages from relatives and conversations at the market. Good listening is not the same as hearing: it means understanding the main point, catching the important details and noticing how the speaker feels.

Listening is part of oral English in the ZIMSEC syllabus and supports the speaking and listening skills in the Cambridge courses. It also helps you in every subject, because taking accurate notes from a teacher is a listening skill.

**About the practice in this lesson:** this platform does not play audio. Instead, the examples and questions use short **written transcripts**: word-for-word records of announcements, messages, interviews and talks. Read each transcript as if you were hearing it once, then answer. The strategies are exactly the same as for real listening.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Gist"}
The general meaning or main point of what is said. Listening for gist means understanding the overall message without every detail.
:::

:::definition{term="Detail"}
A specific piece of information: a time, a place, a price, a name, a number or an instruction.
:::

:::definition{term="Signpost words"}
Words and phrases that show how a talk is organised: *first, next, however, the main reason, to sum up*.
:::

:::definition{term="Attitude"}
The speaker's feelings or opinion about the topic: pleased, worried, critical, enthusiastic, doubtful.
:::

:::definition{term="Transcript"}
A written record of exactly what a speaker said.
:::

## Before you listen: predicting

Good listeners prepare. Before an announcement or a talk, use the title, the situation and any questions you have been given to **predict** what you will hear.

:::example{title="Predicting from the situation"}
Situation: an announcement at a bus terminus in Bulawayo.
Likely content: destinations, departure times, platform or bay numbers, delays and reasons, apologies.
If a question asks *What time will the bus to Victoria Falls leave?*, you know to listen for a time **and** to check whether it is the original time or a new one.
:::

## Listening for gist

When you listen for gist, ignore small details at first and ask: *Who is speaking? To whom? What is the main purpose?* The gist is often stated near the beginning and repeated at the end.

:::example{title="Transcript: a school announcement (read it as if you heard it)"}
*"Good morning, everyone. This is a reminder from the sports department. Because of the heavy rain last night, the inter-house athletics competition planned for this afternoon has been postponed to next Friday. All other afternoon activities will continue as normal. Thank you."*
Gist: the athletics competition has been moved to next Friday because of rain.
Details: the reason (heavy rain), the new day (next Friday), other activities continue.
:::

## Listening for detail

For details, listen for **key words** related to the question. Numbers, times and names are often corrected or changed, so listen to the end of the sentence.

:::example{title="Transcript: a telephone message"}
*"Hello, this is Mrs Ncube from Gweru Medical Supplies. I'm calling for Mr Dube about his order. It was due on Tuesday, but it will now be delivered on Thursday morning, between nine and eleven. If that's not convenient, please call me back on extension 204. Thanks."*
A careless listener writes *Tuesday*. The detail that matters is the **new** day: **Thursday, between 9 and 11 a.m.**
:::

## Taking notes and messages

You cannot write down every word. Good notes are short and organised.

- Write **key words** only: nouns, verbs, numbers.
- Use **abbreviations**: *mtg* (meeting), *govt*, *info*, *approx.*, *&*.
- Use **headings and numbers** for main points.
- When you take a **message** for someone, include: who called, for whom, when, the message, what action is needed and how to reply.

:::example{title="A message written from the telephone call above"}
**To:** Mr Dube   **From:** Mrs Ncube, Gweru Medical Supplies   **Date:** Mon, 10.15 a.m.
**Message:** Your order will now arrive **Thurs, 9–11 a.m.** (not Tues).
**Action:** If not convenient, call her back on ext. 204.
:::

## Signpost words

Signpost words tell you what is coming next, so you can organise your notes.

| Purpose | Signpost words |
| --- | --- |
| Sequence | first, secondly, next, then, finally |
| Adding | also, in addition, furthermore, another point is |
| Contrast | however, but, on the other hand, although |
| Cause and effect | because, as a result, therefore, this means that |
| Emphasis | the most important point, above all, I must stress |
| Concluding | to sum up, in conclusion, so, overall |

When you hear *however*, expect a change of direction. When you hear *the most important thing is*, write it down.

## Recognising attitude

Speakers show attitude through their choice of words, through emphasis and, in real speech, through tone of voice. In a transcript, look at the **words**: *unfortunately, I'm delighted, frankly, I'm not convinced, sadly, at last*.

:::example{title="Transcript: a radio interview with a farmer"}
*Interviewer: "Has the new irrigation scheme helped you?"*
*Farmer: "Well, it's certainly better than nothing. We've had water twice a week, which is a start. But frankly, twice a week isn't enough for tomatoes, and nobody has told us when the second pump will arrive."*
Attitude: **partly satisfied but critical**. *Better than nothing* and *a start* show some approval; *frankly … isn't enough* and *nobody has told us* show frustration.
:::

## Following instructions and directions

Instructions usually come in order. Listen for sequence words and for **landmarks** in directions: *turn left at the clinic, go past the fuel station, it is opposite the post office*. Number the steps as you note them.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing down the first number you hear"}
Speakers often correct themselves or give an old and a new time. Wait for the end of the sentence before you write.
:::

:::mistake{title="Trying to write every word"}
You will fall behind and miss the next point. Write key words and fill in the gaps afterwards.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the speaker's opinion with a fact"}
*The scheme has helped a little* is the farmer's view. Words like *frankly*, *I think* and *sadly* signal opinion and attitude.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Read every question **before** you listen (or before you read the transcript), and underline what each one asks for: a time, a reason, a feeling.
:::

:::exam-tip
For attitude questions, choose a precise adjective (*doubtful, relieved, critical*) and support it with the speaker's words.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- **Predict** from the situation and the questions before you listen.
- Listen first for **gist** (main point), then for **details** (times, places, numbers, names).
- Take short notes with key words, abbreviations and headings; a message needs who, for whom, when, what and what action.
- **Signpost words** show the structure of a talk.
- **Attitude** shows in word choice: *frankly, unfortunately, I'm delighted*.
:::
