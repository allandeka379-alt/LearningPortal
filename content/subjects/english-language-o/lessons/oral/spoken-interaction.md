## Introduction

How you say something matters as much as what you say. *Give me that file* and *Could you pass me that file, please?* ask for the same thing, but the first could offend a colleague and the second will get you help. In Zimbabwe, as in every English-speaking setting, people judge you by whether your words suit the person and the situation: a head teacher, a bank official, a grandmother at a funeral or a friend at the bus stop.

Spoken interaction is part of oral English in the ZIMSEC syllabus and of the speaking skills in the Cambridge courses. Written papers also test it: you may be asked to choose the most appropriate reply in a situation, to write a dialogue, or to write a speech or letter in the right register. This lesson gives you the polite formulae for common situations and shows how to handle telephone calls and interviews.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Register"}
The level of formality of language. **Formal** register suits officials, strangers and serious occasions; **informal** register suits friends and family.
:::

:::definition{term="Polite formula"}
A fixed, polite expression used in a particular situation: *Could you…?*, *I'm sorry to hear…*, *Would you like to…?*
:::

:::definition{term="Turn-taking"}
The way speakers share a conversation: listening, waiting for a pause, then speaking, without interrupting or dominating.
:::

## Choosing the right register

Ask yourself three questions: **Who** am I speaking to? **Where** are we? **What** is the purpose?

:::example{title="Same request, different register"}
To a friend: *Can I borrow your calculator?*
To a teacher: *Excuse me, Mrs Moyo. Could I borrow a calculator, please?*
To a stranger or official: *I wonder if you could help me. Would it be possible to borrow a calculator?*
The more formal the situation, the longer and more indirect the request.
:::

Polite English softens requests with *could, would, may, I wonder if, would you mind*, and always uses *please* and *thank you*. Commands such as *Give me* or *I want* sound rude to most listeners, even if they are grammatically correct.

## Polite formulae for common situations

| Situation | Formal / polite | Informal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Greeting | Good morning, sir. How are you? | Hi! How's it going? |
| Introducing yourself | May I introduce myself? My name is… | Hi, I'm… |
| Introducing others | I'd like you to meet Mr Banda, our deputy head. | This is Tino. |
| Request | Would you mind opening the window? | Can you open the window? |
| Offer | Would you like me to carry that for you? | Want a hand? |
| Invitation | We would be delighted if you could attend… | Do you want to come to…? |
| Accepting | Thank you, I would be delighted to come. | Great, I'd love to! |
| Refusing politely | I'm afraid I can't, but thank you for asking. | Sorry, I can't make it. |
| Apology | I do apologise for the delay. | Sorry I'm late! |
| Complaint | I'm afraid there seems to be a problem with… | This isn't working. |
| Condolence | I'm so sorry for your loss. | I'm really sorry about your gran. |
| Congratulations | Congratulations on your well-deserved success. | Well done! |

:::example{title="Refusing an invitation politely"}
Invitation: *Would you like to come to our school's prize-giving on Friday?*
Rude: *No, I'm busy.*
Polite: *Thank you very much for inviting me. I'm afraid I'm travelling to Masvingo on Friday, but I hope it goes very well.*
A polite refusal **thanks**, **gives a reason** and ends with a **kind wish**.
:::

:::example{title="Making a complaint calmly"}
*Good afternoon. I bought this radio here last week, and I'm afraid it stopped working after two days. I have the receipt with me. Could you repair or replace it, please?*
A good complaint is calm, gives the facts and asks for a specific solution. Anger and insults rarely get results.
:::

## Telephone conversations

The telephone has its own formulae because the speakers cannot see each other.

| Stage | Useful expressions |
| --- | --- |
| Answering | Good morning, Chinhoyi Clinic. Rudo speaking. How can I help you? |
| Identifying yourself | Hello, this is Farai Chikore from Form 4B. |
| Asking for someone | May I speak to Mr Ndlovu, please? |
| Asking the caller to wait | Hold on, please. / One moment, I'll put you through. |
| Person unavailable | I'm afraid he's not available at the moment. Can I take a message? |
| Checking details | Could you spell your surname, please? Let me read that back to you. |
| Ending | Thank you for calling. Goodbye. |

:::example{title="A short telephone conversation"}
*Receptionist: Good morning, Riverside Secondary School. How may I help you?*
*Caller: Good morning. This is Mrs Gumbo. May I speak to the bursar, please?*
*Receptionist: I'm afraid she's in a meeting. Can I take a message?*
*Caller: Yes, please. Could you ask her to call me about my son's fees? My number is 0772 000 111.*
*Receptionist: Certainly. Let me read that back: 0772 000 111. I'll pass the message on.*
:::

## Interviews

Whether it is an interview for a prefect post, a college place or a holiday job, the interviewer is judging both what you say and how you say it.

- **Greet** the panel politely, wait to be invited to sit, and thank them at the end.
- **Answer the question asked.** Give a direct answer, then a reason and an example.
- **Use formal register**: *Yes, I have* rather than *Yeah*. Avoid slang.
- If you do not understand, ask politely: *Could you repeat the question, please?*
- Prepare a question of your own: *What would a typical day in this role involve?*

:::example{title="Weak and strong interview answers"}
Question: *Why do you want to be a prefect?*
Weak: *Because I like it.*
Strong: *I enjoy helping others and I think I set a good example. For instance, last year I organised the Form 2 reading club, and I would like to use that experience to support younger pupils.*
:::

## Conversation skills

Good conversation is shared. Show you are listening (*Really? That's interesting.*), ask follow-up questions, take turns without interrupting, and if you need to interrupt, do it politely: *Sorry to interrupt, but…*. To disagree politely: *I see your point, but I'm not sure I agree because…*

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using commands instead of requests"}
*Give me the register* sounds rude. Say *Could you give me the register, please?*
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong register for the listener"}
*Hi, guys! What's up?* is not a suitable greeting for an interview panel or a guest of honour.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to identify yourself on the phone"}
Begin with your name: *Hello, this is…* The listener cannot see you.
:::

:::mistake{title="Over-short answers in interviews"}
*Yes* or *No* on its own gives the panel nothing to judge. Add a reason and an example.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In questions that ask for the **most appropriate** response, check three things: Is it polite? Does it suit the relationship and setting? Does it actually do what the situation needs (apologise, refuse, request)?
:::

:::exam-tip
When you write a dialogue, use the formulae from this lesson and make each speaker's register suit their role.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Match **register** to the listener, setting and purpose.
- Soften requests with *could, would, may, I wonder if, would you mind*, plus *please* and *thank you*.
- Polite refusals thank, give a reason and end with a kind wish; complaints are calm, factual and ask for a solution.
- On the telephone: greet, identify yourself, ask for the person, take or leave a clear message, check details, close politely.
- In interviews: greet, answer the question with a reason and example, use formal language, thank the panel.
:::
