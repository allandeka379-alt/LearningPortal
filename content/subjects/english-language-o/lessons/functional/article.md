## Introduction

An article is written for **readers who choose whether to keep reading**. Unlike a report for the Head, nobody has to read a magazine article, so it must grab attention and hold it. ZIMSEC guided compositions set articles for the school magazine or a local newspaper (a sports day, a clean-up campaign, the dangers of drug abuse), and Cambridge 0510 Exercise 6 and 0500 directed writing set articles for a school website or a youth magazine.

Examiners look for **format** (headline, possibly a by-line and sub-headings), **content** (every note used and developed), and **style** (engaging, suited to the readers, but never slangy).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Article"}
A piece of writing for a magazine, newspaper or website that informs, entertains or persuades a general readership.
:::

:::definition{term="Headline"}
The short, eye-catching title of an article, often using wordplay, alliteration or a question.
:::

:::definition{term="Strapline"}
A one-line summary under the headline that tells readers what the article is about.
:::

:::definition{term="By-line"}
The line giving the writer's name, for example *By Tariro Mhlanga, Form 4*.
:::

## Layout

1. **Headline** (and an optional strapline).
2. **By-line** with your name or role.
3. **Introduction**: hook the reader and say what the article is about.
4. **Body paragraphs**: one main idea each, developed with examples, quotations from people (invented but plausible) and details. Sub-headings help in longer articles.
5. **Conclusion**: round off with a final thought, a call to action or a look ahead.

## Annotated model article

:::example{title="Model article for the school magazine"}
**From Rubbish to Riches: How 2 Green Turned Waste into a Library** **[Headline: alliteration and contrast]**

*One class, one term and a mountain of plastic bottles* **[Strapline summarises]**

By Nokuthula Sibanda, Form 3 Green **[By-line]**

Last January, the corner behind our classroom block was the ugliest place in the school: a heap of plastic bottles, chip packets and broken crates. Today it is the reason our class has forty new storybooks. How did that happen? **[Hook: before-and-after contrast and a question]**

**A simple idea** **[Sub-heading]**

It began with a Geography lesson on pollution. "We talk about recycling," said our class teacher, Mr Ncube, "but do we actually do it?" The challenge was set: collect, sort and sell recyclable waste for one term. **[Quotation adds life]**

**Hard work and dirty hands**

Every Friday afternoon, teams of five sorted plastic, cans and cardboard into bags. A local recycling company in Bulawayo agreed to collect the bags each month and pay for them by weight. At first, some classmates complained about the smell, but competition between teams soon took over. **[Detail and honest difficulty]**

**The reward**

By the end of term the class had earned enough to buy forty novels and short-story collections, which now fill a shelf in our classroom. Other classes have started to borrow them. **[Result, without invented statistics presented as facts]**

**Your turn** **[Conclusion with call to action]**

Could your class do the same? All you need is a corner, a few bags and the patience to get your hands dirty. The bottles are already out there, waiting.
:::

## Engaging techniques

- **Hooks:** a question, a surprising fact you know is true, a short anecdote, a vivid scene.
- **Direct address:** *Have you ever wondered…?*, *Imagine…*
- **Quotations** from people involved (fictional but realistic).
- **Varied sentences:** a short sentence after a long one adds punch.
- **Precise detail** rather than general praise.

:::example{title="Useful article phrases"}
- Openings: *Imagine…*, *Picture this:*, *It is not every day that…*
- Moving on: *But that was only the beginning.* / *So what happens next?*
- Quoting: *According to…*, *As one Form 2 learner put it…*
- Endings: *So next time you…*, *The message is clear:…*, *Watch this space.*
:::

## Article, report or speech?

The same notes can become any of these. The **article** is the one with a headline, a by-line, a lively voice and flowing paragraphs for a general audience. A **report** has To/From and recommendations; a **speech** addresses listeners.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="No headline"}
An article without a headline loses format marks. Make it short and interesting, not *Article about recycling*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing a letter"}
*Dear Editor* belongs to a letter. An article goes straight from headline to introduction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Lively becomes slangy"}
*"Guys, this was lit!"* is not acceptable. Liveliness comes from good detail, questions and varied sentences.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing statistics"}
Do not make up figures and present them as national facts. Use the information given or general, honest statements.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Spend one minute on your headline after you have planned. A headline that reflects your best idea often comes at the end of planning.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use sub-headings for articles of 250 words or more: they show organisation clearly to the examiner.
:::

## Checklist

- Headline (and strapline) and by-line.
- A hook in the first two sentences.
- One idea per paragraph, developed with details and quotations.
- Lively but suitable tone for the readers; no slang.
- A conclusion with a final thought or call to action.
- Every note in the task used.

## Summary

:::summary
An article is written for general readers who must be persuaded to keep reading. Give it a headline, a by-line and a hooking introduction, develop one idea per paragraph with detail and quotations, use sub-headings when the article is long, and end with a memorable thought or a call to action. Be lively without slang and never invent statistics.
:::
