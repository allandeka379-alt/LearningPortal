## Introduction

Every functional writing task in ZIMSEC Paper 1 Section B and in the Cambridge papers tells you three things, sometimes openly and sometimes between the lines: **who** you are writing to, **why** you are writing and **what kind of text** you must produce. A letter to the District Schools Inspector and a WhatsApp-style email to your cousin in Gweru may contain the same facts, but they must not sound the same.

Examiners award marks for **register** (the level of formality) and **tone** (the attitude your words convey). A brilliant report written in slang loses marks; a friendly letter that sounds like a legal document loses marks too. This lesson shows you how to read a task for audience and purpose, and how to adjust vocabulary, sentences and layout to suit them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Register"}
The level of formality of a piece of language, chosen to suit the situation: formal, semi-formal (neutral) or informal.
:::

:::definition{term="Audience"}
The intended reader or listener. Their age, position and relationship to the writer decide how formal the language should be.
:::

:::definition{term="Purpose"}
What the text is meant to do: inform, persuade, request, complain, apologise, thank, entertain, instruct or recommend.
:::

:::definition{term="Tone"}
The attitude the writer's words convey: polite, firm, warm, grateful, enthusiastic, apologetic, neutral or urgent.
:::

## Reading the task: the GAP check

Before you write, underline three things in the question:

- **G**enre: letter, email, report, speech, article, memo, review.
- **A**udience: the head, a friend, the editor, the community, the school board.
- **P**urpose: to complain, to persuade, to inform, to thank, to recommend.

:::example{title="Applying the GAP check"}
*"You are the secretary of the Environment Club. Write a report to your Head on the clean-up campaign held at Sakubva Market, recommending how it could be improved."*

- Genre: **report** (headings, objective style).
- Audience: **the Head** (formal, respectful).
- Purpose: **to inform and recommend** (facts first, then recommendations).
:::

## The three registers compared

| Feature | Formal | Semi-formal | Informal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical readers | head, official, editor, employer | teacher you know, club members, school magazine readers | friend, sibling, cousin |
| Vocabulary | precise, standard: *request, inform, regret* | clear and plain: *ask, tell, sorry* | everyday: *catch up, sort out* |
| Contractions | avoided: *I am, do not* | some | common: *I'm, don't* |
| Sentences | complete, often complex | a mix | short, can include questions and exclamations |
| Personal feeling | controlled, polite | moderate | open and warm |
| Slang, text speak | never | never | very little (and never *u*, *gr8*) |

Notice that even informal writing in an examination must be **correct**: spelling, punctuation and grammar still count. Informal means friendly, not careless.

## One message, three readers

Here is the same situation written for three audiences. A Form 4 student, Ruvimbo Moyo, broke a borrowed projector cable during a club presentation.

:::example{title="Model text 1: formal (to the Head)"}
Dear Sir

**[Formal salutation; no first name]**

I am writing to inform you that a cable belonging to the school projector was damaged during the Debate Club presentation on Thursday 12 March. **[Purpose stated in the first sentence; full forms, no contractions]**

The cable was caught under a chair leg while the room was being rearranged, and it no longer connects firmly. I accept responsibility, as I was in charge of the equipment. **[Facts in order; controlled, honest tone]**

I have asked the Computer Studies teacher for the cost of a replacement, and the club is willing to pay for it from its funds. I would be grateful if you could advise whether the school would prefer to order the cable itself. **[Polite request with *would be grateful*]**

Yours faithfully **[Correct closing for *Dear Sir*]**

Ruvimbo Moyo (Debate Club Secretary)
:::

:::example{title="Model text 2: semi-formal (notice to club members)"}
**Projector cable: please take care**

At Thursday's presentation one of the projector cables was damaged when chairs were moved. **[Plain, neutral vocabulary]** The club will pay for a replacement. From now on, please unplug all equipment before rearranging the room, and ask the secretary if you are not sure how to pack it away. **[Direct instructions, polite but not stiff]**

Ruvimbo Moyo, Secretary
:::

:::example{title="Model text 3: informal (email to a friend)"}
Hi Chiedza,

You won't believe what happened on Thursday! **[Contraction and exclamation suit a friend]** Right in the middle of our debate presentation, someone shoved a chair onto the projector cable and that was the end of it. Guess who was in charge of the equipment? Yes, me. **[Questions and humour create a conversational tone]**

I've owned up to Mr Chikore, and the club's going to pay for a new one, so it's not as bad as it sounded at first. Still, I'm never packing away chairs near a cable again. **[Personal feelings shared openly]**

Write soon and tell me how your exams went.

Love,\
Ruvi **[Warm closing; nickname]**
:::

All three are correct English. What changes is the **choice** of words, the **sentence types**, how much **feeling** is shown and the **layout**.

## Vocabulary shifts

| Informal | Formal |
|---|---|
| get | obtain, receive |
| ask for | request |
| tell | inform |
| sorry about | regret, apologise for |
| a lot of | a large number of, a great deal of |
| find out | discover, establish |
| put off | postpone |
| kids | children, learners |

Do not overdo it. *"I humbly wish to bring to your esteemed attention"* is not more formal than *"I am writing to inform you"*; it is only more wordy. Good formal English is clear and polite.

## Tone: polite, firm, never rude

A complaint can be **firm** without being **rude**. Compare:

:::example{title="Firm and polite versus rude"}
- Rude: *"Your bus drivers are useless and you should be ashamed."*
- Firm and polite: *"On three occasions this month the 6.30 bus has left before the advertised time. I would appreciate an explanation and an assurance that the timetable will be kept."*
:::

The second version gives facts, makes a clear request and keeps the reader willing to help.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing registers"}
*"Dear Sir, I'm writing coz the borehole at our school is broken"* starts formally and slips into text speak. Keep one register from greeting to closing.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the reader"}
A speech to Form 1 pupils full of long abstract words loses its audience. Ask yourself whether your reader would naturally use the words you have chosen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing informal with careless"}
A letter to a friend still needs paragraphs, full sentences, correct spelling and proper punctuation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overly flowery formal language"}
Piling up phrases such as *"with due respect and humble submission"* makes writing sound unnatural. Plain, polite, precise English scores higher.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Before writing, jot down G, A and P beside the question. Check your final paragraph against them: have you done what the purpose asked (requested, recommended, persuaded)?
:::

:::exam-tip
Use the role the question gives you. If you are "the head boy" or "the club secretary", sign off in that role and write with that responsibility.
:::

## Checklist

- I have identified genre, audience and purpose.
- My greeting and closing match the register.
- Vocabulary and sentence types suit the reader; no slang or text speak.
- Contractions only in informal writing.
- Tone fits the purpose: polite, firm, warm or neutral as needed.
- One register is kept from start to finish.

## Summary

:::summary
Register is the level of formality, chosen to suit the audience, purpose and genre. Formal writing uses precise standard vocabulary, full forms and controlled tone; informal writing is warm and conversational but still correct. Read every task for genre, audience and purpose, then keep one consistent register and a tone that fits what you want the reader to do.
:::
