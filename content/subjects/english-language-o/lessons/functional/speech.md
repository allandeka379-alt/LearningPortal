## Introduction

A speech is written to be **heard**, not read. The audience cannot turn back a page, so the speaker must tell them where the speech is going, repeat key ideas and keep their attention. ZIMSEC guided compositions often ask for a speech: a welcome at a prize-giving, a farewell to a retiring teacher, a talk to Form 1s on bullying, a campaign speech for a prefect election or an address to a community meeting about water. Cambridge directed writing also sets speeches built on ideas from a text.

The marks reward **content** (all the notes used, ideas developed for this audience), **format** (addressing the audience, a speech-like opening and ending) and **style** (direct, persuasive language that suits listeners).

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Speech"}
A text written to be spoken to an audience on a particular occasion, using direct address and features that help listeners follow and remember it.
:::

:::definition{term="Direct address"}
Speaking to the audience as *you* and including them as *we*, so they feel personally involved.
:::

:::definition{term="Rhetorical question"}
A question asked for effect, not for an answer: *Who among us has not wasted water?*
:::

:::definition{term="Signposting"}
Words that tell listeners where the speech is going: *First…*, *My second point is…*, *Finally…*.
:::

## Layout and structure

1. **Salutation to the audience**, in order of rank: *The Guest of Honour, the Head, members of staff, parents, fellow learners…*
2. **Opening**: greet, introduce yourself if needed, and grab attention (a question, a short anecdote, a striking statement).
3. **Purpose**: tell the audience what you will speak about.
4. **Main points**: one paragraph each, clearly signposted and developed with examples.
5. **Conclusion**: summarise, appeal or inspire.
6. **Thanks**: *Thank you for listening.*

There is no title page or address. Some teachers ask for a heading such as *Speech by the Head Girl at the Prize-Giving Day*; include it if the task suggests one.

## Annotated model speech

:::example{title="Model speech: Head Girl at Prize-Giving Day, Mutare"}
The Guest of Honour, Mrs Chiwenga; our Head, Mr Mutasa; members of staff; parents and guardians; fellow learners: good morning. **[Salutation in order of rank]**

Two years ago, our school had no computer laboratory, our netball team trained on a gravel court and our library closed at lunchtime. Look around you today. **[Contrast opening grabs attention and invites the audience to look]**

I stand here as Head Girl to celebrate what we have achieved this year and to thank those who made it possible. **[Purpose stated clearly]**

First, our results. Every learner who received a prize this morning earned it through early mornings and late nights. To the prize-winners, I say: you have shown us all what hard work looks like. **[First signposted point; direct address]**

Second, our sport. Who would have believed that a team training on gravel would reach the provincial netball finals? Not the other teams, certainly! **[Rhetorical question and a touch of humour]**

Finally, our gratitude. To our parents, who paid levies in difficult times; to our teachers, who stayed after hours; and to the community, who helped to build the new court: thank you. **[Triple structure: three groups, rhythmic and memorable]**

Fellow learners, the prizes we see today are not the end of the story. Let us return next year with even more to celebrate. **[Inclusive *let us*; inspiring ending]**

Thank you for listening. **[Courteous close]**
:::

## Rhetorical devices that work

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Rhetorical question | *Can we really afford to waste another drop?* | makes listeners think |
| Rule of three | *clean, safe and proud* | rhythm, easy to remember |
| Repetition | *We can… We can… We can…* | emphasis, builds feeling |
| Direct address | *You, the Form 1s, are the future of this school.* | involves the audience |
| Inclusive pronouns | *we*, *us*, *our* | unity |
| Anecdote | a short, real-sounding story | human interest |

Use them with control. Three or four well-placed devices are better than a device in every sentence.

:::example{title="Useful speech phrases"}
- Opening: *It is an honour to stand before you today…* / *I would like to begin with a question.*
- Purpose: *This morning I want to talk to you about…*
- Signposting: *Let me begin with…* / *This brings me to my second point.* / *Finally…*
- Appealing: *I urge each one of you to…* / *Let us all…*
- Closing: *Thank you for your attention.* / *Thank you for listening.*
:::

## Matching the occasion

- **Welcome speech:** warm, thanks the guests, previews the programme.
- **Farewell speech:** grateful, recalls specific memories, wishes well.
- **Campaign speech:** confident, states policies and how you will achieve them, appeals for votes.
- **Awareness speech** (drugs, bullying, water): informative facts plus a call to action.
- **Vote of thanks:** brief, names those to be thanked and why.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing an essay with a greeting on top"}
A speech that never speaks to the audience after the first line is an essay. Keep using *you* and *we*, and refer to the occasion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Wrong order of salutation"}
Begin with the most important person present (the guest of honour), then work down.
:::

:::mistake{title="No signposts"}
Listeners cannot see paragraphs. Use *first*, *secondly*, *my final point*, so they can follow.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ending abruptly"}
Do not stop after your last point. Summarise, appeal and thank the audience.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Decide who is speaking (the role in the task), who is listening and what you want them to do or feel. Let that shape your opening and your final appeal.
:::

:::exam-tip
Read your speech in your head as if aloud. If a sentence is too long to say in one breath, split it.
:::

## Checklist

- Salutation to the audience in order of rank.
- An opening that grabs attention and states the purpose.
- Signposted main points, each developed.
- Rhetorical devices used purposefully.
- Direct address and inclusive pronouns throughout.
- A memorable conclusion and *Thank you for listening*.

## Summary

:::summary
A speech is written for listeners. Greet the audience in order of rank, open with something striking, state your purpose, signpost each point and develop it with examples, use rhetorical devices with control, and end with a summary, an appeal and thanks. Keep speaking to the audience throughout, in a register suited to the occasion.
:::
