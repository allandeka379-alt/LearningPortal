## Introduction

In **directed writing** you read one or two texts and then write something new using their ideas: a letter to a head teacher, a speech to your class, an article for a local newspaper or a report for a committee. Cambridge 0500 Paper 2 Question 1 is a directed writing task, marked for **reading** (how well you select, develop and evaluate the ideas) and **writing** (how well you organise and express them for the audience). ZIMSEC guided compositions work in a similar way when they give you a passage or notes to respond to.

The trap is copying. Examiners reward candidates who **use** the ideas, **develop** them, and **evaluate** them: judging which points are strong or weak, and saying why.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Directed writing"}
Writing a new text for a specified audience, purpose and form, based on ideas selected from one or more reading texts.
:::

:::definition{term="Evaluation"}
Judging the ideas in a text: how convincing, reliable, balanced or practical they are, and why.
:::

:::definition{term="Development"}
Taking an idea from the text further: explaining its consequences, linking it to another idea, or applying it to the audience's situation.
:::

:::definition{term="Synthesis"}
Combining related ideas from two texts into one point rather than treating each text separately.
:::

## A four-step method

1. **Read the task first.** Underline form, audience, purpose and the bullet points you must cover.
2. **Read the text(s) and annotate** for each bullet: mark ideas for (+) and against (-), and any facts, opinions or weaknesses.
3. **Plan by idea, not by text.** Group related points from both texts; decide your own view.
4. **Write in role**, in your own words, evaluating as you go (*This argument ignores…*, *The most persuasive point is…*).

## Annotated source and model response

:::example{title="Source text: from a council newsletter (about 200 words)"}
**New market for Chikanga**

The council plans to move the vegetable vendors from the roadside in Chikanga to a new covered market on unused land behind the clinic. Councillor M. Takawira says the move will "end the danger of traders sitting a metre from speeding traffic" and will give vendors shelter, toilets and running water.

Stalls will cost a monthly fee, which the council says is "fair and affordable". Construction will take six months, during which traders must continue to operate from the roadside.

Some vendors are worried. Mrs Chari, who has sold tomatoes and onions on the same corner for eleven years, says, "My customers are people walking past to the kombi stop. Behind the clinic, who will see me?" Others fear the fee will be too high for those who make only a small profit each day.

A resident, Mr Dube, welcomes the plan: "The roadside is dirty after market days and the bins overflow. A proper market would be cleaner for everyone."
:::

**Task:** *You are a member of the Chikanga Youth Forum. Write a letter to Councillor Takawira giving your views on the plan. Use ideas from the newsletter and your own ideas.*

:::example{title="Model letter (body, about 230 words)"}
Dear Councillor Takawira

**RE: THE PROPOSED CHIKANGA MARKET**

I am writing on behalf of the Chikanga Youth Forum to support the plan for a covered market, while raising two concerns that we believe must be addressed if it is to succeed. **[Clear position stated at once; signals evaluation]**

The strongest argument for the move is safety. Vendors and their customers currently stand beside fast-moving traffic, and it is surely only a matter of time before someone is hurt. Shelter, toilets and water would also improve conditions for traders who spend whole days in the sun and rain. **[Ideas selected and developed: why safety matters, who benefits]**

However, Mrs Chari's question deserves a serious answer. Her business depends on passing trade from the kombi stop, and a market hidden behind the clinic may simply lose customers. We suggest a clear signboard at the main road and a footpath linking the kombi stop to the market. **[Evaluation: a weakness in the plan identified; a practical solution added]**

The fee is our second concern. The newsletter describes it as "fair and affordable", but it does not say how much it will be. Without a figure, vendors cannot judge this claim. We ask the council to publish the fee and consider a reduced rate for the first year. **[Evaluates the reliability of a claim; not copying]**

Finally, Mr Dube is right that a single site would be easier to keep clean, which benefits all residents. **[Synthesis: supports the plan with a resident's view]**

We hope the council will consult the vendors directly before the move.

Yours sincerely
:::

Notice the model does not repeat the text paragraph by paragraph. It takes a position, selects the most useful ideas, questions a vague claim and adds practical suggestions.

## How to evaluate

| Ask | Example comment |
|---|---|
| Is it supported by evidence? | *The writer gives no figures to support this.* |
| Is it a fact or an opinion? | *This is only one trader's opinion, but it is shared by others.* |
| Is it practical? | *A footpath would cost little and solve the problem.* |
| Is anything missing? | *The article does not mention parking for suppliers' trucks.* |
| Do the texts agree or disagree? | *Both writers agree that…, but they differ on…* |

:::example{title="Useful directed-writing phrases"}
- *The most convincing argument is… because…*
- *While it is true that…, this ignores…*
- *The article claims that…; however, no evidence is given.*
- *Both writers agree that…*
- *I would add that…*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Copying or lifting"}
Long phrases copied from the text earn little. Use your own words and quote only short phrases when you are evaluating them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Following the text's order"}
Plan by idea, not by paragraph. Combine related points from different parts of the text.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring one bullet"}
Each bullet in the task usually carries reading marks. Cover all of them.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the form and audience"}
A speech must sound like a speech; a letter to a councillor must be formal. The writing marks depend on it.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Make your evaluation explicit. Words such as *however*, *although*, *convincing*, *unconvincing*, *exaggerated* and *no evidence* show the examiner you are judging, not just repeating.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use most of the relevant ideas in the texts, but add one or two of your own that grow naturally from them.
:::

## Checklist

- Form, audience and purpose identified and kept.
- Every bullet covered.
- Ideas selected from the whole text (both texts if two), grouped by idea.
- Ideas developed and evaluated, not copied.
- A clear overall view and some ideas of my own.
- Correct register and accurate language.

## Summary

:::summary
Directed writing turns the ideas of one or two texts into a new text for a given audience and purpose. Read the task, annotate the texts, plan by idea, then write in role in your own words. The highest marks go to answers that select relevant ideas, develop them, evaluate how convincing and practical they are, and combine them into a clear argument in the right form and register.
:::
