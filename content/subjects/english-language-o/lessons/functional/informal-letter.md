## Introduction

Writing to a friend sounds easy, and that is the trap. In ZIMSEC guided compositions and in Cambridge 0510 Exercise 5, informal letters and emails are marked for **content** (did you cover every point in the task?) and **language** (is it lively, varied and accurate?). Candidates often lose marks by chatting about irrelevant news, forgetting one of the three bullet points, or writing in text speak.

A good informal letter sounds like a real person talking to someone they like, but it is still planned, paragraphed and correct.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Informal letter"}
A personal letter or email to a friend or relative, written in a friendly, conversational register.
:::

:::definition{term="Bullet prompts"}
The points listed in the task that your letter must cover. Each one usually earns content marks.
:::

:::definition{term="Conversational style"}
Writing that sounds like natural speech: contractions, direct questions to the reader, exclamations and personal feelings, while remaining grammatically correct.
:::

## Layout

**Letter:** your address and the date at the top right (many teachers accept the date alone), *Dear Tatenda,* on the left, paragraphs, a friendly close (*Love,* / *Best wishes,* / *Your friend,*) and your first name only.

**Email:** in the exam you normally write only the greeting, the body and the close; there is no address. A subject line is not required unless the task asks for one.

## Paragraph plan

1. **Opening:** greet, link to the friend (thank them for their message, ask after them) and lead into the purpose. Keep it to two or three sentences.
2. **Body:** one paragraph for each bullet prompt, developed with details, reasons and feelings.
3. **Closing:** wrap up, look forward to something, ask them to reply.

## Annotated model email

The task: *Your friend Rumbi has asked about the cultural festival held at your school. Write an email to her. In your email: describe what happened at the festival; explain what you enjoyed most and why; suggest how she could take part next year.*

:::example{title="Model informal email (about 130 words)"}
Hi Rumbi, **[Friendly greeting with first name]**

Thanks for your message! Sorry I took so long to reply, but last week was all about our cultural festival. **[Opening links to her message and leads into the purpose]**

It was brilliant. Every class represented a different province, so the quad was full of stalls selling roasted mealies, mazhanje and other local food, and groups performed traditional dances. **[Bullet 1: what happened, with specific detail]**

The best part for me was the mbira performance by the Form 3s. I'd never really listened to mbira properly before, and the sound was so calm that the whole crowd went quiet. It made me want to learn! **[Bullet 2: what I enjoyed and why; feelings]**

Next year you should definitely come. Why don't you join our drama club when you transfer? Then you could perform with us instead of watching. **[Bullet 3: suggestion, using a question]**

Write back soon and tell me about your holidays. **[Friendly close looking forward]**

Love,\
Chipo **[First name only]**
:::

Notice: one paragraph per bullet, contractions (*I'd*, *don't*), a question and an exclamation, but full sentences, correct spelling and no text speak.

## Greetings and endings

| Greeting | Closing |
|---|---|
| *Dear Farai,* | *Love,* (family, close friends) |
| *Hi Farai,* | *Best wishes,* / *Take care,* |
| *Hello Farai,* | *Your friend,* / *See you soon,* |

Never use *Yours faithfully* in an informal letter, and never sign with your full name and title.

## Useful phrases

:::example{title="Informal phrase bank"}
- Opening: *It was great to hear from you.* *Sorry I haven't written for ages.* *Guess what?*
- Describing: *You wouldn't believe…* *The best part was…* *It was a bit of a disaster, actually.*
- Advising and suggesting: *Why don't you…?* *If I were you, I'd…* *How about…?*
- Closing: *Can't wait to see you.* *Write back soon.* *Say hello to your family for me.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Missing a bullet point"}
If the task gives three bullets and you answer two, you cannot reach the top content band. Tick each bullet as you write its paragraph.
:::

:::mistake{title="Long, empty openings"}
*"How are you? I am fine. How is your family? My family is fine."* wastes words. One or two warm sentences are enough.
:::

:::mistake{title="Text speak and slang"}
*u*, *gr8*, *lol* and heavy slang lose language marks. Informal does not mean careless.
:::

:::mistake{title="Formal closings"}
*Yours faithfully* or *Yours sincerely, T. Moyo (Miss)* in a letter to your cousin shows you have not adjusted register.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In Cambridge 0510 Exercise 5 the email should be about 120-160 words. Plan three short body paragraphs, one per bullet, so each prompt is clearly developed.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use a range of structures to show your language: a conditional (*If you come, we'll…*), a question, a relative clause and some descriptive adjectives.
:::

## Checklist

- Friendly greeting and closing; first name only.
- Short opening that links to the reader and the purpose.
- Every bullet point covered in its own paragraph and developed.
- Conversational tone: contractions, questions, feelings.
- No text speak; correct spelling, grammar and punctuation.
- Within the word limit.

## Summary

:::summary
An informal letter or email is friendly and conversational but still planned and accurate. Greet warmly, open briefly, give each bullet point its own developed paragraph, use natural features such as contractions and questions, and close by looking forward to a reply. Covering every point in the task matters as much as lively language.
:::
