## Introduction

Read these two versions of the same event:

*The kombi stopped. A goat was on the road. Everyone got out. They pushed the goat away.*

*When the kombi stopped suddenly because a goat was standing in the road, everyone got out and pushed the stubborn animal into the long grass.*

The first is a string of short, flat sentences. The second combines the same facts into one smooth, varied sentence. Knowing how sentences are built lets you do this on purpose.

Sentence structure matters twice in the examination. Language questions ask you to **join** sentences with a given word, to **rewrite** a sentence beginning with given words without changing its meaning, or to identify clauses. Composition markers reward **varied sentence structures**: a mix of simple, compound and complex sentences, used for effect, separates a good script from an average one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Phrase"}
A group of words that works as a unit but has no subject–finite verb pair: *under the msasa tree*, *the tall old man*, *having finished his homework*.
:::

:::definition{term="Clause"}
A group of words with a subject and a finite verb: *the dog barked*, *when the visitors arrived*.
:::

:::definition{term="Main clause"}
A clause that makes complete sense on its own and could be a sentence by itself.
:::

:::definition{term="Subordinate clause"}
A clause that depends on a main clause. It begins with a subordinating word such as *when, because, although, if, who, which, that*.
:::

## Phrases and clauses

The test is simple: find the **subject** and the **finite verb** (a verb showing tense, such as *ran*, *is running*, *has gone*).

:::example{title="Phrase or clause?"}
- *under the old msasa tree* — no subject or finite verb: **phrase**
- *when the sun set* — subject *the sun*, verb *set*: **clause**
- *running across the field* — *running* has no subject and no tense here: **phrase**
:::

## The three main sentence types

| Type | Structure | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple | one main clause | *The kombi stopped suddenly.* |
| Compound | main clause + co-ordinating conjunction + main clause | *The kombi stopped, **and** everyone got out.* |
| Complex | main clause + one or more subordinate clauses | *The kombi stopped **because a goat ran across the road**.* |

A sentence with two main clauses **and** a subordinate clause is sometimes called compound-complex: *The dog barked when the visitors arrived, but nobody came out.* (three clauses)

:::example{title="Using sentence types for effect"}
*We searched the bush for hours, calling his name until our voices cracked and the sun sank behind the hills. Nothing. Then, faintly, a whistle.*

A long complex sentence builds tension; the two very short ones that follow create shock. (*Nothing.* is a deliberate minor sentence, which is acceptable for effect in narrative writing, but not in formal writing.)
:::

## Kinds of subordinate clause

| Kind | Job | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun clause | acts as subject or object | *Everyone knows **that Farai will pass**.* / ***What she said** surprised us.* |
| Adjectival (relative) clause | describes a noun | *The boy **who won the race** is my cousin.* |
| Adverbial clause | tells when, where, why, how, on what condition, despite what | *We left **before the rain started**.* |

Quick tests:

- Can you replace it with *it* or *something*? → **noun clause** (*Everyone knows something.*)
- Does it tell you *which* person or thing? → **adjectival clause**
- Does it answer *when, where, why, how* or *on what condition*? → **adverbial clause**

## Sentence fragments

A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing a main clause:

:::example{title="Fixing fragments"}
- Fragment: *Because the road to Binga was flooded.*
- Fixed: *We waited in Hwange because the road to Binga was flooded.*
- Fragment: *The old woman who sells mangoes at the corner.*
- Fixed: *The old woman who sells mangoes at the corner is my neighbour.*
:::

## Combining sentences

### With relative pronouns

*who* (people), *which* (things), *that* (people or things), *whose* (possession), *where* (place), *when* (time). The relative word **replaces** a word in the second sentence, so that word disappears.

:::example{title="Joining with who and whose"}
- *The nurse treated my brother. She works at Parirenyatwa Hospital.* → *The nurse **who treated my brother** works at Parirenyatwa Hospital.* (*who* replaces *she*)
- *I spoke to a farmer. His cattle had been stolen.* → *I spoke to a farmer **whose cattle** had been stolen.* (*whose* replaces *his*)
:::

### With participle phrases

An *-ing* or *having + past participle* phrase can replace a clause. The subject of the main clause must be the person or thing that did the action in the phrase.

:::example{title="Participle phrases"}
- *Farai finished his homework. Then he went out to play.* → ***Having finished his homework**, Farai went out to play.*
- Incorrect: *Having finished his homework, the football was kicked around.* (the football did not finish the homework: a dangling participle)
:::

### With too ... to and so ... that

:::example{title="Too ... to"}
*The hikers were very tired. They could not walk any further.*
→ *The hikers were **too tired to walk** any further.*
→ *The hikers were **so tired that** they could not walk any further.*

*Too* already contains the negative idea, so do not write *too tired to not walk* or *too tired that*.
:::

## Transforming sentences

A transformation changes the **structure** but keeps the **meaning**. Check three things:

1. Every fact in the original is still there, and nothing is added.
2. The order of events and the tense logic are unchanged.
3. The words given at the start are used exactly.

:::example{title="Keeping the order of events"}
*As soon as Rudo arrived, the meeting began.*

- Correct: *No sooner had Rudo arrived than the meeting began.*
- Wrong meaning: *No sooner had the meeting begun than Rudo arrived.* (the events are reversed)
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Repeating the subject after a relative clause"}
*The nurse who treated my brother she works at the hospital.* Remove *she*: the relative pronoun has already replaced it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Keeping the possessive with whose"}
*a farmer whose his cattle were stolen* uses two possessives. *Whose* replaces *his*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing fragments in compositions"}
Starting a sentence with *Because*, *Although* or *Which* and putting a full stop after the clause leaves a fragment. Attach it to a main clause.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dangling participles"}
*Walking to school, a snake crossed my path* says the snake was walking to school. Write *Walking to school, I saw a snake cross my path.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Before writing a joined sentence, underline the word that the joining word will replace (*she* → *who*, *his* → *whose*). Then check that you have not repeated it.
:::

:::exam-tip
In your compositions, deliberately vary sentence openings and lengths: begin some sentences with a subordinate clause (*As the bus pulled away, ...*) or a participle phrase (*Clutching her bag, ...*), and use an occasional short sentence for impact.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A clause has a subject and a finite verb; a phrase does not.
- Simple = one main clause; compound = main clauses joined by and, but, or, so; complex = main clause + subordinate clause.
- Subordinate clauses are noun clauses (subject or object), adjectival/relative clauses (describe a noun) or adverbial clauses (when, why, how, condition).
- A subordinate clause on its own is a fragment.
- Join sentences with relative words, participle phrases, too ... to and so ... that, removing the word that the joining word replaces.
- Transformations keep every fact, the order of events and the tense logic.
:::
