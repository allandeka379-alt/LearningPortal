## Introduction

Every sentence you write tells the reader *when* something happens. A composition about the day the rains came to Murehwa, a report on a school clean-up campaign or a summary of a passage about Lake Kariba all depend on verbs in the right tense. Examiners in ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Paper 2, and in the Cambridge papers, penalise tense errors because they confuse the reader: if a story jumps from *walked* to *walks* to *had walked* for no reason, the reader loses track of the order of events.

In this lesson you will learn how each tense is formed, when to use it, the irregular verbs that cause most mistakes, and how to keep tenses consistent across a whole piece of writing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tense"}
The form of a verb that shows the time of an action or state: present, past or future.
:::

:::definition{term="Aspect"}
The form of a verb that shows how an action is viewed: as a simple fact, as continuing (continuous), as completed before a point in time (perfect), or as continuing up to a point (perfect continuous).
:::

:::definition{term="Auxiliary verb"}
A helping verb (be, have, do, will) used with a main verb to make a tense, a question or a negative: *is reading*, *has eaten*, *will travel*.
:::

:::definition{term="Past participle"}
The verb form used after *have* in perfect tenses and after *be* in the passive: *written*, *gone*, *taken*, *finished*.
:::

:::definition{term="Sequence of tenses"}
The rule that the tenses in a sentence or passage must fit together logically, especially after a past reporting verb or in a narrative told in the past.
:::

## The twelve tense forms

Each of the three times (present, past, future) combines with four aspects.

| | Simple | Continuous | Perfect | Perfect continuous |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | she cooks | she is cooking | she has cooked | she has been cooking |
| Past | she cooked | she was cooking | she had cooked | she had been cooking |
| Future | she will cook | she will be cooking | she will have cooked | she will have been cooking |

### Present tenses

- **Present simple** for habits, routines and general truths: *The Zambezi flows into the Indian Ocean. Tatenda catches the kombi at six every morning.* Remember the *-s* on the third person singular: *he catches*, *it costs*.
- **Present continuous** for actions happening now or around now, and for fixed future arrangements: *The prefects are setting up the hall. We are travelling to Masvingo on Friday.*
- **Present perfect** for a past action linked to now, with no finished time stated: *Mr Ncube has marked our essays* (they are ready now). Use it with *already, yet, just, ever, never, since, for*.
- **Present perfect continuous** for an action that began in the past and is still going on: *It has been raining since lunchtime.*

:::example{title="Present perfect or past simple?"}
Correct: *I have lost my calculator.* (It is still lost: the result matters now.)
Correct: *I lost my calculator last Tuesday.* (A finished time is named: use the past simple.)
Incorrect: *I have lost my calculator last Tuesday.* (A finished time phrase such as *last Tuesday*, *yesterday* or *in 2019* cannot go with the present perfect.)
:::

### Past tenses

- **Past simple** for completed actions at a stated or understood time: *The bus left Mutare at dawn.*
- **Past continuous** for an action in progress when something else happened, or for background in a story: *We were eating supper when the lights went off.*
- **Past perfect** for the earlier of two past actions: *By the time the ambulance arrived, the nurse had already bandaged the wound.*
- **Past perfect continuous** for an activity that had been going on up to a past moment: *The farmers had been waiting for rain for weeks when the storm finally broke.*

:::example{title="Two past actions"}
*When we reached the stadium, the match **had started**.* We arrived late: the match began first.
*When we reached the stadium, the match **started**.* The match began at the moment we arrived.
The tense changes the meaning, so choose it deliberately.
:::

### Future forms

- **will + verb** for predictions and decisions made now: *I think it will be hot tomorrow. I'll carry the bags for you.*
- **going to** for plans and for predictions based on evidence: *Look at those clouds: it is going to rain.*
- **Future continuous** for an action in progress at a future time: *This time next week we will be writing Paper 2.*
- **Future perfect** for an action completed before a future time: *By December the builders will have finished the new library.*

:::keyfact{title="Time clauses take the present"}
After *when, as soon as, before, after, until* and *if* referring to the future, use a present tense, not *will*: *I will phone you **when** I **arrive** in Bulawayo* (not *when I will arrive*).
:::

## Irregular verbs

Most verbs add *-ed* for the past simple and past participle, but the common irregular ones cause most errors in compositions.

| Base | Past simple | Past participle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| begin | began | begun |
| choose | chose | chosen |
| drink | drank | drunk |
| lie (rest) | lay | lain |
| lay (put down) | laid | laid |
| ring | rang | rung |
| rise | rose | risen |
| seek | sought | sought |
| shake | shook | shaken |
| swim | swam | swum |
| tear | tore | torn |
| write | wrote | written |

:::example{title="Simple past versus participle"}
Correct: *The bell **rang** at eight.* / *The bell **has rung**.*
Incorrect: *The bell **has rang**.* (After *has*, *have* or *had*, use the past participle.)
Incorrect: *She **swum** across the river.* (The past simple is *swam*; *swum* needs *has* or *had*.)
:::

## The sequence of tenses

In a narrative told in the past, stay in the past. Move to the past perfect only for events that happened earlier still, and to the present only for general truths or dialogue.

:::example{title="Correcting a narrative extract"}
Incorrect: *Chipo opened the gate and walks into the yard. The dog that guarded the house is asleep.*
Correct: *Chipo opened the gate and **walked** into the yard. The dog that guarded the house **was** asleep.*
The first sentence set the story in the past, so every action that follows stays in the past.
:::

:::example{title="A model paragraph"}
*The storm **had passed** by the time we **left** the shelter. Water **was still running** down the road, and branches **lay** everywhere. My uncle, who **has lived** in Chimanimani all his life, **said** he **had never seen** such rain.*
Notice the past perfect for the earlier event (the storm passing), the past continuous for the background, and the present perfect inside the relative clause because the uncle still lives there.
:::

After a past reporting verb (*said, told, explained*), the reported verb usually moves back one tense; this is covered fully in the lesson on reported speech.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the present perfect with a finished time"}
*I have seen him yesterday* is wrong. *Yesterday* is finished time: write *I saw him yesterday*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a past form after did"}
*Did you went to church?* and *She didn't came* are wrong. After *did*, *does* or *do*, use the base form: *Did you go? She didn't come.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Drifting between past and present in a story"}
Narratives often slip into the present halfway through. Reread each paragraph and check that every main verb is in the same time frame.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using will after when or if"}
*If it will rain, the match will be cancelled* should be *If it rains, the match will be cancelled.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing lie and lay"}
*Lie* (rest) has the past *lay*; *lay* (put down) has the past *laid*. *She lay on the mat* (rested); *She laid the mat on the floor* (put it down).
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "write the correct form of the verb in brackets" items, look for signal words: *since*, *for*, *already* and *yet* point to a perfect tense; *yesterday*, *ago* and *last week* point to the past simple; *by the time* and *before* in a past sentence often need the past perfect.
:::

:::exam-tip
When you rewrite or correct sentences from a passage, keep the passage's own time frame. If the passage is in the past, your answer should be in the past too.
:::

:::exam-tip
Before handing in a composition, spend two minutes reading only the verbs. Tense errors are among the easiest marks to recover.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- English has three times (present, past, future) and four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous).
- The present perfect links the past to now and cannot be used with finished time phrases; the past perfect shows the earlier of two past actions.
- After *do/does/did* use the base form; after *have/has/had* use the past participle.
- Use a present tense in future time clauses (*when I arrive*, *if it rains*).
- Keep a narrative in one time frame and change tense only for a clear reason.
:::
