## Introduction

*You must report to the office. You should report to the office. You may report to the office.* The main verb is the same in all three sentences, yet the messages are completely different: an order, a piece of advice and a permission. The small words that make the difference are **modal verbs**.

Modals matter in every part of the English examination. In a formal letter to a council or a company you need polite forms (*Could you please ...*, *I would be grateful if ...*). In a speech or an argumentative essay you need to sound confident without being rude (*The school should ...*, *We must not ...*). In comprehension you may be asked what a writer's use of *might* or *must* tells you about their certainty. This lesson covers the helping verbs **be, have** and **do** first and then each modal in turn.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Auxiliary verb"}
A helping verb used with a main verb to form tenses, questions, negatives and the passive. The primary auxiliaries are **be**, **have** and **do**.
:::

:::definition{term="Modal verb"}
A special auxiliary that adds a meaning such as ability, permission, possibility, obligation or advice: *can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would, ought to*, and sometimes *need* and *dare*.
:::

:::definition{term="Deduction"}
A conclusion drawn from evidence. Modals show how sure the speaker is: *She must be at home* (almost certain), *She might be at home* (possible).
:::

## The primary auxiliaries: be, have, do

| Auxiliary | Job | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| be | continuous tenses; the passive | *The girls **are** practising. The hall **was** painted.* |
| have | perfect tenses | *We **have** finished. They **had** left.* |
| do | questions, negatives and emphasis in the present and past simple | ***Do** you live in Gweru? He **didn't** call. I **do** understand.* |

These three verbs can also be main verbs: *She **is** a nurse. I **have** two sisters. He **does** his homework at night.*

## How modals behave

Modals follow four rules that make them different from ordinary verbs:

1. They are followed by the **base form** of the verb, without *to*: *can swim*, *must go* (but *ought **to** go*).
2. They never add *-s*: *she can*, not *she cans*.
3. They make questions and negatives without *do*: *Can you help? You must not leave.*
4. They have no *-ing* or past participle forms, so we use other words in other tenses: *I will **be able to** come*, *We **had to** wait*.

:::example{title="Correct and incorrect forms"}
Incorrect: *She can sings.* / *He musts leave.* / *You should to rest.*
Correct: *She can sing. He must leave. You should rest.*
:::

## Meanings of the modals

### Ability

- *can* (present), *could* (past general ability), *be able to* (any tense, and for a single past success).
- *Tinashe **can** speak three languages. When I was ten I **could** climb any tree. After hours of searching, we **were able to** find the lost goat.*

### Permission and requests

- *can* (informal), *may* (formal), *could* (polite request).
- ***May** I leave the room, sir? **Could** you lend me your ruler? You **can** sit here.*

### Possibility and deduction

- *may, might, could* for possibility: *It **might** rain this afternoon.*
- *must* for a confident positive deduction; *can't* for a confident negative deduction.

:::example{title="Must and can't for deduction"}
*The lights are on and the car is outside. They **must be** at home.* (I am almost sure.)
*He has just eaten a big plate of sadza. He **can't be** hungry.* (I am almost sure he is not.)
For the past, add *have* + past participle: *The road is wet. It **must have rained** in the night.*
:::

### Obligation and necessity

- *must* (obligation from the speaker or a rule), *have to* (obligation from outside), *mustn't* (it is forbidden).
- *don't have to* / *needn't* (it is not necessary).

:::example{title="mustn't and don't have to"}
*You **mustn't** use your phone in the examination room.* (It is not allowed.)
*You **don't have to** wear uniform on Friday.* (It is allowed but not necessary.)
These two look similar but mean very different things.
:::

### Advice

- *should* and *ought to* (advice or what is right): *You **should** see a doctor. We **ought to** thank the volunteers.*
- *should have* + past participle for criticism of the past: *You **should have** told me earlier.*

### Future, willingness and offers

- *will* for future and willingness; *shall* with *I* and *we* for offers and suggestions: ***Shall** I open the window? **Shall** we start?*
- *would* for polite requests, imagined situations and past habits: ***Would** you sign here, please? When we lived in Bindura, we **would** swim in the river every Sunday.*

### Need and dare

*Need* and *dare* can act as modals in questions and negatives: *You **needn't** come. How **dare** you speak to me like that!* More often they act as ordinary verbs: *She **needs to** rest. He didn't **dare to** answer.*

## Modals in formal writing

:::example{title="Making a request polite in a formal letter"}
Too direct: *You must repair the borehole at our school.*
Better: *We would be grateful if you **could** send a technician to repair the borehole at our school.*
The modal *could* and the phrase *would be grateful* make the request courteous, which suits the register of a formal letter.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Adding to after a modal"}
*You must to sign the form* is wrong. Only *ought to*, *have to* and *be able to* take *to*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using must for past obligation"}
*Yesterday we must walk home* is wrong. *Must* has no past form: *Yesterday we **had to** walk home.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing could of or should of"}
In speech *could've* sounds like *could of*, but the correct written form is *could have*, *should have*, *must have*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using mustn't to mean 'not necessary'"}
*You mustn't bring food; lunch is provided* says bringing food is forbidden. The writer means *You **don't have to** (needn't) bring food.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In comprehension, a writer's modals show how certain they are. *This may be the reason* is cautious; *This must be the reason* is confident. Mention the modal when asked about the writer's attitude.
:::

:::exam-tip
In letters and speeches, use *could, would* and *should* to sound polite and reasonable. Overusing *must* can make a letter of complaint sound rude.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- *Be, have, do* help to form tenses, the passive, questions and negatives.
- Modals take the base form without *to*, never add *-s*, and form questions without *do*.
- Ability: can, could, be able to. Permission: can, may, could. Possibility: may, might, could.
- Deduction: must (sure it is true), can't (sure it is not); add *have* + participle for the past.
- Obligation: must, have to; *mustn't* = forbidden; *don't have to / needn't* = not necessary.
- Advice: should, ought to; criticism of the past: should have + participle.
:::
