## Introduction

*The bus left.* That sentence tells us very little. *The overloaded bus left noisily from Mbare at dawn because the driver was already late.* Now we know how, where, when and why. The words and groups of words that add this information are **adverbs** and **adverbials**.

In the examination, adverbs appear in several ways. You may have to choose between an adjective and an adverb (*sang beautiful* or *sang beautifully*), give the correct form of a word in brackets, rewrite a sentence beginning with *Never* or *Hardly*, or join two sentences with *so ... that*. In composition, well-chosen adverbials help you to set scenes and to vary the way your sentences begin.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Adverb"}
A word that gives more information about a verb, an adjective or another adverb: *walked **slowly***, ***very** hot*, ***quite** quickly*.
:::

:::definition{term="Adverbial phrase"}
A group of words without its own subject and verb that does the work of an adverb: *in the morning*, *under the mango tree*, *with great care*.
:::

:::definition{term="Adverbial clause"}
A subordinate clause (it has a subject and a verb) that works like an adverb: *when the bell rang*, *because it was raining*, *unless you hurry*.
:::

## Kinds of adverbs

| Kind | Question it answers | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manner | How? | carefully, quickly, well, hard |
| Time | When? | yesterday, soon, now, later |
| Place | Where? | here, outside, everywhere, nearby |
| Frequency | How often? | always, usually, often, seldom, never |
| Degree | How much? To what extent? | very, extremely, quite, almost, too |
| Reason / linking | Why? With what result? | therefore, consequently |

:::example{title="Adverbs at work"}
*Yesterday the herd boy **quietly** led the cattle **down** to the river. They **often** drink there, but the water was **too** shallow.*

*Yesterday* (time), *quietly* (manner), *down* (place), *often* (frequency), *too* (degree, describing the adjective *shallow*).
:::

## Forming adverbs

Most adverbs of manner are formed by adding **-ly** to an adjective:

- quick → quickly; sad → sadly; careful → carefully (keep both *l*s)
- consonant + *y* → *-ily*: happy → happily; angry → angrily
- consonant + *-le* → *-ly*: gentle → gently; simple → simply; possible → possibly
- *-ic* → *-ically*: basic → basically; dramatic → dramatically (but public → publicly)
- true → truly; whole → wholly

Some adverbs have **the same form** as the adjective: *hard, fast, late, early, straight, high*. Adding *-ly* to some of them creates a different word:

:::example{title="Hard and hardly, late and lately"}
- *The farmers worked **hard**.* (with great effort)
- *I could **hardly** hear the speaker.* (almost not)
- *She arrived **late**.* (not on time)
- *Have you seen Tendai **lately**?* (recently)
:::

The adverb of *good* is **well**: *She is a good player; she plays well.* Do not write *She plays good*.

### Beware of -ly adjectives

*Friendly, lovely, lonely, elderly, costly, silly* and *ugly* are **adjectives**. Use them before nouns (*a friendly nurse*); after a verb, use a phrase: *She greeted us **in a friendly way***.

## Adjective or adverb?

Ask what the word describes. A **noun** needs an adjective; a **verb** needs an adverb.

:::example{title="Choosing correctly"}
- *a **beautiful** song* (adjective, describes *song*) — *She sang **beautifully*** (adverb, describes *sang*)
- *a **careful** driver* — *He drives **carefully***
- After linking verbs (*be, seem, look, feel, smell, taste*) use an adjective: *The food smells **good**. She looks **happy**.*
:::

## Where adverbs go

- **Manner, place, time** usually come after the verb or object, in that order: *She sang **beautifully** **in the hall** **last night**.*
- **Frequency** adverbs come before the main verb (*He **often** visits*), after *be* (*He is **always** late*) and after the first helping verb (*I have **never** been there*).
- **Enough** follows an adjective or adverb (*fast enough*, *old enough*) but comes before a noun (*enough chairs*).
- Time adverbials can begin a sentence for variety or emphasis: ***At dawn**, the market was already busy.*

## Comparing adverbs

One-syllable adverbs and *early* take **-er/-est**: *fast, faster, fastest*; *hard, harder, hardest*; *early, earlier, earliest*. Adverbs ending in *-ly* use **more/most**: *more carefully, most carefully*. Irregular: *well, better, best*; *badly, worse, worst*; *far, farther/further, farthest/furthest*.

## Adverbial clauses

| Type | Typical conjunctions | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | when, while, after, before, until, as soon as | *Wait here **until I return**.* |
| Place | where, wherever | *Sit **wherever you like**.* |
| Reason | because, as, since | *We stayed in **because it rained**.* |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that | ***Unless you hurry**, you will miss the kombi.* |
| Concession | although, even though, though | ***Although she was tired**, she finished.* |
| Purpose | so that, in order that | *He saved **so that he could buy a bicycle**.* |
| Result | so ... that, such ... that | *It was **so hot that** we rested.* |

:::example{title="Joining with so ... that"}
*The rain was very heavy. The match was postponed.* → *The rain was **so heavy that** the match was postponed.* Notice that *very* disappears: *so* takes its place.
:::

## Inversion after negative adverbs

When a negative or limiting adverb such as *never, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely* or *no sooner* begins a sentence, the helping verb comes **before** the subject, as in a question. This is a favourite rewriting task.

:::example{title="Rewriting with inversion"}
- *I have never seen such a sunset.* → ***Never have I** seen such a sunset.*
- *The bus left as soon as we reached the stop.* → ***Hardly had we** reached the stop **when** the bus left.*
- … → ***No sooner had we** reached the stop **than** the bus left.*

*Hardly* and *scarcely* pair with **when**; *no sooner* pairs with **than**.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using an adjective after an action verb"}
*He drives careful* and *She sang beautiful* are wrong: verbs need adverbs (*carefully*, *beautifully*).
:::

:::mistake{title="Double negatives with hardly"}
*I could not hardly see* is wrong, because *hardly* is already negative. Write *I could hardly see*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the inversion pairs"}
*Hardly had we arrived than ...* is wrong. Use *Hardly ... when* and *No sooner ... than*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing forms"}
*Fastly* and *goodly* (as the adverb of good) do not exist. The adverbs are *fast* and *well*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In rewriting questions that begin with a negative adverb, check three things: the helping verb comes before the subject, the tense is unchanged, and the correct partner word is used (*when* or *than*).
:::

:::exam-tip
Vary your sentence openings in compositions with adverbials: *Moments later, ...*, *Without a word, ...*, *Beneath the msasa trees, ...* Examiners reward varied sentence structures.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives and other adverbs: manner, time, place, frequency, degree and reason.
- Most are formed with -ly; watch the spelling changes (happily, gently, basically). The adverb of good is well.
- Hard, fast, late and early are both adjectives and adverbs; hardly and lately have different meanings.
- Frequency adverbs go before the main verb but after be.
- Adverbial clauses show time, place, reason, condition, concession, purpose and result.
- After never, hardly, seldom and no sooner at the start of a sentence, invert the helping verb and subject. Hardly/scarcely ... when; no sooner ... than.
:::
