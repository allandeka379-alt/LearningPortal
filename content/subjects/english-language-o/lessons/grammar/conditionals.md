## Introduction

*If the rains come early, we will plant in October. If I had studied harder, I would have passed.* Conditional sentences talk about what happens, or would happen, when a condition is met. You need them to give advice in a letter, to argue a point in a discursive essay ("If the council repaired the road, fewer accidents would happen") and to express regret in a story.

Conditionals are also a favourite language task in ZIMSEC Paper 2 and in Cambridge exercises: you may be asked to complete a sentence, to rewrite two sentences as one using *if* or *unless*, or to correct a tense error in an *if*-clause. Each type of conditional has a fixed pattern of tenses, so these marks are very learnable.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conditional sentence"}
A sentence with two parts: a condition (usually beginning with *if* or *unless*) and a result that depends on it.
:::

:::definition{term="If-clause"}
The part of the sentence that states the condition: ***If it rains**, the match will be postponed.*
:::

:::definition{term="Main clause (result clause)"}
The part that states what happens when the condition is met: *If it rains, **the match will be postponed**.*
:::

:::definition{term="Hypothetical"}
Imagined or unlikely, rather than real: *If I were the Minister of Education, I would ...*
:::

## The four main types

| Type | Use | If-clause | Main clause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | Facts and things that always happen | present simple | present simple | *If you heat ice, it melts.* |
| First | Real, likely future | present simple | will + base verb | *If we leave now, we will catch the bus.* |
| Second | Unreal or unlikely present/future | past simple | would + base verb | *If I had a bicycle, I would ride to school.* |
| Third | Unreal past (it did not happen) | past perfect | would have + past participle | *If she had revised, she would have passed.* |

### The zero conditional

Use it for general truths and scientific facts. *If* can usually be replaced by *when*: *If (When) you mix blue and yellow, you get green.*

### The first conditional

Use it for a real possibility in the future. Do **not** put *will* in the *if*-clause.

:::example{title="First conditional"}
Correct: *If the bridge floods, the pupils from across the river will stay at home.*
Incorrect: *If the bridge will flood, the pupils will stay at home.*
You may also use *can, may, might* or an imperative in the main clause: *If you see Mr Gumbo, tell him the meeting has moved.*
:::

### The second conditional

Use it for something imaginary, impossible or unlikely now or in the future. In formal English, use *were* for all persons after *if*: *If I **were** you, I would apologise.*

:::example{title="Real or imagined?"}
*If I **win** the competition, I **will buy** a laptop.* The speaker thinks winning is possible (first conditional).
*If I **won** the lottery, I **would buy** a farm.* The speaker sees it as a dream (second conditional).
The past tense *won* does not refer to the past here; it shows the situation is imaginary.
:::

### The third conditional

Use it for the past: you imagine a different past and its result, usually to express regret or criticism. Both halves refer to something that did **not** happen.

:::example{title="Third conditional"}
*If the driver **had slowed down**, the accident **would not have happened**.*
Meaning: the driver did not slow down, and the accident happened.
Incorrect: *If the driver would have slowed down, ...* (never *would have* in the *if*-clause).
:::

## Mixed conditionals

Sometimes a past condition has a present result, or a present fact explains a past result.

:::example{title="Past condition, present result"}
*If I **had taken** the medicine yesterday, I **would feel** better now.*
The *if*-clause is third-conditional (past); the main clause is second-conditional (now).
:::

## Unless, provided that, as long as

**Unless** means *if ... not*. Do not add another *not* after it.

:::example{title="Rewriting with unless"}
*If you do not water the seedlings, they will die.*
→ *Unless you water the seedlings, they will die.*
Incorrect: *Unless you do not water the seedlings, they will die.* (double negative: the meaning is reversed)
:::

**Provided (that), providing, as long as** and **on condition that** mean *only if*: *You may borrow the book provided that you return it on Friday.*

## If only and I wish

These express regret or a strong wish. They follow the same "one step back" pattern as the second and third conditionals.

- About the **present**: past simple. *I wish I **lived** closer to school. If only it **were** the holidays.*
- About the **past**: past perfect. *I wish I **had listened** to my mother. If only we **had left** earlier.*
- About an annoying habit or a wished change in someone else: *would*. *I wish my brother **would stop** borrowing my shoes.*

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting will or would in the if-clause"}
*If he will come, I will tell him* and *If I would have known, ...* are wrong. The *if*-clause takes a present, past or past perfect tense; *will* and *would* belong in the main clause.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the second and third conditionals"}
*If I had money, I would have bought it* mixes a present condition with a past result. Decide the time first: present (*had ... would buy*) or past (*had had ... would have bought*).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using unless with a negative"}
*Unless it does not rain* means the opposite of what the writer intends. *Unless* already contains *not*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing I wish I would"}
*I wish I would be taller* is wrong. Use the past simple for present wishes: *I wish I were taller.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "join the two sentences using *if*" items, decide first whether the situation is real (first conditional), imaginary (second) or past and unchangeable (third). The original sentences usually give the clue: *He did not ...* points to the third conditional.
:::

:::exam-tip
When the *if*-clause comes first, put a comma after it. When the main clause comes first, no comma is needed: *We will stay indoors if it rains.*
:::

:::exam-tip
In discursive and argumentative essays, second conditionals make suggestions sound reasonable rather than bossy: *If the school opened the library at weekends, more pupils would read.*
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Zero: present + present (facts). First: present + will (likely future). Second: past + would (imagined). Third: past perfect + would have + participle (imagined past).
- Never use *will* or *would* in the *if*-clause.
- Use *were* for all persons in formal second conditionals.
- *Unless* = *if not*; do not add another negative.
- *I wish / If only* + past simple for the present, + past perfect for the past, + would for annoying habits.
:::
