## Introduction

*The meeting was put off. The meeting was put on. The meeting was put out.* Only the small word after *put* changes, yet the sentences mean postponed, staged (or organised), and, in the last case, nothing sensible at all. Verbs like these are **phrasal verbs**, and English speakers use thousands of them in everyday conversation.

In the examination, phrasal verbs appear in three ways. Vocabulary questions in comprehension ask you to explain a phrase such as *called off* in the context of the passage. Language items ask you to replace a phrasal verb with a single word, or the reverse. And in your own writing, the right phrasal verb makes a story sound natural, while a formal one-word equivalent suits a report, a formal letter or a speech to dignitaries.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Phrasal verb"}
A verb combined with one or two particles (adverbs or prepositions) to make a new meaning: *give up* (stop), *look after* (care for), *put up with* (tolerate).
:::

:::definition{term="Particle"}
The small word after the verb in a phrasal verb: *up, down, in, out, on, off, away, back, over, through, after*.
:::

:::definition{term="Separable phrasal verb"}
A phrasal verb whose object can go between the verb and the particle: *turn off the radio* or *turn the radio off*.
:::

:::definition{term="Formal equivalent"}
A single, usually Latin-based, verb with the same meaning as a phrasal verb: *carry out* → *conduct*, *put off* → *postpone*.
:::

## Literal and idiomatic meanings

Some phrasal verbs mean exactly what their parts say: *climb up*, *walk out*, *sit down*. Others are **idiomatic**: the meaning cannot be worked out from the separate words. *Give up* has nothing to do with giving or upward movement; it means *stop trying*.

:::example{title="One verb, several particles"}
*The old bus **broke down** outside Kwekwe.* (stopped working)
*Thieves **broke into** the tuckshop.* (entered by force)
*Fighting **broke out** at the end of the match.* (started suddenly)
*The couple **broke up** after five years.* (ended their relationship)
:::

The same phrasal verb can also have several meanings, so always check the context.

:::example{title="One phrasal verb, different meanings"}
*The plane **took off** at noon.* (left the ground)
*Please **take off** your shoes at the door.* (remove)
*Her small sewing business has really **taken off**.* (become successful)
:::

## Common phrasal verbs and their formal equivalents

| Phrasal verb | Meaning / formal equivalent | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| call off | cancel | *The final was called off because of the storm.* |
| carry out | conduct, perform | *The nurses carried out a vaccination campaign.* |
| come across | find by chance | *I came across an old photograph in the drawer.* |
| find out | discover | *We found out who had taken the keys.* |
| give up | stop, abandon | *He gave up smoking last year.* |
| go on | continue | *Please go on with your story.* |
| look after | care for | *My aunt looks after the baby on weekdays.* |
| look into | investigate | *The police are looking into the complaint.* |
| look up to | admire, respect | *Many young players look up to the captain.* |
| make up | invent (a story); reconcile | *She made up an excuse.* |
| put off | postpone, delay | *Don't put off your revision.* |
| put up with | tolerate | *We cannot put up with the noise any longer.* |
| run out of | have none left | *The clinic has run out of bandages.* |
| set up | establish | *The women set up a savings club.* |
| turn down | refuse, reject | *She turned down the job offer.* |

## Separable and inseparable phrasal verbs

With many transitive phrasal verbs the object can go before or after the particle: *She switched off the light* / *She switched the light off*. But when the object is a **pronoun**, it must go **between** the verb and the particle.

:::example{title="Pronoun objects"}
Correct: *She switched it off.* / *They called it off.*
Incorrect: *She switched off it.* / *They called off it.*
:::

Some phrasal verbs cannot be separated at all: *look after the children* (not *look the children after*), *come across a problem*, *get over an illness*. Three-word verbs such as *look forward to*, *put up with* and *run out of* are never separated either.

:::example{title="look forward to + -ing"}
In *look forward to*, the word *to* is a preposition, so it is followed by a noun or an *-ing* form.
Correct: *I look forward to **hearing** from you.*
Incorrect: *I look forward to hear from you.*
This sentence often ends formal letters, so learn it exactly.
:::

## Working out meaning from context

When a comprehension question asks for the meaning of a phrasal verb, do three things: read the whole sentence and the one before it, think of a single word that could replace the phrase, and check that your word makes sense in the sentence.

:::example{title="An annotated extract"}
*For weeks the villagers had **put up with** the smell from the broken sewer. When the council finally **turned down** their request for repairs, they decided to **take the matter up** with the local newspaper.*
- *put up with* = tolerated (they suffered it without complaining officially)
- *turned down* = refused or rejected
- *take the matter up* = raise or pursue the issue
:::

## Choosing the right register

Phrasal verbs are informal to neutral. In a friendly letter or a narrative they sound natural; in a formal report a single-word equivalent often sounds better.

:::example{title="Same message, two registers"}
Informal: *The prefects **looked into** the problem and **came up with** a plan.*
Formal: *The prefects **investigated** the problem and **devised** a plan.*
Neither is wrong, but the second suits a report to the school board.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Putting a pronoun after the particle"}
*Turn on it* and *pick up them* are wrong. A pronoun object goes in the middle: *turn it on*, *pick them up*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong particle"}
*Fill up the form* is common but the standard form for a document is *fill in* (or *fill out*) *the form*. *Fill up* means fill a container completely.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dropping the particle"}
*She was brought in Mutare* is wrong; the phrasal verb is *brought up* (raised). Without the particle the meaning changes or disappears.
:::

:::mistake{title="Following look forward to with a base verb"}
*I look forward to meet you* should be *I look forward to meeting you.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to give the meaning of a phrasal verb "as used in the passage", give a meaning that fits that sentence. A correct dictionary meaning that does not fit the context earns no mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
To check a one-word replacement, put it back into the sentence and read the sentence aloud in your head. If the grammar or the tense no longer fits, adjust it: *called off* → *cancelled*, not *cancel*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A phrasal verb is a verb + particle with its own meaning, often different from the separate words.
- The same verb with different particles (break down, break into, break out) has different meanings.
- A pronoun object goes between the verb and the particle: *turn it off*.
- Some phrasal verbs are inseparable (look after, come across, get over); three-word verbs are never separated.
- Use phrasal verbs in informal writing and single-word equivalents (postpone, cancel, investigate, tolerate) in formal writing.
:::
