## Introduction

Imagine two descriptions of the same morning at a rural bus stop. The first says: *There was a bus. People were waiting.* The second says: *A battered blue bus wheezed to a halt beside the dusty road, where tired, impatient travellers had waited since dawn.* The second picture is far clearer, and most of the difference comes from **adjectives**.

Adjectives matter in every part of the English examination. In composition, precise adjectives turn a flat description into a vivid one. In the language section of the reading paper you may be asked to complete a sentence with the correct form of a word in brackets (*busy → busier*), to rewrite a sentence beginning with given words (*Nyasha is taller ...*), or to correct a sentence such as *It was the most hottest day*. Examiners also notice errors like *I was very boring in the lesson*, which says the opposite of what the writer meant.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Adjective"}
A word that describes a noun or pronoun, telling us what kind, which one, how many or how much: *a **tall** tree*, ***three** goats*, *the road is **dusty***.
:::

:::definition{term="Comparative"}
The form of an adjective used to compare two people or things, made with *-er* or *more* and usually followed by *than*: *taller than*, *more careful than*.
:::

:::definition{term="Superlative"}
The form used to compare one person or thing with all the others in a group of three or more, made with *the ... -est* or *the most*: *the tallest*, *the most careful*.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-gradable adjective"}
An adjective that describes an all-or-nothing state, such as *dead*, *unique*, *empty* or *impossible*. It cannot normally be made stronger or weaker with *very* or *more*.
:::

## Where adjectives go

Most adjectives stand in two positions:

- **Before a noun** (attributive): *a **narrow** path*, *the **new** clinic*.
- **After a linking verb** such as *be, seem, look, feel, become* (predicative): *The path was **narrow**. Gogo seems **happy**.*

:::example{title="Adjectives in a description"}
*The **old** baobab stood alone on the **flat, sun-baked** plain. Its bark looked **grey** and **wrinkled**, like the skin of an **ancient** elephant.*

Six adjectives give size, age, colour and texture; the noun alone would tell the reader very little.
:::

## Forming adjectives

Many adjectives are built from nouns and verbs by adding a **suffix**. Learn the common ones, because exam questions often give a noun or verb in brackets and ask for the adjective.

| Suffix | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| -ful / -less | care → careful, careless; hope → hopeful, hopeless |
| -ous / -ious | danger → dangerous; fame → famous; industry → industrious |
| -al | nation → national; tradition → traditional |
| -able / -ible | comfort → comfortable; sense → sensible |
| -ive | act → active; attract → attractive |
| -ish / -y | fool → foolish; dust → dusty; sun → sunny |

Watch the spelling: *beauty → beautiful* (y becomes i), *fame → famous* (the e is dropped), *sun → sunny* (the consonant doubles).

## Comparatives and superlatives

| Type of adjective | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One syllable | add *-er / -est* | tall, taller, tallest |
| One syllable ending consonant–vowel–consonant | double the last letter | big, bigger, biggest |
| Two syllables ending in *-y* | change *y* to *i*, add *-er / -est* | busy, busier, busiest |
| Most other two-syllable and all longer adjectives | *more / most* | careful, more careful, most careful |
| Irregular | learn them | good, better, best; bad, worse, worst; little, less, least; far, farther/further, farthest/furthest |

:::example{title="Correct and incorrect comparisons"}
- Correct: *Rudo is **cleverer than** her brother.* Incorrect: *Rudo is more cleverer than her brother.* (the comparison is marked twice)
- Correct: *It was **the hottest** day of the year.* Incorrect: *It was the most hottest day.*
- Correct: *This road is **worse** than that one.* Incorrect: *This road is badder / more bad.*
:::

### Comparing one person with the rest of a group

When the person is part of the group, add *other*: *Nyasha is taller than any **other** girl in the team.* Without *other* the sentence claims that Nyasha is taller than herself. This is a favourite rewriting task:

:::example{title="Rewriting a comparison"}
*No other girl in the team is as tall as Nyasha.*

- Begin *Nyasha is the ...* → *Nyasha is **the tallest** girl in the team.*
- Begin *Nyasha is taller ...* → *Nyasha is taller **than any other girl** in the team.*

Both keep the meaning; each follows its given beginning.
:::

### As ... as

To show that two things are equal, use **as + adjective + as**: *Tendai is as tall as his father.* The negative is *not as ... as* (or *not so ... as*): *This test is not as difficult as the last one.*

## The order of adjectives

When two or more adjectives come before a noun, English follows a fixed order:

**opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose → noun**

:::example{title="Stacking adjectives"}
- *a **beautiful old wooden** table* (opinion, age, material)
- *a **lovely long red cotton** scarf* (opinion, size, colour, material)
- *a **small round thatched** hut* (size, shape, material)

Incorrect: *a wooden old beautiful table*. In good writing, two or three adjectives are usually enough.
:::

Use a comma between two opinion adjectives that could be joined by *and* (*a long, tiring journey*), but not between adjectives from different groups (*a small round hut*).

## -ed and -ing adjectives

Many adjectives come in pairs formed from verbs:

- The **-ing** form describes the thing or person that **causes** the feeling: *a **boring** film*, *an **exciting** match*, ***frightening** news*.
- The **-ed** form describes the person who **feels** it: *the **bored** audience*, *the **excited** fans*, *the **frightened** child*.

:::example{title="Same verb, different meaning"}
- *The pupils were **disappointed** when the trip was cancelled.* (their feeling)
- *The cancellation was **disappointing**.* (the cause)
- *I was very **boring** in the lesson* means that you bored everyone else. The writer almost certainly meant *I was very **bored***.
:::

## Non-gradable adjectives

Some adjectives cannot be graded because the state is complete: *dead, unique, impossible, perfect, empty, finished*. Use **completely, totally** or **absolutely** with them, not *very, fairly* or *slightly*.

:::example{title="Grading"}
- Correct: *The old radio is **completely dead**.* Incorrect: *The radio is very dead.*
- Correct: *The task is **absolutely impossible**.* Incorrect: *The task is more impossible than before.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Double comparatives and superlatives"}
*More cleverer*, *most hottest* and *more better* mark the comparison twice. Use either the ending or *more/most*, never both.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing -ed and -ing"}
*I am interesting in science* means you are fascinating to others. Say *I am interested in science*; the subject is *interesting*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Leaving out other in comparisons"}
*Harare is bigger than any city in Zimbabwe* is illogical, because Harare is a city in Zimbabwe. Write *bigger than any other city in Zimbabwe*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Grading absolute adjectives"}
*Very unique* and *very dead* are wrong in formal writing. Something is either unique or it is not.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When completing a sentence with the correct form of a word in brackets, look for signal words: *than* needs a comparative, *of all* or *in the whole school* needs a superlative, *as ... as* needs the plain adjective.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a rewriting task, the words given at the start decide the structure. If you are given *Nyasha is taller ...*, you must continue with *than*; switching to *the tallest* earns no mark even though it means the same.
:::

:::exam-tip
In descriptive compositions, choose one precise adjective rather than piling up vague ones: *a gaunt dog* is stronger than *a very thin, weak, hungry-looking dog*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Adjectives describe nouns and pronouns; they stand before a noun or after a linking verb.
- Many are formed with suffixes: -ful, -less, -ous, -al, -able/-ible, -ive, -ish, -y.
- Comparatives (-er, more) compare two and take *than*; superlatives (the -est, the most) pick one from a group. Never mark the comparison twice. Learn the irregular forms (good, better, best; bad, worse, worst).
- Add *other* when comparing a member with the rest of its own group.
- Order before a noun: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose.
- -ing adjectives describe the cause; -ed adjectives describe the feeling.
- Non-gradable adjectives take completely or absolutely, not very.
:::
