## Introduction

*The rains came late. The maize wilted. The farmers planted again. They had little seed left.* Four short sentences, four separate facts. A skilled writer shows how the facts connect: *Because the rains came late, the maize wilted; the farmers planted again, although they had little seed left.*

The words that make those connections are **conjunctions** and **discourse markers**. They appear in almost every English paper. In the language section you are often given two sentences and asked to *join them using the word given* or to *rewrite beginning with* a word such as *Although*, *Unless* or *Not only*. In argumentative and discursive compositions, markers such as *furthermore* and *on the other hand* guide the reader through your argument and earn credit for organisation.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Conjunction"}
A word that joins words, phrases or clauses and shows how the ideas relate: *and, but, because, although, unless*.
:::

:::definition{term="Co-ordinating conjunction"}
One of the seven conjunctions that join items of equal importance: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so* (FANBOYS).
:::

:::definition{term="Subordinating conjunction"}
A conjunction that begins a subordinate clause, which depends on a main clause: *because, although, if, unless, when, while, until, since, so that*.
:::

:::definition{term="Discourse marker"}
A word or phrase that signals how a sentence or paragraph connects to what came before: *however, therefore, moreover, for instance, in conclusion*.
:::

## Co-ordinating conjunctions

| Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| and | addition | *She cooked sadza **and** I fetched water.* |
| but / yet | contrast | *I was tired, **but** I finished my homework.* |
| or | choice | *We can walk **or** we can take a kombi.* |
| so | result | *It was dark, **so** we lit a candle.* |
| nor | negative addition | *He did not call, **nor** did he write.* |
| for | reason (formal) | *She rested, **for** she had walked far.* |

## Subordinating conjunctions

A subordinate clause cannot stand alone: *Because it was raining.* is not a sentence. It must be attached to a main clause.

| Relationship | Conjunctions | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | when, while, before, after, until, as soon as, since | *We waited **until** the storm had passed.* |
| Reason | because, as, since | ***Since** you are here, you can help me.* |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that, in case | *Take an umbrella **in case** it rains.* |
| Contrast / concession | although, though, even though, whereas, while | ***Although** he trained hard, he lost.* |
| Purpose | so that, in order that | *She saved **so that** she could buy a radio.* |
| Result | so ... that, such ... that | *It was **so** hot **that** we rested.* |

:::example{title="Unless and in case"}
- ***Unless** you hurry, you will miss the bus.* (= If you do not hurry ...)
- Incorrect: *Unless you do not hurry ...* (*unless* already contains *not*)
- *Take a jersey **in case** it gets cold.* (as a precaution: it may or may not get cold)
:::

## Correlative conjunctions

These work in pairs, and each half should be followed by the same kind of word or phrase:

- *either ... or*: *You can **either** walk **or** cycle.*
- *neither ... nor*: ***Neither** Rudo **nor** her sister came.*
- *both ... and*: ***Both** the teacher **and** the pupils laughed.*
- *not only ... but also*: *She is **not only** strict **but also** fair.*
- *whether ... or*: *I do not know **whether** to stay **or** leave.*

:::example{title="Joining with not only ... but also"}
*Chipo sings in the school choir. She also plays netball for the province.*

→ *Chipo **not only** sings in the school choir **but also** plays netball for the province.*

Both halves begin with a verb (*sings*, *plays*), so the sentence is balanced.
:::

## Linking adverbs and discourse markers

*However, therefore, moreover, furthermore, nevertheless, consequently, otherwise* are **linking adverbs**, not conjunctions. They cannot join two main clauses with only a comma.

:::example{title="Punctuating however"}
- Correct: *The bus broke down; **however,** we arrived on time.*
- Correct: *The bus broke down. **However,** we arrived on time.*
- Correct (with a conjunction): *The bus broke down, **but** we arrived on time.*
- Incorrect: *The bus broke down, however we arrived on time.*
:::

| Function | Markers |
| --- | --- |
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides, also |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, on the other hand, in contrast |
| Result | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as |
| Sequence | firstly, then, subsequently, finally |
| Conclusion | in conclusion, to sum up, overall |

## Joining sentences in the examination

When joining with a given word, follow three checks:

1. **Keep the meaning.** Do not add or remove facts.
2. **Use only one joining word for one relationship.** *Although ... but* and *Because ... so* are double joins.
3. **Follow the words given exactly**, and adjust the rest (pronouns, *not*, *also*) so that the sentence is grammatical.

:::example{title="Worked joins"}
*The pupils were exhausted. They continued to dig the school garden.* (Begin: *Although ...*)
→ ***Although the pupils were exhausted, they continued to dig the school garden.***

*You must hurry. If you do not, you will miss the bus.* (Begin: *Unless ...*)
→ ***Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus.***

*The bridge was washed away. The pupils could not reach school.* (Use: *therefore*)
→ *The bridge was washed away; **therefore,** the pupils could not reach school.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Double joining words"}
*Although it rained, but we played* uses two contrast words. Keep only one: *Although it rained, we played* or *It rained, but we played*.
:::

:::mistake{title="The comma splice with however"}
*It was late, however we continued* joins two sentences with a comma. Use a semicolon or a full stop before *however*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Starting a sentence fragment"}
*Because the bus was late.* on its own is not a sentence. Attach the subordinate clause to a main clause.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mismatched pairs"}
*Neither ... or*, *not only ... and also* and *both ... as well as* are wrong. Learn the pairs exactly.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
After joining two sentences, read your answer aloud and compare it with the original: same facts, one joining word, the given beginning used exactly, and correct punctuation.
:::

:::exam-tip
In argumentative writing, start new paragraphs with varied markers (*Furthermore, ...*, *On the other hand, ...*, *In conclusion, ...*) instead of repeating *and* or *also*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Co-ordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) join equal clauses; subordinating conjunctions begin clauses that cannot stand alone.
- Choose the conjunction by the relationship: addition, contrast, reason, result, condition, time, purpose.
- Correlatives come in fixed pairs: either/or, neither/nor, both/and, not only/but also.
- However, therefore and moreover are linking adverbs: use a semicolon or full stop before them and a comma after.
- Use one joining word per relationship: never although ... but. Unless already means if not.
:::
