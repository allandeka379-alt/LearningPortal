## Introduction

*Tatenda bought a new pen. Tatenda put Tatenda's pen in Tatenda's bag.* Without pronouns, writing becomes clumsy and repetitive. With them: *Tatenda bought a new pen. **He** put **it** in **his** bag.*

Pronouns are small words, but examiners watch them closely. A wrong case (*him and me went*), a reflexive used as a subject (*myself and my friend*), an apostrophe in a possessive (*it's tail*) or a pronoun that could refer to two different people all cost marks in compositions and in the language section of ZIMSEC Paper 2. This lesson shows you each type of pronoun and the simple tests that help you choose the right one.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pronoun"}
A word used in place of a noun or noun phrase to avoid repeating it: *he, it, theirs, herself, who, this, everyone*.
:::

:::definition{term="Antecedent"}
The noun that a pronoun refers back to. In *The boys finished their work*, *the boys* is the antecedent of *their*.
:::

:::definition{term="Case"}
The form a pronoun takes according to its job: **subject** form (*I, he, she, we, they, who*) or **object** form (*me, him, her, us, them, whom*).
:::

:::definition{term="Relative pronoun"}
A pronoun that introduces a clause giving more information about a noun: *who, whom, whose, which, that*.
:::

## Personal pronouns

| | Subject | Object | Possessive determiner | Possessive pronoun | Reflexive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st singular | I | me | my | mine | myself |
| 2nd | you | you | your | yours | yourself / yourselves |
| 3rd singular | he / she / it | him / her / it | his / her / its | his / hers / — | himself / herself / itself |
| 1st plural | we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| 3rd plural | they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

Use the **subject** form when the pronoun does the action, and the **object** form after a verb or a preposition.

:::example{title="The cover-up test for pairs"}
*My cousin and (I/me) are going to Hwange.* Cover *my cousin and*: *I am going* → ***My cousin and I** are going.*
*The coach picked Tafara and (I/me).* Cover *Tafara and*: *The coach picked me* → *The coach picked **Tafara and me**.*
It is polite to mention yourself last: *my cousin and I*, not *I and my cousin*.
:::

:::example{title="Comparisons with than and as"}
*My brother runs faster than (I/me).* Finish the sentence in your head: *faster than I (run)*. In formal writing: *faster than **I***.
*She is as tall as (he/him).* → *as tall as **he** (is)*.
:::

## Possessive pronouns

Possessive pronouns stand alone: *The red bicycle is **ours**. Is this jersey **yours**?* They **never** take an apostrophe: *hers, its, ours, yours, theirs*.

:::example{title="its or it's?"}
*It's* means *it is* or *it has*: ***It's** raining. **It's** been a long day.*
*Its* shows possession: *The school celebrated **its** fiftieth anniversary.*
Test: if you can replace the word with *it is*, use *it's*.
:::

## Reflexive and emphatic pronouns

Reflexive pronouns (*-self, -selves*) are used when the subject and the object are the same person: *Rufaro hurt **herself** while playing netball.* They can also emphasise: *The minister **himself** opened the bridge.*

:::example{title="Reflexives cannot be subjects"}
Incorrect: *The secretary and myself will prepare the hall.*
Correct: *The secretary and **I** will prepare the hall.*
Incorrect: *Please send the forms to Mr Zulu or myself.*
Correct: *Please send the forms to Mr Zulu or **me**.*
Also avoid *hisself* and *theirselves*: the correct forms are *himself* and *themselves*.
:::

## Relative pronouns

| Pronoun | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| who | people (subject) | *The girl **who** won the race is my neighbour.* |
| whom | people (object, formal) | *The man **whom** we met at the gate was the bursar.* |
| whose | possession | *The boy **whose** goat strayed came to collect it.* |
| which | animals and things | *The road **which** leads to the mission is tarred.* |
| that | people, animals and things (defining clauses) | *The phone **that** I lost has been found.* |

:::example{title="who or whom?"}
To decide in formal writing, answer the question in the clause with *he* or *him*:
*The athlete (who/whom) the coaches selected ...* → *the coaches selected **him*** → ***whom***.
*The athlete (who/whom) broke the record ...* → ***he** broke the record* → ***who***.
:::

Do not use *which* for people (*the teacher which* is wrong) and do not use *what* as a relative pronoun (*the book what I read* is wrong).

## Demonstrative and indefinite pronouns

**Demonstratives** point: *this/these* (near), *that/those* (far). *This is my seat; those are yours.*

**Indefinite pronouns** do not refer to a particular person or thing: *someone, anybody, nothing, each, either, neither, everyone, all, both, several*. Most ending in *-one, -body, -thing* are singular: ***Everything is** ready.*

:::note{title="Everyone ... their"}
In modern British English, *Everyone must bring their own lunch* is widely accepted. In very formal writing some people prefer *his or her*. Either way, make sure the verb is singular: *Everyone **has**...*
:::

## Clear reference

Every pronoun must point clearly to one antecedent.

:::example{title="Removing confusion"}
Unclear: *When Mr Gumbo met the new teacher, he was nervous.* (Who was nervous?)
Clear: *Mr Gumbo was nervous when he met the new teacher.* or *The new teacher was nervous when Mr Gumbo met him.*
Unclear: *The bag was on the table, so I cleaned it.* (The bag or the table?)
Clear: *The bag was on the table, so I moved it and cleaned the table.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Me and my friend as a subject"}
*Me and my friend went to the dam* is wrong in writing. Write *My friend and I went to the dam.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Apostrophes in possessive pronouns"}
*Your's, her's, our's* and *their's* are always wrong. No possessive pronoun has an apostrophe.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shifting pronouns"}
*If one practises daily, you will improve* switches from *one* to *you*. Keep the same pronoun: *If you practise daily, you will improve.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Using they for a singular noun with the wrong verb"}
*The school has changed their uniform* mixes a singular verb with a plural pronoun. Write *The school has changed **its** uniform.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For any *I/me*, *he/him* or *who/whom* choice, remove the other words and test the pronoun on its own, or answer the question with *he/him*.
:::

:::exam-tip
When proofreading a composition, check every *it*, *they* and *he*: can the reader tell at once whom or what it means? If not, repeat the noun.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Use subject pronouns (*I, he, we, who*) for the doer; object pronouns (*me, him, us, whom*) after verbs and prepositions.
- Possessive pronouns never take apostrophes; *it's* means *it is*.
- Reflexive pronouns need an antecedent in the same sentence and cannot be subjects.
- *Who* for people, *which* for things, *whose* for possession, *that* for either in defining clauses.
- Every pronoun must agree with, and clearly refer to, its antecedent.
:::
