## Introduction

*The list of candidates are on the notice board.* Many readers would not notice the error, but an examiner will. The verb must agree with the subject *list*, not with *candidates*, so the sentence should read *The list of candidates **is** on the notice board*.

Agreement errors appear in every kind of writing: compositions, letters to the editor, reports for a school magazine, and the sentences you are asked to correct in the language section of ZIMSEC Paper 2. Most of them come from a small number of patterns. Once you know the patterns, you can find the subject quickly and choose the right verb every time.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Subject"}
The person, thing or idea that does the action of the verb or that the sentence is about: ***The new clinic** opens on Monday.*
:::

:::definition{term="Agreement (concord)"}
The rule that a verb takes a singular form after a singular subject and a plural form after a plural subject: *the girl **runs**, the girls **run***.
:::

:::definition{term="Compound subject"}
Two or more subjects joined by *and*, *or* or *nor*: *Rudo and Tendai*, *either the bus or the train*.
:::

:::definition{term="Indefinite pronoun"}
A pronoun that does not refer to a particular person or thing: *everyone, somebody, nothing, each, either, neither*.
:::

## The basic rule

In the present simple, a singular third-person subject takes a verb ending in *-s*; a plural subject does not. The same applies to *is/are*, *was/were* and *has/have*.

:::example{title="Singular and plural verbs"}
*The farmer **grows** maize. The farmers **grow** maize.*
*My cousin **was** late. My cousins **were** late.*
*The pump **has** broken. The pumps **have** broken.*
Notice that the *-s* goes on the noun in the plural but on the verb in the singular.
:::

## Finding the true subject

Phrases beginning with *of, with, along with, as well as, together with, including* and *in addition to* do not change the subject. Cover them up and look at what is left.

:::example{title="Covering the phrase in the middle"}
Incorrect: *The price of school shoes **have** gone up.*
Correct: *The price (of school shoes) **has** gone up.* The subject is *price*.
Correct: *The headmaster, together with two teachers, **is** attending the workshop.* The subject is *the headmaster*; *together with two teachers* is extra information.
:::

The same care is needed when the subject comes after the verb, as in sentences beginning with *there* or *here*.

:::example{title="Sentences beginning with there"}
*There **is** a dam near the village.* (one dam)
*There **are** three boreholes near the village.* (three boreholes)
Incorrect: *There **is** many reasons for the drought.* The subject *reasons* is plural, so write *There **are** many reasons*.
:::

## Compound subjects

- Subjects joined by **and** are usually plural: *Chipo and her brother **are** in Form 3.*
- When the two parts form **one idea or one person**, use a singular verb: *Sadza and relish **is** our usual supper. The secretary and treasurer of the club **has** resigned* (one person holds both jobs).
- With **either ... or** and **neither ... nor**, the verb agrees with the subject **nearer to it**: *Neither the captain nor the players **were** happy. Neither the players nor the captain **was** happy.*

:::example{title="The nearer subject decides"}
*Either my parents or my aunt **is** coming to the prize-giving.* (*aunt* is nearer: singular)
*Either my aunt or my parents **are** coming to the prize-giving.* (*parents* is nearer: plural)
Both are correct; the second usually sounds more natural, so put the plural subject last.
:::

## Each, every and indefinite pronouns

*Each, every, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, anyone, nobody, no one* and *nothing* take a **singular** verb, even when they seem to describe many people.

:::example{title="Singular after each and every"}
*Every pupil and every teacher **was** given a badge.*
*Each of the girls **has** a library card.* (The subject is *each*, not *girls*.)
*Everybody **knows** the answer.*
:::

*Both, few, many* and *several* are always plural: *Many **have** applied, but few **were** chosen.*

## Collective nouns

In British English, a collective noun (*team, committee, family, class, government*) may take a singular verb when the group acts as one unit, and a plural verb when you are thinking of the members as individuals. Whichever you choose, **be consistent** in the rest of the sentence.

:::example{title="Being consistent"}
*The committee **has** made **its** decision.* (one unit: singular verb, singular pronoun)
*The committee **are** arguing among **themselves**.* (individual members: plural verb, plural pronoun)
Incorrect: *The committee **has** made **their** decision.* (singular verb but plural pronoun)
:::

Some nouns are always plural: *police, cattle, people, clothes, trousers, scissors*. *The police **are** investigating the break-in.*

## Nouns that look plural

Some nouns end in *-s* but are singular: *news, mathematics, physics, economics, measles*. *The news **was** better than we expected. Mathematics **is** my favourite subject.*

Amounts of money, time and distance seen as one total are also singular: *Fifty dollars **is** a lot for a textbook. Ten kilometres **is** too far to walk to school.*

## A number of / the number of

*A number of* means "several" and takes a plural verb; *the number of* refers to the figure itself and takes a singular verb.

:::example{title="Two similar phrases"}
*A number of pupils **were** absent because of the rain.*
*The number of pupils in Form 1 **has** doubled.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Agreeing with the nearest noun instead of the subject"}
*The quality of the roads are poor* is wrong: the subject is *quality*, so write *is*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating 'one of' as plural"}
*One of my friends live in Kwekwe* is wrong. The subject is *one*: *One of my friends **lives** in Kwekwe.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating 'as well as' like 'and'"}
*The principal as well as the teachers were present* is wrong. *As well as* adds information but does not make the subject plural: *The principal, as well as the teachers, **was** present.*
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the verb in a there sentence"}
*There's lots of things to do* is common in speech, but in formal writing use *There **are** many things to do*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To check a verb, say the subject and the verb on their own: *list ... is*, *one ... lives*, *each ... has*. If the short pair sounds right, the full sentence is right.
:::

:::exam-tip
In correction items, the error is often hidden by a long phrase between the subject and the verb. Underline the subject first.
:::

:::exam-tip
Avoid testing yourself on collective nouns that can go either way. In your own writing, choose one form and keep it, including in the pronouns that follow.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.
- Ignore phrases between the subject and the verb (*of ..., as well as ..., together with ...*).
- *And* usually makes a plural subject; with *either/or* and *neither/nor*, the nearer subject decides.
- *Each, every* and *-one/-body/-thing* words are singular; *both, few, many, several* are plural.
- *News, mathematics, measles* and single amounts take singular verbs; *police, cattle, people* take plural verbs.
- *A number of* is plural; *the number of* is singular.
:::
