## Introduction

*Let's eat, Gogo.* *Let's eat Gogo.* One comma decides whether your grandmother is invited to dinner or served as it. Punctuation is not decoration: it tells the reader where ideas begin and end, who is speaking, and what belongs to whom.

Examiners notice punctuation in every piece of writing you produce. In compositions, missing full stops (sentences that run on and on joined by commas) are among the most common reasons for losing accuracy marks. In the language section you may be asked to punctuate a sentence, to choose the correctly punctuated version, or to rewrite reported speech as direct speech. A sign at a market reading *Fresh tomato's* is a reminder of how often apostrophes go wrong.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Punctuation"}
The marks used in writing to separate sentences and parts of sentences and to make the meaning clear: . , ? ! ' : ; – ( ) " and the hyphen.
:::

:::definition{term="Comma splice"}
The error of joining two complete sentences with only a comma: *It was late, we went home.*
:::

:::definition{term="Direct speech"}
The exact words someone said, placed inside speech marks: *"We leave at dawn," said Baba.*
:::

## Ending sentences

- **Full stop** ( . ): ends statements and commands. *Close the gate.*
- **Question mark** ( ? ): ends direct questions only. *Where are the keys?* An indirect question is a statement: *She asked where the keys were.*
- **Exclamation mark** ( ! ): shows strong feeling or a sharp command. *Watch out!* Use it sparingly in formal writing, and never use several at once.

## Capital letters

Use capitals for the first word of a sentence, the pronoun *I*, names of people and places (*Tendai, Kwekwe, the Zambezi*), days and months (*Monday, March*), titles before names (*Mrs Moyo, Dr Sibanda*), organisations (*ZIMSEC*), languages and nationalities (*Shona, Zambian*), and the main words of titles (*Our Home in the Hills*). Seasons do not usually take capitals: *in winter*.

## The comma

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Between items in a list | *We bought tomatoes, onions, rice and cooking oil.* |
| After an introductory clause or phrase | *When the bell rang, we left.* / *However, nobody came.* |
| Around extra (non-defining) information | *My brother, who lives in Bulawayo, is a teacher.* |
| Before a co-ordinating conjunction joining long clauses | *The bus was full, so we decided to walk.* |
| To mark off the person spoken to | *Let's eat, Gogo.* / *Thank you, Doctor.* |
| In direct speech | *"I am ready," she said.* |

:::example{title="Commas change meaning"}
- *My brother, who lives in Bulawayo, is a teacher.* — the writer has one brother; the clause adds information.
- *My brother who lives in Bulawayo is a teacher.* — the writer has more than one brother; the clause tells us which one.
:::

Never use a comma alone to join two sentences:

:::example{title="Fixing a comma splice"}
- Wrong: *It was late, we went home.*
- Right: *It was late. We went home.* / *It was late, so we went home.* / *It was late; we went home.*
:::

## The apostrophe

### Possession

| Owner | Rule | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One owner | add *'s* | *the girl's bag*, *Mr Banda's kombi*, *James's book* |
| Plural owner ending in *s* | add *'* | *the girls' bags*, *the teachers' room* |
| Plural owner not ending in *s* | add *'s* | *the children's books*, *the women's hats* |
| Time expressions | same rules | *a day's work*, *two weeks' time* |

### Omission (missing letters)

*don't* (do not), *can't* (cannot), *I'm* (I am), *it's* (it is / it has), *they're* (they are), *who's* (who is).

:::example{title="Its and it's"}
- ***It's** late, so the dog has gone to **its** kennel.*
- *It's* always means *it is* or *it has*. *Its* (belonging to it) has no apostrophe, like *his, hers, ours, theirs*.
:::

Never use an apostrophe to make a plural: *Fresh tomatoes for sale*, not *tomato's*.

## Colons and semicolons

**Colon** ( : ) — after a complete clause, introduces a list, an explanation or a quotation:

- *Bring the following tools: a hoe, a bucket and a hat.*
- *There was only one problem: nobody had the keys.*
- Do not put a colon directly after a verb: *Bring: a hoe ...* is wrong.

**Semicolon** ( ; ) — joins two closely related main clauses without a conjunction, or separates long list items that already contain commas:

- *The rains were late; the farmers were worried.*
- *The team visited Mutare, in the east; Hwange, in the west; and Beitbridge, in the south.*
- Each side of a joining semicolon must be able to stand alone as a sentence.

## Dashes, brackets and hyphens

- **Dash** ( – ): a sudden break, an afterthought or a dramatic pause. *We opened the box and found – nothing.* A pair of dashes can enclose an added comment.
- **Brackets** ( ) : extra information that could be removed without damaging the sentence. *The clinic (built in 1998) is being repaired.*
- **Hyphen** ( - ): joins words into one unit. *a well-known musician*, *a four-year-old child*, *twenty-one*, *ex-pupil*. Hyphenate compound adjectives before a noun.

## Direct speech

Follow these rules:

1. Put speech marks around the **exact words** spoken.
2. Start the spoken words with a **capital letter**.
3. Put the speaker's punctuation (**, ? !** or **.**) **inside** the closing speech marks.
4. If the sentence continues after the speech (*asked the teacher*), use a **small letter**.
5. Start a **new paragraph** for each new speaker.

:::example{title="Punctuated dialogue"}
"Don't touch that wire!" shouted Mr Dube. "It's still live."

"Is it dangerous?" asked Rudo.

"Very," he replied, pulling her back.
:::

:::example{title="From reported to direct speech"}
Reported: *The headmaster told the pupils that they could go home early because the pump had broken.*

Direct: *"You may go home early because the pump has broken," the headmaster told the pupils.*

The pronoun changes (*they* → *you*), the tense moves forward (*had broken* → *has broken*) and only the spoken words go inside the speech marks.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="The comma splice"}
*We were tired, we went to bed* joins two sentences with a comma. Use a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Apostrophes in plurals and possessive pronouns"}
*Banana's for sale*, *it's tail*, *her's*: plurals and possessive pronouns never take apostrophes.
:::

:::mistake{title="Question marks after indirect questions"}
*He asked me what time it was?* is wrong. The sentence is a statement: end it with a full stop.
:::

:::mistake{title="Punctuation outside speech marks"}
*"Where are you going"? she asked.* The question mark belongs to the spoken words, so it goes inside.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Leave time to proofread your composition for punctuation only: look for comma splices, missing full stops, apostrophes in plurals and unpunctuated dialogue. Reading your work aloud in your head helps you hear where sentences end.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to punctuate a sentence, work in order: capital letters first, then end marks, then speech marks and the punctuation inside them, then commas, colons and apostrophes.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Full stops end statements and commands; question marks only follow direct questions.
- Capitals begin sentences and proper nouns, titles, days, months and the pronoun I.
- Commas separate list items, follow introductory clauses, enclose extra information and mark off the person addressed. Never join two sentences with a comma alone.
- Apostrophes show possession (girl's, girls', children's) or missing letters (don't, it's), never plurals. Its = belonging to it.
- Colons introduce lists and explanations; semicolons join closely related main clauses.
- Dashes mark breaks, brackets enclose removable information, hyphens join compound words.
- In direct speech, the speaker's punctuation goes inside the speech marks, and each new speaker starts a new paragraph.
:::
