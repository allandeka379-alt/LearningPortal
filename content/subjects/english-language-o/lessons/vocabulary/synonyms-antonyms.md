## Introduction

A good writer does not say *nice* five times in one paragraph. A meal can be *delicious*, a view *breathtaking*, a teacher *patient* and a day *pleasant*. Each word is more precise than *nice*, and each paints a clearer picture.

Synonyms and antonyms appear throughout the English examinations. Comprehension questions ask you to *give one word with the same meaning as* a word in the passage, or to explain a phrase *in your own words*. Summary questions reward you for rewording the passage rather than copying it. In compositions, examiners look for a **wide and precise vocabulary**. All of these depend on knowing words with similar and opposite meanings, and on choosing the one that fits.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Synonym"}
A word with the same or nearly the same meaning as another word: *begin* and *start*; *brave* and *courageous*.
:::

:::definition{term="Antonym"}
A word with the opposite meaning: *generous* and *mean*; *ancient* and *modern*.
:::

:::definition{term="Connotation"}
The feeling or idea a word suggests beyond its basic meaning. *Slim* and *skinny* both mean *thin*, but *slim* sounds positive and *skinny* sounds negative.
:::

:::definition{term="Register"}
How formal or informal a word is. *Purchase* is more formal than *buy*; *kids* is less formal than *children*.
:::

## Synonyms are rarely identical

Very few synonyms can replace each other in every sentence. They differ in **strength**, **connotation**, **register** and **collocation** (the words they normally go with).

:::example{title="Strength"}
*warm → hot → scorching*: each is stronger than the one before.
*dislike → hate → detest*: *detest* is the strongest.
Choose the strength that matches the situation. A day of 25 °C in Harare is *warm*, not *scorching*.
:::

:::example{title="Connotation"}
*The minister was **determined** to finish the project.* (positive: shows strength of will)
*The minister was **stubborn** about the project.* (negative: suggests refusing to listen)
Both describe someone who will not give up, but the writer's attitude is completely different.
:::

:::example{title="Register"}
Formal letter: *I wish to **request** further information about the post.*
Informal email: *Can I **ask** you something about the job?*
In a formal letter to a council, *kids*, *loads of* and *get* should be replaced with *children*, *many* and *obtain* or *receive*.
:::

:::example{title="Collocation"}
*Large* and *big* are synonyms, but we say *a **large** amount of money* and *a **big** mistake* more naturally than the other way round. *Strong* and *powerful* are synonyms, but we say *strong tea*, not *powerful tea*.
:::

## Finding the synonym that fits the context

When an examination asks for a word with the same meaning as a word **in the passage**, the right answer depends on how the word is used there. Many words have more than one meaning.

:::example{title="One word, several meanings"}
*The rally drew a **fair** crowd.* → fair = *reasonably large*
*The referee's decision was **fair**.* → fair = *just, reasonable*
*She has **fair** hair.* → fair = *light in colour*
Always replace the word in the sentence with your answer and check that the sentence still makes sense and keeps the same meaning.
:::

The same rule works for antonyms: the opposite of *light* in *a light bag* is *heavy*, but the opposite of *light* in *a light room* is *dark*.

## Forming antonyms

Many antonyms are separate words: *rich / poor, victory / defeat, accept / refuse, expand / contract*. Others are formed with a **negative prefix**.

| Prefix | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| un- | unkind, unusual, unfortunate |
| in- | incomplete, inaccurate, inexpensive |
| im- | impatient, impolite, immature |
| il- / ir- | illegible, irresponsible |
| dis- | disagree, dishonest, disloyal |
| mis- | misunderstand, mislead |

Some words take a suffix instead: *careful / careless*, *useful / useless*, *harmful / harmless*.

:::example{title="Antonyms in context"}
*The new manager was **generous** with praise, unlike her **mean** predecessor.*
*Some rural schools have **modern** laboratories, while others still use **outdated** equipment.*
Using antonyms in this way creates **contrast**, which is useful in argumentative and discursive essays.
:::

## Building your word bank

Learn words in groups rather than singly. Here are some useful groups for compositions.

| Plain word | More precise choices |
| --- | --- |
| said | replied, whispered, insisted, admitted, complained, announced |
| walked | strolled, marched, staggered, wandered, hurried, crept |
| happy | delighted, content, cheerful, overjoyed, relieved |
| big | huge, enormous, vast, spacious, massive |
| bad | poor, harmful, dreadful, severe, faulty |

Notice that these are not interchangeable: you *stagger* when you are injured or exhausted, but *stroll* when you are relaxed.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing a synonym of the wrong meaning"}
In *The farmer will **fair** well this year*, the writer means *fare* (to get on). Make sure the meaning you choose is the one used in the sentence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a thesaurus word you do not understand"}
*The party was very **voracious*** is wrong: *voracious* describes a huge appetite, not a lively event. Only use words whose meaning and usage you know.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing registers"}
*Dear Sir, I am writing to ask for loads of information* mixes a formal opening with an informal phrase. Write *a considerable amount of information* or simply *further information*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked for a one-word synonym from a passage, give a word of the **same word class** as the original: a noun for a noun, an adjective for an adjective. Then test it in the sentence.
:::

:::exam-tip
In summaries and "own words" questions, replace the key words of the passage with synonyms, but keep the meaning exact. A synonym with a different strength or connotation can lose the mark.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- **Synonyms** have similar meanings; **antonyms** have opposite meanings.
- Synonyms differ in strength, connotation (positive or negative feeling), register (formal or informal) and collocation.
- The right synonym or antonym depends on the meaning of the word **in its context**.
- Many antonyms are formed with prefixes (*un-, in-, im-, il-, ir-, dis-*) or with *-less* instead of *-ful*.
- Test every synonym by putting it in the original sentence.
:::
