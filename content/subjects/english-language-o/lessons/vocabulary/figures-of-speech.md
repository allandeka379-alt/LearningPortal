## Introduction

*The sun was a furnace over the Zambezi Valley.* The sun is not really a furnace, but the sentence makes you feel the heat far more strongly than *It was very hot.* This is a **figure of speech**: language used in a non-literal or patterned way to create an effect.

Figures of speech matter in two parts of the examinations. In reading, Cambridge Paper 1 and the ZIMSEC comprehension ask you to explain how a writer's choice of words creates an effect, and naming the device is only the start: you must say **what it makes the reader see, feel or think**. In writing, a few well-chosen images lift a descriptive or narrative composition from ordinary to memorable.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Figure of speech"}
A use of words that goes beyond their ordinary, literal meaning to create an image, emphasis or sound effect.
:::

:::definition{term="Literal meaning"}
The plain, exact meaning of the words. *It is raining heavily* is literal; *It is raining cats and dogs* is not.
:::

:::definition{term="Effect"}
What a figure of speech makes the reader see, feel or understand: for example, a sense of danger, admiration, humour or speed.
:::

## Comparisons: simile and metaphor

A **simile** compares two different things using **like** or **as**. A **metaphor** says that one thing **is** another, without *like* or *as*.

:::example{title="Simile and metaphor compared"}
Simile: *The old bus groaned up the hill **like** a tired ox.*
Metaphor: *The old bus **was** a tired ox groaning up the hill.*
Both suggest the bus is slow, heavy and struggling. The metaphor is more direct and forceful because it does not say *like*.
:::

Be careful: not every *like* is a simile. *I like mangoes* and *She looks like her mother* are literal statements. A simile compares two things that are **basically different**.

## Personification

**Personification** gives human qualities or actions to something that is not human.

:::example{title="Personification"}
*The wind **whispered** through the msasa trees.*
*The angry river **swallowed** the bridge.*
Wind cannot whisper and rivers are not angry. The first creates a calm, gentle mood; the second makes the river seem violent and deliberate, which builds a sense of danger.
:::

## Exaggeration and understatement

**Hyperbole** is deliberate exaggeration for emphasis or humour: *I have told you a million times to close the gate.* Nobody has really said it a million times; the speaker is showing frustration.

A **euphemism** is a mild or polite way of saying something unpleasant: *passed away* (died), *between jobs* (unemployed), *let go* (dismissed). Euphemisms show tact or sensitivity, but they can also be used to hide an unpleasant truth.

## Sound devices

**Alliteration** is the repetition of the same consonant sound at the start of nearby words: *the silent, shimmering sands*. It makes a phrase memorable and the sound can match the mood: soft *s* sounds suggest calm; hard *b* and *k* sounds suggest force.

**Onomatopoeia** is a word that sounds like the noise it describes: *buzz, crash, hiss, sizzle, thud, splash*.

:::example{title="Sound devices in description"}
*The **boerewors sizzled** on the braai while **bees buzzed** busily in the bougainvillea.*
*Sizzled* and *buzzed* are onomatopoeia; *bees buzzed busily … bougainvillea* is alliteration, which gives the sentence a lively, busy rhythm.
:::

## Contrast and surprise: irony and oxymoron

**Irony** is when the intended meaning is the opposite of the words, or when events turn out the opposite of what was expected. *What lovely weather*, said during a thunderstorm, is verbal irony. A fire station burning down is situational irony.

An **oxymoron** places two opposite words side by side: *deafening silence, open secret, living dead, cruel kindness*. It captures a feeling that is complicated or contradictory.

## Devices of persuasion: rhetorical questions and repetition

A **rhetorical question** is asked for effect, not to get an answer: *Do we really want our rivers full of plastic?* The answer is obvious, so the reader is pushed to agree.

**Repetition** of words or structures adds emphasis and rhythm: *We will plant trees in our schools, trees in our streets and trees in our homes.* Both devices are common in speeches and argumentative writing.

## Explaining effects in the examination

Naming a device earns little or nothing on its own. A full answer has three parts: **identify** the words, **name or describe** the device, and **explain the effect** in context.

:::example{title="A weak answer and a strong answer"}
Extract: *The crowd was a hungry lion, roaring for a goal.*
Weak: *This is a metaphor. It is effective.*
Strong: *The metaphor compares the crowd to a hungry lion, suggesting that the spectators are fierce, impatient and almost dangerous in their desire for a goal. "Roaring" adds to this by making their noise sound like an animal's.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every comparison a simile"}
A simile needs *like* or *as* and a comparison between different things. *The crowd was a hungry lion* is a metaphor.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming the device but not the effect"}
*This is personification* earns little. Explain what the personification makes the reader feel or picture.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overloading your own writing"}
A paragraph with six similes reads like a list. Use a few original images where they matter most, and avoid tired clichés such as *as white as snow*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Use the formula **quote, name, explain**: give the words, name the device if you can, then explain the picture or feeling it creates and why that suits the passage.
:::

:::exam-tip
Words like *suggests*, *conveys*, *emphasises* and *creates a sense of* help you write about effects precisely.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- **Simile** compares with *like/as*; **metaphor** says one thing *is* another; **personification** gives human qualities to non-human things.
- **Hyperbole** exaggerates; **euphemism** softens; **irony** means the opposite of what is said or expected; an **oxymoron** joins opposites.
- **Alliteration** repeats initial consonant sounds; **onomatopoeia** imitates sounds.
- **Rhetorical questions** and **repetition** persuade and emphasise.
- In the exam, always explain the **effect** in context, not just the name of the device.
:::
