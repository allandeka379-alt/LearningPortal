## Introduction

If a friend tells you that the mathematics test was *a piece of cake*, they do not mean that anyone was eating. They mean the test was very easy. Phrases like this, whose meaning cannot be worked out from the separate words, are called **idioms**. English is full of them, and so are Shona and Ndebele: every language has sayings that only make sense once you know them.

Idioms and proverbs appear in comprehension passages, where you may be asked what a phrase means, and they can add colour to narrative and descriptive writing when used carefully. **Collocations**, the natural partnerships between words, matter even more: *do a mistake* instead of *make a mistake* immediately tells an examiner that your English is not yet secure. This lesson covers all three.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Idiom"}
A fixed group of words whose meaning is different from the meanings of the individual words: *to be over the moon* means *to be extremely happy*.
:::

:::definition{term="Proverb"}
A short, well-known saying that gives advice or states a general truth: *Look before you leap.*
:::

:::definition{term="Collocation"}
Two or more words that are often used together and sound natural to native speakers: *make a decision*, *heavy rain*, *strong tea*, *deeply grateful*.
:::

## Common idioms and their meanings

Learn idioms in groups. The table gives standard British English idioms that you will meet in reading passages.

| Idiom | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a piece of cake | very easy | The first question was a piece of cake. |
| over the moon | extremely happy | She was over the moon with her results. |
| under the weather | slightly unwell | I feel under the weather today. |
| break the ice | make people feel relaxed at first meeting | A game helped to break the ice. |
| hit the nail on the head | describe exactly what is right | You hit the nail on the head. |
| let the cat out of the bag | reveal a secret by mistake | He let the cat out of the bag about the party. |
| once in a blue moon | very rarely | It snows here once in a blue moon. |
| cost an arm and a leg | be very expensive | That phone cost an arm and a leg. |
| burn the midnight oil | work or study late at night | She burnt the midnight oil before exams. |
| a blessing in disguise | something that seems bad but turns out good | Missing the bus was a blessing in disguise. |
| on the same page | in agreement | Let's make sure we are on the same page. |
| turn a blind eye | pretend not to notice | The guard turned a blind eye to latecomers. |
| keep an eye on | watch carefully | Keep an eye on your bag at the bus rank. |
| get cold feet | become nervous and change your mind | He got cold feet before the speech. |

:::example{title="Idiom in a narrative paragraph"}
*Rufaro had **burnt the midnight oil** for weeks. When the results were pinned on the noticeboard, she found her name at the top of the list and was **over the moon**.*
Two idioms in a paragraph add life. Ten would make the writing sound forced.
:::

## Proverbs

Proverbs give advice. You may be asked what a proverb means or to choose the situation it fits.

| Proverb | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Look before you leap. | Think carefully before you act. |
| Too many cooks spoil the broth. | Too many people working on one task can ruin it. |
| Don't count your chickens before they are hatched. | Don't rely on something good before it has happened. |
| Actions speak louder than words. | What you do matters more than what you say. |
| Every cloud has a silver lining. | Something good can come out of a bad situation. |
| A stitch in time saves nine. | Fixing a small problem early prevents a bigger one. |
| Many hands make light work. | A job is easier when many people help. |
| Where there's a will, there's a way. | Determination finds a solution. |

:::example{title="Matching a proverb to a situation"}
*The school borehole pump was leaking slightly, but nobody repaired it. A month later the whole pump broke down and the school had no water for two weeks.*
The proverb that fits is ***A stitch in time saves nine***: a small repair early would have prevented the bigger breakdown.
:::

Notice that some proverbs seem to contradict each other: *Many hands make light work* but *Too many cooks spoil the broth*. Each is true in a different situation.

## Collocations

Collocations are not idioms: their meaning is clear from the words. But they are fixed in the sense that a different partner word sounds wrong.

| Pattern | Natural | Unnatural |
| --- | --- | --- |
| make + noun | make a decision, make a mistake, make progress, make an effort, make a noise | do a decision, do a mistake |
| do + noun | do homework, do the dishes, do business, do your best, do damage | make homework, make your best |
| adjective + noun | heavy rain, strong wind, strong tea, high temperature, heavy traffic | strong rain, powerful tea, big temperature |
| verb + noun | pay attention, keep a promise, catch a cold, take an examination | give attention, hold a promise |
| adverb + adjective | deeply grateful, highly unlikely, bitterly cold, fully aware | strongly grateful, heavily unlikely |

:::example{title="Collocations in a formal letter"}
*I am **fully aware** that the council has **made every effort** to repair the road, but the **heavy traffic** during the rainy season continues to **cause** serious **damage**.*
Every bold phrase is a natural collocation. Errors such as *do every effort* or *strong traffic* would lower the language mark.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Taking an idiom literally"}
If a passage says a man *had his head in the clouds*, do not answer that he was on a mountain. The idiom means he was daydreaming and not paying attention to reality.
:::

:::mistake{title="Changing the words of an idiom"}
Idioms are fixed. *A piece of bread* or *over the sun* are not idioms. Use the exact form: *a piece of cake*, *over the moon*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using idioms in formal writing"}
*The minister's speech was a piece of cake* is too informal for a report or formal letter. Idioms suit narratives, informal letters and dialogue; in formal writing use plain, precise words.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing make and do"}
*Make* is mostly for creating or producing (*make a cake, make a plan, make a mistake*); *do* is mostly for tasks and activities (*do homework, do the shopping*). Learn the common ones as fixed pairs.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked what an idiom means in a passage, give the **meaning in plain words**, not a word-for-word explanation. Check your answer fits the context of the sentence.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use one or two well-chosen idioms in a narrative or informal letter. Never use slang, and avoid idioms in formal letters, reports and speeches to officials.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An **idiom** has a meaning that cannot be worked out from its words: *over the moon* = very happy.
- A **proverb** is a short saying that gives advice: *Look before you leap.*
- A **collocation** is a natural word partnership: *make a decision, do homework, heavy rain, strong tea*.
- Idioms are fixed: do not change their words. Use them sparingly and not in formal writing.
- In comprehension, explain an idiom's meaning in plain words that fit the context.
:::
