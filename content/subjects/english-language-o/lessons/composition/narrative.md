## Introduction

Narrative titles are the most popular choice in composition papers: *"The stranger at the gate"*, *"A promise I could not keep"*, *"Write a story that ends with the words '…and that is why I never borrow anything again.'"* Many candidates choose them because they seem easy. That is exactly why so many narratives score in the middle band: they retell a string of events without shape, tension or a point.

Examiners in ZIMSEC Paper 1 and Cambridge 0500 Paper 2 reward stories that are **controlled**: one main event, a problem that builds, characters who feel real, and an ending that satisfies. This lesson shows you how.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Plot"}
The planned sequence of events in a story, usually: exposition (setting and characters), rising action (complications), climax (the turning point), falling action and resolution.
:::

:::definition{term="Conflict"}
The problem or struggle at the heart of a story: a person against another person, against nature, against society or against their own fear or conscience.
:::

:::definition{term="Point of view"}
Who tells the story. First person (*I*) gives closeness to one character's thoughts; third person (*she*, *he*, *they*) allows the narrator to stand back or move between characters.
:::

:::definition{term="Pace"}
The speed at which the story moves. Short sentences and quick action speed it up; description and reflection slow it down.
:::

:::definition{term="Flashback"}
A scene from an earlier time inserted into the main story to explain a character's feelings or a past event.
:::

## Shape: one event, one problem

A 350-450 word story has room for **one main event** told well, not a whole year. Use the plot mountain: a short opening, a rising problem, a climax, then a quick resolution. Most weak stories spend half their words getting out of bed.

:::example{title="Story shapes that work in an exam"}
- **The problem and the choice:** a character faces a dilemma (keep the lost money or return it?) and decides.
- **The misunderstanding:** something seems to be one thing and turns out to be another.
- **The test:** a character must survive or achieve something under pressure.
- **The circle:** the ending returns to an image or line from the opening, now changed in meaning.
:::

## Characters and dialogue

Show character through **action, speech and detail** rather than labels. *"He was a kind man"* tells; *"He slipped the last of his bread into the boy's pocket without a word"* shows.

Dialogue should move the story forward or reveal character, not report greetings. Keep it short and punctuate it correctly: new speaker, new paragraph; punctuation inside the inverted commas.

:::example{title="Dialogue punctuated correctly"}
"Did you lock the shed?" asked Baba, not looking up from the radio.

"Yes," I lied. "Twice."

He nodded slowly. "Good, because the Chiweshe boys were seen near the road again."
:::

## Pace, suspense and sensory detail

Slow down at the climax: describe the seconds that matter in detail. Speed through the less important parts with a summary sentence (*The next three days passed in a blur of rehearsals.*). Build **suspense** by delaying the answer, using short sentences and including small sounds or details. Include **senses** beyond sight: the smell of rain on hot soil, the clank of a gate chain.

## An annotated model story

**Title: "The parcel"** (about 420 words)

*[Opening that starts close to the problem]* The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, wrapped in brown paper and tied with blue string, and it was addressed to my grandmother, who had been dead for three months.

*[Character shown through action]* Mai Rufaro, the post office clerk at Murewa, slid it across the counter with both hands, the way people pass something fragile or sacred. "It was posted in Bulawayo in June," she said quietly. "It must have been lost in the sorting."

*[Building the problem: reactions, a decision]* I carried it home on my lap in the kombi, feeling every bump. At home, my mother turned it over twice and put it on the top shelf of the kitchen cupboard. "Not today," she said. Not today became not this week, and not this week became a silence nobody would break.

*[Flashback explains the tension]* Gogo and my mother had argued the last time they saw each other. I remembered the raised voices through the bedroom wall, the scrape of a chair, the gate slamming, and how my mother had sat on the step afterwards with her hands pressed against her eyes.

*[Rising action: a choice]* On the fourth evening, while my mother was at choir practice, I climbed onto a chair and lifted the parcel down. The string was knotted tightly. My fingers shook. *[Slowed pace at the climax]* I untied the first knot, then the second. The paper crackled like dry leaves. Inside lay a cardigan, knitted in uneven green wool, with a note pinned to the sleeve in Gogo's careful, slanting hand: *For Chiedza. I should have listened. Forgive a stubborn old woman.*

*[Short sentence for emphasis]* I sat very still.

*[Resolution through action, not explanation]* When my mother came home, I did not say anything. I simply held out the note. She read it once, then again, and her face folded the way paper folds along an old crease. She pulled the cardigan on over her church blouse, although the night was warm, and she wore it until she fell asleep in the chair.

*[Ending that echoes the opening]* The brown paper and blue string are still in my drawer. Some parcels, I learnt that year, take longer than three months to arrive.

### What makes it work

- It covers **four days**, focused on **one object**.
- The conflict (mother and grandmother) is revealed through a **flashback**, not a long explanation.
- Dialogue is brief and each line matters.
- Pace slows at the climax (each knot, the sound of paper).
- The last line returns to the parcel and gives it a new meaning.

## Endings

Avoid *"Then I woke up and it was all a dream"* (it cancels the story) and *"We went home and slept"* (it fades out). Strong endings resolve the problem, show a change in the narrator or echo the opening with a twist of meaning. If the title gives you an ending line, the whole story must lead naturally to it.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Too many events"}
A week-long trip with six adventures becomes a list. Choose one event and tell it in depth.
:::

:::mistake{title="Shifting tenses"}
Choose past tense (usual) or present tense and keep to it. *"I opened the door and I see a man"* is a tense slip.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dialogue that does nothing"}
*"Good morning." "Good morning, how are you?" "I am fine."* adds words but no story. Cut greetings.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the title"}
If the title is *"A promise I could not keep"*, the promise must be made early and the reader must see why it was broken.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Plan your climax first, then decide where to begin. The opening should be no more than a short paragraph away from the problem.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use names and settings you know (a kombi rank, a borehole, a school sports day) so that details feel real, but keep people fictional.
:::

## Checklist

- One main event with a clear conflict, climax and resolution.
- Consistent point of view and tense.
- Characters shown through action and speech.
- Dialogue that matters, punctuated correctly, new speaker on a new line.
- Pace slowed at the climax; suspense; detail from several senses.
- An ending that resolves, reflects or echoes; the title clearly answered.

## Summary

:::summary
A strong narrative tells one event well: an opening close to the problem, rising tension, a slowed-down climax and a satisfying ending. Show character through action and dialogue, keep tense and point of view consistent, use senses and flashback with purpose, and make sure the story answers the title.
:::
