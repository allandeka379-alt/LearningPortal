## Introduction

Informative (or expository) titles ask you to **explain**: *"How to prepare for examinations"*, *"The importance of wetlands"*, *"Explain how a traditional ceremony in your community is carried out"*, *"The causes and effects of drug abuse among young people."* The reader wants to understand something, so clarity matters more than drama.

In ZIMSEC Paper 1, informative titles are common and often connect to cross-cutting themes such as health, the environment, heritage and financial literacy. In Cambridge 0510, the formal article, report and essay tasks all draw on the same skills.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Expository writing"}
Writing that explains or informs, presenting facts, processes, causes and effects in a clear, logical and neutral way.
:::

:::definition{term="Neutral tone"}
A calm, objective voice that presents information without strong personal opinion or emotive language.
:::

:::definition{term="Signposting"}
Words and phrases that guide the reader through the structure: *firstly*, *the next stage*, *another cause*, *as a result*, *in summary*.
:::

## Choose an organising pattern

| Pattern | Use it for | Example title |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sequence (process) | Steps in order | *How to start a small poultry project* |
| Categories | Types or aspects | *The main kinds of pollution in towns* |
| Cause and effect | Why something happens and what follows | *The causes and effects of deforestation* |
| Problem and solution | A difficulty and ways to address it | *Reducing waste at school* |

Choose the pattern before you plan, and make each paragraph one stage, category, cause or solution.

## Accuracy without a library

You cannot check facts in an examination, so write what you **know**. Explain processes you understand, use general statements that are clearly true (*Wetlands store water and release it slowly*) and use examples from everyday life. Do **not** invent statistics, dates or quotations to sound impressive. If you need figures in a practice task, make it clear they come from a fictional survey, such as *a survey of forty learners at our school*.

## Define and explain

:::example{title="Defining a term inside a sentence"}
Define key terms the first time you use them, briefly and in your own words: *A wetland, known in many parts of Zimbabwe as a vlei, is low ground that stays waterlogged for much of the year.* Follow every general statement with an explanation or example.
:::

## An annotated model explanation

**Title: How to start a small vegetable garden at home** (about 330 words)

*[Introduction: purpose + why it matters + preview]* A small vegetable garden can supply a family with fresh food for much of the year, save money and make use of space that would otherwise be bare. Starting one needs no special equipment, only planning, patience and a few simple steps.

*[Stage 1 with signpost; explanation]* **The first step is choosing the site.** Vegetables need at least six hours of sunlight a day, so a spot away from tall trees and buildings is best. It should also be close to a water source, because carrying water a long way soon becomes tiring.

*[Stage 2; definition built in]* **Next, the soil must be prepared.** Dig the bed to about the depth of a spade, remove stones and roots, and mix in compost, which is decayed plant and kitchen waste that feeds the soil. Raised or sunken beds help in different seasons: raised beds drain well in the rains, while sunken beds hold moisture in the dry months.

*[Stage 3; examples suited to the reader]* **The third stage is planting.** Beginners do well with hardy crops such as covo, rape, onions and tomatoes. Seedlings can be bought or raised in a seedbed, and they should be planted in the late afternoon so that they do not wilt in the midday sun.

*[Stage 4; cause and effect]* **Finally, the garden needs regular care.** Watering in the early morning reduces loss through evaporation. Covering the soil with dry grass, a practice called mulching, keeps it moist and stops weeds from growing. Checking leaves every few days allows pests to be removed by hand before they spread.

*[Conclusion: summary + benefit, not a new topic]* In summary, a successful garden depends on a sunny site, rich soil, sensible crops and steady care. With these in place, even a small bed behind the kitchen can provide vegetables for the family table.

### What makes it work

- A clear **sequence** signposted by *first*, *next*, *third*, *finally*.
- Each paragraph is one stage with explanation (*because…*, *so that…*).
- Terms (*compost*, *mulching*) are defined briefly.
- The tone is **neutral and practical**; there are no invented statistics.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Turning information into a story"}
*"One day my grandfather showed me how to plant tomatoes…"* may appear briefly as an example, but the composition must stay an explanation.
:::

:::mistake{title="Muddled order"}
Explaining watering before choosing the site confuses the reader. Plan the order.
:::

:::mistake{title="Invented facts and figures"}
*"According to the United Nations, 63.2 per cent of…"* is unconvincing when made up. Explain what you know.
:::

:::mistake{title="Emotional or preachy tone"}
Informative writing explains; it does not lecture. *"Anyone who pollutes is evil"* belongs nowhere.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Turn the title into a question (*How do you…? What are the effects of…?*) and make sure every paragraph answers it.
:::

:::exam-tip
Subheadings are acceptable in reports and articles but usually not in a composition; use clear topic sentences instead.
:::

## Checklist

- A clear organising pattern (sequence, categories, cause and effect, problem and solution).
- One stage or aspect per paragraph, each with explanation or example.
- Key terms defined briefly.
- Signposts guide the reader.
- A neutral tone and no invented facts.
- A conclusion that summarises and stresses the benefit or importance.

## Summary

:::summary
Informative writing explains clearly: choose an organising pattern, give one stage or aspect per paragraph, define terms, explain with examples, signpost the structure, keep a neutral tone, write only what you know and finish with a summary of the key points.
:::
