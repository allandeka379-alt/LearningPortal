## Introduction

Argumentative titles ask you to take a side: *"Boarding schools do more harm than good. Do you agree?"*, *"Zimbabwe should ban single-use plastic bags."*, *"Young people today have life easier than their parents did."* An argumentative composition is judged on how **convincing** it is: a clear position, sound reasons, real-seeming examples and a confident tone.

These skills matter far beyond the examination room. Debates at school, letters to the council and applications for jobs and bursaries all depend on making a case that others will accept.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Thesis (position)"}
The writer's clear answer to the question, stated in the introduction and kept throughout: *I strongly believe that…*
:::

:::definition{term="Counter-argument"}
A point that the other side would make against your position.
:::

:::definition{term="Rebuttal"}
Your answer to a counter-argument, showing why it is weaker than it first seems or how the problem can be solved.
:::

:::definition{term="Persuasive devices"}
Techniques that influence the reader: rhetorical questions, the rule of three, direct address (*you*), emotive language, repetition and appeals to shared values.
:::

## Argument or discussion?

If the title says *Do you agree?*, *Argue for or against* or states a view that you are asked to support or oppose, it wants an **argument**: one side, defended. If it says *Discuss*, *Consider both sides* or *What are the advantages and disadvantages?*, it wants a **discursive** (balanced) piece. Read the instruction words carefully.

## The structure of an argument

1. **Introduction:** hook the reader, introduce the issue, state your position clearly.
2. **Reason 1** (your strongest or second strongest): point, explanation, example.
3. **Reason 2**: point, explanation, example.
4. **Reason 3**: point, explanation, example.
5. **Counter-argument and rebuttal**: acknowledge a fair point from the other side, then answer it.
6. **Conclusion:** restate your position in fresh words, sum up the strongest reason and finish with a call to action or a memorable final line.

Each body paragraph follows **PEE**: **P**oint (topic sentence), **E**xplanation, **E**xample.

## Evidence without inventing facts

In the exam you cannot look up statistics, and inventing them (*93 per cent of learners…*) is unconvincing and dishonest. Use evidence you can honestly give:

:::example{title="Four honest kinds of evidence"}
- **Examples** from everyday experience: *In my own neighbourhood, the stream behind the shops is lined with plastic bags.*
- **Reasoning**: *If a bag is used for ten minutes but lasts for centuries, the cost is paid by people not yet born.*
- **Common knowledge**: *Most families in towns now buy bread in plastic bags.*
- **Hypothetical cases**: *Imagine a farmer whose goat chokes on a bag blown from the road.*
:::

## Persuasive devices: use them with judgement

| Device | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Rhetorical question | *Would we accept a school that taught only half its learners?* |
| Rule of three | *It is cheaper, cleaner and fairer.* |
| Direct address | *You have seen the bags caught in the fences.* |
| Emotive language | *a river choking on rubbish* |
| Repetition | *Not next year. Not next month. Now.* |

One or two of each are powerful; a composition built only of rhetorical questions sounds like shouting.

## An annotated model argument (extract)

**Title: "Every secondary school in Zimbabwe should teach financial literacy." Do you agree?**

*[Introduction: hook + issue + clear position]* Most of us will earn money for forty years or more, yet almost nobody teaches us how to keep it. We learn to calculate the area of a trapezium but not the cost of a loan. I firmly believe that every secondary school should teach financial literacy, because it protects young people from debt, prepares them to build businesses and helps whole families make better decisions.

*[Reason 1: Point]* The first and most urgent reason is protection. *[Explanation]* Young people today can borrow money through a phone in seconds, often without understanding the interest they will pay. *[Example]* A school leaver who takes a small loan to buy a phone may, without understanding interest, end up paying back far more than the phone was worth. *[Link]* A few lessons on interest and budgeting could prevent years of struggle.

:::example{title="A counter-argument paragraph with concession and rebuttal"}
*[Counter-argument]* Some argue that the timetable is already overloaded and that financial matters should be taught at home. *[Concession]* It is true that teachers already struggle to finish their syllabuses. *[Rebuttal]* However, financial literacy need not be a new subject; it can be woven into mathematics, commerce and guidance lessons that already exist. Furthermore, many parents never had the chance to learn these skills themselves, so leaving the task to homes simply passes on the gap.
:::

*[Conclusion: restated position + call to action]* Money will shape our lives whether we understand it or not. Schools that teach financial literacy give learners a tool they will use every single week of their adult lives. The question is not whether we can afford to teach it, but whether we can afford not to.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Sitting on the fence"}
*"There are good and bad points, so it depends"* is not an argument. Choose a side and keep to it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Lists of reasons with no development"}
Five reasons in one sentence each are weaker than three reasons explained with examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the other side"}
A composition that pretends there are no objections looks naive. Answer the strongest counter-argument.
:::

:::mistake{title="Invented statistics"}
*"Research shows 87 per cent of…"* will not impress an examiner who knows you made it up. Use reasoning and examples.
:::

:::mistake{title="Rude or extreme tone"}
*"Anyone who disagrees is stupid"* loses the reader. Be firm and confident, but respectful.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Put your strongest reason either first (to make an impression) or last before the counter-argument (to build to a peak). Never hide it in the middle.
:::

:::exam-tip
Phrases for rebuttal: *It is true that… However…*, *Critics claim… but this overlooks…*, *Although some argue…, the evidence suggests…*
:::

## Checklist

- My position is clear in the introduction and never wavers.
- Three developed reasons, each with point, explanation and example.
- At least one counter-argument, fairly stated and rebutted.
- Persuasive devices used sparingly and appropriately.
- No invented statistics; examples are realistic.
- A conclusion that restates my view and ends strongly.

## Summary

:::summary
An argument takes one side and defends it: a clear thesis, three developed reasons (point, explanation, example), a counter-argument answered with rebuttal, persuasive devices in moderation, honest evidence and a firm conclusion.
:::
