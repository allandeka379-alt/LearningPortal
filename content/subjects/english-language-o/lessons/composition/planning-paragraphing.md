## Introduction

In the composition section of the examination, two candidates can have equally good ideas and still score very differently. The difference is usually **organisation**: one candidate spends five minutes deciding what to say and in what order; the other starts writing at once and discovers halfway down the second page that the story has wandered off the title. Examiners reward compositions that are relevant, clearly paragraphed and easy to follow, whether you sit ZIMSEC Paper 1 or a Cambridge writing paper.

This lesson gives you a routine you can use for every composition: **analyse, plan, write, check**. The other composition lessons (narrative, descriptive, argumentative, discursive and informative) build on it.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Topic sentence"}
The sentence, usually the first in a paragraph, that states the main idea of that paragraph. Every other sentence in the paragraph supports it.
:::

:::definition{term="Unity"}
A paragraph has unity when every sentence in it is about the one idea announced in the topic sentence.
:::

:::definition{term="Coherence"}
The quality of writing that flows logically, so that the reader can follow how each sentence and paragraph connects to the one before it. Connectives, pronouns and repeated key words create coherence.
:::

:::definition{term="Connective (linking device)"}
A word or phrase that shows how ideas are related: *however*, *as a result*, *meanwhile*, *for example*, *on the other hand*, *finally*.
:::

## Step 1: Analyse the title

Read the title twice and underline the **key words**. Ask three questions: *What type of writing is wanted? What exactly is the topic? Is there a limit, such as a time, a place or a person?*

:::example{title="Reading a title closely"}
**Title:** *Describe the scene at a bus terminus on the last day before the Christmas holiday.*

- Type: **descriptive** (not a story about a robbery at the terminus).
- Topic: **a scene**, so people, sounds, smells and movement.
- Limit: **the last day before the holiday**, so crowds, luggage, excitement and delays belong; an ordinary quiet Tuesday does not.
:::

If a title is a statement in quotation marks, such as *"Money cannot buy happiness." Discuss.*, you must deal with the statement, not just the general topic of money.

## Step 2: Plan in five minutes

A plan is not a draft. It is a list of paragraph headings with two or three details under each. For a 400-word composition, five or six paragraphs is usual.

:::example{title="A five-minute plan for 'A day I will never forget'"}
1. **Opening:** the morning of the inter-schools athletics final in Gweru; nerves; my spikes borrowed from my cousin.
2. **Build-up:** the bus journey; Coach Ndlovu's warning about my slow start.
3. **Problem:** a strap on the borrowed spike snaps during warm-up.
4. **Climax:** running the 800 m with one shoe tied with a hair band; last 100 m.
5. **Resolution:** I finish second, not first; my coach's reaction.
6. **Ending:** what I learnt about preparing my own equipment.
:::

Notice that the plan already has a **problem** and a **turning point**. Planning is where you decide that your story will have shape, not a list of events.

## Step 3: Write strong paragraphs

### The introduction

An introduction should catch interest and point the reader in the right direction. Avoid long throat-clearing such as *"In this composition I am going to write about…"*. Good openings include a striking statement, a short scene, a question, a line of dialogue or, for arguments, a clear statement of your position.

:::example{title="Weak and strong openings compared"}
**Weak:** *It was a nice day and I woke up and brushed my teeth and went to school.*

**Strong:** *The envelope from the examinations office sat on the kitchen table for three hours before anyone dared to open it.*

The strong opening raises a question in the reader's mind (what is in the envelope?) and starts close to the important moment.
:::

### The body paragraph

A body paragraph has a **topic sentence**, **development** (explanation, examples, detail) and often a **closing or linking sentence**.

:::example{title="An annotated body paragraph"}
*(Topic sentence)* **Small home gardens could do more for family budgets than most people realise.** *(Development: explanation)* A bed of covo, tomatoes and onions behind the kitchen needs little more than waste water and compost from food scraps. *(Development: example)* My aunt in Chitungwiza stopped buying vegetables for most of the rainy season after she dug three beds along her fence. *(Linking sentence)* However, gardens also bring problems that cannot be ignored.

The final sentence prepares the reader for the next paragraph, which will discuss the problems.
:::

### Unity: stay on the topic

The most common paragraph fault is **drift**: a sentence that belongs to another paragraph or to no paragraph at all.

:::example{title="Spot the drifting sentence"}
*Our school library has become the busiest room on the campus. Learners queue at lunchtime to use the four new computers. The librarian has started a booking sheet so that everyone gets twenty minutes. My brother prefers football to reading.*

The last sentence has nothing to do with the library's popularity. Cut it.
:::

### Coherence: link your ideas

Use connectives to show the relationship between ideas:

| Purpose | Connectives |
| --- | --- |
| Adding | furthermore, in addition, moreover, also |
| Contrasting | however, on the other hand, nevertheless, yet |
| Cause and effect | as a result, consequently, therefore, so |
| Time | meanwhile, later, eventually, at that moment |
| Example | for instance, for example, such as |
| Concluding | finally, in conclusion, on balance |

Do not overuse them. Three *furthermore*s in one paragraph sound mechanical. Pronouns (*this*, *these problems*) and repeated key words also link sentences quietly.

### Vary your sentences

Mix short and long sentences, and begin them in different ways: with a time phrase (*By midnight,*), an *-ing* phrase (*Gripping the rail,*), a subordinate clause (*Although the rain had stopped,*) or occasionally a single dramatic word. A short sentence after several long ones creates emphasis.

### The conclusion

A conclusion should **close**, not repeat. In a story it resolves the problem or shows what changed; in an argument it restates the position firmly and may end with a recommendation; in a discussion it weighs both sides and gives a reasoned view.

## Step 4: Check

Leave five minutes to reread. Correct tense slips, missing full stops, spelling of names and words you used often, and any sentence that is unclear. Neat crossings-out are acceptable.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing without a plan"}
Unplanned compositions often drift, repeat themselves or end suddenly because time runs out. Five minutes of planning saves far more time than it costs.
:::

:::mistake{title="One giant paragraph, or one-sentence paragraphs"}
A page with no breaks tires the reader; a page of single sentences looks like a list. Start a new paragraph for a new idea, a new time, a new place or a new speaker.
:::

:::mistake{title="Answering the topic, not the title"}
*"The day I was proved wrong"* is not the same as *"My best day"*. If the title names a situation, the situation must be central.
:::

:::mistake{title="Conclusions that only repeat"}
*"In conclusion, as I said, …"* followed by the introduction in other words adds nothing. Add a final judgement, a consequence or a reflection.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Spend roughly 10 per cent of your time planning, 75 per cent writing and 15 per cent checking. In a one-hour composition that is about 5, 45 and 10 minutes.
:::

:::exam-tip
Write your plan on the answer paper and cross it out neatly afterwards. Examiners do not mark it, but it keeps you on track.
:::

:::exam-tip
Before you write each paragraph, glance at the title. If the paragraph does not help to answer it, it does not belong.
:::

## Checklist

- Have I identified the type of writing, the topic and any limits in the title?
- Does my plan have five or six paragraphs with details under each?
- Does every paragraph have a clear topic sentence, and does every sentence support it?
- Have I linked paragraphs with suitable connectives without overusing them?
- Have I varied sentence length and openings?
- Does my conclusion add a judgement, consequence or reflection?

## Summary

:::summary
Analyse the title (type, topic, limits), plan five or six paragraphs, open with something that earns attention, build each paragraph around a topic sentence with unified development, link ideas with connectives, vary your sentences, close with a conclusion that adds something, and check your work.
:::
