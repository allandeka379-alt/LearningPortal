## Introduction

Some titles do not ask you to win an argument; they ask you to **think**. *"Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of living in a city."* *"Social media: a blessing or a curse for young people? Discuss."* *"Should traditional ceremonies be preserved in modern Zimbabwe?"* A discursive composition explores an issue from more than one side, treats each side fairly and then reaches a **reasoned conclusion**.

Examiners look for balance, logical organisation, a calm and mature tone, and a conclusion that grows out of the discussion rather than appearing from nowhere.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Discursive writing"}
Writing that explores an issue from several points of view in a balanced way before reaching a reasoned judgement.
:::

:::definition{term="Balance"}
Giving each side a fair hearing, with developed points and examples on both sides, not a token sentence for one of them.
:::

:::definition{term="Impersonal language"}
Phrasing that presents views without the writer's *I*, such as *It is often argued that…*, *Supporters claim…*, *Many people feel…*. It keeps the tone objective until the conclusion.
:::

:::definition{term="Reasoned conclusion"}
A final judgement that weighs the arguments already presented and explains which side is stronger, or under what conditions.
:::

## Two ways to organise

**Block structure**

1. Introduction: the issue and why it matters; no verdict yet.
2. Points in favour (two paragraphs).
3. Points against (two paragraphs).
4. Conclusion: weigh up and give your view.

**Point-by-point structure**

1. Introduction.
2. Aspect 1 (for example, cost): one side, then the other.
3. Aspect 2 (for example, health): one side, then the other.
4. Aspect 3 (for example, culture): one side, then the other.
5. Conclusion.

Block structure is simpler and safer under time pressure. Point-by-point shows more sophisticated thinking because it keeps comparing the two sides directly.

## Language of discussion

| Purpose | Phrases |
| --- | --- |
| Introducing a view | *It is often argued that…*, *Supporters of… claim…*, *One view is that…* |
| Adding | *Furthermore*, *In addition*, *A further advantage is…* |
| Turning to the other side | *On the other hand*, *However*, *Critics point out that…*, *Conversely* |
| Conceding | *Admittedly*, *While it is true that…* |
| Concluding | *On balance*, *Weighing these arguments*, *In the final analysis* |

## Annotated model paragraphs

**Title: "Should mobile phones be allowed in secondary schools?" Discuss.**

*[Introduction: context + issue + both sides signalled, no verdict]* Ten years ago, few secondary learners in Zimbabwe owned a phone; today many carry one in their school bag. Head teachers, parents and learners disagree sharply about whether these devices belong in the classroom. There are strong arguments on both sides, and they deserve careful consideration.

*[Side 1: point + explanation + example, impersonal tone]* Supporters of phones in schools argue that they are powerful learning tools. A single phone can hold dictionaries, past lessons and current news, and a learner in a rural school without a library can look up information that would otherwise be out of reach. Teachers can also send reminders and revision material to class groups, extending learning beyond the school day.

:::example{title="Turning to the other side"}
*[Side 2: turn with a connective, equally developed]* On the other hand, critics point out that phones bring distraction and harm. Messages and games compete for attention during lessons, and some learners use phones to bully others or to cheat in tests. There is also the problem of fairness: learners whose families cannot afford a phone may feel left out or fall behind if lessons depend on one.
:::

:::example{title="A qualified conclusion"}
*[Reasoned conclusion: weighs both, gives a qualified judgement]* On balance, banning phones completely seems to throw away a valuable tool, while allowing them without limits invites the problems critics describe. The most sensible approach is a middle path: phones kept switched off in bags except when a teacher plans an activity that uses them, with shared devices available for learners who do not own one.
:::

Notice that the conclusion is **qualified** (it sets conditions) and that it follows logically from both sides. It does not introduce new arguments.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="A disguised argument"}
Four paragraphs for one side and two weak sentences for the other is not a discussion. Develop both sides properly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using I throughout"}
*"I think… I feel… I believe…"* in every paragraph makes the piece sound like an opinion column. Keep personal views for the conclusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="No conclusion, or a conclusion from nowhere"}
*"It depends on the person"* gives no judgement. A conclusion that suddenly brings in a new argument is just as weak.
:::

:::mistake{title="Repeating the same point in different words"}
*Phones are distracting* and *phones take attention away from lessons* are one point. Plan distinct points for each side.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Plan by drawing two columns, *For* and *Against*, and aim for at least two developed points in each before you start writing.
:::

:::exam-tip
A qualified conclusion (*…provided that…*, *…as long as…*) often shows more maturity than a simple yes or no.
:::

## Checklist

- The introduction explains the issue without giving a verdict.
- Both sides are developed with points, explanations and examples.
- A clear structure: block or point-by-point.
- Impersonal phrasing in the body; contrast connectives between sides.
- A reasoned conclusion that weighs the arguments and adds no new points.

## Summary

:::summary
A discursive composition explores an issue fairly: an introduction that sets out the issue, balanced and developed paragraphs on each side (block or point-by-point), impersonal language and contrast connectives, and a reasoned, often qualified, conclusion that grows out of the discussion.
:::
