## Introduction

A descriptive title asks you to **paint with words**: *"Describe a busy kombi rank at rush hour"*, *"Describe a person who has influenced you"*, *"Describe the moments before a storm breaks."* It is not a story. You may include a small amount of movement and a few events, but your job is to make a reader who has never been there experience the scene.

Cambridge 0500 Paper 2 offers a descriptive title in every exam, and ZIMSEC Paper 1 regularly includes one. Descriptive compositions reward precise vocabulary and controlled structure, two skills that also help you in every other type of writing.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Sensory detail"}
Detail that appeals to the five senses: sight, sound, smell, touch and taste.
:::

:::definition{term="Imagery"}
Language that creates pictures in the reader's mind, including similes (*like a flock of startled birds*), metaphors (*the road was a river of red dust*) and personification (*the wind tugged at the washing*).
:::

:::definition{term="Atmosphere"}
The mood or feeling of a place or scene: peaceful, tense, joyful, eerie, chaotic. Every detail you choose should help to build it.
:::

:::definition{term="Zoom structure"}
Organising a description like a camera: begin with a wide view, move closer to a group or object, then focus on one small detail, and perhaps pull back again at the end.
:::

## Choose a structure

Description needs organisation as much as a story does. Four structures work well:

| Structure | How it moves | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spatial | Left to right, near to far, entrance to back | Rooms, markets, landscapes |
| Chronological | Through a short period of time | *Dawn in the village*, *the hour before the final* |
| Zoom | Wide view → close-up → wide view | Crowds, stadiums, festivals |
| Contrast | Before and after, quiet and busy | *The school on the first and last day of term* |

## Precise words beat many words

Weak description piles up adjectives: *the very big, nice, beautiful tree*. Strong description chooses **one precise noun and one strong verb**: *the msasa flared copper in the September sun*.

:::example{title="Upgrading a sentence"}
**Weak:** *There were lots of people at the market and it was very noisy.*

**Stronger:** *Vendors bellowed prices over the rattle of wheelbarrows, and a radio somewhere played the same chorus again and again.*

The stronger version shows *who* makes the noise and *what* it sounds like.
:::

## Use all the senses

Sight dominates most descriptions. Add at least two other senses in every paragraph: the smell of paraffin and frying dough, the grit of dust between your teeth, the cold metal of a gate latch, the taste of sugar cane.

## Imagery with care

A fresh simile or metaphor is powerful; a tired one (*as white as snow*, *as busy as a bee*) adds nothing. Make comparisons from the world of your scene: in a description of a rural homestead, *the smoke rose as straight as a grain stalk* fits better than a comparison to a city skyscraper.

## An annotated model description

**Title: Describe a kombi rank at the end of a working day** (about 360 words)

*[Wide view; atmosphere established]* By half past five, the rank at Fourth Street has stopped being a place and become a tide. Hundreds of people pour from offices and shops towards it, and the kombis, packed nose to tail, seem to breathe them in.

*[Sound; strong verbs]* The noise arrives before anything else. Touts bellow destinations like auctioneers, "Chitungwiza! Budiriro! Two seats, two seats!", while horns argue with one another and a speaker on a vendor's table thumps out a song that nobody is listening to.

*[Moving closer; smell and touch]* Closer to the vehicles, the air thickens with diesel fumes and the sweet, oily smell of freshly fried maputi. A woman in a bright headscarf presses past me, a sack of potatoes balanced on her hip, and the coarse weave of it scratches my arm. Underfoot, the tarmac is sticky with spilled drinks and the day's dust.

*[Zoom to one detail; a small human moment]* At the edge of the crowd, a small boy of about six stands perfectly still, clutching a plastic bag of bread to his chest. His eyes move from kombi to kombi, reading the cardboard signs in the windscreens. When his mother finally appears, he does not cry or shout; he simply takes her hand and lets out a breath he seems to have been holding all afternoon.

*[Time passing within the scene]* As the light turns orange, the queues shorten. The touts' voices grow hoarse. One by one the kombis swing out into the traffic, their doors still sliding shut, until gaps appear in the long line.

*[Pulling back; closing image]* By seven, the rank belongs to the sweepers and the stray dogs. A single kombi waits with its engine ticking as it cools, its driver asleep with his cap over his face, while above him the first stars appear, faint behind the city's glow.

### What makes it work

- A **zoom structure**: wide crowd → vehicles → one boy → wide view again.
- **Time** moves only a little (five to seven o'clock), giving shape without becoming a story.
- **Senses**: sound, smell, touch, sight.
- **Precise verbs**: *pour*, *bellow*, *argue*, *thump*, *swing*.
- **Imagery**: the rank as a *tide*; the kombis *breathe them in*.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Turning the description into a story"}
If your kombi-rank description becomes a story about a pickpocket chase, you have changed the task. Keep events small and use them to reveal the scene.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adjective overload"}
*The big, tall, huge, dark, scary building* is weaker than *the warehouse loomed*. Prefer strong nouns and verbs.
:::

:::mistake{title="Only using sight"}
A scene with no sound, smell or texture feels flat. Aim for at least three senses in the composition.
:::

:::mistake{title="A list of things"}
*There was a table. There was a chair. There was a lamp.* Link details and give them life: *A lamp leaned over the desk as if reading the open letter.*
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Choose present or past tense and keep to it. Present tense (*the touts bellow*) gives a sense of immediacy and suits many descriptions.
:::

:::exam-tip
End with an image, not a summary. *"In conclusion, the rank was busy"* wastes your last impression.
:::

## Checklist

- A clear structure (spatial, chronological, zoom or contrast).
- At least three senses and precise nouns and verbs.
- Fresh imagery that fits the scene.
- A consistent atmosphere.
- Events kept small; the scene stays central.
- A closing image.

## Summary

:::summary
Descriptive writing makes the reader experience a scene. Organise it (spatial, chronological, zoom or contrast), use precise nouns and verbs and all the senses, add fresh imagery, build one atmosphere, keep events small and finish with a memorable image.
:::
