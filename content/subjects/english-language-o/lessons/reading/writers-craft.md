## Introduction

Writers choose every word for a reason. A storm that *howls* feels different from one that *blows*; a crowd that *surges* is more frightening than one that *moves*. Questions on the writer's craft ask you to notice these choices and explain **how they affect the reader**. In Cambridge 0500 Paper 1 this is the language question on Text C; in ZIMSEC Paper 2 it appears as "Explain what the writer means by…" or "Why does the writer use the word…?".

Many candidates can spot a simile. Far fewer can explain what it **does**. This lesson gives you a four-step method that turns spotting into explaining.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Connotation"}
The feelings and associations a word carries beyond its basic meaning. *Home* and *house* mean similar things, but *home* suggests warmth and belonging.
:::

:::definition{term="Imagery"}
Language that creates a picture or sensation in the reader's mind, often through comparison (simile, metaphor) or appeals to the senses.
:::

:::definition{term="Simile and metaphor"}
A **simile** compares using *like* or *as* (*the bus wheezed like an old man*). A **metaphor** says one thing *is* another (*the bus was an old man wheezing up the hill*).
:::

:::definition{term="Personification"}
Giving human qualities or actions to something that is not human (*the wind whispered through the msasa trees*).
:::

:::definition{term="Effect"}
What a word or technique makes the reader see, feel or think, and why the writer wanted that.
:::

## The four-step method: identify, meaning, connotation, effect

:::example{title="Explaining one word choice"}
**Extract.** *The floodwater **swallowed** the low bridge at Birchenough in a single night.*

1. **Identify** the word: *swallowed*.
2. **Meaning** in context: the water covered the bridge completely.
3. **Connotation**: swallowing is what a living creature does to food; it suggests something hungry, powerful and final — what is swallowed disappears.
4. **Effect on the reader**: the flood seems like a huge, greedy animal; the reader feels how quickly and completely the bridge vanished and senses the danger.

**Written answer.** *The word "swallowed" makes the flood sound like a hungry creature that gulped the bridge down, suggesting it vanished completely and suddenly, which makes the reader feel the frightening power of the water.*
:::

![Writer's craft method applied to the word swallowed in the flood sentence: identify the word, give its meaning in context (covered completely), explain its connotation (hungry, powerful, final) and state the effect on the reader (a greedy animal, danger felt).](media:diagrams/writers-craft-word-chain.png "Figure: identify, meaning, connotation, effect for the word swallowed")

Notice that the answer explains the **literal meaning**, the **association** and the **effect** — not just "it is personification".

## Word choice and connotation

Compare words with similar meanings:

| Neutral | Positive connotation | Negative connotation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| thin | slender | skinny, scrawny |
| walked | strolled | trudged, staggered |
| talked | chatted | gossiped, ranted |
| smell | fragrance | stench |

A writer who chooses *trudged* tells you the walker is tired or unhappy without saying so.

## Imagery: similes, metaphors, personification

:::example{title="Explaining a simile"}
*The heat pressed down on Kariba **like a heavy wet blanket**.*

- Meaning: it was very hot and humid.
- Association: a heavy, wet blanket is suffocating and impossible to throw off.
- Effect: the reader feels the airless, oppressive heat and the discomfort of the people.
:::

:::example{title="Explaining a metaphor"}
*Mbare Musika at dawn **is a beehive**.*

- Meaning: the market is full of busy people.
- Association: a beehive is crowded, buzzing with noise and constant movement, everyone working.
- Effect: the reader pictures the noise and energy, and senses that everyone has a job to do.
:::

## Sound effects

- **Alliteration**: repeated first sounds (*the **s**low **s**ad **s**ong of the **s**hepherd*) can create a soft or harsh rhythm.
- **Onomatopoeia**: words that sound like what they describe (*crack, hiss, clatter, thud*), making the scene vivid.

Always say what the sound **adds**: *the hard "c" and "k" sounds in "the cart clattered and cracked" echo the jolting noise of the cart.*

## Sentence structure

Writers also create effects through the **shape** of sentences.

- **Short sentences** create tension, shock or emphasis: *Then the lights went out. Silence.*
- **Long sentences** with lists can show abundance, chaos or a rush of events.
- **Repetition** emphasises an idea: *They waited. They waited through the night. They waited until dawn.*
- **Questions** (rhetorical questions) make readers think or involve them.
- **Paragraph structure**: a one-line paragraph stands out and marks a turning point.

:::example{title="Effect of a short sentence"}
*Tafadzwa ran past the tuck shop, through the gate and down the road towards the river, calling her brother's name over and over. Nobody answered.*

The short final sentence contrasts with the long, breathless one before it. It stops the reader suddenly, just as her calling stops, creating fear.
:::

## Writing effect answers

- Quote the word or short phrase.
- Explain what it means **here**.
- Explain its connotations or the picture it creates.
- Say how it makes the reader feel or what it makes them understand, and link this to the writer's purpose.

Avoid empty phrases such as *it makes the passage more interesting* or *it helps the reader imagine*. Say **what** the reader imagines and **why** it matters.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Naming the technique without explaining it"}
*"Like a heavy wet blanket" is a simile.* This earns nothing on its own. Explain what it suggests.
:::

:::mistake{title="Generic comments"}
*It makes the writing vivid.* Every good image is vivid; say what is vivid and what feeling results.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining a long quotation"}
Choose the single most powerful word or a short phrase. You cannot analyse a whole sentence precisely.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring the context"}
*Swallowed* in a story about a flood suggests danger; in a story about a child's homework it might be comic. Always link to the passage.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Zoom in on single words"}
Even inside a phrase, pick out the strongest word and explore its connotations.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use precise verbs in your answer"}
*Suggests, conveys, emphasises, creates a sense of, highlights* are better than *shows*.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Two or three choices well explained"}
In Cambridge 0500 Question 2(d)-style tasks, explaining fewer choices in depth beats listing many.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Writers choose words for their connotations, images, sounds and sentence shapes.
- Use the method: identify, meaning, connotation, effect on the reader.
- Explain similes, metaphors and personification through what they suggest, not just their names.
- Short sentences, lists, repetition and questions create effects too.
- Avoid generic comments; be precise about what the reader sees, feels or understands.
:::
