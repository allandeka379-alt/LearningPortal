## Introduction

Notes are how busy people keep information: a nurse's notes on a patient, a reporter's notes at a meeting, your own notes before a test. Good notes are **short, accurate and organised**. They keep only what matters for a purpose and leave out the rest.

Note-making is tested in several ways. Cambridge 0510 asks you to complete a set of notes under given headings from an article. Cambridge 0500 and ZIMSEC summary tasks begin with selecting points, which is a form of note-making. And making your own notes from a passage is the fastest route to good summaries and extended answers. In this lesson you will learn how to take notes that score.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Note"}
A brief record of one piece of information, written in as few words as possible, usually without full sentences.
:::

:::definition{term="Heading"}
A label that groups related notes, for example *Benefits*, *Problems* or *Advice for visitors*.
:::

:::definition{term="Note form"}
A shortened style that keeps the key content words (nouns, verbs, numbers) and drops words such as *the*, *a*, *is* and *there are*.
:::

## Step 1: read the headings first

When notes are given with headings, the headings tell you exactly what to look for. Read them **before** the article.

:::example{title="Headings guide your reading"}
**Headings:** *Reasons the community garden was started* · *Problems faced in the first year* · *Plans for the future*

As you read, you now know to watch for reasons (often after *because*, *so that*), problems (*difficult*, *unfortunately*, *however*) and plans (*will*, *hope to*, *next year*).
:::

## Step 2: find details that fit each heading exactly

Each note must answer its heading. A detail about the garden's *success* does not belong under *Problems*. Some details look relevant but answer a different heading — check every time.

## Step 3: write each note briefly

Keep the content words; cut the rest.

:::example{title="From sentence to note"}
**Text:** *One of the main problems was that goats from neighbouring homesteads kept breaking through the fence and eating the young vegetables.*

**Note:** *goats broke through fence, ate young vegetables*

The note keeps the essential information (who, what) and loses the filler.
:::

Rules for good notes:

- One idea per note.
- Usually three to eight words.
- No need for full sentences, but the meaning must be clear to someone else.
- Keep important qualifiers: *some*, *most*, *only at night*.
- Do not add anything that is not in the text.

## Step 4: avoid repeating the heading

If the heading is *Problems*, write *goats ate vegetables*, not *a problem was goats ate vegetables*.

## A worked example

:::example{title="Completing notes"}
**Text (extract).** *The Kwekwe Youth Library opened in March with a small collection of donated books. It quickly became popular with secondary pupils, who used it for homework after school because few homes had electricity for evening study. However, in its first year the library struggled. The roof leaked during the rains, damaging over a hundred books, and volunteers were hard to find during the harvest months. Next year the committee hopes to install solar lighting and to start a weekend reading club for primary children.*

**Notes:**

*Why pupils used the library*
- homework after school
- few homes had electricity for evening study

*Problems in the first year*
- roof leaked in rains, damaged books
- volunteers scarce at harvest time

*Plans*
- install solar lighting
- weekend reading club for primary children
:::

Notice that each note is short, fits its heading, and keeps essential detail (*for primary children*, *in rains*).

## Making your own notes

When no headings are given, make your own:

1. Skim the text and decide what the main topics are.
2. Turn each topic into a heading.
3. List points under each, one idea per line.
4. Use numbers, arrows and common abbreviations (*e.g.*, *&*, *→*) if they help, but keep notes readable.

These notes are the foundation of a good summary: you select the points first, then join them into sentences.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Copying whole sentences"}
Notes are brief. Copying a whole sentence wastes time and may include irrelevant detail.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting a detail under the wrong heading"}
A future plan is not a problem; a reason is not a result. Check each note against its heading.
:::

:::mistake{title="Cutting too much"}
*roof, books* is too short to show meaning. *roof leaked, damaged books* keeps the idea clear.
:::

:::mistake{title="Repeating the same idea twice"}
*volunteers scarce* and *hard to find helpers* are the same point. Each note should be a new idea.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the number of bullet points"}
If a heading has three bullet points, there are probably three separate details to find under that heading.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Spelling counts"}
In note completion, a note that is spelled so badly it becomes another word may not be credited. Copy names and key words carefully.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Keep to the order"}
Details for the notes usually appear in the same order as the headings, but check the whole text for scattered points.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Read the headings first; they tell you what to look for.
- Choose details that fit each heading exactly.
- Write brief notes: content words only, one idea each, meaning still clear.
- Keep qualifiers, add nothing, and avoid repetition.
- Your own notes are the first step to a strong summary.
:::
