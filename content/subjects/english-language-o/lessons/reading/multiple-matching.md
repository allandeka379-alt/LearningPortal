## Introduction

In a multiple-matching task you are given several short texts — for example four students describing a school trip, or five people talking about their jobs — and a list of statements. For each statement you decide **which person or text** it describes. Cambridge English as a Second Language (0510) uses this task in its reading paper, with sections labelled A to E.

The task tests careful, detailed reading. The statements almost never use the same words as the texts: they **paraphrase**. And the writers deliberately include **distractors**, details in the wrong texts that look like a match but are not. This lesson shows you how to work quickly and accurately.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Multiple matching"}
A reading task in which each statement in a list must be matched to one of several texts or sections. A text may match more than one statement.
:::

:::definition{term="Paraphrase"}
The same idea expressed in different words. Statements in matching tasks are usually paraphrases of the texts.
:::

:::definition{term="Distractor"}
A detail in the wrong text that seems to match a statement because it shares a word or topic, but does not express the same idea.
:::

## Step 1: skim each text for its general content

Spend a minute reading each section quickly. Note in a few words what each person is mainly about: *A — enjoyed the trip, complained about food*; *B — was nervous at first*. This helps you know where to look later.

## Step 2: read the statements and underline key ideas

For each statement, underline the **key idea**, not just key words.

:::example{title="Key ideas, not key words"}
**Statement:** *This person changed their mind about something.*

The key idea is **a change of opinion**. Look for phrases like *at first I thought… but now*, *I used to…*, *I was wrong about…*. The word *mind* may never appear.
:::

## Step 3: look for paraphrases

| Statement says | The text might say |
| --- | --- |
| was nervous beforehand | *my stomach was in knots the night before* |
| received help from a relative | *my uncle lent me the money* |
| would recommend it to others | *anyone thinking of it should go for it* |
| found it more difficult than expected | *I thought it would be easy — how wrong I was* |

## Step 4: check for distractors

Before you decide, read the whole sentence in the text around your match. Ask: does it say **exactly** what the statement says?

:::example{title="Avoiding a distractor"}
**Statement:** *This person was disappointed by the weather.*

- **Text A:** *We were lucky with the weather — clear skies all week.*
- **Text C:** *I had hoped to see the sun set over the Zambezi, but it rained every evening.*

Both texts mention the weather. Only **Text C** expresses disappointment. Text A is a distractor.
:::

## Step 5: one statement, one text — but texts can repeat

Each statement has only one correct text, but one text can be the answer to several statements. Do not assume each text is used the same number of times.

## A worked example

:::example{title="Four students on a work-experience week"}
**Text A (Tariro).** *I spent the week at a vet's surgery in Marondera. I had imagined cuddling puppies, but most of the time I cleaned cages. Still, I watched an operation on a dog, and that convinced me to study science seriously.*

**Text B (Kudzai).** *My placement was at a radio station. My cousin works there and arranged it for me. I was shy at first and hardly spoke, but by Friday I was reading the weather report live.*

**Text C (Nomsa).** *I worked in a hotel kitchen in Victoria Falls. The hours were long and my feet ached, and I don't think I could do that job every day. But I learned more about teamwork than in a whole year at school.*

**Text D (Brian).** *I helped at a primary school. The children were noisy, and the teachers were more patient than I could ever be. I'd tell anyone in Form 4 to try teaching for a week: you'll respect your own teachers more.*

| Statement | Answer | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Got the placement through a family member | B | *My cousin … arranged it* |
| Found the work less exciting than expected | A | *I had imagined cuddling puppies, but … cleaned cages* |
| Became more confident during the week | B | *shy at first … by Friday I was reading the weather report live* |
| Would recommend the experience to other students | D | *I'd tell anyone in Form 4 to try* |
| Decided on a future direction | A | *convinced me to study science* |
| Found the job physically demanding | C | *long hours, feet ached* |

Notice that Text B and Text A each answer two statements, and that Text C mentions not wanting the job every day (a distractor for "decided on a future direction", because it rules something out rather than choosing a direction).
:::

## Managing time

- Do the statements you are sure about first.
- Mark possible matches with a question mark and come back to them.
- Never leave a blank: an intelligent guess may earn the mark.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Matching on a single shared word"}
The statement says *money*; Text B mentions *money*. That does not make B the answer. Match the **idea**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Stopping at the first possible match"}
Check the other texts too. Another one may match more exactly.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring negatives and contrasts"}
*I didn't mind the long hours* does not match *complained about the long hours*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Underline the evidence"}
When you choose a text, underline the exact words that match. If you cannot find them, think again.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Watch for opposite meanings"}
Distractors often say the opposite of the statement: *I wasn't nervous at all* sits in one text while another person really was nervous.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Skim each text first and note its main content.
- Underline the key idea in each statement.
- Look for paraphrases, not repeated words.
- Check the surrounding sentence to avoid distractors and opposite meanings.
- Each statement has one answer; a text can be used more than once.
:::
