## Introduction

"Give the meaning of the word *scarce* as used in paragraph 3." "Explain what the writer means by *a thorn in his side*." Questions like these appear in every comprehension paper. ZIMSEC usually asks you to give a word or short phrase with the same meaning; Cambridge papers ask you to explain words in your own words or to choose a meaning.

The key phrase is **as used in the passage**. Many English words have several meanings. *Bank* can be the side of a river or a place to keep money; *cool* can describe the weather or a calm person. You are rewarded for the meaning that fits **this** sentence, not the first meaning that comes into your head. In this lesson you will learn how to use context to find that meaning, even for words you have never seen before.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Context"}
The words, sentences and situation surrounding a word, which help to show what it means.
:::

:::definition{term="Synonym"}
A word or phrase with the same or nearly the same meaning, which could replace the word in the sentence.
:::

:::definition{term="Figurative phrase"}
A phrase whose meaning is not the literal meaning of its words, such as an idiom (*a thorn in his side*) or a metaphor.
:::

## Five kinds of context clue

**1. Definition or restatement.** The writer explains the word nearby, often after a comma, a dash or *that is*.

:::example{title="Restatement"}
*The village was **parched** — the soil cracked, the river bed dry and dusty.*

The dash introduces a description of dryness: **parched** means *extremely dry*.
:::

**2. Synonym nearby.** The writer repeats the idea with a different word.

:::example{title="Synonym clue"}
*The headmaster was **adamant**. No matter how hard the prefects pleaded, he remained firm.*

*Remained firm* repeats the idea: **adamant** means *refusing to change his mind*.
:::

**3. Contrast.** Words such as *but*, *although*, *unlike* and *however* show the word means the opposite of something nearby.

:::example{title="Contrast clue"}
*Unlike her **garrulous** sister, who chatted all the way to Masvingo, Chiedza hardly said a word.*

Garrulous is the opposite of hardly saying a word: it means *very talkative*.
:::

**4. Example.** A list of examples shows the general meaning.

:::example{title="Example clue"}
*The market sold all kinds of **produce**: tomatoes, onions, groundnuts and fresh mealies.*

The examples are all things grown on farms: **produce** means *farm crops for sale*.
:::

**5. General sense of the situation.** Sometimes you must use the whole situation.

:::example{title="Situation clue"}
*After walking for six hours without water, the hikers were **famished**, and they finished the whole pot of sadza in minutes.*

Six hours of walking and eating a whole pot quickly suggest **famished** means *extremely hungry*.
:::

## Use the grammar of the sentence

Your answer must be the **same part of speech** as the word in the passage, so that it could replace it.

- *She **scrutinised** the receipt.* (verb, past tense) → *examined closely* — not *close examination*.
- *His **scrutiny** made the clerk nervous.* (noun) → *close examination*.
- *The crowd grew **restive**.* (adjective) → *impatient and restless*.

**The substitution test:** put your answer in place of the original word. If the sentence still makes sense and keeps its meaning, your answer is probably right.

## Word parts help

Prefixes, roots and suffixes give useful hints:

- *un-, in-, im-, dis-, mis-* often mean *not* or *wrongly*: *inaudible* (cannot be heard), *misjudged*.
- *-less* means *without*: *fearless*, *relentless* (without stopping).
- *re-* means *again* or *back*: *rebuild*, *revive*.

Use word parts as a guide, but always check the meaning against the context.

## Words with several meanings

:::example{title="Choosing the meaning that fits"}
*The new rule was meant to **check** the spread of litter in the school grounds.*

- *Check* can mean *examine* (check your answers) or *stop / limit* (check the spread).
- Here, rules **limit** the spread of litter. Answer: *to control* or *to limit*.
:::

## Explaining figurative phrases

For an idiom or image, give the plain meaning in this context, not a word-by-word translation.

:::example{title="An idiom in context"}
*The noisy generator next door had become **a thorn in Mr Banda's side**.*

Not: *a sharp point stuck in his body.* But: *a constant source of irritation to him.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving a dictionary meaning that does not fit"}
*Bank* in *the river bank was steep* is not *a place to keep money*. Always read the whole sentence.
:::

:::mistake{title="Changing the part of speech"}
For *the guard was **vigilant***, answer *watchful* (adjective), not *watching* or *watchfulness*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the word itself in the answer"}
"*Unhelpful* means *not helpful*" may be accepted for a simple word, but for a test of understanding, avoid using the original word or its root.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving several different meanings"}
Listing three possible meanings in the hope that one is right usually earns nothing. Commit to the one that fits.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Read around the word"}
Read at least the sentence before and the sentence after. The clue is often there.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="One word or a short phrase"}
If the question asks for one word, give one word. If it asks you to explain, a short phrase is fine.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Do the substitution test"}
Read the sentence again with your answer in place of the original word. If it sounds wrong, think again.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- The meaning that matters is the meaning **in this passage**.
- Use context clues: restatement, synonym, contrast, examples and the general situation.
- Match the part of speech so your answer could replace the word.
- Use word parts as hints and check them against the context.
- Explain figurative phrases in plain words; do not translate them literally.
:::
