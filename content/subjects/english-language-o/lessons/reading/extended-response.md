## Introduction

Cambridge IGCSE First Language English (0500) ends its reading paper with an **extended response**: you read a text and then write as one of the people in it. You might be a passenger writing a journal entry after the events of a story, a headteacher giving a speech based on an article, or a journalist interviewing the main character. The task is marked for **reading** (how well you use and develop ideas from the text) and for **writing** (how well you write in the voice and form).

This is not a creative writing task where you can invent freely, and it is not a summary where you just repeat points. It sits in between: you **use** the text's details, **develop** them (explain, expand, connect) and **interpret** them (show what lies beneath, such as feelings and motives), all in a convincing voice.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Writing in role"}
Writing as a particular person (a character or participant), from their point of view, with their knowledge, feelings and voice.
:::

:::definition{term="Register"}
The level of formality and the style of language suited to the audience and form (for example a formal speech, a personal journal, a friendly letter).
:::

:::definition{term="Develop and interpret"}
To develop is to expand and explain details from the text; to interpret is to show what they suggest (feelings, motives, consequences) without contradicting the text.
:::

## Step 1: understand the task's bullet points

The task usually gives two or three bullet points. Each one points to a part of the text, and together they cover the whole passage. Your answer should give roughly equal attention to each bullet.

:::example{title="Reading the task"}
*Imagine you are Rumbi. Write a journal entry on the evening your grandmother arrives. In your entry you should write about:*

- *what happened on the day your mother left*
- *how you managed the household and what you found difficult*
- *your thoughts and feelings now that your grandmother has come.*

The first two bullets ask you to **use** details; the third asks you to **interpret** and look forward.
:::

## Step 2: collect and plan ideas from the text

Make brief notes for each bullet. For every detail, ask: *What does this show? How would my character feel about it? What might it lead to?*

:::example{title="From detail to development"}
- Detail: *the mother sold two goats and borrowed a suitcase.*
- Developed: *I knew how serious it was when Amai sold the goats — she had been keeping them for my school fees next year.*

The development is **plausible** (it fits the text) and adds depth.
:::

## Step 3: find the voice

Your character has a personality, an age, a situation and an attitude. A worried fifteen-year-old writes differently from a confident headteacher.

- Use the first person (*I*, *we*).
- Show feelings through what the character notices and how they speak.
- Choose vocabulary the character would use, but keep your English accurate and varied.

:::example{title="Voice in a journal"}
*I didn't ask Amai how long she'd be away. I think I already knew she couldn't tell me, and I didn't want to see her try.*

The short, honest sentences show a thoughtful, slightly sad teenager who understands more than she says.
:::

## Step 4: match the form and register

| Form | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Journal / diary | Date, first person, private thoughts, reflective, informal but well written |
| Letter | Greeting, paragraphs, sign-off; formal or informal depending on recipient |
| Speech | Addresses the audience directly, rhetorical questions, clear structure, memorable ending |
| Report | Heading, factual tone, may use sub-headings, recommendations at the end |
| Interview | Questions and answers in a clear layout; each answer develops ideas |
| Article | Headline, engaging opening, paragraphs, appeals to readers |

## Step 5: write, staying faithful to the text

- **Do** use specific details from the text (names, places, events).
- **Do** develop them with explanation, feeling and consequence.
- **Don't** contradict the text or invent major new events.
- **Don't** copy long phrases from the passage; express ideas in your own words.
- **Don't** let one bullet take up the whole answer.

## A model paragraph

:::example{title="Developing ideas in role"}
**Task.** You are the head of the school in an article about a bicycle scheme. Write a speech to parents explaining the scheme and asking for support.

*Good evening, parents. Two years ago I watched your children walk through our gates at half past seven, already exhausted after eight kilometres in the dark. Some of them fell asleep in my own mathematics lessons, and I could not blame them. Today forty of those children arrive on bicycles, fresh and on time. But the scheme is fragile: a single broken brake cable can keep a child at home for a week. That is why I am asking you tonight…*

This uses details from the article (eight kilometres, forty bicycles, brake cables), develops them (pupils falling asleep, a broken cable keeping a child home) and speaks directly to the audience.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Retelling the passage"}
A list of events in order, with no development, earns low reading marks. Explain, interpret and connect.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the role"}
Writing *Rumbi felt sad* in a journal written as Rumbi breaks the role. Use *I felt…*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inventing too much"}
Adding a car crash or a lottery win that has no link to the text loses reading marks.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ignoring one bullet"}
Each bullet carries reading marks. Cover all of them.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Aim for balance"}
Spend roughly a third of your answer on each bullet. Plan for five minutes before you write.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Interpret the final bullet"}
The last bullet often asks for feelings or future plans. This is where you show interpretation; make sure your ideas grow from the text.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Write as the character, using their voice, feelings and knowledge.
- Use, develop and interpret details from the text; do not merely retell or invent.
- Cover every bullet point in balance.
- Match the form (journal, letter, speech, report, interview, article) and register.
- Use your own words and keep the writing accurate and varied.
:::
