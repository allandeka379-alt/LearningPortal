## Introduction

Every comprehension paper begins with the easiest marks: questions that ask you to find something the writer has **stated directly**. "Where did the bus break down?" "What two things did the girl carry?" "How long had the drought lasted?" The answer is printed in the passage. You do not have to guess, judge or interpret.

Yet candidates lose these marks all the time. Some copy the wrong sentence, some write a long paragraph that hides the answer, and some answer from their own knowledge instead of the text. In this lesson you will learn a reliable method for **explicit** (stated) information that works for ZIMSEC Paper 2 and for the Cambridge reading papers.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Explicit information"}
Information that the text states openly in its words. You can point to the exact phrase that gives the answer.
:::

:::definition{term="Implicit information"}
Information that the text suggests but does not state. You work it out from clues (this is **inference**, covered in its own lesson).
:::

:::definition{term="Skimming"}
Reading quickly over a whole text to get the gist: the topic, the main idea of each paragraph and how the text is organised.
:::

:::definition{term="Scanning"}
Running your eyes over a text to find one particular item, such as a name, a date, a number or a key word, without reading every word.
:::

## Step 1: skim the passage first

Before you look at the questions, read the passage through once at a steady pace. Ask yourself: *What is this about? Who is involved? What happens in each paragraph?* A two-or-three-word label in the margin for each paragraph ("arrival at market", "the argument", "the solution") makes later questions much faster.

## Step 2: read the question and underline its key words

Each question has **key words** (what it wants) and **limiting words** (where or how much).

:::example{title="Reading the question"}
*From paragraph 2, give **two** reasons why Mrs Ncube closed her shop early.*

- Where: **paragraph 2** only.
- What: **reasons** (why), not what she did next.
- How many: **two**. A third reason will not earn a mark and a wrong extra one can cancel a right one.
:::

## Step 3: scan for the answer

Scan the named paragraph for words linked to the question: the same words, synonyms or related ideas. Questions often use different words from the passage, so look for the *idea*, not only the exact word.

:::example{title="Scanning an extract"}
**Extract.** *The rains came late to Gutu that year. By December the maize in Mr Hove's field was no higher than his knee, and the borehole at the business centre, which usually served forty families, had begun to cough up mud. Mr Hove decided to plant sorghum on the lower field, where the soil held water longer.*

**Question.** *What did Mr Hove plant on the lower field?*

Scan for "lower field": it appears in the last sentence. The answer is **sorghum**.

**Question.** *Why did he choose the lower field?*

The reason follows *where*: **the soil there held water longer**.
:::

## Step 4: write a short, precise answer

Recall answers should be brief. A phrase is usually enough unless the question says "in a sentence".

:::example{title="Precise versus padded answers"}
**Question.** *How many families usually used the borehole?*

- Good: **forty families**.
- Too much: *The borehole at the business centre which usually served forty families had begun to cough up mud.* (The answer is hidden in copied material, and the extra detail is not needed.)
- Wrong: *many families*. (The text gives an exact number; use it.)
:::

## Lifting: when copying is allowed

For a **recall** question you may use the words of the passage, as long as you copy only the part that answers the question. Copying a whole sentence that includes extra or wrong information is called **excessive lifting**: examiners may refuse the mark because they cannot tell whether you understood. When the question says **"in your own words"**, copying the key words loses the mark (see the lesson on own-words answers).

## Questions with more than one part

Some questions ask for details from different places: "Name **two** things the visitors saw at Great Zimbabwe." Find each detail separately and write them as a list or on separate lines. Check that the two answers are genuinely different ideas, not the same idea said twice.

:::example{title="Two different details"}
**Extract.** *At the ruins the visitors climbed to the Hill Complex, admired the tall conical tower in the Great Enclosure, and bought soapstone carvings from the craft sellers near the gate.*

**Question.** *Name two things the visitors saw or did at the ruins.*

Any two of: **climbed to the Hill Complex; admired the conical tower; bought soapstone carvings**. "They climbed the hill and went up to the Hill Complex" is one idea, not two.
:::

## Numbers, dates and names

Scanning is fastest for numbers and capital letters, because they stand out on the page. Check units and quantities carefully: "three weeks" is not "three days"; "about 2,000" is not "exactly 2,000". Spell names exactly as they appear in the passage.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Answering from general knowledge"}
If the passage says the bus left Mutare at six, the answer is six, even if you know the real timetable is different. Answer from the text only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong paragraph"}
When a question names a paragraph, the answer must come from that paragraph. A detail from paragraph 4 earns nothing for a question on paragraph 2.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving more answers than asked"}
If the question asks for two points and you give four, the examiner may mark only the first two. Choose the best two.
:::

:::mistake{title="Copying a whole sentence"}
A copied sentence that contains the answer plus unrelated detail can cost the mark. Copy only the words that answer the question.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Marks tell you how much to write"}
One mark usually means one detail. Two marks usually means two details, or one detail plus a reason. Let the mark allocation guide the length of your answer.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Questions follow the passage"}
Comprehension questions usually follow the order of the passage. If question (c) is in paragraph 3, question (d) is probably in paragraph 3 or 4.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Short answers are not lazy answers"}
A precise phrase is a strong answer. Examiners look for the right detail, not for long sentences.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Explicit information is stated directly in the text; you can point to it.
- Skim first for the gist; then scan for the words or ideas in the question.
- Underline key words and limiting words: which paragraph, what kind of answer, how many.
- Write short, exact answers: copy only the words that answer the question, unless told to use your own words.
- Give exactly the number of details asked for, and keep each one a different idea.
:::
