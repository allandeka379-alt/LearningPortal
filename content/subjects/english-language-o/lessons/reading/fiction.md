## Introduction

Many comprehension passages are taken from stories: a girl's first day at a new school, a fisherman caught in a storm on Lake Kariba, a family quarrel over land. To answer well you need to read fiction the way a writer builds it — through **characters**, **setting**, **plot**, **theme** and **style**. The ZIMSEC syllabus also expects you to read widely for enjoyment and to talk and write about books you have read.

This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the questions to ask of any story, so that you can explain not just *what* happens but *how* and *why* the writer makes it happen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Character"}
A person (or animal or being) in a story. **Major** characters drive the story and usually change; **minor** characters support the plot or reveal something about the major ones.
:::

:::definition{term="Characterisation"}
The methods a writer uses to present a character: description, actions, speech, thoughts, and how others react to them.
:::

:::definition{term="Setting"}
Where and when a story takes place, including the social world (village, city, school, a particular period).
:::

:::definition{term="Plot"}
The sequence of events and how they are connected, usually built around a **conflict**.
:::

:::definition{term="Theme"}
A central idea or message a story explores, such as greed, belonging, courage, the gap between generations, or the cost of ambition.
:::

:::definition{term="Narrator and point of view"}
The voice that tells the story: **first person** (*I*, a character in the story) or **third person** (*he, she, they*, a voice outside the story).
:::

## Characters: how they are revealed

Writers rarely say "Farai was selfish." They **show** it.

:::example{title="Showing, not telling"}
*When the plate of roasted mealies came round, Farai took the two biggest cobs, then looked over his shoulder to see whether anyone had noticed.*

- **Action**: takes the biggest cobs → greedy.
- **Detail**: looks over his shoulder → he knows it is wrong; he is sly as well as greedy.
:::

Ask of every major character: *What do they want? What stands in their way? How do they change?* A character who changes is called **dynamic**; one who stays the same is **static**.

## Setting: more than a backdrop

Setting creates **atmosphere** and can shape events.

:::example{title="Setting creating atmosphere"}
*The homestead stood alone at the end of a sandy track, its thatch grey with age. In the evenings the only sound was the creak of the windmill, turning slowly whether there was water to pump or not.*

The isolation, the old thatch and the windmill turning uselessly suggest loneliness and decline. The setting prepares us for a story about loss.
:::

## Plot and conflict

Most stories follow a pattern:

1. **Exposition**: characters and setting introduced.
2. **Rising action**: a conflict develops.
3. **Climax**: the moment of greatest tension.
4. **Falling action**: consequences unfold.
5. **Resolution**: the conflict is settled (or deliberately left open).

**Conflict** can be **external** (a character against another person, nature or society) or **internal** (a struggle within a character's own mind, such as guilt or a hard choice).

## Themes

A theme is not a single word but an idea about it. *Family* is a topic; *family loyalty can demand painful sacrifices* is a theme. To find themes, ask: *What does the main character learn? What does the ending suggest? What problems keep returning?*

## Style

Style is **how** the writer tells the story: vocabulary, imagery, sentence length, dialogue, humour, the narrator's voice. A first-person narrator gives intimacy but a limited view; a third-person narrator can move between characters. Short, plain sentences can create tension; rich imagery can slow a scene down.

## Writing about a book you have read

You may be asked to recommend a book, review it, or discuss a character. A strong answer:

- gives the **title and author**, and the setting briefly;
- summarises the plot **without** retelling everything (and without spoiling the ending in a review);
- discusses one or two **characters** or **themes** with specific examples;
- gives a **personal response** with reasons (what affected you, and why).

:::example{title="A short book review paragraph"}
*The story follows a boy from a small village who is sent to live with his uncle in the city after his father's death. What I admired most was how the writer shows the boy's loneliness through small details: he keeps his father's old radio under his pillow, though it no longer works. By the end, he has learned that belonging is something you build rather than something you are given — a message every new pupil at a boarding school will understand.*
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Retelling the plot instead of analysing"}
Questions about character or theme need explanation and evidence, not a summary of events.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating characters as real people"}
Remember they are created by a writer. Say *the writer presents Farai as greedy by…*, not only *Farai is greedy*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing narrator and writer"}
A first-person narrator is a character. Their opinions are not necessarily the writer's.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Use the method of characterisation"}
For a character question, say what the character is like **and** how you know: action, speech, thought, description, others' reactions.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Name themes as ideas"}
Write *the story suggests that pride can isolate a person*, not just *the theme is pride*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Characters are revealed through description, actions, speech, thoughts and others' reactions.
- Setting (place, time, social world) creates atmosphere and shapes events.
- Plot follows exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution, driven by conflict.
- Themes are ideas the story explores; express them as statements.
- Style is how the story is told; when writing about a book, combine evidence with personal response.
:::
