## Introduction

Imagine two people saying "Well done" to you after a test. One smiles warmly; the other rolls her eyes. The words are the same, but the **tone** is completely different. In writing there is no voice or face, so the writer creates tone through word choice, punctuation and sentence structure.

Reading papers ask: "What is the writer's attitude to the new road?", "Describe the tone of paragraph 4", "How does the writer feel about city life?" Cambridge 0500 Question 2(b) asks for attitudes and opinions, and Cambridge 0510 includes questions on the writer's or speaker's attitude. You need a good vocabulary of tone words and the habit of backing them up with evidence.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Tone"}
The writer's or speaker's voice: the feeling or manner in which something is said (for example angry, playful, sarcastic, respectful).
:::

:::definition{term="Mood"}
The atmosphere a text creates and the feeling it produces in the reader (for example tense, peaceful, gloomy, joyful).
:::

:::definition{term="Attitude"}
The writer's opinion of, or feelings towards, the subject: approving, disapproving, doubtful, enthusiastic, nostalgic and so on.
:::

## Tone and mood are different

- Tone belongs to the **writer**: *The writer's tone is bitter.*
- Mood belongs to the **reader's experience** of the text: *The description creates a gloomy mood.*

A ghost story may have a calm, matter-of-fact tone while creating a terrifying mood.

## A bank of tone words

Learn precise words; *happy* and *sad* are too vague.

| Positive | Negative | Neutral or mixed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| admiring, affectionate, enthusiastic, hopeful, nostalgic, respectful, humorous, celebratory | bitter, critical, sarcastic, ironic, scornful, resentful, alarmed, despairing, indignant | objective, factual, detached, reflective, cautious, ambivalent, matter-of-fact |

:::definition{term="Ironic and sarcastic"}
Irony says the opposite of what is meant, or shows a gap between what is expected and what happens. Sarcasm is irony used sharply, to mock or criticise.
:::

:::definition{term="Nostalgic"}
Looking back fondly, with longing, at the past.
:::

## How to identify tone: three clues

**1. Word choice.** Positive or negative connotations reveal attitude.

:::example{title="Two attitudes to the same event"}
- *The council has finally **unveiled** its **long-awaited** market, a **bright** new home for traders.* → approving, enthusiastic.
- *The council has finally **dumped** its **overdue** market on traders, a **concrete box** with no shade.* → critical, scornful.
:::

**2. Punctuation and sentence type.** Exclamation marks can show excitement or outrage; rhetorical questions can show frustration or disbelief; short sentences can sound firm or angry.

:::example{title="Punctuation and tone"}
*Nineteen potholes. Nineteen! And the council calls this a road?*

The repetition, exclamation and question create an **indignant**, **sarcastic** tone.
:::

**3. What the writer chooses to include.** A writer who mentions only the dust, noise and cost of a new mine is critical, even if no single word is openly negative.

## Writing about attitude: the claim + evidence pattern

:::example{title="A complete attitude answer"}
**Extract.** *When I was a boy, the river behind our homestead in Chimanimani ran so clear that you could count the pebbles. We swam there every afternoon until our mothers called us home. Now my grandchildren stand on its bank and see only brown water and plastic bottles.*

**Question.** *What is the writer's attitude to the changes to the river?*

**Answer.** *He is **saddened and regretful**. He remembers the past fondly (the water was so clear "you could count the pebbles"), and the contrast with "only brown water and plastic bottles" shows his disappointment at what has been lost.*
:::

The answer names the attitude precisely, then quotes briefly as evidence, and explains how the evidence shows the attitude.

## Changing tone

Tone can shift within a passage. A story may move from **light-hearted** to **tense**; an article may begin **hopeful** and end **cautious**. Questions such as "How does the tone change in paragraph 5?" need two tone words and evidence for each.

:::example{title="A shift in tone"}
*The first week at boarding school was an adventure: midnight feasts, new friends, the thrill of the dining hall bell. By the third week the adventure had worn thin. I counted the days on the back of my exercise book.*

The tone moves from **excited** to **homesick and weary**. The word *thrill* contrasts with *worn thin* and the image of counting days.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Vague tone words"}
*The tone is negative* or *the writer is sad* is too general. Choose *bitter*, *disappointed*, *nostalgic* or *indignant*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Confusing the writer's attitude with a character's"}
In a story, a character may be angry while the narrator's tone is amused. Read the question: whose attitude?
:::

:::mistake{title="Missing irony"}
Words of praise can be sarcastic. Check the context: *What a wonderful idea to close the only clinic* is criticism, not praise.
:::

:::mistake{title="Naming a tone with no evidence"}
Every tone or attitude answer needs a short quotation or close reference.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Ask: how would this sound read aloud?"}
Imagine the passage spoken. Would the voice be warm, angry, mocking, calm? That is the tone.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use two words if needed"}
Mixed attitudes are common: *grudgingly admiring*, *amused but critical*. Precise combinations score well.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tone is the writer's voice; mood is the atmosphere the reader feels; attitude is the writer's opinion of the subject.
- Identify tone from word choice, punctuation, sentence type and what the writer includes.
- Use precise tone words and support every claim with brief evidence.
- Watch for irony and sarcasm, and for shifts in tone within a passage.
:::
