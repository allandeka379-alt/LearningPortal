## Introduction

Good writers do not repeat the same nouns over and over. Instead of "The borehole broke. The borehole had been drilled in 1998. The borehole served the whole village," they write "The borehole broke. **It** had been drilled in 1998 and served the whole village." Words like *it*, *this*, *they*, *such* and *the latter* point back to something already mentioned. Linking words like *however*, *therefore* and *meanwhile* show how one idea relates to the next.

Together these are called **cohesive devices**: the glue of a text. Comprehension papers test them directly: "What does *it* in line 12 refer to?", "What does *this* refer to in paragraph 3?", "Which word shows a contrast?" Understanding them also helps every other reading question, because misreading a single *they* can make you misunderstand a whole paragraph.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cohesion"}
The way the sentences and paragraphs of a text are connected so that they read as a whole.
:::

:::definition{term="Reference word"}
A word that points to something mentioned elsewhere in the text, such as a pronoun (*it, they, she*), a demonstrative (*this, that, these, those*) or words like *such*, *the former*, *the latter*.
:::

:::definition{term="Antecedent (referent)"}
The word or idea that a reference word stands for.
:::

:::definition{term="Linking word (connective)"}
A word or phrase that shows how two ideas are related: *and, but, because, however, therefore, for example, meanwhile*.
:::

## Reference words

**Backward reference** is the most common: the word points back to something already said.

:::example{title="Finding the antecedent"}
*The farmers asked the council for a new dip tank. **It** promised to build **one** before the rains.*

- *It* = **the council** (the council promised; the farmers did not).
- *one* = **a dip tank**.
:::

**Reference to a whole idea.** *This*, *that* and *such* often refer to a whole sentence or situation, not a single noun.

:::example{title="'This' referring to an idea"}
*Many pupils walk more than ten kilometres to school each day. **This** means that they arrive tired and often late.*

*This* = **the fact that many pupils walk more than ten kilometres to school each day**.
:::

**The former and the latter**: *the former* = the first of two things mentioned; *the latter* = the second.

:::example{title="Former and latter"}
*Chipo was offered places at a boarding school in Masvingo and a day school near home. She chose **the latter** because she could help her mother in the evenings.*

*The latter* = **the day school near home**.
:::

### How to answer "What does X refer to?"

1. Look **back** from the word (usually to the previous sentence or two).
2. Find the noun or idea that matches in **number** (singular/plural) and **sense**.
3. **Substitute** your answer for the reference word and check that the sentence makes sense.
4. Write the referent clearly and fully: *the council*, not *it*; *the long walk to school*, not *this*.

## Linking words and what they signal

| Function | Linking words |
| --- | --- |
| Addition | and, also, furthermore, moreover, in addition, besides |
| Contrast | but, however, although, yet, on the other hand, whereas, nevertheless |
| Cause | because, since, as, owing to, due to |
| Effect / result | so, therefore, consequently, as a result, thus |
| Time / sequence | first, then, meanwhile, afterwards, eventually, finally |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, including |
| Summing up | in conclusion, overall, in short, to sum up |

:::example{title="What does the link tell you?"}
*The rains were good this year. **Nevertheless**, many families went hungry.*

*Nevertheless* signals a **contrast** with what you would expect: good rains usually mean food, so something else must have caused the hunger. A careful reader now looks for the reason.
:::

## Linking words help you predict

When you see *however*, expect a change of direction. When you see *for example*, expect an illustration of the point just made (in a summary you would usually leave it out). When you see *therefore*, expect a conclusion based on what came before. Following these signals makes long passages much easier to read.

## Other cohesive devices

- **Repetition of key words** keeps the topic clear.
- **Synonyms** avoid repetition: *the drought … the dry spell … the long months without rain*.
- **Ellipsis** leaves out repeated words: *Tendai wanted to go, but Rudo didn't [want to go].*

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Choosing the nearest noun automatically"}
In *The farmers asked the council for a dip tank. It promised…*, the nearest noun is *dip tank*, but a dip tank cannot promise. Always check the sense.
:::

:::mistake{title="Answering with another pronoun"}
*"It" refers to the thing they wanted* is not clear. Name the referent: *the dip tank*.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one word when 'this' refers to an idea"}
If *this* refers to a whole situation, explain the situation, not just one noun from it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up cause and effect"}
*Because* introduces the cause; *so* and *therefore* introduce the effect.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Substitute to check"}
Put your answer in place of the reference word. If the sentence makes sense, your answer is right.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use links to find answers"}
Questions about reasons often have answers near *because*, *since* or *as*; questions about results often have answers after *so*, *therefore* or *as a result*.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Reference words (it, they, this, such, the former, the latter) point to an antecedent, usually earlier in the text.
- Find the antecedent by looking back, matching number and sense, and substituting.
- *This*, *that* and *such* often refer to a whole idea.
- Linking words signal addition, contrast, cause, effect, time, example and summing up.
- Use these signals to follow and predict the writer's line of thought.
:::
