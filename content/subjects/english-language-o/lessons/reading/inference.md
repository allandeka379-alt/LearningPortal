## Introduction

Writers do not tell you everything. A story rarely says "Chipo was terrified"; instead it says her hands shook as she knocked on the head's door. A news report may never say "the council has failed", but it lists three promises that were not kept. Working out these unstated meanings is called **inference**, and it is the skill that separates average answers from good ones.

Inference questions appear in every reading paper: "What does paragraph 3 suggest about the father?", "Why do you think the villagers were suspicious?", "What impression does the writer give of the town?" In this lesson you will learn a four-step method: **clue, meaning, inference, evidence**.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Inference"}
A reasonable conclusion you draw from clues in a text, about something the text suggests but does not state.
:::

:::definition{term="Evidence"}
The words from the text that support your inference, either quoted briefly or referred to closely.
:::

:::definition{term="Implied meaning"}
A meaning that is hinted at by details, actions, word choice or what characters say, rather than stated outright.
:::

## Where the clues are

Writers hint at meanings through:

- **actions**: what a person does (slamming a door, avoiding someone's eyes);
- **speech**: what is said and how it is said (snapping, whispering, refusing to answer);
- **appearance and setting**: torn shoes, an empty kitchen, a freshly painted house;
- **word choice**: *crept*, *marched* and *strolled* all mean "walked" but suggest different feelings;
- **what is missing**: a character who never mentions her father, a report that never quotes the other side.

## The four-step method

:::example{title="Clue, meaning, inference, evidence"}
**Extract.** *When the results were pinned on the board, Nyasha read down the list twice. Then she walked quickly to the toilets, keeping her face turned towards the wall, and did not come out until the bell had rung for the end of break.*

**Question.** *What can you infer about Nyasha's results? Support your answer.*

1. **Clue**: she read the list twice; she hid her face; she stayed in the toilets.
2. **Meaning of the clue**: people hide their faces and avoid others when they are upset or embarrassed.
3. **Inference**: Nyasha's results were probably disappointing.
4. **Evidence**: she kept *her face turned towards the wall* and did not come out until the bell rang.

**Answer.** *Her results were probably worse than she had hoped: she hid her face and stayed away from the others until the end of break.*
:::

## A good inference is reasonable, not wild

Your inference must be the most likely meaning the clues support. "Nyasha had failed and would leave school" goes much further than the evidence. "Nyasha was upset by her results" is safe and supported. If you cannot point to a clue, the inference is a guess.

:::example{title="Strong and weak inferences"}
**Extract.** *Mr Sibanda's shop had once been the busiest in Lupane. Now the shelves held a few tins of beans and a single bag of salt, and the bell over the door had not rung all morning.*

- Strong: *The shop is no longer doing well, because the shelves are almost empty and no customers have come in.*
- Weak: *Mr Sibanda is lazy.* (Nothing in the extract suggests why the shop is struggling.)
- Weak: *The shop is closed.* (The bell has not rung, but the shop is still open with goods on the shelves.)
:::

## Inferring feelings and attitudes

Questions often ask how a character feels. Name the feeling precisely (*anxious*, *resentful*, *relieved*, *ashamed*) rather than just "happy" or "sad", and then give the clue.

:::example{title="Naming the feeling"}
**Extract.** *"Of course you can borrow my bicycle," said Tawanda, staring at his shoes. "Again."*

*Inference*: Tawanda is **reluctant and quietly annoyed**. He agrees, but he does not look at the speaker, and the single word *Again* shows that he is tired of being asked.
:::

## Inferring reasons and consequences

Some questions ask *why* something happened or *what is likely to happen next*, when the passage does not say directly.

:::example{title="Inferring a reason"}
**Extract.** *The headmistress looked at the cracked water tank, then at the sky, and sent the boys to fill every bucket in the school from the borehole before lunch.*

*Why did she send the boys to fill buckets?* She expected the tank to leak or fail and wanted water stored, perhaps because no rain was coming soon (she looked at the sky).
:::

## Inferring the writer's purpose

For non-fiction, ask *Why did the writer include this?* A detail about a child walking twelve kilometres to school in an article about bursaries is there to make readers sympathetic and willing to donate. Purpose words to use: *to persuade, to inform, to warn, to entertain, to criticise, to make the reader sympathise*.

## How to write inference answers

- Begin with your inference in your own words.
- Support it with brief evidence: a short quotation or a close reference.
- Use cautious language when appropriate: *suggests*, *probably*, *seems*, *implies*.
- For two marks, give the inference **and** the evidence, or two different inferences, depending on the question.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Retelling instead of inferring"}
Writing "She read the list twice and went to the toilets" only repeats the text. The question wants what those actions **show**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Inference with no evidence"}
"He is jealous" earns little if you do not show which words suggest jealousy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Going beyond the text"}
Do not invent backstory. Stay with what the clues reasonably support.
:::

:::mistake{title="Vague feeling words"}
"She felt bad" is too general. Choose a precise word such as *guilty*, *humiliated* or *disappointed*.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Spot the inference question"}
Words such as *suggest*, *imply*, *what impression*, *why do you think*, *what does this show* and *how can you tell* signal inference.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Quote briefly"}
Two to six words of quotation are usually enough. Long quotations waste time and hide your point.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Use the whole context"}
Read the sentence before and after the clue. A single word can mislead you if you ignore what surrounds it.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Inference means working out what the text suggests but does not state.
- Look for clues in actions, speech, appearance, setting, word choice and what is left out.
- Use the method: clue, meaning, inference, evidence.
- Keep inferences reasonable and precise, and always support them with brief evidence.
:::
