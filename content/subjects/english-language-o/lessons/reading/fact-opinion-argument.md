## Introduction

Every day you read texts that want something from you: an advert for a phone, a letter to a newspaper about school fees, a social media post about a new law. A careful reader asks: *Which of these statements can be checked? Which are only someone's view? Is the writer being fair?*

Reading papers test these skills directly. ZIMSEC asks you to identify facts and opinions and to comment on a writer's argument; Cambridge 0500 asks about writers' views and how they are presented; Cambridge 0510 includes questions on writers' opinions and attitudes. These skills will also make you a sharper writer of argumentative compositions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fact"}
A statement that can be checked and proved true or false, for example by measuring, counting or consulting records.
:::

:::definition{term="Opinion"}
A statement of what someone believes, feels or judges. Other people may reasonably disagree with it.
:::

:::definition{term="Argument"}
A line of reasoning: a main claim (point of view) supported by reasons and evidence.
:::

:::definition{term="Bias"}
A one-sided presentation that favours one view, for example by leaving out the other side, using loaded words or choosing only helpful evidence.
:::

:::definition{term="Stereotype"}
An over-simple, fixed idea about a whole group of people (*all teenagers are lazy*).
:::

## Telling facts from opinions

Ask: **Could this be checked?**

:::example{title="Fact or opinion?"}
- *The new clinic in Murehwa has twelve beds.* → **Fact** (countable).
- *The new clinic is the best thing that has happened to Murehwa.* → **Opinion** (a judgement).
- *Football is played by eleven players per team.* → **Fact**.
- *Football is more exciting than netball.* → **Opinion**.
:::

**Opinion signals**: judging words (*best, worst, beautiful, terrible, unfair*), words of belief (*I think, I believe, in my view, clearly, surely*), and words of obligation (*should, ought to, must*).

**Careful:** a statement can be a fact even if it is false: *Harare is the capital of Zambia* is a (wrong) factual claim, because it can be checked. And opinions can be dressed up as facts: *Everyone knows that the plan will fail* sounds certain, but it is still an opinion.

## Following an argument

A clear argument has a **main claim** supported by **reasons**, each backed by **evidence or examples**.

:::example{title="Main points versus examples"}
*Schools should grow their own vegetables. First, gardens teach practical skills: pupils at one rural school learned to plan beds, water efficiently and make compost. Second, the produce improves school meals, which can be poor. Finally, selling surplus vegetables can raise money for books.*

- Main claim: **schools should grow their own vegetables**.
- Reason 1: gardens teach practical skills (example: planning beds, watering, compost).
- Reason 2: produce improves school meals.
- Reason 3: selling surplus raises money.
:::

Signal words help you track the structure: *first, secondly, furthermore, moreover* (more reasons); *however, on the other hand* (counter-argument); *for example, for instance, such as* (examples); *therefore, consequently, so* (conclusions).

## Persuasive techniques

| Technique | Example | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhetorical question | *Do we want our children to walk in the dark?* | Makes the reader feel the answer is obvious |
| Emotive language | *heartless, innocent, devastating* | Stirs strong feelings |
| Statistics or numbers | *Nine out of ten users agree* | Sounds scientific and convincing |
| Expert opinion | *Doctors recommend…* | Borrows authority |
| Repetition / rule of three | *Safe, simple, smart.* | Makes ideas memorable |
| Direct address | *You deserve better.* | Involves the reader personally |
| Exaggeration | *The worst decision in history* | Emphasises strongly |

## Recognising bias

A biased writer may:

- give only one side of an issue;
- use **loaded language** (*mob* instead of *crowd*, *scheme* instead of *plan*);
- generalise from one example (*one driver was rude, so all kombi drivers are rude*);
- quote only supporters, or quote opponents unfairly;
- use stereotypes.

:::example{title="Neutral versus loaded"}
- Neutral: *About two hundred residents gathered outside the council offices to protest about water cuts.*
- Loaded: *A noisy mob of troublemakers besieged the council offices, complaining yet again.*
:::

## Evaluating how convincing a text is

To judge an argument, ask:

1. Are the claims supported by evidence, or only asserted?
2. Is the evidence reliable and relevant? (Who said it? Is the sample large enough?)
3. Does the writer consider the other side fairly?
4. Is the language reasonable, or does it rely on emotion and exaggeration?

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a confident statement is a fact"}
*Clearly, homework is useless* uses a confident word, but it is still an opinion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating examples as main points"}
In a summary or argument question, *pupils learned to make compost* is an example of the main point (practical skills).
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every number a fact"}
Numbers can be invented or misleading. *Nine out of ten users agree* needs a source before it is convincing.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip{title="Test with 'Can it be checked?'"}
If it can be measured, counted or found in records, it is a fact. If people could reasonably disagree, it is an opinion.
:::

:::exam-tip{title="Name the technique and its effect"}
When asked how a writer persuades, identify the technique **and** explain its effect on the reader.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Facts can be checked; opinions are beliefs or judgements others may dispute.
- An argument has a main claim, reasons and evidence; distinguish main points from examples.
- Persuasive techniques include rhetorical questions, emotive language, statistics, expert opinion, repetition and direct address.
- Bias shows in one-sidedness, loaded words, generalisation and stereotypes.
- Judge a text by the quality of its evidence and its fairness to other views.
:::
