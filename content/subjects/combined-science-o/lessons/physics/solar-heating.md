## Introduction

Zimbabwe has sunshine on most days of the year. On a clear day around noon, every square metre of ground facing the Sun receives roughly $1000$ joules of energy from sunlight each second. A family in Gokwe that cooks on firewood, or a clinic in Chiredzi that heats water with electricity, could use some of that free energy instead.

Two devices do this without any moving parts or fuel: the **solar cooker**, which cooks food, and the **solar water heater**, which heats water for washing and bathing. Both use ideas you met when you studied heat transfer: dull black surfaces are good absorbers of radiation, shiny surfaces are good reflectors, trapped air and other insulators are poor conductors, and warm fluids rise by convection.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Solar cooker"}
A device that uses energy from sunlight, without any fuel, to heat food to cooking temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Solar water heater"}
A device that uses energy from sunlight to heat water, usually with a collector panel on a roof and a storage tank.
:::

:::definition{term="Absorber"}
A surface that takes in radiation and becomes hot. Dull (matt) black surfaces are the best absorbers.
:::

:::definition{term="Reflector"}
A surface that sends radiation back without absorbing much of it. Shiny, light-coloured or silvered surfaces are good reflectors.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermosiphon"}
The natural circulation of water in a closed loop, caused by convection: heated water becomes less dense and rises, and cooler, denser water sinks to take its place.
:::

## Design rules shared by every solar heater

Every design in this lesson uses the same four ideas. Learn them and you can explain any part of any solar heater.

| Idea | Why it helps | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Collect more radiation** | Shiny reflectors send extra sunlight onto the food or water | Aluminium foil flaps, a polished curved dish |
| **Absorb it well** | A dull black surface absorbs most of the radiation that falls on it and becomes hot | Black paint inside a cooker, black pots, a black collector plate |
| **Reduce losses by conduction and convection** | Insulation (materials that trap air) slows conduction; a closed box or a glass cover stops warm air from escaping | Newspaper, straw, wool or polystyrene in the walls; a glass lid |
| **Reduce losses by radiation** | Glass lets sunlight in but blocks much of the infrared radiation given out by the warm surfaces inside; shiny outer surfaces are poor emitters | Glass cover, shiny metal casing on a hot-water tank |

:::keyfact{title="The glass cover traps heat"}
Sunlight passes through glass easily. The black surfaces inside absorb it, warm up and give out **infrared** radiation of much longer wavelength. Glass does not let much of this infrared out, and it also stops the warm air from blowing away. The temperature inside rises until heat is lost as fast as it arrives. This is the same "greenhouse" effect that keeps a closed car hot on a sunny day.
:::

## The box solar cooker

A **box cooker** is an insulated box with a glass (or clear plastic) lid.

1. The **inside is painted matt black**, and the food is cooked in **dark pots with lids**, so that most of the radiation entering the box is absorbed.
2. The walls are **double, with insulation** such as crumpled newspaper, straw or polystyrene between them. Trapped air is a poor conductor, so little heat is conducted out.
3. The **glass lid** lets sunlight in, traps the infrared given out by the hot pot and stops hot air escaping by convection.
4. A **reflector flap** lined with aluminium foil is propped above the box at an angle so that it reflects extra sunlight through the glass.

A well-made box cooker reaches about $100$ to $150\ ^\circ\text{C}$ inside: enough to boil rice, cook beans or stew vegetables, but slowly (often two to four hours). It does not burn food, so it can be left while the family works, and it only needs turning to face the Sun every hour or so.

## The curved-reflector (parabolic) solar cooker

A **curved-reflector cooker** has a large, shiny, curved dish (often called a *parabolic* reflector), lined with polished aluminium or mirror pieces.

- Sunlight arrives as almost **parallel rays**. The curved dish reflects all of them towards one small region, the **focus**.
- A **black pot** is held at the focus, so it absorbs a large amount of radiation on a small area and becomes very hot.
- It can reach $200\ ^\circ\text{C}$ or more and cooks about as fast as a stove; it can boil water, fry and roast.
- Because the focus moves as the Sun moves across the sky, the dish must be **turned every few minutes** to keep the pot at the focus.
- The focused light is dangerous: it can burn skin and damage the eyes, so users should not look into the dish and should handle the pot with thick cloth.

:::worked-solution[Example: comparing the two cookers]
A school's environment club must choose a solar cooker for porridge on sports days. The cook must also supervise the tuck shop.

1. A box cooker cooks slowly, but it needs little attention and will not burn the porridge.
2. A curved-reflector cooker cooks quickly, but it must be turned towards the Sun every few minutes and its focus is a burn hazard near children.
3. Choice: the box cooker suits a cook who cannot watch it all the time; the curved reflector suits someone who needs food quickly and can stay with it.
:::

## Advantages and disadvantages of solar cookers

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| The energy is free and renewable; no firewood, paraffin or gas is bought | They only work in direct sunshine: not at night, and poorly on cloudy days |
| No smoke, so no damage to the lungs and eyes of the cook | Cooking is slower than on a fire or stove (box cookers especially) |
| Less firewood is cut, which helps to reduce deforestation | They must be turned to follow the Sun |
| No flames, so a lower risk of fires and burns (box cooker) | They need space in an open, sunny yard; curved reflectors can dazzle and burn |

## The solar water heater

The most common design is the **flat-plate collector** with a **thermosiphon** tank, often seen on roofs of houses, schools and clinics.

**The collector** is a shallow box mounted on the roof:

- a **black metal absorber plate** with **copper pipes** fixed to it (copper is a good conductor, so heat passes quickly from the plate to the water inside the pipes);
- a **glass cover** on top that lets sunlight in, traps infrared and stops convection losses;
- **insulation** under and around the plate to cut conduction losses through the back and sides.

**The tank** stores the hot water. It is **insulated** (lagged) and often has a **shiny outer casing**, a poor emitter of radiation, so the water stays hot overnight.

**How the water circulates (thermosiphon).** The tank is placed **higher than the collector**.

1. Water in the collector pipes is heated, expands and becomes **less dense**.
2. The warm water **rises** up the pipe into the **top** of the tank.
3. Cooler, **denser** water from the **bottom** of the tank sinks down the other pipe into the **bottom** of the collector, where it is heated in turn.
4. This **convection current** continues while the Sun shines, with no pump. Hot water for use is drawn from the top of the tank, and cold mains water refills it at the bottom.

In Zimbabwe, which is south of the equator, the midday Sun is in the **northern** part of the sky for most of the year, so collectors face **north** and are tilted at roughly $20^\circ$ to the horizontal. Some systems add an electric element for cloudy days; another common design uses glass **evacuated tubes**, in which a vacuum (like that of a vacuum flask) stops heat loss by conduction and convection.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Painting the reflector black"}
Reflectors must be **shiny** so that they reflect radiation onto the pot or plate. Only the **absorbing** surfaces (inside of the box, pots, collector plate) are painted black.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the glass cover keeps the sunlight out"}
The glass lets sunlight **in**. It keeps heat in: it blocks much of the infrared given out by the warm surfaces and stops warm air escaping.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the tank below the collector"}
In a thermosiphon system hot water rises. The tank must be **above** the collector, with hot water entering at the **top** of the tank and cold water leaving from the **bottom**; otherwise a pump is needed.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For every part of a solar cooker or water heater, name the part, say what it does and give the reason in terms of **conduction, convection or radiation** ("black plate: good absorber of radiation"; "glass cover: prevents heat loss by convection").
:::

:::exam-tip
When you explain the thermosiphon, use the chain: heated → expands → less dense → rises; cooler → denser → sinks. Mention that no pump is needed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Solar cookers and solar water heaters use energy from sunlight, which is free and renewable, instead of fuel.
- Box cooker: matt black interior and pots (absorb), insulated walls (reduce conduction), glass lid (traps heat, stops convection), foil reflector flap (more sunlight); slow, about 100–150 °C.
- Curved-reflector cooker: a shiny curved dish focuses parallel rays onto a black pot at the focus; fast and hot, but must be turned often and is a burn and glare hazard.
- Flat-plate water heater: black absorber plate with copper pipes, glass cover and insulation; an insulated tank above the collector.
- Thermosiphon: heated water becomes less dense and rises into the top of the tank; cooler, denser water sinks to the collector, so water circulates without a pump.
- In Zimbabwe collectors face north, towards the midday Sun for most of the year.
:::
