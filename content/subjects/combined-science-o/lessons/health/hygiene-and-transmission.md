## Introduction

During the rainy season a secondary school near Chiredzi had a problem. Several learners in one boarding hostel fell ill with diarrhoea in the same week. The school health master checked the hostel and found an overflowing refuse heap behind the kitchen, flies on uncovered food in the dining hall and a blocked drain beside the hand-washing taps. None of these things is a disease, but each one helps germs to reach people.

This Form 1 lesson is about staying healthy. You will learn what it means to be healthy, why personal and food hygiene matter, how litter and waste should be disposed of, and the four main ways in which diseases spread.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Health"}
A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not just the absence of disease.
:::

:::definition{term="Hygiene"}
The habits and practices that keep the body, food and surroundings clean so that disease does not spread.
:::

:::definition{term="Pathogen"}
A disease-causing organism (a germ), such as a virus, a bacterium or a parasite.
:::

:::definition{term="Vector"}
An animal that carries a pathogen from one host to another, such as the mosquito that carries the malaria parasite.
:::

:::definition{term="Disinfectant"}
A chemical, such as diluted bleach, that kills pathogens on surfaces, in drains and in toilets. It is not used on the body.
:::

## What is a healthy person?

Health has three parts, and a healthy person is well in all three.

| Part of health | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Physical** | The body works properly: the person eats well, has energy, sleeps well, is not ill and can do daily work and exercise. |
| **Mental** | The person thinks clearly, copes with stress and worry, and feels generally positive about life. |
| **Social** | The person gets on well with family, friends and neighbours, and takes part in the life of the school and community. |

A learner who is never ill but is constantly anxious, or who is isolated and has no friends, is not fully healthy. Good food, exercise, rest, friendships and hygiene all contribute to health.

## Personal hygiene

Personal hygiene means keeping your own body clean. Good habits include:

- washing your hands with soap and clean water **after using the toilet** and **before handling or eating food**;
- bathing every day and wearing clean clothes, especially underwear and socks;
- brushing your teeth at least twice a day;
- keeping your fingernails short and clean;
- covering your mouth and nose when you cough or sneeze;
- not sharing toothbrushes, razors or towels.

**Why personal hygiene is important**

- Soap and water wash pathogens off the skin, so they are not carried to the mouth or to food.
- It prevents skin infections such as ringworm and scabies, tooth decay and gum disease.
- It stops you passing pathogens on to other people.
- It prevents body odour and helps you feel confident with others, which is part of social health.

:::example{title="Example 1: the tippy-tap"}
Many rural schools and homes use a **tippy-tap**: a container of water hung from a frame and tipped by pressing a foot pedal, with a bar of soap on a string beside it. Because the hands never touch the container, germs are not passed from one user to the next, and the small amount of water used means that hand-washing is possible even where there are no taps. The wet ground below should drain into a soak pit of stones so that it does not become muddy or form a puddle where mosquitoes can breed.
:::

## Food hygiene

Food hygiene means handling, cooking and storing food so that it does not carry pathogens.

- Wash your hands before preparing or serving food.
- Wash fruit and vegetables in clean water.
- Cook food, especially meat, eggs and fish, thoroughly, and eat it while it is hot.
- Cover food so that flies cannot land on it.
- Keep raw meat away from cooked food.
- Wash plates, cups and pots after use and dry them on a raised dish rack, not on the ground.
- Store drinking water in clean, covered containers; boil it or treat it with chlorine if its source is not safe.
- Do not buy food from vendors who leave it uncovered.

**Why food hygiene is important**: pathogens in food and drinking water cause cholera, typhoid, dysentery and food poisoning. These diseases cause diarrhoea and vomiting, which can kill through **dehydration** (loss of water and salts from the body), especially in young children.

## Hygiene at school

Learners help to keep the school healthy by:

- **cleaning classrooms**: sweeping, then mopping with water containing a disinfectant;
- **cleaning toilets**: scrubbing the bowl, seat and floor with a brush and a disinfectant, wearing gloves, and keeping a supply of water and soap for hand-washing;
- **disinfecting drains**: removing rubbish that blocks them, then pouring in a disinfectant so that water flows away and does not stand where mosquitoes can breed and germs can multiply;
- **picking up litter** and putting it in bins or a refuse pit.

:::note{title="Safety"}
Never mix different cleaning products, for example bleach with another toilet cleaner. Some mixtures give off poisonous gases. Use disinfectants diluted as the label says, in a well-ventilated place.
:::

## Disposing of litter and waste

Litter that is simply dropped, or waste dumped in an open heap, attracts flies, rats and stray dogs, blocks drains, collects rainwater in tins and tyres where mosquitoes breed, and can choke livestock that eat plastic. The **Environmental Management Agency (EMA)** is responsible for protecting the environment in Zimbabwe and can fine people who litter. Waste can be disposed of in three main ways.

| Method | How it is done | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Burying** | Waste is put in a refuse pit and covered with a layer of soil each time. | Cheap and simple; covering the waste keeps away flies, rats and dogs; food and plant waste rots and adds nutrients to the soil. | Needs land, and the pit fills up; plastics, glass and tins do not rot; the waste can pollute underground water if the pit is near a well or borehole; an open pit is dangerous to children and animals. |
| **Burning** | Waste is burned in a pit or an incinerator. | Quickly reduces the amount of waste; kills pathogens; little land needed. | Smoke and poisonous gases, especially from burning plastics and rubber, pollute the air and harm health; carbon dioxide is released; a fire can spread and start a veld fire; aerosol cans and batteries can explode; ash still has to be disposed of. |
| **Recycling** | Paper, glass, metal cans and some plastics are collected, sorted and made into new products. | Saves raw materials and energy; less waste needs to be buried or burned; collectors and recycling businesses earn money and create jobs. | Waste must be sorted and kept clean; collecting and transporting it costs money; not every material can be recycled; buyers or recycling plants may be far away. |

Food and garden waste can also be **composted** (left to rot in a heap or pit) to make manure for the school garden. Reducing the waste you make, and reusing containers, is better still.

:::example{title="Example 2: choosing a disposal method"}
A rural day school produces three kinds of waste: food scraps from the kitchen, waste paper from classrooms and empty soft-drink cans and plastic bottles from the tuck shop.

- **Food scraps**: compost them or bury them in a refuse pit away from the borehole; they rot quickly and enrich the soil.
- **Paper**: recycle it if a collector is available; otherwise it can be burned safely in a pit, away from buildings and dry grass, because paper gives far less poisonous smoke than plastic.
- **Cans and plastic bottles**: collect them for recycling, since they do not rot when buried and plastics give off poisonous fumes when burned.

The best method depends on the type of waste: there is no single method that suits everything.
:::

## How diseases spread

Pathogens reach new hosts in four main ways. The syllabus links each route with a disease.

| Route | How the pathogen spreads | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Water** | Drinking water contaminated with faeces from an infected person carries bacteria into the body. | **Cholera**, also typhoid and dysentery |
| **Food** | Food is contaminated by dirty hands, by flies that have landed on faeces, or by unsafe water used to wash it. | Typhoid and food poisoning |
| **Contact** | Touching an infected person, especially their blood, vomit or other body fluids, or things soiled with them. | **Ebola**, also ringworm and scabies |
| **Vectors** | An animal carries the pathogen to people. The female *Anopheles* mosquito injects the malaria parasite when it bites. In bilharzia, freshwater snails carry the parasite: its larvae leave the snail and burrow through the skin of people who wade, wash or swim in the water. | **Malaria**, **bilharzia** |

Houseflies also act as carriers: they pick up bacteria on their feet and mouthparts from faeces and rubbish and leave them on uncovered food. Some diseases, such as tuberculosis and influenza, are spread through the **air** in droplets from coughs and sneezes.

### Breaking the chain

Every hygiene habit in this lesson blocks one of these routes.

| Route | How to block it |
| --- | --- |
| Water | Use safe water (protected boreholes, treated water) or boil or chlorinate it; use toilets so that faeces do not reach water sources. |
| Food | Wash hands, cover food, cook it thoroughly and keep flies away by disposing of waste properly. |
| Contact | Wash hands with soap; avoid touching body fluids; wear gloves when caring for the sick; isolate patients with dangerous diseases. |
| Vectors | Sleep under treated mosquito nets; drain stagnant water; avoid wading or swimming in water that may have snails; use toilets so that eggs in urine and faeces do not reach water. |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Health means not being ill"}
A person can be free of disease but still not healthy. Health includes mental and social well-being as well as physical health.
:::

:::mistake{title="Clear water is safe water"}
Bacteria are far too small to see. Clear water from an unprotected well can still carry cholera.
:::

:::mistake{title="Burning is always the best method"}
Burning plastics and rubber releases poisonous fumes, and fires can spread. Each method of waste disposal has advantages and disadvantages, so the method should suit the waste.
:::

:::mistake{title="All animals that carry germs are vectors in the same way"}
The mosquito carries the malaria parasite inside its body and injects it. A housefly carries bacteria on the outside of its body. Both spread disease, but in different ways.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When a question asks why a hygiene practice is important, say what it does **and** which disease it prevents, for example "washing hands after using the toilet removes bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid".
:::

:::exam-tip
For "advantages and disadvantages" questions, give at least one of each for **every** method asked about, and make each point specific (such as "plastics release poisonous fumes when burned", not just "it causes pollution").
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn one example disease for each route: cholera (water), typhoid (food), Ebola (contact) and malaria or bilharzia (vectors).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Health is physical, mental and social well-being, not just the absence of disease.
- Personal hygiene (hand-washing, bathing, clean teeth and clothes) and food hygiene (clean hands, covered and well-cooked food, safe water) stop pathogens reaching the mouth.
- Waste can be buried, burned or recycled: burying is cheap but needs land and can pollute groundwater; burning reduces volume and kills pathogens but pollutes the air and risks fires; recycling saves resources but needs sorting, collection and a buyer.
- Diseases spread through water (cholera), food (typhoid), contact (Ebola) and vectors (malaria, bilharzia).
- Each route can be blocked by a hygiene practice: safe water, covered and cooked food, hand-washing, mosquito nets and drained water.
:::
