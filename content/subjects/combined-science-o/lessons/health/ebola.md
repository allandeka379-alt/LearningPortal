## Introduction

Ebola virus disease (EVD) is one of the most feared infectious diseases in the world. On average about half of the people who catch it die, although the proportion has varied from about a quarter to nine-tenths in different outbreaks. It was first identified in 1976 in outbreaks in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, near the Ebola River, and in South Sudan. The largest outbreak, in Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone from 2014 to 2016, infected more than 28 000 people and killed more than 11 000.

Ebola does not spread through the air like influenza, so it can be stopped by careful hygiene. In this lesson you will learn what causes Ebola, how it spreads, its signs and symptoms and how it is treated and prevented, and you will compare it with three other diseases in the syllabus: malaria, typhoid and cholera.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Virus"}
A very small pathogen that can only reproduce inside the living cells of its host. Antibiotics do not kill viruses.
:::

:::definition{term="Incubation period"}
The time between catching a pathogen and the first symptoms appearing. For Ebola it is 2 to 21 days.
:::

:::definition{term="Signs and symptoms"}
A **sign** is something another person can observe or measure, such as a high temperature or bleeding. A **symptom** is something the patient feels and describes, such as a headache or tiredness.
:::

:::definition{term="Isolation"}
Keeping an infected person apart from other people, in a separate ward or treatment centre, so that the pathogen cannot spread to them.
:::

## Cause and spread

Ebola is caused by the **Ebola virus**. Fruit bats are thought to be its natural hosts. The virus first reaches people through contact with the blood, organs or body fluids of infected wild animals, such as fruit bats, monkeys, chimpanzees or forest antelope, that are handled or butchered for meat.

It then spreads from person to person by **direct contact**, through broken skin or the lining of the eyes, nose and mouth, with:

- the **blood and body fluids** (vomit, faeces, urine, saliva, sweat, breast milk or semen) of a person who is ill with Ebola or has died from it;
- **objects soiled with these fluids**, such as bedding, clothing and used needles.

People do not spread Ebola before their symptoms begin, and it is not spread through the air. The risk is highest for those who **care for the sick** and for those who **wash and touch the bodies of the dead** at funerals, because the virus is present in very large amounts late in the illness and after death. Health workers have often been infected when they treated patients without full protective clothing. The virus can stay in the semen of men who have recovered for many months.

## Signs and symptoms

After the incubation period of 2 to 21 days, the illness usually begins **suddenly**:

- **Early**: fever, extreme tiredness and weakness, muscle pain, headache and sore throat.
- **Later**: vomiting, diarrhoea and stomach pain, a rash, and signs that the kidneys and liver are failing. Some patients **bleed**, for example from the gums or nose, or have blood in their vomit or stools.

Because the early symptoms are like those of malaria and typhoid, a laboratory **blood test** is needed to confirm Ebola.

## Treatment

There is no medicine that can be bought at a pharmacy to cure Ebola, and antibiotics do not work against viruses. Patients are treated in **isolation** in special treatment centres, where staff give:

- **supportive care**: fluids and salts by mouth or by drip (intravenously) to replace what is lost through vomiting and diarrhoea, and medicines to reduce fever and pain;
- **antibody medicines**: since 2020, medicines that attack the most common type of Ebola virus have been approved, and they work best when given early.

Patients who receive early supportive care are much more likely to survive.

## Prevention and control

| Measure | Why it works |
| --- | --- |
| Isolating patients in treatment centres | Keeps their body fluids away from family and the community. |
| Protective clothing for health workers: gloves, gowns, masks and goggles | Stops body fluids touching the skin, eyes, nose and mouth. |
| Safe burials by trained teams | Prevents mourners touching bodies that carry large amounts of virus. |
| Hand-washing with soap and disinfecting soiled objects | Removes or kills virus picked up by touch. |
| Contact tracing | Everyone who had contact with a patient is checked daily for 21 days, so that anyone who falls ill is isolated at once. |
| Vaccination | A vaccine against the most common type of Ebola virus is given to contacts of patients and to health workers during outbreaks. |
| Not handling sick or dead wild animals or their raw meat | Prevents the virus passing from animals to people. |

:::example{title="Example 1: why 21 days?"}
A health worker in an outbreak area is told to visit a patient's family every day for 21 days after their last contact with the patient, and to take their temperature on each visit.

The reason is the incubation period. Someone infected on the last day of contact could show symptoms on any day from day 2 to day 21. If no one has fallen ill by day 21, none of them was infected. Anyone who develops a fever before then is isolated immediately, before they can infect others.
:::

## Comparing four diseases

The syllabus asks you to describe the causes, signs and symptoms and treatment of malaria, typhoid, Ebola and cholera. The pathogens are of three different types: a **parasite** (*Plasmodium*), **bacteria** and a **virus**.

| Disease | Cause (type of pathogen) | How it spreads | Main signs and symptoms | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Malaria | *Plasmodium*, a single-celled parasite | Bite of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito (vector) | Fever with shivering and sweating that come and go, headache, joint pain, vomiting; anaemia | Antimalarial drugs, given after a rapid diagnostic test |
| Typhoid | A bacterium (*Salmonella* Typhi) | Food and water contaminated with the faeces or urine of an infected person | High fever that rises over several days, headache, weakness, stomach pain, constipation or diarrhoea | Antibiotics, and fluids |
| Cholera | A bacterium (*Vibrio cholerae*) | Water and food contaminated with faeces | Sudden, severe watery diarrhoea and vomiting, leading quickly to dehydration | Oral rehydration solution (ORS) at once; drip fluids and antibiotics for severe cases |
| Ebola | The Ebola virus | Contact with the body fluids of the sick or dead, and with infected wild animals | Sudden fever, weakness, muscle pain; later vomiting, diarrhoea and sometimes bleeding | Isolation, fluids and supportive care; antibody medicines |

:::example{title="Example 2: which medicine?"}
A clinic nurse is told that a patient has a bacterial disease. Which two of the four diseases could it be, and would antibiotics help?

1. The bacterial diseases in the table are **typhoid** and **cholera**.
2. Antibiotics kill bacteria, so they help in both (for cholera, rehydration comes first, because dehydration kills fastest).
3. Antibiotics would not cure malaria (caused by a parasite, treated with antimalarials) or Ebola (caused by a virus).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating Ebola with antibiotics"}
Ebola is caused by a virus, and antibiotics do not kill viruses. Antibiotics are only given to Ebola patients to treat other bacterial infections.
:::

:::mistake{title="Ebola spreads through the air"}
Ebola spreads by direct contact with body fluids, not by breathing the same air. This is why gloves, gowns, hand-washing and safe burials control it.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling Plasmodium a bacterium"}
The malaria pathogen *Plasmodium* is a single-celled parasite (a protoctist), not a bacterium or a virus.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "describe the treatment" questions on Ebola, mention **isolation** and **supportive care** (fluids to replace those lost, and medicines for fever and pain).
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the pathogen type for each disease: malaria, *Plasmodium* (parasite); typhoid and cholera, bacteria; Ebola, a virus. The pathogen type tells you which medicine works.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Ebola is caused by the Ebola virus; fruit bats are thought to be its natural hosts.
- It spreads by direct contact with the blood and body fluids of the sick or dead, with soiled objects and with infected wild animals; not through the air, and not before symptoms begin.
- After 2 to 21 days: sudden fever, weakness, muscle pain, headache and sore throat, then vomiting, diarrhoea and sometimes bleeding.
- Treatment: isolation, supportive care with fluids, and antibody medicines; antibiotics do not kill viruses.
- Prevention: isolation, protective clothing, safe burials, hand-washing, contact tracing for 21 days and vaccination.
- Malaria (*Plasmodium*, mosquito vector, antimalarials), typhoid and cholera (bacteria, contaminated food and water, antibiotics and rehydration) and Ebola (virus, contact, supportive care) differ in pathogen, spread and treatment.
:::
