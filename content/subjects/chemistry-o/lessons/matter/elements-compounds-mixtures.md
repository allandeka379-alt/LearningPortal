## Introduction

Zimbabwe is rich in minerals. Gold is mined around Kadoma and Shurugwi, platinum and chrome come from the Great Dyke, and lithium from Bikita. Gold is an **element**: it cannot be broken down into anything simpler. The salt in your kitchen is a **compound** of two elements, sodium and chlorine. The air you breathe is a **mixture** of several gases.

Every substance you will meet in chemistry belongs to one of these three groups. Knowing which one tells you what the substance is made of and how it could be separated. In this lesson you will learn the differences, how to use chemical symbols, and how to classify substances from particle diagrams.

You should already know that all matter is made of particles, and that the smallest particle of an element is an atom.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Element"}
A substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions.
:::

:::definition{term="Compound"}
A substance made of two or more different elements chemically combined (bonded together) in a fixed proportion.
:::

:::definition{term="Mixture"}
Two or more substances (elements or compounds) that are together but not chemically combined. The proportions can vary.
:::

:::definition{term="Molecule"}
A particle made of two or more atoms joined by chemical bonds. A molecule may be an element ($\ce{O2}$) or a compound ($\ce{H2O}$).
:::

## Chemical symbols

Every element has a **chemical symbol** of one or two letters. The first letter is always a **capital** and the second, if there is one, is always **lower case**. This matters: $\ce{Co}$ is the element cobalt, but $\ce{CO}$ is the compound carbon monoxide.

Many symbols come from the English name, but some come from Latin.

| Element | Symbol | Element | Symbol |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrogen | H | Sodium (Latin *natrium*) | Na |
| Carbon | C | Potassium (Latin *kalium*) | K |
| Nitrogen | N | Iron (Latin *ferrum*) | Fe |
| Oxygen | O | Copper (Latin *cuprum*) | Cu |
| Chlorine | Cl | Silver (Latin *argentum*) | Ag |
| Chromium | Cr | Gold (Latin *aurum*) | Au |
| Lithium | Li | Lead (Latin *plumbum*) | Pb |
| Platinum | Pt | Nickel | Ni |

A formula shows the elements in a compound and the fixed ratio of their atoms. Every molecule of water, $\ce{H2O}$, contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom, wherever the water comes from.

## Comparing compounds and mixtures

| | Compound | Mixture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Composition | Fixed proportions (shown by a formula) | Proportions can vary |
| Bonding | Elements chemically combined | Substances not chemically combined |
| Properties | Different from those of its elements | Each part keeps its own properties |
| Separation | Only by a chemical reaction (for example electrolysis) | By physical methods: magnet, filtration, evaporation, distillation |
| Forming it | Usually an energy change (heat or light given out or taken in) | Usually no noticeable energy change |

:::example{title="Iron, sulfur and iron sulfide"}
Stir grey iron filings into yellow sulfur powder. You have a **mixture**: you can still see yellow and grey specks, you can mix the two in any proportions, and a magnet pulls the iron out, leaving the sulfur behind.

Now heat the mixture strongly in a test tube (in a fume cupboard). It glows red, and the glow spreads through the mixture even after the burner is removed, because energy is given out. A black solid, **iron(II) sulfide**, $\ce{FeS}$, forms. It is a **compound**. It is not yellow or grey, and a magnet no longer separates iron from sulfur, because the two elements are chemically combined in a new substance.
:::

:::example{title="Sodium chloride: new properties"}
Sodium is a soft metal that reacts violently with water; chlorine is a poisonous, pale yellow-green gas. When they react they form sodium chloride, the white solid we sprinkle on food. A compound has its own properties, quite different from those of the elements in it.
:::

## Classifying particle diagrams

Exam questions often show boxes of circles. In words, ask yourself three questions:

1. How many **different kinds** of particle are in the box? One kind means a pure substance; two or more kinds means a mixture.
2. Is each particle made of one kind of atom, or of **different kinds joined together**?
3. Are atoms joined into molecules, or separate?

:::worked-solution[Classifying four boxes of particles]
1. Box 1: separate single atoms, all identical. One kind of particle, one kind of atom: an **element** (like neon).
2. Box 2: identical molecules, each made of two identical atoms joined together. One kind of atom: an **element** made of molecules (like oxygen, $\ce{O2}$).
3. Box 3: identical molecules, each made of one large atom joined to two small atoms of a different kind. Different elements are chemically combined in a fixed ratio: a **compound** (like water, $\ce{H2O}$).
4. Box 4: some molecules of two identical atoms and some molecules made of different atoms joined together. Two kinds of particle: a **mixture** of an element and a compound.
:::

Examples to remember: oxygen, copper and gold are elements; water, carbon dioxide and sodium chloride are compounds; air, sea water, borehole water and alloys such as brass and steel are mixtures.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a compound 'a mixture of elements'"}
In a compound the elements are chemically **combined**; in a mixture they are only mixed. Heating iron with sulfur turns the mixture into a compound.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking every molecule is a compound"}
An oxygen molecule, $\ce{O2}$, contains two atoms, but both are oxygen, so oxygen is an element.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying air is a compound"}
Air is a mixture: its composition varies slightly from place to place, and its gases can be separated physically by fractional distillation of liquid air.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing symbols with the wrong capitals"}
Write $\ce{Mg}$, not MG or mg. $\ce{CO}$ (carbon monoxide) and $\ce{Co}$ (cobalt) are completely different substances.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **describe the difference** between a compound and a mixture, give a pair of contrasting points, for example "in a compound the elements are chemically combined in a fixed proportion; in a mixture they are not chemically combined and the proportions can vary".
:::

:::exam-tip
For particle diagrams, first count the kinds of particle, then look inside each particle for different kinds of atom. Name an example that fits each box if you are asked to.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An element contains only one type of atom; a compound contains different elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion; a mixture contains substances that are not chemically combined.
- Compounds have properties different from their elements and can only be separated into them by chemical reactions.
- Mixtures can have any composition, keep the properties of their parts and can be separated by physical methods.
- Symbols have a capital first letter and a lower-case second letter; some come from Latin names (Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Au, Pb).
- In particle diagrams: one kind of particle is a pure substance; different atoms joined together make a compound; two or more kinds of particle make a mixture.
:::
