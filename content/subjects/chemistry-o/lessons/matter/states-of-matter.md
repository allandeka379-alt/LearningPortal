## Introduction

On a winter morning in Nyanga you may find frost on the grass and water warming in a pot on the stove. When the water boils, bubbles of steam form inside it and escape. Frost, liquid water and steam (water vapour) are all the same substance, water, in three different **states of matter**: solid, liquid and gas.

Chemists explain the behaviour of all three states with one model, the **kinetic particle theory**. It says that all matter is made of tiny particles (atoms, molecules or ions) that are always moving. "Kinetic" means "to do with motion".

In this lesson you will use the theory to explain the properties of solids, liquids and gases, the changes of state between them, and what happens to the volume of a gas when its temperature or pressure changes. You should already know that matter has mass and takes up space, and that heating gives a substance energy.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Kinetic particle theory"}
The model in which all matter is made of very small particles in constant, random motion. The particles attract one another, and the higher the temperature, the faster they move (the more kinetic energy they have).
:::

:::definition{term="Melting point"}
The temperature at which a pure solid turns into a liquid. A pure liquid freezes at the same temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Boiling point"}
The temperature at which a pure liquid turns into a gas throughout the liquid, with bubbles of gas forming inside it.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The five changes of state"}
- **Melting**: solid to liquid (energy is taken in).
- **Freezing**: liquid to solid (energy is given out).
- **Boiling**: liquid to gas throughout the liquid, at the boiling point (energy is taken in).
- **Evaporation**: liquid to gas from the surface only, below the boiling point (energy is taken in).
- **Condensation**: gas to liquid (energy is given out).
:::

## Properties of solids, liquids and gases

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of its container | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Not fixed: spreads out to fill the container |
| Flows? | No | Yes | Yes |
| Can be compressed? | Hardly at all | Hardly at all | Easily |

## The particles in each state

Particle diagrams are usually drawn as circles in a box. In words, they show this:

| | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrangement | Regular pattern (a lattice) | Irregular (random) | Irregular (random) |
| Separation | Very close together, touching | Close together, mostly touching | Far apart, with large spaces |
| Motion | Vibrate about fixed positions | Move around, sliding past each other | Move quickly and randomly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | Strong | Weaker, but still significant | Very weak |

The particle picture explains the properties. A solid keeps its shape because strong forces hold its particles in fixed positions. A liquid flows because its particles can slide past one another, but its volume is fixed because they stay close together. A gas fills any container because its particles move freely in all directions, and it can be squashed because there is so much empty space between them.

:::example{title="Why a gas can be compressed but a liquid cannot"}
Pushing in the plunger of a sealed syringe full of air is easy: the air particles are far apart, so pushing in the plunger only reduces the empty space between them. If the syringe is full of water, the plunger hardly moves, because the water particles are already touching and cannot be pushed much closer together.
:::

## Changes of state and the kinetic particle theory

When a solid is heated, its particles gain kinetic energy and vibrate more strongly. At the **melting point** they have enough energy to overcome some of the forces that hold them in fixed positions. They break out of the regular arrangement and begin to slide past each other: the solid melts.

Heating the liquid makes its particles move faster still. At the **boiling point** the particles have enough energy to overcome the forces of attraction between them. They escape and move far apart as a gas, and bubbles of gas form all through the liquid.

When a gas is cooled, the reverse happens. Its particles lose kinetic energy and slow down, the forces of attraction pull them close together and the gas **condenses** to a liquid. Further cooling lets the forces pull the particles into fixed positions in a regular arrangement: the liquid **freezes**. Condensing and freezing give energy out to the surroundings.

:::example{title="Evaporation: why washing dries on the line"}
On a sunny day in Bulawayo a wet shirt dries in an hour or two, although the water never gets anywhere near its boiling point. At any temperature, some particles near the surface of the water are moving faster than average. These particles have enough energy to overcome the attraction of their neighbours and escape into the air: the water **evaporates**.

Evaporation is faster when it is warmer (more particles are fast enough to escape), when it is windy (the water vapour is carried away) and when the water is spread out (a larger surface).
:::

| Evaporation | Boiling |
| --- | --- |
| Happens at temperatures below the boiling point | Happens only at the boiling point |
| Only at the surface of the liquid | Throughout the liquid, with bubbles |
| Slow | Fast |

## Using melting and boiling points to predict a state

A substance is a **solid** below its melting point, a **liquid** between its melting point and its boiling point, and a **gas** above its boiling point.

:::worked-solution[Predicting the state of four substances at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$]
The table gives data for four substances, A to D.

| Substance | Melting point / °C | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | −210 | −196 |
| B | 44 | 280 |
| C | −95 | 69 |
| D | 1083 | 2567 |

1. A: $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is above its boiling point of $-196\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so A is a **gas**.
2. B: $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is below its melting point of $44\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so B is a **solid**.
3. C: $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ lies between $-95\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $69\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so C is a **liquid**.
4. D: $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is far below $1083\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so D is a **solid**.
5. Check one more temperature: at $50\ ^\circ\text{C}$, B would be a liquid, because $50$ lies between $44$ and $280$.
:::

## How temperature and pressure affect the volume of a gas

These two rules describe what you see:

- At **constant pressure**, heating a gas makes its volume **increase** (it expands); cooling it makes its volume decrease.
- At **constant temperature**, increasing the pressure on a gas makes its volume **decrease**; reducing the pressure lets it expand.

:::example{title="Supplement: explaining the rules with particles"}
**Heating.** A balloon left in the sun on a hot afternoon in Kariba gets bigger. The air particles inside gain kinetic energy and move faster, so they hit the inside of the balloon harder and more often. The balloon is stretchy, so the gas pushes outwards until the pressure inside again balances the pressure outside: its volume increases.

**Increasing the pressure.** When the plunger of a sealed syringe of air is pushed in, the same number of particles is forced into a smaller space. The particles are pushed closer together, so the volume of the gas decreases (and they now hit the walls more often).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the particles in a solid do not move"}
They do move: they **vibrate** about fixed positions. Only their positions are fixed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the particles get bigger when a substance expands"}
The particles themselves stay the same size. On heating they move faster and further apart, so the **spaces between them** get bigger.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up evaporation and boiling"}
Evaporation happens at the surface at temperatures below the boiling point; boiling happens throughout the liquid, at the boiling point.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying there are no forces between gas particles"}
The forces between gas particles are very weak, not absent. They are what pull the particles together when a gas condenses.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **describe** the particles in a state, give three things: the **arrangement** (regular or random), the **separation** (close together or far apart) and the **motion** (vibrating, sliding past each other, or moving quickly in all directions). Each is usually worth a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to **explain** a change of state, mention **energy** (the particles gain or lose kinetic energy) and the **forces of attraction** between particles (overcome when melting or boiling; pulling the particles together when condensing or freezing).
:::

:::exam-tip
Take care with negative temperatures in data tables: $-30\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is colder than $-5\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- All matter is made of particles in constant random motion: the kinetic particle theory.
- Solid particles are close together in a regular arrangement and vibrate about fixed positions; liquid particles are close together, randomly arranged and slide past each other; gas particles are far apart and move quickly in all directions.
- Melting, boiling and evaporation take in energy so that particles can overcome the forces between them; freezing and condensation give energy out.
- A substance is solid below its melting point, liquid between its melting and boiling points, and gas above its boiling point.
- A gas expands when heated at constant pressure, and its volume decreases when the pressure on it increases at constant temperature.
:::
