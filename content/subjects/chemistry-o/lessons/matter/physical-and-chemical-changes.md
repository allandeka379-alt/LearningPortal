## Introduction

Think about an evening at home. Candle wax melts and runs down the candle. Firewood burns to ash and smoke. Sugar disappears into a cup of tea. A jug of fresh milk left in a warm kitchen turns into thick, sour amasi. All of these are changes, but they are not all the same kind of change.

Chemists sort changes into two groups: **physical changes**, in which no new substance is made, and **chemical changes** (chemical reactions), in which new substances form. In this short lesson you will learn how to tell them apart.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Physical change"}
A change in which no new substance is formed. The substance may change state, shape or size, or dissolve, but it is still the same substance. Physical changes are usually easy to reverse.
:::

:::definition{term="Chemical change"}
A change in which one or more new substances are formed, with different properties from the starting substances. Chemical changes are usually difficult to reverse.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Signs that a chemical change may have happened"}
- A new substance with different properties appears.
- A gas is given off (fizzing, also called effervescence).
- A colour change that is not just dilution or mixing.
- A precipitate (an insoluble solid) forms when two solutions are mixed.
- Energy is given out or taken in (heat, light or sound), often a lot of it.
:::

## Telling them apart

The one test that always works is: **has a new substance been formed?** The signs above help you decide, but each one on its own can mislead. Water boiling makes bubbles, and condensing steam gives out energy, yet both are physical changes.

| Change | Type | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting candle wax | Physical | Still wax; it sets again on cooling |
| Dissolving sugar in tea | Physical | Still sugar; evaporating the water would leave it behind |
| Boiling water for tea | Physical | The steam is still water |
| Crushing rock salt | Physical | Smaller pieces of the same salt |
| Burning firewood | Chemical | Ash, carbon dioxide and water vapour form; heat and light are given out |
| Milk souring to amasi | Chemical | Bacteria turn milk sugar into a new substance, lactic acid |
| Rusting of an iron roof sheet | Chemical | Brown rust is a new substance |
| Cooking an egg | Chemical | The egg cannot be uncooked; new substances form |

:::example{title="A burning candle: both kinds of change"}
When a candle burns, the heat of the flame **melts** the wax near the wick: a physical change, because the melted wax sets again into wax if it drips onto a cold plate. The liquid wax then rises up the wick, turns into vapour and **burns**: a chemical change, because the wax reacts with oxygen to form new substances, carbon dioxide and water vapour, giving out heat and light. You cannot turn these gases back into wax.
:::

:::example{title="Is dissolving a chemical change?"}
Stir salt into water and the crystals disappear. No new substance has formed: the salt is still there, spread through the water, and you can taste it. Evaporating the water gets the salt back. Dissolving is a **physical** change.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking every change involving heat is chemical"}
Melting, boiling and condensing all involve energy changes but are physical. Some salts even make water noticeably colder when they dissolve, and this is still a physical change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking 'reversible' always means physical"}
Some chemical changes can be reversed: heating blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate makes white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, and adding water turns it blue again. Decide by asking whether a new substance forms, not only whether the change can be reversed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a dissolved substance has disappeared"}
A dissolved solid is still present, spread out between the solvent particles; that is why sea water tastes salty.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked whether a change is physical or chemical, always give the **reason**: "a new substance is formed" or "no new substance is formed". The reason usually earns the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
Use observations as evidence: fizzing (a gas forms), a precipitate, a colour change or a large release of heat. Then name the new substance if you can.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- In a physical change no new substance forms; it is usually easy to reverse (melting, boiling, dissolving, crushing).
- In a chemical change new substances with different properties form; it is usually hard to reverse (burning, rusting, cooking, souring of milk).
- Signs of a chemical change include fizzing, a precipitate, a colour change and energy given out or taken in.
- No single sign is proof: the deciding question is whether a new substance has been formed.
:::
