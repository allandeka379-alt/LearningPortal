## Introduction

At about half past twelve, the smell of sadza and beef stew drifts from the school kitchen into a classroom in Masvingo. Nobody carried the smell there. Particles from the food escaped into the air and spread out on their own, moving among the particles of the air. This spreading is called **diffusion**.

Diffusion is good evidence for the kinetic particle theory: it only makes sense if particles are constantly moving. In this lesson you will define diffusion, explain it, compare diffusion in gases and liquids, and (Supplement) use relative molecular masses to predict which gas diffuses faster.

You should already know the kinetic particle theory and how the particles are arranged in solids, liquids and gases.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Diffusion"}
The net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, as a result of their random motion.
:::

:::definition{term="Random motion"}
Movement in which each particle moves in a straight line until it collides with another particle or a wall, then changes direction unpredictably.
:::

:::definition{term="Relative molecular mass, $M_r$"}
The sum of the relative atomic masses of the atoms in a molecule, for example $M_r(\ce{NH3}) = 14 + (3 \times 1) = 17$.
:::

## How diffusion happens

Imagine many perfume particles crowded near an open bottle and none at the back of a room. Every particle moves randomly. Each one is as likely to move towards the crowded region as away from it, but because there are many more particles in the crowded region, **more particles move out of it than into it**. The overall (net) result is that particles spread from where they are concentrated to where they are not, until they are evenly spread.

When the particles are evenly spread they **keep moving**, but now as many move one way as the other, so there is no further net movement.

:::example{title="A crystal of potassium manganate(VII) in still water"}
Drop a small purple crystal of potassium manganate(VII) into a beaker of water and leave it completely still. The crystal dissolves, and the purple colour slowly spreads upwards through the water, even though nothing was stirred. After a few days the whole beaker is evenly purple.

The colour spreads because the dissolved particles move randomly among the water particles, from where they are concentrated (around the crystal) to where they are not. It is slow because liquid particles are close together: each particle collides with its neighbours constantly, so it makes very little progress in any one direction.
:::

## Diffusion in gases, liquids and solids

- **Gases:** fast. Gas particles move quickly and have large spaces between them, so they travel some distance before each collision.
- **Liquids:** slow. The particles are crowded together, so each one collides with its neighbours constantly and makes little progress in any one direction.
- **Solids:** extremely slow. The particles only vibrate about fixed positions.

**Temperature** matters too. At a higher temperature the particles have more kinetic energy and move faster, so diffusion is faster. A tea bag colours hot water much faster than cold water.

## Supplement: relative molecular mass and the rate of diffusion

At the same temperature, the particles of different gases have the same average kinetic energy. Lighter particles must therefore move **faster** than heavier ones. So:

:::keyfact{title="Supplement rule"}
At the same temperature, a gas with a **lower** relative molecular mass diffuses **faster** than a gas with a higher relative molecular mass.
:::

### The ammonia and hydrogen chloride experiment

Cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is pushed into one end of a long, dry glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid into the other end, at the same moment. Both ends are closed with bungs. After a few minutes a **white ring** forms inside the tube where the two gases meet and react:

$$
\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}
$$

The white solid is ammonium chloride. The ring forms **closer to the hydrochloric acid end**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: explaining where the ring forms]
1. Work out the relative molecular masses (Ar: H = 1, N = 14, Cl = 35.5): $M_r(\ce{NH3}) = 14 + 3 = 17$ and $M_r(\ce{HCl}) = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5$.
2. Ammonia has the lower $M_r$, so at the same temperature its molecules move faster on average.
3. Both gases start at the same moment, so in the same time the ammonia travels further along the tube.
4. The gases therefore meet nearer the hydrochloric acid end, and that is where the white ring of ammonium chloride forms.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: using the position of the ring]
In a 100 cm tube, the ring forms 59 cm from the ammonia end. How many times further did the ammonia travel than the hydrogen chloride?
1. Distance travelled by ammonia $= 59\ \text{cm}$.
2. Distance travelled by hydrogen chloride $= 100 - 59 = 41\ \text{cm}$.
3. Ratio $= \dfrac{59}{41} = 1.44$, so the ammonia travelled about 1.4 times as far.
4. Both gases moved for the same time, so ammonia diffused about 1.4 times faster.
:::

:::example{title="Ranking gases by how fast they diffuse"}
Put hydrogen, $\ce{H2}$; oxygen, $\ce{O2}$; and carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$, in order, fastest first (Ar: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16).

$M_r$ values: $\ce{H2} = 2$, $\ce{O2} = 32$, $\ce{CO2} = 12 + 32 = 44$. Lowest $M_r$ diffuses fastest, so the order is hydrogen, then oxygen, then carbon dioxide.
:::

:::note{title="Safety"}
Concentrated ammonia solution and concentrated hydrochloric acid give off choking, corrosive fumes. Set up the tube in a fume cupboard, handle the cotton wool with tongs and wear eye protection and gloves.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying particles 'want' to move to empty regions"}
Particles move randomly in all directions. The net movement from high to low concentration happens only because more particles start in the crowded region.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the ring at the wrong end"}
The faster gas travels **further**, so the ring is far from the ammonia end and close to the hydrochloric acid end. A ring near the ammonia end would mean that hydrogen chloride had diffused faster.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying heavier gases diffuse faster"}
At the same temperature it is the reverse: the lower the relative molecular mass, the faster the diffusion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the particles stop when they are evenly spread"}
They keep moving randomly; there is simply no further net movement.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
A full definition of diffusion has three parts: **net movement**, **from higher to lower concentration**, and **because of random motion** of the particles.
:::

:::exam-tip
For the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube, a complete answer quotes both $M_r$ values (17 and 36.5), says that ammonia molecules move faster, and concludes that ammonia travels further in the same time, so the ring forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end.
:::

:::exam-tip
If you are told the experiment was stirred or that the air was moving, the spreading is not diffusion alone. Diffusion experiments are always left still.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, caused by their random motion.
- Diffusion is fast in gases, slow in liquids and extremely slow in solids.
- Diffusion is faster at higher temperatures because the particles move faster.
- Supplement: at the same temperature, gases with a lower $M_r$ diffuse faster.
- In the ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube, white ammonium chloride forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end because ammonia ($M_r = 17$) diffuses faster than hydrogen chloride ($M_r = 36.5$).
:::
