## Introduction

Hospitals in Harare and Bulawayo need a steady supply of oxygen for patients who struggle to breathe. Engineering workshops cut and weld steel with oxygen flames. Dairy and beef farmers store frozen bull semen for artificial insemination in flasks of liquid nitrogen, and the Haber process needs huge amounts of nitrogen to make ammonia.

All of these gases come from the same free raw material: **air**. Air is a **mixture**, and its gases can be separated because they have different boiling points. First the air must be turned into a liquid, and then it is **fractionally distilled**.

You should already know how fractional distillation separates liquids with different boiling points, and the properties of the noble gases.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Liquefaction"}
Turning a gas into a liquid by cooling it, usually after compressing it.
:::

:::definition{term="Fractional distillation"}
Separating a mixture of liquids by their different boiling points, using a fractionating column.
:::

:::definition{term="Fraction"}
A part of a mixture collected over a narrow range of boiling points.
:::

## The composition of air

| Gas | Approximate percentage in clean, dry air (by volume) | Boiling point / °C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nitrogen, $\ce{N2}$ | 78 | −196 |
| oxygen, $\ce{O2}$ | 21 | −183 |
| argon, $\ce{Ar}$ | 0.9 | −186 |
| carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | 0.04 | turns from solid to gas at −78 |
| neon, helium, krypton, xenon | tiny traces | various |

Air also contains a variable amount of **water vapour**.

## Step 1: cleaning the air

1. The air is **filtered** to remove dust.
2. **Carbon dioxide** is removed (for example by passing the air through sodium hydroxide solution) and **water vapour** is removed (by cooling and drying). At the very low temperatures used later, they would turn into solid "dry ice" and ice and **block the pipes**.

## Step 2: liquefying the air

1. The clean air is **compressed** to a high pressure (about 200 atmospheres in the classic method). Compressing a gas warms it, so the heat is removed with cooling water.
2. The cold, compressed air is allowed to **expand** quickly through a narrow valve. A gas that expands like this **cools down**.
3. The cooled air is used to cool the next batch of incoming air, so the temperature falls further on each cycle, until the air **liquefies** at about **−200 °C**.

## Step 3: fractional distillation

The liquid air is fed into a tall **fractionating column**, which is slightly warmer at the bottom than at the top.

![Flow chart of air separation: air is filtered, carbon dioxide and water vapour are removed, the air is compressed and cooled, then expanded through a valve until it liquefies at about −200 °C, and the liquid air is fractionally distilled in a column where nitrogen (−196 °C) leaves as a gas at the top, argon (−186 °C) part-way up and oxygen (−183 °C) as a liquid at the bottom.](media:diagrams/air-separation-flow.png "Figure: liquefying and fractionally distilling air")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: the order of separation]
Boiling points: nitrogen −196 °C, argon −186 °C, oxygen −183 °C.

1. The most negative boiling point is the **lowest**: nitrogen, at −196 °C. Nitrogen boils most easily, so it rises to the **top** of the column and leaves as a gas.
2. Argon (−186 °C) boils at a slightly higher temperature and is drawn off **part-way** up the column.
3. Oxygen has the **highest** boiling point (−183 °C), so it stays as a **liquid** and collects at the **bottom**.
:::

Oxygen and argon boil only 3 °C apart, so separating them completely needs a very tall column. The noble gases krypton and xenon, which have higher boiling points, end up with the liquid oxygen, while neon and helium, with much lower boiling points, stay as gases with the nitrogen.

## Uses of the gases

| Gas | Uses |
| --- | --- |
| oxygen | hospitals (patients with breathing problems); with ethyne in torches for welding and cutting metal; steelmaking (blowing oxygen through molten iron burns off carbon); breathing apparatus for climbers at high altitude; liquid oxygen to burn the fuel in rocket engines |
| nitrogen | making ammonia in the Haber process; liquid nitrogen for freezing food and for storing cells, such as semen for cattle breeding; an unreactive gas in food packets (crisps) to stop the food going stale |
| carbon dioxide | fire extinguishers (it is denser than air and does not support burning); fizzy drinks; dry ice for keeping things cold |
| noble gases | argon as a shielding gas in welding and to fill light bulbs; neon in advertising signs; helium for balloons |

Carbon dioxide is removed **before** air is liquefied, so carbon dioxide for sale is usually collected from other processes instead, such as fermentation and the making of hydrogen for ammonia.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: oxygen in a volume of air]
How much oxygen is in 500 dm³ of air at room temperature and pressure? (One mole of gas occupies 24 dm³; $A_r$: O = 16)

1. Volume of oxygen $= 21\%$ of $500 = 0.21 \times 500 = 105$ dm³.
2. Moles of oxygen $= 105 \div 24 = 4.375$ mol.
3. Mass of oxygen $= 4.375 \times 32 = 140$ g.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking oxygen boils off first"}
The gas with the **lowest** (most negative) boiling point boils first. −196 °C is lower than −183 °C, so nitrogen boils first and oxygen is left as a liquid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling air a compound"}
Air is a mixture: its gases are not chemically combined, its composition can vary, and it can be separated by a physical method.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting why carbon dioxide and water are removed"}
They are removed because they would **freeze** and block the pipes, not because they are poisonous.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When explaining the order of separation, quote the boiling points and use the words "lowest boiling point" and "boils off first" or "stays as a liquid".
:::

:::exam-tip
For uses, link each use to a property: oxygen **supports burning and breathing**; nitrogen and argon are **unreactive**; liquid nitrogen is **very cold**; carbon dioxide is **denser than air** and **does not support burning**.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Clean, dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and 0.04% carbon dioxide.
- Dust, carbon dioxide and water vapour are removed first, because the last two would freeze and block the pipes.
- Air is liquefied by compressing it, cooling it and letting it expand, repeatedly, until it reaches about −200 °C.
- In the fractionating column nitrogen (−196 °C) leaves at the top, argon (−186 °C) part-way, and oxygen (−183 °C) collects as a liquid at the bottom.
- Oxygen is used in medicine, welding and steelmaking; nitrogen for ammonia, freezing and packaging; carbon dioxide in fire extinguishers and drinks; noble gases in welding, lamps and balloons.
:::
