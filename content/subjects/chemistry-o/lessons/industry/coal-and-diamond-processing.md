## Introduction

Zimbabwe has two very different kinds of carbon underground. Around **Hwange** in Matabeleland North lie large deposits of **coal**, used in power stations, boilers, tobacco barns and the iron and steel and ferrochrome industries. The **Marange** diamond fields in Manicaland, in the east, and the **Murowa** mine near Zvishavane, in the Midlands, produce **diamonds**: the same element, carbon, but in its hardest and most valuable form.

Neither is used straight from the ground. Coal is washed, and can be turned into coke, gas and chemicals; diamonds must be separated from tonnes of rock, then cut and polished. In this lesson (ZIMSEC Form 4) you will learn these processes, how they add value to the raw materials, and their effects on the environment. You should already know about destructive distillation of coal and the giant covalent structure of diamond.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Coal washing"}
Removing rock (such as shale) and minerals (such as pyrite) from crushed coal, usually by separating them according to density.
:::

:::definition{term="Coking"}
Heating coal strongly in the absence of air (destructive distillation) to make coke.
:::

:::definition{term="Coal gasification"}
Reacting coal with steam (and a limited amount of oxygen) at a high temperature to make a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, called synthesis gas.
:::

:::definition{term="Value addition"}
Processing a raw material into a product that is worth more, for example cutting and polishing a rough diamond.
:::

:::definition{term="Beneficiation"}
Processing minerals in the country where they are mined, instead of exporting the raw material.
:::

## Processing coal

### Washing

Mined coal is mixed with shale, sandstone and pyrite (iron sulfide, $\ce{FeS2}$). After crushing, it is put into a **dense liquid** made by suspending fine magnetite powder in water. Coal (density about 1.3–1.5 g/cm³) floats; the heavier rock and pyrite (about 2.4 g/cm³ and more) sink.

Washed coal produces less ash, has a higher heating value, is cheaper to transport and, because pyrite is removed, gives less sulfur dioxide (and so less acid rain) when it burns.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: choosing the liquid for coal washing]
Coal has a density of about 1.4 g/cm³ and shale about 2.5 g/cm³. Which liquid density, 1.0, 2.0 or 3.0 g/cm³, would separate them, and how?

1. A solid floats in a liquid that is denser than it and sinks in one that is less dense.
2. In 1.0 g/cm³ both sink; in 3.0 g/cm³ both float: no separation.
3. In **2.0 g/cm³** the coal (1.4) floats and the shale (2.5) sinks, so the clean coal is skimmed off the top.
:::

### Coking and coal-tar chemicals

Heating coal to about 1000 °C without air gives **coke**, coal gas, coal tar and ammoniacal liquor (see the lesson on fuels from coal). Coke is the fuel and **reducing agent** in blast furnaces and ferrochrome smelters. Coal tar is separated into many chemicals:

| Coal-tar product | Use |
| --- | --- |
| creosote | preserving wooden poles and fence posts |
| naphthalene | mothballs; making dyes |
| phenol | disinfectants; making plastics and medicines |
| benzene and similar liquids | solvents; making dyes and other chemicals |
| pitch | road surfacing and roofing |

### Gasification

Coal (carbon) reacts with steam at a high temperature:

$$
\ce{C + H2O -> CO + H2}
$$

This reaction is **endothermic**, so heat is supplied by burning some of the coal in a limited supply of oxygen:

$$
\ce{2C + O2 -> 2CO}
$$

The product, **synthesis gas** (carbon monoxide and hydrogen), is a fuel gas, because both gases burn. It can also be used to make **hydrogen** (for ammonia and fertilisers) and **methanol**. Gasification lets a coal-rich country turn coal into chemicals it would otherwise import.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how much gas from gasification?]
Calculate the total volume of gas, at room temperature and pressure, made when 36 kg of carbon reacts completely with steam. ($A_r$: C = 12; 1 mol of gas = 24 dm³ at r.t.p.)

1. $n(\ce{C}) = 36\,000 \div 12 = 3000$ mol.
2. $\ce{C + H2O -> CO + H2}$: 3000 mol of C gives 3000 mol of CO **and** 3000 mol of $\ce{H2}$: 6000 mol of gas.
3. Volume $= 6000 \times 24 = 144\,000\ \text{dm}^3 = 144\ \text{m}^3$.
:::

## Processing diamonds

Diamond is pure carbon with a giant covalent structure. It is **unreactive**, so it cannot be extracted by a chemical reaction the way metals are; instead it is separated from the ore using its physical properties. Diamond is very **hard**, **dense** (3.5 g/cm³, denser than most rock), **water-repellent** (it sticks to grease) and most diamonds **glow under X-rays**.

1. **Crushing:** the ore is crushed carefully, with the crushers set so that large diamonds are not broken.
2. **Scrubbing and screening:** mud is washed off and the material is sorted by size.
3. **Dense-medium separation:** the gravel is mixed with a dense liquid (fine ferrosilicon powder in water). Diamonds and other heavy minerals sink; the lighter waste floats off.
4. **Recovery:** the heavy concentrate passes under **X-rays**. Diamonds glow; a detector sees each flash and fires a jet of air that blows the diamond into a collecting box. On a **grease table**, wet waste slides off while water-repellent diamonds stick to the grease.
5. **Hand sorting:** the final sorting is done by hand in secure glove boxes.

### Cutting and polishing: value addition

Rough diamonds are sorted by size, colour and clarity. Gem-quality stones are **cut**, by sawing with diamond-edged blades or lasers, and **polished** on wheels coated with diamond powder: only diamond is hard enough to cut and polish diamond. The flat **facets** reflect light inside the stone so that it sparkles. Cutting removes much of the mass, yet the polished gem is worth far more than the rough stone. Poorer stones become **industrial diamonds** for drill bits, saw blades and abrasives.

:::example{title="Example 3: why cut and polish in Zimbabwe?"}
Exporting rough diamonds earns money once. Cutting and polishing them in Zimbabwe (beneficiation) means the country sells a more valuable product, creates skilled jobs for cutters and polishers, develops related businesses such as jewellery making, and keeps more of the earnings in the country.
:::

## Environmental and safety effects

| Industry | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| coal | coal dust (lung disease); methane in mines (explosions); waste dumps containing pyrite form sulfuric acid that drains into rivers (acid mine drainage); coal dumps can catch fire by themselves; burning coal gives carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide |
| diamonds | large areas of land dug up; alluvial mining can silt rivers if waste is not controlled; dust and noise; large volumes of water used |

Mines reduce these effects by watering roads to keep dust down, treating mine water, controlling waste dumps and **rehabilitating** land (refilling pits, replacing topsoil and replanting).

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying diamonds are extracted by a chemical reaction"}
Diamond is unreactive and is already pure carbon. It is **separated** from ore by physical methods: density, X-ray glow and grease.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing the gasification products as carbon dioxide and hydrogen"}
Coal and steam give **carbon monoxide** and hydrogen: $\ce{C + H2O -> CO + H2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking cutting reduces value because it reduces mass"}
A polished gem is lighter than the rough stone but worth much more, because of its sparkle and the skill added: that is value addition.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Coal washing removes shale and pyrite by density, giving cleaner coal with less ash and sulfur.
- Coking gives coke (reducing agent and fuel) and coal-tar chemicals such as creosote, naphthalene, phenol and pitch.
- Gasification: $\ce{C + H2O -> CO + H2}$ (endothermic); synthesis gas is a fuel and a source of hydrogen and methanol.
- Diamonds are separated by physical methods: crushing, dense-medium separation, X-ray sorting, grease tables, hand sorting.
- Cutting and polishing add value; processing in Zimbabwe (beneficiation) creates jobs and income; both industries must control dust, water pollution and land damage.
:::
