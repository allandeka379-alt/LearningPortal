## Introduction

Limestone is one of the most useful rocks in Zimbabwe. It is quarried to make cement for houses, bridges and dams, and it is burnt in kilns to make **lime**. Farmers spread lime on acidic fields so that maize and other crops can grow well, builders mix it into mortar, and many rural homes are painted white with limewash.

In this lesson you will follow the **lime cycle**, from limestone to quicklime to slaked lime and back to calcium carbonate, and see how each substance is used. You should already know how acids react with bases and carbonates, and the meaning of thermal decomposition and exothermic and endothermic changes.

Lime in agriculture and construction is **ZIMSEC** Form 3 content (metals and non-metals).
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Limestone"}
A rock that is mainly calcium carbonate, $\ce{CaCO3}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Quicklime"}
Calcium oxide, $\ce{CaO}$: a white solid made by heating limestone.
:::

:::definition{term="Slaked lime"}
Calcium hydroxide, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$: made by adding water to quicklime. A solution of it in water is **limewater**.
:::

:::definition{term="Thermal decomposition"}
The breaking down of a compound into simpler substances by heating.
:::

## Making quicklime in a lime kiln

Calcium carbonate decomposes when it is heated strongly (at about 900 °C or more):

$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}
$$

The reaction is **endothermic**, so the kiln needs a lot of fuel.

**A lime kiln (in words).** A tall, upright furnace is loaded at the top with lumps of limestone (often mixed with coal or another fuel). Hot air is blown in at the bottom. As the limestone moves slowly down through the hottest part it decomposes, quicklime is drawn off at the bottom, and carbon dioxide and the waste gases leave at the top.

In a closed container the change is reversible, $\ce{CaCO3 <=> CaO + CO2}$, and it would stop when enough carbon dioxide had built up. In the kiln the stream of air **carries the carbon dioxide away**, so the decomposition goes to completion.

## Slaking quicklime

When water is sprinkled on lumps of quicklime, they hiss, give off steam and crumble into a white powder of **slaked lime**:

$$
\ce{CaO(s) + H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(s)}
$$

The reaction is very **exothermic**. Quicklime is caustic: it burns skin and eyes and reacts violently with water, so gloves and eye protection are essential.

Calcium hydroxide dissolves slightly in water to give **limewater**, an alkaline solution used to test for carbon dioxide (it turns milky as calcium carbonate forms):

$$
\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) -> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}
$$

## Lime in agriculture

Soils become **acidic** over time because of rain, the decay of plant material and the use of ammonium fertilisers, and many sandy soils are acidic to begin with. Most crops, including maize, grow poorly in strongly acidic soil.

Farmers add a base to **neutralise** the acid. Quicklime and slaked lime act quickly; powdered limestone (often sold as **agricultural lime**) acts more slowly but is cheaper, safer to handle and lasts longer. For example:

$$
\ce{Ca(OH)2 + 2HNO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2H2O}
$$

Lime should be applied a few weeks before planting and **not** at the same time as ammonium fertilisers, because an alkali releases ammonia gas from ammonium salts.

## Lime in construction

- **Mortar** is slaked lime (often with some cement) mixed with sand and water. It holds bricks together and slowly hardens as water evaporates and the calcium hydroxide reacts with carbon dioxide from the air, forming hard calcium carbonate.
- **Cement** is made by heating limestone with clay to about 1450 °C in a long rotating kiln. Mixed with sand, gravel and water it makes **concrete**, which sets hard.
- **Limewash** (whitewash) is slaked lime stirred in water. Painted on walls, it reacts with carbon dioxide to form a white coat of calcium carbonate.

:::keyfact{title="The lime cycle"}
limestone ($\ce{CaCO3}$) → heat → quicklime ($\ce{CaO}$) → add water → slaked lime ($\ce{Ca(OH)2}$) → carbon dioxide from the air → calcium carbonate again.
:::

## Calculations

:::worked-solution[Example 1: quicklime from limestone]
How much quicklime can be made from 25 tonnes of pure limestone? ($A_r$: C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40)

1. $M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100$; $M_r(\ce{CaO}) = 40 + 16 = 56$.
2. From the equation, 100 t of $\ce{CaCO3}$ gives 56 t of $\ce{CaO}$ (and 44 t of $\ce{CO2}$).
3. $25 \times \dfrac{56}{100} = 14$ tonnes of quicklime; the carbon dioxide released is $25 - 14 = 11$ tonnes.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: slaked lime to neutralise acid]
A soil sample contains acid equivalent to 2.0 mol of nitric acid. What mass of slaked lime neutralises it? ($A_r$: H = 1)

1. $\ce{Ca(OH)2 + 2HNO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2H2O}$: 1 mol of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ neutralises 2 mol of acid.
2. Moles of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ needed $= 2.0 \div 2 = 1.0$ mol.
3. $M_r(\ce{Ca(OH)2}) = 40 + 2 \times 17 = 74$, so the mass is $74$ g.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the names"}
Limestone is $\ce{CaCO3}$; quicklime is $\ce{CaO}$; slaked lime is $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$; limewater is a solution of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling the kiln reaction exothermic"}
Decomposing limestone takes in energy (endothermic). Slaking quicklime gives out energy (exothermic).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying lime is acidic"}
Lime (calcium oxide or hydroxide) is a **base**. That is why it neutralises acidic soil.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked how lime helps crops, say that it **neutralises** acids in the soil and **raises the pH**, and give a balanced equation if you can.
:::

:::exam-tip
In reacting-mass questions remember: 100 g of $\ce{CaCO3}$ gives 56 g of $\ce{CaO}$ and 44 g of $\ce{CO2}$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Limestone (calcium carbonate) is heated in a kiln: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$ (endothermic).
- Quicklime reacts exothermically with water to form slaked lime: $\ce{CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2}$.
- Lime and powdered limestone neutralise acidic soils; they should not be applied together with ammonium fertilisers.
- Mortar and limewash harden as calcium hydroxide reacts with carbon dioxide to form calcium carbonate; cement is made from limestone and clay.
- Quicklime and slaked lime are caustic: protect skin and eyes.
:::
