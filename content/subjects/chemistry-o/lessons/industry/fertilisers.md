## Introduction

At planting time many Zimbabwean maize farmers put a **basal** fertiliser called **Compound D** in the planting holes. A few weeks after the crop comes up they **top-dress** it with **ammonium nitrate (AN)**. Every harvest of grain, tobacco or cotton carries nutrients away from the field, and fertilisers put them back so that the next crop can grow well.

In this lesson you will learn which elements plants need, how fertilisers supply them, how two important nitrogen fertilisers are made, and how to use the numbers on a fertiliser bag. You should already know how salts are made by neutralisation and how to calculate percentages by mass.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
Ammonium salts, nitrates and NPK fertilisers are **Cambridge Core** (10.2). Making ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate is **ZIMSEC Form 4**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fertiliser"}
A substance added to soil to supply the elements that plants need for healthy growth.
:::

:::definition{term="NPK fertiliser"}
A fertiliser that supplies the three main plant nutrients: nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K). It is usually a mixture of salts.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication"}
The over-enrichment of a lake or river with nutrients such as nitrates and phosphates, which leads to rapid growth of algae and then loss of dissolved oxygen.
:::

## What the three main nutrients do

| Element | Taken up as | Main effect on the plant | If it is lacking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nitrogen, N | nitrate ions $\ce{NO3-}$ and ammonium ions $\ce{NH4+}$ | growth of leaves and stems; needed to make proteins and chlorophyll | stunted growth, yellow leaves |
| phosphorus, P | phosphate ions | healthy roots; ripening of seeds and fruit | poor roots, slow growth |
| potassium, K | potassium ions $\ce{K+}$ | flowering and fruiting; resistance to disease and drought | poor flowers and fruit |

## Common fertilisers

| Fertiliser | Formula | Supplies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ammonium nitrate (AN) | $\ce{NH4NO3}$ | N (two nitrogen atoms in each formula unit) |
| ammonium sulfate | $\ce{(NH4)2SO4}$ | N, and also sulfur |
| urea | $\ce{CO(NH2)2}$ | N |
| potassium nitrate | $\ce{KNO3}$ | N and K |
| ammonium phosphate | $\ce{(NH4)3PO4}$ | N and P |
| potassium chloride | $\ce{KCl}$ | K |

Ammonium salts and nitrates are soluble, so plant roots can take up their ions quickly.

## Reading a fertiliser bag

Compound fertilisers are labelled with three numbers. **Compound D** is 7 : 14 : 7. The numbers are the percentages by mass of nitrogen (as N), phosphorus (expressed as $\ce{P2O5}$) and potassium (expressed as $\ce{K2O}$).

:::worked-solution[Example 1: nutrients in a bag of Compound D]
A 50 kg bag of Compound D (7 : 14 : 7).

1. Nitrogen: 7% of 50 kg $= 0.07 \times 50 = 3.5$ kg.
2. Phosphorus (as $\ce{P2O5}$): 14% of 50 kg $= 0.14 \times 50 = 7.0$ kg.
3. Potassium (as $\ce{K2O}$): 7% of 50 kg $= 3.5$ kg.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: nitrogen in ammonium nitrate]
1. $M_r(\ce{NH4NO3}) = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80$, and it contains $2 \times 14 = 28$ of nitrogen.
2. Percentage of nitrogen in pure ammonium nitrate $= \dfrac{28}{80} \times 100 = 35.0\%$.
3. Fertiliser-grade AN is labelled about 34.5% N, slightly less than the pure compound. A 10 kg bag therefore supplies $0.345 \times 10 = 3.45$ kg of nitrogen.
:::

## Making ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate (ZIMSEC)

Both are made by **neutralising** ammonia, a base, with an acid:

$$
\ce{NH3(aq) + HNO3(aq) -> NH4NO3(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{2NH3(aq) + H2SO4(aq) -> (NH4)2SO4(aq)}
$$

**In the laboratory** (the titration method, because aqueous ammonia is an alkali):

1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of aqueous ammonia into a conical flask and add a few drops of methyl orange.
2. Add the acid from a burette until the indicator just changes from yellow to orange. Record the volume.
3. Repeat with the same volumes but **no indicator**, so the salt is not contaminated.
4. Evaporate the solution until it reaches its **crystallisation point** (crystals form on a glass rod dipped in and cooled), then leave it to cool.
5. Filter off the crystals, wash them with a little cold water and dry them between filter papers. Ammonium nitrate must never be heated to dryness, because it decomposes and can explode.

**In industry**, ammonia gas reacts with nitric acid in a neutraliser. The reaction is exothermic, and the heat helps to evaporate water. The hot, concentrated solution is sprayed down a tall tower against rising air, and the droplets solidify into small pellets called **prills**, which are coated so they do not stick together.

## Problems with fertilisers

- **Eutrophication:** rain washes excess nitrate and phosphate into rivers and lakes. Algae grow rapidly; when they die, bacteria decompose them and use up the dissolved oxygen, so fish die. Lakes near cities, such as Lake Chivero near Harare, have suffered from algal blooms and water hyacinth fed by nutrients from sewage and farm run-off.
- **Nitrates in drinking water** can be harmful, especially to babies.
- **Soil acidity:** ammonium fertilisers make soil more acidic over time, so the soil may need liming.
- **Lost nitrogen:** ammonium fertilisers should not be applied at the same time as lime. Alkalis release ammonia gas from ammonium salts, for example $\ce{2NH4NO3 + Ca(OH)2 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O}$, so nitrogen is lost to the air.

Applying the right amount at the right time, not just before heavy rain, and leaving strips of grass beside rivers all reduce these problems.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking the second number is the percentage of phosphorus"}
On a fertiliser label, phosphorus and potassium are given as the oxides $\ce{P2O5}$ and $\ce{K2O}$. Only the first number is simply the percentage of the element (nitrogen).
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting both nitrogen atoms in ammonium nitrate"}
$\ce{NH4NO3}$ contains two nitrogen atoms: one in the ammonium ion and one in the nitrate ion. Use $2 \times 14 = 28$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing lime with ammonium fertilisers"}
The alkali releases ammonia gas, wasting the nitrogen. Apply lime some weeks before or after an ammonium fertiliser.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "explain how fertiliser causes fish to die", give the full chain: nutrients run off → algae grow → algae die → bacteria decompose them → oxygen used up → fish die.
:::

:::exam-tip
In a fertiliser calculation, write the percentage as a decimal and multiply: 34.5% of 120 kg is $0.345 \times 120$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Plants need nitrogen (leaves, proteins), phosphorus (roots, ripening) and potassium (flowers, fruit, disease resistance).
- Ammonium salts and nitrates are soluble nitrogen fertilisers; NPK fertilisers such as Compound D (7 : 14 : 7) supply all three main nutrients.
- Ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate are made by neutralising ammonia with nitric acid or sulfuric acid, then crystallising.
- The mass of a nutrient in a fertiliser = percentage ÷ 100 × mass of fertiliser.
- Overuse causes eutrophication and nitrate pollution; ammonium fertilisers acidify soil and should not be mixed with lime.
:::
