## Introduction

Acids and bases are everywhere in a Zimbabwean home. Lemon juice and vinegar taste sour because they are acidic, and so does *amasi* (*mukaka wakakora*), milk that has soured naturally as bacteria turn its sugar into lactic acid. A car battery contains sulfuric acid, and your stomach makes hydrochloric acid to help digest food. On the other side are the bases: indigestion tablets, toothpaste, a solution of wood ash, and the agricultural lime that farmers spread on acidic soils before planting maize.

In this lesson you will learn the reactions that **all** acids share, what makes a base different from an alkali, and why mixing an acid with an alkali produces water. These reactions are the foundation for making salts, testing for ions and controlling soil pH. You should already be able to write word equations and simple balanced symbol equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Acid"}
A substance that produces hydrogen ions, $\ce{H+(aq)}$, when it dissolves in water. Examples: hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl}$; sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$; nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$; ethanoic acid (in vinegar).
:::

:::definition{term="Base"}
An oxide or hydroxide of a metal. A base reacts with an acid to form a salt and water. Examples: copper(II) oxide, $\ce{CuO}$; magnesium hydroxide, $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Alkali"}
A base that is soluble in water. Alkalis produce hydroxide ions, $\ce{OH-(aq)}$, in solution. Examples: sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide (slightly soluble) and aqueous ammonia.
:::

:::definition{term="Salt"}
A compound formed when the hydrogen of an acid is replaced by a metal ion (or by the ammonium ion). Hydrochloric acid makes **chlorides**, sulfuric acid makes **sulfates** and nitric acid makes **nitrates**.
:::

:::definition{term="Neutralisation"}
The reaction of an acid with a base to form a salt and water. For an acid and an alkali the reaction is between hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions: $\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali"}
Copper(II) oxide is a base because it neutralises acids, but it does not dissolve in water, so it is **not** an alkali. Sodium hydroxide is both.
:::

## The typical reactions of acids

Dilute acids behave in the same way because they all contain $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions. Three general reactions are worth learning by heart.

### 1. Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen

Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series, such as magnesium, zinc and iron, react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. You **observe** bubbles (effervescence), the metal getting smaller and the mixture getting warm. The gas pops with a lighted splint: it is hydrogen.

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

Copper, silver and gold are below hydrogen, so they do **not** react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid.

### 2. Acid + base → salt + water

Black copper(II) oxide dissolves in warm dilute sulfuric acid to give a blue solution of copper(II) sulfate. No gas is given off.

$$
\ce{CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

With an alkali the reaction happens in solution and there is nothing to see, except a temperature rise:

$$
\ce{KOH(aq) + HCl(aq) -> KCl(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

### 3. Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide

Carbonates fizz in acids. The gas turns limewater milky: it is carbon dioxide.

$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}
$$

This is why vinegar removes the white scale (calcium carbonate) from inside a kettle.

## The reactions of bases and alkalis

Bases neutralise acids, as above. Alkalis have one more reaction you need: when an alkali is **warmed** with an ammonium salt, ammonia gas is given off. Ammonia has a sharp smell and turns damp red litmus paper blue.

$$
\ce{NH4Cl(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + NH3(g)}
$$

:::example{title="Lime and ammonium fertilisers on a farm near Chinhoyi"}
A farmer wants to lime an acidic field and top-dress the same maize crop with ammonium nitrate (AN). Lime is a base; here it is slaked lime, calcium hydroxide. If the two are spread together, the base reacts with the ammonium ions and ammonia escapes into the air:

$$
\ce{Ca(OH)2 + 2NH4NO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2NH3 + 2H2O}
$$

Nitrogen that the crop needed is lost. Liming is therefore done well before ammonium fertilisers are applied.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: magnesium carbonate and dilute nitric acid]
Write the word equation and a balanced symbol equation, and state two observations.

1. The salt of nitric acid is a nitrate, so: magnesium carbonate + nitric acid → magnesium nitrate + water + carbon dioxide.
2. Formulae: $\ce{MgCO3}$, $\ce{HNO3}$, $\ce{Mg(NO3)2}$, $\ce{H2O}$, $\ce{CO2}$.
3. Two nitrate ions are needed, so two $\ce{HNO3}$: $\ce{MgCO3 + 2HNO3 -> Mg(NO3)2 + H2O + CO2}$.
4. Observations: effervescence (bubbles), and the white solid disappears to leave a colourless solution.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: deciding what a white powder could be]
A white powder fizzes with dilute hydrochloric acid, and the gas turns limewater milky. When the powder is warmed with sodium hydroxide solution, no ammonia is given off. Suggest what kind of compound it is.

1. Fizzing with an acid and a gas that turns limewater milky means carbon dioxide, so the powder is a **carbonate**.
2. No ammonia on warming with an alkali means it is **not** an ammonium compound.
3. So it is a metal carbonate, for example calcium carbonate or sodium carbonate. Further tests (such as a flame test) would be needed to identify the metal.
:::

## Acids, alkalis and their ions

All acids in water give $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions; all alkalis give $\ce{OH-(aq)}$ ions. When they react, these ions join to make water molecules. The other ions (such as $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ above) are left in solution as the salt. So the neutralisation of **any** acid by **any** alkali can be summed up as:

$$
\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}
$$

The same idea explains everyday uses of bases. Indigestion tablets containing magnesium hydroxide or calcium carbonate neutralise excess stomach acid, and most toothpastes are mildly alkaline so that they neutralise the acids made by bacteria on your teeth.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying that every base is an alkali"}
Only **soluble** bases are alkalis. Copper(II) oxide and magnesium hydroxide are bases but not alkalis.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving hydrogen as the gas from a carbonate"}
Acid + carbonate gives **carbon dioxide** (and water). Hydrogen comes from acid + metal.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the water"}
Acid + base and acid + carbonate both make water. Leaving it out loses the mark and leaves the equation unbalanced.
:::

:::mistake{title="Expecting copper to fizz in dilute acid"}
Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. To make a copper(II) salt from a dilute acid, start from a base or a carbonate instead: copper(II) oxide, copper(II) hydroxide or copper(II) carbonate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up sulfate and sulfide"}
Sulfuric acid makes sulfate salts ($\ce{SO4^2-}$). A sulfide ($\ce{S^2-}$) contains no oxygen.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"State what you **observe**" means what you would see: "bubbles", "the solid dissolves", "a blue solution forms". "Carbon dioxide is produced" is a product, not an observation, unless you add the limewater test.
:::

:::exam-tip
Name the salt from the acid: hydrochloric → chloride, sulfuric → sulfate, nitric → nitrate. Then build the formula from the ion charges before balancing.
:::

:::exam-tip
For the alkali + ammonium salt reaction, mention **warming** and the test for ammonia (damp red litmus turns blue). Both details often carry marks.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Acids produce $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions in water; alkalis produce $\ce{OH-(aq)}$ ions.
- Bases are metal oxides and hydroxides; alkalis are bases that dissolve in water.
- Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen; acid + base → salt + water; acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
- Alkali + ammonium salt (warmed) → salt + water + ammonia.
- Neutralisation of an acid by an alkali: $\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$.
:::
