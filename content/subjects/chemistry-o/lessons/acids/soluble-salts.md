## Introduction

Salts are some of the most useful products of the chemical industry. Ammonium nitrate (AN) and other fertilisers used on Zimbabwe's maize and tobacco crops are salts. Copper(II) sulfate, mixed with lime, makes Bordeaux mixture, a spray that protects crops from fungal diseases, and zinc sulfate is added to some fertilisers and animal feeds because crops and livestock need small amounts of zinc. In industry and in the school laboratory, salts are made from acids.

The aim of a salt preparation is always a **pure, dry** sample of the salt. In this lesson you will learn how to choose the right method for a soluble salt and how to carry it out step by step. Insoluble salts, made by precipitation, are covered in the next lesson. You should already know the reactions of acids with metals, bases and carbonates.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soluble salt"}
A salt that dissolves in water, so it stays in solution when it forms and must be obtained by crystallisation. All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts and all nitrates are soluble, as are most chlorides and sulfates.
:::

:::definition{term="Excess"}
More of a reactant than is needed to react completely with the other reactant. Adding an insoluble solid in excess makes sure that **all** the acid is used up.
:::

:::definition{term="Filtrate"}
The liquid that passes through the filter paper. The solid left on the paper is the **residue**.
:::

:::definition{term="Crystallisation"}
Forming crystals of a solid from a hot, saturated solution as it cools.
:::

:::definition{term="Saturated solution"}
A solution that contains as much dissolved solute as possible at that temperature.
:::

## Choosing a method

Ask two questions about the salt you want.

1. **Is the salt soluble?** If not, make it by precipitation (next lesson).
2. **Is it a sodium, potassium or ammonium salt?** These must be made from an **alkali** (a soluble base), so the titration method is used. Any other soluble salt is made from an **insoluble** starting material in excess.

| Salt wanted | Method | Example reagents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| soluble, not Na, K or $\ce{NH4+}$ | acid + excess insoluble solid, filter, crystallise | zinc oxide + dilute hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride |
| soluble Na, K or $\ce{NH4+}$ salt | titration of acid with alkali, then crystallise | potassium hydroxide + nitric acid → potassium nitrate |
| insoluble | precipitation | two soluble salts mixed |

When choosing the solid, remember the reactivity series. Very reactive metals such as sodium and potassium react dangerously with acids. Copper, silver and gold are below hydrogen: they do not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid at all. For a copper salt use copper(II) oxide or copper(II) carbonate instead of the metal.

## Method A: acid + excess insoluble solid

This method is used with a metal (such as magnesium, zinc or iron), an insoluble base (a metal oxide or hydroxide) or an insoluble carbonate.

![Flow chart for making a soluble salt from an acid and an excess of an insoluble solid: warm the dilute acid, add the solid until some is left over, filter off the excess, heat the filtrate until crystals form on a cold glass rod, cool so crystals form, then filter, wash with a little cold distilled water and dry the crystals.](media:diagrams/soluble-salt-preparation-flow.png "Figure: Method A, acid plus excess insoluble solid")

1. Pour some dilute acid into a beaker and warm it gently (this speeds up the reaction with an oxide).
2. Add the solid a little at a time, stirring, until **no more dissolves** and some is left over. With a metal or a carbonate, fizzing stops. The excess shows that all the acid has reacted.
3. **Filter** to remove the excess solid. The filtrate is a solution of the salt in water.
4. Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin to boil off some of the water, until the **crystallisation point** is reached: a drop on a cold glass rod forms crystals.
5. Leave the solution to cool. Crystals form as the solubility falls.
6. Filter off the crystals, **wash** them with a little cold distilled water to remove the remaining solution, and **dry** them between filter papers or in a warm place.

:::note{title="Why not evaporate to dryness?"}
Many salts, such as copper(II) sulfate, form **hydrated** crystals. Boiling all the water away would drive off the water of crystallisation and could decompose the salt; any soluble impurities would also end up in the solid. Crystallising from a saturated solution gives purer, well-shaped crystals.
:::

## Method B: titration (sodium, potassium and ammonium salts)

Alkalis dissolve in water, so an excess of alkali cannot be filtered off: it would contaminate the salt. Instead, the exact volumes that neutralise each other are found.

1. Use a pipette to put a measured volume of alkali (for example $25.0\ \text{cm}^3$) into a conical flask and add a few drops of indicator, such as methyl orange.
2. Add the acid from a burette until the indicator **just** changes colour (the end-point). Record the volume of acid used.
3. Mix the same volumes of acid and alkali again **without** indicator, so the salt is not coloured by it. (Alternatively, add activated charcoal, which removes the indicator by adsorbing it onto its surface, then filter.)
4. Evaporate and crystallise the salt solution as in Method A.

## Displacement methods (ZIMSEC)

ZIMSEC also describes salt preparations as **displacement**. When a metal reacts with an acid, the metal displaces hydrogen from the acid: zinc and dilute sulfuric acid give zinc sulfate and hydrogen. A more reactive metal can also displace a less reactive metal from a solution of its salt. For example, excess zinc powder stirred into copper(II) sulfate solution removes the blue colour; after filtering off the copper and the excess zinc, the filtrate is zinc sulfate solution, which is crystallised as usual.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: choosing methods"}
Choose a method and reagents for each salt.

1. **Zinc chloride**: soluble, not a Na, K or ammonium salt. Add excess zinc, zinc oxide or zinc carbonate to dilute hydrochloric acid, filter, then crystallise.
2. **Potassium nitrate**: a potassium salt. Titrate potassium hydroxide solution with dilute nitric acid, repeat without indicator, then crystallise.
3. **Copper(II) nitrate**: copper is below hydrogen, so it gives no hydrogen with dilute acids (with nitric acid it gives toxic oxides of nitrogen instead). Add excess copper(II) oxide or copper(II) carbonate to dilute nitric acid, filter and crystallise.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: theoretical and percentage yield of copper(II) sulfate crystals]
Excess copper(II) oxide is added to $40.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $1.00\ \text{mol/dm}^3$ sulfuric acid. The crystals are $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ ($M_r = 250$). The student obtains $7.50\ \text{g}$ of dry crystals.

1. $\ce{CuO + H2SO4 -> CuSO4 + H2O}$: one mole of acid gives one mole of salt.
2. $n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 1.00 \times 0.0400 = 0.0400$ mol, so at most $0.0400$ mol of $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ forms.
3. Maximum mass $= 0.0400 \times 250 = 10.0\ \text{g}$.
4. Percentage yield $= \dfrac{7.50}{10.0} \times 100 = 75.0\%$. Some salt stays dissolved after crystallising, and some is lost when filtering and transferring.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Stopping before the solid is in excess"}
If you stop adding the solid while it is all still dissolving, some acid is left over and ends up in the crystals. Keep adding the solid to the acid until some stays undissolved; then all the acid has reacted and the excess solid is filtered off.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using copper metal to make a copper salt"}
Copper is below hydrogen in the reactivity series and does not react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid. Use copper(II) oxide or carbonate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using excess alkali"}
An excess of a soluble alkali cannot be removed by filtering. Use the titration method for sodium, potassium and ammonium salts.
:::

:::mistake{title="Washing the crystals with lots of water"}
The crystals are soluble. Wash them with only a **little cold** distilled water.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Method questions are marked point by point. Make sure your answer names the reagents, says the solid is added **in excess**, and includes **filter**, **heat to the crystallisation point**, **cool**, **filter, wash and dry** the crystals.
:::

:::exam-tip
"How do you know the solid is in excess?" Answer with an observation: "solid remains undissolved" (and, for a metal or carbonate, "fizzing stops").
:::

:::exam-tip
For a titration preparation, mention repeating **without the indicator** using the same volumes; this point is often missed.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Soluble salts are made from an acid; insoluble salts are made by precipitation.
- Sodium, potassium and ammonium salts: titrate the acid with the alkali, repeat without indicator, then crystallise.
- Other soluble salts: add an excess of a metal, insoluble base or insoluble carbonate to the acid, then filter.
- Heat the filtrate to the crystallisation point, cool, filter, wash with a little cold water and dry.
- Avoid metals that are too reactive (sodium, potassium) or unreactive (copper, silver).
:::
