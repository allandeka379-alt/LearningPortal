## Introduction

Dilute hydrochloric acid from the laboratory and the ethanoic acid in the vinegar used to pickle vegetables are both acids. Yet even when the two acids have exactly the same concentration, a strip of magnesium fizzes vigorously in the hydrochloric acid and only slowly in the ethanoic acid. The difference is the **strength** of the acid: how completely its molecules split up into ions in water.

This lesson is **Cambridge IGCSE Supplement** content (topic 7.1). You will meet a deeper definition of acids and bases in terms of protons, learn the difference between strong and weak acids, and see how experiments tell them apart. You should already know that acids produce $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions and that rates of reaction depend on concentration.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Proton"}
In acid–base chemistry, a hydrogen ion, $\ce{H+}$. A hydrogen atom that has lost its only electron is just a proton.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid (proton donor)"}
A substance that donates protons ($\ce{H+}$ ions) to another substance.
:::

:::definition{term="Base (proton acceptor)"}
A substance that accepts protons ($\ce{H+}$ ions) from an acid.
:::

:::definition{term="Strong acid"}
An acid that is **completely** dissociated (split into ions) in aqueous solution. Hydrochloric, nitric and sulfuric acids are strong acids.
:::

:::definition{term="Weak acid"}
An acid that is only **partially** dissociated in aqueous solution: most of its molecules stay whole. Ethanoic acid, citric acid and carbonic acid are weak acids.
:::

:::definition{term="Concentration"}
The amount of acid dissolved in a given volume of solution, for example in mol/dm³. A **concentrated** acid has a lot of acid per dm³; a **dilute** acid has little.
:::

## Acids and bases as proton donors and acceptors

When ammonia gas meets hydrogen chloride gas, white smoke of ammonium chloride forms:

$$
\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}
$$

The hydrogen chloride molecule gives a proton to the ammonia molecule, which becomes an ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$. So hydrogen chloride is the **acid** (proton donor) and ammonia is the **base** (proton acceptor). No water is involved, which is why the proton definition is more general than "an acid produces $\ce{H+}$ ions in water".

Every neutralisation is a proton transfer:

- a hydroxide ion accepts a proton: $\ce{OH- + H+ -> H2O}$;
- an oxide ion in a metal oxide accepts two protons: $\ce{O^2- + 2H+ -> H2O}$;
- a carbonate ion accepts protons and the product breaks down: $\ce{CO3^2- + 2H+ -> H2O + CO2}$.

## Strong and weak acids

When hydrogen chloride dissolves in water, **every** molecule dissociates:

$$
\ce{HCl(aq) -> H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}
$$

The single arrow shows that the reaction goes to completion. Hydrochloric acid is a **strong** acid.

Ethanoic acid, the acid in vinegar, behaves differently. Only a small fraction of its molecules (roughly 1 in 100 in a 0.1 mol/dm³ solution) split into ions at any moment, and the ions keep re-forming molecules:

$$
\ce{CH3COOH(aq) <=> H+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}
$$

The reversible arrow shows a **partial** dissociation. Ethanoic acid is a **weak** acid. Only the hydrogen of the $\ce{-COOH}$ group can be released; the three hydrogens of $\ce{CH3}$ stay attached.

:::keyfact{title="Strength and concentration are different ideas"}
**Strength** is about how much of the acid dissociates. **Concentration** is about how much acid is dissolved per dm³. You can have a dilute strong acid or a concentrated weak acid.
:::

## Comparing a strong and a weak acid by experiment

A fair comparison uses the **same concentration**, volume and temperature of each acid.

| Test (both acids 0.10 mol/dm³) | Hydrochloric acid | Ethanoic acid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH | about 1 | about 3 |
| Magnesium ribbon | vigorous fizzing | slow fizzing |
| Calcium carbonate | rapid fizzing | slow fizzing |

The weak acid has a much lower concentration of $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions, so its pH is higher and hydrogen ions collide with the metal or carbonate less often, giving a slower reaction.

The **total** amount of gas, however, is the same. As $\ce{H+}$ ions are used up, more ethanoic acid molecules dissociate, until all the acid has reacted. Both solutions contain the same number of moles of acid, so with excess magnesium both give the same final volume of hydrogen, and both need the same volume of alkali for neutralisation.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: spotting the acid and the base]
Identify the proton donor and the proton acceptor: $\ce{HNO3 + NH3 -> NH4NO3}$.

1. Nitric acid loses a proton and becomes a nitrate ion, $\ce{NO3-}$.
2. Ammonia gains that proton and becomes an ammonium ion, $\ce{NH4+}$.
3. So $\ce{HNO3}$ is the acid (proton donor) and $\ce{NH3}$ is the base (proton acceptor).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: equal amounts, different rates]
$25.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $0.100\ \text{mol/dm}^3$ hydrochloric acid and $25.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $0.100\ \text{mol/dm}^3$ ethanoic acid each react with excess magnesium. Compare the final volumes of hydrogen at r.t.p.

1. Moles of each acid $= 0.100 \times 0.0250 = 0.00250$ mol.
2. Both acids give one $\ce{H+}$ per molecule, and $\ce{Mg + 2H+ -> Mg^2+ + H2}$, so moles of $\ce{H2} = 0.00250 \div 2 = 0.00125$ mol for each acid.
3. Volume $= 0.00125 \times 24\,000 = 30.0\ \text{cm}^3$ for **both** acids.
4. The hydrochloric acid produces its $30.0\ \text{cm}^3$ much faster.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: a concentrated weak acid against a dilute strong acid"}
Compare 2.0 mol/dm³ ethanoic acid with 0.0010 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid.

- The ethanoic acid is far more **concentrated**: 2000 times as much acid per dm³.
- The hydrochloric acid is the **stronger** acid: it is completely dissociated.
- Their pH values are about 2.2 and 3.0. Because so much ethanoic acid is present, even a small fraction dissociating gives it the higher hydrogen ion concentration and the lower pH.

pH can only be used to compare the **strengths** of two acids when their concentrations are the same.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using 'strong' to mean 'concentrated'"}
A strong acid is fully dissociated; a concentrated acid has a lot dissolved. Describe 0.001 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid as "dilute" and "strong", not "weak".
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking a weak acid makes less gas"}
With the same moles of acid, a weak acid gives the **same** total volume of hydrogen or carbon dioxide; it just takes longer.
:::

:::mistake{title="A single arrow for a weak acid"}
Write $\ce{<=>}$ for ethanoic acid. A single arrow suggests complete dissociation, which is wrong.
:::

:::mistake{title="Releasing all four hydrogens of ethanoic acid"}
Only the hydrogen of the $\ce{-COOH}$ group is released: $\ce{CH3COOH <=> H+ + CH3COO-}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Definitions earn marks only with the key words: "**completely** dissociated **in aqueous solution**" (strong) and "**partially** dissociated" (weak). "Ionised" is accepted in place of "dissociated".
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain a rate difference, link it to the **concentration of hydrogen ions**: fewer $\ce{H+}$ ions in the weak acid, so fewer collisions per second with the magnesium.
:::

:::exam-tip
In an experiment to compare acid strength, state the control variables: same concentration and volume of acid, same temperature, same mass (or length) of magnesium or the same size of carbonate pieces.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Acids are proton ($\ce{H+}$) donors; bases are proton acceptors.
- Strong acids are completely dissociated in water: $\ce{HCl(aq) -> H+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$.
- Weak acids are partially dissociated: $\ce{CH3COOH(aq) <=> H+(aq) + CH3COO-(aq)}$.
- At the same concentration a weak acid has a higher pH and reacts more slowly, but gives the same total amount of product.
- Strength (how much dissociates) is not the same as concentration (how much is dissolved).
:::
