## Introduction

Some salts refuse to dissolve. The white crust inside a kettle in Bulawayo is mostly calcium carbonate. The "barium meal" a patient swallows before an X-ray of the stomach is barium sulfate: barium ions are poisonous, but barium sulfate is so insoluble that almost none dissolves, so it passes through the body safely while showing up clearly on the X-ray. Many tests for ions also depend on a precipitate forming.

Insoluble salts cannot be made by crystallising a solution, because they never stay in solution. Instead they are made by **precipitation**. In this lesson you will learn the general solubility rules, use them to predict precipitates, and describe how to prepare a pure, dry insoluble salt. The solubility rules are Cambridge Core; the precipitation method is Cambridge Supplement and ZIMSEC Form 3; ionic equations are Supplement.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Soluble / insoluble"}
A soluble substance dissolves in water; an insoluble substance does not dissolve (or dissolves only very slightly).
:::

:::definition{term="Precipitate"}
An insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed.
:::

:::definition{term="Precipitation reaction"}
A reaction in which ions from two solutions combine to form an insoluble solid.
:::

:::definition{term="Spectator ions (Supplement)"}
Ions that are present in the solution but do not take part in the reaction. They are left out of the ionic equation.
:::

## The general solubility rules

:::keyfact{title="Solubility of common salts and hydroxides"}
| Compound | Soluble | Insoluble |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sodium, potassium and ammonium compounds | all | none |
| nitrates | all | none |
| chlorides | most | silver chloride, lead(II) chloride |
| sulfates | most | barium sulfate, calcium sulfate, lead(II) sulfate |
| carbonates | sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates | all others |
| hydroxides | sodium, potassium and ammonium hydroxides (calcium hydroxide is partially soluble) | all others |
:::

Calcium hydroxide dissolves only a little: its solution is limewater. Most other metal hydroxides, such as copper(II) hydroxide and iron(III) hydroxide, are insoluble and form coloured precipitates, which is the basis of the tests for cations.

## Predicting a precipitate

When two salt solutions are mixed, imagine the ions swapping partners. If either new combination is insoluble, it forms a precipitate.

$$
\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}
$$

Silver chloride is insoluble, so a white precipitate forms. Sodium nitrate is soluble and stays in solution. If both new combinations are soluble, for example when potassium nitrate solution is mixed with sodium chloride solution, nothing happens: the ions simply stay in solution together.

## Preparing an insoluble salt by precipitation

1. Choose two **soluble** salts: one containing the metal ion you need and one containing the negative ion you need. A **nitrate** of the metal and a **sodium** or **potassium** salt of the negative ion work well, because these are always soluble.
2. Dissolve each in distilled water and mix the solutions. Stir. The insoluble salt forms as a precipitate.
3. **Filter** the mixture. The precipitate stays on the filter paper as the residue.
4. **Wash** the residue on the filter paper with distilled water to remove the soluble salt left with it.
5. **Dry** the solid in a warm oven or between filter papers.

:::note{title="Why not use an acid and an insoluble base?"}
If you tried to make barium sulfate from barium carbonate and sulfuric acid, a layer of insoluble barium sulfate would form on the carbonate and stop the reaction. The product would be mixed with unreacted carbonate, and two solids cannot be separated by filtering.
:::

## Ionic equations for precipitation (Supplement)

Only the ions that form the solid are written in the ionic equation, with state symbols. For silver chloride, the sodium and nitrate ions are spectator ions:

$$
\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}
$$

The charges must balance on both sides. For a 2+ ion and a 1− ion, two of the negative ions are needed.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: will a precipitate form?"}
1. **Silver nitrate + sodium chloride**: the new pairs are silver chloride (insoluble) and sodium nitrate (soluble), so a white precipitate of silver chloride forms.
2. **Potassium nitrate + sodium chloride**: the new pairs are potassium chloride and sodium nitrate, both soluble, so there is no precipitate.
3. **Barium chloride + magnesium sulfate**: barium sulfate is insoluble, so a white precipitate forms; magnesium chloride stays in solution.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: planning lead(II) iodide]
Plan the preparation of lead(II) iodide and write the ionic equation.

1. Lead(II) iodide is insoluble, so use precipitation.
2. A soluble lead salt: lead(II) nitrate. A soluble iodide: potassium iodide.
3. Mix the solutions: a bright yellow precipitate forms. Filter, wash with distilled water and dry.
4. Ionic equation: one $\ce{Pb^2+}$ needs two $\ce{I-}$: $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: mass of precipitate]
$25.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of $0.200\ \text{mol/dm}^3$ silver nitrate is mixed with excess sodium chloride solution. Calculate the mass of silver chloride formed. ($M_r$ of $\ce{AgCl} = 143.5$)

1. $n(\ce{AgNO3}) = 0.200 \times 0.0250 = 0.00500$ mol.
2. $\ce{Ag+ + Cl- -> AgCl}$, so $n(\ce{AgCl}) = 0.00500$ mol.
3. Mass $= 0.00500 \times 143.5 = 0.7175 \approx 0.718\ \text{g}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that all nitrates and all sodium salts are soluble"}
These ions never form precipitates, which is why they are used to supply the other ion.
:::

:::mistake{title="Washing with tap water"}
Tap water contains dissolved ions. Wash precipitates with **distilled** water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing spectator ions in an ionic equation"}
$\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ appear on both sides and take no part, so they are left out.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving the precipitate the state symbol (aq)"}
A precipitate is a solid: $\ce{BaSO4(s)}$, not $\ce{BaSO4(aq)}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a method answer, name **both** solutions, say "mix", then "filter", "wash the residue with distilled water" and "dry". Each step is usually one mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the exceptions as short lists: insoluble chlorides **Ag, Pb**; insoluble sulfates **Ba, Ca, Pb**; soluble carbonates and hydroxides only **Na, K, NH₄** (calcium hydroxide partly).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- All sodium, potassium and ammonium salts and all nitrates are soluble.
- Insoluble: silver and lead(II) chlorides; barium, calcium and lead(II) sulfates; most carbonates and hydroxides.
- An insoluble salt is made by mixing two soluble salts, then filtering, washing with distilled water and drying.
- Supplement: ionic equations show only the ions that form the precipitate, e.g. $\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}$.
:::
