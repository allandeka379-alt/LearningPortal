## Introduction

Before the planting season, many farmers in Mashonaland take soil samples to be tested. One of the first results on the report is the **pH** of the soil. Most crops grow best in soil that is only slightly acidic, so a very acidic soil is treated with lime before the maize goes in. The same measurement is used in swimming pools, at water works and in hospitals.

You cannot judge how acidic a liquid is by looking at it, and you must never taste laboratory chemicals. Instead chemists use **indicators**: dyes whose colour depends on whether they are in an acid or an alkali. In this lesson you will learn the colours of the indicators named in the syllabus, how the pH scale works and what pH tells you about the ions in a solution. You should already know that acids produce $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions and alkalis produce $\ce{OH-(aq)}$ ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Indicator"}
A substance that has different colours in acidic and alkaline solutions, so it shows whether a solution is acidic, neutral or alkaline.
:::

:::definition{term="pH scale"}
A scale, usually numbered from 0 to 14, that shows how acidic or alkaline a solution is. pH 7 is neutral, below 7 is acidic and above 7 is alkaline.
:::

:::definition{term="Universal indicator"}
A mixture of indicators that changes through a whole range of colours as the pH changes, so it shows **how** acidic or alkaline a solution is, not just whether it is an acid or an alkali.
:::

:::definition{term="Neutral"}
Neither acidic nor alkaline: pH 7. Pure water and a solution of sodium chloride are neutral.
:::

## Indicators with two colours

Three indicators named in the syllabus each have one colour in acids and another in alkalis.

| Indicator | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali |
| --- | --- | --- |
| litmus | red | blue |
| methyl orange | red | yellow |
| thymolphthalein | colourless | blue |

Litmus is used as paper strips or as a solution. **Blue** litmus paper turns red in an acid; **red** litmus paper turns blue in an alkali. In a neutral solution litmus is purple, so neither paper changes.

:::keyfact{title="Learn the pairs exactly"}
Methyl orange: **red** in acid, **yellow** in alkali. Thymolphthalein: **colourless** in acid, **blue** in alkali. Writing "orange" for methyl orange in an alkali, or "pink" for thymolphthalein, will not score.
:::

:::note{title="Why two-colour indicators cannot measure pH"}
Each of these indicators changes colour quite suddenly at one particular pH. Methyl orange is already yellow in a solution that is only very slightly acidic, and thymolphthalein stays colourless in a weakly alkaline solution. They are ideal for showing the end-point of a titration, but they only sort solutions into two groups.
:::

:::note{title="Phenolphthalein"}
Many school laboratories also use **phenolphthalein**. Like thymolphthalein it is colourless in acid and changes colour only in alkaline solutions, but its alkali colour is **pink**, not blue. Do not mix up the two names.
:::

## Universal indicator and the pH scale

Universal indicator gives a different colour for each part of the pH scale. The exact colours on the chart vary a little from one manufacturer to another, but the pattern is always the same:

| pH | 0–2 | 3–4 | 5–6 | 7 | 8–10 | 11–14 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Colour | red | orange | yellow | green | blue | purple (violet) |
| Description | strongly acidic | acidic | weakly acidic | neutral | weakly alkaline | strongly alkaline |

To use universal indicator paper, put a drop of the solution on the paper with a clean glass rod and compare the colour with the chart straight away. A **pH meter** (an electronic probe) gives a more precise reading, such as 5.4, instead of a colour.

Some everyday values, which are only approximate: stomach acid about 1–2, lemon juice about 2, vinegar about 3, sour milk about 4–5, pure water 7, a solution of baking soda about 8, soapy water about 9–10, and drain or oven cleaners containing sodium hydroxide about 13–14.

### pH and hydrogen ions

pH is a measure of the concentration of hydrogen ions:

- **the lower the pH, the higher the concentration of $\ce{H+(aq)}$ ions**, and the more acidic the solution;
- at pH 7 the solution is neutral: hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions are balanced;
- above pH 7 hydroxide ions are in excess; the higher the pH, the more alkaline the solution.

So a solution of pH 2 contains more hydrogen ions than a solution of pH 5 of the same volume.

### pH changes during neutralisation

If an alkali is added a little at a time to an acid containing universal indicator, the pH rises. The colour moves from red through orange and yellow to green at the point of neutralisation (pH 7). Adding more alkali makes the solution blue and then purple. The same idea explains why lime raises the pH of an acidic soil.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: ranking four solutions"}
Rudo tests four solutions with universal indicator. P turns red, Q green, R purple and S yellow.

1. Match the colours to pH: P about 1, S about 5–6, Q 7, R about 13.
2. In order of increasing pH: P, S, Q, R.
3. P is the most strongly acidic, so P has the highest concentration of hydrogen ions.
4. R is strongly alkaline; Q is neutral and could be pure water or a salt solution such as sodium chloride.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: an end-point with thymolphthalein"}
A few drops of thymolphthalein are added to sodium hydroxide solution in a conical flask, and dilute hydrochloric acid is run in from a burette.

1. At the start the solution is alkaline, so thymolphthalein is **blue**.
2. As acid is added, the hydroxide ions are used up: $\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}$.
3. When all the alkali has been neutralised, the next drop of acid turns the indicator **colourless**. This is the end-point.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: indicators from plants"}
Many red, purple or blue flowers and leaves, such as red cabbage, contain coloured substances that change colour with pH. To make an indicator, crush the plant material with a little water or ethanol, warm it, then filter. Test the extract with a known acid, water and a known alkali and record its colours. The extract can then be used to test other solutions, although it cannot give an exact pH.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking a high pH means a strong acid"}
A **low** pH (0–2) means strongly acidic. A high pH (11–14) means strongly alkaline.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reversing litmus"}
Litmus is **red in acid** and **blue in alkali**. Red litmus paper can only show an alkali; blue litmus paper can only show an acid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying neutral water has no ions"}
At pH 7 hydrogen and hydroxide ions are present in equal, very small amounts. Neutral means they are balanced, not absent.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using universal indicator to find an end-point"}
Universal indicator changes gradually through many colours, so it does not give a sharp end-point in a titration. Use methyl orange or thymolphthalein instead.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked for a colour **change**, give both colours in order: "blue to colourless", not just "colourless".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Suggest a pH" questions accept a sensible value or range that matches the description: strongly acidic, like dilute hydrochloric acid, 0–2; acidic, like vinegar or fruit juice, 3–6; neutral 7; weakly alkaline 8–10; strongly alkaline, like sodium hydroxide solution, 11–14. Always give a number, not just "acidic".
:::

:::exam-tip
If a question asks which solution has the most hydrogen ions, choose the **lowest** pH. If it asks which has the most hydroxide ions, choose the highest pH.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Litmus: red in acid, blue in alkali. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in alkali. Thymolphthalein: colourless in acid, blue in alkali.
- The pH scale runs from 0 to 14: below 7 acidic, 7 neutral, above 7 alkaline.
- Universal indicator shows how acidic or alkaline a solution is: red (strong acid) through green (neutral) to purple (strong alkali).
- The lower the pH, the higher the concentration of hydrogen ions.
- Plant extracts can act as indicators; a pH meter gives the most precise value.
:::
