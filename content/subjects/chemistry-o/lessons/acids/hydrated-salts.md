## Introduction

The bright blue crystals of copper(II) sulfate sold to farmers in Zimbabwe for cattle footbaths and fungicide sprays look completely dry. Yet if you heat them, steam pours out and they crumble into a white powder. The water was not "wet" water: it was locked into the crystal structure. Crystals like these are called **hydrated** salts, and the water inside them is **water of crystallisation**.

This lesson is part of the **Cambridge IGCSE** syllabus (7.3). The definitions of hydrated and anhydrous are Core; water of crystallisation and the calculations are **Supplement**. The colour changes are also used as tests for water, and the reversible reaction is studied again in the lesson on reversible reactions. You should be able to calculate $M_r$ and amounts in moles.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hydrated substance"}
A substance that is chemically combined with water. Example: blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Anhydrous substance"}
A substance that contains no water. Example: white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Water of crystallisation (Supplement)"}
The water molecules present in hydrated crystals, written after a dot in the formula: $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ has five molecules of water of crystallisation for every $\ce{CuSO4}$.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Hydrated is not the same as aqueous"}
An **aqueous** solution is a substance **dissolved** in water. A **hydrated** solid is a dry crystal with water **built into** its structure.
:::

## Two important colour changes

| Substance | Hydrated form | Anhydrous form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| copper(II) sulfate | blue crystals, $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$ | white powder, $\ce{CuSO4}$ |
| cobalt(II) chloride | pink, $\ce{CoCl2.6H2O}$ | blue, $\ce{CoCl2}$ |

**Heating** drives off the water of crystallisation:

$$
\ce{CuSO4.5H2O(s) -> CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(g)}
$$

Blue crystals turn into a white powder, and droplets of a colourless liquid (water) condense in the cooler part of the test tube.

**Adding water** back reverses the change: white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns blue, and the mixture gets hot (the change is exothermic). Blue anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride turns pink.

:::example{title="Testing for water"}
Anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (white → blue) and cobalt(II) chloride paper (blue → pink) show that a liquid **contains** water. To show that it is **pure** water, check that it freezes at 0 °C, or that it boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure. (At the altitude of Harare the air pressure is lower and pure water boils at about 95 °C.)
:::

## Calculations with water of crystallisation (Supplement)

To find the relative formula mass of a hydrated salt, add the mass of the water molecules.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: Mr and percentage of water in hydrated copper(II) sulfate]
Data: H = 1, O = 16, S = 32, Cu = 64.

1. $M_r(\ce{CuSO4}) = 64 + 32 + 64 = 160$.
2. Five water molecules: $5 \times 18 = 90$.
3. $M_r(\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}) = 160 + 90 = 250$.
4. Percentage of water $= \dfrac{90}{250} \times 100 = 36.0\%$.
:::

### Finding x in a formula such as CuSO₄·xH₂O

Heat a weighed sample of the hydrated salt in a crucible, then cool and weigh it. Repeat the heating, cooling and weighing until two masses agree: this is **heating to constant mass**, and it shows that all the water has gone. Then:

1. mass of water $=$ mass of hydrated salt $-$ mass of anhydrous salt;
2. amount of anhydrous salt $=$ its mass $\div$ its $M_r$;
3. amount of water $=$ mass of water $\div 18$;
4. $x =$ amount of water $\div$ amount of anhydrous salt.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: copper(II) sulfate]
5.00 g of hydrated copper(II) sulfate, $\ce{CuSO4.xH2O}$, is heated to constant mass. 3.20 g of anhydrous $\ce{CuSO4}$ remains.

1. Mass of water $= 5.00 - 3.20 = 1.80$ g.
2. $n(\ce{CuSO4}) = 3.20 \div 160 = 0.0200$ mol.
3. $n(\ce{H2O}) = 1.80 \div 18 = 0.100$ mol.
4. $x = 0.100 \div 0.0200 = 5$, so the formula is $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: Epsom salt]
2.46 g of hydrated magnesium sulfate, $\ce{MgSO4.xH2O}$, leaves 1.20 g of anhydrous $\ce{MgSO4}$ after heating to constant mass. (Mg = 24)

1. Mass of water $= 2.46 - 1.20 = 1.26$ g; $n(\ce{H2O}) = 1.26 \div 18 = 0.0700$ mol.
2. $M_r(\ce{MgSO4}) = 24 + 32 + 64 = 120$; $n = 1.20 \div 120 = 0.0100$ mol.
3. $x = 0.0700 \div 0.0100 = 7$: $\ce{MgSO4.7H2O}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling a hydrated salt 'aqueous'"}
Hydrated copper(II) sulfate crystals are a dry solid, $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O(s)}$. "Aqueous copper(II) sulfate" is a solution, $\ce{CuSO4(aq)}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the hydrated mass to find the moles of salt"}
The amount of $\ce{CuSO4}$ must be calculated from the mass of the **anhydrous** salt left after heating, divided by $M_r(\ce{CuSO4})$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heating only once"}
If some water remains, the mass of water is too small and $x$ comes out too low. Heat to constant mass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the colours"}
Copper(II) sulfate: blue when hydrated, white when anhydrous. Cobalt(II) chloride: pink when hydrated, blue when anhydrous. The test result for water is "white to blue" or "blue to pink".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a test for water, state both colours: "anhydrous copper(II) sulfate turns from white to blue". Writing only "turns blue" may not earn the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
Set out x-calculations in the four steps above. Round $x$ to the nearest whole number only at the end, and only if the value is very close to it.
:::

:::exam-tip
In evaluation questions, decide which way an error moves $x$: water left in the solid makes $x$ too low; extra mass lost because the salt itself decomposed makes $x$ too high.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hydrated: chemically combined with water; anhydrous: contains no water (Core).
- Copper(II) sulfate is blue when hydrated and white when anhydrous; cobalt(II) chloride is pink when hydrated and blue when anhydrous.
- Heating removes water of crystallisation; adding water reverses the change and releases heat.
- Supplement: $M_r$ of a hydrate includes $x \times 18$ for the water molecules.
- Supplement: $x =$ moles of water lost ÷ moles of anhydrous salt, after heating to constant mass.
:::
