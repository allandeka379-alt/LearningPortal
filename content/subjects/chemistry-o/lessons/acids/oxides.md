## Introduction

An oxide is a compound of an element with oxygen, and oxides surround us. The rust on a car, the quicklime spread on fields, the zinc oxide in sunscreen and nappy-rash cream, and the sulfur dioxide that leaves the chimneys of coal-burning power stations are all oxides. Some of them behave like bases, some like acids, and a few like both.

Knowing which kind of oxide you have tells you what it will react with. This matters in industry: in the blast furnace a basic oxide is used to remove an acidic one, and in power stations a basic oxide can be used to stop acidic gases escaping into the air. You should already know the typical reactions of acids and bases and the difference between metals and non-metals.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oxide"}
A compound of an element with oxygen only, for example $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{CO2}$ or $\ce{Al2O3}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Basic oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water. Most **metal** oxides are basic, for example $\ce{CaO}$, $\ce{CuO}$, $\ce{MgO}$ and $\ce{Na2O}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Acidic oxide"}
An oxide that reacts with a base (or alkali) to form a salt and water. Most **non-metal** oxides are acidic, for example $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{SO2}$, $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{NO2}$ and $\ce{SiO2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Amphoteric oxide (Supplement; ZIMSEC)"}
An oxide that reacts with **both** acids and bases to form a salt and water. Aluminium oxide, $\ce{Al2O3}$, and zinc oxide, $\ce{ZnO}$, are amphoteric.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Metal or non-metal decides the type"}
The more metallic the element, the more basic its oxide; the more non-metallic the element, the more acidic its oxide. Across Period 3 the oxides change from basic ($\ce{Na2O}$, $\ce{MgO}$) through amphoteric ($\ce{Al2O3}$) to acidic ($\ce{SiO2}$, then the oxides of phosphorus and sulfur).
:::

## Basic oxides

Basic oxides neutralise acids, forming a salt and water:

$$
\ce{CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CuCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

Most basic oxides are insoluble in water. The oxides of Group I metals and calcium oxide do react with water, forming **alkalis**:

$$
\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{CaO(s) + H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq)}
$$

Basic oxides do **not** react with alkalis: copper(II) oxide stays undissolved in sodium hydroxide solution.

## Acidic oxides

Acidic oxides neutralise alkalis and other bases, forming a salt and water:

$$
\ce{CO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2CO3(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

You have already used this kind of reaction: limewater is calcium hydroxide solution, and it turns milky because carbon dioxide reacts with it to form insoluble calcium carbonate.

Many acidic oxides dissolve in water to give acidic solutions:

$$
\ce{SO3(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO4(aq)}
$$

$$
\ce{SO2(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO3(aq)}
$$

Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide from burning fuels dissolve in rain in this way, forming **acid rain**. Silicon dioxide (sand) does not dissolve in water, but it is still acidic: it reacts with basic calcium oxide in the blast furnace to form calcium silicate, $\ce{CaO + SiO2 -> CaSiO3}$.

Acidic oxides do **not** react with dilute acids.

## Amphoteric oxides (Supplement; ZIMSEC)

Zinc oxide and aluminium oxide are insoluble in water, but they react with dilute acids **and** with alkalis such as warm sodium hydroxide solution. In both cases a salt and water form.

$$
\ce{ZnO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

$$
\ce{ZnO(s) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2ZnO2(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

The salt formed with sodium hydroxide is sodium zincate. Aluminium oxide behaves in the same way, giving aluminium chloride with hydrochloric acid and sodium aluminate with sodium hydroxide.

## Classifying an unknown oxide by experiment

| Result | Basic | Acidic | Amphoteric |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reacts with (dissolves in) dilute acid | yes | no | yes |
| Reacts with (dissolves in) sodium hydroxide solution | no | yes | yes |
| If it dissolves in water, universal indicator shows | blue or purple | red, orange or yellow | (insoluble) |

Warm the mixtures gently, because many oxides react only slowly in the cold.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: classifying five oxides"}
Classify $\ce{K2O}$, $\ce{SO3}$, $\ce{CuO}$, $\ce{ZnO}$ and $\ce{CO2}$.

1. $\ce{K2O}$: potassium is a metal, so its oxide is basic (and dissolves to give the alkali $\ce{KOH}$).
2. $\ce{SO3}$: sulfur is a non-metal, so its oxide is acidic (it forms sulfuric acid with water).
3. $\ce{CuO}$: copper is a metal, so it is basic.
4. $\ce{ZnO}$: amphoteric; it reacts with acids and with alkalis.
5. $\ce{CO2}$: carbon is a non-metal, so it is acidic.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: interpreting results"}
Three solid oxides, A, B and C, are tested. The acid and alkali are warmed gently with each solid.

| Oxide | Water and universal indicator | Dilute nitric acid | Sodium hydroxide solution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (white) | dissolves; purple | dissolves | no change |
| B (white) | does not dissolve | dissolves | dissolves |
| C (black) | does not dissolve | dissolves to give a blue solution | no change |

1. A reacts with the acid only and dissolves in water to give an alkaline solution: a soluble **basic** oxide, such as calcium oxide.
2. B reacts with both the acid and the alkali: **amphoteric**, such as zinc oxide.
3. C reacts with the acid only: an insoluble **basic** oxide. The blue solution suggests copper(II) oxide.
4. An oxide that dissolved in water to give a red or orange colour with universal indicator, such as sulfur dioxide, would be **acidic**.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: removing sulfur dioxide with lime]
Waste gases from burning coal can be passed through slaked lime, calcium hydroxide. Write the equation and explain the type of reaction.

1. Sulfur dioxide is acidic and calcium hydroxide is a base, so they neutralise each other to give a salt and water.
2. The salt of sulfur dioxide is a sulfite, $\ce{SO3^2-}$, so calcium sulfite forms.
3. $\ce{SO2 + Ca(OH)2 -> CaSO3 + H2O}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking every oxide that does not dissolve is basic"}
Insoluble oxides can be basic ($\ce{CuO}$), acidic ($\ce{SiO2}$) or amphoteric ($\ce{ZnO}$). Test them with an acid **and** an alkali.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing that an acidic oxide reacts with an acid"}
Acidic oxides react with **bases**. Basic oxides react with **acids**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying amphoteric oxides react with water"}
Aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are insoluble in water. Amphoteric means they react with both acids and bases.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
To justify a classification, give the evidence: "basic, because it reacts with hydrochloric acid to form a salt and water but does not react with sodium hydroxide". A single word earns less.
:::

:::exam-tip
Link the type of oxide to the element: "metal, so basic oxide"; "non-metal, so acidic oxide". Learn aluminium and zinc as the amphoteric pair.
:::

:::exam-tip
When writing equations, the product of an acidic oxide with an alkali is a salt of the matching acid: $\ce{CO2}$ gives carbonates, $\ce{SO2}$ gives sulfites, $\ce{SO3}$ gives sulfates.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Metal oxides are generally basic: they react with acids to form a salt and water.
- Non-metal oxides are generally acidic: they react with bases or alkalis to form a salt and water.
- Soluble basic oxides form alkalis with water; many acidic oxides form acids with water (acid rain).
- Supplement and ZIMSEC: $\ce{Al2O3}$ and $\ce{ZnO}$ are amphoteric, reacting with both acids and bases.
- Classify an unknown oxide by testing it with water and indicator, a dilute acid and sodium hydroxide solution.
:::
