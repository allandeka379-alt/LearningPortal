## Introduction

Very few metal objects are made of a pure metal. Pure gold is so soft that a 24-carat ring scratches and bends easily, pure iron is too soft for building, and pure aluminium is too weak for an aircraft. Mixing a metal with small amounts of other elements produces an **alloy** with much more useful properties.

Zimbabwe plays a part in this: chromite from the Great Dyke is smelted into **ferrochrome**, an alloy of iron and chromium that steelmakers use to make stainless steel. In this lesson you will learn what alloys are, the composition and uses of the important ones, and why they are harder and stronger than pure metals. You should already know the structure of metals and the meaning of a mixture.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alloy"}
A mixture of a metal with other elements, usually other metals or carbon. Because it is a mixture, its composition can vary.
:::

:::keyfact{title="An alloy is a mixture, not a compound"}
The elements in an alloy are not chemically combined in a fixed ratio. Brass, for example, can contain different proportions of copper and zinc, giving brasses with slightly different properties.
:::

## Important alloys

| Alloy | Main elements | Useful properties | Typical uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| brass | copper and zinc | harder than copper, resists corrosion, attractive gold colour | musical instruments, door handles, electrical plug pins, ornaments |
| bronze (ZIMSEC) | copper and tin | hard, strong, resists corrosion | statues, medals, bells, ship propellers |
| steel | iron with a small amount of carbon | strong and hard, cheap | buildings, bridges, car bodies, tools |
| stainless steel | iron with chromium, nickel and some carbon | hard and resists rusting | cutlery, kitchen sinks, surgical instruments, chemical plant |
| duralumin (ZIMSEC) | aluminium with some copper (and small amounts of magnesium and manganese) | low density but much stronger than aluminium | aircraft bodies, bicycle frames |

Stainless-steel cutlery is often stamped "18/8", meaning about 18% chromium and 8% nickel by mass.

:::note{title="Steels for different jobs"}
Changing the amount of carbon changes the properties of steel. Low-carbon (mild) steel is softer and easily shaped, so it is used for car bodies, wire and roofing sheets. High-carbon steel is harder but more brittle, so it is used for tools such as chisels, hammers and knife blades.
:::

Alloys can be **harder and stronger** than the pure metals they are made from, which makes them more useful. Some, like stainless steel and bronze, also resist corrosion better.

## Why alloys are harder: structure (Supplement)

In a **pure metal**, all the atoms (positive ions) are the same size and are packed in regular layers. When a force is applied, the layers slide over each other easily, so the metal is soft and easily bent.

In an **alloy**, atoms of a different size are mixed in among the metal atoms. These different-sized atoms disrupt the regular layers, so the layers can no longer slide over each other easily. More force is needed to change the shape of the metal, so the alloy is **harder and stronger**.

:::example{title="Recognising an alloy in a particle diagram"}
In a diagram of particles drawn as circles:

- a **pure metal** shows identical circles in neat rows;
- an **alloy** shows mostly identical circles in rows, with some circles of a **different size** scattered among them, distorting the rows;
- two separate blocks of different circles side by side is **not** an alloy, because the elements are not mixed;
- pairs of joined circles spread far apart represent a gas made of molecules, not a metal.
:::

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: explaining hardness (Supplement)]
Explain why bronze is harder than pure copper.

1. Pure copper has layers of identical copper atoms that slide easily over each other.
2. In bronze, tin atoms, which are a different size, are mixed in among the copper atoms.
3. The tin atoms disrupt the layers, so the layers cannot slide as easily.
4. More force is needed to deform the metal, so bronze is harder.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: composition of a brass ornament]
A brass ornament has a mass of 250 g and contains 65% copper by mass, the rest being zinc. Calculate the mass of zinc.

1. Percentage of zinc $= 100 - 65 = 35\%$.
2. Mass of zinc $= \dfrac{35}{100} \times 250 = 87.5\ \text{g}$.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: choosing an alloy"}
A hospital needs scalpels that stay sharp and can be cleaned in hot water many times. Stainless steel is chosen: it is hard (keeps a sharp edge) and does not rust in water.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling an alloy a compound"}
An alloy is a **mixture**. Its composition can vary, and the metals keep many of their own properties.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying alloys are harder because they contain 'stronger bonds'"}
The key idea is that **different-sized atoms** stop the layers sliding, not that new bonds form.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up brass and bronze"}
Brass = copper + **zinc**; bronze = copper + **tin**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When explaining hardness, use all three steps: different-sized atoms; layers distorted; layers cannot slide easily. One step alone rarely earns all the marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
For uses, link a property to the use: "stainless steel is used for cutlery because it is hard and resists rusting".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements.
- Brass: copper + zinc. Stainless steel: iron + chromium, nickel and carbon. Steel: iron + carbon. ZIMSEC also: bronze (copper + tin) and duralumin (aluminium + copper).
- Alloys can be harder and stronger than pure metals, so they are more useful.
- Supplement: different-sized atoms disrupt the layers so they cannot slide easily.
- In a particle diagram, an alloy shows a few different-sized atoms mixed into a regular metal lattice.
:::
