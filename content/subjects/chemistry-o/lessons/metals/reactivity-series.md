## Introduction

Gold panners on Zimbabwe's rivers find gold as shiny flakes of the metal itself, but nobody has ever found a nugget of sodium. Iron has to be won from its ore in a blast furnace, and aluminium needs huge amounts of electricity. These differences all come from one idea: some metals are far more **reactive** than others.

Placing metals in order of reactivity, the **reactivity series**, lets you predict how a metal reacts with water and acids, whether it will displace another metal from a solution, and how it can be extracted. In this lesson you will learn the series, the evidence for it and how to deduce an order from experimental results. You should already know the reactions of metals with water, steam and dilute acids.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reactivity series"}
A list of metals in order of how vigorously they react, most reactive first. Carbon and hydrogen are included for comparison.
:::

:::definition{term="Displacement reaction"}
A reaction in which a more reactive metal takes the place of a less reactive metal in a compound, for example in a solution of its ions.
:::

:::definition{term="Reactivity (Supplement)"}
The tendency of a metal to lose electrons and form positive ions. The more easily a metal forms positive ions, the more reactive it is.
:::

## The series

| Position | Element | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| most reactive | potassium, K | reacts violently with cold water |
| | sodium, Na | reacts vigorously with cold water |
| | calcium, Ca | reacts steadily with cold water |
| | magnesium, Mg | very slow with cold water; reacts with steam |
| | aluminium, Al | protected by an oxide layer |
| | **carbon, C** | non-metal included for comparison |
| | zinc, Zn | reacts with dilute acids |
| | iron, Fe | reacts slowly with dilute acids |
| | **hydrogen, H** | non-metal included for comparison |
| | copper, Cu | no reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid |
| | silver, Ag | no reaction with dilute hydrochloric acid |
| least reactive | gold, Au | found native (as the metal) |

One way to remember the order: **K**ind **N**urses **Ca**re **M**ore **A**bout **C**hildren; **Z**ealous **F**armers **H**elp **C**ommunities **A**nd **A**nimals.

:::keyfact{title="What carbon and hydrogen tell you"}
- Metals **below carbon** (zinc, iron, copper) can be extracted by heating their oxides with carbon; metals above carbon cannot.
- Metals **above hydrogen** react with dilute acids to give hydrogen; metals below hydrogen (copper, silver, gold) do not.
:::

## Evidence from water, steam and acids

**Cold water.** Potassium reacts violently (the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame), sodium vigorously and calcium steadily. Each forms a hydroxide and hydrogen, for example $\ce{Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$.

**Steam.** Magnesium reacts only extremely slowly with cold water, but heated magnesium burns brightly in steam: $\ce{Mg + H2O -> MgO + H2}$.

**Dilute hydrochloric acid.** Magnesium fizzes vigorously, zinc steadily and iron slowly. Copper, silver and gold show no reaction. (Potassium, sodium and calcium react dangerously with acids, so this test is never done with them.)

$$
\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

### Why aluminium seems unreactive (Supplement)

Aluminium is high in the series, yet aluminium pots do not fizz in water. Its surface is covered by a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide that stops water and acids reaching the metal. If the layer is removed, aluminium reacts vigorously; in dilute hydrochloric acid, fizzing starts only after a delay, once the acid has dissolved the oxide layer.

## Displacement reactions (Supplement; ZIMSEC)

A more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of a solution of its salt. Put a piece of magnesium ribbon into blue copper(II) sulfate solution: a red-brown coating of copper appears, the blue colour fades and the mixture warms up.

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> MgSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

The sulfate ions do not change, so the ionic equation is:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Mg^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

Magnesium atoms **lose** electrons to become ions, and copper(II) ions **gain** those electrons to become atoms. The more reactive metal has the greater tendency to form positive ions. If the metal is **less** reactive than the metal in solution, nothing happens: copper in magnesium sulfate solution shows no change.

## Using the series (ZIMSEC)

- **Extraction**: metals above carbon are extracted by electrolysis; metals below carbon by heating with carbon; the least reactive, such as gold, are found native.
- **Cells**: two different metals in a salt solution make a simple cell. The further apart the metals are in the series, the larger the voltage, and the more reactive metal forms the negative electrode.
- **Electroplating and corrosion protection** also depend on which metal more readily forms ions.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: deducing an order from displacement results"}
Metals W, X and Y are each placed in solutions of the nitrates of the other two. ✓ = a reaction is seen; ✗ = no reaction.

| Metal | W nitrate | X nitrate | Y nitrate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| W | – | ✗ | ✗ |
| X | ✓ | – | ✓ |
| Y | ✓ | ✗ | – |

1. X displaces both W and Y, so X is the most reactive.
2. Y displaces W but not X, so Y is in the middle.
3. W displaces neither, so W is the least reactive.
4. Order: X > Y > W.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: copper in silver nitrate (Supplement)]
Predict what happens when a copper wire is placed in silver nitrate solution, and write the equations.

1. Copper is above silver, so copper displaces silver.
2. Observations: grey-white crystals of silver grow on the wire; the colourless solution slowly turns blue as copper(II) ions form.
3. $\ce{Cu(s) + 2AgNO3(aq) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$.
4. Ionic equation: $\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}$. Two silver ions are needed to take the two electrons lost by each copper atom.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reversing a displacement"}
Only a **more** reactive metal displaces a less reactive one. Copper does not react with zinc sulfate solution.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that hydrogen and carbon are not metals"}
They are placed in the series only as reference points, for acids and for extraction.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling aluminium unreactive"}
Aluminium only **appears** unreactive because of its oxide layer.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing sulfate ions in the ionic equation"}
Sulfate ions are spectators in metal/metal-ion displacements: leave them out.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "deduce the order" questions, count how many other metals each metal displaces (or how fast it fizzes). The metal with the most reactions is the most reactive.
:::

:::exam-tip
For displacement observations, give **two**: the coloured solution fades or changes colour, and a coating of the less reactive metal forms. A temperature rise can be a third.
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the order in both directions; questions may ask for the least reactive metal or for metals "below hydrogen".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Order: K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, (C), Zn, Fe, (H), Cu, Ag, Au.
- K, Na and Ca react with cold water; Mg reacts with steam; Mg, Zn and Fe react with dilute hydrochloric acid; Cu, Ag and Au do not.
- Supplement: reactivity is the tendency to form positive ions, shown by displacement reactions such as $\ce{Mg + Cu^2+ -> Mg^2+ + Cu}$.
- Supplement: aluminium appears unreactive because of its protective oxide layer.
- An order of reactivity is deduced from displacement results, temperature rises or rates of fizzing.
:::
