## Introduction

Zimbabwe has deposits of iron ore, coking coal (at Hwange) and limestone, the three raw materials of the blast furnace, and the town of Redcliff near Kwekwe grew up around an iron and steel works. Iron is still the most widely used metal in the world, and almost all of it is made in blast furnaces.

<!-- Reviewer: the Redcliff iron and steel works (ZISCO) is cited only historically; no claim is made about current operations. -->

Most metals are found in the Earth's crust as **compounds** in rocks called ores. Getting the metal out, **extraction**, means breaking down those compounds. How hard this is depends on how reactive the metal is. In this lesson you will link extraction methods to the reactivity series and then study the blast furnace in detail. Symbol equations for the furnace are Cambridge **Supplement**; Core candidates describe the reactions in words. You should already know the reactivity series and the meaning of oxidation and reduction as gain and loss of oxygen.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Ore"}
A rock that contains enough of a metal or a metal compound to make it worth extracting.
:::

:::definition{term="Extraction"}
Obtaining a metal from its ore, usually by reducing a metal compound to the metal.
:::

:::definition{term="Reduction"}
The removal of oxygen from a substance (or the gain of electrons). Metal oxides are reduced to metals.
:::

:::definition{term="Reducing agent"}
A substance that removes oxygen from another substance. Carbon and carbon monoxide are reducing agents.
:::

:::definition{term="Slag"}
The molten waste, mainly calcium silicate, formed when limestone removes sandy (silica) impurities in the blast furnace.
:::

## Extraction and the reactivity series

The more reactive a metal, the more strongly it holds on to the other elements in its compounds, and the harder it is to extract.

| Metals | Method of extraction |
| --- | --- |
| potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium (above carbon) | electrolysis of a molten compound |
| zinc, iron, copper (below carbon) | heating the oxide with carbon (reduction) |
| silver, gold (least reactive) | found native, as the uncombined metal |

Carbon can remove oxygen only from the oxides of metals **below** it in the reactivity series. For example, zinc oxide is reduced by carbon in a furnace:

$$
\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}
$$

Metals above carbon, such as aluminium, cannot be extracted this way, so electrolysis, which needs a lot of electricity, is used. Gold is so unreactive that it is found as the metal itself, which is why it was one of the first metals people used.

## The blast furnace

The blast furnace is a tall steel tower, often more than 30 metres high, lined with heat-resistant bricks.

**Raw materials** (the charge), added at the top:

- **haematite**, the iron ore, which is mainly iron(III) oxide, $\ce{Fe2O3}$, mixed with sandy impurities (silicon dioxide);
- **coke**, an almost pure form of carbon made by heating coal;
- **limestone**, calcium carbonate, $\ce{CaCO3}$.

**Hot air** is blown in through pipes near the bottom.

### The reactions, step by step

1. **Coke burns** in the hot air, releasing heat and forming carbon dioxide (exothermic): $\ce{C + O2 -> CO2}$.
2. **Carbon dioxide is reduced** by more hot coke to carbon monoxide: $\ce{C + CO2 -> 2CO}$.
3. **Carbon monoxide reduces the iron(III) oxide** to molten iron, which trickles to the bottom: $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$.
4. **Limestone is thermally decomposed** by the heat: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$.
5. **Calcium oxide reacts with silicon dioxide** (the sandy impurity) to form slag. This is a basic oxide neutralising an acidic oxide: $\ce{CaO + SiO2 -> CaSiO3}$.

![Blast furnace: haematite, coke and limestone are fed in at the top and hot air is blown in near the bottom; carbon burns to carbon dioxide (C + O2), carbon dioxide is reduced to carbon monoxide (C + CO2), carbon monoxide reduces iron(III) oxide to iron (Fe2O3 + 3CO), limestone decomposes and its calcium oxide reacts with silicon dioxide to make slag; molten slag floats on molten iron and both are tapped off at the bottom, while waste gases leave at the top.](media:diagrams/blast-furnace-schematic.png "Figure: the blast furnace with its main reactions")

The molten slag is less dense than molten iron, so it floats on top. Iron and slag are tapped off separately from the bottom of the furnace. Waste gases (mainly nitrogen, with carbon dioxide and some unused carbon monoxide) leave at the top. Slag is used for building roads and making cement.

:::note{title="From iron to steel"}
Iron from the blast furnace contains about 4% carbon, which makes it hard but brittle. Most of it is turned into steel by blowing oxygen through the molten iron to burn off most of the carbon.
:::

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: choosing an extraction method"}
1. **Magnesium** is above carbon: electrolysis of molten magnesium chloride.
2. **Zinc** is below carbon: heat zinc oxide with carbon (coke).
3. **Gold** is very unreactive: it is found native and only needs separating from the rock.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of iron from haematite]
Calculate the maximum mass of iron that can be made from 320 tonnes of iron(III) oxide. (Ar: O = 16, Fe = 56)

1. $M_r(\ce{Fe2O3}) = 2 \times 56 + 3 \times 16 = 160$.
2. 160 tonnes of $\ce{Fe2O3}$ contain $2 \times 56 = 112$ tonnes of iron.
3. Mass of iron $= 320 \times \dfrac{112}{160} = 224$ tonnes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying carbon (coke) reduces the iron oxide directly"}
In the furnace description the reducing agent is **carbon monoxide**, formed from coke in two steps.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying limestone 'reacts with impurities'"}
Be precise: limestone decomposes to **calcium oxide**, which reacts with **silicon dioxide** to form slag (calcium silicate).
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the blast furnace for aluminium"}
Aluminium is above carbon in the reactivity series, so it must be extracted by electrolysis.
:::

:::exam-tip

:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Metals above carbon are extracted by electrolysis; metals below carbon by heating with carbon; gold and silver are found native.
- Blast furnace raw materials: haematite, coke, limestone and hot air.
- Coke burns to carbon dioxide, which is reduced to carbon monoxide; carbon monoxide reduces iron(III) oxide to iron.
- Limestone decomposes to calcium oxide, which reacts with silicon dioxide to form slag.
- Supplement: $\ce{C + O2 -> CO2}$, $\ce{C + CO2 -> 2CO}$, $\ce{Fe2O3 + 3CO -> 2Fe + 3CO2}$, $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$, $\ce{CaO + SiO2 -> CaSiO3}$.
:::
