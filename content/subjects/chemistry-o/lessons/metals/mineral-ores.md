## Introduction

Zimbabwe sits on some of the richest geology in Africa. The **Great Dyke**, a narrow band of ancient igneous rock about 550 km long running through the middle of the country, holds large deposits of chromite and the platinum-group metals. Gold has been mined in Zimbabwe for roughly a thousand years, and pegmatite rocks in areas such as Bikita contain lithium minerals now in demand for rechargeable batteries.

<!-- Reviewer: place names (Great Dyke length about 550 km; lithium pegmatites at Bikita) are given as geography only, with no production or company claims. Please confirm. -->

But a rock that contains a metal is not automatically worth mining. In this lesson you will learn what makes a rock an **ore**, the composition of the main ores of the metals in the ZIMSEC syllabus, and how to calculate how much metal an ore contains. This is ZIMSEC Form 4 content. You should already be able to calculate relative formula masses.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Mineral"}
A naturally occurring solid with a definite chemical composition, such as haematite, $\ce{Fe2O3}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Ore"}
A rock that contains enough of a metal, or a metal compound, for the metal to be extracted at a profit.
:::

:::definition{term="Gangue"}
The unwanted rock and minerals mixed with the valuable mineral in an ore. It must be separated off and disposed of.
:::

:::definition{term="Native metal"}
A metal found in the ground as the uncombined element, such as gold.
:::

:::definition{term="Ore grade"}
How much metal (or valuable mineral) an ore contains, for example as a percentage or in grams per tonne.
:::

## What makes a rock an ore?

Whether a rock is an ore depends on economics as well as chemistry:

- **the grade**: how much metal it contains;
- **the value of the metal**: platinum is worth mining even when a tonne of rock contains only a few grams, but iron ore must contain a large percentage of iron;
- **the cost of mining and processing**: depth, transport, electricity, water and the amount of waste rock;
- **demand**: when the price of a metal rises, rock that was not worth mining can become an ore.

## The main ores in the ZIMSEC syllabus

| Metal | Ore mineral | Composition | Type |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| iron | haematite | iron(III) oxide, $\ce{Fe2O3}$ | oxide |
| iron | magnetite | $\ce{Fe3O4}$ (magnetic) | oxide |
| copper | chalcopyrite (copper pyrites) | copper iron sulfide, $\ce{CuFeS2}$ | sulfide |
| nickel | pentlandite | nickel iron sulfide, $\ce{(Fe,Ni)9S8}$ | sulfide |
| chromium | chromite | iron chromium oxide, $\ce{FeCr2O4}$ | oxide |
| platinum-group metals | tiny amounts in sulfide minerals of nickel, copper and iron in a layer of the Great Dyke | mixed sulfides | sulfide |
| lithium | petalite and spodumene | $\ce{LiAlSi4O10}$ and $\ce{LiAlSi2O6}$ | silicates |
| gold | native gold, often in quartz veins | Au | native metal |

A few points to notice:

- In pentlandite, nickel and iron share the same positions in the crystal, so their proportions vary; the formula $\ce{(Fe,Ni)9S8}$ shows nine metal atoms (iron and nickel together) for every eight sulfur atoms.
- The **platinum-group metals** (platinum, palladium, rhodium and others) occur in very low concentrations, mixed with nickel and copper sulfides. That is why huge amounts of rock are processed, and why nickel and copper are produced as by-products.
- **Gold** is so unreactive that it occurs as the metal itself, either in quartz veins in rock or as grains in river sands.
- **Lithium** minerals are silicates that contain only a small percentage of lithium.

## Calculating the percentage of a metal in a mineral

:::formula{name="Percentage of an element in a compound"}
$$
\%\ \text{metal} = \frac{\text{number of metal atoms} \times A_r}{M_r} \times 100
$$
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: iron in magnetite, $\ce{Fe3O4}$ (Ar: O = 16, Fe = 56)]
1. $M_r(\ce{Fe3O4}) = 3 \times 56 + 4 \times 16 = 168 + 64 = 232$.
2. Mass of iron in one formula unit $= 3 \times 56 = 168$.
3. Percentage of iron $= \dfrac{168}{232} \times 100 = 72.4\%$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: copper in chalcopyrite, $\ce{CuFeS2}$ (Ar: S = 32, Fe = 56, Cu = 64)]
1. $M_r(\ce{CuFeS2}) = 64 + 56 + 2 \times 32 = 184$.
2. Percentage of copper $= \dfrac{64}{184} \times 100 = 34.8\%$.
:::

### Mass of metal in a sample of ore

Real ores are mixtures: the valuable mineral is only part of the rock. Use two steps:

1. mass of mineral $=$ mass of ore $\times$ percentage of mineral in the ore;
2. mass of metal $=$ mass of mineral $\times$ percentage of metal in the mineral.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: copper from a low-grade ore]
A copper ore contains 2.0% chalcopyrite by mass. Calculate the mass of copper in 1.00 tonne (1000 kg) of ore.

1. Mass of chalcopyrite $= 1000 \times \dfrac{2.0}{100} = 20\ \text{kg}$.
2. Mass of copper $= 20 \times \dfrac{64}{184} = 6.96\ \text{kg}$ (about 7 kg).
3. So about 993 kg of every tonne is waste: this is why ores are crushed and concentrated before smelting.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling any rock with metal in it an ore"}
An ore must contain enough metal to make extraction **profitable**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the number of metal atoms"}
In $\ce{Fe3O4}$ there are **three** iron atoms: multiply the $A_r$ of iron by 3.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the percentage of the mineral as the percentage of the metal"}
A rock that is 2% chalcopyrite contains much less than 2% copper, because chalcopyrite is only 34.8% copper.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show the $M_r$ calculation in full; marks are given for the working even if a final rounding slip is made.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked "Explain why rock X is mined although it contains very little metal", mention the **high value** of the metal (for example platinum or gold) compared with the cost of extraction.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- An ore is a rock from which a metal can be extracted at a profit; gangue is the waste rock.
- Iron: haematite ($\ce{Fe2O3}$) and magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$). Copper: chalcopyrite ($\ce{CuFeS2}$). Nickel: pentlandite. Chromium: chromite ($\ce{FeCr2O4}$).
- The Great Dyke holds chromite and platinum-group metals; PGMs occur in tiny amounts with nickel and copper sulfides.
- Lithium comes from silicate minerals such as petalite and spodumene; gold occurs native.
- Percentage of metal $= \dfrac{\text{atoms} \times A_r}{M_r} \times 100$; multiply by the percentage of the mineral in the ore to find the metal in the rock.
:::
