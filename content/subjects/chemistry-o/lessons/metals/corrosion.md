## Introduction

Iron and steel are the most widely used metals in the world, from roofing sheets and window frames to bridges, borehole pumps and wire fences. Their great weakness is **rusting**. A scratched car door or an unpainted gate soon develops flaky orange-brown patches, and the rust slowly eats into the metal and weakens it. Replacing and protecting rusting steel costs a great deal of money every year.

In this lesson you will find out exactly what iron needs in order to rust, how simple experiments prove it, and how rusting is prevented. This topic is in the Cambridge IGCSE syllabus (9.5); galvanising and sacrificial protection are **Supplement** content. You should already know the reactivity series.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Corrosion"}
The gradual destruction of a metal by chemical reaction with substances around it, such as oxygen and water.
:::

:::definition{term="Rusting"}
The corrosion of iron and steel. Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form **hydrated iron(III) oxide**, $\ce{Fe2O3.xH2O}$, which is rust.
:::

:::definition{term="Barrier method"}
A way of preventing rusting by covering the iron with a layer that keeps out oxygen and water, such as paint, grease or plastic.
:::

:::definition{term="Sacrificial protection (Supplement)"}
Protecting iron by attaching or coating it with a more reactive metal, which corrodes instead of the iron.
:::

## What iron needs in order to rust

:::keyfact{title="Both water and oxygen"}
Iron and steel rust only when **both water and oxygen** (from air) are present:

iron + oxygen + water → hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust)
:::

Dissolved salt and acidic pollution make rusting faster, which is why cars rust quickly near the sea, but neither is needed for rust to form.

### The classic experiment

Three clean iron nails are set up in test tubes and left for a week.

| Tube | Conditions | Result after a week |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | nail in tap water, open to the air | rusts |
| B | nail in water that has been boiled, with a layer of oil on top | no rust |
| C | nail in dry air, with anhydrous calcium chloride in the tube and a stopper | no rust |

- **Boiling** the water removes the dissolved air (oxygen); the **oil layer** stops air dissolving in the water again. So tube B has water but no oxygen.
- **Anhydrous calcium chloride** is a drying agent: it absorbs water vapour. The stopper keeps moist air out. So tube C has oxygen but no water.
- Only tube A, with water **and** oxygen, rusts.

### Rusting uses up oxygen

Damp iron wool pushed into the end of a measuring tube of air, with the open end in water, slowly rusts. As oxygen is used up, water rises up the tube until about one fifth of the air has gone, the proportion of oxygen in air.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: percentage of oxygen from a rusting experiment]
A tube contains $80.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of air and some damp iron wool. After a week, $63.2\ \text{cm}^3$ of gas remains. Calculate the percentage of the air used up.

1. Volume used $= 80.0 - 63.2 = 16.8\ \text{cm}^3$ (this is the oxygen).
2. Percentage $= \dfrac{16.8}{80.0} \times 100 = 21.0\%$.
:::

## Barrier methods

A barrier keeps oxygen and water away from the iron surface.

| Method | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| painting | car bodies, bridges, gates, window frames | cheap; must be repainted when it chips |
| greasing or oiling | bicycle chains, machine parts, tools | suits moving parts, where paint would rub off |
| coating with plastic | wire fences, dish racks, garden furniture | tough, colourful layer |
| plating with another metal | tin cans (tin), taps (chromium) | attractive and hard-wearing |

The weakness of every barrier is damage: once it is scratched, water and oxygen reach the iron and rusting starts.

## Galvanising and sacrificial protection (Supplement)

**Galvanising** means coating iron or steel with a layer of **zinc**, usually by dipping it into molten zinc. Corrugated roofing sheets, buckets and wire for fences are often galvanised. The zinc protects in two ways:

1. as a **barrier**, keeping out oxygen and water;
2. as **sacrificial protection**: zinc is more reactive than iron, so if the coating is scratched, the zinc corrodes instead of the iron.

Zinc has a greater tendency to lose electrons than iron, so it is oxidised first:

$$
\ce{Zn(s) -> Zn^2+(aq) + 2e-}
$$

While zinc is present, the iron does not lose electrons to form iron ions, so it does not rust.

The same idea protects large steel structures that cannot easily be painted, such as ships' hulls, underground pipelines and oil rigs. Blocks of **magnesium** or **zinc** are bolted to the steel. These more reactive metals corrode away instead of the iron and are replaced from time to time.

:::note{title="Why tin plating is different"}
Tin is **less** reactive than iron, so tin plating is only a barrier. If a tin-plated can is scratched, the exposed iron rusts, and it rusts faster than unplated iron would.
:::

## Worked example

:::example{title="Example 2: choosing a method"}
1. **Bicycle chain**: moving parts, so oil or grease it.
2. **Steel gate**: paint it (or coat it with plastic).
3. **Steel water tank on a farm**: galvanise it, so scratches do not cause rust.
4. **Ship's hull**: attach magnesium or zinc blocks for sacrificial protection, as well as painting.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying iron rusts in water alone, or in air alone"}
Both water **and** oxygen are needed.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling rust 'iron oxide'"}
Rust is **hydrated** iron(III) oxide. The word "hydrated" matters.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying zinc protects because it is unreactive"}
Zinc protects because it is **more** reactive than iron, so it corrodes first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting the purpose of each tube"}
Boiled water + oil = no oxygen; calcium chloride + stopper = no water. Explain what each set-up removes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For "how does painting prevent rusting?", say it **keeps out oxygen and water**; naming both scores the mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
For sacrificial protection (Supplement), use the reactivity series **and** electrons: "zinc is more reactive than iron, so it loses electrons more readily and is oxidised instead of iron".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide; iron rusts only when both water and oxygen are present.
- Experiments: boiled water with an oil layer removes oxygen; a drying agent such as anhydrous calcium chloride removes water.
- Barrier methods (paint, grease, plastic) keep out oxygen and water.
- Supplement: galvanising coats steel with zinc, which is both a barrier and sacrificial protection.
- Supplement: a more reactive metal (Mg, Zn) loses electrons in preference to iron, so the iron does not rust.
:::
