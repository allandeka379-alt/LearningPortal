## Introduction

Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust, yet for most of history nobody could obtain it. In the middle of the 1800s it was so hard to extract that it was treated as a precious metal. Only when electrolysis became practical on a large scale, in the late 1880s, did aluminium become cheap enough for pots, cans and aircraft.

Today aluminium is still extracted by electrolysis, a process that uses huge amounts of electricity. That is why aluminium smelters are built where electricity is plentiful and cheap, often near large hydroelectric power stations. In this lesson you will learn why electrolysis is needed and how the process works. The ore and the method are Cambridge **Core**; the details of the cell, cryolite and the half-equations are **Supplement**. You should already know the basic principles of electrolysis and the reactivity series.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Bauxite"}
The main ore of aluminium: impure aluminium oxide, $\ce{Al2O3}$. It is purified to give aluminium oxide (alumina) before electrolysis.
:::

:::definition{term="Electrolysis"}
The breakdown of an ionic compound, when molten or in solution, by passing an electric current through it.
:::

:::definition{term="Cryolite (Supplement)"}
Sodium aluminium fluoride, $\ce{Na3AlF6}$. Purified aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite for electrolysis.
:::

## Why electrolysis?

Aluminium is **above carbon** in the reactivity series. Carbon cannot remove oxygen from aluminium oxide, so the blast-furnace method used for iron does not work. Instead, the ions in aluminium oxide are separated by electricity.

For electrolysis, the ions must be free to move, so the aluminium oxide must be molten or dissolved. Pure aluminium oxide melts at over 2000 °C, which would need an enormous amount of energy.

## The electrolysis cell (Supplement)

- The **electrolyte** is purified aluminium oxide dissolved in molten **cryolite**, at about 950 °C.
- The **cathode** (negative electrode) is the carbon (graphite) lining of the steel tank.
- The **anodes** (positive electrodes) are large blocks of carbon (graphite) that dip into the electrolyte.

![Electrolysis cell for aluminium: purified aluminium oxide dissolved in molten cryolite at about 950 degrees Celsius in a steel tank lined with carbon, which is the negative cathode; carbon anodes dip in from above; aluminium ions gain electrons to form molten aluminium that sinks to the bottom and is tapped off, oxide ions lose electrons to form oxygen which burns the carbon anodes away as carbon dioxide.](media:diagrams/aluminium-electrolysis-cell.png "Figure: the cell used to extract aluminium")

:::keyfact{title="The role of cryolite"}
Dissolving aluminium oxide in molten cryolite **lowers the operating temperature** from over 2000 °C to about 950 °C, saving a great deal of energy, and the solution **conducts electricity better** than molten aluminium oxide alone.
:::

### At the cathode (negative electrode)

Aluminium ions are attracted to the cathode and **gain** electrons (reduction):

$$
\ce{Al^3+ + 3e- -> Al}
$$

Molten aluminium collects at the bottom of the cell and is siphoned or tapped off.

### At the anode (positive electrode)

Oxide ions are attracted to the anode and **lose** electrons (oxidation), forming oxygen:

$$
\ce{2O^2- -> O2 + 4e-}
$$

### Why the anodes must be replaced

The anodes are very hot, and the oxygen produced reacts with the carbon to form carbon dioxide:

$$
\ce{C + O2 -> CO2}
$$

So the carbon anodes slowly burn away and must be replaced regularly.

The overall reaction is the decomposition of aluminium oxide:

$$
\ce{2Al2O3 -> 4Al + 3O2}
$$

## Why aluminium is expensive and why recycling matters

Large amounts of electricity are needed to keep the electrolyte molten and to decompose the aluminium oxide, and the anodes have to be replaced. Recycling aluminium cans and scrap needs only a small fraction of the energy used to extract new aluminium, and it saves bauxite and reduces the waste produced by mining.

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: counting electrons]
How many moles of electrons are needed to produce 1 mole of aluminium? How many moles of oxygen molecules form at the same time?

1. $\ce{Al^3+ + 3e- -> Al}$: each aluminium ion needs 3 electrons, so 1 mol of aluminium needs **3 mol** of electrons.
2. $\ce{2O^2- -> O2 + 4e-}$: 4 mol of electrons are released for each mole of oxygen.
3. The same 3 mol of electrons is released at the anode, so $3 \div 4 = 0.75$ mol of $\ce{O2}$ forms.
4. Check with the overall equation: 4 Al for 3 $\ce{O2}$, so 1 Al for 0.75 $\ce{O2}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of aluminium from aluminium oxide]
Calculate the maximum mass of aluminium that can be obtained from 204 tonnes of aluminium oxide. (Ar: O = 16, Al = 27)

1. $M_r(\ce{Al2O3}) = 2 \times 27 + 3 \times 16 = 102$.
2. 102 tonnes of $\ce{Al2O3}$ contain $2 \times 27 = 54$ tonnes of aluminium.
3. Mass of aluminium $= 204 \times \dfrac{54}{102} = 108$ tonnes.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying cryolite is a catalyst"}
Cryolite is a **solvent**: it dissolves the aluminium oxide so the mixture is molten at a lower temperature. It is not a catalyst.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting aluminium at the anode"}
Aluminium ions are positive, so they go to the **negative** electrode (cathode). Oxygen forms at the positive anode.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing O instead of O₂"}
Oxygen is released as molecules: $\ce{2O^2- -> O2 + 4e-}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the anodes 'dissolve'"}
The carbon anodes **react with oxygen** to form carbon dioxide; they burn away.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Bauxite (impure aluminium oxide) is the main ore; aluminium is extracted by electrolysis because it is above carbon in the reactivity series.
- Supplement: aluminium oxide is dissolved in molten cryolite, which lowers the operating temperature and improves conductivity.
- Supplement: cathode $\ce{Al^3+ + 3e- -> Al}$; anode $\ce{2O^2- -> O2 + 4e-}$.
- Supplement: the oxygen reacts with the hot carbon anodes to form carbon dioxide, so the anodes burn away and are replaced.
- The process needs a lot of electricity, so recycling aluminium saves energy.
:::
