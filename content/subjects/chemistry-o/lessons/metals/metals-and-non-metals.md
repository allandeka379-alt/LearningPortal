## Introduction

Zimbabwe is a mining country. Gold, platinum, chromium, nickel and lithium are dug from its rocks, and steel and copper are used in every town. Non-metals matter just as much: the carbon in Hwange coal, the oxygen we breathe and the sulfur used to make sulfuric acid. About three-quarters of the elements are metals, and the rest are non-metals. The two groups behave so differently that you can usually classify an unknown element from a few simple tests.

In this lesson you will compare the physical properties of metals and non-metals, explain them using what you know about bonding, and learn the general reactions of metals. You should already know about metallic bonding and simple molecular structures.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Malleable"}
Able to be hammered or pressed into a new shape without breaking. Metals are malleable.
:::

:::definition{term="Ductile"}
Able to be drawn out (stretched) into a wire. Copper is very ductile.
:::

:::definition{term="Lustrous"}
Shiny when freshly cut or polished.
:::

:::definition{term="Brittle"}
Breaking or shattering when hit or bent. Solid non-metals such as sulfur are brittle.
:::

## Comparing physical properties

| Property | Metals | Non-metals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| electrical conductivity | good conductors | poor conductors (insulators) |
| thermal conductivity | good conductors | poor conductors |
| malleability and ductility | malleable and ductile | brittle when solid |
| melting and boiling points | usually high | usually low |
| density | usually high | usually low |
| appearance | shiny (lustrous) | usually dull |
| state at room temperature | solids (except mercury) | many gases, some solids, one liquid (bromine) |

:::keyfact{title="Learn the exceptions"}
- **Mercury** is a metal but a liquid at room temperature.
- **Sodium and potassium** are metals with low melting points and densities low enough to float on water.
- **Graphite**, a form of the non-metal carbon, conducts electricity and has a very high melting point.
- **Diamond** (carbon) has a very high melting point; **iodine** is a non-metal with a shiny appearance.
:::

## Explaining the properties

In a metal, positive metal ions are arranged in a regular lattice and surrounded by a "sea" of **delocalised electrons**. The attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is the metallic bond.

- **Conductivity**: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the structure, carrying charge (electrical conduction) and transferring energy (thermal conduction).
- **Malleability and ductility**: the layers of ions can slide over each other when a force is applied. The delocalised electrons move with them, so the metallic bonding is not broken.
- **High melting points**: the strong attraction between the ions and the electrons needs a lot of energy to overcome.

Most non-metal elements are made of small molecules, such as $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{S8}$. The forces **between** molecules are weak, so little energy is needed to separate them: their melting and boiling points are low. There are no delocalised electrons or ions to carry charge, so they do not conduct. Graphite is the exception because each carbon atom has one delocalised electron.

## General chemical properties of metals

Metals form **positive ions** by losing electrons, and their oxides are **basic**. Most metals react with oxygen, many react with water or steam, and those above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute acids.

### With oxygen

Metals burn or oxidise to form metal oxides. Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame, forming a white ash:

$$
\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}
$$

A piece of copper does not burst into flame, but when it is heated in air its surface slowly turns black as copper(II) oxide forms: $\ce{2Cu + O2 -> 2CuO}$.

### With water or steam

The most reactive metals react with cold water to form a metal hydroxide and hydrogen. Sodium floats, melts into a ball and fizzes as it moves about; potassium is so vigorous that the hydrogen burns with a lilac flame; calcium sinks and fizzes steadily.

$$
\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

Magnesium reacts only extremely slowly with cold water, but heated magnesium reacts rapidly with **steam**, glowing brightly and forming magnesium oxide:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}
$$

### With dilute acids

Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series react with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid to form a salt and hydrogen. Magnesium fizzes rapidly, zinc steadily and iron slowly. Copper, silver and gold do not react.

$$
\ce{Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> FeCl2(aq) + H2(g)}
$$

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: classifying elements from data"}
| Element | Melting point / °C | Conducts electricity as a solid? | Appearance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 1085 | yes | shiny, pinkish-brown |
| B | about 115 | no | yellow, brittle solid |
| C | above 3500 | yes | black, soft, slippery |
| D | −39 | yes | silvery liquid |

1. A: high melting point, conducts, shiny: a metal (copper).
2. B: low melting point, does not conduct, brittle: a non-metal (sulfur).
3. C: conducts, but black, soft and slippery with a very high melting point: graphite, a non-metal.
4. D: conducts and is shiny, although it is a liquid: a metal (mercury).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: equations for calcium with water]
State two observations and write the equation for calcium reacting with cold water.

1. Calcium is a reactive metal: it forms its hydroxide and hydrogen.
2. Calcium ions are $\ce{Ca^2+}$, so the hydroxide is $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$.
3. $\ce{Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$.
4. Observations: steady fizzing as the calcium sinks; the water turns cloudy because calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="'Metals conduct because ions move'"}
In a solid metal the **electrons** move, not the ions. Moving ions explain conduction in molten or dissolved ionic compounds.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying all non-metals are gases"}
Many non-metals are solids (carbon, sulfur, iodine, phosphorus) and bromine is a liquid.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing a hydroxide for the steam reaction"}
Metals that react with steam form the metal **oxide** and hydrogen: $\ce{Mg + H2O -> MgO + H2}$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain why metals conduct" needs the words **delocalised electrons** and **free to move** (or mobile). Just "free electrons" may lose a mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to classify an element from data, use several properties together. One property alone (such as conductivity) can mislead, as graphite shows.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Metals: good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable, ductile, shiny, usually high melting points and densities.
- Non-metals: poor conductors, brittle when solid, usually low melting points and densities (graphite and diamond are exceptions).
- Metallic bonding: positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons explains conductivity, malleability and high melting points.
- Metals react with oxygen to form basic oxides, with water or steam to form hydroxides or oxides and hydrogen, and with dilute acids to form salts and hydrogen.
:::
