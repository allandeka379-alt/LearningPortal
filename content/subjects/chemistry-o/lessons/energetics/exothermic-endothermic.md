## Introduction

When a cook lights mopane wood under a pot of sadza, a chemical reaction releases energy that heats the food. Other changes do the opposite. Dissolve a spoonful of ammonium nitrate fertiliser in a cup of water and the cup soon feels cold. Chemists describe these two kinds of energy change as **exothermic** and **endothermic**.

Energy changes decide which fuels we burn, how hand warmers and cold packs work, and why a lime kiln must be heated all the time. You should already know how to read a thermometer and write simple word and symbol equations.

This lesson covers Cambridge IGCSE topic 5.1 and ZIMSEC Form 3 chemical energetics. The explanation in terms of bonds and the sign of $\Delta H$ are **Cambridge Supplement** content; ZIMSEC Form 3 students also describe the changes in terms of bond breaking and bond forming.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Exothermic reaction"}
A reaction that transfers thermal energy **to** the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings **rises**.
:::

:::definition{term="Endothermic reaction"}
A reaction that takes in thermal energy **from** the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings **falls**.
:::

:::definition{term="Surroundings"}
Everything around the reacting chemicals: the water they are dissolved in, the container, the thermometer and the air.
:::

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change, ΔH (Cambridge Supplement)"}
The thermal energy transferred during a reaction, usually in kJ/mol. $\Delta H$ is **negative** for an exothermic reaction and **positive** for an endothermic reaction.
:::

## Recognising the two types

### Use a thermometer

When a reaction happens in solution, the water is part of the surroundings. If the thermometer reading **rises**, the reacting particles have transferred thermal energy to the water: the change is exothermic. If the reading **falls**, the reacting particles have taken in thermal energy from the water: the change is endothermic.

:::example{title="Example 1: two experiments in a polystyrene cup"}
| Experiment | Start / °C | End / °C | Change | Type |
| --- | :---: | :---: | --- | --- |
| dilute hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide solution | 21.5 | 28.0 | rise of 6.5 °C | exothermic |
| ammonium nitrate dissolving in water | 23.0 | 15.0 | fall of 8.0 °C | endothermic |

The neutralisation transfers thermal energy to the water, so its temperature rises. Dissolving ammonium nitrate takes in thermal energy from the water, so the water cools. Dissolving is a physical change, but its energy change is described with the same words.
:::

### Everyday and laboratory examples

| Change | Type | What you notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| burning wood, charcoal, coal, LPG or paraffin | exothermic | heat and light are given out |
| neutralising an acid with an alkali | exothermic | the solution warms up |
| respiration in living cells | exothermic | energy is released for the cell |
| magnesium reacting with dilute hydrochloric acid | exothermic | the test tube becomes warm |
| adding water to quicklime (calcium oxide) | exothermic | the mixture steams and may boil |
| thermal decomposition of limestone (calcium carbonate) | endothermic | needs strong heating all the time |
| photosynthesis | endothermic | energy is absorbed from sunlight |
| dissolving ammonium nitrate in water | endothermic | the solution becomes cold |
| sodium hydrogencarbonate with citric acid or vinegar | endothermic | the mixture fizzes and cools |

:::example{title="Example 2: burning mealie-meal and sawdust"}
In a teacher demonstration, a pinch of fine mealie-meal or sawdust sprinkled into a Bunsen flame burns with a bright flash. The fine powder has a very large surface area, so it burns almost instantly, giving out thermal energy and light: an exothermic reaction. The same dust can explode if it catches fire in a grain mill or a sawmill, which is why mills keep dust levels low.
:::

### Uses of energy changes

- **Hand warmers** contain iron powder that reacts slowly with oxygen (an exothermic reaction).
- **Self-heating cans** use the exothermic reaction of calcium oxide with water.
- **Cold packs** for sports injuries contain ammonium nitrate and water in separate compartments: squeezing the pack mixes them, and the endothermic dissolving cools the pack.

## Explaining energy changes with bonds

*Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC Form 3.*

During a reaction, bonds in the reactants break and new bonds form in the products.

:::keyfact{title="Breaking and making bonds"}
**Breaking** a bond needs energy: bond breaking is **endothermic**. **Making** a bond releases energy: bond making is **exothermic**.
:::

The overall energy change depends on which is larger:

- If **more** energy is released making the new bonds than is taken in breaking the old ones, the reaction is **exothermic** and $\Delta H$ is negative.
- If **more** energy is taken in breaking bonds than is released making bonds, the reaction is **endothermic** and $\Delta H$ is positive.

:::example{title="Example 3: burning hydrogen"}
$$
\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)} \qquad \Delta H = -572\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

- Bonds broken: two $\ce{H-H}$ bonds and one $\ce{O=O}$ bond, which takes in energy.
- Bonds made: four $\ce{O-H}$ bonds (two in each water molecule), which gives out energy.
- The energy given out when the $\ce{O-H}$ bonds form is greater than the energy taken in to break the $\ce{H-H}$ and $\ce{O=O}$ bonds, so the reaction is exothermic and $\Delta H$ is negative.
:::

:::example{title="Example 4: the lime kiln"}
$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)} \qquad \Delta H = +178\ \text{kJ/mol}
$$

More energy is needed to break the bonds holding calcium carbonate together than is released when the bonds in calcium oxide and carbon dioxide form. The reaction is endothermic, so a lime kiln must keep supplying thermal energy: when the heating stops, the decomposition stops.
:::

### The sign of ΔH

*Cambridge Supplement.*

The sign of $\Delta H$ describes what happens to the **reacting chemicals**, not to the surroundings. In an exothermic reaction the chemicals lose energy to the surroundings, so $\Delta H$ is negative. In an endothermic reaction the chemicals gain energy from the surroundings, so $\Delta H$ is positive.

| Reaction | $\Delta H$ / kJ/mol | Type |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| methane burning: $\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}$ | $-890$ | exothermic |
| hydrogen burning: $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$ | $-572$ | exothermic |
| limestone decomposing: $\ce{CaCO3 -> CaO + CO2}$ | $+178$ | endothermic |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying an endothermic reaction gives out cold"}
"Cold" is not a form of energy. An endothermic reaction **takes in** thermal energy from its surroundings, and that is why the surroundings become colder.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking that breaking bonds releases energy"}
Breaking a bond always **needs** energy. Energy is released only when new bonds **form**. Fuels release energy because the bonds made in carbon dioxide and water release more energy than is needed to break the bonds in the fuel and oxygen.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling a reaction endothermic because it needs heating to start"}
Charcoal needs a match to light it, but once burning it gives out far more energy than it took in: it is exothermic. An endothermic change takes in energy for as long as it continues. A thermal decomposition, such as decomposing limestone, therefore needs heat **all the time** and stops when the heating stops.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the sign of ΔH the wrong way round"}
Exothermic means $\Delta H$ is **negative** (the chemicals lose energy); endothermic means $\Delta H$ is **positive**. A rising thermometer reading goes with a negative $\Delta H$.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"State whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic" needs one word. "Explain how you know" needs the evidence too: "the temperature rose from 21.5 °C to 28.0 °C, so thermal energy was transferred to the surroundings".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain, in terms of bond breaking and bond making, why the reaction is exothermic" usually has three marks: breaking bonds takes in energy; making bonds gives out energy; **more** energy is given out making bonds than is taken in breaking them. Always make the comparison.
:::

:::exam-tip
When a question gives $\Delta H$, read the sign first: a minus sign means exothermic. Write the sign every time you quote a value, for example $\Delta H = +178\ \text{kJ/mol}$.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Exothermic reactions transfer thermal energy to the surroundings, so the temperature rises (burning fuels, neutralisation, respiration).
- Endothermic reactions take in thermal energy from the surroundings, so the temperature falls (thermal decomposition, photosynthesis, dissolving ammonium nitrate).
- Bond breaking is endothermic and bond making is exothermic; whichever is larger decides the overall change.
- $\Delta H$ is negative for exothermic reactions and positive for endothermic reactions (Cambridge Supplement).
- Needing heat to **start** a reaction does not make it endothermic.
:::
