## Introduction

Ethanol made from sugar cane at Chisumbanje is blended into the petrol sold in Zimbabwe. How much energy does a mole of ethanol release when it burns, and how could a school laboratory measure it? The answer is to let the reaction heat a known mass of water and measure the temperature change. The same idea works for neutralisation and for dissolving a solid.

This lesson is **ZIMSEC Form 4** content (enthalpy changes). Cambridge IGCSE students describe temperature changes but do not need these calculations. You should already know the meaning of exothermic, endothermic and $\Delta H$, and how to find amounts with $n = \dfrac{m}{M_r}$ and $n = c \times V$.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change of combustion"}
The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is burnt completely in oxygen. It is always negative.
:::

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change of neutralisation"}
The enthalpy change when an acid reacts with an alkali to form one mole of water. It is negative.
:::

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change of solution"}
The enthalpy change when one mole of a solute dissolves in water. It can be negative (the solution warms up) or positive (the solution cools down).
:::

:::definition{term="Specific heat capacity, c"}
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. For water, $c = 4.2$ J/g/°C.
:::

:::formula{name="Energy transferred to the water or solution"}
$$
q = mc\Delta T
$$

$q$ = energy transferred (J); $m$ = mass of the water or solution heated or cooled (g); $c$ = 4.2 J/g/°C; $\Delta T$ = temperature change (°C).
:::

:::formula{name="Enthalpy change per mole"}
$$
\Delta H = -\frac{q}{n}
$$

$q$ in kJ; $n$ = amount in moles of fuel burnt, water formed or solute dissolved. The minus sign makes $\Delta H$ negative when the temperature rises.
:::

## The apparatus

**Combustion** of a liquid fuel such as ethanol or paraffin:

- a **spirit burner** containing the fuel, with a cap to stop the fuel evaporating, weighed before and after burning;
- a **copper can** (calorimeter) containing a measured volume of water, clamped a few centimetres above the wick (copper conducts heat well to the water);
- a **thermometer** and a stirrer;
- a **draught shield** around the burner and can to cut heat loss.

**Neutralisation and solution**:

- a **polystyrene cup** with a lid (polystyrene is a good insulator), standing in a beaker so it cannot tip over;
- measuring cylinders or pipettes for the solutions, a balance for solids, and a thermometer reading to 0.5 °C or better.

For dilute solutions, take the mass of the solution from its volume: 1 cm³ has a mass of about 1 g. In neutralisation, use the **total** volume of acid and alkali. When a solid dissolves, use the mass of the water; the small mass of the solid is usually ignored (a question will tell you if it is not).

## Worked examples

:::worked-solution[Example 1: enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol, $\ce{C2H5OH}$]
Burning 1.15 g of ethanol raised the temperature of 200 cm³ of water by 24.0 °C. ($M_r$ of ethanol $= 46$)

1. Mass of water $= 200$ g (200 cm³).
2. $q = mc\Delta T = 200 \times 4.2 \times 24.0 = 20\,160$ J $= 20.16$ kJ.
3. $n(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 1.15 \div 46 = 0.0250$ mol.
4. $\Delta H = -20.16 \div 0.0250 = -806$ kJ/mol (to 3 significant figures).
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: enthalpy change of neutralisation]
50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ hydrochloric acid was mixed with 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol/dm³ sodium hydroxide in a polystyrene cup. The temperature rose by 6.5 °C. $\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}$

1. Total volume $= 100$ cm³, so $m = 100$ g.
2. $q = 100 \times 4.2 \times 6.5 = 2730$ J $= 2.73$ kJ.
3. $n(\ce{HCl}) = 1.00 \times 0.0500 = 0.0500$ mol, so $0.0500$ mol of water forms.
4. $\Delta H = -2.73 \div 0.0500 = -54.6$ kJ/mol.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: enthalpy change of solution of ammonium nitrate]
4.00 g of ammonium nitrate, $\ce{NH4NO3}$, was dissolved in 50.0 cm³ of water. The temperature **fell** by 6.0 °C. ($M_r = 80$)

1. $q = 50.0 \times 4.2 \times 6.0 = 1260$ J $= 1.26$ kJ, taken **in** from the water.
2. $n(\ce{NH4NO3}) = 4.00 \div 80 = 0.0500$ mol.
3. The temperature fell, so the change is endothermic: $\Delta H = +1.26 \div 0.0500 = +25.2$ kJ/mol.
:::

## Why measured values are too small

The data-book enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol is $-1367$ kJ/mol, but Example 1 gave only $-806$ kJ/mol, about 59% of it. Simple experiments always underestimate the energy released, because:

- thermal energy is **lost** to the air, the can, the clamp and the thermometer instead of heating the water;
- combustion is **incomplete**: a yellow flame and black soot on the can show that some carbon did not burn to carbon dioxide;
- some fuel **evaporates** from the wick, so the mass loss is larger than the mass actually burnt.

Improvements: use a draught shield and a lid, put the can close to the flame, stir the water, and keep the cap on the burner except while it is burning. For solutions, use an insulated cup with a lid and read the highest (or lowest) temperature reached.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the mass of the fuel in q = mcΔT"}
$m$ is the mass of the **water** (or solution) whose temperature changes. The mass of fuel is used only to find the number of moles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to convert joules to kilojoules"}
$q = mc\Delta T$ gives joules. Divide by 1000 before dividing by moles, or the answer will be 1000 times too large.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using only one solution's volume"}
In a neutralisation, both solutions are warmed, so $m$ is the **total** volume: 50.0 cm³ + 50.0 cm³ gives 100 g.
:::

:::mistake{title="Losing the sign"}
A temperature **rise** means an exothermic change, so $\Delta H$ is negative. A temperature **fall** means $\Delta H$ is positive. Use the size of the temperature change in $q = mc\Delta T$, then decide the sign.
:::

:::mistake{title="Moles of acid instead of moles of water"}
The enthalpy change of neutralisation is per mole of **water formed**. One mole of sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$, neutralised by sodium hydroxide forms **two** moles of water.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Lay out the calculation in the order of the examples: mass of water, $q$ in J, $q$ in kJ, moles, $\Delta H$. Each step can earn a method mark even if a later step goes wrong.
:::

:::exam-tip
Give the final answer with a sign and the unit kJ/mol, usually to 3 significant figures: $\Delta H = -806$ kJ/mol.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Suggest why the experimental value is less exothermic than the data-book value": the best answer is heat loss to the surroundings. Incomplete combustion is a second, separate reason for fuels.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Energy transferred: $q = mc\Delta T$, with $m$ the mass of water or solution (1 cm³ ≈ 1 g) and $c = 4.2$ J/g/°C.
- Convert $q$ to kJ, then divide by the moles of fuel burnt, water formed or solute dissolved.
- Temperature rise: exothermic, $\Delta H$ negative. Temperature fall: endothermic, $\Delta H$ positive.
- Use a spirit burner and copper can for combustion, and an insulated polystyrene cup for neutralisation and solution.
- Measured values are smaller than data-book values mainly because of heat loss and incomplete combustion.
:::
