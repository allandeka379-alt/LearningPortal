## Introduction

Burning charcoal is strongly exothermic, yet a bag of charcoal can sit in a shop in Mutare for months without catching fire. To start a braai you need a match or a fire-lighter. Why does an energy-releasing reaction need energy to get going? A **reaction pathway diagram** answers this by showing the energy of the reactants, the energy of the products and the "energy hill" the particles must climb in between.

Cambridge calls these diagrams **reaction pathway diagrams**; the ZIMSEC syllabus calls them **energy profile diagrams**. They are the same thing. Interpreting them is **Cambridge Core**; drawing and labelling them with $\Delta H$ and the activation energy is **Cambridge Supplement** and ZIMSEC Form 3 content. You should already know the meaning of exothermic, endothermic and $\Delta H$.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reaction pathway (energy profile) diagram"}
A diagram of energy (vertical axis) against the progress of the reaction (horizontal axis), showing the energy levels of the reactants and products and the energy barrier between them.
:::

:::definition{term="Activation energy, Ea"}
The minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react. On the diagram it is the height of the barrier above the reactants.
:::

:::definition{term="Enthalpy change, ΔH"}
The energy of the products minus the energy of the reactants. On the diagram it is the vertical distance from the reactant level to the product level.
:::

:::definition{term="Catalyst"}
A substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged at the end. It provides a different pathway with a lower activation energy.
:::

## Reading a reaction pathway diagram

The figure below shows two reaction pathway diagrams. Their features, as you would describe them in an exam answer, are:

![Two reaction pathway diagrams with energies relative to the reactants: reaction A is exothermic, the products lie 45 kJ/mol below the reactants (ΔH = −45) and the peak is 120 kJ/mol above the reactants (Ea = 120); reaction B is endothermic, the products lie 65 kJ/mol above the reactants (ΔH = +65) and the peak is 210 kJ/mol above (Ea = 210).](media:diagrams/energy-profiles-exo-endo.png "Figure: Ea is measured from the reactants to the peak; ΔH is measured from the reactants to the products")

- The **vertical axis** is labelled "energy". The **horizontal axis** is labelled "progress of reaction".
- A short horizontal line on the left shows the energy of the **reactants**, labelled with their formulae.
- A short horizontal line on the right shows the energy of the **products**.
- A smooth curve rises from the reactant line to a **peak**, then falls to the product line.
- A vertical arrow from the reactant line up to the peak is labelled $E_\text{a}$.
- A vertical arrow from the reactant line to the product line is labelled $\Delta H$.

| Feature | Exothermic reaction | Endothermic reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| products compared with reactants | lower in energy | higher in energy |
| $\Delta H$ arrow | points down | points up |
| sign of $\Delta H$ | negative | positive |
| thermal energy | given out to the surroundings | taken in from the surroundings |

The activation energy is always a positive quantity, for exothermic and endothermic reactions alike: the particles always have to climb **up** to the peak.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: reading values from two diagrams]
Two diagrams give these energies, measured relative to the reactants (in kJ/mol):

| | reactants | peak | products |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| reaction A | 0 | +120 | −45 |
| reaction B | 0 | +210 | +65 |

1. Activation energy = peak − reactants. A: $120 - 0 = 120$ kJ/mol. B: $210 - 0 = 210$ kJ/mol.
2. $\Delta H$ = products − reactants. A: $-45 - 0 = -45$ kJ/mol. B: $+65 - 0 = +65$ kJ/mol.
3. A has products below the reactants and a negative $\Delta H$: exothermic.
4. B has products above the reactants and a positive $\Delta H$: endothermic.
:::

## Drawing and labelling a diagram

*Cambridge Supplement; ZIMSEC Form 3.*

1. Draw and label the axes: "energy" up the side and "progress of reaction" along the bottom.
2. Draw a short horizontal line on the left for the reactants and write their formulae on it.
3. Draw a second line on the right for the products: **lower** than the reactants for an exothermic reaction, **higher** for an endothermic one.
4. Join the lines with a smooth curve that rises well above **both** lines and then falls.
5. Draw a vertical arrow from the reactant line up to the top of the curve and label it $E_\text{a}$.
6. Draw a vertical arrow from the reactant line to the product line, pointing towards the products, and label it $\Delta H$ with its sign and value.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: the diagram for $\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}$, $\Delta H = +178$ kJ/mol]
1. $\Delta H$ is positive, so the reaction is endothermic: draw the products line ($\ce{CaO + CO2}$) **above** the reactants line ($\ce{CaCO3}$).
2. Draw the curve rising from the $\ce{CaCO3}$ line to a peak **above** the products line, then falling to the products line.
3. Draw the $E_\text{a}$ arrow upwards from the $\ce{CaCO3}$ line to the peak.
4. Draw the $\Delta H$ arrow upwards from the $\ce{CaCO3}$ line to the products line and label it $+178$ kJ/mol.
5. Because the peak is above the products, $E_\text{a}$ must be **larger** than 178 kJ/mol: for an endothermic reaction the activation energy is always larger than $\Delta H$.
:::

## Catalysts on the diagram

A catalyst gives the reaction a different route with a **lower activation energy**, so the peak is lower. It does not change the energy of the reactants or of the products, so $\Delta H$ stays the same. Examples are iron in the Haber process, vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process, manganese(IV) oxide for decomposing hydrogen peroxide, and enzymes in living things.

![Reaction pathway diagram with and without a catalyst: the reactants are at 0 and the products at −40 kJ/mol in both cases, so ΔH = −40 is unchanged, but the catalysed pathway (dashed) peaks at +110 kJ/mol instead of +180 kJ/mol, so the activation energy is 70 kJ/mol lower.](media:diagrams/energy-profiles-catalyst.png "Figure: a catalyst lowers the peak (Ea) but leaves the reactant and product levels, and so ΔH, unchanged")

:::worked-solution[Example 3: the effect of a catalyst]
For one reaction, the energies relative to the reactants are: peak without a catalyst $+180$ kJ/mol, peak with a catalyst $+110$ kJ/mol, products $-40$ kJ/mol.

1. Activation energy without the catalyst: $180 - 0 = 180$ kJ/mol.
2. Activation energy with the catalyst: $110 - 0 = 110$ kJ/mol, which is $180 - 110 = 70$ kJ/mol lower.
3. $\Delta H = -40$ kJ/mol in both cases: the reactants and products are the same.
4. More of the colliding particles have at least the lower activation energy, so more collisions lead to reaction and the reaction is faster.
:::

## For top grades: the reverse reaction

The reverse reaction starts at the products and climbs to the **same peak**. Its activation energy is the height of the peak above the **products**, and its $\Delta H$ has the same size but the opposite sign.

:::worked-solution[Example 4: reversing reaction A]
Reaction A had reactants at 0, the peak at $+120$ and products at $-45$ kJ/mol.

1. Activation energy of the reverse reaction $= 120 - (-45) = 165$ kJ/mol.
2. $\Delta H$ of the reverse reaction $= +45$ kJ/mol: the reverse reaction is endothermic.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing ΔH from the peak"}
The arrow from the peak down to the products is **not** $\Delta H$. $\Delta H$ is measured between the reactant and product lines only.
:::

:::mistake{title="Starting the Ea arrow in the wrong place"}
The activation energy arrow starts at the **reactant** line, not at the axis and not at the product line, and ends at the top of the curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Making the catalyst change ΔH"}
A catalyst lowers the peak only. The reactant and product lines stay where they are, so $\Delta H$ is unchanged.
:::

:::mistake{title="Labelling the horizontal axis 'time'"}
The horizontal axis shows the progress of the reaction (reactants on the left, products on the right), not time.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Interpret the diagram" or "Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic? Explain your answer": compare the levels in your answer, for example "the products are at a lower energy than the reactants, so energy is given out: exothermic".
:::

:::exam-tip
"Draw and label" questions award marks for each feature: labelled axes, reactants and products at the correct relative levels, a curve with a peak above both, an $E_\text{a}$ arrow and a $\Delta H$ arrow pointing the right way. Use a ruler for the energy levels.
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain why a catalyst speeds up a reaction, link the diagram to collisions: the catalyst lowers $E_\text{a}$, so a greater proportion of colliding particles have enough energy to react, giving more successful collisions per second.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A reaction pathway (energy profile) diagram plots energy against the progress of the reaction.
- Exothermic: products below reactants, $\Delta H$ negative. Endothermic: products above reactants, $\Delta H$ positive.
- $E_\text{a}$ is the minimum energy colliding particles need to react: the height of the peak above the reactants.
- $\Delta H$ is measured from the reactant line to the product line, never from the peak.
- A catalyst lowers $E_\text{a}$ but does not change $\Delta H$.
:::
