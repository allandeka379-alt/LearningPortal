## Introduction

A corrugated iron roof slowly turns orange-brown with rust. Charcoal glows in a brazier on a cold Bulawayo morning. Iron ore is turned into iron in a blast furnace, and a car battery pushes electrons round a circuit to start an engine. All of these are **redox reactions**: reactions in which one substance is **oxidised** while another is **reduced**.

In this lesson you will meet three ways of deciding what has been oxidised and what has been reduced: by **oxygen**, by **electrons** and by **oxidation numbers**. You will also learn to name **oxidising agents** and **reducing agents** and to recognise redox reactions from colour changes.

You should already know how ions form (metals lose electrons, non-metals gain them) and how to write chemical formulae.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
For Cambridge IGCSE, the oxygen definitions and Roman numerals are **Core**; electrons, oxidation-number rules, oxidising and reducing agents and the colour tests are **Supplement**. ZIMSEC Form 3 expects electron transfer, oxidation numbers and oxidising and reducing agents.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Oxidation (oxygen definition)"}
The gain of oxygen by a substance. Example: magnesium is oxidised when it burns to form magnesium oxide.
:::

:::definition{term="Reduction (oxygen definition)"}
The loss of oxygen by a substance. Example: copper(II) oxide is reduced to copper when it is heated in hydrogen.
:::

:::definition{term="Redox reaction"}
A reaction in which oxidation and reduction take place at the same time.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxidation and reduction (electron definitions, Supplement)"}
Oxidation is the **loss of electrons**; reduction is the **gain of electrons**.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxidation number (Supplement)"}
A number given to an atom in a substance to show how many electrons it has lost (a positive number) or gained (a negative number) compared with the uncombined element; in a covalent compound it is the number the atom would have if the compound were made of ions. Oxidation is an **increase** in oxidation number; reduction is a **decrease**.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxidising agent and reducing agent (Supplement)"}
An **oxidising agent** oxidises another substance and is itself reduced. A **reducing agent** reduces another substance and is itself oxidised.
:::

:::keyfact{title="OIL RIG"}
**O**xidation **I**s **L**oss (of electrons); **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain (of electrons).
:::

## Gain and loss of oxygen (Core)

When black copper(II) oxide is heated in a stream of hydrogen, it turns pink-brown as copper forms and droplets of water appear:

$$
\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}
$$

Copper(II) oxide **loses** oxygen, so it is **reduced**. Hydrogen **gains** oxygen, so it is **oxidised**. The two changes happen together, so this is a redox reaction.

:::example{title="Example 1: zinc from zinc oxide"}
Zinc oxide can be heated with carbon: $\ce{ZnO + C -> Zn + CO}$.

- Zinc oxide loses oxygen: it is **reduced** to zinc.
- Carbon gains oxygen: it is **oxidised** to carbon monoxide.
:::

## Roman numerals in names (Core)

Some elements, especially transition metals, can form ions with different charges. The Roman numeral in a name gives the **oxidation number** of that element in the compound.

| Name | Formula | Oxidation number of the metal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| iron(II) sulfate | $\ce{FeSO4}$ | $+2$ |
| iron(III) oxide | $\ce{Fe2O3}$ | $+3$ |
| copper(I) oxide | $\ce{Cu2O}$ | $+1$ |
| copper(II) oxide | $\ce{CuO}$ | $+2$ |
| manganese(IV) oxide | $\ce{MnO2}$ | $+4$ |
| potassium manganate(VII) | $\ce{KMnO4}$ | manganese is $+7$ |

The numeral belongs to **one element**, not to the whole compound: in iron(III) oxide each iron is $+3$, even though there are two iron atoms.

## Electron transfer (Supplement)

Many redox reactions involve no oxygen at all. When magnesium ribbon is placed in blue copper(II) sulfate solution, the blue colour fades and a pink-brown coating of copper forms. The sulfate ions do not change, so the ionic equation is:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Mg^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}
$$

Split it into two **half-equations**:

- $\ce{Mg -> Mg^2+ + 2e-}$: magnesium **loses** electrons, so it is **oxidised**.
- $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$: copper(II) ions **gain** electrons, so they are **reduced**.

The electrons lost by magnesium are exactly the electrons gained by the copper(II) ions. That is why oxidation and reduction always happen together.

## Oxidation numbers (Supplement)

Oxidation numbers let you spot redox in any reaction. Use these rules:

1. An uncombined element has oxidation number $0$ (for example $\ce{Cu}$, $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$).
2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation number equal to its charge ($\ce{Na+}$ is $+1$, $\ce{O^2-}$ is $-2$).
3. The oxidation numbers in a compound add up to $0$.
4. The oxidation numbers in an ion add up to the charge on the ion.

Useful values: oxygen is usually $-2$ in compounds and hydrogen is usually $+1$ in compounds with non-metals.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: oxidation numbers of sulfur and manganese]
1. In $\ce{H2SO4}$ the total is $0$: $2(+1) + \text{S} + 4(-2) = 0$, so $\text{S} = +6$.
2. In the manganate(VII) ion, $\ce{MnO4-}$, the total is $-1$: $\text{Mn} + 4(-2) = -1$, so $\text{Mn} = +7$. This is the "VII" in the name.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 3: is this a redox reaction?]
Iron(II) chloride solution reacts with chlorine: $\ce{2FeCl2 + Cl2 -> 2FeCl3}$.

1. Iron: $+2$ in $\ce{FeCl2}$, $+3$ in $\ce{FeCl3}$. The oxidation number increases, so iron is **oxidised**.
2. Chlorine in $\ce{Cl2}$: $0$. In $\ce{FeCl3}$: $-1$. It decreases, so chlorine is **reduced**.
3. Both changes happen, so the reaction is redox. Chlorine is the **oxidising agent**; iron(II) chloride is the **reducing agent**.
:::

In a neutralisation such as $\ce{NaOH + HCl -> NaCl + H2O}$ no oxidation number changes (Na stays $+1$, Cl stays $-1$, H stays $+1$, O stays $-2$), so it is **not** a redox reaction.

## Oxidising and reducing agents (Supplement)

In $\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}$, hydrogen takes the oxygen away from copper(II) oxide, so hydrogen is the **reducing agent** (and it is oxidised). Copper(II) oxide gives oxygen to the hydrogen, so it is the **oxidising agent** (and it is reduced).

| Common oxidising agents | Common reducing agents |
| --- | --- |
| oxygen, chlorine, acidified potassium manganate(VII), hydrogen peroxide | hydrogen, carbon, carbon monoxide, reactive metals, iodide ions, iron(II) ions, sulfur dioxide |

## Recognising redox from colour changes (Supplement)

| Reagent | Colour change | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | **purple to colourless** | the substance added is a **reducing agent** (the manganate(VII) is reduced) |
| aqueous potassium iodide | **colourless to brown** | the substance added is an **oxidising agent** (iodide ions are oxidised to iodine) |

:::example{title="Example 4: testing two solutions"}
A pale green solution of iron(II) sulfate turns acidified potassium manganate(VII) from purple to colourless: iron(II) ions are a reducing agent and are oxidised to iron(III) ions. Chlorine water turns aqueous potassium iodide brown: chlorine is an oxidising agent and is reduced to chloride ions, $\ce{Cl2 + 2I- -> 2Cl- + I2}$.
:::

:::note{title="ZIMSEC note"}
You may also meet acidified potassium dichromate(VI), which turns from **orange to green** when it oxidises a reducing agent.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the oxidising agent is oxidised"}
The oxidising agent causes oxidation in something else, so it is itself **reduced**. In $\ce{CuO + H2 -> Cu + H2O}$, copper(II) oxide is the oxidising agent and it is reduced.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading the Roman numeral as the charge on the compound"}
Iron(III) oxide is a neutral compound. The (III) tells you that **each iron** has oxidation number $+3$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that elements are zero"}
$\ce{O2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Cu}$ and $\ce{Mg}$ all have oxidation number $0$, however many atoms are in the molecule.
:::

:::mistake{title="Giving only one colour"}
"Potassium manganate(VII) goes colourless" may lose the mark. Write "from purple to colourless".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Explain, in terms of electrons, why magnesium is oxidised" needs the idea of **loss**: "each magnesium atom loses two electrons to form a $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ion".
:::

:::exam-tip
When you are asked to **identify** the oxidising or reducing agent, name the reactant (for example "chlorine" or "copper(II) oxide"), not a product.
:::

:::exam-tip
For oxidation-number questions, write the sum out in full, for example $2(+1) + \text{S} + 4(-2) = 0$. The working earns marks even if you slip at the end.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Core: oxidation is gain of oxygen, reduction is loss of oxygen, and a redox reaction has both at the same time.
- A Roman numeral gives the oxidation number of one element in a compound, as in iron(III) chloride.
- Supplement: oxidation is loss of electrons or an increase in oxidation number; reduction is gain of electrons or a decrease (OIL RIG).
- Supplement: oxidation numbers: elements $0$; monatomic ions equal their charge; a compound sums to $0$; an ion sums to its charge.
- Supplement: an oxidising agent is reduced; a reducing agent is oxidised.
- Supplement: acidified potassium manganate(VII) turns purple to colourless with a reducing agent; potassium iodide turns brown with an oxidising agent.
:::
