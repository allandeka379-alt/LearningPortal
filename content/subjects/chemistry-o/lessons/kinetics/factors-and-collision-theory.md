## Introduction

Milk left out on a hot October afternoon in Kariba turns sour within hours, but in a fridge it keeps for days. Thin sticks of kindling catch fire easily, while a thick log only smoulders. Fine flour or mealie-meal dust in a grain mill, and coal dust in an underground mine, can even explode. All of these are about the **rate of reaction**: how fast a reaction goes.

Chemists and engineers control rates all the time. A fertiliser factory wants its reactions to be fast, so that it makes more product every hour. A farmer storing grain, or a family storing food, wants spoiling reactions to be slow.

In this lesson you will learn which conditions change the rate of a reaction and, using **collision theory**, why they do. You should already know that particles in liquids and gases are always moving, and that they move faster at higher temperatures.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
Describing the effects is **Cambridge Core**. Explaining them with collision theory and activation energy is **Cambridge Supplement**, and ZIMSEC answers are expected to use these ideas too. Stirring is listed in the ZIMSEC syllabus.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Rate of reaction"}
How fast a reactant is used up or a product is formed: the change in the amount of a substance per unit time.
:::

:::definition{term="Activation energy, Ea (Supplement)"}
The minimum energy that colliding particles must have in order to react.
:::

:::definition{term="Catalyst"}
A substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is unchanged (chemically, and in mass) at the end of the reaction.
:::

:::definition{term="Enzyme"}
A biological catalyst: a protein made by living cells that speeds up a particular reaction.
:::

## Collision theory (Supplement)

For two particles to react they must:

1. **collide** with each other, and
2. collide with energy **equal to or greater than the activation energy**.

Collisions with less energy than this simply bounce apart. Anything that makes **successful collisions** happen more often (a higher **frequency** of successful collisions, that is, more per second) makes the reaction faster.

## The factors and how they work

| Change | Effect on rate | Explanation (Supplement) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher **concentration** of a solution | faster | more reacting particles **per unit volume**, so collisions are more frequent |
| Higher **pressure** of a gas | faster | the same particles are squeezed into a smaller volume, so there are more per unit volume and collisions are more frequent |
| Larger **surface area** (smaller pieces, powder) | faster | more particles of the solid are exposed, so collisions with it are more frequent |
| Higher **temperature** | faster | particles have more kinetic energy: they collide more often **and** a much larger proportion of collisions have energy at least equal to Ea |
| Adding a **catalyst** | faster | it provides another route with a **lower activation energy**, so a larger proportion of collisions are successful |
| **Stirring** (ZIMSEC) | faster | mixes the reactants and keeps bringing fresh reactant into contact with the surface of a solid |

:::keyfact{title="A small temperature rise has a big effect"}
A small rise in temperature makes many more particles reach the activation energy. For many reactions a rise of $10\ ^\circ\text{C}$ roughly doubles the rate.
:::

:::example{title="Example 1: lumps or powder?"}
Two flasks contain the same volume and concentration of dilute hydrochloric acid. One gets 5 g of marble chips, the other 5 g of powdered marble. The powder fizzes much more vigorously.

**Explanation (Supplement):** powdering the marble increases its surface area, so many more calcium carbonate particles are exposed to the acid particles. The frequency of collisions between acid particles and the solid increases, so the rate increases. The same total amount of carbon dioxide is made in each flask, because the masses of marble and the amounts of acid are the same.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: warming magnesium and acid"}
Magnesium ribbon reacts slowly with dilute hydrochloric acid at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ but much faster at $40\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

**Explanation (Supplement):** at the higher temperature the acid particles have more kinetic energy. They move faster, so they hit the magnesium more often, and a greater proportion of the collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. The frequency of **successful** collisions increases.
:::

## Catalysts and enzymes

A catalyst takes part in the reaction but is re-formed at the end, so a small amount can be used again and again. It does **not** change the amount of product formed; it only makes it form faster. Different reactions need different catalysts:

- manganese(IV) oxide speeds up the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, $\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}$;
- iron is the catalyst in the Haber process, and vanadium(V) oxide in the Contact process;
- platinum and rhodium in a car's catalytic converter speed up the removal of polluting gases.

**Enzymes** are catalysts made by living things. Potato and liver contain **catalase**, an enzyme that decomposes hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.

:::example{title="Example 3: catalase in potato and liver"}
A student drops equal-sized pieces of four materials into separate tubes of the same hydrogen peroxide solution.

| Added to hydrogen peroxide | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| raw liver | very vigorous fizzing; the gas relights a glowing splint |
| raw potato | steady fizzing |
| boiled potato | almost no bubbles |
| manganese(IV) oxide powder | vigorous fizzing; the black powder is still there at the end |

The gas is oxygen. Liver contains more catalase than potato. Boiling has **denatured** the enzyme in the boiled potato: its shape has changed permanently, so it no longer works. Enzymes work best at a moderate **optimum** temperature (for human enzymes, about $37\ ^\circ\text{C}$); above it they are denatured and the rate falls sharply.
:::

Hydrogen peroxide irritates the skin and eyes, so eye protection must be worn for these experiments.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying a catalyst gives more product"}
A catalyst only makes the same amount of product form **faster**. The final amount depends on how much of the limiting reactant there is.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 'more collisions' without 'per second'"}
Rate depends on how **often** particles collide. Write "more frequent collisions" or "more collisions per second".
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a higher concentration gives particles more energy"}
Concentration changes only how crowded the particles are. Only temperature changes their energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying enzymes are killed"}
Enzymes are not alive. At high temperatures they are **denatured**: their shape changes so that they stop working.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
For temperature, two points earn two marks: particles move faster so collide **more frequently**, **and** more of the collisions have energy **at least equal to the activation energy**.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Describe the effect" needs only the direction ("the rate increases"). "Explain" needs collision theory: particles per unit volume, frequency of collisions, activation energy.
:::

:::exam-tip
For a catalyst, write "provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy". It does not give particles more energy.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Rate increases with higher concentration, higher gas pressure, larger surface area, higher temperature, stirring and a catalyst.
- Supplement: particles must collide with energy at least equal to the activation energy; rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions.
- Concentration and pressure: more particles per unit volume, so more frequent collisions; surface area: more exposed particles.
- Temperature: more frequent collisions and a greater proportion of collisions with enough energy.
- A catalyst lowers the activation energy and is unchanged at the end; enzymes such as catalase are biological catalysts that are denatured by heat.
:::
