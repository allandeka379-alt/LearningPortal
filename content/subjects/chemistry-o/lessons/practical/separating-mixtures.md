## Introduction

Very few substances are found pure in nature. Rock salt bought in bulk for cattle contains sand and clay. Water from a borehole contains dissolved minerals, and river water carries mud. Chemists, water engineers and medicine makers all need ways of separating the substances in a mixture and purifying the one they want.

Because the substances in a mixture are not chemically combined, they can be separated by **physical methods** that use differences in their properties: whether they dissolve, the size of their particles and their boiling points. In this lesson you will learn the key terms and the main methods, and how to choose the right one.

You should already know the difference between a mixture and a compound.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Solvent, solute and solution"}
The **solvent** is the liquid that does the dissolving (often water). The **solute** is the substance that dissolves. Together they form a **solution**.
:::

:::definition{term="Saturated solution"}
A solution that contains the maximum concentration of solute that can dissolve in the solvent at a stated temperature.
:::

:::definition{term="Residue and filtrate"}
In filtration, the **residue** is the solid left on the filter paper and the **filtrate** is the liquid (or solution) that passes through.
:::

:::definition{term="Soluble and insoluble"}
A **soluble** substance dissolves in a particular solvent; an **insoluble** one does not. Salt is soluble in water; sand is insoluble.
:::

## Choosing a method

| You want to separate | Property used | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An insoluble solid from a liquid (sand from water) | The solid particles cannot pass through filter paper | **Filtration** |
| A dissolved solid from its solution (salt from salt water) | The solvent evaporates; the solute does not | **Evaporation** or **crystallisation** |
| The solvent from a solution (pure water from salt water) | Very different boiling points | **Simple distillation** |
| A soluble solid from an insoluble one (salt from sand) | Only one dissolves in the solvent | **Dissolve**, then **filter**, then **evaporate** |
| Liquids that mix completely (ethanol and water) | Different boiling points | **Fractional distillation** |
| Coloured substances dissolved together (dyes in ink) | Different solubility in a solvent | **Chromatography** |

The last two methods have lessons of their own. You may also meet simple methods such as using a **magnet** to pull iron filings out of a mixture.

## Filtration

A filter paper is folded into a cone and placed in a filter funnel resting in a conical flask. The mixture is poured in. The tiny holes in the paper let the liquid and any dissolved substances through, but not the larger solid particles.

- The solid left on the paper is the **residue**.
- The liquid collected in the flask is the **filtrate**.
- Washing the residue with a little distilled water removes any solution left on it.

![Filtration apparatus: a mixture of sand and salt solution is poured into a filter paper cone in a funnel; the insoluble sand stays on the paper as the residue and the salt solution runs through into the conical flask as the filtrate.](media:diagrams/separating-filtration-apparatus.png "Figure: filtration separates an insoluble solid (residue) from a liquid (filtrate)")

Filtration **cannot** remove a dissolved substance: dissolved salt particles pass straight through the paper. That is why filtered borehole water may still taste salty. Water works use beds of sand and gravel as giant filters to remove suspended mud, but not dissolved substances.

## Evaporation and crystallisation

To recover a dissolved solid, the solvent is removed.

- **Evaporation to dryness:** heat the solution in an evaporating basin until all the water has gone. It is quick, but it can make the solid spit, gives tiny crystals, and can decompose some solids.
- **Crystallisation:** heat the solution only until it is **saturated**. Test by dipping in a cold glass rod: if small crystals form on it, the **crystallisation point** has been reached. Leave the solution to cool. Most solids are less soluble in cold water, so crystals form. Filter them off, wash them with a little cold distilled water and dry them between filter papers or in a warm oven.

Crystallisation is used for salts that contain **water of crystallisation**, such as blue copper(II) sulfate. Heating them to dryness drives off this water and leaves a white powder instead of blue crystals.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: purifying rock salt]
Rock salt is a mixture of sodium chloride with sand and clay. Describe how to obtain pure, dry salt.
1. **Crush** the rock salt with a pestle and mortar, to increase the surface area so the salt dissolves faster.
2. Add **warm water** and **stir**: the sodium chloride dissolves, but the sand and clay do not.
3. **Filter**: the sand and clay stay on the paper as the residue; the salt solution passes through as the filtrate.
4. **Evaporate** the filtrate in an evaporating basin until it is saturated, then leave it to **crystallise**. (Sodium chloride does not decompose, so evaporating to dryness also works.)
5. Dry the crystals between filter papers or in a warm oven.
:::

The amount of solid that crystallises can be worked out from **solubility** data, given in grams of solute per $100\ \text{g}$ of water.

![Solubility curve of potassium nitrate: solubility rises from 13.3 g per 100 g of water at 0 degrees Celsius to 31.6 g at 20 degrees and 110 g at 60 degrees, so cooling a saturated solution from 60 to 20 degrees crystallises 78.4 g per 100 g of water.](media:diagrams/solubility-curve-potassium-nitrate.png "Figure: solubility of potassium nitrate in water against temperature")

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of crystals formed on cooling]
At $60\ ^\circ\text{C}$, $110\ \text{g}$ of potassium nitrate dissolves in $100\ \text{g}$ of water to give a saturated solution. At $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$ only $31.6\ \text{g}$ dissolves. The solution is cooled from $60\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$. What mass of crystals forms?
1. The water can hold $110\ \text{g}$ at $60\ ^\circ\text{C}$ but only $31.6\ \text{g}$ at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
2. The rest must come out of solution: $110 - 31.6 = 78.4\ \text{g}$ of crystals.
3. If the solution had contained only $50\ \text{g}$ of water, both masses would halve, giving $39.2\ \text{g}$.

:::

## Simple distillation

Simple distillation separates a **solvent** from a solution, for example pure water from sea water or salty borehole water.

The apparatus, described in words:

- a round-bottomed or distillation flask holding the solution and a few **anti-bumping granules** (for smooth boiling), heated from below;
- a **thermometer** with its bulb level with the **side arm**, so it reads the temperature of the vapour leaving the flask;
- a **Liebig condenser**: a tube surrounded by a water jacket, with cold water entering at the **bottom** and leaving at the **top**, so the jacket stays full of cold water;
- a beaker or conical flask to collect the **distillate**.

![Simple distillation apparatus: salt solution is heated in a flask with a thermometer bulb level with the side arm; steam passes into a Liebig condenser with cold water entering at the bottom and leaving at the top, and pure water (distillate) drips into a beaker while the salt stays in the flask.](media:diagrams/separating-simple-distillation.png "Figure: simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution")

The water boils, the steam passes into the condenser, cools and **condenses** back to liquid, and pure water drips into the collecting flask. The dissolved salt has a much higher boiling point, so it stays behind in the flask. The thermometer reads $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ while water distils. Distilled water is used for experiments and for topping up car batteries because it contains no dissolved impurities.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Filtering to remove a dissolved solid"}
Dissolved particles pass through filter paper. To get salt out of salt water you must evaporate or crystallise; to get the water out, distil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up residue and filtrate"}
**R**esidue **r**emains on the paper; the **filtrate** has been through the filter.
:::

:::mistake{title="Heating hydrated crystals to dryness"}
Blue copper(II) sulfate crystals must be made by crystallisation; heating to dryness leaves white anhydrous powder.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the thermometer in the liquid"}
In distillation the bulb goes level with the side arm, where the vapour leaves the flask.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "suggest a method" questions, look for the property that differs: **solubility** points to dissolving and filtering; **boiling point** points to distillation. Name the method and say which property it uses.
:::

:::exam-tip
When you **describe** a separation, give the steps in order and name the apparatus: "add water and stir; filter; heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin until crystals start to form; leave to cool; filter and dry the crystals."
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Solute + solvent = solution; a saturated solution holds the maximum amount of solute at that temperature.
- Filtration separates an insoluble solid (the residue) from a liquid (the filtrate).
- Evaporation or crystallisation recovers a dissolved solid; crystallisation gives larger, purer crystals and keeps water of crystallisation.
- Simple distillation recovers the solvent: thermometer at the side arm, cold water in at the bottom of the condenser.
- Choose a method from the property that differs: solubility, particle size or boiling point.
:::
