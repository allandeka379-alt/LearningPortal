## Introduction

Every result in chemistry starts with a measurement, and a result can never be better than the measurements behind it. Choosing a beaker when a pipette was needed, or reading a thermometer from the wrong angle, can make a careful experiment worthless. Practical papers (ZIMSEC Paper 3, Cambridge Papers 5 and 6) award many marks simply for naming the right apparatus, reading scales correctly and recording values to the right number of decimal places.

In this lesson you will meet the standard measuring instruments, learn how to read their scales, and learn how to choose between them. In examinations, scales are often shown as pictures; here they are described in words, which is also how you should think about them: find the value of one small division first.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Resolution (precision of an instrument)"}
The smallest change an instrument can show, usually the value of its smallest scale division (for an electronic instrument, its last digit).
:::

:::definition{term="Accuracy"}
How close a measured value is to the true value.
:::

:::definition{term="Meniscus"}
The curved surface of a liquid in a narrow tube. For water and solutions, read the **bottom** of the meniscus.
:::

:::definition{term="Parallax error"}
An error caused by reading a scale with the eye not level with the mark being read.
:::

## Choosing the right instrument

| Quantity | Instrument | Typical resolution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| time | stop-watch or stop-clock | a digital display shows 0.01 s, but hand timing is only good to about 0.5 s, so record times to the nearest 1 s |
| temperature | thermometer (or a digital temperature probe) | 1 °C (read to 0.5 °C), or 0.1 °C |
| mass | electronic balance | 0.01 g or 0.1 g |
| volume of a liquid, approximate | measuring cylinder | 1 cm³ for a 100 cm³ cylinder |
| volume of a liquid, variable and accurate | burette | 0.1 cm³ divisions, read to 0.05 cm³ |
| volume of a liquid, fixed and accurate | volumetric pipette; volumetric flask | one mark: e.g. 25.0 cm³; 250.0 cm³ |
| volume of a gas | gas syringe | 1 cm³ |
| length | ruler | 1 mm |

A beaker or conical flask has rough volume markings only: never use one to measure a volume in a quantitative experiment.

## Reading a scale

1. Find the value of **one small division**: (difference between two numbered marks) ÷ (number of divisions between them).
2. Count the divisions from the last numbered mark, noting which way the numbers increase.
3. Estimate to the nearest **half division** if the reading lies between two marks.
4. Record the value with its unit, to the precision of the instrument.

![Two scale readings worked out in the lesson: a thermometer with one small division equal to 1 degree Celsius whose liquid stops halfway between the third and fourth marks above 20 degrees, reading 23.5 degrees Celsius; and a measuring cylinder with five small divisions between each 10 cm3 mark, so one division is 2 cm3, with the bottom of the meniscus on the second small mark above 70, reading 74 cm3.](media:diagrams/reading-thermometer-measuring-cylinder.png "Figure: find the value of one division first, then count up from the last numbered mark")

:::worked-solution[Example 1: a thermometer described in words]
The marks labelled 20 °C and 30 °C have ten small divisions between them, and the top of the liquid column is halfway between the 3rd and 4th small marks above 20 °C.

1. One division $= (30 - 20) \div 10 = 1$ °C.
2. Three divisions above 20 °C is 23 °C; halfway to the next mark adds 0.5 °C.
3. Reading: 23.5 °C.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a measuring cylinder]
A 100 cm³ measuring cylinder has numbered marks every 10 cm³ with five small divisions between them. The bottom of the meniscus is on the 2nd small mark above 70 cm³.

1. One division $= 10 \div 5 = 2$ cm³.
2. Reading $= 70 + 2 \times 2 = 74$ cm³.
:::

Remember that a **burette** is numbered from the top downwards, so readings increase as you move down the scale.

## Using instruments correctly

- **Liquids:** place the measuring cylinder or burette on a level surface (or clamp it vertically) and read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
- **Balances:** check that the display reads zero before weighing, or press "tare" with the empty container on the pan. To weigh by difference, weigh the container with the solid, tip the solid out, and reweigh the container.
- **Thermometers:** stir the liquid, keep the bulb fully immersed and away from the bottom of the container, and wait for the reading to become steady.
- **Gas syringes:** check the plunger moves freely and starts at zero; the syringe must be large enough for the volume of gas expected.

## Advantages and disadvantages of methods

Examiners often ask you to compare two ways of doing the same job.

:::example{title="Measuring the volume of a gas"}
A **gas syringe** measures any gas directly to about 1 cm³, but holds only about 100 cm³ and is fragile. **Collecting the gas over water** in an inverted measuring cylinder is cheap and can collect larger volumes, but it cannot be used for gases that dissolve readily in water (ammonia, hydrogen chloride, and to some extent carbon dioxide), because some of the gas would dissolve instead of being measured.
:::

:::example{title="Measuring a volume of acid"}
For a titration, 25.0 cm³ must be measured accurately, so a volumetric pipette is used. For dissolving a base when making a salt, the volume only needs to be approximate, so a measuring cylinder is quicker and good enough.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Reading from above or below the mark"}
Looking down on a measuring cylinder makes the volume appear too large or too small. Bend down so that your eye is level with the meniscus.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming every small division is 1 unit"}
On many scales one division is 2 cm³, 0.5 °C or 0.1 cm³. Always work out the value of a division first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Recording with the wrong precision"}
A balance that reads to 0.01 g shows 4.50 g, not 4.5 g; a burette reading is 23.40 cm³, not 23.4 cm³. Do not invent extra figures either: a thermometer read to 0.5 °C gives 23.5 °C, not 23.50 °C.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a beaker as a measuring instrument"}
Beaker markings can be several cm³ out. Use a measuring cylinder, pipette or burette.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to "name the apparatus", give its full name: "volumetric pipette" or "gas syringe", not "tube" or "syringe thing".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked for an advantage of an instrument, link it to the measurement: "a burette measures to 0.05 cm³, so it is more accurate than a measuring cylinder", not just "it is better".
:::

:::exam-tip
In tables of readings, every value in a column should have the same number of decimal places, matching the instrument.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Use a stop-watch for time, a thermometer for temperature, a balance for mass, a gas syringe for gas volume and a ruler for length.
- For liquid volumes, choose a measuring cylinder (approximate), burette (variable, accurate) or pipette (fixed, accurate).
- Read scales by finding the value of one division, reading to the nearest half division and reading the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
- Record measurements to the resolution of the instrument, with units.
- Compare methods by their accuracy, the size of quantity they can handle and any limitation, such as gases dissolving in water.
:::
