## Introduction

A pharmacist in Bulawayo receiving a batch of paracetamol, a food company checking a delivery of sugar, and a forensic scientist testing a seized powder all ask the same question: is this substance pure? A quick and cheap answer comes from measuring its melting point (for a solid) or boiling point (for a liquid) and comparing it with the value in a data book.

This lesson covers Cambridge 12.4 (identifying substances and judging purity from melting and boiling point data) and the ZIMSEC Form 4 objective on the effects of impurities, which links to heating and cooling curves. You should already know what happens to particles when a substance melts and boils.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pure substance"}
A single element or a single compound with nothing else mixed with it.
:::

:::definition{term="Impurity"}
An unwanted substance mixed with the substance you want, even in a small amount.
:::

:::definition{term="Melting point and boiling point"}
The temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid, and the temperature at which a liquid boils and turns into a gas throughout. Data-book values are for pure substances at standard atmospheric pressure.
:::

:::keyfact{title="The purity rules"}
- A **pure** substance melts and boils at **sharp, fixed** temperatures.
- **Impurities lower the melting point** and make the solid melt over a **range** of temperatures.
- **Impurities raise the boiling point** (and the liquid may boil over a range).
:::

## Using the rules with data

Measured values are compared with data-book values. Two things matter: **where** the substance melts or boils, and **how sharply**.

:::example{title="Comparing two samples of a solid"}
The data-book melting point of pure aspirin is 135 °C.

| sample | melting range / °C |
| --- | --- |
| A | 134.5–135.0 |
| B | 126–131 |

Sample A melts sharply at the data-book value, so it is pure (or very nearly). Sample B melts **below** 135 °C **and** over a **5 °C range**, so it is impure.
:::

:::example{title="Comparing liquids"}
Pure water boils at 100 °C at standard pressure. A sample that boils from 102 °C to 104 °C contains a dissolved impurity, for example salt. Salt water also freezes below 0 °C.
:::

:::example{title="Identifying a substance"}
An unknown colourless liquid boils sharply at 78 °C. A data book lists ethanol at 78 °C and water at 100 °C, so the liquid is probably pure ethanol. A matching value is strong evidence, but not proof: a different substance could have the same boiling point, so other tests may be needed.
:::

## Purity and heating and cooling curves

If a pure solid is heated steadily and its temperature recorded, the graph has a **flat section** at the melting point: the temperature stays constant while the solid melts, because the energy supplied is used to overcome the forces between particles. A cooling liquid shows a flat section at the same temperature while it freezes.

An impure solid shows no sharp flat section. Instead the curve **slopes gently** over a range of temperatures, starting below the pure substance's melting point.

## Pressure matters for boiling points

Boiling points depend on atmospheric pressure. Data books give values at sea-level pressure. Harare is about 1500 m above sea level, where the air pressure is lower, so pure water there boils at about 95 °C. When you compare a measured boiling point with a data-book value, remember to consider the pressure. Melting points are hardly affected by pressure.

## Why purity matters

- **Medicines and drugs:** impurities could be harmful or toxic, and they change the dose a patient receives.
- **Foods and drinks:** impurities could make food unsafe or change its taste and quality.
- **Industry:** impurities can poison catalysts or spoil products.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying impurities raise the melting point"}
Impurities **lower** the melting point and **raise** the boiling point. A common way to remember it: impurities make it harder to stay solid and harder to boil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Looking only at the value, not the range"}
A sample melting between 115 °C and 121 °C is impure even though the range reaches close to a data-book value of 122 °C. A pure sample melts over 1 °C or less.
:::

:::mistake{title="Treating a match as proof"}
A sharp melting point equal to the data-book value shows the sample is probably pure and probably that substance. It is not absolute proof of identity.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In "is it pure?" questions, use both pieces of evidence: "it melts below the data-book value **and** over a range of 5 °C, so it is impure".
:::

:::exam-tip
Quote the data you are using, with units, for example "the boiling point of 102–104 °C is above 100 °C".
:::

:::exam-tip
If asked how to make an impure solid purer, describe recrystallisation, then say you would check that the melting point has become sharp and equal to the data-book value.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Pure substances melt and boil at sharp, fixed temperatures that match data-book values.
- Impurities lower the melting point and spread it over a range; they raise the boiling point.
- Compare both the temperature and the sharpness of a measured melting or boiling point with data.
- A pure substance shows a flat section on a heating or cooling curve; an impure one shows a gradual slope.
- Purity matters in medicines, foods and industry; boiling points also depend on pressure.
:::
