## Introduction

In the Lowveld, sugar-cane juice is fermented to make ethanol, which is blended with petrol to make fuel. The fermented liquid is mostly water, so the ethanol has to be separated from it. Ethanol and water mix completely and their boiling points are only $22\ ^\circ\text{C}$ apart, so simple distillation is not good enough. The answer is **fractional distillation**, the same method used to split crude oil into petrol, diesel and other fractions and to separate liquid air into nitrogen, oxygen and argon.

In this lesson you will learn how fractional distillation works and how to interpret the thermometer readings. ZIMSEC students will also meet **steam distillation**, used to extract oils from plants.

You should already know how simple distillation works.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Miscible and immiscible liquids"}
**Miscible** liquids mix completely to form one layer (ethanol and water). **Immiscible** liquids do not mix and form separate layers (oil and water).
:::

:::definition{term="Fractional distillation"}
A method of separating a mixture of miscible liquids with different boiling points, using a fractionating column between the flask and the condenser.
:::

:::definition{term="Fraction"}
A portion of the distillate collected over a particular range of temperature.
:::

## The apparatus

Described in words, from the bottom up:

- a **round-bottomed flask** containing the mixture and a few anti-bumping granules, heated gently by an electric heater (ethanol is **flammable**, so naked flames are avoided where possible);
- a **fractionating column**: a vertical glass tube packed with glass beads (or rings), fitted on top of the flask;
- a **thermometer** at the top of the column, with its bulb level with the side arm leading to the condenser;
- a **Liebig condenser** with cold water entering at the bottom and leaving at the top;
- a conical flask (the **receiver**) to collect each fraction, changed when the temperature changes.

## How the fractionating column works

When the mixture boils, the vapour contains both liquids, but it is richer in the liquid with the **lower boiling point** (the more volatile one). As the vapour rises, the column gets **cooler towards the top**.

1. The vapour condenses on the cool glass beads, then is re-heated by more hot vapour from below and evaporates again.
2. Each time this happens, the vapour becomes richer in the lower-boiling liquid.
3. The liquid with the **higher boiling point** (water, $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$) condenses lower down and runs back into the flask.
4. Almost pure vapour of the **lower-boiling** liquid (ethanol, $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$) reaches the top, passes into the condenser and is collected.

The glass beads give a very large surface area, so the vapour condenses and evaporates many times on the way up. That is what makes the separation much better than in simple distillation.

:::keyfact{title="Reading the thermometer"}
- While ethanol distils, the thermometer stays at about $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
- When almost all the ethanol has gone, the temperature **rises**. Change the receiver.
- When the thermometer reads $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, water is distilling.
:::

:::note{title="How pure is the ethanol?"}
Some water vapour always travels up with the ethanol, so the first fraction is mostly ethanol, not 100% ethanol. Even a very efficient column cannot give more than about 96% ethanol from a water mixture.
:::

:::note{title="Thermometer readings at high altitude"}
Boiling points fall when the air pressure is lower. In Harare, about 1 500 m above sea level, ethanol distils at about $74\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and water at about $95\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Examination questions use the sea-level values, $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, unless they tell you otherwise.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: interpreting a distillation of ethanol and water]
A mixture of ethanol and water is fractionally distilled. The thermometer reads $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for the first $30\ \text{cm}^3$ of distillate, then rises quickly to $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and stays there. Explain these readings.
1. The first fraction distils at $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the boiling point of ethanol: it is (mostly) ethanol.
2. Ethanol distils first because it has the lower boiling point; the water condenses in the column and runs back.
3. Once nearly all the ethanol has distilled, only water is left to boil, so the temperature rises.
4. At $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ water distils. The receiver should be changed as soon as the temperature starts to rise, to keep the two fractions separate.
:::

## Mixtures of more than two liquids

The liquids distil in order of boiling point, **lowest first**.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: predicting the order of the fractions]
A mixture contains methanol (boiling point $65\ ^\circ\text{C}$), ethanol ($78\ ^\circ\text{C}$) and water ($100\ ^\circ\text{C}$). Predict the order in which they distil and the thermometer readings.
1. List the boiling points from lowest to highest: methanol $65$, ethanol $78$, water $100$.
2. First fraction: methanol, collected while the thermometer reads about $65\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
3. Second fraction: ethanol, at about $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
4. Third fraction: water, at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
5. Between fractions the temperature rises and the receiver is changed.
:::

The same idea is used on a huge scale. In an oil refinery, crude oil is separated into fractions in a tall fractionating column, with the lightest, lowest-boiling fractions (refinery gas, petrol) leaving at the top. Liquid air is separated in the same way: nitrogen (boiling point $-196\ ^\circ\text{C}$) boils off before argon ($-186\ ^\circ\text{C}$) and oxygen ($-183\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

## Steam distillation (ZIMSEC)

Many plants contain fragrant **essential oils**: eucalyptus leaves, lemon grass and orange peel, for example. These oils boil at high temperatures and would **decompose** (break down) if heated to their own boiling points. They can be extracted gently by **steam distillation**.

1. Steam from a separate boiling flask is passed through the crushed plant material.
2. The oil vaporises and is carried out with the steam at a temperature **below** $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$, far below the oil's own boiling point, so it is not decomposed.
3. The mixture of steam and oil vapour is condensed in a Liebig condenser.
4. Oil and water are **immiscible**, so the distillate forms **two layers**. They are separated with a **separating funnel**.

Steam-distilled oils are used in perfumes, flavourings, medicines (for example eucalyptus oil in cough remedies) and insect repellents.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the higher-boiling liquid distils first"}
The liquid with the **lower** boiling point (the more volatile one) reaches the top of the column first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing the column as a filter"}
The beads do not trap anything. They give a large surface on which vapour condenses and re-evaporates many times.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using simple distillation for close boiling points"}
Simple distillation is fine for water from salt water ($100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ against over $1400\ ^\circ\text{C}$). For liquids whose boiling points are close, use a fractionating column.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting to change the receiver"}
If the receiver is not changed when the temperature rises, the fractions mix again.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When explaining the separation, use comparative words: the column is **hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top**; the liquid with the **lower boiling point** reaches the top first; the **higher-boiling** liquid condenses and runs back.
:::

:::exam-tip
Label diagrams carefully: the thermometer bulb is level with the side arm, the column is between the flask and the condenser, and water enters the condenser at the bottom.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Fractional distillation separates miscible liquids with different boiling points, such as ethanol and water.
- The fractionating column is hotter at the bottom and cooler at the top; repeated condensation and evaporation on the beads sends the lower-boiling liquid to the top first.
- The thermometer (bulb at the side arm) stays at one boiling point while each fraction distils; change the receiver when it rises.
- The method is used for crude oil, liquid air and ethanol from fermentation.
- **ZIMSEC:** steam distillation extracts heat-sensitive plant oils below 100 °C; the oil and water layers are separated with a separating funnel.
:::
