## Introduction

Is the bright orange colouring in a freezit a permitted food dye or a banned one? Was a signature on a document written with the suspect's pen? Is a batch of tablets pure? Laboratories answer questions like these with **chromatography**, a method that separates tiny amounts of dissolved substances so that each one can be identified.

In this lesson you will learn how paper chromatography works, how to set it up so that it gives reliable results, and how to read a chromatogram. Extended (Supplement) candidates also need **Rf values** and **locating agents**; ZIMSEC candidates also meet **thin-layer chromatography**.

You should already know the meaning of solvent, solute and soluble.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Chromatography"}
A method of separating the soluble substances in a mixture as a solvent moves through paper (or a thin layer of solid), carrying the substances different distances.
:::

:::definition{term="Chromatogram"}
The dried paper (or plate) showing the separated spots after chromatography.
:::

:::definition{term="Base line and solvent front"}
The **base line** is the pencil line on which the samples are spotted. The **solvent front** is the furthest point the solvent reaches up the paper.
:::

## Setting up paper chromatography

1. Draw a **pencil** line about $1.5$ to $2\ \text{cm}$ above the bottom of the chromatography paper. Pencil (graphite) does not dissolve in the solvent; ink would dissolve and separate too.
2. Use a capillary tube to put a **small, concentrated spot** of each sample on the line, including known substances for comparison. Let each spot dry and add more to concentrate it.
3. Stand the paper in a beaker containing a little solvent (water, ethanol or another suitable solvent). The solvent level must be **below the base line**, or the spots would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker.
4. Cover the beaker with a **lid**, so the air inside is saturated with solvent vapour and the solvent does not evaporate from the paper.
5. The solvent soaks up the paper, carrying the dissolved substances with it. Remove the paper before the solvent reaches the top, **mark the solvent front** with a pencil and let the paper dry.

## Why the substances separate

Each substance in the mixture has a different **solubility** in the solvent and a different attraction to the paper. A substance that is very soluble in the solvent spends more time dissolved in it and travels further up the paper. A substance that is strongly attracted to the paper travels a shorter distance. A substance that is **insoluble** in the solvent stays on the base line; a different solvent must be used to separate it.

## Interpreting a chromatogram

:::keyfact{title="What a chromatogram tells you"}
- **One spot**: the sample is probably **pure** (a single substance).
- **Several spots**: the sample is a **mixture** of at least that many soluble substances.
- A spot at the **same height** as a known substance, run on the same paper with the same solvent, is probably **that substance**.
- A spot left **on the base line** is a substance that is insoluble in that solvent.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: which pen was used?]
Ink from a forged note (N) and ink from three pens, P, Q and R, are run side by side on the same paper. The heights of the spots above the base line are:

| Sample | Heights of spots / cm |
| --- | --- |
| N | 1.8, 4.5 |
| P | 1.8, 3.2 |
| Q | 1.8, 4.5 |
| R | 2.6, 4.5, 5.9 |

1. N gives two spots, so its ink contains at least two dyes.
2. Compare each pen with N: Q has spots at exactly the same heights (1.8 cm and 4.5 cm) and no others.
3. P has a 3.2 cm spot that N does not have, and lacks N's 4.5 cm spot; R has spots N does not have.
4. Conclusion: the ink on the note matches pen Q.
:::

## Rf values (Supplement)

The distance a spot moves depends on how far the solvent travels, so chemists compare substances using the **retention factor**, Rf:

$$
R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the substance}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}
$$

Both distances are measured **from the base line**, to the **centre** of the spot and to the solvent front. Because a substance can never move further than the solvent, an Rf value is always **between 0 and 1**, and it has **no units**. For a given solvent and paper, each substance has its own Rf value, so Rf values can be compared with data tables.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: calculating and using Rf]
The solvent front is $8.0\ \text{cm}$ above the base line. The centre of a spot is $3.6\ \text{cm}$ above the base line.
1. $R_f = \dfrac{3.6}{8.0} = 0.45$.
2. A data table for the same solvent gives Rf values of 0.45 for dye A and 0.70 for dye B, so the spot is probably dye A.
3. Working backwards: on the same chromatogram, dye B would be $0.70 \times 8.0 = 5.6\ \text{cm}$ above the base line.
:::

## Colourless substances: locating agents (Supplement)

Many substances, such as amino acids and sugars, are **colourless**, so their spots cannot be seen. After the chromatogram has dried, it is sprayed with a **locating agent**, a chemical that reacts with the substances to form coloured spots. (Some substances can instead be seen under ultraviolet light.) The positions can then be measured and Rf values calculated as usual. For example, a protein can be broken down into its amino acids, which are separated and then made visible with a locating agent.

## Thin-layer chromatography (ZIMSEC)

In **thin-layer chromatography (TLC)** the paper is replaced by a glass, plastic or aluminium plate coated with a thin layer of a solid such as **silica gel**. It is run in the same way: pencil base line, small spots, solvent below the line, a lid, then mark the solvent front. TLC is **faster**, gives **sharper, better-separated spots** and works with a wider range of solvents, so it is widely used to check the purity of medicines. Rf values are calculated in exactly the same way.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Drawing the base line in ink"}
Ink dissolves in the solvent and runs up the paper, adding extra spots. Always use pencil.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting the solvent above the base line"}
The samples would dissolve into the solvent in the beaker instead of travelling up the paper.
:::

:::mistake{title="Measuring from the bottom of the paper"}
Rf distances are measured from the **base line**. Measuring from the edge of the paper gives a wrong Rf value.
:::

:::mistake{title="An Rf value bigger than 1"}
If you get more than 1, you have divided the wrong way round: the substance's distance goes on top.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked how a chromatogram shows that a substance is pure, write "it gives only **one** spot". To show two samples contain the same substance, say the spots are at the **same height** (same Rf) **in the same solvent**.
:::

:::exam-tip
Give Rf values as decimals to two significant figures or two decimal places, **without units**, and show the division.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Paper chromatography separates soluble substances by how far the solvent carries them up the paper.
- Draw the base line in pencil, keep the solvent below it, use a lid and mark the solvent front.
- One spot means a pure substance; matching heights with a known substance identify it.
- **Supplement:** Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent (between 0 and 1, no units); locating agents reveal colourless substances.
- **ZIMSEC:** TLC uses a thin layer of silica gel on a plate and gives faster, sharper separations.
:::
