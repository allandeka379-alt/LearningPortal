## Introduction

When a district water laboratory tests a new borehole, it checks for negative ions as well as metals. Too much chloride makes water taste salty, sulfate in large amounts can upset the stomach, and nitrate can seep into groundwater from fertilisers and pit latrines. Each of these anions can be detected with a simple test-tube reaction.

The tests in this lesson identify the **anion** (negative ion) in a compound; the tests for cations identify the positive ion. Together they let you name an unknown salt. Most anion tests produce either a gas or a precipitate, so you should already know the solubility rules and the tests for carbon dioxide and ammonia.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anion"}
A negative ion, such as $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ or $\ce{CO3^2-}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Acidify"}
To add a little dilute acid to a solution before the test reagent, to remove ions that would otherwise give a misleading result.
:::

:::definition{term="Halide ion"}
An ion of a Group VII element: chloride $\ce{Cl-}$, bromide $\ce{Br-}$ or iodide $\ce{I-}$.
:::

## The tests

| Anion | Test | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| carbonate, $\ce{CO3^2-}$ | add dilute acid, then test the gas with limewater | effervescence; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky |
| chloride, $\ce{Cl-}$ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | white precipitate |
| bromide, $\ce{Br-}$ (Cambridge) | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | cream precipitate |
| iodide, $\ce{I-}$ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous silver nitrate | yellow precipitate |
| nitrate, $\ce{NO3-}$ | add aqueous sodium hydroxide and a piece of aluminium foil; warm carefully | ammonia given off; it turns damp red litmus paper blue |
| sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$ | acidify with dilute nitric acid, then add aqueous barium nitrate | white precipitate |
| sulfite, $\ce{SO3^2-}$ (Cambridge) | add a small volume of acidified aqueous potassium manganate(VII) | the solution changes from purple to colourless |

:::note{title="Another sulfate test you may meet (ZIMSEC)"}
Many schools test for sulfate by acidifying with dilute **hydrochloric** acid and adding aqueous **barium chloride**. The result is the same white precipitate of barium sulfate. Either pair of reagents is accepted, as long as the acid does not contain sulfate ions.
:::

## Why acidify first?

Carbonate ions also form precipitates with silver ions and with barium ions. Adding dilute nitric acid first destroys any carbonate (it fizzes away as carbon dioxide), so a precipitate can only come from the halide or sulfate ion being tested.

The choice of acid matters:

- In a **halide** test, never acidify with hydrochloric acid: it contains chloride ions, which would give a white precipitate with silver nitrate every time.
- In a **sulfate** test, never acidify with sulfuric acid: it contains sulfate ions.
- Nitric acid is safe for both tests, because all nitrates are soluble.

## The nitrate test

Nitrate ions give no precipitates, because all nitrates are soluble. Instead, aluminium reduces nitrate ions to ammonia in the alkaline mixture. Warm gently and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube: it turns blue.

Ammonium ions also give ammonia when warmed with sodium hydroxide. So first warm the solution with sodium hydroxide **alone**. If ammonia appears before any aluminium is added, the compound contains ammonium ions, and the test must be read with care.

## Ionic equations (Supplement)

$$
\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{CO3^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) -> CO2(g) + H2O(l)}
$$

Silver bromide and silver iodide form in the same way as silver chloride. The carbonate equation is for a carbonate in solution. For an insoluble solid carbonate, write the whole formula of the solid, for example $\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2H+(aq) -> Ca^2+(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: which anion?"}
A colourless solution S is tested.

- Dilute nitric acid, then aqueous silver nitrate: white precipitate.
- Dilute nitric acid, then aqueous barium nitrate: no change.

1. The white precipitate with acidified silver nitrate shows a chloride.
2. No precipitate with acidified barium nitrate shows there is no sulfate.
3. S contains chloride ions, $\ce{Cl-}$.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: a test that went wrong"}
Sipho acidifies a solution of sodium nitrate with dilute **hydrochloric** acid and adds silver nitrate. A white precipitate forms, and he concludes that the solution contains chloride ions.

1. Sodium nitrate contains no chloride.
2. The chloride ions came from the hydrochloric acid he added, forming silver chloride.
3. He should have acidified with dilute **nitric** acid.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: naming an unknown salt"}
A green solution gives a green precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide (insoluble in excess, turning brown on standing). With dilute nitric acid and aqueous barium nitrate it gives a white precipitate.

1. The cation is iron(II), $\ce{Fe^2+}$.
2. The anion is sulfate, $\ce{SO4^2-}$.
3. The salt is iron(II) sulfate, $\ce{FeSO4}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Acidifying with the wrong acid"}
Hydrochloric acid in a chloride test, or sulfuric acid in a sulfate test, adds the very ion you are testing for.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying 'gas given off' without a test"}
For a carbonate, say that the gas turns limewater milky; for a nitrate, that the gas turns damp red litmus blue.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the silver halide colours"}
Chloride white, bromide cream, iodide yellow. The colours get darker down Group VII.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Give both parts of a test: the reagent(s) **and** the result. "Add silver nitrate" is incomplete; "acidify with dilute nitric acid, add aqueous silver nitrate: white precipitate" earns full marks.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "identify the salt" questions, use the cation tests and the anion tests separately, then combine the names: iron(II) + sulfate = iron(II) sulfate.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Carbonate: fizzes with dilute acid; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky.
- Halides: dilute nitric acid then silver nitrate: chloride white, bromide cream (Cambridge), iodide yellow.
- Sulfate: dilute nitric acid then barium nitrate (or hydrochloric acid then barium chloride): white precipitate.
- Nitrate: sodium hydroxide and aluminium foil, warm: ammonia (damp red litmus turns blue).
- Sulfite (Cambridge): acidified potassium manganate(VII) changes from purple to colourless.
:::
