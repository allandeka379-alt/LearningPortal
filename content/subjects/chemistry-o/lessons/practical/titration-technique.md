## Introduction

Titrations appear again and again in practical examinations. In the ZIMSEC practical paper and in Cambridge Paper 5, a well-performed titration earns marks for accurate readings, a neat table and concordant results, before any calculation begins; Cambridge Paper 6 tests the same skills on paper, with burette readings shown in diagrams. The technique is not difficult, but every step has a reason, and small slips, such as rinsing the burette with water or reading the top of the meniscus, cost marks.

This lesson describes the technique for both boards. The calculations that follow a titration are covered in the lesson on titration calculations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Burette"}
A long graduated tube with a tap, used to add a variable, accurately measured volume of solution. It is read to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
:::

:::definition{term="Volumetric pipette"}
A pipette with a single mark that delivers one fixed volume, such as 25.0 cm³, very accurately. It is always filled with a pipette filler, never by mouth.
:::

:::definition{term="Indicator"}
A substance that changes colour at a particular pH, used to show when the acid and alkali have exactly reacted.
:::

:::definition{term="End-point"}
The point at which the indicator just changes colour permanently, showing that the reaction is complete.
:::

## Preparing the apparatus

| Apparatus | Rinse with | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| burette | a little of the solution it will hold (e.g. the acid) | water left inside would dilute the solution |
| pipette | a little of the solution it will measure (e.g. the alkali) | water left inside would dilute the solution |
| conical flask | distilled water only | extra water does not change the amount (moles) of alkali pipetted into it |

Fill the burette using a funnel, with the burette below eye level, then **remove the funnel** (drips from it would change the reading). Run some solution through the tap so that the jet below the tap is full and contains **no air bubble**.

## Carrying out the titration

1. Pipette 25.0 cm³ of the alkali into a conical flask and add two or three drops of indicator.
2. Stand the flask on a **white tile** so that the colour change is easy to see.
3. Read the burette and record the **initial reading**.
4. Add the acid while swirling the flask. Do a quick **rough titration** first to find the approximate end-point.
5. Repeat accurately: add acid quickly to about 1 cm³ before the rough end-point, then **drop by drop**, swirling, until one drop makes the colour change permanently.
6. Record the **final reading**. Titre $=$ final reading $-$ initial reading.
7. Repeat until you have at least two **concordant** titres (within 0.10 cm³ of each other).

## Choosing an indicator

| Indicator | Colour in acid | Colour in alkali |
| --- | --- | --- |
| methyl orange | red | yellow (orange at the end-point) |
| thymolphthalein | colourless | blue |
| phenolphthalein | colourless | pink |

Universal indicator is **not** used: it passes gradually through many colours, so there is no sharp end-point.

## Reading the burette

The burette scale reads **downwards**, with 0.00 near the top. Read the **bottom of the meniscus** with your eye level with it, to avoid parallax error. Each small division is 0.10 cm³, and you estimate half a division, so every reading ends in 0 or 5 in the second decimal place.

:::example{title="Reading a scale described in words"}
The bottom of the meniscus lies exactly on the third small mark **below** the line labelled 23. Because the numbers increase downwards, the reading is $23 + 0.30 = 23.30$ cm³. If it lay halfway between the third and fourth marks, the reading would be 23.35 cm³.
:::

## Recording results

Use a table with units in the headings and every reading to two decimal places:

| | rough | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| --- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| final reading / cm³ | 26.10 | 24.85 | 25.40 | 24.95 |
| initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 0.70 | 1.15 | 0.80 |
| titre / cm³ | 26.10 | 24.15 | 24.25 | 24.15 |

Titres 1, 2 and 3 agree within 0.10 cm³, so all three are concordant; tick them and calculate their mean, $(24.15 + 24.25 + 24.15) \div 3 = 24.18$ cm³ (to 2 decimal places).

## Making a salt by titration

To make a pure soluble salt such as sodium chloride from an acid and an alkali, first titrate with an indicator to find the volume of acid needed. Then repeat with **the same volumes but no indicator**, so that the salt is not contaminated. Evaporate the solution to the crystallisation point and leave it to crystallise.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Rinsing the burette with water"}
Water left in the burette dilutes the acid, so a larger titre is needed. Rinse with the acid itself.
:::

:::mistake{title="Reading the top of the meniscus or from an angle"}
Always read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level. Looking from above or below gives a parallax error.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing 23.3 instead of 23.30"}
Burette readings are recorded to two decimal places. "23.3" suggests the reading was only taken to 0.1 cm³ and loses the precision mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Overshooting the end-point"}
Adding acid too fast near the end-point gives a titre that is too large. Add the last part drop by drop and discard any overshot result.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked why a step is done, give the reason in terms of accuracy: "the white tile makes the colour change easier to see", "rinsing with the acid prevents it from being diluted".
:::

:::exam-tip
Describe colour changes with both colours, in order: "yellow to orange" for methyl orange when acid is added to alkali.
:::

:::exam-tip
Before you calculate a mean, check which titres are concordant and say so. Never include the rough titre.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A pipette measures a fixed volume of one solution; a burette adds the other and is read to 0.05 cm³.
- Rinse the burette and pipette with their own solutions; rinse the flask with distilled water only.
- Use a suitable indicator (methyl orange, thymolphthalein or phenolphthalein), a white tile and dropwise addition near the end-point.
- Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level and record readings to two decimal places.
- Repeat until titres are concordant (within 0.10 cm³) and average only those.
:::
