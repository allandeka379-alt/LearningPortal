## Introduction

An experiment is only as good as the way its results are recorded and understood. In practical papers, many marks come after the practical work is finished: completing a table, plotting a graph, reading a value from it, spotting an anomaly, explaining an error and suggesting an improvement. These skills are the same whether you are timing a reaction in a Harare school laboratory or analysing borehole water for a Matabeleland project.

In this lesson graphs are described as tables of data and in words, which is exactly what you must be able to turn into a sketch or a conclusion.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Anomalous result"}
A result that does not fit the pattern of the other results. It is left out when calculating a mean or drawing a line of best fit, and its cause should be suggested.
:::

:::definition{term="Line of best fit"}
A straight line or smooth curve drawn through or close to as many points as possible, with points scattered evenly on both sides. It is not drawn dot to dot.
:::

:::definition{term="Interpolation and extrapolation"}
Interpolation is reading a value from a graph **between** the plotted points; extrapolation is extending the line **beyond** the plotted points to estimate a value.
:::

:::definition{term="Gradient"}
The steepness of a line: change in the $y$ quantity divided by the change in the $x$ quantity. On a graph of volume of gas against time, the gradient is the rate of reaction.
:::

## Recording results in tables

- Put the independent variable in the first column and the dependent variable(s) in the next columns.
- Head each column with the **quantity and its unit**, separated by a solidus: "time / s", "volume of gas / cm³". Do not write units in the body of the table.
- Record every value in a column to the **same number of decimal places**, matching the instrument.
- Include columns for repeats and the mean where needed.

## Plotting graphs

1. Put the independent variable on the $x$-axis and the dependent variable on the $y$-axis.
2. Label each axis with quantity and unit.
3. Choose scales that use **more than half** of the grid in each direction and go up in easy steps (1, 2, 5 or 10 per square).
4. Plot each point accurately with a small cross.
5. Draw a straight line or a smooth curve of best fit, ignoring anomalous points.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: describing a volume–time graph]
Magnesium ribbon reacts with excess acid. The volume of hydrogen is recorded every 20 s.

| time / s | 0 | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| volume of gas / cm³ | 0 | 28 | 48 | 62 | 70 | 72 | 72 |

1. Plotted, these points give a curve that starts steeply and gradually levels off at 72 cm³.
2. The reaction is fastest at the start: 28 cm³ in the first 20 s, a mean rate of $28 \div 20 = 1.4$ cm³/s.
3. The curve is flat from 100 s, so the reaction has stopped: the magnesium is used up.
4. Mean rate over the first 60 s $= 62 \div 60 = 1.03$ cm³/s.
:::

## Gradients and tangents

For a **straight line**, choose two points far apart on the line (not data points that are off the line) and calculate gradient $= \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}$, with units.

For a **curve**, the gradient changes. To find it at one particular point, draw a **tangent**: a straight line that touches the curve at that point without crossing it, then find the gradient of the tangent. Tangents are required for **Cambridge Extended** candidates only.

## Anomalies, means and errors

:::worked-solution[Example 2: a mean with an anomaly]
Four repeat measurements of a temperature rise are 6.4, 6.6, 4.1 and 6.5 °C.

1. 4.1 °C is far from the others: it is anomalous (perhaps the thermometer was read too early, or heat was lost).
2. Mean of the others $= (6.4 + 6.6 + 6.5) \div 3 = 6.5$ °C.
:::

When you **evaluate** an experiment, link each source of error to its effect and to an improvement:

| Source of error | Effect | Improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| heat lost to the surroundings | temperature change too small | insulate (polystyrene cup with a lid) |
| gas escapes before the bung is fitted | volume too small | add the solid from a small tube inside the flask, or use a divided flask |
| reaction time when using a stop-watch | times slightly wrong | use a light sensor or data logger, or time longer intervals |
| only one measurement at each value | anomalies not spotted | repeat and take a mean |

## Drawing conclusions

A conclusion describes the trend **and** quotes data, then explains it using chemistry: "As the concentration doubled from 0.5 to 1.0 mol/dm³, the time halved, so the rate is proportional to concentration, because there are twice as many acid particles to collide."

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Units in the table body"}
Writing "25 s" in every cell instead of "time / s" in the heading costs the table mark.
:::

:::mistake{title="Dot-to-dot lines"}
Joining each point to the next with a ruler is not a line of best fit. Draw one smooth line or curve.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a data point that is off the line for a gradient"}
Read both points for a gradient from the line itself, and use a large triangle to reduce reading errors.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling every unexpected result an 'error'"}
Name the specific source: "heat was lost through the sides of the cup", not "human error".
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe the trend" needs words and numbers: "the volume increases quickly at first, then more slowly, and stays at 72 cm³ after 100 s".
:::

:::exam-tip
When asked to identify an anomalous point, give its values and say why it is anomalous ("too far below the curve"), then suggest a specific cause.
:::

:::exam-tip
For an improvement, say exactly what you would change and why it reduces the error. "Use a lid on the cup to reduce heat loss" beats "be more careful".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Tables: independent variable first, headings as quantity / unit, consistent decimal places.
- Graphs: labelled axes with units, scales using over half the grid, accurate points, a line or smooth curve of best fit.
- Gradient = change in $y$ ÷ change in $x$; for a curve (Extended), draw a tangent first.
- Identify anomalies, leave them out of means and lines, and suggest specific causes.
- Link each source of error to its effect and an improvement, and support conclusions with data.
:::
