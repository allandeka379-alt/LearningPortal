## Introduction

Laboratories at mines, water works and fertiliser factories in Zimbabwe check every day which metals are present in a sample. A borehole water sample that leaves brown stains might contain iron; a fertiliser label claiming ammonium must be checked. Modern instruments do much of this work, but the simple chemical tests in this lesson are still used, and they are a favourite of practical papers.

The tests work because most metal hydroxides are **insoluble**. Adding hydroxide ions to a solution of a metal salt produces a precipitate whose colour, and whether it dissolves in more reagent, identifies the metal ion. You should already know the solubility rules and how to write ionic equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Cation"}
A positive ion, such as $\ce{Cu^2+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ or $\ce{NH4+}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Qualitative analysis"}
Finding out **which** substances or ions are present in a sample, rather than how much.
:::

:::definition{term="Soluble in excess"}
The precipitate that formed with a few drops of reagent disappears when more reagent is added, leaving a solution.
:::

## How to carry out the tests

1. Put about $2\ \text{cm}^3$ of the solution in a test tube.
2. Add aqueous sodium hydroxide **a few drops at a time**, shaking. Note the colour of any precipitate.
3. Keep adding the reagent until it is in **excess** (several cm³ more), shaking. Note whether the precipitate dissolves.
4. Repeat steps 1–3 on a fresh sample using aqueous ammonia.

## The results

| Cation | Aqueous sodium hydroxide | Aqueous ammonia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| aluminium, $\ce{Al^3+}$ | white precipitate, soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| ammonium, $\ce{NH4+}$ | ammonia gas produced on warming | – |
| chromium(III), $\ce{Cr^3+}$ | green precipitate, soluble in excess | green precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| copper(II), $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | light blue precipitate, insoluble in excess | light blue precipitate, soluble in excess, giving a dark blue solution |
| iron(II), $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | green precipitate, insoluble in excess; turns brown near the surface on standing | green precipitate, insoluble in excess; turns brown near the surface on standing |
| iron(III), $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | red-brown precipitate, insoluble in excess | red-brown precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| zinc, $\ce{Zn^2+}$ | white precipitate, soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution | white precipitate, soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution |
| calcium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (Cambridge) | white precipitate, insoluble in excess | no precipitate or a very slight white precipitate |
| magnesium, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ (ZIMSEC) | white precipitate, insoluble in excess | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| lead(II), $\ce{Pb^2+}$ (ZIMSEC) | white precipitate, soluble in excess, giving a colourless solution | white precipitate, insoluble in excess |

### The ammonium ion

Ammonium compounds give **no precipitate**. Instead, warm the solution with aqueous sodium hydroxide and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube. Ammonia gas turns it blue:

$$
\ce{NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> NH3(g) + H2O(l)}
$$

### Ionic equations (Supplement)

Each precipitate is a metal hydroxide. The number of hydroxide ions equals the charge on the metal ion:

$$
\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> Cu(OH)2(s)}
$$

$$
\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + 3OH-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}
$$

Green iron(II) hydroxide turns brown on standing because oxygen in the air oxidises it to iron(III) hydroxide.

## Telling similar ions apart

- **Aluminium or zinc?** Both give a white precipitate that dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide. Use **aqueous ammonia**: the zinc precipitate dissolves in excess, the aluminium precipitate does not.
- **Iron(II) or iron(III)?** Green precipitate for iron(II), red-brown for iron(III).
- **Aluminium or lead(II)? (ZIMSEC)** These two give **identical** results with both reagents, so a further test is needed. Add aqueous potassium iodide: lead(II) ions give a **yellow precipitate** of lead(II) iodide, $\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}$; aluminium ions give no precipitate. (A white precipitate of lead(II) chloride or lead(II) sulfate with a chloride or sulfate solution also shows lead.)

## Flame tests (Cambridge)

Some metal ions give a coloured flame. Dip a clean nichrome (or platinum) wire in concentrated hydrochloric acid, then into the solid, and hold it in the edge of a hot, **non-luminous** (blue) Bunsen flame.

| Ion | Flame colour |
| --- | --- |
| lithium, $\ce{Li+}$ | red |
| sodium, $\ce{Na+}$ | yellow |
| potassium, $\ce{K+}$ | lilac |
| calcium, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | orange-red |
| barium, $\ce{Ba^2+}$ | light green |
| copper(II), $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | blue-green |

Clean the wire between tests by dipping it in the acid and heating it until it gives no colour.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: identifying a blue solution"}
A blue solution gives a light blue precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide, which does not dissolve in excess. With aqueous ammonia, the light blue precipitate dissolves in excess to give a dark blue solution.

1. A light blue precipitate with hydroxide ions points to copper(II).
2. The dark blue solution with excess ammonia confirms it.
3. The cation is $\ce{Cu^2+}$, and the ionic equation for the first precipitate is $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2OH- -> Cu(OH)2}$.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: a white precipitate"}
A colourless solution gives a white precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide that dissolves in excess, and a white precipitate with aqueous ammonia that does **not** dissolve in excess.

1. Both aluminium and zinc dissolve in excess sodium hydroxide.
2. Only zinc dissolves in excess ammonia, so the ion is **not** zinc.
3. Cambridge answer: aluminium. ZIMSEC answer: aluminium **or** lead(II); add potassium iodide solution, and a yellow precipitate would show lead(II).
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Writing 'blue' for copper(II) hydroxide"}
The precipitate is **light blue**; the solution with excess ammonia is **dark blue**. Use the exact colours.
:::

:::mistake{title="Adding all the reagent at once"}
If you pour in excess straight away, a precipitate that dissolves in excess may never be seen. Add a few drops first.
:::

:::mistake{title="Expecting a precipitate for ammonium"}
Ammonium ions give ammonia gas on warming with sodium hydroxide, and no precipitate.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using a yellow flame for flame tests"}
A luminous (yellow) flame hides the colours. Use a hot, non-luminous flame.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Describe a result fully: colour of the precipitate **and** whether it is soluble or insoluble in excess, for each reagent. "White precipitate" alone is never enough to identify an ion.
:::

:::exam-tip
In "identify the ion" questions, give the name and charge, for example "iron(III), Fe³⁺". The Roman numeral matters.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Add aqueous sodium hydroxide, then aqueous ammonia, a few drops at a time and then in excess.
- Coloured precipitates: copper(II) light blue, iron(II) green, iron(III) red-brown, chromium(III) green.
- White precipitates: aluminium and zinc dissolve in excess NaOH; only zinc dissolves in excess ammonia.
- Ammonium: ammonia gas on warming with NaOH (damp red litmus turns blue).
- Cambridge: calcium and flame tests. ZIMSEC: magnesium, and lead(II), which needs the iodide test to tell it from aluminium.
:::
