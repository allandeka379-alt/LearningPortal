## Introduction

Look closely at a bicycle's handlebars, a bathroom tap or a set of "silver" cutlery. Underneath the shiny surface there is usually a cheaper metal such as steel or brass, covered by a very thin layer of chromium, nickel or silver. The layer was put there by **electroplating**.

The copper in the electrical wiring of your home has also passed through an electrolysis cell. Copper straight from a smelter is about 99% pure, which is not good enough for wiring, so it is **purified by electrolysis** to about 99.99%. Some of Zimbabwe's copper is recovered from ores mined mainly for platinum and nickel, and electrolysis is one of the ways such metals are refined.

You should already know the parts of an electrolytic cell and what happens when copper(II) sulfate solution is electrolysed with copper electrodes.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Electroplating"}
Using electrolysis to coat an object with a thin layer of a metal.
:::

:::definition{term="Anode slime (anode sludge)"}
The insoluble material that falls to the bottom of the cell below the anode during the purification of copper. It contains unreactive metals such as silver, gold and platinum.
:::

:::definition{term="Refining"}
Purifying a metal. Copper is refined by electrolysis.
:::

## Why objects are electroplated

- **Appearance:** a shiny coat of chromium, silver or gold makes an object attractive.
- **Resistance to corrosion:** chromium, nickel and tin do not corrode easily, so a thin layer protects the steel underneath. The inside of a "tin can" is steel plated with tin.
- **Cost:** a thin layer of an expensive metal on a cheap one gives the look of the expensive metal at a fraction of the price.

## How to electroplate an object

Three rules decide the set-up:

![Cell for copper-plating an iron key: the key is connected to the negative terminal and is the cathode, a strip of copper joined to the positive terminal is the anode, and the electrolyte is copper(II) sulfate solution; copper ions gain electrons and coat the key while the copper anode dissolves to replace them.](media:diagrams/electroplating-copper-key-cell.png "Figure: the object to be plated is the cathode and the anode is the plating metal")

1. The **object to be plated is the cathode** (negative electrode).
2. The **anode is made of the plating metal**.
3. The **electrolyte is a solution containing ions of the plating metal**.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: copper-plating an iron key]
1. Clean the key with sandpaper and detergent so that the copper sticks to it (grease and rust stop the coating forming evenly).
2. Connect the key to the negative terminal of a d.c. supply: it is the **cathode**.
3. Connect a strip of **copper** to the positive terminal: it is the **anode**.
4. Dip both into **copper(II) sulfate solution**, the electrolyte.
5. Pass a small current. A pink-brown layer of copper builds up on the key.
6. Supplement half-equations: cathode $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$; anode $\ce{Cu -> Cu^2+ + 2e-}$.
:::

Copper ions leave the solution at the cathode, but the anode dissolves and replaces them, so the concentration of the electrolyte (and its blue colour) stays the same. A small current, a clean surface and turning the object during plating all help to give an even coat that does not flake off.

:::note{title="The object must conduct"}
Only a conductor can be a cathode. A plastic object must first be given a thin conducting coat (for example of graphite) before it can be electroplated.
:::

## Purifying copper by electrolysis

The cell is set up like a copper-plating cell:

| Part | What it is made of |
| --- | --- |
| anode (+) | a thick block of **impure copper** |
| cathode (−) | a thin sheet of **pure copper** |
| electrolyte | **copper(II) sulfate solution**, acidified with dilute sulfuric acid |

**At the anode** the copper atoms lose electrons and go into solution:

$$
\ce{Cu(s) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2e-}
$$

**At the cathode** copper ions gain electrons and pure copper is deposited:

$$
\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + 2e- -> Cu(s)}
$$

Over several days the impure anode gets **smaller** and the pure cathode gets **thicker**. The electrolyte stays blue.

### What happens to the impurities

- Impurities that are **more reactive** than copper, such as zinc, iron and nickel, also lose electrons and dissolve as ions. They **stay in solution**: their ions are harder to discharge than copper(II) ions, so they are not deposited on the cathode.
- Impurities that are **less reactive** than copper, such as silver, gold and platinum, do **not** dissolve. As the copper around them dissolves, they fall to the bottom of the cell as **anode slime**.

The anode slime is a valuable **by-product**: the silver, gold and platinum in it are recovered and sold, which helps to pay for the refining.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: purity of an anode]
During refining, the impure anode loses $12.0$ g and the pure cathode gains $11.4$ g.

1. All the copper that leaves the anode is deposited on the cathode: mass of copper $= 11.4$ g.
2. The rest of the mass lost, $12.0 - 11.4 = 0.6$ g, was impurities (some dissolved, some fell as slime).
3. Percentage of copper in the part of the anode used $= \dfrac{11.4}{12.0} \times 100 = 95.0\%$.
:::

## Uses of copper

| Use | Property that makes copper suitable |
| --- | --- |
| electrical wiring and cables | very good electrical conductor; ductile (can be drawn into wires) |
| water pipes and plumbing | does not corrode easily; malleable, so it can be bent |
| cooking pans, car radiators | very good thermal conductor |
| alloys: brass (with zinc) and bronze (with tin) | the alloys are harder and stronger than pure copper |

Copper for wiring must be very pure, because even small amounts of impurity noticeably increase its electrical resistance.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Making the object the anode"}
The anode dissolves. If the object is the anode, it gets thinner instead of being plated. The object is always the **cathode**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Putting zinc or iron in the anode slime"}
Metals more reactive than copper dissolve and stay in solution as ions. Only metals **less** reactive than copper (silver, gold, platinum) form the slime.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using any electrolyte"}
The electrolyte must contain ions of the plating metal: silver nitrate for silver, nickel sulfate for nickel, copper(II) sulfate for copper.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe how a spoon is electroplated with silver" needs three linked points: spoon as cathode, silver as anode, a solution of a silver salt as electrolyte. Mention cleaning the spoon for a fourth mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why the colour of the electrolyte does not change" needs both halves: copper(II) ions are removed at the cathode **and** replaced at the same rate by the dissolving anode.
:::

:::exam-tip
In purity calculations, use the **mass gained by the pure cathode** for the copper, and the **mass lost by the anode** for the total.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Objects are electroplated to improve their appearance and their resistance to corrosion.
- Object = cathode; plating metal = anode; electrolyte contains ions of the plating metal.
- In copper purification the impure copper is the anode, pure copper the cathode and acidified copper(II) sulfate the electrolyte.
- Copper dissolves at the anode and pure copper is deposited at the cathode; the electrolyte stays blue.
- More reactive impurities stay in solution; silver, gold and platinum fall as anode slime, a valuable by-product.
- Pure copper is used for wiring, pipes, pans and alloys such as brass and bronze.
:::
