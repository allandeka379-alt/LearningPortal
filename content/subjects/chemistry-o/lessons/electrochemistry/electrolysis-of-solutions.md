## Introduction

The chlorine that keeps tap water in Harare, Bulawayo and Mutare safe to drink is made by passing electricity through **brine**, a concentrated solution of sodium chloride. The same process gives hydrogen and sodium hydroxide, which is used to make soap and paper. Passing electricity through acidified water splits it into hydrogen and oxygen, and the volumes of the two gases even reveal the formula of water.

Electrolysing a **solution** is a little more complicated than electrolysing a molten salt, because the water provides ions of its own. In this lesson you will learn which ions are discharged and why.

You should already know the parts of an electrolytic cell, that cations go to the cathode and anions go to the anode, and the order of reactivity of the common metals.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
Dilute sulfuric acid and concentrated sodium chloride are **Cambridge Core**; the electrolysis of acidified water and the formula of water are **ZIMSEC Form 3**. Copper(II) sulfate and the effect of concentration on halide solutions are **Cambridge Supplement**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Aqueous solution"}
A solution in which water is the solvent, shown by the state symbol (aq).
:::

:::definition{term="Discharge"}
An ion is **discharged** when it gains or loses electrons at an electrode and becomes an atom or molecule.
:::

:::definition{term="Acidified water"}
Water with a little dilute sulfuric acid added to provide enough ions for it to conduct well.
:::

:::definition{term="Brine"}
A concentrated solution of sodium chloride in water.
:::

## Ions from the water

Water is ionised very slightly:

$$
\ce{H2O <=> H+ + OH-}
$$

So an aqueous solution always contains **hydrogen ions** and **hydroxide ions** as well as the ions of the dissolved compound. Two kinds of ion are attracted to each electrode, but usually only one is discharged.

:::keyfact{title="Rules for inert electrodes"}
**Cathode:** if the metal is **less reactive than hydrogen** (copper, silver), the metal forms. If it is **more reactive** (potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium), **hydrogen** forms instead.

**Anode:** a **concentrated** chloride, bromide or iodide gives the **halogen**. Otherwise (sulfates, nitrates, very dilute halides) **oxygen** forms from the hydroxide ions.
:::

These rules give the right answer for every solution in this lesson. For metals in the middle of the reactivity series, such as zinc and nickel, the result also depends on the conditions: from concentrated solutions of their salts the metal itself can be deposited.

## Electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid (acidified water)

Pure water conducts very poorly because it has very few ions, so a little dilute sulfuric acid is added.

**Apparatus (in words).** A **Hofmann voltameter** is often used: a glass apparatus with three vertical tubes joined at the bottom. The two outer tubes are graduated and each has a platinum electrode at its base; the middle tube is a reservoir for the acidified water. Taps at the top of the outer tubes let the gases be collected and tested.

**Results.**

| Electrode | Product | Half-equation (Supplement) | Test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| cathode (−) | hydrogen | $\ce{2H+ + 2e- -> H2}$ | burns with a squeaky pop |
| anode (+) | oxygen | $\ce{4OH- -> O2 + 2H2O + 4e-}$ | relights a glowing splint |

Sulfate ions are not discharged. The overall change is the decomposition of water, $\ce{2H2O -> 2H2 + O2}$, so the sulfuric acid slowly becomes more concentrated.

The volume of hydrogen is **twice** the volume of oxygen. At first the oxygen volume is slightly less than half, because oxygen is a little more soluble in water than hydrogen.

### Deducing the formula of water (ZIMSEC)

Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: from gas volumes to a formula]
After ten minutes a Hofmann voltameter holds $40.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of hydrogen and $20.0\ \text{cm}^3$ of oxygen.

1. Volume ratio $\ce{H2} : \ce{O2} = 40.0 : 20.0 = 2 : 1$.
2. So for every 2 molecules of $\ce{H2}$ there is 1 molecule of $\ce{O2}$.
3. Atoms: $2 \times 2 = 4$ hydrogen atoms for every $1 \times 2 = 2$ oxygen atoms.
4. Ratio H : O $= 4 : 2 = 2 : 1$, so the simplest formula of water is $\ce{H2O}$.
:::

**Uses of the products.** Hydrogen is used to make ammonia and margarine and as a fuel (rockets and fuel cells). Oxygen is used in hospitals, for cutting and welding metals, and in steelmaking.

## Electrolysis of concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (brine)

The solution contains $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions.

- **Cathode:** sodium is more reactive than hydrogen, so **hydrogen** forms: $\ce{2H+ + 2e- -> H2}$.
- **Anode:** the chloride ions are concentrated, so **chlorine** forms: $\ce{2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-}$.
- **Left in solution:** $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions, that is, **sodium hydroxide**. The solution becomes alkaline.

**Observations:** bubbles at both electrodes; the gas at the anode is pale yellow-green and bleaches damp litmus paper; if universal indicator is added, the solution near the cathode turns blue or purple. Slightly less chlorine than hydrogen is collected, because chlorine is quite soluble in water.

## Copper(II) sulfate solution (Supplement)

:::worked-solution[Example 2: graphite electrodes compared with copper electrodes]
**With inert graphite electrodes**

1. Cathode: copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper is deposited as a pink-brown coating: $\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}$.
2. Anode: sulfate ions are not discharged, so **oxygen** bubbles off: $\ce{4OH- -> O2 + 2H2O + 4e-}$.
3. The blue colour **fades** as copper(II) ions are removed, and the solution becomes acidic (sulfuric acid is left).

**With copper electrodes**

1. Cathode: copper is deposited as before, so the cathode gains mass.
2. Anode: the copper anode itself **dissolves**: $\ce{Cu -> Cu^2+ + 2e-}$. No gas is given off and the anode loses mass.
3. Copper(II) ions are added at the anode as fast as they are removed at the cathode, so the blue colour **stays the same**.
:::

## Dilute or concentrated halide solutions (Supplement)

The concentration of a halide decides what forms at the anode:

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode | Anode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| concentrated sodium chloride | hydrogen | chlorine |
| **dilute** sodium chloride | hydrogen | mainly **oxygen** |
| concentrated potassium bromide | hydrogen | bromine (the solution turns orange) |
| concentrated copper(II) chloride | copper | chlorine |

In a very dilute chloride solution the chloride ions are outnumbered, so hydroxide ions are discharged and oxygen forms. As a concentrated chloride solution is electrolysed for a long time, the chloride ions are used up and oxygen begins to form as well.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Sodium at the cathode from brine"}
In aqueous solution, hydrogen ions are discharged instead of sodium ions, because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen. Sodium can only be made from **molten** sodium chloride.
:::

:::mistake{title="Sulfate ions forming a product"}
Sulfate and nitrate ions are not discharged from solution. Oxygen forms at the anode instead.
:::

:::mistake{title="Getting the 2 : 1 ratio the wrong way round"}
Twice as much **hydrogen** as oxygen is produced, matching the two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom in $\ce{H2O}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Copper electrodes giving oxygen"}
With copper electrodes in copper(II) sulfate solution, the anode dissolves. No oxygen is produced.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **explain** a product, compare reactivity with hydrogen: "hydrogen forms at the cathode because sodium is more reactive than hydrogen".
:::

:::exam-tip
Give full gas tests: hydrogen "burns with a squeaky pop with a lighted splint"; oxygen "relights a glowing splint"; chlorine "bleaches damp litmus paper".
:::

:::exam-tip
For the formula of water, state the key idea: equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. Then convert molecules to atoms.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water provides $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ions in every aqueous solution.
- Cathode: a metal less reactive than hydrogen (copper, silver) is deposited; hydrogen forms from solutions of very reactive metals (potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium).
- Anode: a concentrated halide gives the halogen; otherwise oxygen forms.
- Dilute sulfuric acid gives hydrogen and oxygen in a 2 : 1 volume ratio, which shows the formula $\ce{H2O}$.
- Brine gives hydrogen, chlorine and sodium hydroxide solution.
- Supplement: copper(II) sulfate gives copper and oxygen with graphite electrodes (the blue fades) but copper is transferred from anode to cathode with copper electrodes (the blue stays).
:::
