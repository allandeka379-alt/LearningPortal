## Introduction

Every petrol or diesel engine on the road between Harare and Beitbridge burns fuel in air and sends carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and oxides of nitrogen out of its exhaust. A **hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell** powers a vehicle in a completely different way: it combines hydrogen with oxygen from the air to make electricity, and the only thing that comes out of the "exhaust" is water.

Fuel cells are not new. They supplied electricity, and drinking water, to the astronauts of the Apollo missions to the Moon. Today they power some buses, cars and trucks in a number of countries.

You should already know how a simple cell produces electricity from a chemical reaction, and how to balance an equation.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
This is a **Cambridge IGCSE** topic (4.2). What a fuel cell does is **Core**; comparing fuel cells with petrol engines is **Supplement**.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fuel cell"}
A cell that produces electricity from a fuel and oxygen that are supplied to it continuously, without burning the fuel.
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell"}
A fuel cell that uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity, with **water as the only chemical product**.
:::

:::formula{name="Overall reaction in a hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell"}
$$
\ce{2H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2H2O(l)}
$$
:::

## How a fuel cell works

A fuel cell has two electrodes separated by an electrolyte.

1. **Hydrogen** is fed to one electrode. There it gives up electrons, so this electrode is negative.
2. The electrons flow through the external circuit, where they do useful work, for example driving an electric motor.
3. **Oxygen**, usually from the air, is fed to the other electrode. It takes in the electrons and combines with hydrogen to form **water**.

The hydrogen is never set alight: the energy is released directly as electrical energy (with some heat). A fuel cell does not run flat like a battery. As long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied, it keeps producing electricity, so it is **refuelled**, not recharged.

:::note{title="For interest: the electrode reactions"}
With an acidic electrolyte the reactions are $\ce{H2 -> 2H+ + 2e-}$ at the negative electrode and $\ce{O2 + 4H+ + 4e- -> 2H2O}$ at the positive electrode. Adding them, with the first doubled, gives $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: water produced by a fuel cell]
How much water is made when $1.0$ kg of hydrogen is used? ($A_r$: H = 1, O = 16)

1. From the equation, $\ce{2H2}$ ($2 \times 2 = 4$ g) gives $\ce{2H2O}$ ($2 \times 18 = 36$ g).
2. So every 4 g of hydrogen gives 36 g of water: 9 times the mass.
3. $1.0$ kg of hydrogen gives $9 \times 1.0 = 9.0$ kg of water.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: volume of oxygen needed]
A fuel cell uses $60\ \text{dm}^3$ of hydrogen. What volume of oxygen does it need, at the same temperature and pressure?

1. From the equation, 2 volumes of hydrogen react with 1 volume of oxygen.
2. Volume of oxygen $= 60 \div 2 = 30\ \text{dm}^3$.
:::

## Fuel cells compared with petrol engines (Supplement)

| Advantages of hydrogen fuel cells | Disadvantages of hydrogen fuel cells |
| --- | --- |
| The only product is **water**: no carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbons or particulates come from the vehicle. | Hydrogen is a gas that is **difficult to store**: it needs strong high-pressure tanks (or cooling to a very low temperature) and takes up a lot of space. |
| There is no high-temperature combustion in air, so **no oxides of nitrogen** are formed. | Hydrogen is **highly flammable**, so leaks are dangerous. |
| A fuel cell is **more efficient**: more of the energy in the fuel becomes useful energy. | Most hydrogen is made from **methane** (a fossil fuel), releasing carbon dioxide; making it by electrolysis needs a lot of electricity, which may come from burning fossil fuels. |
| Electric motors are **quiet** and have fewer moving parts. | There are **few hydrogen filling stations**, and fuel cells are **expensive** (they use platinum catalysts). |

A hydrogen fuel cell is only as "clean" as the way its hydrogen is made. In a sunny country such as Zimbabwe, hydrogen could in future be made by electrolysing water with electricity from solar panels, which would avoid carbon dioxide emissions altogether.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying fuel cells cause no pollution at all"}
The vehicle releases only water, but making, compressing and transporting the hydrogen can release carbon dioxide and other pollutants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the fuel cell burns hydrogen"}
There is no flame. The hydrogen reacts at an electrode and its energy is released as electrical energy.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fuel cells need recharging"}
A fuel cell keeps working as long as hydrogen and oxygen are supplied. It is a battery that needs recharging.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Make every advantage or disadvantage **comparative**: "a fuel cell produces no carbon dioxide **whereas** a petrol engine does".
:::

:::exam-tip
In an "evaluate" question, give points on both sides and finish with a reasoned conclusion, for example about how the hydrogen is produced.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell uses hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity; water is the only chemical product.
- Overall reaction: $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$; the hydrogen is not burnt.
- It keeps working as long as fuel is supplied: it is refuelled, not recharged.
- Supplement advantages: only water is emitted (no carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide or oxides of nitrogen), higher efficiency, quiet.
- Supplement disadvantages: hydrogen is hard to store and flammable, is mostly made from methane, and there are few filling stations; fuel cells are expensive.
:::
