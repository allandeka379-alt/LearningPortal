## Introduction

Zimbabwe's Great Dyke holds some of the world's largest deposits of chromite, the ore of chromium. The shiny chromium on a bathroom tap or a car's trim is a layer far thinner than a sheet of paper, and it is put there by **electrolysis**. Electrolysis is also how sodium and aluminium are extracted, how chlorine for water treatment is made and how copper for electrical wiring is purified.

The word means "splitting with electricity". In this lesson you will learn how an electric current can break down an ionic compound, what happens at each electrode, and how to predict the products when a molten compound is electrolysed.

You should already know that ionic compounds are made of positive and negative ions held in a lattice, and how ions form from atoms.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Electrolysis"}
The decomposition (breaking down) of an ionic compound, when **molten or in aqueous solution**, by passing an electric current through it.
:::

:::definition{term="Electrolyte"}
The molten or aqueous substance that conducts electricity and is decomposed during electrolysis.
:::

:::definition{term="Electrodes"}
The two conducting rods that carry the current into and out of the electrolyte. The **anode** is the **positive** electrode; the **cathode** is the **negative** electrode.
:::

:::definition{term="Inert electrode"}
An electrode that conducts electricity but does not react with the electrolyte or the products. Graphite (carbon) and platinum are used.
:::

:::keyfact{title="PANIC"}
**P**ositive **A**node, **N**egative **I**s **C**athode. Positive ions (**cations**) go to the cathode; negative ions (**anions**) go to the anode.
:::

## Why the compound must be molten or dissolved

An electric current is a flow of charged particles. In a **solid** ionic compound the ions are held in fixed positions in the lattice. They can vibrate but cannot move from place to place, so the solid does not conduct.

When the compound is **melted** or **dissolved in water**, the lattice breaks down and the ions become **free to move**. Now they can carry charge through the liquid, and the compound is decomposed at the electrodes.

:::note{title="Electrolytes and conductors are different"}
A copper wire or a graphite rod conducts because **electrons** move through it, and it is not changed by the current. An electrolyte conducts because **ions** move through it, and it **is** decomposed. Covalent substances such as ethanol or sugar solution have no ions, so they do not conduct at all.
:::

## The electrolytic cell

A simple electrolysis circuit has these parts:

- a **d.c. power supply** (a battery or power pack);
- connecting wires and a switch;
- a **bulb or an ammeter** in series, to show when a current flows;
- two **graphite electrodes** dipping into the electrolyte: the one joined to the positive terminal is the anode, the one joined to the negative terminal is the cathode;
- the **electrolyte** in a container: a crucible or boiling tube for a molten salt, a beaker for a solution.

## What happens at the electrodes

- **At the cathode (−):** positive ions are attracted. They **gain electrons** and become atoms. This is **reduction**. Metals (or hydrogen) are formed at the cathode.
- **At the anode (+):** negative ions are attracted. They **lose electrons** and become atoms, which usually join to form molecules. This is **oxidation**. Non-metals (other than hydrogen) are formed at the anode.

:::keyfact{title="Where the products form"}
Metals or hydrogen form at the **cathode**. Non-metals other than hydrogen form at the **anode**.
:::

## Electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide

Lead(II) bromide, $\ce{PbBr2}$, is a white solid made of $\ce{Pb^2+}$ and $\ce{Br-}$ ions.

**Method (described in words).** Put lead(II) bromide powder in a crucible and dip two graphite electrodes into it. Connect them to a d.c. supply with a bulb in the circuit. Heat the crucible strongly in a **fume cupboard**, because bromine vapour and lead compounds are toxic.

**Observations.**

1. While the solid is cold the bulb does **not** light: the ions cannot move.
2. When the lead(II) bromide melts (at about 370 °C) the bulb lights.
3. **At the anode:** red-brown fumes of **bromine** are given off.
4. **At the cathode:** a silvery-grey liquid, **lead**, forms and collects at the bottom of the crucible. It can be seen as a bead of metal when the melt is poured away.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: half-equations for molten lead(II) bromide (Supplement)]
1. Cathode: each lead(II) ion gains two electrons: $\ce{Pb^2+ + 2e- -> Pb}$ (reduction).
2. Anode: two bromide ions each lose one electron and the atoms pair up: $\ce{2Br- -> Br2 + 2e-}$ (oxidation).
3. The two electrons released at the anode match the two used at the cathode, so the overall change is $\ce{PbBr2(l) -> Pb(l) + Br2(g)}$.
:::

## How the charge moves (Supplement)

A complete circuit needs charge to flow all the way round:

1. **In the external circuit (wires):** electrons flow from the anode, through the power supply, to the cathode.
2. **At the cathode:** cations gain electrons from the electrode.
3. **In the electrolyte:** **ions** move, not electrons. Cations move towards the cathode and anions move towards the anode.
4. **At the anode:** anions lose electrons to the electrode, and these electrons continue round the circuit.

## Predicting the products for molten binary compounds

A **binary compound** contains just two elements. When it is molten, the only ions present are the metal ions and the non-metal ions, so the metal forms at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: molten potassium iodide and molten magnesium chloride]
1. Molten $\ce{KI}$ contains $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{I-}$ ions. Cathode: potassium, $\ce{K+ + e- -> K}$. Anode: iodine, $\ce{2I- -> I2 + 2e-}$.
2. Molten $\ce{MgCl2}$ contains $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ ions. Cathode: magnesium, $\ce{Mg^2+ + 2e- -> Mg}$. Anode: chlorine, $\ce{2Cl- -> Cl2 + 2e-}$.
3. In magnesium chloride two electrons are needed per magnesium atom and two are released per chlorine molecule, so equal numbers of magnesium atoms and chlorine molecules form.
:::

| Molten compound | Product at the cathode | Product at the anode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| sodium chloride | sodium | chlorine |
| calcium bromide | calcium | bromine |
| aluminium oxide | aluminium | oxygen |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying electrons flow through the electrolyte"}
Electrons flow only through the wires and electrodes. In the molten or dissolved electrolyte the current is carried by **moving ions**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up anode and cathode"}
Remember PANIC: the anode is positive and attracts negative ions; the cathode is negative and attracts positive ions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing Br or Cl as the product"}
Bromine, chlorine and iodine are formed as diatomic molecules: $\ce{Br2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{I2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking solid ionic compounds contain no ions"}
Solid lead(II) bromide is full of ions. It does not conduct because the ions cannot **move**, not because they are absent.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe what is observed" needs things you could **see**: "red-brown fumes at the anode", "silvery liquid at the cathode". Naming the product alone ("bromine") is an identification, not an observation.
:::

:::exam-tip
When explaining conduction, use the words **ions** and **free to move** (or mobile). "Molten lead(II) bromide conducts because its ions are free to move" earns the mark; "because it is a liquid" does not.
:::

:::exam-tip
For half-equations, put electrons on the left at the cathode (reduction) and on the right at the anode (oxidation), and check the charges balance.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound, molten or in aqueous solution, by an electric current.
- The anode is positive, the cathode is negative and the electrolyte is the substance decomposed; graphite and platinum are inert electrodes.
- Ionic compounds conduct only when molten or dissolved, because only then are their ions free to move.
- Metals or hydrogen form at the cathode (reduction); non-metals form at the anode (oxidation).
- Molten lead(II) bromide gives lead at the cathode and red-brown bromine at the anode.
- Supplement: electrons flow in the wires, ions move in the electrolyte, and electrons are gained at the cathode and lost at the anode.
:::
