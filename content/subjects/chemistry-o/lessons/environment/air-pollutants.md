## Introduction

Stand beside a busy road in Harare at rush hour and you can smell the exhaust from kombis, buses and lorries. In the dry season, smoke from veld fires and from rubbish being burnt hangs in the air. Coal-fired power stations and metal smelters release gases from tall chimneys. On a cold night, a family may bring a brazier of glowing charcoal indoors, or run a generator during a power cut, not knowing that it can fill the room with a poisonous gas.

These are all sources of **air pollution**. In this lesson you will learn what clean air contains, which pollutants come from where, what harm they do, and how chemistry helps to reduce them.

You should already know how fuels burn and what is meant by acids, bases and oxides.

:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pollutant"}
A substance released into the environment, usually by human activity, that harms living things or the environment.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete and incomplete combustion"}
**Complete** combustion of a carbon-containing fuel, in plenty of oxygen, gives carbon dioxide and water. **Incomplete** combustion, in too little oxygen, also gives carbon monoxide and soot (carbon).
:::

:::definition{term="Particulates"}
Tiny solid particles in the air, such as soot, that can be breathed deep into the lungs.
:::

:::definition{term="Acid rain"}
Rain with a lower pH than normal rain because acidic gases such as sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen have dissolved in it.
:::

## Clean air

Clean, dry air is about **78% nitrogen** and **21% oxygen**. The remaining 1% is mostly noble gases (mainly argon) with a small amount (about 0.04%) of carbon dioxide.

## The main air pollutants

| Pollutant | Main sources | Harmful effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| carbon monoxide, $\ce{CO}$ | incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels: car engines, generators, charcoal braziers, veld fires | **toxic**: it stops red blood cells carrying oxygen; it has no colour or smell |
| particulates (soot) | incomplete combustion: diesel engines, burning rubbish, veld fires, coal | breathing problems such as asthma and bronchitis, and an increased risk of **cancer** |
| oxides of nitrogen, $\ce{NO}$ and $\ce{NO2}$ | car and lorry engines | **acid rain**, photochemical smog and breathing problems |
| sulfur dioxide, $\ce{SO2}$ | burning fossil fuels that contain sulfur compounds (coal-fired power stations, diesel); roasting sulfide ores | **acid rain** and breathing problems |
| carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$ | complete combustion of carbon-containing fuels | increased **global warming**, leading to climate change |
| methane, $\ce{CH4}$ | decomposition of vegetation and rubbish; waste gases from digestion in animals such as cattle | increased **global warming**, leading to climate change |

:::example{title="Example 1: why braziers indoors are dangerous"}
In a closed room, charcoal in a brazier soon runs short of oxygen. Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide, for example $\ce{2C + O2 -> 2CO}$. Carbon monoxide has no smell, so people do not notice it. It combines with the haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than oxygen does, so less oxygen reaches the brain, causing drowsiness and, in high doses, death. Braziers and generators must only be used where there is plenty of fresh air.
:::

## Acid rain

Normal rain is slightly acidic (about pH 5.6) because carbon dioxide dissolves in it. Acid rain is much more acidic:

- sulfur dioxide dissolves in rain water and is oxidised in the air to **sulfuric acid**;
- nitrogen dioxide reacts with oxygen and water to form **nitric acid**: $\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}$.

Acid rain **damages buildings and statues** made of limestone or marble ($\ce{CaCO3 + H2SO4 -> CaSO4 + H2O + CO2}$), **corrodes metals** such as iron bridges and roofs, **damages trees and crops** by washing nutrients out of the soil, and makes lakes and rivers too acidic for **fish**.

## Oxides of nitrogen and catalytic converters (Supplement)

Nitrogen is very unreactive, but inside a car engine the burning fuel makes the temperature extremely high, hot enough for nitrogen and oxygen **from the air** to combine:

$$
\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}
$$

In the air, nitrogen monoxide then reacts with more oxygen to form brown nitrogen dioxide: $\ce{2NO + O2 -> 2NO2}$.

A **catalytic converter** in the exhaust pipe contains a honeycomb coated with a thin layer of platinum, rhodium and palladium, giving a very large surface area. As the hot exhaust gases pass through, harmful gases are changed into less harmful ones, for example:

$$
\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}
$$

Carbon monoxide is also oxidised to carbon dioxide, and unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised to carbon dioxide and water. A catalytic converter does **not** remove carbon dioxide, and it works properly only when it is hot.

## Reducing acid rain and other pollution

- **Catalytic converters** in vehicles remove oxides of nitrogen and carbon monoxide.
- **Low-sulfur fuels:** sulfur is removed from petrol and diesel at the refinery.
- **Flue-gas desulfurisation:** waste gases from power stations are passed through calcium oxide (lime), a base, which reacts with the acidic sulfur dioxide: $\ce{CaO + SO2 -> CaSO3}$.
- **Filters** remove particulates from factory and power-station smoke, and well-tuned engines produce less soot and carbon monoxide.
- **Recycling and reducing waste**, instead of burning rubbish, and preventing veld fires cut smoke and particulates.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: sulfur dioxide from coal]
A power station burns 1.0 tonne of coal containing 2.0% sulfur by mass. ($A_r$: O = 16, S = 32, Ca = 40)

1. Mass of sulfur $= 0.020 \times 1000 = 20$ kg.
2. $\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}$: 32 kg of sulfur gives 64 kg of sulfur dioxide, so 20 kg gives $20 \times \dfrac{64}{32} = 40$ kg of $\ce{SO2}$.
3. To remove it, $\ce{CaO + SO2 -> CaSO3}$: 64 kg of $\ce{SO2}$ needs 56 kg of $\ce{CaO}$, so 40 kg needs $40 \times \dfrac{56}{64} = 35$ kg of calcium oxide.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Mixing up carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide"}
Carbon **mon**oxide (CO) is the **toxic** gas from incomplete combustion. Carbon **di**oxide ($\ce{CO2}$) is not poisonous in normal amounts but is a greenhouse gas.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the nitrogen in oxides of nitrogen comes from the fuel"}
Petrol and diesel contain almost no nitrogen. The nitrogen comes from the **air** drawn into the engine.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying catalytic converters remove carbon dioxide"}
They turn CO, NO and unburnt fuel into $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$. They do not reduce carbon dioxide emissions.
:::

:::mistake{title="Linking acid rain to global warming"}
Acid rain is caused by sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen. Global warming is caused by greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Clean, dry air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, with noble gases and about 0.04% carbon dioxide.
- Incomplete combustion gives toxic carbon monoxide and particulates that cause breathing problems and cancer.
- Sulfur dioxide (from burning sulfur-containing fuels) and oxides of nitrogen (from engines) cause acid rain; oxides of nitrogen also cause smog and breathing problems.
- Carbon dioxide and methane add to global warming.
- Acid rain is reduced by catalytic converters, low-sulfur fuels and flue-gas desulfurisation with calcium oxide.
- Supplement: in engines $\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}$; catalytic converters carry out reactions such as $\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}$.
:::
