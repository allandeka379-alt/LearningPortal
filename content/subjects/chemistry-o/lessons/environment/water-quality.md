## Introduction

Households in Zimbabwe get their water from four main kinds of source: rain collected from roofs, rivers, dams such as Lake Chivero and Lake Mutirikwi, and boreholes that draw on groundwater. Each source picks up different substances on its way to the tap or the bucket. Some of them are useful, like the dissolved oxygen that fish need; others, like the microbes in sewage, can make people seriously ill.

In this lesson you will learn how to test a liquid for water and for **pure** water, why chemists use distilled water, and which substances in natural water are beneficial or harmful. ZIMSEC students also compare water from different sources and study **eutrophication** in detail; Cambridge IGCSE students need only its outcome. You should already know about hydrated salts and melting and boiling points.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Pure water"}
Water with nothing else in it. Pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure.
:::

:::definition{term="Distilled water"}
Water that has been purified by distillation, so it contains far fewer dissolved substances than tap water.
:::

:::definition{term="Pollutant"}
A substance that harms living things or the environment when it is added to air, water or soil.
:::

:::definition{term="Eutrophication (ZIMSEC)"}
The process in which excess nutrients (nitrates and phosphates) in water cause rapid growth of algae and plants, followed by loss of dissolved oxygen and the death of fish and other aquatic life.
:::

## Testing for water

Two chemical tests show that a liquid **contains** water:

| Test reagent | Colour before | Colour with water |
| --- | --- | --- |
| anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | white | blue |
| anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride (often as test paper) | blue | pink |

These tests do **not** show that the water is pure: salty water, juice or tea would give the same colour changes because they all contain water.

To show that water is **pure**, measure its melting and boiling points. Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and, at normal atmospheric pressure, boils at exactly 100 °C. Dissolved impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point, and the water boils over a range of temperatures.

:::note{title="Boiling points depend on air pressure"}
The value of 100 °C is for normal (sea-level) atmospheric pressure. Much of Zimbabwe is well above sea level, where the air pressure is lower: in Harare, about 1500 m up, pure water boils at about 95 °C. So compare a sample with the boiling point of pure (distilled) water measured in the same place. Melting points hardly change with pressure.
:::

### Why distilled water is used in the laboratory

Tap water contains dissolved substances such as chloride, calcium and sulfate ions, and chlorine compounds from water treatment. These could react with the chemicals in an experiment, for example giving a white precipitate with silver nitrate. Distilled water contains far fewer chemical impurities, so it does not interfere with results.

## Substances in natural water

| Substance | Where it comes from | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| dissolved oxygen | from the air | **beneficial**: needed by fish and other aquatic life |
| metal compounds (e.g. of calcium and magnesium) | dissolved from rocks | **beneficial**: provide essential minerals |
| some metal compounds (e.g. of lead or mercury) | mining waste, old pipes, industry | **harmful**: toxic |
| plastics | litter, waste dumping | **harmful**: harm aquatic animals that swallow them or get trapped |
| sewage | toilets, pit latrines, leaking pipes | **harmful**: contains microbes that cause diseases such as cholera and typhoid |
| nitrates | fertilisers | **harmful** in excess: lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life |
| phosphates | fertilisers and detergents | **harmful** in excess: lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life |

## Comparing water sources (ZIMSEC)

The typical properties below vary from place to place and season to season.

| Source | Colour and clarity | Smell | pH | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| rain | colourless and clear | none | slightly acidic (about 5.6) | dissolves carbon dioxide from the air; may collect dust from roofs |
| river | often brown and cloudy, especially in the rainy season | earthy, or unpleasant if polluted | about neutral | suspended clay, microbes, fertiliser and sewage run-off |
| dam | clearer than rivers, as mud settles; may be green when algae grow | can be musty in hot weather | about neutral | algal blooms where nutrients are high |
| borehole | usually clear and colourless | usually none | about neutral, depending on the rocks | filtered by soil and rock, so fewer microbes; may contain dissolved calcium, magnesium or iron (hard water, metallic taste) |

Rainwater is slightly acidic because carbon dioxide dissolves in it to form carbonic acid. Borehole water can still be contaminated if the borehole is too close to a pit latrine.

## Eutrophication (ZIMSEC detail)

Cambridge IGCSE students only need to know that nitrates and phosphates lead to deoxygenation of water and damage to aquatic life. ZIMSEC students should be able to describe the steps:

1. Nitrates and phosphates from fertilisers, sewage and detergents wash into a river or dam.
2. Algae and water plants grow very rapidly (an algal bloom), covering the surface.
3. The algae block sunlight, so plants below the surface cannot photosynthesise and die; the algae also die.
4. Bacteria decompose the dead material, using up dissolved oxygen as they respire.
5. The oxygen level falls so low that fish and other aquatic animals die.

Lake Chivero, Harare's main water source, has suffered from algal blooms and water hyacinth linked to nutrient pollution, which also makes the water more difficult and more expensive to treat.

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: is it water, and is it pure?"}
A colourless liquid turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate from white to blue. At normal atmospheric pressure it boils at 101.5 °C.

1. The colour change shows that the liquid **contains water**.
2. At this pressure pure water boils at 100 °C. A higher boiling point shows **dissolved impurities**.
3. Conclusion: the liquid is water containing dissolved substances, not pure water.
:::

:::example{title="Example 2: which source is safest to start from?"}
A school can use water from a muddy river, a dam with green scum, or a borehole far from any latrine.

1. The river water carries suspended solids and possibly sewage microbes.
2. The dam water has algae, which suggests high nutrient levels.
3. The borehole water has been filtered through rock and is likely to contain the fewest microbes, so it is the best starting point. It should still be tested, and treated if necessary, before drinking.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the copper(II) sulfate test shows pure water"}
It shows only that water is present. Purity is shown by a boiling point of exactly 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure (or a melting point of exactly 0 °C).
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up the colours"}
Copper(II) sulfate: white to blue. Cobalt(II) chloride: blue to pink.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying fish die because nitrates poison them"}
In eutrophication, fish die mainly because **bacteria** decomposing dead algae use up the **dissolved oxygen**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
In a test for water, always give the starting colour **and** the final colour of the anhydrous reagent.
:::

:::exam-tip
For "beneficial or harmful" questions, give the substance and a reason: "dissolved oxygen is beneficial because fish need it for respiration".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Water turns anhydrous copper(II) sulfate from white to blue and anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride from blue to pink.
- Pure water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure (about 95 °C in Harare); distilled water is used in experiments because it has fewer impurities.
- Natural water may contain beneficial dissolved oxygen and minerals, and harmful toxic metal compounds, plastics, sewage microbes, nitrates and phosphates.
- ZIMSEC: rain is slightly acidic; river water is often muddy; dam water may contain algae; borehole water is usually clear but may be hard.
- ZIMSEC: nitrates and phosphates cause eutrophication, which removes dissolved oxygen and kills aquatic life.
:::
