## Introduction

Every home, market, farm, mine and factory produces waste. Walk past an uncollected heap of rubbish in a town and you will see food scraps, plastic bags and bottles, tins and ash; nearby there may be a smell of rotting food and swarms of flies. Around mines there are dumps of crushed rock, and some factories release smoke, dust or coloured water. If waste is not controlled it harms health, pollutes water and air, and spoils the land.

In Zimbabwe the Environmental Management Agency (EMA) sets rules for how waste is handled and checks that industries follow them. In this lesson (ZIMSEC Form 3) you will classify wastes, identify their sources, learn how chemists identify industrial wastes and suggest ways of controlling them. How waste is disposed of and recycled is covered in Form 4.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Waste"}
Any unwanted material produced by human activities, at home, in farming, in mining or in industry.
:::

:::definition{term="Biodegradable waste"}
Waste that microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) can break down into simpler substances, such as food scraps, paper and dung.
:::

:::definition{term="Non-biodegradable waste"}
Waste that microorganisms cannot break down, so it lasts for a very long time, such as plastics, glass and most metals.
:::

:::definition{term="Effluent"}
Liquid waste, such as sewage or used water from a factory, released into drains, rivers or the ground.
:::

:::definition{term="Hazardous waste"}
Waste that is poisonous, corrosive, flammable or infectious, such as used batteries, mercury, cyanide solutions and medical waste.
:::

## Classifying waste

| Solid | Liquid | Gaseous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| food scraps, paper, plastics, glass, tins, old tyres, ash, mine tailings | sewage, waste oil, factory effluent, spent acids and alkalis, dye water | smoke, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, methane from rotting rubbish |

Dust, such as cement dust, is made of tiny **solid** particles, but it travels through the air like a gas and is controlled as an air pollutant.

| Biodegradable | Non-biodegradable |
| --- | --- |
| food and garden waste, paper and cardboard, dung, cotton cloth, wood | plastics, glass, metal cans, rubber tyres, synthetic fibres, batteries |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: classifying household and school waste]
Classify vegetable peelings, a broken glass bottle, old newspaper, a plastic chair, used engine oil and smoke from a rubbish fire.

1. Vegetable peelings: **solid**, **biodegradable**.
2. Broken glass bottle: **solid**, **non-biodegradable**.
3. Old newspaper: **solid**, **biodegradable**.
4. Plastic chair: **solid**, **non-biodegradable**.
5. Used engine oil: **liquid** (and hazardous: it must not be poured down drains).
6. Smoke: **gaseous** waste (with solid soot particles).
:::

## Sources of waste

**Household waste:** kitchen scraps, packaging (plastic, paper, glass, tins), ash, old clothes, nappies, batteries and sewage.

**Industrial waste:**

| Industry | Typical wastes |
| --- | --- |
| gold processing | **tailings** (finely ground rock); solutions containing **cyanide**, used to dissolve the gold; **mercury**, used by some small-scale miners to collect gold, which escapes as vapour when the gold–mercury mixture is heated; acidic water |
| soap making | waste **oils and fats**; strongly **alkaline**, salty liquid left after the soap is removed |
| tyre manufacture | rubber dust and off-cuts, fumes from heating the rubber, scrap tyres |
| cement manufacture | **dust** from quarrying, grinding and the kilns; **carbon dioxide** from heating limestone and burning fuel |
| others | slag and sulfur dioxide from smelters; spent grain and effluent from breweries; medical waste from hospitals |

## Identifying wastes from industries

Chemists identify what a factory releases by observation and simple tests:

- **observation:** colour, cloudiness, an oily film or foam on effluent; smoke or dust in the air;
- **smell:** for example the rotten-egg smell of hydrogen sulfide or the sharp smell of ammonia;
- **pH:** universal indicator or a pH meter shows whether effluent is acidic or alkaline;
- **tests for ions:** sodium hydroxide solution gives coloured precipitates with metal ions (blue for copper(II), green for iron(II), red-brown for iron(III)); acidified barium chloride gives a white precipitate with sulfate ions;
- **tests for gases:** sulfur dioxide turns acidified potassium dichromate(VI) from orange to green; carbon dioxide turns limewater milky;
- **dust:** sticky plates or filters left near a factory collect dust that can be weighed.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: identifying an effluent]
Water draining from an electroplating works has pH 2. When sodium hydroxide solution is added, a blue precipitate forms. What does the effluent contain, and why is it harmful?

1. pH 2 shows the effluent is strongly **acidic**.
2. A blue precipitate with sodium hydroxide shows **copper(II) ions**.
3. The acid and the copper ions are poisonous to fish and plants, so the effluent must be treated before it reaches a river.
:::

## Controlling waste

- **Reduce, reuse, recycle:** buy less packaging, reuse bags and containers, recycle paper, glass, metal and plastic.
- **Separate waste at source** so that food waste can be composted and recyclable materials collected.
- **Collect rubbish regularly** and use proper bins and dump sites, not rivers or roadsides.
- **Treat effluent before release:** neutralise acidic waste with lime and alkaline waste with acid; precipitate poisonous metal ions; let solids settle out.
- **Trap dust and gases:** filters, water sprays and electrostatic precipitators remove dust; scrubbers remove acidic gases.
- **Manage mining waste safely:** lined tailings dams, destroying cyanide before water is released, using closed retorts to recover mercury.
- **Laws and education:** licences, inspections and fines, and awareness campaigns in communities and schools.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: neutralising acidic effluent]
An effluent contains 73 kg of hydrogen chloride. Calculate the mass of calcium hydroxide (slaked lime) needed to neutralise it: $\ce{2HCl + Ca(OH)2 -> CaCl2 + 2H2O}$. ($M_r$: HCl = 36.5, Ca(OH)₂ = 74)

1. $n(\ce{HCl}) = 73\,000 \div 36.5 = 2000$ mol.
2. 2 mol of HCl react with 1 mol of $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, so 1000 mol is needed.
3. Mass $= 1000 \times 74 = 74\,000$ g $= 74$ kg.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Thinking metals are biodegradable because they rust"}
Rusting and corrosion are slow chemical reactions, not breakdown by microorganisms. Metal cans are classed as non-biodegradable.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing vague sources"}
"Factories" is too vague. Name the industry and its waste: cement works give dust and carbon dioxide; gold processing gives tailings, cyanide and mercury.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Identify" waste questions often give a list: sort each item by state (solid, liquid, gas) and by whether it is biodegradable.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Suggest ways of controlling waste" needs practical, specific actions linked to the waste: neutralise acidic effluent with lime, fit dust filters, compost food waste, recycle cans.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Waste can be solid, liquid or gaseous, and biodegradable or non-biodegradable.
- Household waste: food scraps, packaging, ash, sewage. Industrial waste: tailings, cyanide and mercury (gold), oils and alkali (soap), rubber dust and fumes (tyres), dust and carbon dioxide (cement).
- Industrial wastes are identified by observation, smell, pH and chemical tests for ions and gases.
- Control waste by reducing, reusing, recycling, separating, treating effluent, trapping dust and gases, and enforcing laws.
:::
