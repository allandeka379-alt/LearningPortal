## Introduction

In many homes the inside of the kettle slowly becomes coated with a hard, white crust, and soap that lathers easily in rainwater makes only a grey scum in borehole water. Both problems have the same cause: **hard water**. Water that has soaked through rocks such as limestone, chalk or dolomite on its way to a borehole or spring picks up dissolved calcium and magnesium compounds.

Hard water is not harmful to drink; it even supplies some of the calcium we need. But it wastes energy and soap and can damage kettles, geysers (electric water heaters), boilers and pipes. This lesson is ZIMSEC Form 3 content. You should already know the reactions of acids with carbonates; how soap itself works is covered in the organic chemistry lessons.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Hard water"}
Water that contains dissolved calcium ions, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and/or magnesium ions, $\ce{Mg^2+}$. It does not form a lather easily with soap.
:::

:::definition{term="Soft water"}
Water that contains few calcium or magnesium ions, so it lathers easily with soap. Rainwater and distilled water are soft.
:::

:::definition{term="Temporary hardness"}
Hardness that is removed by boiling. It is caused by dissolved calcium (or magnesium) hydrogencarbonate, $\ce{Ca(HCO3)2}$.
:::

:::definition{term="Permanent hardness"}
Hardness that is not removed by boiling. It is caused by dissolved calcium or magnesium sulfate (and chloride).
:::

:::definition{term="Scale (limescale)"}
The hard deposit, mainly calcium carbonate, that forms when hard water is heated.
:::

## How water becomes hard

Rainwater is slightly acidic because it contains dissolved carbon dioxide. When it flows through limestone (calcium carbonate), it slowly reacts to form calcium hydrogencarbonate, which is soluble:

$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(aq) -> Ca(HCO3)2(aq)}
$$

This gives **temporary hardness**. Water that dissolves calcium sulfate (from rocks such as gypsum) or magnesium sulfate gets **permanent hardness**.

## Effects of hard water

When temporarily hard water is heated, the calcium hydrogencarbonate decomposes and calcium carbonate is deposited as scale:

$$
\ce{Ca(HCO3)2(aq) -> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}
$$

- **Kettles, geysers and boilers**: scale coats the heating element. Scale is a poor conductor of heat, so more electricity is needed to heat the water, and elements can overheat and fail.
- **Hot-water pipes**: scale builds up inside and narrows or blocks them.
- **Soap**: calcium and magnesium ions react with soap to form an insoluble scum. More soap is needed before a lather forms, and the scum leaves marks on baths, basins and clothes.

Hard water also has advantages: its calcium is good for teeth and bones, and many people prefer its taste.

## Testing for hardness

### With soap solution

Hard water needs more soap to form a lasting lather than soft water does.

1. Measure a fixed volume of the water sample (for example $10.0\ \text{cm}^3$) into a conical flask.
2. Add soap solution from a burette, $0.5\ \text{cm}^3$ at a time, stoppering and shaking the flask after each addition.
3. Stop when a lather forms that lasts for a set time (for example 30 seconds). Record the volume of soap solution.
4. Repeat with distilled water as a comparison (control), and with a sample of the same water after it has been boiled and cooled.

The more soap solution needed, the harder the water. If boiling reduces the volume needed, the water had temporary hardness.

### By evaporation

Evaporate a measured volume of the water to dryness in a clean evaporating basin. Hard water leaves a white solid residue of dissolved salts; distilled water leaves none.

## Softening water and removing scale

- **Boiling** removes temporary hardness only (calcium carbonate precipitates as scale).
- **Washing soda** (sodium carbonate) removes both kinds: $\ce{Ca^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) -> CaCO3(s)}$.
- **Ion exchange**: a resin swaps calcium and magnesium ions for sodium (or hydrogen) ions; it removes both kinds.
- **Distillation** removes all hardness but uses a lot of energy.

Scale that has already formed is removed with a weak **acid**, such as vinegar (ethanoic acid) or lemon juice (citric acid). The acid reacts with the calcium carbonate, which fizzes as carbon dioxide is given off:

$$
\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2CH3COOH(aq) -> (CH3COO)2Ca(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}
$$

## Worked examples

:::example{title="Example 1: interpreting a soap test"}
| Water | Soap solution needed / cm³ (before boiling) | Soap solution needed / cm³ (after boiling) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| distilled | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| borehole | 11.0 | 4.0 |

1. The borehole water needs far more soap than distilled water, so it is **hard**.
2. Boiling reduces the volume from 11.0 to 4.0 cm³, so it had **temporary** hardness.
3. After boiling it still needs more soap than distilled water, so it also has **permanent** hardness.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: mass of scale from boiling]
1.00 dm³ of water contains 0.162 g of dissolved calcium hydrogencarbonate. Calculate the mass of scale formed when it is boiled. (Ar: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Ca = 40)

1. $M_r(\ce{Ca(HCO3)2}) = 40 + 2 \times (1 + 12 + 48) = 162$.
2. $n = 0.162 \div 162 = 0.00100$ mol.
3. $\ce{Ca(HCO3)2 -> CaCO3 + H2O + CO2}$: ratio $1 : 1$, so $0.00100$ mol of $\ce{CaCO3}$ ($M_r = 100$).
4. Mass of scale $= 0.00100 \times 100 = 0.100\ \text{g}$.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying boiling removes all hardness"}
Boiling removes only **temporary** hardness. Calcium and magnesium sulfates stay dissolved.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling scale 'calcium hydrogencarbonate'"}
Calcium hydrogencarbonate is the **dissolved** compound; scale is insoluble **calcium carbonate**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using strong acid to descale"}
Weak acids such as vinegar or lemon juice are used at home: strong acids could damage the kettle or pipes and are dangerous.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When describing a soap test, mention a **fixed volume** of water, adding soap a little at a time with **shaking**, and a lather that **lasts** for a set time. These are the fair-test points examiners look for.
:::

:::exam-tip
Write the descaling reaction as acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide; the fizzing is the observation.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium ions; soft water lathers easily with soap.
- Temporary hardness (calcium hydrogencarbonate) is removed by boiling, forming scale; permanent hardness (sulfates) is not.
- Scale in kettles, geysers, boilers and pipes wastes energy and blocks pipes; hard water forms scum with soap.
- Hardness is compared by the volume of soap solution needed for a lasting lather.
- Scale (calcium carbonate) is removed with acids such as vinegar or lemon juice, giving off carbon dioxide.
:::
