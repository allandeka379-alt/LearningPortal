## Introduction

Almost all the petrol, diesel and paraffin sold in Zimbabwe began as **crude oil** (petroleum) pumped from rocks deep underground in other countries. Zimbabwe has no crude-oil refinery, so these fuels arrive already refined, much of them through the pipeline from the port of Beira in Mozambique. The chemistry that turns black, sticky crude oil into clear petrol and paraffin happens at refineries elsewhere, but it explains the properties of the fuels we use every day.

In this lesson you will learn which fuels are fossil fuels, what crude oil is made of, how it is separated by **fractional distillation**, how the fractions differ and what each one is used for. You should already know that hydrocarbons in the same homologous series have boiling points that rise with chain length, and how fractional distillation separates liquids with different boiling points.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Fossil fuels"}
Fuels formed over millions of years from the remains of living things: **coal**, **natural gas** and **petroleum** (crude oil).
:::

:::definition{term="Hydrocarbon"}
A compound that contains hydrogen and carbon only.
:::

:::definition{term="Petroleum (crude oil)"}
A thick, dark liquid that is a **mixture of hydrocarbons**, mostly alkanes, with chains from one to more than fifty carbon atoms.
:::

:::definition{term="Fraction"}
A mixture of hydrocarbons with similar chain lengths and boiling points that is collected together.
:::

:::keyfact
Natural gas is mainly **methane**, $\ce{CH4}$. Coal is mainly carbon. Both, like crude oil, are non-renewable.
:::

## Fractional distillation of crude oil

Crude oil cannot be used as it is: it is a mixture of thousands of compounds with different boiling points. It is separated in a tall steel tower called a **fractionating column**.

![Fractionating column for crude oil: hot vapour enters near the bottom and the column is cooler towards the top; from the top down the fractions drawn off are refinery gas (1 to 4 carbon atoms), gasoline, naphtha, kerosene, diesel oil, fuel oil, lubricating oil and bitumen (more than 50), so chain length and boiling point increase down the column.](media:diagrams/crude-oil-fractionating-column.png "Figure: the fractions of crude oil, top to bottom")

1. The crude oil is heated in a furnace until most of it turns into **vapour**.
2. The hot vapour enters the column near the **bottom**. The column is **hot at the bottom and cooler towards the top**.
3. The vapour rises through the column, passing through trays with bubble caps. As it rises it cools.
4. Each hydrocarbon **condenses** when it reaches a level where the temperature is just below its boiling point. Liquids condensing at each level are drawn off as a **fraction**.
5. The smallest molecules (refinery gas) do not condense at all and leave as gas from the **top**. The largest molecules do not vaporise and are drawn off as a thick residue (bitumen) from the **bottom**.

Fractional distillation is a **physical** process: the molecules are separated, not changed.

## The fractions and their uses

Listed from the **top** of the column to the **bottom**:

| Fraction | Approximate number of carbon atoms | Use |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| refinery gas | 1–4 | bottled gas for heating and cooking |
| gasoline (petrol) | 5–10 | fuel for cars |
| naphtha | 6–12 | chemical feedstock (for making plastics and other chemicals) |
| kerosene (paraffin) | 10–16 | jet fuel; paraffin for lamps and stoves |
| diesel oil (gas oil) | 14–20 | fuel for diesel engines in lorries, buses, tractors and generators |
| fuel oil | 20–40 | fuel for ships and for home heating |
| lubricating oil | 30–50 | lubricants for engines and machines, waxes and polishes |
| bitumen | more than 50 | surfacing roads and roofing |

The carbon numbers are rough guides that differ between sources; you need to know the **order** of the fractions and their **uses**.

## How the fractions change up the column

From the bottom to the top of the column the fractions have:

- **shorter chains** (smaller molecules);
- **lower boiling points**, because smaller molecules have weaker forces between them;
- **higher volatility** (they evaporate more easily);
- **lower viscosity** (they are runnier and flow more easily).

Lighter fractions are also paler in colour and catch fire more easily, which is why petrol vapour is so dangerous near a flame.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: why petrol is collected above diesel]
Explain why petrol is drawn off higher up the column than diesel oil.

1. Petrol molecules have shorter chains (about 5–10 carbon atoms) than diesel molecules (about 14–20).
2. Smaller molecules have weaker forces between them, so petrol has lower boiling points.
3. The temperature falls up the column, so petrol vapour rises further before it is cool enough to condense.
4. Petrol therefore condenses, and is collected, higher up than diesel.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: placing a hydrocarbon in a fraction]
Octane, $\ce{C8H18}$, boils at 126 °C. Use the table to decide which fraction it is in and what it is used for.

1. Octane has 8 carbon atoms.
2. In the table, 5–10 carbon atoms is the gasoline (petrol) fraction.
3. Octane is part of petrol, a fuel for cars.
:::

:::example{title="Example 3: comparing two fractions"}
Fuel oil and kerosene are poured into two beakers. The kerosene is pale and runny and evaporates quickly, giving a strong smell; the fuel oil is dark and thick (viscous) and hardly evaporates. Both observations fit the trend: kerosene has shorter molecules than fuel oil, so it is more volatile and less viscous.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the column is hottest at the top"}
The column is **hottest at the bottom**, where the heated vapour enters, and coolest at the top.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying each fraction is a pure compound"}
A fraction is still a **mixture** of hydrocarbons, but with similar chain lengths and a narrow range of boiling points.
:::

:::mistake{title="Calling fractional distillation a chemical reaction"}
No bonds are broken: fractional distillation separates substances by boiling point. Breaking molecules into smaller ones is **cracking**, a chemical change.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe how petroleum is separated" needs: heat to vaporise; column with a temperature gradient (hot at the bottom, cool at the top); vapours rise and condense at different heights according to boiling point; fractions collected.
:::

:::exam-tip
Learn the fractions **in order** with one use each. Questions often give a fraction's position and ask for its use, or give a use and ask which fraction.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Describe how the fractions change" has four standard points: chain length, boiling point, volatility, viscosity. Always say which direction (towards the top or the bottom).
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Coal, natural gas (mainly methane) and petroleum are fossil fuels; hydrocarbons contain hydrogen and carbon only.
- Petroleum is a mixture of hydrocarbons, separated into fractions by fractional distillation (a physical process).
- The column is hot at the bottom and cool at the top; each fraction condenses at its own level.
- Up the column: shorter chains, lower boiling points, more volatile, less viscous.
- Fractions top to bottom: refinery gas, petrol, naphtha, kerosene (paraffin), diesel, fuel oil, lubricating oil, bitumen, each with its use.
:::
