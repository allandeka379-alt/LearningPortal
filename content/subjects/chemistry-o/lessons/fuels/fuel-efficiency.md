## Introduction

In Zimbabwe, meals are cooked over firewood, charcoal, paraffin, LPG or electricity. A family choosing a fuel asks practical questions. How much heat does a kilogram give? How much of that heat actually reaches the pot? What does it cost, and how much smoke does it make? Chemistry answers the first two questions with the **heating value** of a fuel and the **efficiency** with which it is used.

This lesson is **ZIMSEC Form 4** content (fuel efficiency). It builds on the lesson on measuring enthalpy changes, which introduced $q = mc\Delta T$. The kinds of fuel (solid, liquid and gas) are covered in the lesson on types of fuels.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Heating value (calorific value)"}
The energy released when 1 g of a fuel burns completely, usually in kJ/g. (kJ/g and MJ/kg give the same number.)
:::

:::definition{term="Efficiency"}
The percentage of the energy released by a fuel that is transferred usefully, for example to the water in a pot.
:::

:::definition{term="Complete combustion"}
Burning in a plentiful supply of oxygen: the carbon in the fuel forms carbon dioxide and the hydrogen forms water. The most energy is released.
:::

:::definition{term="Incomplete combustion"}
Burning in a limited supply of oxygen: carbon monoxide (a toxic gas) and soot (carbon) form, and less energy is released.
:::

:::formula{name="Heating value and efficiency"}
$$
\text{heating value} = \frac{\text{energy transferred (kJ)}}{\text{mass of fuel burnt (g)}}
$$

$$
\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful energy transferred}}{\text{energy released by the fuel}} \times 100\%
$$
:::

## Measuring a heating value

1. Weigh a spirit burner containing the fuel (or a candle) with its cap on.
2. Measure a volume of water into a copper can and record its temperature.
3. Light the burner under the can, behind a draught shield, and stir the water.
4. After a temperature rise of about 20 °C, put the cap on to put out the flame. Record the highest temperature.
5. Reweigh the burner. Calculate the mass of fuel burnt, $q = mc\Delta T$ and the heating value.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: heating value of paraffin]
A paraffin burner lost 0.80 g while heating 200 g of water from 21.5 °C to 41.5 °C.

1. $\Delta T = 41.5 - 21.5 = 20.0$ °C.
2. $q = mc\Delta T = 200 \times 4.2 \times 20.0 = 16\,800$ J $= 16.8$ kJ.
3. Heating value $= 16.8 \div 0.80 = 21.0$ kJ/g.
4. The data-book heating value of paraffin is about 46 kJ/g, so this experiment transferred $\dfrac{21.0}{46} \times 100 = 46\%$ of the fuel's energy to the water. The rest was lost to the surroundings or not released because of incomplete combustion.
:::

## Typical heating values

These approximate values vary from sample to sample. In a question, always use the values given.

| Fuel | Heating value / kJ/g (approximate) |
| --- | :---: |
| hydrogen | 142 |
| methane (natural gas; the main gas in biogas) | 55 |
| LPG (propane and butane) | 50 |
| paraffin (kerosene) | 46 |
| diesel | 45 |
| ethanol | 30 |
| charcoal | 30 |
| coal | 25–30 (depends on the grade) |
| dry wood | 16 (less when damp) |

## Why some fuels give more energy than others

**Composition.** Hydrocarbon fuels (LPG, petrol, paraffin, diesel) contain only carbon and hydrogen, and release a lot of energy per gram. Fuels that already contain oxygen, such as ethanol ($\ce{C2H5OH}$) and the cellulose in wood, are partly "burnt" already: the oxygen adds mass but releases no energy, so they give less energy per gram. Hydrogen has the highest value of all because its atoms are so light.

**Moisture.** Damp firewood wastes energy evaporating its water. It burns at a lower temperature and makes more smoke, so less useful heat reaches the pot. Dry wood, or charcoal, is better.

**Complete or incomplete combustion.** A clean blue flame shows complete combustion. A yellow, smoky flame and soot on the pot show incomplete combustion: some carbon leaves as soot or carbon monoxide instead of carbon dioxide, so less energy is released, and carbon monoxide is poisonous.

**How the fuel is used.** An open fire loses most of its heat to the air around the pot. An enclosed, insulated stove (such as the tsotso stove, which burns small twigs), a lid on the pot and shelter from the wind all raise the efficiency.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: efficiency of a wood fire]
On a calm day, 0.50 kg of dry wood (heating value 16 kJ/g) was burnt to heat 3.0 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C.

1. Useful energy: $q = 3000 \times 4.2 \times 80 = 1\,008\,000$ J $= 1008$ kJ.
2. Energy released by the wood: $500\ \text{g} \times 16\ \text{kJ/g} = 8000$ kJ.
3. Efficiency $= \dfrac{1008}{8000} \times 100 = 12.6\%$. Nearly nine-tenths of the energy did not heat the water.
:::

## Choosing a fuel

A fuel with a high heating value is not automatically the best choice. Cost, availability, safety and pollution matter too. One way to compare costs is to work out the energy you get for each dollar.

:::worked-solution[Example 3: energy per dollar]
Suppose LPG costs US\$2.00 per kg (heating value 50 kJ/g) and paraffin costs US\$1.20 per litre (heating value 46 kJ/g; 1 litre has a mass of 0.80 kg).

1. LPG: 1 kg $= 1000$ g releases $1000 \times 50 = 50\,000$ kJ, so it gives $50\,000 \div 2.00 = 25\,000$ kJ per US\$1.
2. Paraffin: 1 litre $= 800$ g releases $800 \times 46 = 36\,800$ kJ, so it gives $36\,800 \div 1.20 \approx 30\,700$ kJ per US\$1.
3. At these prices paraffin gives more energy per dollar, even though LPG has the higher heating value per gram. LPG may still be chosen because it burns more cleanly and its stoves are more efficient.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Using the mass of water instead of the mass of fuel"}
$q = mc\Delta T$ uses the mass of **water**. The heating value divides $q$ by the mass of **fuel burnt**.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up per gram and per mole"}
Heating values are **per gram** (kJ/g). Enthalpy changes of combustion are **per mole** (kJ/mol). To convert, divide the value per mole by the molar mass.
:::

:::mistake{title="Forgetting that experiments underestimate"}
A school measurement is always lower than the data-book value because of heat loss and incomplete combustion. Say so when you compare the two.
:::

:::mistake{title="Assuming the highest heating value is the best fuel"}
The best fuel for a purpose also depends on its cost per unit of energy, availability, safety, how efficiently it can be used and the pollution it causes.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Determine the heating value" questions: show the mass of fuel burnt (a subtraction of two balance readings), $q$ in J and kJ, then $q \div$ mass, with the unit kJ/g.
:::

:::exam-tip
"Explain why fuel X is more efficient than fuel Y" needs a reason from the chemistry: composition (oxygen already present), moisture, or complete versus incomplete combustion, linked to more useful energy per gram.
:::

:::exam-tip
In efficiency calculations, the useful energy (the water heated) goes on top and the energy released by the fuel goes underneath. An efficiency above 100% means the fraction is upside down.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Heating value = energy released ÷ mass of fuel burnt, in kJ/g; measure it with a burner, a can of water and $q = mc\Delta T$.
- Hydrocarbons release more energy per gram than fuels that contain oxygen, such as ethanol and wood; hydrogen has the highest value.
- Damp fuel and incomplete combustion (yellow, sooty flame; carbon monoxide) reduce the useful energy.
- Efficiency = useful energy ÷ energy released × 100%; enclosed stoves, lids and wind shields raise it.
- Choose fuels by energy per dollar, efficiency, availability, safety and pollution, not heating value alone.
:::
