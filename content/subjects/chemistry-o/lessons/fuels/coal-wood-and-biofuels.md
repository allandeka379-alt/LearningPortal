## Introduction

Zimbabwe has large reserves of coal around Hwange, wide woodlands, millions of cattle and a farming industry that produces plenty of plant waste. All of these can be turned into fuels. Coal and wood can be heated without air to make **coke** and **charcoal**, cleaner-burning solid fuels, along with useful gases and liquids. Cattle dung and crop waste can be digested by bacteria to make **biogas**. Oil from the seeds of the jatropha plant can be made into **biodiesel**, and sugar cane can be fermented to make **ethanol**.

In this lesson (ZIMSEC Form 3) you will learn how these fuels are produced and compare their advantages and disadvantages. You should already know the difference between fossil and renewable fuels and the properties of a good fuel.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Destructive distillation"}
Heating a substance such as coal or wood strongly **in the absence of air**, so that it decomposes into new products, which are collected.
:::

:::definition{term="Biomass"}
Material from living or recently living things (wood, crop waste, dung) that can be used as a fuel or to make fuels.
:::

:::definition{term="Biogas"}
A fuel gas, mainly methane with carbon dioxide, made when bacteria break down animal and plant waste in the absence of oxygen (anaerobic digestion).
:::

:::definition{term="Biodiesel"}
A diesel fuel made from plant oils (such as jatropha oil) or animal fats.
:::

## Destructive distillation of coal

Coal is heated to a high temperature (about 1000 °C) in closed ovens with no air. If air were present, the coal would simply burn to carbon dioxide; without air it **decomposes** into four products:

![Destructive distillation of coal: powdered coal is heated strongly without air in a hard-glass tube; coke stays behind, black tar and a watery ammoniacal layer condense in a U-tube in cold water, and coal gas burns at the jet.](media:diagrams/coal-destructive-distillation-apparatus.png "Figure: laboratory destructive distillation of coal, with the four products")

| Product | What it is | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **coke** | a grey, porous solid, nearly pure carbon | smokeless fuel; reducing agent and fuel in the blast furnace for making iron |
| **coal gas** | a mixture of mainly hydrogen and methane, with some carbon monoxide | fuel gas for heating |
| **coal tar** | a thick, black, oily liquid | source of chemicals for dyes, medicines, disinfectants and naphthalene; pitch for roads and roofing |
| **ammoniacal liquor** | a solution of ammonia in water | making ammonium sulfate fertiliser |

:::example{title="Example 1: destructive distillation of coal in the laboratory"}
Powdered coal is heated strongly in a hard-glass test tube connected to a U-tube standing in cold water, with a jet at the far end. A yellow-brown smoke passes along; in the U-tube a black, sticky tar collects, with a watery liquid above it that turns damp red litmus blue (ammonia: the ammoniacal liquor). The gas leaving the jet burns with a flame (coal gas). A grey, porous solid, coke, is left in the test tube.
:::

## Destructive distillation of wood

Wood heated without air gives:

- **charcoal**: a black solid, mainly carbon, that burns with a hot, almost smokeless flame;
- **wood gas**: a flammable mixture of carbon monoxide, hydrogen and methane, with carbon dioxide;
- **wood tar**: a thick, dark liquid used to preserve wood;
- **pyroligneous acid**: a watery, **acidic** liquid containing ethanoic acid, methanol and propanone, from which these chemicals can be obtained.

In a traditional earth kiln, wood is stacked, covered with soil and lit with very little air. Part of the wood **burns** to heat the rest, which turns into charcoal. The gases and liquids escape as smoke, so much of the energy and many useful chemicals in the wood are wasted. Closed metal kilns and retorts that collect the gases are more efficient.

## Biogas

Animal dung, crop waste and food waste are mixed with water and fed into a sealed tank called a **digester**. With no oxygen, bacteria break the waste down (**anaerobic digestion**), giving biogas: mainly **methane** (often about 50–70%) with **carbon dioxide** and small amounts of hydrogen sulfide.

![A biogas digester: dung and water are fed into an airtight tank where bacteria break the waste down without oxygen; biogas, about 60% methane, collects at the top and is piped to stoves, and the slurry is drawn off as fertiliser. 2.0 m3 of biogas a day holds 1.2 m3 of methane.](media:diagrams/biogas-digester-anaerobic.png "Figure: an airtight biogas digester, with the gas and the fertiliser slurry as products")

- The digester must be **airtight**: the bacteria that make methane only work without oxygen, and a mixture of methane and air is explosive.
- It works best when **warm** and fed regularly.
- The gas is piped to stoves and lamps; the leftover **slurry** is a good fertiliser.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: how much methane?]
A family digester produces 2.0 m³ of biogas a day. The biogas is 60% methane by volume. What volume of methane is produced each day?

1. Volume of methane $= \dfrac{60}{100} \times 2.0$.
2. $= 1.2\ \text{m}^3$ of methane per day.
:::

## Biodiesel from jatropha oil (outline)

Jatropha is a hardy shrub, often grown as a hedge, whose seeds contain about one-third oil. The seeds are **poisonous**, so the oil is not needed as food, and the plant grows on poor, dry land where food crops do badly.

1. The seeds are dried and **crushed** (pressed) to extract the oil, which is filtered.
2. The oil is heated and reacted with **methanol**, using **sodium hydroxide** (or potassium hydroxide) as a catalyst.
3. Two layers form: **biodiesel** on top and **glycerol** (the by-product) below. The glycerol is drained off.
4. The biodiesel is washed and dried, and can be used in diesel engines on its own or blended with diesel.

## Ethanol as a fuel

Sugar cane juice or molasses can be fermented with yeast to make ethanol, which is distilled and blended with petrol. Fermentation is described in the lesson on making ethanol.

## Comparing these fuels

| Fuel | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| coke | high heating value; burns without smoke | made from coal, which is non-renewable; makes carbon dioxide |
| charcoal | light; burns hot with little smoke | traditional kilns waste wood, causing deforestation; makes carbon monoxide in closed rooms |
| biogas | renewable; uses waste; slurry is a fertiliser; saves firewood | digester costs money to build; needs a steady supply of waste; gas at low pressure |
| biodiesel | renewable; works in diesel engines; jatropha grows on poor land and is not a food | needs land, labour and processing; methanol and sodium hydroxide are hazardous |

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Forgetting \"in the absence of air\""}
Destructive distillation needs strong heating **without air**. With air, coal or wood simply burns.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up coke and charcoal"}
Coke comes from **coal**; charcoal comes from **wood**. Both are mainly carbon.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying biogas is pure methane"}
Biogas is a **mixture**, mainly methane with a lot of carbon dioxide; only the methane burns.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Destructive distillation: strong heating without air. Coal gives coke, coal gas, coal tar and ammoniacal liquor.
- Wood gives charcoal, wood gas, wood tar and acidic pyroligneous acid; earth kilns waste much of the wood.
- Biogas (mainly methane + carbon dioxide) is made by anaerobic digestion of waste in an airtight digester; the slurry is a fertiliser.
- Biodiesel: jatropha oil + methanol (NaOH catalyst) → biodiesel + glycerol.
- Renewable biofuels reduce the use of fossil fuels and firewood, but need land, money or careful management.
:::
