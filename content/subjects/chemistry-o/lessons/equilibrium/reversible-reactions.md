## Introduction

Some weather cards, and the indicating crystals in some packets used to keep goods dry, are blue on a dry winter day but turn pink in the damp air of the rainy season. They contain cobalt(II) chloride, which picks up water vapour from humid air and gives it out again when the air is dry. The same chemical change runs forwards and backwards, again and again.

Most reactions only go one way: once firewood has burnt, you cannot turn the ash and smoke back into wood. A **reversible reaction** is different: its products can react to re-form the original reactants. In this lesson you will learn how to show this in an equation and how changing the conditions decides which way the reaction goes.

You should already know the colours of hydrated and anhydrous copper(II) sulfate and cobalt(II) chloride, and the meaning of endothermic and exothermic.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
The ⇌ sign and the hydrated salts are **Cambridge Core**. The chromate(VI) and dichromate(VI) change is a **ZIMSEC Form 3** example. What happens in a closed system (dynamic equilibrium) is in the next lesson.
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Reversible reaction"}
A chemical reaction in which the products can react to form the original reactants again. It is shown with the sign $\rightleftharpoons$.
:::

:::definition{term="Forward and reverse reactions"}
The **forward** reaction goes from left to right in the equation as written; the **reverse** (backward) reaction goes from right to left.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Energy in both directions"}
If the forward reaction is **endothermic**, the reverse reaction is **exothermic**, and the amount of energy is the same in both directions.
:::

## Hydrated salts: heat one way, water the other

$$
\ce{CuSO4.5H2O(s) <=> CuSO4(s) + 5H2O(g)}
$$

- **Heating** blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate drives the **forward** reaction: water is driven off and white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate forms. The forward reaction is endothermic: it takes in heat.
- **Adding water** to the white powder drives the **reverse** reaction: it turns blue again and gets hot, because the reverse reaction is exothermic.

Cobalt(II) chloride behaves in the same way:

$$
\ce{CoCl2.6H2O(s) <=> CoCl2(s) + 6H2O(g)}
$$

Heating turns the pink hydrated crystals blue; adding water, or leaving them in moist air, turns the blue solid pink again.

:::example{title="Example 1: the weather card"}
A cobalt(II) chloride card hangs in a classroom in Mutare.

- On a humid day in January, water vapour from the air combines with the anhydrous cobalt(II) chloride: the **reverse** reaction happens and the card turns **pink**.
- Left in the sun on a dry day in July, the card warms and loses water: the **forward** reaction happens and it turns **blue**.

The direction of the reaction depends on the conditions: how much water is present and the temperature.
:::

## Ammonium chloride

When white ammonium chloride is heated at the bottom of a test tube, it seems to disappear and then reappear as a white solid near the cooler top of the tube. It has not simply sublimed: it has decomposed into two gases, which react together again when they cool.

$$
\ce{NH4Cl(s) <=> NH3(g) + HCl(g)}
$$

- At the hot bottom of the tube, the forward (endothermic) decomposition happens.
- In the cooler upper part, the reverse (exothermic) reaction re-forms ammonium chloride.

## Chromate(VI) and dichromate(VI) (ZIMSEC)

A yellow solution of potassium chromate(VI) turns **orange** when dilute acid is added, because orange dichromate(VI) ions form. Adding an alkali turns it **yellow** again. This can be repeated many times:

$$
\ce{2CrO4^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) <=> Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}
$$

| Added | Effect on $\ce{H+}$ ions | Direction | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| dilute acid | more $\ce{H+}$ | forward | orange (dichromate) |
| alkali | $\ce{OH-}$ removes $\ce{H+}$ to form water | reverse | yellow (chromate) |

:::example{title="Example 2: switching the colour back and forth"}
Start with an orange solution of potassium dichromate(VI). Adding sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop turns it yellow: the hydroxide ions react with hydrogen ions to form water, removing a reactant from the left-hand side, so the reverse reaction takes over and chromate(VI) ions form. Adding dilute acid then turns it orange again, because the extra hydrogen ions drive the forward reaction.
:::

Chromium(VI) compounds are **toxic** and can cause cancer, so this is done as a teacher demonstration with small amounts, gloves and eye protection.

Indicators work in a similar way. Litmus paper dipped in an acid turns red, and the same paper dipped in an alkali turns blue again: its colour change is reversible.

## Open and closed systems

When hydrated copper(II) sulfate is heated in an **open** test tube, the steam escapes, so the reverse reaction cannot happen and all the crystals eventually turn white. If the reactants and products are kept together in a **closed** container, both reactions can happen at the same time. This leads to **equilibrium**, which is the subject of the next lesson.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Calling every reversible change a reversible reaction"}
Melting and boiling are reversible **physical** changes: no new substance forms. A reversible **reaction** is a chemical change that can go both ways.
:::

:::mistake{title="Thinking the energy change is the same sign both ways"}
If heating drives the forward reaction (endothermic), the reverse reaction gives out the same amount of heat (exothermic).
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying ammonium chloride sublimes"}
Ammonium chloride decomposes into ammonia and hydrogen chloride when heated; these gases recombine on cooling. That is a reversible chemical reaction.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When describing a reversible reaction, give the colour **before and after** and say which direction is happening: "heating turns blue hydrated copper(II) sulfate white: the forward reaction".
:::

:::exam-tip
To explain a change of direction, say what is **added or removed** (heat, water, acid, alkali) and which side of the equation that favours.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- A reversible reaction can go in both directions and is shown with $\rightleftharpoons$.
- Changing the conditions (heating, adding water, adding acid or alkali) can change the direction of a reversible reaction.
- Heating hydrated copper(II) sulfate (blue to white) or cobalt(II) chloride (pink to blue) removes water; adding water reverses the change.
- If the forward reaction is endothermic, the reverse is exothermic by the same amount.
- Ammonium chloride decomposes to ammonia and hydrogen chloride on heating, and they recombine on cooling.
- ZIMSEC: acid turns yellow chromate(VI) orange (dichromate(VI)); alkali turns it yellow again.
:::
