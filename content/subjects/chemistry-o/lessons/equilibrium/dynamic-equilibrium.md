## Introduction

Watch a busy kombi rank in Harare at midday. People arrive all the time and kombis leave full all the time, yet the number of people waiting can stay much the same for an hour. Nothing seems to change, but a great deal is happening: arrivals and departures are going on at the same rate.

A reversible reaction in a closed container behaves in the same way. Eventually the forward and reverse reactions run at the same rate, and the amounts of reactants and products stop changing. This state is called **dynamic equilibrium**. Industry needs to understand it, because the conditions chosen decide how much product a reversible reaction can give.

You should already know what a reversible reaction is, how temperature, pressure and concentration affect rates, and the meaning of endothermic and exothermic.

:::note{title="Syllabus coverage"}
This lesson is **Cambridge Supplement** content and **ZIMSEC Form 3** (dynamic equilibrium and predicting the effect of changing conditions).
:::

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Closed system"}
A system from which no substances can escape and into which no substances can enter, for example a sealed flask.
:::

:::definition{term="Dynamic equilibrium"}
The state of a reversible reaction in a closed system when the **rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction**, so the **concentrations** of reactants and products **no longer change**.
:::

:::definition{term="Position of equilibrium"}
The relative amounts of reactants and products at equilibrium. If the position "shifts to the right", more products form; "to the left" means more reactants.
:::

:::keyfact{title="Equilibrium is dynamic"}
At equilibrium both reactions are still happening; they simply happen at the same rate. The concentrations are **constant**, but they are usually **not equal**.
:::

## How equilibrium is reached

Suppose only reactants are put in a sealed flask.

1. At first the forward reaction is fast (reactants are concentrated) and the reverse reaction does not happen (there are no products yet).
2. As products build up, the forward reaction slows down and the reverse reaction speeds up.
3. Eventually the two rates become equal. From then on, every second, as much product is made as is turned back into reactants, so the concentrations stay constant.

On a graph of concentration against time, the reactant line falls and the product line rises until both become flat.

## The iodine sealed tube (ZIMSEC)

A few crystals of iodine are sealed inside a glass tube, and the tube is warmed in a water bath. Purple iodine vapour forms above the crystals. After a few minutes the colour of the vapour stops getting deeper, although crystals are still present:

$$
\ce{I2(s) <=> I2(g)}
$$

Iodine molecules are still leaving the crystals and returning to them, but at the **same rate**, so the amount of vapour stays constant: a dynamic equilibrium. (Turning solid iodine into vapour is a physical change, not a reaction, but the equilibrium behaves in exactly the same way.) Warming the tube further makes the purple colour deeper (turning solid into vapour takes in energy); cooling one end makes more crystals form there. The tube must be sealed (a closed system), or the vapour would simply escape. Iodine vapour is harmful, so the tube is never opened.

## Changing the conditions (Supplement)

If the conditions of a system at equilibrium are changed, the position of equilibrium **shifts so as to oppose the change**. (This rule is often called **Le Chatelier's principle**.)

| Change | Effect on the position of equilibrium |
| --- | --- |
| add more of a reactant (or remove a product) | shifts to the **right**, making more product |
| increase the **pressure** (gases) | shifts towards the side with **fewer gas molecules**; no change if both sides have the same number |
| increase the **temperature** | shifts in the **endothermic** direction |
| decrease the temperature | shifts in the **exothermic** direction |
| add a **catalyst** | **no change**: both rates increase equally, so equilibrium is simply reached sooner |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: nitrogen dioxide and dinitrogen tetroxide]
$\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}$, where $\ce{N2O4}$ is colourless, $\ce{NO2}$ is brown and the forward reaction is **endothermic** ($\Delta H = +57\ \text{kJ/mol}$). A sealed syringe of the mixture is used.

1. **Heating:** the endothermic direction is the forward one, so the position shifts right: more brown $\ce{NO2}$; the mixture becomes darker.
2. **Cooling in ice:** the position shifts left (exothermic direction): the mixture becomes paler.
3. **Pushing the plunger in (higher pressure):** left side 1 molecule, right side 2 molecules, so the position shifts left, towards fewer molecules: more colourless $\ce{N2O4}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 2: making hydrogen from carbon monoxide and steam]
$\ce{CO(g) + H2O(g) <=> CO2(g) + H2(g)}$, $\Delta H = -41\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (the forward reaction is exothermic).

1. **Pressure:** 2 gas molecules on each side, so changing the pressure does **not** move the position (it only changes the rate).
2. **Temperature:** a lower temperature favours the exothermic (forward) direction, so more hydrogen forms at equilibrium, but the reaction is slower.
3. **Concentration:** removing carbon dioxide as it forms shifts the position to the right, giving more hydrogen.
4. **Catalyst:** no effect on the amount of hydrogen at equilibrium; it is reached faster.
:::

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the reaction stops at equilibrium"}
Both reactions continue at equal rates. That is what "dynamic" means.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying the concentrations are equal at equilibrium"}
They are **constant**, not necessarily equal. A mixture at equilibrium can be mostly products or mostly reactants.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a catalyst increases the yield"}
A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so the position of equilibrium does not change.
:::

:::mistake{title="Applying pressure to solids and liquids"}
Only gas molecules count when you decide the effect of pressure. If both sides have the same number of gas molecules, pressure has no effect on the position.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Show your reasoning in two steps: the **fact** ("the forward reaction is exothermic" or "the right-hand side has fewer gas molecules") and the **conclusion** ("so increasing the temperature shifts the position to the left").
:::

:::exam-tip
When the question gives a $\Delta H$ value, check its sign: negative means the forward reaction is exothermic, so a higher temperature lowers the yield of products.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Dynamic equilibrium happens in a closed system when the forward and reverse rates are equal and the concentrations stop changing.
- At equilibrium both reactions continue; concentrations are constant but not usually equal.
- Adding a reactant or removing a product shifts the position to the right.
- Higher pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules; higher temperature favours the endothermic direction.
- A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium; it only makes equilibrium reached sooner.
- ZIMSEC: iodine in a sealed tube shows a dynamic equilibrium between solid and vapour.
:::
