## Introduction

Farmers around Marondera spread agricultural lime on acidic soils before planting maize. Builders mix slaked lime into mortar. A distress flare at sea burns with a dazzling white light. All three depend on **Group II** elements: calcium in lime and limestone, magnesium in the flare. Even the chlorophyll in a maize leaf has a magnesium atom at its centre.

In this lesson you will study three Group II metals, magnesium, calcium and barium, and how they react with **oxygen** and with **water**. You will see that they behave in a similar way because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons, and that their reactions become more vigorous **down the group**.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Group II trends are ZIMSEC O-Level Form 3 content (Periodic table: group trends). Cambridge IGCSE does not have a Group II topic, although the reactions of magnesium and calcium with water also appear in the reactivity series.
:::

You should already be able to write electronic configurations for the first 20 elements and balance simple equations.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Group II (the alkaline earth metals)"}
The vertical column of the Periodic Table containing beryllium, magnesium, calcium, strontium, barium and radium. Each atom has **two** electrons in its outer shell.
:::

:::definition{term="Valence electrons"}
The electrons in the outer shell of an atom, which take part in bonding. For Groups I to VII the number of valence electrons equals the group number.
:::

:::definition{term="Basic oxide"}
A metal oxide that reacts with an acid to form a salt and water. Magnesium oxide, calcium oxide and barium oxide are basic oxides.
:::

## Electrons and ions

Each Group II atom loses its **two** outer-shell electrons to form an ion with a $2+$ charge and the electronic configuration of a noble gas.

| Element | Electronic configuration | Ion | Configuration of ion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium, Mg | 2,8,2 | $\ce{Mg^2+}$ | 2,8 |
| Calcium, Ca | 2,8,8,2 | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | 2,8,8 |
| Barium, Ba | two outer-shell electrons (six shells) | $\ce{Ba^2+}$ | full outer shell |

Because the ion is $2+$, one oxide ion ($\ce{O^2-}$) balances it, but **two** hydroxide or chloride ions are needed: MgO, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$.

:::keyfact{title="Group II compounds to know"}
- Oxides: MgO, CaO, BaO (white solids, basic).
- Hydroxides: $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$, $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$, $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$.
- Chlorides: $\ce{MgCl2}$, $\ce{CaCl2}$, $\ce{BaCl2}$.
:::

## Reactions with oxygen

When heated in air or oxygen, all three metals burn to form a white metal oxide:

$$
\ce{2M(s) + O2(g) -> 2MO(s)}
$$

| Metal | What you see when it burns | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium | Burns with a brilliant white flame, leaving a white powder | Magnesium oxide, MgO |
| Calcium | Burns with an orange-red (brick-red) flame | Calcium oxide, CaO |
| Barium | Burns with a pale green flame | Barium oxide, BaO |

Calcium and barium tarnish in air at room temperature much faster than magnesium, and barium is stored under oil like the Group I metals. Magnesium ribbon is usually cleaned with sandpaper before an experiment to remove its thin, dull layer of oxide.

The oxides are **bases**. They react with acids to form a salt and water, for example $\ce{MgO + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2O}$. Calcium oxide (quicklime) reacts with water, giving out a lot of heat, to form calcium hydroxide (slaked lime): $\ce{CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2}$.

:::exam-tip
Never look directly at burning magnesium: the white light is intense enough to damage your eyes. "Use tongs and do not look directly at the flame" is a common mark-earning precaution.
:::

## Reactions with water

| Metal | With cold water | With steam |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnesium | Reacts **very slowly**: a few bubbles of hydrogen form on the ribbon over several days | Burns with a bright white glow, forming white **magnesium oxide** and hydrogen |
| Calcium | Fizzes steadily; the metal sinks at first, warms the water and gets smaller; the water turns **cloudy white** | (reacts readily with cold water) |
| Barium | Fizzes rapidly, **more vigorously than calcium**; a colourless alkaline solution forms | (reacts readily with cold water) |

The general equation with cold water is

$$
\ce{M(s) + 2H2O(l) -> M(OH)2 + H2(g)}
$$

With **steam**, magnesium forms the **oxide**, not the hydroxide:

$$
\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}
$$

The cloudiness with calcium is a suspension of calcium hydroxide, which is only **slightly soluble** in water. If it is filtered, the clear filtrate is **limewater**. Barium hydroxide is much more soluble, so its solution stays clear. All the hydroxide solutions are alkaline: they turn red litmus blue.

:::worked-solution[Example 1: calcium and cold water]
A small piece of calcium is added to a beaker of water. Write the equation and describe what is seen.
1. Word equation: calcium + water $\rightarrow$ calcium hydroxide + hydrogen.
2. Formulae: calcium forms $\ce{Ca^2+}$, so its hydroxide needs two $\ce{OH-}$ ions: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. Hydrogen gas is $\ce{H2}$.
3. Balance: two water molecules supply the two OH groups and the $\ce{H2}$: $\ce{Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}$. (The calcium hydroxide that does not dissolve forms the cloudiness.)
4. Observations: steady fizzing, the calcium gets smaller and disappears, the beaker feels warm, the water turns cloudy white.
5. Test the gas: a lighted splint gives a squeaky pop, so the gas is hydrogen. The solution turns red litmus blue.
:::

### Magnesium and steam: the experiment in words

A boiling tube is clamped horizontally. Mineral wool soaked in water sits at the closed end, and a coil of clean magnesium ribbon lies in the middle. The magnesium is heated strongly, then the flame is moved briefly to the wet mineral wool so that steam passes over the hot metal. The magnesium glows with a bright white light and a white solid (magnesium oxide) is left. Hydrogen leaves through a delivery tube and can be lit at its end. If the gas is collected over water, the delivery tube must be lifted out of the water before heating stops, to prevent **suck-back** of cold water into the hot tube.

## The trend: reactivity increases down the group

Magnesium hardly reacts with cold water, calcium reacts steadily and barium reacts rapidly. The same order appears with oxygen. So reactivity **increases down Group II**: Mg < Ca < Sr < Ba.

The reason is the same as in Group I. When a Group II metal reacts, its atoms **lose their two outer electrons**. Down the group the atoms have more shells, so the outer electrons are **further from the nucleus** and less strongly attracted. They are lost more easily, so the metal reacts faster.

Group II metals are less reactive than the Group I metal in the same period: calcium reacts with water much more gently than potassium, because two electrons must be lost instead of one.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: predicting the properties of strontium]
Strontium, Sr, lies between calcium and barium in Group II. Predict how it behaves.
1. Outer electrons: two, like every Group II element, so it forms $\ce{Sr^2+}$ ions.
2. Formulae, by analogy with calcium: oxide SrO, hydroxide $\ce{Sr(OH)2}$, chloride $\ce{SrCl2}$.
3. With cold water: it reacts more vigorously than calcium but less vigorously than barium, fizzing and forming an alkaline solution: $\ce{Sr + 2H2O -> Sr(OH)2 + H2}$.
4. With oxygen: it burns to form a white oxide, $\ce{2Sr + O2 -> 2SrO}$. (Strontium compounds give the crimson colour in fireworks.)
:::

## Uses linked to properties

- **Magnesium** burns with an intense white light, so it is used in flares and fireworks. Magnesium hydroxide is a mild base used in indigestion remedies.
- **Calcium compounds**: limestone (calcium carbonate) is used to make cement and quicklime; slaked lime neutralises acidic soils and is used in mortar and whitewash.
- **Barium sulfate** is insoluble, so it can be swallowed safely as a "barium meal" before an X-ray of the gut. Soluble barium compounds are poisonous.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Giving the hydroxide as the product with steam"}
Magnesium and steam form magnesium **oxide** and hydrogen. The hydroxide forms (very slowly) with cold water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing CaOH"}
Calcium forms $\ce{Ca^2+}$, so two hydroxide ions are needed: $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. The same applies to $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ and $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$.
:::

:::mistake{title="Explaining the cloudiness as a gas or as 'the calcium dissolving'"}
The white cloudiness is solid calcium hydroxide, which is only slightly soluble. The gas (hydrogen) is invisible: you see bubbles.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying magnesium does not react with water"}
Magnesium does react with cold water, but very slowly. It reacts readily with steam.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
Keep **observations** (fizzing, white flame, cloudy solution, metal disappears) separate from **products** (hydrogen, calcium hydroxide). "Hydrogen is given off" is not an observation; "bubbles" or "fizzing" is.
:::

:::exam-tip
To **explain** the trend in reactivity, give three linked points: more shells down the group, outer electrons further from the nucleus (less attraction), so they are lost more easily.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Group II atoms have two outer-shell (valence) electrons and form $2+$ ions such as $\ce{Mg^2+}$ (2,8) and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (2,8,8).
- They burn in oxygen to form white basic oxides: Mg (brilliant white flame), Ca (orange-red), Ba (pale green).
- Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water but readily with steam: $\ce{Mg + H2O -> MgO + H2}$.
- Calcium and barium react with cold water to form a hydroxide and hydrogen: $\ce{Ca + 2H2O -> Ca(OH)2 + H2}$.
- Reactivity increases down the group because the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and are lost more easily.
:::
