## Introduction

Zimbabwe is rich in transition metals. The Great Dyke, which runs across the middle of the country, holds large deposits of chromium ore and of the platinum-group metals (platinum, palladium and rhodium). Nickel is mined near Bindura, and iron ore has fed the country's steel industry for decades. Chromium and nickel end up in stainless-steel sinks, and platinum-group metals in the catalytic converters that clean car exhaust gases all over the world.

In this lesson you will learn what makes the **transition elements** different from the metals of Groups I and II, why their compounds are so colourful, and why industry and living things depend on them.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Transition elements"}
The block of metals in the middle of the Periodic Table, between Group II and Group III. In Period 4 they run from scandium to zinc and include vanadium, chromium, manganese, iron, cobalt, nickel and copper.
:::

:::definition{term="Oxidation number"}
A number that shows how many electrons an atom has lost (positive) or gained (negative) in a compound. For a simple metal ion it equals the charge: iron in $\ce{Fe^3+}$ has oxidation number $+3$. It is written as a Roman numeral in the name: iron(III).
:::

:::definition{term="Catalyst"}
A substance that increases the rate of a reaction and is chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction.
:::

## Properties compared with Group I metals

All metals conduct electricity and heat and are shiny when clean. Transition elements differ from the Group I metals in several important ways.

| Metal | Group | Density / g/cm³ | Melting point / °C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium | I | $0.97$ | $98$ |
| Potassium | I | $0.86$ | $63$ |
| Iron | transition | $7.87$ | $1538$ |
| Copper | transition | $8.96$ | $1085$ |
| Nickel | transition | $8.91$ | $1455$ |
| Chromium | transition | $7.19$ | $1907$ |

:::keyfact{title="Typical properties of transition elements"}
- **High densities** and **high melting points**.
- Hard and strong (Group I metals are soft).
- Much **less reactive** than Group I and II metals: iron rusts slowly, and copper does not react with water or with dilute hydrochloric or sulfuric acid.
- They form **coloured compounds**. Compounds of Group I and II metals are usually white and give colourless solutions; the exceptions, such as purple potassium manganate(VII), are coloured because they also contain a transition element.
- They and their compounds often act as **catalysts**.
- **Supplement:** they form ions with **different oxidation numbers**, such as $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
:::

:::worked-solution[Example 1: is element X a transition element?]
Element X melts at $1495\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and has a density of $8.9\ \text{g/cm}^3$. It forms pink and blue compounds, and its compounds speed up some reactions without being used up. Decide whether X is a transition element.
1. A high melting point and a high density are typical of transition metals, not Group I or II metals.
2. Coloured compounds (pink and blue) are typical of transition elements; compounds of Group I and II metals are usually white.
3. Speeding up reactions without being used up means X or its compounds act as catalysts.
4. Conclusion: every piece of evidence points to a transition element. (X is cobalt.)
:::

## Coloured compounds

Many transition-metal compounds are coloured, both as solids and in solution. Copper(II) sulfate crystals are blue, iron(III) oxide is red-brown and chromium(III) oxide is green.

**ZIMSEC:** learn the colours of these ions in aqueous solution.

| Ion | Name | Colour in solution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\ce{Cu^2+}$ | copper(II) | blue |
| $\ce{Fe^2+}$ | iron(II) | pale green |
| $\ce{Fe^3+}$ | iron(III) | yellow-brown |
| $\ce{Cr^3+}$ | chromium(III) | green |
| $\ce{MnO4-}$ | manganate(VII) (permanganate) | purple |
| $\ce{Cr2O7^2-}$ | dichromate(VI) | orange |
| $\ce{CrO4^2-}$ | chromate(VI) | yellow |

## Variable oxidation numbers (Supplement)

A Group I metal always forms $1+$ ions and a Group II metal always forms $2+$ ions. Transition elements can form ions with **different charges**, so their compounds need a Roman numeral in the name.

| Element | Lower oxidation number | Higher oxidation number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron | iron(II): FeO, $\ce{FeCl2}$, $\ce{FeSO4}$ | iron(III): $\ce{Fe2O3}$, $\ce{FeCl3}$ |
| Copper | copper(I): $\ce{Cu2O}$ (red) | copper(II): CuO (black), $\ce{CuSO4}$ |
| Manganese | manganese(II): $\ce{MnSO4}$ | manganese(VII): $\ce{KMnO4}$ |

To find an oxidation number in a compound, use the rule that the oxidation numbers add up to zero (or to the charge of an ion), taking oxygen as $-2$, a sulfate ion as $-2$ overall and potassium as $+1$.

:::worked-solution[Example 2: working out oxidation numbers]
Find the oxidation number of the transition element and name each compound: (a) $\ce{V2O5}$, the catalyst in the Contact process; (b) $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$.
1. (a) Let vanadium be $x$. Two V and five O: $2x + 5(-2) = 0$, so $2x = 10$ and $x = +5$.
2. Name: vanadium(V) oxide.
3. (b) Each sulfate ion is $2-$, so three sulfates give $-6$. Two Fe: $2x - 6 = 0$, so $x = +3$.
4. Name: iron(III) sulfate.
:::

An iron(II) solution is pale green, but left open to the air it slowly turns yellow-brown: oxygen **oxidises** $\ce{Fe^2+}$ to $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Each iron(II) ion loses one electron: $\ce{Fe^2+ -> Fe^3+ + e-}$.

## Transition elements as catalysts

| Catalyst | Process |
| --- | --- |
| Iron | Haber process: making ammonia for fertilisers |
| Vanadium(V) oxide, $\ce{V2O5}$ | Contact process: making sulfuric acid |
| Nickel | Hydrogenation: hardening vegetable oils to make margarine |
| Platinum, palladium, rhodium | Catalytic converters in car exhausts |
| Manganese(IV) oxide, $\ce{MnO2}$ | Decomposing hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen (laboratory) |

Catalysts let industry make products faster at lower temperatures, which saves energy and money. Because a catalyst is not used up, a small amount works for a long time.

## Importance in alloys and in living things (ZIMSEC)

- **Alloys:** steel is iron with a little carbon; **stainless steel** contains chromium and nickel, so it resists rusting and is used for sinks, cutlery and surgical instruments. Manganese steel is very hard (rock crushers in mines), vanadium steel is tough (spanners and springs), and brass (copper and zinc) and cupronickel (copper and nickel, used for coins) are copper alloys.
- **Uses of the metals:** copper for electrical wiring and water pipes; chromium plating for shiny, corrosion-resistant taps; cobalt in rechargeable batteries.
- **Living things:** **haemoglobin**, the red pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen, contains iron. A diet short of iron (found in beans, liver and dark green vegetables such as covo) can cause anaemia. Cobalt is part of vitamin $\text{B}_{12}$.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Treating transition metals like sodium"}
Transition metals are hard, dense, high-melting and much less reactive. Iron and copper do not fizz in cold water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Using the wrong Roman numeral"}
The Roman numeral is the oxidation number, not the number of atoms. $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is iron(III) oxide, not iron(II) oxide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Saying a catalyst is used up"}
A catalyst speeds up a reaction and is **unchanged** at the end. The same mass can be recovered.
:::

:::mistake{title="Mixing up similar colours"}
Iron(II) is **pale green** and iron(III) **yellow-brown**. Dichromate(VI) is **orange** and chromate(VI) **yellow**.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
When asked to **compare** transition elements with Group I metals, make paired statements: "transition metals have **higher** melting points and **higher** densities than Group I metals". Quote data when it is given.
:::

:::exam-tip
For catalyst questions, name both the catalyst and the process (iron in the Haber process), and state that the catalyst is unchanged at the end.
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Transition elements are the block of metals between Groups II and III, such as iron, copper, nickel and chromium.
- They have high densities and melting points, are hard and are less reactive than Group I metals.
- They form coloured compounds: $\ce{Cu^2+}$ blue, $\ce{Fe^2+}$ pale green, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ yellow-brown, $\ce{MnO4-}$ purple.
- **Supplement:** they have variable oxidation numbers, shown by Roman numerals: iron(II) and iron(III).
- They and their compounds are important catalysts (iron in the Haber process, $\ce{V2O5}$ in the Contact process, nickel for margarine), and iron is essential in haemoglobin.
:::
