## Introduction

Lithium mined at Bikita ends up in the rechargeable batteries of phones and electric cars. Yet you will never find a lump of lithium, sodium or potassium metal lying in a rock: these **alkali metals** are so reactive that in nature they only exist in compounds, such as the sodium chloride in salt or the potassium compounds in fertilisers.

In the laboratory, the metals are kept under oil to stop them reacting with air and water. In this lesson you will learn their properties, the trends down the group, and how they react with water.

:::note{title="Syllabus"}
Group I is in the Cambridge IGCSE syllabus (topic 8.2). ZIMSEC O-Level studies Groups II and VII instead.
:::

You should know that Group I atoms have one outer-shell electron and form $1+$ ions.

## Key definitions

:::definition{term="Alkali metals"}
The elements of Group I: lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium and francium. They react with water to form alkaline solutions.
:::

:::definition{term="Alkali"}
A base that dissolves in water, forming a solution with a pH above 7. Group I hydroxides, such as sodium hydroxide, are alkalis.
:::

## Physical properties

Lithium, sodium and potassium are:

- **soft**: they can be cut with a knife;
- **shiny** when freshly cut, but they **tarnish** (go dull) within seconds as they react with oxygen and water vapour in the air;
- of **low density**: all three float on water;
- of **low melting point** for metals (sodium melts at $98\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

:::keyfact{title="General trends down Group I"}
- **Melting point decreases.**
- **Density increases** (as a general trend; potassium is an exception, being less dense than sodium).
- **Reactivity increases.**
:::

## Reactions with water

Every Group I metal reacts with cold water to form a **metal hydroxide** and **hydrogen**:

$$
\text{metal} + \text{water} \rightarrow \text{metal hydroxide} + \text{hydrogen}
$$

The hydroxide dissolves, making an **alkaline** solution: universal indicator turns blue or purple.

| Metal | Observations |
| --- | --- |
| Lithium | Floats; fizzes steadily; moves slowly around the surface; gradually disappears |
| Sodium | Floats; melts into a shiny ball (the reaction gives out heat and sodium has a low melting point); fizzes rapidly; darts around the surface; disappears quickly |
| Potassium | Floats; melts; fizzes violently; the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame; may spit and crackle; disappears very quickly |

:::worked-solution[Example 1: potassium and water]
1. Word equation: potassium + water $\rightarrow$ potassium hydroxide + hydrogen.
2. Formulae: K, $\ce{H2O}$, KOH, $\ce{H2}$.
3. Balance: hydrogen gas is $\ce{H2}$, so two water molecules and two potassium atoms are needed: $\ce{2K(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2KOH(aq) + H2(g)}$.
4. Observations: the potassium floats, melts, fizzes violently and the hydrogen burns with a lilac flame.
5. The solution turns universal indicator purple: potassium hydroxide is a strong alkali.
:::

## Predicting the properties of other Group I elements

Because all Group I elements have one outer electron, they react in the same way. The trends let you predict how rubidium and caesium behave: both react **even more violently** (explosively) with water, forming rubidium hydroxide, RbOH, or caesium hydroxide, CsOH, and hydrogen.

![Bar chart: the melting points of the Group I metals fall down the group, lithium 181, sodium 98, potassium 63, rubidium 39 and caesium 28 degrees Celsius (measured), so the decreases get smaller.](media:diagrams/group-i-melting-points-trend.png "Figure: melting points of lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium and caesium")

:::worked-solution[Example 2: predicting the melting point of caesium]
Melting points: lithium $181\ ^\circ\text{C}$, sodium $98\ ^\circ\text{C}$, potassium $63\ ^\circ\text{C}$, rubidium $39\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Predict the melting point of caesium.
1. Trend: the melting point decreases down the group.
2. The decreases get smaller: $83$, $35$, $24$.
3. So caesium should melt a little below $39\ ^\circ\text{C}$: a prediction of about $25$ to $35\ ^\circ\text{C}$ is sensible. (The measured value is $28\ ^\circ\text{C}$: caesium would melt on a hot day in Kariba.)
:::

:::note{title="Why reactivity increases down the group"}
When a Group I metal reacts, each atom loses its single outer electron. Going down the group, the outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus, so it is held less strongly and is lost more easily. This explanation is useful, although the syllabus only asks you to describe the trend.
:::

## Safety

Group I metals react violently with water and can burn the skin. Use only rice-grain-sized pieces, handle them with tongs (never fingers), use a large trough of water behind a safety screen and wear eye protection. Potassium is only demonstrated by the teacher.

## Common mistakes

:::mistake{title="Saying the product is an oxide"}
With water, Group I metals form **hydroxides**: sodium hydroxide, NaOH, not sodium oxide.
:::

:::mistake{title="Writing H instead of H₂"}
Hydrogen gas is diatomic: write $\ce{H2}$, then balance with 2 atoms of metal and 2 molecules of water.
:::

:::mistake{title="Describing a gas you cannot see"}
Write "fizzing" or "effervescence", not "hydrogen is seen". Gases are colourless and invisible; bubbles are what you see.
:::

## Exam tips

:::exam-tip
"Describe what you would **see**" questions want observations: floats, fizzes, moves around, melts into a ball, gets smaller and disappears, lilac flame (potassium). Each distinct observation is usually one mark.
:::

:::exam-tip
In prediction questions, compare the new element with its neighbours: "rubidium is below potassium, so it reacts more vigorously than potassium".
:::

## Summary

:::summary
- Group I metals are soft, shiny when cut, of low density and low melting point, and very reactive, so they are stored under oil.
- Down the group: melting point decreases, density generally increases and reactivity increases.
- With water they form a metal hydroxide (an alkali) and hydrogen: $\ce{2M + 2H2O -> 2MOH + H2}$.
- Lithium fizzes steadily, sodium melts into a ball and darts about, potassium bursts into a lilac flame.
- Rubidium and caesium can be predicted to react even more violently.
:::
